1,25D3 reprograms mitochondrial quality control via sirtuin and sensitizes glioblastoma to chemotherapy
Authors: Karrie M. Kiang, Yixiong Shen, Yogesh K.H. Wong, Bo Chen, Junbo Liao, Anza Mnahal, Wanjun Tang, Zhiyuan Zhu, Shuhan Cao, Carmen S.C. Lo, Sang Jin Lee, Hongbo Guo, Liyang Zhang, Gilberto Ka-Kit Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor cells adapt to therapeutic stress by preserving mitochondrial integrity through mitophagy, but excessive mitophagy can overwhelm this adaptative mechanism and precipitate mitochondrial collapse. Here, we demonstrate that 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1,25D3) reduces glioblastoma resistance to the standard chemotherapeutic temozolomide by driving mitophagic overload and mitochondrial dysfunction. We identified mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT4 as a key downstream effector of mitochondrial metabolism and quality control triggered by 1,25D3-induced mitochondrial stress. Pharmacological levels of 1,25D3 activate mitophagy by transcriptionally upregulating SIRT4 through vitamin D receptor (VDR) signaling. SIRT4, which is frequently downregulated in glioblastoma, suppresses glioblastoma glutamine metabolism by inhibiting glutamate dehydrogenase activity and limiting α-ketoglutarate availability, thereby integrating metabolic stress with enhanced mitophagy. This VDR-SIRT4 axis shifts mitophagy from a cytoprotective process to a lethal pathway, selectively sensitizing tumor cells while sparing normal astrocytes and brain tissue. By exploiting mitochondrial quality control as a metabolic vulnerability, 1,25D3 enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy and provides a translational rationale for repurposing 1,25D3 in resistant glioblastoma.

Introduction

Although Warburg’s theory suggests aerobic glycolysis as the dominant energy pathway in cancer cells, accumulating evidence now highlights the continued reliance on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as an alternative energy source in cancer.1 OXPHOS not only fuels ATP production to support tumor growth but also drives tumorigenesis and confers metastatic competence.2,3 Thus, mitochondria function as central regulators of cancer cell survival by sustaining bioenergetic demand, buffering oxidative stress, and controlling multiple cell death pathways.4,5,6 This alteration of mitochondrial functions is considered a ubiquitous cancer tactic to enhance the cancer cell survival threshold and promote chemoresistance.2,7 Nevertheless, mitochondrial alterations are frequently observed in cancer, and despite frequent mutations in mitochondrial genes and the accumulation of structurally compromised “ghost” mitochondria, tumor cells can adapt to and survive the unfavorable tumor microenvironment.8

One essential mechanism that maintains mitochondrial function in living tissues is mitophagy—the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria.9 In cancer, mitophagy imparts metabolic flexibility by removing dysfunctional organelles and recycling nutrients, thereby sustaining tumor growth under stress.10 Genetic models have demonstrated that the loss of Atg7, an essential autophagy gene, results in defective autophagy and suppresses K-ras-driven cancer progression while promoting the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria.11 Deletion of Atg7 in BrafV600E-driven cancer also results in glutamine deficiency and compromised tumor growth in the late stage of tumorigenesis.12 Furthermore, the expression of ARIH1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, triggers mitophagy in cancer cells in a PINK1-dependent manner and protects cancer cells against chemotherapy-induced cell death.13 These findings support the notion that autophagy may confer a survival advantage to tumor cells, and yet, paradoxically, defective mitophagy has also been associated with increased tumor initiation14 and enhanced metastatic capacity,15 underscoring a context-dependent role for mitophagy in cancer.16

From a therapeutic perspective, autophagy is widely considered a survival mechanism,16 making it an attractive target in cancer therapy. However, only a small number of autophagy modulators have been described thus far, and pharmacological inhibitors, such as hydroxychloroquine, have shown limited efficacy.17 The dual role of autophagy further complicates therapeutic targeting. Intriguingly, recent study has shown that selective autophagy pathways, such as chaperone-mediated autophagy, can regulate key metabolic enzymes in glioblastoma,18 highlighting how autophagy intersects with tumor metabolism. Moreover, under certain conditions, further stimulation of mitophagy can push cancer cells toward metabolic collapse, a concept known as lethal mitophagy.16 Leveraging this vulnerability could therefore possibly enhance the efficacy of conventional chemotherapies, particularly in tumors that exhibit high autophagic dependence during treatment-induced stress.19

In this context, vitamin D has emerged as a potential chemosensitizing agent,20 as reports have shown its capacity to enhance cytotoxic autophagy in glioblastoma when combined with temozolomide (TMZ).21 This response is consistent with a multitude of other reports that support the therapeutic benefits of using vitamin D during chemotherapy treatment.22,23 However, no evidence has yet confirmed that vitamin D might drive mitophagy to a level that disrupts mitochondrial integrity and sensitizes tumors to chemotherapy. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms mediating vitamin D-induced mitophagy and whether this process can be therapeutically exploited remain far from clear.

In the present study, we identified a critical vulnerability in recurrent glioblastomas that are characterized by increased mitophagy following prolonged TMZ treatment. We hypothesized that further activation of mitophagy using the active vitamin D metabolite 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (1,25D3; calcitriol) could drive mitophagy to exceed the adaptive threshold and promote mitochondrial dysfunction via the mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT4. Here, we demonstrate that 1,25D3 induces the transcription of SIRT4 through the direct binding of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) to its promoter and triggers mitophagic overload and tumor suppression. We further demonstrate that SIRT4 is frequently downregulated in glioblastoma and is also associated with enhanced glutamine metabolism. Our findings reveal a therapeutic mechanism by which 1,25D3 can enhance chemotherapy-induced mitophagy and provides a rationale for targeting mitochondrial quality control to overcome chemotherapy resistance in glioblastoma.

Assessment of the therapeutic effect of combined 1,25D3 and TMZ treatment in tumor cells transfected with the mCherry-GFP-LC3 plasmid (Figure S1B) demonstrated increased autophagic flux compared with control cells. This flux was further augmented by the combination treatment with TMZ and 1,25D3, as indicated by a significant increase in red puncta (mCherry+/GFP−, autolysosomes) in the cells (Figures 1C and 1D). When compared to cells treated with TMZ alone, overactivation of autophagy is further confirmed by the increased conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II and a degradation of P62 following combined treatment. We also observed an upregulation of the VDR in 1,25D3-treated tumors (Figure 1E), indicating that the effect may be mediated through VDR signaling mechanisms.

We also evaluated the therapeutic potential of combining 1,25D3 and TMZ. It is demonstrated to enhance the sensitivity of TMZ and further retarded tumor growth in primary glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) (Figures 1F and S1C), which also reduced tumor proliferation (Figure 1G) and the clonogenic capacity of tumor cells (Figure 1H). Adding 1,25D3 to TMZ resulted in more superior suppression of tumor growth in immunocompetent mice (Figure S1D); however, given the widely reported function of vitamin D in immune modulation, here, we used the immunodeficient mice model to minimize the effects of the 1,25D3-mediated immune response on tumor growth (Figures 1I, 1J, and S1E). Reinforcing these results with the orthotopic xenograft model, we are able to confirm that adding 1,25D3 to TMZ treatment can sensitize tumor cells to the cytotoxic effects of TMZ, and it further inhibited tumor growth by inducing apoptosis (Figures 1K and 1L), possibly regardless of the immunomodulatory function of 1,25D3. It is noteworthy that we found treatment with 1,25D3 alone was also effective in suppressing tumor growth, although to a lesser extent than TMZ or the combination treatment. From a clinical perspective, we measured serum calcium and serum phosphate levels to confirm that the treated mice were well tolerated to high-dose 1,25D3 treatment (Figure S1F), and we observed no associated morphological changes in liver tissue (Figure S1G).

1,25D3 enhanced temozolomide sensitivity by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction

Vitamin D has been intricately linked to mitochondrial functions in different ways, thereby regulating cellular bioenergetics in both normal and cancer cells. We next sought to determine whether and how 1,25D3 affects mitochondrial dynamics in cancer cells. By using a two-photon microscopy through a cranial window (Figure S2A), mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) in the tumor region was visualized by intravenous injection of fluorescence dye rhodamine-123 as a measure of mitochondrial function. Here, we revealed a remarkable reduction of the mitochondrial membrane potential in tumor cells following 1,25D3 treatment (Figure 2A), suggesting a direct action of 1,25D3 on tumor cell mitochondria. Following this observation, we reasoned that mitochondrial damage may have occurred. Consistent with our expectation, examination of the tumor cells by electron microscopy revealed mitochondrial damage in the form of mitochondrial swelling and degradation of cristae in all the treated groups (Figure 2B). The expression of mitochondrial biogenesis markers are also in line with our observation, implicating impaired biogenesis, particularly following the combined treatment, as suggested by the upregulation of DRP1 and MFF, which are regulators of mitochondrial fission, and the downregulation of OPA1, a regulator of mitochondrial fusion (Figure 2C). Surprisingly, treatment with 1,25D3, either as monotherapy or in combination therapy with TMZ, selectively induced mitochondrial dysfunction in glioblastoma cells, as indicated by a reduction in the mitochondrial membrane potential (determined using MitoTracker red) (Figure 2D). No similar effect was seen in normal cells (normal human astrocytes) treated with 1,25D3 (Figure S2B), in agreement with the neuroprotective function of vitamin D reported in the literature. With the growing appreciation that mitochondria act as important mediators in driving cancer and harbor mitochondrial plasticity, allowing cancer cells to adapt to cellular and extrinsic alterations, metabolic reprogramming often underlies chemoresistance in cancer.24 To further evaluate the intrinsic mitochondrial respiration (i.e., OXPHOS), we measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) following 1,25D3 and TMZ treatment as a readout of mitochondrial function (Figures 2E and S2C). It is noticeable that TMZ treatment enhanced respiratory chain capacity (spare capacity) (Figure 2F), indicating that TMZ-treated cancer cells increased their capacity to respond to the increased energy demand when under stress. However, the combination of 1,25D3 and TMZ effectively suppressed mitochondrial function, as determined by changes in all measured parameters, when compared to TMZ treatment alone (Figure 2F), underscoring the metabolic vulnerabilities created by 1,25D3, which circumvented the gain in metabolic capacity induced by TMZ treatment.

Combination treatment compromised mitochondrial function by inducing lethal mitophagy

We further asked whether the metabolic vulnerabilities created by 1,25D3 could result from excessive mitophagy. To confirm that 1,25D3 induces mitophagy in cancer cells, we generated mitochondrial-mKeima-expressing (mt-mKeima) glioblastoma cells to enable quantitative measurement of mitophagy (Figure S2D). As anticipated, 1,25D3 profoundly activated mitophagy (mitolysosome formation at pH 4), as detected by excitation/emission at 561/620nm. The signal was abolished by the mitophagy inhibitor liensinine, but the subsequent addition of 1,25D3 partially restored mitophagy (Figure 3A). To allow quantification of mitophagy in 1,25D3- and TMZ-treated tumor cells, flow cytometry not only confirmed that TMZ treatment triggered mitophagy but that the degree of mitophagy was dramatically increased by the combination of 1,25D3 and TMZ (Figures 3B and 3C). We also used a mito-QC-reporter mice model,25 which has an intrinsic pH-sensitive fluorescent mitochondrial signal that enables the in vivo assessment of mitophagy (Figure 3E). In particular, by using a retroviral-induced tumor model (Figure 3F), we are able to visualize mitolysosomes in tumor cells undergoing mitophagy. Upon the formation of mitolysosome, GFP signal will be quenched in an acidic microenvironment, resulting in punctate mCherry-only foci. In line with our previous findings, treatments with 1,25D3 and TMZ increased the numbers and sizes of mitolysosomes, with the most profound effect seen following the combined treatment (Figure 3G). This over-induction of mitophagy in tumor cells may represent the underlying mechanism leading to reduced tumor growth in mice (Figure 3H). Taken together, our findings suggest that 1,25D3 can circumvent the chemoresistant mechanism imposed by TMZ by triggering an excessive mitophagy.

SIRT4 is a transcriptional target of VDR and a mitophagy regulator

Next, we aim to elucidate the possible molecular mechanisms by which 1,25D3 induces mitophagy and alters mitochondrial function. Previous studies provided robust evidence on the role of sirtuins in regulating mitophagy and cancer metabolism.26,27 We speculated that sirtuins may take part in this process mediated by VDR, which is a nuclear transcription factor regulating diverse cellular functions. To confirm the anti-tumor effect of 1,25D3 was mediated through VDR, we knocked down VDR expressions in tumor cells (shVDR) (Figure S3A) and showed that the tumor-suppressive effects of 1,25D3 were abolished in VDR-deficient cells, implicating that VDR is required for 1,25D3-mediated anti-tumor effect (Figures 4A and S3B). Of the seven known human sirtuins (SIRT1–7), SIRT4 primarily resides within the mitochondria.28 Consistent with the mitochondrial targeting function of 1,25D3, SIRT4 was found to be more highly upregulated in 1,25D3-treated tumors than any of the other sirtuins (Figure S3C). SIRT4 expression was also induced in vivo in mouse orthotopic xenografts following 1,25D3 and TMZ treatment (Figures 4C and 4D) accompanied by reduced tumor proliferation, as indicated by a lower Ki67 proliferation index (Figure S3E). We speculate that SIRT4 is transcriptionally regulated by VDR. We performed chromatin immunoprecipitation using VDR antibody and found that it binds to its cognate DR3 response element in the SIRT4 promoter region (Figure 4E). To further confirm that 1,25D3 mediates SIRT4 expression through this putative DR3 binding site, we generated a mutant clone of SIRT4 with the DR3 sequence truncated in the promoter region (Figure 4F). Results from dual-luciferase reporter assay indicated that 1,25D3 was able to initiate transcription of SIRT4 with a wild-type (WT) promoter but not with a mutant (MUT) promoter (Figure 4G), suggesting that SIRT4 is a target gene of VDR mediated by 1,25D3.

Recent evidence demonstrates a novel function of SIRT4 in the regulation of mitophagy through interaction with OPA1.29 We surmise that upregulation of SIRT4 may underlie the mechanisms leading to mitophagic cell death in tumor cells treated with combination regimen of TMZ and 1,25D3. As expected, overexpression of SIRT4 (SIRT4-OE) significantly increased the percentage of mitophagy in tumor cells (Figures 4H and S3F), and the activation of SIRT4 in vivo was also correlated with enhanced DNA damage in tumor cells (Figure S3G). Indeed, overexpression of SIRT4 induced mitochondrial dysfunction that mimicked the effects seen following 1,25D3 treatment (Figure 4I), as the addition of 1,25D3 to SIRT4-OE cells remarkably reduced key parameters of mitochondrial function (Figure 4J). These results underlie the anti-tumor mechanisms that 1,25D3 exerts through mitochondrial targeting.

It echoes in harmony with our findings of 1,25D3 and TMZ combined treatment, implicating that SIRT4 deficiency imparts a survival advantage to tumor cells.

Early findings reported the ADP-ribosylate function of SIRT4 on glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), enabling SIRT4 to inhibit the conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG).28 In light of these metabolic functions, it is not surprising that overexpression of SIRT4 in tumor cells induces metabolic changes to trigger mitophagy. This is also supported by studies demonstrating metabolic control of mitophagy.30 As expected, we showed that tumor cells with SIRT4 overexpression significantly suppressed the availability of α-ketoglutarate (Figure 5G), and we also confirmed that upregulation of SIRT4 rewired this metabolic pathway by suppressing GDH activity (Figure 5H), thereby limiting glutamine as the fuel source for cancer cell proliferation. Discussion

Our findings have uncovered a previously unrecognized mechanism by which 1,25D3 sensitizes glioblastoma cells to TMZ through the induction of mitophagy overload and mitochondrial dysfunction. Although TMZ remains the standard-of-care chemotherapy for newly diagnosed glioblastomas, its efficacy is limited by the rapid development of chemoresistance. Studies have shown that TMZ induces pro-survival autophagy, enabling glioblastoma cells to escape therapy-induced cell death.31 Consistent with this, we observed that glioblastoma patients with extended exposure to TMZ reinstate a high level of autophagy, particularly mitophagy, possibly as an adaptive mechanism to eliminate damaged mitochondria. This serves as an adaptive mechanism that overcomes the effectiveness of chemotherapies by eliminating damaged mitochondria and mitigating the accumulation of reactive oxygen species.

In addition to mitophagy, TMZ-resistant tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming, with a shift toward OXPHOS and the rewiring of glucose and glutamine metabolism, implicating that these cells are highly dependent on mitochondrial function.32,33 In the present study, we exploited this vulnerability by repurposing 1,25D3 (calcitriol), which is an active metabolite of vitamin D. Compared to its precursor cholecalciferol, 1,25D3 has a higher potency and engages the VDR more rapidly. Notably, 1,25D3 has been reported to activate autophagy in neuroprotective, renoprotective, and hepatoprotective contexts.34,35 Consistent with these protective roles in non-cancer cells, our previous studies demonstrated that vitamin D enhances neurological recovery after intracerebral hemorrhage36 and promotes remyelination following spinal cord injury by supporting oligodendrocyte lineage differentiation,37 without evidence of neural toxicity, supporting a broad neuroprotective role of vitamin D across multiple central nervous system cell types beyond astrocytes.

Several phase I/II clinical trials have investigated 1,25D3 in cancer therapy, including combination treatment of carboplatin with 1,25D3 for solid tumors (NCT00008086); a taxane-based chemotherapy with 1,25D3 for recurrent disease (NCT01588522); and single treatment of 1,25D3 (45 μg/week) for breast cancer (NCT01293682). 1,25D3 has a short half-life of 5–8 h, and high doses are typically administered intermittently. In our animal study, the dose of 0.5 μg/kg was used, which is comparable to clinical regimens; however, daily administration was necessary as limited by the short experimental duration. For comparison, intermittent combination therapy of 1,25D3 with TMZ for patients with metastatic melanoma was given at 0.2–0.5 μg/kg for 2 days every 28 days (NCT00301067), and combination regimen with docetaxel, zoledronic acid, and 1,25D3 was given at 0.5 μg/kg/week and was well tolerated in patients with castration-resistant prostate cancer.38 This approach allows us to achieve clinically relevant exposure while accommodating the constraints of the mouse tumor model.

Based on these studies, repurposing of 1,25D3 could be a promising regimen in conjunction with TMZ for the treatment of glioblastoma, especially because 1,25D3 has low toxicity and can cross the blood-brain barrier. While the anti-tumor effects of vitamin D have been identified with chemosensitizing function across different cancer types,39,40 the molecular mechanisms by which it enhances chemotherapy-induced inhibition remains poorly understood. The present study demonstrates the induction of a mitophagy overload by 1,25D3 as an anti-cancer mechanism. Our findings suggest that lethal mitochondrial damage occurs when 1,25D3 is used in combination with TMZ, but that 1,25D3 is also effective on its own as a monotherapy. Most importantly, we showed that the 1,25D3 effects are tumor specific, as its administration does not induce mitochondrial damage in normal human astrocytes or in brain tissues, in support of the pro-survival role of 1,25D3-induced mitophagy in non-cancer cells. This tumor selectivity likely reflects fundamental differences in metabolic demand and basal autophagy between normal and tumor cells, rendering tumor cells particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial and metabolic stress. However, in TMZ-treated cancer cells, 1,25D3 potentiates excessive mitophagy, as shown by the accumulation of mitophagy markers PINK1 and Parkin, and it promotes a surge in mitolysosome formation in tumors of mito-QC mice exposed to combined 1,25D3 and TMZ treatment. Mechanistically, this response is coupled to SIRT4-mediated metabolic reprogramming, which restricts mitochondrial metabolic flexibility and lowers the threshold for mitophagy-induced mitochondrial collapse. These changes were accompanied by pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion, suggesting that when mitophagy surpasses a protective threshold, it becomes detrimental to tumor cell survival.

Our findings also identified possible molecular mechanisms by which 1,25D3 regulates mitochondrial function. We found that mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT4 was profoundly upregulated following 1,25D3 treatment. SIRT4 is a NAD+-dependent ADP-ribosyltransferase that is known to play an important role in metabolic regulation by inhibiting GDH activity, thereby reducing the conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate.28 A recent study also reported an interaction between SIRT4 and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) in the regulation of mitochondrial quality control through mitophagy.27,29 Our findings revealed that upregulation of SIRT4 in response to 1,25D3 was mediated through transcription regulation of VDR. Furthermore, SIRT4 overexpression alone phenocopied the mitochondrial stress induced by combination treatment, supporting a role for SIRT4 as a downstream effector of VDR-mediated mitophagy activation. In addition to its role in mitophagy, SIRT4 may also contribute to TMZ sensitization through metabolic reprogramming, as it inhibits GDH activity and reduces α-ketoglutarate availability that impairs OXPHOS, thereby interfering with the metabolic flexibility in tumor cells and suppressing tumor growth.

Our findings support a model in which 1,25D3 activates VDR-dependent transcriptional induction of SIRT4, thereby integrating metabolic stress with mitochondrial quality control to impair tumor survival.41 However, questions remain as to whether these SIRT4-mediated effects are due to the metabolic control of mitophagy or whether mitophagy occurs independent of metabolic stress. In this regard, the lower SIRT4 expression observed in glioblastomas relative to lower-grade tumors aligns with its tumor-suppressive function, as SIRT4 deficiency may support GDH activity and enhance glutamine metabolism. This possibility is in line with current literature regarding the tumor-suppressive function of SIRT4 that arises through its upregulation of p53 signaling and autophagy to inhibit tumorigenesis.26

Contrary to Warburg’s original hypothesis that cancer cells rely exclusively on glycolysis due to mitochondrial dysfunction, accumulating evidence now indicates that functional mitochondria are essential for cancer cell viability and therefore represent a therapeutic vulnerability.42 Our findings provide compelling evidence that pharmacological doses of 1,25D3 can shift mitophagy from a survival mechanism to a lethal pathway, enabling selective targeting of cancer cell mitochondria in the presence of chemotherapy drugs. We also demonstrated that this combined treatment strategy led to significant tumor regression, thereby validating the therapeutic relevance of this approach, particularly for overcoming chemoresistance in glioblastoma. In conclusion, our study highlights a mechanism for chemosensitization of glioblastomas via VDR-SIRT4-mediated mitophagic overload. These findings provide a strong rationale for targeting mitochondrial quality control as a therapeutic strategy. Further studies are warranted to explore the safety and efficacy of high doses of 1,25D3 in clinical settings as well as its potential synergy of 1,25D3 with other emerging metabolic therapies.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. Clinical specimens were collected with informed consent from all patients to acknowledge the use of their resected tumor tissues for research purposes. Study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong and the Hospital Authority of Hong Kong West Cluster (HKU/HA HKW/IRB) (reference no. UW 07–273). Experimental procedures on animals were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines for reporting animal experiments. Experimental protocol is approved by the Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research (CULATR) of the University of Hong Kong.

Cell culture

Human glioblastoma cell lines U87 and U251 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Both cell lines were authenticated by short-tandem repeat profiling (Genetica). Mycoplasma screening was performed for all cell lines used to confirm that cells were mycoplasma free. Cells were routinely cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (12561056, Gibco), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10270106, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, 15140122, Gibco). Patient-derived GSCs, GSC16 and GSC21, were cultured in a serum-free medium enriched with growth factors: human EGF (PHG0311, Gibco), B-27 (12587010, Gibco), N-2 (17502048, Gibco), human FGF-basic (PHG0024, Gibco), and L-glutamine (StemCell technologies). GSCs were cultured as spheroids using the 24-well plate of the AggreWell system (34415, StemCell technologies). Glioblastoma patient-derived cells, GPDC923, were cultured in 1:1 ratio of RPMI (2240089, Gibco) and DMEM (10569010, Gibco) supplemented with 1× MEM non-essential amino acids (11140–050), 10% heat-inactivated FBS, and 1% PS. All cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator under an air/CO2 (95:5, v/v) atmosphere.

In vitro drug treatment

TMZ (T2577, Sigma) stock solution was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and reconstituted in the base MEM medium at a concentration of 10 mM. 1,25D3 (1α,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol, D1530, Sigma) stock solution was reconstituted in 95% ethanol at a concentration of 50 μM. Cells were treated with TMZ and/or 1,25D3 for 3 days unless otherwise indicated.

In situ cell death assay

Cell death was assessed by TUNEL assay using In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (11684817910, Roche) using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from orthotopic xenografts. TUNEL assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Apoptotic cell population was calculated by the number of TUNEL-positive cells/DAPI-stained cells within each of five randomly selected fields under Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope.

Cell proliferation and clonogenic assay

Cell viability after treatment was assessed by MTT (thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide) (M5655, Sigma). In the MTT assay, 5,000 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates. MTT reagent (stock concentration 5 mg/mL) was added to each well at the experiment endpoint and incubated at 37°C incubator for 2 h. Formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO followed by measuring absorbance at 595 nm with a Thermo Varioskan Flash reader. In a clonogenic assay, 500 cells/well were seeded in triplicates in 6-well plates and maintained in culture for 12 days after treatment. Cell colonies were fixed with 75% ethanol and stained with 0.25% crystal violet (w/v). Numbers of colonies were counted using a Zeiss Axio Zoom V16 light microscope under bright field.

EdU cell proliferation assay

The EdU incorporation assay kit was purchased from Ribobio. EdU reagent was added to each of the wells after treatment and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Staining reagents were added according to manufacturer’s protocol, and cells were subsequently stained with Hoechst 33342 (H3570, Thermo Scientific). Fluorescence staining was measured with a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope. EdU incorporation rate was calculated as percentage of EdU-positive cells/Hoechst-positive cells.

Transmission electron microscopy

Mouse brain or tumor tissue (thickness of 2 mm) was fixed using 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH7.4) for 4 h at 4°C–8°C; samples were then transferred to cacodylate buffer with 0.1 M sucrose to stop fixation. Samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) in cacodylate buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then washed in several changes using cacodylate buffer. After dehydration, tissue blocks were infiltrated with epoxy resin/propylene oxide 1:1 mixture for 1 h 30 min at 37°C, followed by epoxy resin for 1 h at 37°C. The tissue blocks were then embedded in epoxy resin and polymerized at 60°C overnight. Sections were imaged at 6,600× with accelerating voltage of 100 kV by transmission electron microscope Philips CM100.

Establishment of stable glioblastoma cell lines

Firefly luciferase-expressing cell line was generated using the lentiviral vector pGLVU6/Firefly/Puro purchased from GenePharma. U87-mt-mKeima (U87-mKeima) and U251-mt-mKeima (U251-mKeima) cell lines were generated using lentiviral vector pHAGE-mt-mKeima (131626, Addgene). For stable knockdown of VDR, lentiviral vector containing a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting human VDR sequence was used (5′GGAGTTCATTCTGACAGATGA3′). Lentiviral packaging plasmids psPAX2 (12260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (12259, Addgene) were transfected into HEK293T cells along with the target lentiviral vector using Polyjet transfection reagent (SL100688, SignaGen). Glioblastoma cells were transduced with lentiviral particles for 72 h with the addition of polybrene (5 μg/mL, TR-1003G-Millipore). For luciferase-expressing and shCtrl/shVDR clones, cells were isolated after 2 weeks of puromycin selection. For mt-mKeima-expressing clones, cells were sorted by positive fluorescence signal (Qdot 605) using a BD FACSAria SORP Cell Sorter. For human SIRT4-overexpressing (SIRT4-OE) clones, cells were directly infected with lentiviral particles carrying empty vector (Ctrl) or vector with ORF of human SIRT4 gene (NM_012240.3) purchased from Genechem; stable clones were selected using puromycin for 2 weeks.

RNA interference

For small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated transient knockdown of human VDR, transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150, Invitrogen). siRNA oligonucleotides and their respective scramble controls were purchased from GenePharma.

Clinical tissue samples: QMH cohort

Human glioma tissues were collected by surgical resections from patients admitted to QMH in Hong Kong. Tumor tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until extraction for protein and mRNA for further analysis. Part of the tumor tissues were collected in MACS Tissue storage solution (130-100-008, Miltenyi) for primary culture. Patient FFPE sections were obtained from the Department of Pathology in QMH and stored at room temperature until use. Paired primary and recurrent tumor tissues from glioblastoma patients were obtained for snRNA-seq.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Tumor tissue samples were snap-frozen and homogenized, and both tissue and cell lysates were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (9806, CST) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor (A32961, Thermo Scientific). Protein lysates were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and subjected to electrophoresis. The separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked with a 5% non-fat milk solution in 5% TBST for 1 h. For immunodetection, primary antibodies of VDR (12550, CST), SQSTM1/P62 (5114, CST), LC3A/B (4108, CST), GAPDH (2118, CST), DRP1 (8570, CST), MFF (84580, CST), OPA1 (80471, CST), β-tubulin (2146, CST), Parkin (sc32282, Santa Cruz Biotech), PINK1 (sc518052, Santa Cruz Biotech), and SIRT4 (sc135797, Santa Cruz Biotech) were used. Antibody binding was detected by enhanced chemiluminescent HRP substrate detection kit (WBKLS0500, Millipore). Bands were exposed to X-ray film. Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software and were normalized to the value of the housekeeping.

Autophagy analysis

The U87 cell line expressing mCherry-GFP-LC3 was obtained from Dr. Vincent Wong (Macau University of Science and Technology). Cells were seeded onto confocal plates. After treatment, cells were washed by PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (sc281692, Santa Cruz Biotech). Cells were then stained with DAPI (62248, Thermo Scientific) and observed under a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope. Autophagosome and autolysosome numbers were counted as number of punctate structures per cell. Autophagosomes were labeled as yellow puncta (GFP-positive/mCherry-positive), and the matured autolysosomes were in red (GFP-negative/mCherry-positive). The percentage of red puncta was calculated as the number of red/(red + yellow).

Mt-mKeima mitophagy assay

The Mt-mKeima assay was used to measure mitophagy with both confocal imaging and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis. The Keima fluorophore has a bimodal excitation under neutral (488/620 at pH7) and acidic (561/620 at pH 4) conditions. It is targeted to mitochondria to accurately reveal the formation of mitolysosomes. By measuring the proportion of the high ratio signal area to the total mitochondria area, it represents as an index of mitophagy. Live cells were harvested and stained with DAPI for confocal and FACS analysis using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope and Agilent NovoCyte Quanteon, respectively, and percentage mitophagy was measured under Qdot 605 and PE-Texas Red channels.

In vitro detection of mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured with MitoTracker Green (20 nM, M7514, Invitrogen) and MitoTracker Red (100 nM, M22425, Invitrogen) staining. Cells seeded onto confocal plates were stained for 30 min at 37°C. Cells were then washed twice with PBS and stained with Hoechst 33342 (H3570, Thermo Scientific) for 15 min at room temperature. Live images were captured with a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope.

Two-photon intravital imaging

A cranial window was constructed in mice 3 weeks after orthotopic tumor injection of U87 cells. Mice were treated with either vehicle (PBS) or 1,25D3 (0.5 μg/kg) oral solution (Roxane laboratories) for 7 days before imaging. Intravital mitochondrial membrane potential in tumor cells was detected by intravenous injection of 50 μL rhodamine-123 at 10 mg/mL (R302, Sigma) and 50 μL of Hoechst 33342 at 5 mg/mL (H3570, Thermo Scientific) 15 min before imaging. Fluorescence images were acquired with a 25× objective by two-photon laser scanning microscope (Olympus FVMPE-RS Hybrid).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (E1910, Promega) was used to measure the ability of 1,25D3/VDR to induce transcription activation of SIRT4 promoter. SIRT4 promoter sequence containing the VDR-binding motif (SIRT4-Promoter-WT, 757 bp) or SIRT4 promoter sequence with VDR-binding motif truncated (SIRT4-Promoter-MUT, 733 bp) was cloned into GPL3-Basic plasmid (containing Firefly luciferase) and was co-transfected into glioblastoma cells with an internal control REN (Renilla luciferase RL-TK plasmid) using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001, Invitrogen). Constructs were purchased from GenePharma. Firefly luciferase expression was driven by transcription activation of the SIRT4 promoter, and transcription activity of SIRT4 was assayed with the WT and MUT construct. Cells transfected with these constructs were treated with 1,25D3 for 48 h before measurement. Absolute LUC/REN intensity was measured in a multifunctional plate reader BMG CLARIOstar Plus. The transactivation ability was calculated as fold change of RLU (Firefly/Renilla) normalized to its corresponding control. At least five biological replicates were assayed for each combination. Sequences of the WT and MUT construct are provided in Table S1.

α-Ketoglutarate measurement

Intracellular α-KG levels were measured using the α-KG fluorometric assay kit (E-BC-047, Elabscience). 1 × 106 cells were homogenized in cold normal saline at 4°C and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min to remove insoluble materials. Supernatants or standards were mixed with assay reagents in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37°C for 20 min in the dark. Fluorescent signal (Ex/Em: 535/587 nm) was detected with a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech).

GDH activity assay

Glutamate dehydrogenase activity was detected using the GDH detection kit (ab102527, abcam). Briefly, 1 × 106 glioblastoma cells were lysed in assay buffer, and intracellular NADH concentration was measured every 15 min colorimetrically at 450 nm with a spectrophotometer, and GDH activity was quantified according to the amount of NADH produced in the given time.

Oxygen consumption rate analysis

Mitochondrial function was determined by measuring OCR using Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test (103015–100, Agilent). 5,000 cells were seeded onto each well of the microplate 24 h before measurement. Sequential injections of oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (2 μM), and rotenone with antimycin A (0.5 μM) were added to the cells through the Agilent Seahorse XFe96 analyzer at the indicated time points. Data analysis was performed using the Seahorse Wave Pro software.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (16696026, Invitrogen) from cells and tumor tissues. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA eraser (RR047A, Takara). Quantitative PCR was conducted in Vii 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied biosystems) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus) (RR802A, Takara) to measure mRNA expressions of SIRT4, VDR, and housekeeping genes. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Animal experiments

BALB/cAnN-nu mice were purchased from Center for Comparative Medicine Research (CCMR) at the LKS Faculty of Medicine, The University of Hong Kong, and were housed in individually vented cages (IVCs). IVCs were maintained under controlled conditions with no more than five mice per cage, including consistent temperature, positive air pressure, and filtered air, following standard 12-h light/dark cycles. The mice were housed at least for a week before starting the downstream processes. In all animal experiments, 6- to 8-week-old male mice were used. All operations and handling of animals adhered to the guidelines set by the CULATR at The University of Hong Kong. For intracranial xenograft model, a small skin incision was made to expose the bregma suture at the right forebrain. A small burr hole at 2 mm lateral and 1 mm dorsal to bregma was created with micromotor drill. 5 × 105 cells were injected into the right striatum at a depth of 3.5 mm from dura using a 26G Hamilton syringe on a stereotactic apparatus. Tumor growth was measured by bioluminescence measurements using the PE-IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer) after intraperitoneal injection of the reporter substrate D-luciferin (15 mg/mL, LUCK-100, Goldbio). On day 14 post injection, tumor-bearing mice were subjected to different treatments with vehicle (PBS), 1,25D3 oral solution (Roxane laboratories), TMZ (Sigma), or combination treatment with 1,25D3 and TMZ , all administrated by oral gavage. 1,25D3 (0.5 μg/kg) was given daily for 2 weeks, and TMZ (5 mg/kg) was given daily on the first 3 days in a week for 2 weeks.

Retrovirus-induced glioblastoma model in mito-QC-reporter mice

Tumor was induced with a retroviral vector system that induces overexpression of PDGFB and dominant-negative mutant p53. It provides a rapid and efficient induction of glioblastoma in mice with full penetrance.43 Retrovirus was prepared using the packaging plasmid pCL-Eco (12371, Addgene) and retroviral vector pQCXIX-PDGFB-IRES-DNp53. Retrovirus was concentrated and titrated to viral titer of >107/mL. 3 μL of viral solution was injected into the right striatum of mito-QC mice using a 30G Hamilton syringe. Orthotopic tumor was induced in mito-QC reporter mice (Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(CAG-mCherry/GFP)Ganl) with C57BL/6 background, obtained from Dr. Ian Ganley from University of Dundee through UKRI-MRC.25 Mito-QC mice have mCherry-GFP tag fused to mitochondrial targeting sequence of FIS1, and this allows fixation for confocal analysis in tissue specimens.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin from U87 glioblastoma cells was cross-linked with 4% formaldehyde. DNA fragments were immunoprecipitated using VDR antibody (12550, CST), Histone H3, and IgG control from SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit with magnetic Beads (9003, CST). The immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by PCR with primers targeting different DR3 regions identified in the SIRT4 promoter. The binding region was confirmed using primer sequence (F3) forward 5′-GAGTCAAATCTAGGCCGGGA-3′ and reverse 5′-CAGGCTGGTCTCGAACTCC-3’.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining of FFPE clinical specimens and tumor xenograft tissues was performed on consecutive 5-μm-thick sections. Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight in a moist chamber. Slides were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. DAB+ chromogen substrate (K3468, Agilent Dako) was used to detect signals. Tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted in DPX mounting solution. The following antibodies were used: SIRT4 (A7585, Abclonal), Ki67 (NB110-89719, Novus), cleaved caspase 8 (NB100-56116, Novus), and Envision+ system HRP-labeled polymer anti-rabbit secondary antibody (K4003, Agilent Dako). All sections were quantified using QuPath software.

Survival analysis from the QMH cohort

Glioblastoma patients were stratified into two groups (low SIRT4 and high SIRT4) by using the median expression value as cutoff. Survival data were analyzed from 13 patients, a Kaplan-Meier survival curve was generated, and log rank test was used to assess the difference between groups.

Gene Expression Omnibus data analysis

SIRT4 mRNA expression values in human glioma (268 patients, inclusive of IDH WT and IDH mutant tumors) and normal brain tissues (eight samples) were retrieved from the GSE16011 database available at the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Glioblastoma patients were stratified into two groups (low SIRT4 and high SIRT4) by using the median expression value as cutoff. Survival data were analyzed from 149 patients, a Kaplan-Meier survival curve was generated, and a log rank test was used to assess the difference between groups.

Single-nuclei RNA sequencing analysis

Single nuclei were extracted from snap-frozen human glioblastoma specimens using the Chromium Nuclei Isolation Kit with RNase inhibitor (PN-1000494, 10X Genomics). Single-nuclei suspensions were sorted as DAPI positive through BD FACSAria SORP Cell Sorter. Library preparation and Illumina sequencing (pair-end sequencing of 151 bp) were conducted at The University of Hong Kong, LKS Faculty of Medicine, Center for PanorOmic Sciences (CPOS), Genomics Core. The libraries were denatured and diluted to optimal concentration. Illumina NovaSeq 6000 was used for pair-end 151-bp sequencing. One patient-paired primary glioblastoma (IDH WT) and recurrent glioblastoma (IDH WT) treated with standard chemotherapy TMZ for at least six-cycles were used in this study.

snRNA-seq data processing

The raw sequencing data were aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome, and count matrices were generated using CellRanger (v6.1.2). Then, ambient RNA and empty droplets were removed using CellBender (v0.2.0). Subsequent data analysis was conducted using the standard Seurat (v 5.0.1) processing pipeline (https://satijalab.org/seurat/). Specially, low-quality cells with (1) <500 genes, (2) >20% mitochondrial transcripts, or (3) >50% ribosomal transcripts were filtered. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells were excluded. We also removed cell doublets using the R package “DoubletFinder” (v2.0.3). Moreover, the gene expression matrix was normalized using the “SCTransform” function. The batch effect was diminished using the R package “harmony” (v1.1.0). The top 2,000 highly variably expressed genes were identified using the “FindVariableFeatures” function. Based on these highly variable genes, we employed the principal component analysis and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analyses to decrease the matrix dimensionality. Cells were clustered using the “FindClusters” function, and cell types were annotated based on classical markers from a prior study,44 along with the automatic cell type annotation using the R package “SingleR” (v2.4.0).

To distinguish the tumor cells, immune cells were selected as the reference to investigate the somatic copy number variations (CNVs) using the R package “inferCNV” (v1.18.0). Cells with CNV scores above 0.05 and CNV correlations above 0.5 were considered tumor cells.45 Moreover, we utilized another CNV inference R package, “CopyKAT” (v1.0.5), to validate the accuracy of tumor cells identified by inferCNV. After that, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected by the function “FindMarker” of the R package “Seurat,” using false discovery rate (FDR) p value <0.05 as the cutoff value. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses on DEGs were performed by the R package “clusterProfiler” (v4.10.0). The levels of autophagy and mitophagy, characterized by the gene sets extracted from GO terms “GOBP_REGULATION_OF_AUTOPHAGY (GO:0010506)” and “GOBP_MITOPHAG (GO:0000423),” were quantified utilizing the “gsva” function from the R package “GSVA” with method specified as “ssGSEA.”46

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless stated otherwise. All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism 10.0 using two-tailed Student’s t test and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test when appropriate. p value <0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
