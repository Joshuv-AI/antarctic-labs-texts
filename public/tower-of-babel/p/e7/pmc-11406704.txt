Discovery of a 6-Aminobenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-Dioxide Derivative (K2071) with a Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 Inhibitory, Antimitotic, and Senotherapeutic Activities
Authors: Patrik Oleksak, David Rysanek, Marketa Vancurova, Pavla Vasicova, Alexandra Urbancokova, Josef Novak, Dominika Maurencova, Pavel Kashmel, Jana Houserova, Romana Mikyskova, Ondrej Novotny, Milan Reinis, Pavel Juda, Miroslav Hons, Jirina Kroupova, David Sedlak, Tetyana Sulimenko, Pavel Draber, Marketa Chlubnova, Eugenie Nepovimova, Kamil Kuca, Miroslav Lisa, Rudolf Andrys, Tereza Kobrlova, Ondrej Soukup, Jiri Janousek, Lukas Prchal, Jiri Bartek, Kamil Musilek, Zdenek Hodny
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract6-Nitrobenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide (Stattic) is a potent signal transducer and activator of the transcription 3 (STAT3) inhibitor developed originally for anticancer therapy. However, Stattic harbors several STAT3 inhibition-independent biological effects. To improve the properties of Stattic, we prepared a series of analogues derived from 6-aminobenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide, a compound directly obtained from the reduction of Stattic, that includes a methoxybenzylamino derivative (K2071) with optimized physicochemical characteristics, including the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier. Besides inhibiting the interleukin-6-stimulated activity of STAT3 mediated by tyrosine 705 phosphorylation, K2071 also showed cytotoxicity against a set of human glioblastoma-derived cell lines. In contrast to the core compound, a part of K2071 cytotoxicity reflected a STAT3 inhibition-independent block of mitotic progression in the prophase, affecting mitotic spindle formation, indicating that K2071 also acts as a mitotic poison. Compared to Stattic, K2071 was significantly less thiol-reactive. In addition, K2071 affected cell migration, suppressed cell proliferation in tumor spheroids, exerted cytotoxicity for glioblastoma temozolomide-induced senescent cells, and inhibited the secretion of the proinflammatory cytokine monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) in senescent cells. Importantly, K2071 was well tolerated in mice, lacking manifestations of acute toxicity. The structure–activity relationship analysis of the K2071 molecule revealed the necessity of the para-substituted methoxyphenyl motif for antimitotic but not overall cytotoxic activity of its derivatives. Altogether, these results indicate that compound K2071 is a novel Stattic-derived STAT3 inhibitor and a mitotic poison with anticancer and senotherapeutic properties that is effective on glioblastoma cells and may be further developed as an agent for glioblastoma therapy.Keywords: covalent modifiers, glioblastoma, mitotic poisons, cellular senescence, senolytics, inflammation

Despite significant advancements in deciphering the complex biology of cancer and the advent of targeted therapies, malignant diseases remain a formidable health problem. Certain malignant human tumors, including pancreatic and ovarian cancers, melanoma, and glioblastoma, the most aggressive form of human brain tumors, still resist treatment efforts. The incidence of glioblastoma is increasing, and the average survival time postdiagnosis barely exceeds 15 months. The glioblastoma treatment faces two primary obstacles. The first is the characteristic tumor diffuse growth pattern, which makes surgical removal impossible. The second is the frequent onset of therapeutic resistance, leading to disease recurrence. Consequently, there is an urgent need to explore new therapeutic targets and glioblastoma-specific treatment strategies.

Covalently modifying drugs have garnered considerable attention in cancer treatment due to their unique mechanism of action1—forming irreversible bonds with specific nucleophilic amino acid (lysine, serine, or cysteine) residues in the target protein, thereby altering its function. This is achieved by specific groups, known as electrophilic warheads, incorporated into the drug molecule during the synthesis. This approach has demonstrated high efficiency, and evidence suggests a significantly reduced risk of developing resistance against covalent drugs.2 Compared to noncovalent drugs, covalent drugs allow high potency to be routinely achieved in low-molecular mass compounds, and the main concern is the potential for off-target toxicity due to their high reactivity, which could lead to idiosyncratic side effects. Another risk is the formation of new haptens activating an undesirable immune response. Additionally, many small molecules, including covalent drugs, have suboptimal tissue transport abilities, which can limit their effectiveness. However, despite these limitations, approximately 20% of FDA-approved anticancer drugs belong to covalent modifiers.

Several warheads have been successfully used in the design of covalent drugs. For instance, acrylamide has been utilized as an effective warhead in FDA-approved targeted covalent inhibitors that target cysteine residues.2 Covalent protein kinase inhibitors, such as ibrutinib, a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase covalent inhibitor, and dacomitinib, an epidermal growth factor receptor covalent inhibitor, have been approved. In addition, covalent inhibitors for viral proteases, such as boceprevir, narlaprevir, and nirmatrelvir, represent another successful achievement in covalent drug development.3

Stattic (6-aminobenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide), a small molecule selective inhibitor of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), inhibits binding of tyrosine-phosphorylated peptide motifs to the STAT3 SH2 domain, effectively blocking STAT3 activation, dimerization, and nuclear translocation.4 It has been utilized in many studies aiming to investigate its anticancer effects both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, Stattic is recognized as a potential warhead in drug research due to its Michael addition reactivity. However, emerging evidence suggests that Stattic exhibits several biological effects that are independent of its STAT3 inhibitory activity.5,6

A STAT3 signaling pathway is functionally involved in most hallmarks of cancer, as defined by Hannahan and Weinberg,7 including tumor cell proliferation, tumor-promoting inflammation, tumor cell invasion and metastasis, angiogenesis, resistance to cell death, and deregulation of cancer cell energetics.8−11 Moreover, IL-6/STAT3 signaling essentially contributes to general inflammatory responses driven by the immune system and to effects of the senescent cell secretome on the proinflammatory tissue microenvironment. Therefore, considerable effort is devoted to developing inhibitors of this signaling pathway (reviewed in, e.g., refs (12 and 13)).

In this study, our objective was to develop new 6-amino-1-benzothiophene 1,1-dioxide scaffolds4 possessing favorable physicochemical properties that increase the blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability. From the newly synthesized set of compounds, we identified 6-[(4-methoxybenzyl)amino]benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide (K2071) as the most promising candidate due to its optimal physicochemical features for BBB penetration and cytotoxicity. We assessed its antiproliferative, cytotoxic, senolytic, and antimigratory effects on a cohort of proliferating and senescent-like glioblastoma cell lines using both 2-D and 3-D cell culture models. The novel 6-amino[b]benzothiophene 1,1-dioxide derivative demonstrated cytotoxic and STAT3-inhibition-unrelated antimitotic effects, arresting cells in the prophase, in addition to its STAT3 inhibitory activity. Structure–activity relationship analysis revealed the structural determinants necessary for cytotoxicity and mitotic poisoning. As a result, the double bond in the core of the benzothiophene 1,1-dioxide between the C2 and C3 positions plays a crucial role in the activity of these scaffolds. In addition, the aminomethyl linker and para-methoxyphenyl motifs are essential for mitotic poisoning and cytotoxicity.

Results

Molecular Design and Prediction of Physicochemical Properties of Stattic Analogues

Oxidation of the sulfur atom in benzo[b]thiophene (1) using mCPBA provided desired 1,1-dioxide 2 in 97% yield. Then, the regioselective nitration to the C6 position was performed in the nitrating mixture and provided Stattic (3) in excellent 93% yield. Reduction of the nitro group in the structure of Stattic (3) was achieved using Fe powder and NH4Cl in a mixture of H2O/CH3OH. This reduction provided the corresponding amine 4 in 65% yield (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of the Amine 4 from benzo[b]thiophene (1)

The prepared amine 4 was utilized in three different reaction types to provide structurally variable scaffolds of final products. First, the amine 4 underwent a condensation-type reaction with a methoxy- or hydroxy-substituted benzaldehyde (5) to provide a corresponding imine intermediate (6). Subsequent direct reduction of the imine intermediate (6) with NaBH3(CN) resulted in desired monomethoxy derivatives (K2071, K2885, and K2886) or monohydroxy derivatives (K2887, K2263, and K2888) in 22–83% yields (Scheme 2).

General Synthesis of Substituted 6-(benzylamino)benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-Dioxides (K2071, K2885, K2886, K2887, K2263, and K2888) from the Amine 4

In addition to the prepared substituted 6-(benzylamino)benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxides, compound K2071 was used to undergo the oxa-Michael addition of methanol to the double bond located at the C2 and C3 positions of the benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide ring. Thus, the compound K2071 was heated in a mixture of LiOH in methanol to provide K2891 in 47% yield as an enantiomeric product of the oxa-Michael addition reaction (Scheme 3).

Synthesis of K2891 from K2071 via the Oxa-Michael Addition Reaction

Second, the double bond in the amine 4 located between the C2 and C3 positions was reduced under a hydrogen atmosphere with 5% palladium on the carbon catalyst. This procedure provided the desired C2–C3 saturated amine 7 in an 87% yield. The reduced amine 7, in the following reaction step, underwent a condensation reaction with p-anisaldehyde to provide a corresponding C2–C3 saturated imine intermediate 8, which was subsequently reduced to a secondary amine K2946 in 46% yield (Scheme 4).

Reduction of C2=C3 Bond in the Amine 4 to the Saturated Amine 7, Followed by Condensation with p-Anisaldehyde and Subsequent Imine Reduction to Provide C2–C3 Saturated K2946

Third, the amine 4 underwent an addition reaction to 4-methoxyphenyl isocyanate to provide urea derivative K2260 in 20% yield (Scheme 5).

Inhibition of IL-6-Induced STAT3 Phosphorylation by Novel Stattic Derivatives

To evaluate the STAT3 inhibitory activity of novel Stattic derivatives, we examined their impact on receptor-mediated STAT3 tyrosine 705 (Y705) phosphorylation. For this purpose, the telomerase-immortalized nontransformed RPE-1 hTERT cells with the intact IL-6-STAT3 response were pretreated with increasing concentrations of all nine compounds in a concentration range 5–50 μM for 15 min before stimulation of cells with IL-6 (50 ng/mL, 15 min14,15), and the levels of phosphorylated Y705 STAT3 and total STAT3 were detected by immunoblotting using specific antibodies (Figure 2). Note that this short-term experimental setup excludes the indirect effects on STAT3 signaling caused, for instance, by lowered cell density due to cell death.16−18 Compounds K2071, K2885, K2886, and K2888 retained the ability to inhibit IL-6-stimulated STAT3 Y705 phosphorylation in a concentration-dependent manner, the most effective being compound K2888. Compounds K2887, K2263, and K2260 had an inhibitory effect on STAT3 phosporylation at the 50 μM concentration. As expected, compounds K2946 and K2891, lacking the double bond between carbons C2 and C3, were ineffective in the inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation.

Inhibition of IL-6-induced phosphorylation of STAT3 by novel Stattic derivatives. RPE-1 hTERT cells were exposed to 5, 10, 20, and 50 μM of all nine compounds for 15 min prior to IL-6 (50 ng/mL) treatment (15 min), and the levels of STAT3 phosphorylation (Y705) and total STAT3 were detected by immunoblotting. GAPDH was used as a loading control. Quantification of the pSTAT3 (Y705) signal expressed as a fold-change of the IL-6-induced phosphorylation (mean ± SD; n = 2).

In summary, the data suggest that the novel Stattic derivatives, which maintain the capability of the Michael reaction, also preserve their dose-dependent inhibitory effect on STAT3 phosphorylation.

Effects of Novel Stattic Derivatives on Cell Growth

A growth inhibitory effect of the novel Stattic derivatives was tested by the resazurin assay in the noncancerous cell line RPE-1 hTERT in a concentration range of 0.1–100 μM for 24 h. All substances, except those that do not accept the Michael reaction, decreased the formation of resofurin (Figure 3). Notably, compound K2071, unlike other growth inhibition-effective compounds, exhibited a plateau effect starting at the concentration of 5 μM.

Growth inhibitory effect of novel Stattic-derived compounds. RPE-1 hTERT cells were exposed to 0.1–100 μM K2071, K2885, K2885, K2887, K2263, K2888, K2891, K2946, and K2260 for 24 h, and the cell metabolic activity was measured by the resazurin assay. Relative values as a percentage of fluorescence (RFU) of the untreated cells were plotted (mean ± SD; n = 2).

In conclusion, with the exception of non-Michael addition compounds, all newly synthesized Stattic derivatives demonstrate a concentration-dependent inhibition of cell growth. Notably, compound K2071 exhibits the most pronounced effect on cell growth.

Novel STAT3 Inhibitors Are Designed to Cross the BBB

The parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA) is a high-throughput screening tool applicable for the prediction of the passive transport of potential drugs across the BBB.19 To predict and compare BBB penetration, all novel 6-(benzylamino)benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxides (K2071, K2885, K2886, K2887, K2263, and K2888), the oxa-Michael addition product of K2071 (K2891), the C2–C3 hydrogenated product of K2071 (K2946), and the urea derivative of K2071 (K2260) were subjected to PAMPA. The PAMPA evaluation (Table 1) demonstrated that all nine newly synthesized compounds may passively diffuse across the BBB.

K2071 Exerts Dose-Dependent Cytostatic and Cytotoxic Effects, Inducing Cellular Senescence-like Phenotype and Apoptosis in Glioblastoma Cell Lines

Due to its favorable physicochemical properties and predicted BBB permeability and the strongest effect on cell growth of all nine compounds, we selected K2071 for further testing of its anticancer properties. First, we assayed the effect of K2071 on cell growth of human glioblastoma cell lines A-172, T-98 G, U-87 MG, and U-251 MG. The cells were exposed to K2071 in a concentration range of 0.1–100 μM for 24 h and subjected to the resazurin assay (Figure 4A–E). The K2071 suppressed the resofurin production in a micromolar range in all glioblastoma cell lines tested. The IC50 of K2071 could not be calculated correctly (Figure 4E) due to a plateau effect observable with concentrations of K2071 higher than 5 μM. However, the IC50 could be set to approximately ∼2 μM, which corresponds well to IC50 1.5 μM obtained in the breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231 in pilot experiments using the ATP assay (72 h) and is equivalent to the parental compound Stattic (IC50 1.3 μM in MDA-MB-231, 72 h; Figure S1A). Although there were no significant differences in the sensitivity of individual glioblastoma cell lines to K2071, A-172 cells were the most responsive (40% of resofurin formation at 5 μM), and the U-251 MG and U-87 MG cells appeared the most resistant (65% of resofurin formation at 5 μM). Even temozolomide (TMZ)-resistant T-98 G cells were sensitive to K2071 to a similar extent (55% of resofurin formation at 5 μM) as other glioblastoma cell lines.

To further describe the mechanisms of K2071 cytotoxicity, the percentage of glioblastoma U-87 MG cells undergoing cell death by apoptosis after exposure to compound K2071 in a concentration range 1–100 μM for 24 h was determined by annexin V-FITC/Hoechst33258 staining (Figures 4F and S1B). As apparent from the FACS analysis, a subpopulation of annexin V-FITC/Hoechst33258-positive cells increased with the K2071 dose, showing induction of regulated cell death.

Furthermore, the long-term growth inhibitory effect of K2071 was analyzed by a colony-forming assay (CFA; Figure 4G–J). The CFA showed the significantly suppressed colony-forming ability of U-87 MG, T-98 G, and A-172 cell lines exposed to a single dose of 5 μM K2071 and followed for 10 days. Note that the cells losing adhesion were also included in CFA. Close microscopic inspection of surviving cells showed a high occurrence of cells with a senescent-like morphology in colonies exposed to K2071, indicating a cytostatic, cellular senescence-inducing effect of K2071 when used at lower doses (Figure S1C). The induction of a senescence-like phenotype was confirmed by senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining of U-87 MG and A-172 cells exposed to 5 μM K2071 (Figures 4K and S1D–F). The antiproliferative effect of K2071 was further analyzed by the DNA/EdU incorporation assay. U-87 MG, T-98 G, and A-172 cells were exposed to a single dose of K2071 (5 μM) for 24 h. Subsequently, the compound was removed, and the cells were pulsed with EdU for 24 h upon treatment and recovery phase (day 0, 1, 4, and 6; see the scheme in Figure S1G) to follow long-term effects. An increased proportion of EdU-negative cells with time after K2071 exposure was observed (Figure 4L). Microscopic examination of cells at several time points revealed a higher frequency of EdU-positive and EdU-negative cells with aberrant nuclei in treated than control cells. The frequent occurrence of micro-, bi-, and multinucleated cells indicated mitotic and cytokinetic defects in the surviving cell population (Figure S1H). Asynchronous DNA replication in some cell nuclei was observed as well.

Altogether, these findings indicate that the novel Stattic derivative K2071 impairs the growth of glioblastoma cell lines in vitro, inducing a cellular senescence-like phenotype predominantly at lower doses and apoptotic cell death at higher doses.

K2071 Inhibits the Growth of Glioblastoma Tumor Spheroids

To examine the effect of K2071 (0.1–100 μM) on glioblastoma tumor cell growth in 3-D culture conditions, we utilized U-87 MG glioblastoma cells ectopically expressing the green fluorescent protein (GFP) to follow their growth in the form of tumor spheroids (see the Experimental Section). Notably, K2071 in doses higher than 1.6 μM robustly affected the growth of tumor spheroids during the 7 days following the drug exposure and caused cell death (Figure 5A,B). Also, note the effect of K2071 on the compactness of the spheroid margin, indicating the disruption of cell-to-cell adhesion apparatus, the phenomenon already reported for chemical STAT3 inhibition.20

K2071 Cytotoxicity is Independent of STAT3-Inhibitory Activity

As the cytotoxicity of the core compound Stattic was found to be independent of the presence of STAT3,5 we explored next whether the K2071 antiproliferative and cytotoxic effects are mediated by STAT3 pathway inhibition or by a different mechanism. The prostate carcinoma cell line PC-3, known for the genetic ablation of STAT321,22 was verified for the absence of STAT3 protein expression by immunoblotting (Figure 6A). PC-3 cells were exposed to K2071 (0.1–100 μM) for 24 h, and as shown in Figure 6B, K2071 exerted suppressive effects on the growth of PC-3 cells detected by the resazurin assay in a similar concentration range as in STAT3-positive cells (Figure 4A–D), indicating the STAT3 inhibition-independent effect on cell growth. As shown in Figures 6C and S2, a dose-dependent increase in annexin V-FITC/Hoechst33258 staining positivity was observed in PC-3 cells after treatment (24 h) with compound K2071 (1–100 μM), indicating that a significant part of the cell population is dying by regulated cell death.

(C) PC-3 cells were exposed to 1–100 μM K2071 for 24 h and analyzed for apoptosis by annexin V-FITC/Hoechst33258 staining. The percentage of double-positive (i.e., dead) cells was plotted (n = 1).

In conclusion, K2071 is cytotoxic also for the cells with STAT3-negative background, which is in line with the previous report demonstrating the STAT3-independent antiproliferative effect of Stattic.5

K2071 Exerts Low Thiol Reactivity Compared to Stattic

Previous reports indicate that Stattic is a thiol-reactive compound.23,24 It was proposed that an intact double bond in the benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide ring of Stattic is prone to Michael reaction and is a prerequisite for Stattic activity.4 To decipher whether Stattic analogue K2071 retains thiol reactivity, T-98 G, U-87 MG, and PC-3 cells were treated with Stattic or K2071 (10–100 μM) in combination with glutathione (GSH; 200 μM). The amount of remaining attached cells compared to control cells after 24 h exposure was determined by the crystal violet assay (Figure 7A–C). In contrast to Stattic, where the GSH addition significantly suppressed its effect on a crystal violet readout, the addition of GSH to K2071 had no impact on growth inhibitory activity. Interestingly, this experiment also showed that the cytotoxicity of low concentrations of K2071 (<50 μM) in a -SH-rich environment is higher than that of Stattic.

The formation of thiol-containing conjugates for compounds 3 (Stattic), 4, and K2071 was assessed by incubation with cysteine, N-acetylcysteine, and glutathione in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Figure 7D). This experiment demonstrated the ability of the tested compounds to interact with thiols spontaneously. Compound 3 (Stattic) exhibited the highest reactivity with almost complete transformation into its corresponding conjugate (over 95% conversion). Compounds 4 and K2071 displayed significantly lower reactivity (30–40% conversion). Conjugates with all tested thiols were identified, with the most significant interaction observed with cysteine followed by glutathione, while N-acetylcysteine demonstrated the most negligible reactivity. These findings were further supported by incubating K2071 with U-251 MG cells or a cell-free culture medium. No conjugates were obtained after the incubation of K2071 with cell-free culture media (Figure 7E). However, after 24 h of incubation of U-251 MG cells with 50 μM K2071, the presence of its cysteine conjugate was confirmed via LC–MS/MS analysis with an observed mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of 423.1 [M + H]+ (Figure 7F). Furthermore, the product ion spectra analysis of the isolated conjugate revealed fragments corresponding to the parental molecule (m/z 302.2) and cysteine (m/z 321.0), providing additional confirmation of the K2071-cysteine conjugate identity (Figure 7G,H).

Altogether, K2071 exerts substantially weaker thiol reactivity compared to the parental compound Stattic, a differentiating feature that is important for its biological activity.

K2071 Harbors STAT3 Inhibition-Independent Mitotic Toxicity

To highlight the mechanism of K2071 antiproliferative activity, first, we examined the effect of K2071 on cellular DNA content by DNA DAPI staining in U-87 MG, RPE-1 hTERT, and PC-3 cells exposed to K2071 (1–100 μM) for 24 h. FACS analysis of these cell lines showed a significant increase of cells with 4N or noninteger DNA content compared to control cells, indicating accumulation of cells in the S/G2/M phase of the cell cycle or increased tetraploidy/aneuploidy (Figure 8A). These effects were already detected in PC-3 and U-87 MG cells after treatment with 1 μM K2071. At doses higher than 20 μM, the accumulation of 4N cells was diminishing; however, it was accompanied by the disintegration of the typical DNA content profile, indicating the effect of higher doses on cell cycle progression, accumulation of cells in the G1 phase, and increasing aneuploidy.

Furthermore, when we searched in RPE-1 hTERT cells for the manifestation of DNA damage as a possible cause of aberrant cell cycle progression, we did not find evidence of the significantly increased number of DNA double-strand breaks detected as serine 139 (S139)-phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX)/53BP1 foci in cells surviving 2, 4, and 8 h exposure to 5 μM K2071 (Figure S3A–C; the DNA damaging compound doxorubicin was used as a positive control). Moreover, we did not find activated checkpoint kinases Chk1 and Chk2 in U-87 MG cells at the population level of the 24 h surviving subpopulation (as detected by the phosphorylation-activated forms by immunoblotting), excluding the possibility of delayed cell cycle progression due to the activation of DNA damage checkpoints at the G1/S or G2/M boundary (Figure S3D). We should note that the observed dose-dependent increase of threonine 180/tyrosine 182 (T180/Y182)-phosphorylated MAPK p38α in U-87 MG surviving cells analyzed 24 h post K2071 exposure indicated a general stress response (Figure S3E). Note that we also excluded replication stress as a source of cell cycle perturbation. Unlike the RPE-1 hTERT cells exposed to 2 mM hydroxyurea, cells treated with K2071 did not show marked elevation of serine 33 (S33)-phosphorylated RPA32 (pRPA), a marker of DNA resection and replication stress (Figure S3F,G), consistent with the absence of Chk1 activation.

To characterize the dynamics of changes in cell cycle progression and cell death after the exposure of cells to K2071 and Stattic, we used time-lapse microscopy using the IncuCyte SX1 platform. Interestingly, the sequential accumulation of partially detached rounded cells was observed after exposure to 2.5 μM K2071, indicating a block of mitotic progression (Figure S3H), which is in accord with the accumulation of cells with 4N DNA content. Note, after the Stattic treatment (10 and 20 μM), most cells exhibited the round-shaped phenotype that was observable 30 min after Stattic addition, impeding the sequential accumulation of rounded cells. Therefore, to prove unequivocally that the 4N cells with rounded appearance are blocked in mitosis, we followed the cell population for a marker of mitotic cells, serine 10 (S10)-phosphorylated histone H3 (pH3 (S10)25), by FACS using a specific anti-pH3 (S10) antibody. As shown in Figures 8B and S3I, most RPE-1 hTERT cells exposed to the range of K2071 concentration (2.5–20 μM) for 24 h were pH3 (S10)-positive, indicating accumulation of mitotic cells (a known mitotic poison nocodazole was used as a positive control). Importantly, this experiment also showed significantly higher pH3 (S10)-positive cells at higher concentrations of Stattic (10 and 20 μM) compared to the control, which could result from a combination of a weak mitotic block and pH3 (S10)-positivity linked to apoptosis.26 Nevertheless, the number of Stattic-induced pH3 (S10)-positive cells was markedly lower than that after the K2071 treatment, indicating that K2071 toxicity relies predominantly on its ability to affect mitosis-associated processes. Using STAT3-negative PC-3 cells, we proved that the mitotic arrest observed after K2071 treatment is STAT3 inhibition-independent as the accumulation of mitotic cells was also present (Figures 8C and S3J).

K2071 Impairs Mitotic Spindle Formation

Next, we analyzed the mitotic cells microscopically, aiming to describe the mechanism of the mitotic block induced by K2071. The formation of mitotic spindle and spindle poles (microtubule organizing centers; MTOC) was followed using indirect immunofluorescence staining of α-tubulin and γ-tubulin in RPE-1 hTERT cells during a 24 h time course of K2071 (5 μM) and Stattic (5 μM) exposure. After 2 h of exposure to K2071, the structure of the mitotic spindle was altered. After 4 and 8 h, the polymerized microtubules in mitotic cells were absent (Figure 8D). Notably, Stattic also induced disruption of the mitotic spindle. The complete disruption was observed even after 2 h of Stattic treatment. As this effect of Stattic on the mitotic spindle did not result in the accumulation of mitotic cells, and the DNA content and cell cycle distribution were comparable to control cells (see Figure S3K,L), Stattic, contrary to K2071, affected the cell cycle progression in all phases. Note that the standard shaped γ-tubulin signal of centrosomes reappeared in mitotic cells captured after 6 h of K2071 exposure and later, however, without the formation of spindle microtubules. The fraction of cells showed partial breakup of MTOC, indicating the unaffected first phase of MTOC separation.

To investigate whether K2071 directly impairs microtubules during mitotic spindle formation, we next analyzed tubulin polymerization in vitro in the absence or presence of K2071 using purified αβ-tubulin heterodimer.27 As shown in Figure S3M, the tubulin polymerization in the presence of 5 and 10 μM K2071 was slightly increased. However, it fell far short of the elevated level achieved in the presence of 10 μM taxol, which decreases the critical concentration of tubulin and enhances tubulin assembly. On the other hand, nocodazole, an inhibitor of microtubule assembly, completely inhibited polymerization at a concentration of 10 μM. This finding suggests that the profound effect of K2071 on mitotic spindle formation is not a result of modulation in tubulin polymerization.

In conclusion, K2071 harbors antimitotic activity arresting cells in prophase by altering mitotic spindle formation, resulting in cell death in nontransformed cells or mitotic slippage and developing senescent-like phenotype in cancer cells.

K2071 Suppresses Cell Migration

As STAT3 signaling regulates cancer cell migratory activity (for a review, see, e.g., ref (28)), which also depends on the intact cytoskeleton function, we investigated whether K2071 can affect cell migration. First, the in vitro scratch wound healing assay was used to follow the K2071 effect on the healing capability of the glioblastoma cell line U-251 MG. However, as the accumulation of cells in the mitosis interfered with the assay (Figure S4A), we followed the random migration of glioblastoma U-87 MG cells exposed to K2071 at subcytotoxic doses (1–3 μM) by time-lapse microscopy. Figure 9A shows significantly slower migration speed and shorter displacement distances of cells exposed to K2071 than control cells (see Figure S4B for representative images of tracked cells). Interestingly, the more sensitive glioblastoma U-251 MG cell line showed signs of cell toxicity and the disruption of cellular lamellipodia. K2071 treatment (3 μM) caused U-251 MG cells to lose the ability to effectively migrate despite extensive movement of lamellipodia, often leading to lamellipodia detachment and fragmentation (Video S1 and Figure S4C), indicating disrupted movement coordination. To further analyze the disruption of cellular movement, we analyzed the movement of RPE-1 hTERT exposed to K2071 (5 μM, 6 h) by using quantitative phase imaging. RPE-1 hTERT cells exposed to K2071 showed impaired cell polarization and defects in directional migration (Figure 9B and Video S2).

Discussion

Cancer is one of the most common causes of death worldwide. Developing novel anticancer drugs is critical for overcoming the current limitations in treatment. Many structurally variable molecular scaffolds with desired properties are being discovered from natural sources31 and by computational methods.32 These promising candidates can then be further optimized through diverse synthetic derivatizations. It is worth noting that many of these compounds belong to the class of sulfur-containing heterocycles. The unique chemical properties of the sulfur atom, including its variable oxidation states, polarizability, and electronic properties, contribute to the continued importance of sulfur-containing heterocycles as medicinally active compounds.33 In recent years, the scientific literature has documented diverse thioheterocyclic structures with promising anticancer activities. These structures include derivatives of thiophene,34 thiazepine,35 thiazole,36 thiazolidine-2,4-dione,37 1,3,4-thiadiazole,38 and tetrahydrobenzothiophene.39 Notably, a previously reported derivative of benzothiophene, 6-nitrobenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide (Stattic), inhibits STAT3 activation and dimerization.4 Components of the STAT3 signaling pathway are promising targets for the therapy of several human solid malignancies, including glioblastoma. In this study, we synthesized several novel derivatives of Stattic. Among them, compound K2071 was selected for its favorable physicochemical properties, particularly its ability to cross the BBB. The biological evaluation showed that K2071 harbors STAT3 inhibitory activity, cytotoxicity, and antimigratory properties in several human glioblastoma cell lines, comparable to those of the core compound Stattic. Notably, we found that the cytotoxicity of K2071 does not depend on its STAT3 inhibitory activity and is based on the inhibition of the cell cycle progression in the prophase by blocking mitotic spindle formation, which is followed by cell death in apoptosis or induction of senescent phenotype. Moreover, apart from senescence induction, K2071 possesses senolytic and senomorphic activities on glioblastoma senescent-like cells. Thus, the diverse properties of K2071 establish this compound as a promising candidate for formulating novel antitumor medications.

Discussion to Synthesis

The linear synthesis of methoxy- (K2071, K2885, and K2886) and hydroxy-substituted (K2887, K2263, and K2888) 6-(benzylamino)benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxides from benzo[b]thiophene involves five reaction steps. The oxidation of benzo[b]thiophene to dioxide 2 has been performed upon conditions reported by Boyd et al.40 Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis performed after heating to reflux for 1.5 h revealed that benzo[b]thiophene has been completely oxidized to the desired dioxide 2, and an undesired byproduct of mono-oxidation has not been observed. The following regioselective nitration was based on the reaction conditions reported by Du et al.41 Dioxide 2 involves a benzene core fused with thiophene 1,1-dioxide, which directs the SEAr reaction to the C6 position. TLC analysis revealed the formation of the desired product in the absence of other regioisomers. These conditions provided Stattic (3), a potent STAT3 inhibitor.4 Reduction of a nitro group in the structure of Stattic to an amino group was achieved under modified conditions reported by Villar et al.42 The conversion of Stattic into the corresponding amine 4 has been monitored by noncalibrated UV detection on LC–MS to improve reaction conditions. The ratio of Stattic and the amine product (3:4) has been determined from the crude reaction mixture after 2 h (31:69) and 18.5 h (25:75). Additional portions of Fe and NH4Cl significantly improved the conversion of Stattic to amine 4. Thus, only traces of Stattic were present in the reaction after 23.5 h (5:95). Modified reaction conditions reported by Serby et al. were performed to finalize the synthesis.43 The final reaction involved the following two reaction steps. First, a condensation reaction of an amine with the activated substituted benzaldehyde (5) provided an imine intermediate (6). It is remarkable that after 4 h at 50 °C, TLC analysis revealed that the reaction mixture still includes a considerable amount of initial amine 4. However, with the subsequent addition of NaBH3(CN) and an additional 19.5 h of stirring at the ambient temperature, only traces of amine 4 were observed on the TLC.

Preparation of K2891 was accomplished upon the oxa-Michael addition of methanol on K2071. For this purpose, we modified the reaction conditions previously reported by Chen et al. on a 6-substituted derivative of benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide.44 The additional reaction provided desired K2891 in 47% yield. Analysis of the crude reaction mixture via TLC revealed the presence of additional byproducts that were not further analyzed.

To reduce the C2=C3 bond of K2071, we first subjected derivative K2071 to modified conditions of reduction reported by Madec et al.44 However, under conditions of direct reduction, the desired saturated product has not been isolated. Additional analysis revealed 6-amino-2,3-dihydrobenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide (7), as a major product of the reaction. Thus, we slightly adjusted our strategy and first reduced the amine 4 to the saturated amine 7, upon similar reaction conditions. The saturated amine 7 was obtained in 87% yield and, upon two-step reaction methodology, previously applied for the synthesis of methoxyphenyl and hydroxyphenyl derivatives of K2071, was transformed into the desired saturated K2946. These conditions allowed the isolation of K2946 in 46% yield from saturated amine 7.

Direct condensation of the amine 4 with 4-methoxyphenyl isocyanate has been performed upon modified reaction conditions reported by Lawson et al.45 The reaction was running at ambient temperature, and despite the presence of the initial amine 4, after 24 h, the reaction mixture was processed and provided the desired urea derivative K2260 in a yield of 20%.

Discussion of PAMPA Evaluation

Based on PAMPA evaluation, all nine newly prepared compounds showed potential for passive diffusion across the BBB. Each novel derivative has shown a significantly higher permeability (Pe) value than Stattic. Moreover, Pe values of the novel derivatives corresponded to those of standard drugs with high CNS permeability. Among the newly synthesized compounds, the derivative K2071 had the lowest mean PAMPA value (Pe = 10.08 × 10–6 cm/s). However, its predicted BBB permeability was still significantly higher than that of tacrine (Pe = 5.96 × 10–6 cm/s), a drug known to cross the BBB readily and compared to K2071; its meta-regioisomer (K2885), ortho-regioisomer (K2886), and demethylated derivative (K2887) exhibited slightly higher PAMPA values (Pe = 12.75 × 10–6, 12.70 × 10–6, and 12.85 × 10–6 cm/s, respectively). Even higher PAMPA values were observed in meta-hydroxy derivative (K2263) and ortho-hydroxy derivative (K2888) (Pe = 15.98 × 10–6 cm/s and 16.95 × 10–6 cm/s, respectively). Significantly increased permeability was predicted for the oxa-Michael addition product of K2071 (K2891) and the urea derivative of K2071 (K2260) (Pe = 17.98 × 10–6 cm/s and 19.37 × 10–6 cm/s, respectively). The highest potential for the passive transport, based on PAMPA evaluation, was predicted for the C2–C3 hydrogenated derivative of K2071 (K2946) (Pe = 23.14 × 10–6 cm/s) which is even higher than the PAMPA value predicted for donepezil (Pe = 21.93 × 10–6 cm/s), a drug known by the ability to be transported across the BBB.46 In summary, the PAMPA evaluation of all newly synthesized derivatives provides promising results that suggest a relatively high potential for BBB permeability.

Biological Effects

Like its parental compound Stattic, K2071 potently inhibited the IL-6-induced activity of the STAT3 signaling pathway with IC50 in the micromolar range, a property shared with other novel Stattic derivatives that retain the ability of Michael addition. Moreover, it inhibited the proliferation of the nontransformed human RPE-1 hTERT cell line, again consistently with other newly developed Stattic derivatives that can act as Michael acceptors. Due to its optimal BBB crossing prediction analysis score, we broadened the cytotoxic tests of K2071 on a cohort of four human glioma cell lines, including the generally more resistant glioblastoma T-98 G cells. The K2071 antiproliferative effects were also reproduced using 3-D glioblastoma tumor spheroids with a dose of 1.6 μM sufficient to block spheroid growth for at least 1 week. Notably, the K2071 cytotoxicity was not STAT3-dependent as it was also efficiently toxic for PC-3 cell line deficient for STAT3.21,22 STAT3 inhibition and STAT3 inhibition-independent cytotoxicity were shared with the parental compound Stattic. These findings forced us to investigate the molecular mechanism determining the K2071 cellular activity. By analyzing the cell cycle distribution, we found that lower doses (5 μM) of K2071 resulted in the accumulation of cells with 4N DNA content. However, a more complex pattern of DNA content was observed for doses >5 μM, suggesting that K2071 affects more than one cellular target, or higher doses are needed to afflict different cell cycle stages. Focusing on the effect of lower doses, we found that the accumulation of 4N cells after 24 h exposure to K2071 is due to blocking cells in prophase. This effect was well pronounced in noncancerous RPE-1 hTERT cells with preserved effectiveness of mitotic checkpoints. Time-lapse microscopy revealed a prolonged mitotic duration of several hours in K2071-treated cells compared to 30 min in unperturbed RPE-1 hTERT cells. In concert with the notion that normal cells undergo cell death due to perturbation of mitosis, RPE-1 hTERT cells started to die progressively after 24 h of K2071 exposure. In contrast, a significant fraction of glioblastoma cells survived by mitotic slippage and, besides cell death, developed the senescent-like phenotype. Only a very low fraction of glioblastoma cells escaped the 5 μM K2071 treatment as determined by colony-forming assay. The block of cells in prophase using the low dose (5 μM) of K2071 is consistent with other biochemical parameters—downregulation of MCL-1 protein,47 upregulation of activated p38 kinase,48 and lack of Chk1 and Chk2 phosphorylation.49

When exploring the molecular mechanism of the mitotic block, we observed the defect of mitotic spindle formation, which is likely due to a modulation of microtubule organization. Nevertheless, the disturbance of microtubule organization was not cell cycle stage-specific, as interphase cells showed signs of depolymerized tubulin early after K2071 administration. There is evidence that STAT3 is involved in transcription-independent microtubule regulation. Ng et al. reported that STAT3 interacts with microtubule-destabilizing protein stathmin, and the expression of STAT3 is required for microtubule stabilization and cell migration.50 Notably, Morris et al. discovered by chemical library screening for compounds afflicting centrosome clustering in cancer cells a Stattic-derivative KM08165 with the STAT3 inhibitory effect.51 Authors attributed the biological effects of KM08165 to its metabolite, Stattic. However, we observed similar mitotic defects in STAT3-negative PC-3 cells, indicating that the effect of K2071 on the microtubule cytoskeleton is STAT3-independent. This effect could be accounted for by substituting STAT3 functions with other members of the STAT family and warrants further investigation. Remarkably, some microtubule-based chemotherapeutics (reviewed in ref (52)), such as paclitaxel and vinorelbine, affect constitutive STAT3 phosphorylation in cancer cells, and paclitaxel also inhibits cytokine-induced STAT3 activation.53 This cell response depended on ongoing translation, as cycloheximide inhibition of protein translation reverted the effect. In such a scenario, provided that K2071 is primarily a microtubule inhibitor, the inhibition of STAT3 could be secondary. The dual effect on inhibition of STAT3 tyrosine 705 phosphorylation and microtubule organization was already described for different compounds, such as LTP-1,54 indicating an intimate relationship between the microtubule cytoskeleton and STAT3 signaling. However, inhibiting IL-6-induced STAT3 tyrosine 705 phosphorylation after a short (15 min) exposure to K2071 directly affects STAT3. Further research is warranted to solve this uncertainty.

K2071, compared to taxol or nocodazole, affected in vitro tubulin polymerization only weakly, indicating that the interaction with the αβ-tubulin dimer would not be involved in mitotic poisoning. However, we cannot exclude the interaction of K2071 with γ-tubulin, which is essential for microtubule nucleation. Such an interaction could interfere with the formation of γ-tubulin ring complexes55 and nucleation of microtubules from mitotic spindle poles.

Significantly, besides common properties such as inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation and cytotoxicity, K2071 differs from its parental compound, Stattic, in several features. In concert with previous reports,24 we confirmed the thiol reactivity of Stattic being inactivated by incubation with glutathione, which effect was suppressed in the case of K2071. Thiol reactivity of Stattic considerably contributes to its pleiotropic mechanism of action.5 However, as incubation with glutathione abrogated Stattic cytotoxicity, its pharmacologic properties in vivo could also be affected by its high chemical reactivity.

Indeed, spontaneous interaction of Stattic, amine 4, and K2071 with thiol derivatives (cysteine, N-acetylcysteine, and glutathione) demonstrated that Stattic is significantly more reactive toward nucleophilic attack of the thiol group, proceeding via the modified mechanism of Michael addition than amine 4 and K2071. The rationale of these results is given by the electron density of the benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide core, which is mainly affected by the substituent attached to the C6 position. In the structure of Stattic, the nitro group located at the C6 position acts as an electron-withdrawing group (EWG), which also reduces the electron density at the C3 position of the conjugated core. Thus, the C3 position of Stattic is more reactive toward the nucleophilic attack of the thiol group. In contrast, amine 4 and K2071 bear a secondary amine group at the C6 position, which increases the electron density of the aromatic core, including the C3 position. As a result, the C3 position in the structures of both amine 4 and K2071 is less reactive toward the nucleophilic attack of thiol groups. The proposed mechanism of the thia-Michael reaction is initiated by the nucleophilic addition of Michael donor’s thiol group to the C3 position of the benzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide scaffold, acting as a Michael acceptor. The final conjugate, a thia-Michael adduct, is formed upon the addition of an electrophile (proton) to the C2 position (Scheme 6).

Proposed Mechanism of Thia-Michael Addition of the Cysteine on K2071 Providing the K2071-Cysteine Conjugate

The inhibition of STAT3 signaling is reported to affect cell migration (see, e.g., refs (56 and 57)). Indeed, K2071 affected cell motility in wound healing and random migration assays. The increase in T cell migration speed at low concentrations of K2071 can be attributed to the effect of K2071 on microtubules since microtubule depolymerization was shown to increase cellular contractility and speed through the release of RhoA GEF GEF-H1/ARHGEF-2.58,59 Significantly, K2071 dramatically suppressed the invasiveness of glioblastoma U-87 MG cells from Matrigel-embedded spheroids to the surrounding extracellular matrix.

In subapoptotic doses, STAT3 inhibitors, including K2071, convert cancer cells into the senescent-like state.57 It is debated whether IL-6-STAT3 signaling is the promoter of cellular senescence (see, e.g., refs (60−63)). However, somehow paradoxically, K2071 also harbors cytotoxicity for glioblastoma senescent-like cells. Although we did not explore the mechanism of K2071-mediated senolysis, this finding deserves attention in further studies. Last but not least, although K2071 harbored a little effect on the secretion of most proinflammatory cytokines tested, it significantly suppressed the secretion of MCP-1, a chemokine controlling migration and infiltration of monocytes and macrophages and involved in cancer progression.64

The possible cytotoxic effects of K2071, compared with the effects of Stattic, were also investigated in mice. Indeed, only mild signs of toxicity after higher administration doses were observed; notably, increased spleen weights after the treatments were found.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have designed, synthesized, and tested the biological effects of novel Stattic derivative K2071 selected based on its permissive physicochemical features to cross the blood–brain barrier. K2071 harbors STAT3 inhibitory activity and STAT3-independent cytotoxicity against human glioblastoma cell lines partly on the basis of inhibiting mitotic progression by blocking mitotic spindle formation. Antimigratory, senolytic, and senomorphic effects combined with good in vivo tolerability and lack of toxicity manifestations predetermine K2071 for further development as anticancer therapeutics.

General Synthetic Method

All used commercial reagents and solvents were purchased in the highest available purity from the supplier Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). The prepared compounds were purified by column chromatography on silica gel Kieselgel 60 (0.040–0.063 mm, 230–400 mesh, Merck, Kenilworth, NJ) or Büchi Pure C-815 Flash System using Flash Pure ID cartridges with silica gel. TLC was performed on Merck silica gel 60 F254 analytical plates using a mobile phase corresponding to the eluent used for the glass column chromatography. Structures on TLC were detected using either UV light (254 nm) or spraying with the detection reagent (10% solution of phosphomolybdic acid in CH3OH) with subsequent heating. Melting points of solid compounds were recorded in a Melting Point Apparatus-Büchi M-565 apparatus and were uncorrected. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) was evaluated in an Agilent 6470 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA) in positive or negative mode. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was determined by a Q Exactive Plus hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap spectrometer (ThermoFisher, Bremen, Germany). NMR spectra were recorded in CDCl3 at ambient temperature in a Bruker Avance NEO 500 MHz (499.87 MHz for 1H and 125.71 MHz for 13C). Chemical shifts were recorded as δ values in parts per million (ppm), referred to as solvent signal (CDCl3: 7.26 ppm for 1H and 77.16 ppm for 13C; DMSO-d6: 2.50 ppm for 1H and 39.52 ppm for 13C). Spin multiplicities are given as s (singlet), bs (broad singlet), d (doublet), dd (doublet of doublets), ddd (doublet of doublets of doublets), or m (multiplet). Coupling constants (J) are given in hertz (Hz). The purity of all the final products was >95%, based on LC with UV detection (λ = 254 nm).

TMZ-Induced Cellular Senescence

TMZ-induced senescent U-87 MG (TMZ-U-87 MG) cells were generated by treatment of cells (15,000 cells/cm2) with 100 μM TMZ for 14 days, with media exchange every 4 days. The development of the senescent phenotype was followed by observing cell morphology, senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity, and DNA replication (EdU incorporation29), see below.

Senescence-Associated β-galactosidase Activity

Development of cellular senescence was followed by the determination of senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity.66 Briefly, cells were fixed with 0.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Cells were washed twice with 1 mM MgCl2/PBS and incubated with an X-Gal staining solution at 37 °C for 3 h. The staining was terminated by three consecutive washes with ddH2O. Finally, the cells were allowed to dry, mounted with Mowiol containing DAPI, and imaged on the Leica DM6000 fluorescent microscope using the HC PLAN APO 20×/0.70 DRY PH2 objective and color CCD camera Leica DFC490 (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

DNA Replication Assay

Cells were incubated with 10 μM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) for 24 h and fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. To visualize EdU incorporation, click chemistry was performed with Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 647 dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the cells were stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI for 4 min to visualize nuclei, then washed three times with PBS for 5 min, rinsed with H2O, let dry, and mounted with Antifade Pro-long Gold Mounting Media. The stained cells were acquired by high-content imaging using an inverted wide-field microscope (Olympus IX81) equipped with UPLXAPO 20×/0.8 DRY CORR; FWD 0.6; CG 0–2 objective and sCMOS camera Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash4.0 LT+90, 6.5 μm pixel. The level of EdU incorporation in individual nuclei was evaluated using an Olympus ScanR analysis software, and the percentage of EdU-negative cells was determined.

Preparation of Tumor Spheroids and Determination of Spheroid Growth

Cell culture 96-well round-bottom plates were coated with 60 μL/well of 5 mg/mL poly-HEMA (poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) diluted in 95% ethanol. Plates covered with lids were left to dry by evaporation at room temperature under sterile conditions for 72 h. U-87 GFP cells grown as 2D cell culture were first washed with PBS; then, the cells were detached by incubation with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA solution at 37 °C for 5 min. After that, a cell suspension of concentration 5000 cells/mL was prepared. 200 μL of the prepared cell suspension (5000 cells/mL) was applied to each well of the 96-well poly-HEMA coated (1000 cells per spheroid/well). To increase cell–cell interactions and facilitate the spheroid formation, the plate was centrifuged at 200g at room temperature for 5 min and then incubated on a rotary shaker in the cell culture incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

After 3 days of incubation, 100 μL of the conditioned medium was changed with a fresh medium with two times concentrated K2071 (50 mM stock solution) or diluent DMSO (ctrl).

Spheroid growth and size were observed daily by microscopy in bright-field and fluorescence mode for 5 days using a ZEISS AxioZoom V.16 microscope (each spheroid in a triplicate). Images were analyzed by using ImageJ and made-to-measure macros. The fold change of the total green fluorescence to the starting time point was plotted.

Resazurin Assay

The resazurin reduction assay was performed as described previously.29 Cells were seeded onto a 96-well plate (12,000 cells/cm2) in hexaplicate and, the following day, treated with the compounds (dissolved in DMSO as a 50 mM stock) in a concentration range as indicated. After 24 h, 200 μL of the cell culture medium of each well was exchanged for 100 μL of resazurin (stock 30 mg/mL; Sigma) 10 times diluted in the culture medium, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1–3 h. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Envision, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Absolute fluorescence values were related to the values of control and untreated cells (RFU).

Crystal Violet Assay

For the crystal violet assay, cells were seeded onto a 96-well plate (12,000 cells/cm2) and after 24 h treated with the compounds in a concentration range as indicated. After the 24 h incubation, cells were washed twice with 150 μL of PBS and stained with 30 μL of 0.5% (w/v) crystal violet in 20% methanol for 15 min. Next, the plates were washed five times with ddH2O and left to dry overnight. Crystal violet was solubilized with 75 μL of 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma) in PBS for 15 min. Absorbance was read at 595 nm by using a microplate reader (Envision, PerkinElmer). The absorbance of crystal violet in treated cells was expressed as a percentage of the absorbance in untreated cells (RAU). Each condition was measured in at least in triplicate.

Cell Viability Luciferase Assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were propagated in a monolayer in a DMEM growth medium and incubated under a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37 °C. A day before the experiment, cells were harvested, counted, and seeded in cell culture-treated, white, solid 384-well plates (Corning, Inc., NY, USA). In each well, 1000 or 2000 cells were added to a total volume of 20 μL of the media for 24 h and 72 h compound incubation, respectively. Stattic and K2071 were dissolved in DMSO and predispensed in the 384-well plates compliant with acoustic dispensing. They were subsequently acoustically transferred to the test wells containing cells with ECHO 655 (Beckman, Inc., USA) integrated into the fully automated robotic HTS station (HighRes Biosolutions, USA). Each compound was tested in the range from 45 nM to 80 μM; each concentration was tested in three replicates. Cells were incubated with the compounds for 72 h. Cell viability was assessed by determining the level of intracellular ATP using CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Cell Viability Assay (Promega, WI, USA), and the luciferase signal was recorded with the multimode plate reader Envision (PerkinElmer, USA). Data were collected, normalized, and further processed using the LIMS system ScreenX.67 EC50 values were obtained by nonlinear regression analysis (Y = bottom + (Top-Bottom)/(1 + 10̂((LogEC50-X) × HillSlope))).

Detection of Apoptosis

U-87 MG or RPE-1 hTERT cells were seeded on a 6-well plate (12,000 cells/cm2), treated with the K2071 compound (1–100 μM) for 24 h or with Stattic (10 μM), harvested (floating, wash, and adherent fractions pooled), centrifuged at 200g and 4 °C for 5 min, and washed with PBS and Annexin V binding buffer (Apronex, Czech Republic) with repeated centrifugation. The pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of Annexin V binding buffer containing annexin V-FITC (1 μL/200 μL reaction; Apronex/Exbio, the Czech Republic) and 1 μg/mL Hoechst33258 (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark on ice. Data were obtained with an LSRII flow cytometer; 100,000 events were collected per condition. Data were analyzed using FlowJo 10 software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA), and the percentage of apoptotic (annexin V-FITC-positive/Hoechst33258-positive) cells, or of individual cell subpopulations (negative, single- and double-positive) was plotted.

Time Lapse Microscopy

For automonitoring cells, IncuCyte SX1 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany), located in an incubator maintained at 37 °C under the 5% CO2 atmosphere and 95% humidity, was utilized. The images (1408 × 1040 pixels at 1.24 μm/pixel) were acquired using a 10× objective lens in phase contrast, red fluorescence (excitation: 585 ± 20 nm, emission: 635 [625, 705] nm, acquisition time: 100 ms) channels every 60 min during 72 h. The first image was acquired 30 min after adding the compounds. The images were processed to measure cell confluence using IncuCyte software (version 2022A). Annexin V—Dy647, Apronex (diluted 1:2000)—was used for apoptotic cell staining.

Migration of RPE-1 hTERT cells was followed using a TESCAN Q-PHASE holographic microscope equipped with an incubation unit maintaining 37 °C and 5% CO2, Nikon PLAN FLUOR 10×/0.3 objective, and XIMEA MR4021MC monochromatic CCD camera.

Cell Cycle/DNA Profile Assay and Detection of Mitotic Cells

U-87 MG, RPE-1 hTERT, or PC-3 cells were seeded on a 6-well plate (12,000 cells/cm2), treated with the K2071 compound (1–100 μM) for 24 h, harvested (floating, wash, and adherent fractions were pooled), centrifuged at 200g and 4 °C for 5 min, and fixed in 70–80% ethanol. After at least 24 h, the cells were centrifuged at 500g in the swing-out rotor at room temperature for 5 min. The pellet was washed with PBS and resuspended in 200 μL of PBS with 0.5% Triton TX-100 and DAPI (final 5 μg/mL), incubated for at least 5 min, and analyzed using flow cytometry for DNA content (DAPI signal).

To detect mitotic cells, RPE-1 hTERT or PC-3 cells were seeded on a 6-well (30,000 cell/cm2), treated 2.5–20 μM K2071 or Stattic or nocodazole (a positive control for accumulation of mitotic cells), harvested, fixed, and permeabilized as described above for cell cycle/DNA profiling. After permeabilization, the cells were washed in 100 μL of PBS, centrifuged, and then blocked in 1% BSA in PBS (100 μL, 30 min, RT). After blocking, the cells were centrifuged and stained with rabbit phospho-S10 H3 antibody [Sigma-Aldrich/Merck Millipore (Darmstadt, Germany), 06-570, dilution 1:200] in 1% BSA in PBS (100 μL, 1 h; RT), centrifuged, washed in 100 μL of PBS and incubated with goat antirabbit Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody [Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific (Eugene, Oregon, USA), cat. no A-11036, dilution 1:1000] in 1% BSA in PBS (100 μL, 1 h, RT), centrifuged and washed in 100 μL of PBS. After the final centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in 100 μL of DAPI in PBS (5 μg/mL), incubated for at least 5 min, and analyzed using flow cytometry for pH3 (S10) and DAPI signal. All centrifugations were done on the swing-out rotor (500g, 5 min, RT).

Wound Healing/Scratch/Spheroid Cell Invasion Assays

U-251 MG cells were seeded on a six-well plate (50,000 cell/cm2), the scratch was made using the 10 μL pipet tip across the well, the medium was exchanged, and K2071 compound at concentration 2 μM was added. On the inverted wide-field microscope Leica DMI8 and LAS X software (Leica) with a fully automated stage, four positions for each condition were set, and images were captured (OBJ HC PL FLUOTAR 5×/0.25, FWD 12 | BF, POL, PH, IMC) in 30 min intervals for 26 h/24 h in total. Analysis of the images was done in ImageJ using a made-to-measure macro, which counted the size (μm2) of the scratch area covered.

To track movement of U-87 MG and U-251 MG cells, an IncuCyte SX1 platform was utilized essentially as described previously.68 Cells were seeded at 5000 cells/cm2 in a 24-well plate, labeled with 0.5 μM SiR-DNA (Spirochrome, 251SC007), and images were acquired every 15 min for 24 h. Images were preprocessed by the Lucas-Kanade algorithm implemented in the Image Stabilizer ImageJ plugin and cropped to 1200 × 900 pixels to eliminate edge artifacts. Tracking was performed by the StarDist Versatile (fluorescent nuclei) model69 in the TrackMate70 plugin of ImageJ/Fiji71 using a LAP Tracker (linking 50 μm, gap closing 50 μm, 2 frames, track segment splitting 10 μm). Nuclei tracked for less than 2/3 of the experiment duration were not analyzed.

To evaluate the effect of K2071 compound on the invasiveness of U-87 MG cells in a complex 3D microenvironment, U-87 MG spheroids were prepared and embedded into a Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning). First, U-87 MG spheroids were prepared in poly-HEMA-coated well plates as described above. Each spheroid cultivated for 6 days was transferred from the well of a poly-HEMA-coated well plate into the Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of cell growth medium. Then, the supernatant was carefully removed, and the spheroid was carefully placed in the center of a Matrigel droplet (50 μL, cproteins = 9 mg/mL) using a pipette tip. Thereafter, the dish containing a spheroid embedded in the Matrigel droplet was placed in an incubator for 30 min (37 °C, 5% CO2) to allow the Matrigel to polymerize. Afterward, 2 mL of cell growth medium with or without K2071 was added to the dish [the final concentration of DMSO was 0.01% (v/v), and spheroids treated with the same concentration of DMSO in cell growth medium without K2071 were used as a negative control]. Spheroids were incubated with tested solutions for 72 h. The invasiveness of the cells was observed by ZEISS AxioZoom V.16 microscope with PlanNeoFluar Z 1.0×/0.25 objective and 40× zoom. The images were obtained after embedding the spheroids and after 24, 48, and 72 h of incubation with tested solutions. Images were acquired as several Z-slices with 10 μm step to cover the whole spheroid, processed as maximal projection in Z and by Enhance Local Contrast (CLAHE) ImageJ plugin.

After 3 days of incubation, spheroids were stained with calcein-AM and propidium iodide to distinguish live and dead cells. Spheroids embedded in Matrigel were first washed with 2 mL of PBS, and then, the solution of calcein-AM (8 μM) and propidium iodide (30 μM) was added. Spheroids were incubated with the staining solution for 1 h in an incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2). Afterward, the staining solution was removed; 2 mL of PBS was added; and images of spheroids were captured. Images were acquired by an Andor Dragonfly 503 spinning disk confocal microscope equipped with a 10× objective (HC PL APO 10×/0.40 CS2). Calcein-AM was excited by a 488 nm wavelength laser and viewed with a 525/50 nm band-pass filter; propidium iodide was excited by a 561 nm wavelength laser and viewed with a 600/50 nm bandpass filter.

Colony Forming Assays

Cells were seeded on 24-well plates (12,500 cells/cm2) and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, cells were treated with 5 μM K2071 or an adequate volume of vehicle control (DMSO). After 24 h of treatment, two different fractions of cells were collected (floating and attached fractions). The attached cells were collected via trypsinization, counted, and reseeded in triplicate on 6 cm dishes. Seeding density for T-98 G and U-87 MG cell lines was 4000 cells per dish and 10,000 cells per dish for the A-172 cell line. Cells were maintained for 14 days to allow for colony formation. The floating fraction was collected subsequently: medium and two washes with PBS were collected from 24 wells separately and centrifuged at 200g for 3 min. Pellets were resuspended in the fresh medium and seeded on a six-well plate. The floating fraction was maintained the same as that of the attached fraction. The experiment was done in a duplicate. Cells were fixed with a staining solution (0.05% w/v crystal violet, 1% formaldehyde, and 1% methanol) for 20 min and washed with ddH2O. Images were obtained with a scanner. Powdered sugar was inserted into the plate to obtain a completely white background upon the scanning. The experiment was performed in three independent repeats. Images of individual colonies were captured on a ZEISS AxioZoom V.16 microscope using the objective PlanNeoFluar Z 1.0×/0.25 and ZEISS Axiocam 305-color CMOS camera.

Immunoblotting

Cells were washed with PBS and harvested into the 95 °C-heated sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample lysis buffer (2% SDS, 50 mM Tris–HCl, 10% glycerol in double-distilled water) and sonicated (3 × 10 s at 3 μm amplitude with 30 s cooling intervals) using a Soniprep 150 (MSE, London, UK). In Vitro Thiol Reactivity Assay

U-251 MG cells were seeded on a six-well plate (20,000 cells/cm2), and after 24 h, the medium was exchanged with a fresh medium without or with 50 μM K2071. After another 24 h, the medium and cells were harvested by a cell scraper (Sarstedt), transferred with the medium to a 15 mL centrifuge tube with 2 mL of methanol (Honeywell, 34860), and frozen at −20 °C until analysis. To remove proteins, the samples were mixed with acetonitrile in a 2:1 ratio (200 μL of sample and 100 μL of ACN) and left on the vortex for 10 min. Precipitated protein was removed by centrifugation at 25,000g for 25 min.

In vitro SH-group reactivity for compounds 3 (Stattic), 4, and K2071 was assessed by incubation with equimolar concentrations (50 μM final concentrations) of relevant thiol-containing molecules (cysteine, N-acetylcysteine, and glutathione). Incubations were performed in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 24 h.

Supernatants from both experiments were transferred into an HPLC vial and separated using reverse-phase chromatography on the Zorbax Eclipse RRHD C18 (50 mm × 2.1 mm; 1.8 μm) column with a mobile phase (A) 5 mM NH4HCO2 in 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile/methanol (1:1) with 0.1% formic acid. The amount of parent compound and thiol-conjugates was determined by UV–Vis spectrometry (Agilent 1290 DAD) and mass spectrometry (Agilent QqQ 6470). The identity of the K2071-cysteine conjugate was further confirmed by the measurement of product ion spectra.

To assess the “neutralization” effect of glutathione on Stattic and K2071 in biological settings, T-98 G, U-87 MG, and PC-3 cells were seeded on a 96-well plate (15,000 cells/cm2). After 24 h, the medium was exchanged with a fresh medium supplemented with Stattic (10–100 μM) or K2071 (10–100 μM) alone or in combination with glutathione (20–200 μM). After 24 h of exposure to compounds in a concentration range as indicated, cell viability was determined by the crystal violet assay as described above.

Tubulin Polymerization Assay

Tubulin was purified from porcine brain through temperature-dependent cycles of assembly and disassembly, followed by phosphocellulose chromatography as described by Dráberová et al.27 Microtubule assembly was monitored via turbidimetry74 at 350 nm and 37 °C using a Shimadzu UV-1800 recording spectrophotometer, equipped with a temperature-controlled cuvette holder (Shimadzu, Canby, OR, USA). The resulting assembly mixture contained 15 μM tubulin in BRB80 buffer (80 mM PIPES pH 6.8, 1 mM EGTA, and 1 mM MgCl2), supplemented with 1 mM GTP and 3 M glycerol. In some cases, polymerization was carried out in the presence of 10 μM nocodazole or 10 μM taxol.

Mice

C57BL/6 (B6) male mice, 16–20 weeks old, were obtained from the Institute of Molecular Genetics of the Czech Academy of Sciences, Prague, Czech Republic. Experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of the Institute of Molecular Genetics of the Czech Academy of Sciences, Prague.

In Vivo Toxicity Study

To evaluate the tolerance and acute toxicity, K2071 and Stattic (a control compound) were i.p. administered in B6 mice (three mice per group) at increasing doses (four times on days 0, 2, 4, and 7). Compounds were dissolved in DMSO (20 mg/mL) and completed with the physiological solution before injection. The working solution contained 10% DMSO, and the injection volume was 200 μL/dose. During the experiment, the mice’s behavior and possible apparent signs of toxicity were monitored daily. The body weights were determined on days 0 and 8. On day 8, blood (25 μL) was withdrawn from the retroorbital sinus into EDTA-containing tubes using a capillary pipet containing an anticoagulant (heparin). Basic hematological parameters were measured using a Mindray BC-5300 Vet. Mice were then sacrificed and autopsied, and the abdominal organs (spleen, liver, and kidney) were removed, examined visually, and imaged by a digital camera. Spleens were weighted, and single-cell suspensions from the spleens were prepared by homogenization through a cell strainer (70 μm; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Erythrocytes were osmotically lysed using ammonium chloride-potassium lysis buffer. The cell suspension was washed three times in the RPMI-1640 medium and used for further analysis by flow cytometry for selected cell populations.

Spleen Cell Analysis by Flow Cytometry

Spleen cell suspensions were washed and preincubated with anti-CD16/CD32 (2.4G2) antibody to minimize nonspecific binding for 20 min at 4 °C following the washing step and incubation with labeled primary antibody for 20 min at 4 °C. Relevant isotype controls of irrelevant specificity were used. FACS buffer (PBS, 1% FBS, and 0.1% NaN3) was used for all washing steps and analysis. The following antibodies were used for FACS analyses: BV421 antimouse CD45 (30-F11), PE antimouse CD8α (53–6.7), BV711 antimouse CD4 (RM4-5), APC antimouse Ly-6g/Ly-6C (Gr-1) (RB6–8C5), and BV711 antimouse CD11b (M1/70). All antibodies were obtained from BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA. FACS analysis was performed using flow cytometer BD FACSymphony A5 Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo 10 software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).
