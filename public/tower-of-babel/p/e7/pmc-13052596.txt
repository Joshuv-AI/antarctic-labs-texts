Impact of parent education programmes on confidence and self-efficacy in parents of preterm infants: a systematic review
Authors: Puja Padbidri, Veena Joshi, Hollie Hix-Small, Pooja Bhavarth Dekhane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFamilies of preterm infants can experience high stress during neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) hospitalisation, which interferes with the development of parenting confidence and self-efficacy through active engagement with their infants. Parent NICU support programmes provide guidance and education to build this connection. This systematic review examined the effects of parent education programmes on confidence and self-efficacy among parents of preterm infants.DesignSystematic review in PubMed/Medline, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Google Scholar and Web of Science.SettingNICU.ParticipantsParents of preterm infants.InterventionEducation programmes offered to parents in the NICU.Main outcome measuresQuantitative surveys measuring parent confidence or self-efficacy.Results14 studies met inclusion criteria from an initial 220 identified. Five were randomised controlled trials and nine were non-randomised studies. Programmes combining information delivery with opportunities for parent−infant engagement were most effective. Infant access and time to practise skills were key factors in transferring programme knowledge to increase parent confidence and self-efficacy. While 6 of the 14 studies did not show statistically significant differences between groups, all interventions demonstrated improvements in parental confidence or self-efficacy from baseline. Vast variability in duration of intervention, proper descriptions of facilitator trainings, methodological limitations and potential confounding factors reduced the overall quality of evidence to a low level.ConclusionBoth unit-specific programmes developed within individual NICUs and commercially available, copyrighted parent education programmes hold value. They are generally effective in building parent confidence and self-efficacy among parents of preterm infants. Integrating either type of programme into standard NICU care may hold promise for strengthening parental competence and promoting family-centred outcomes. Clarity on content/topics of programmes, duration and level of facilitator trainings can strengthen the process of supporting parents in the NICU, even in low resource settings.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024524063.

Introduction

First-time parents often seek information to prepare for their newborn’s care. Parenting self-efficacy develops through bidirectional interactions between parents, infants and their social environment1 and is strengthened through repeat caregiving experiences and social support from extended family, social networks and healthcare professionals.24 Higher parental confidence, a term used interchangeably with self-efficacy,2 is associated with improved infant outcomes, especially in the social-emotional and cognitive domains.5

Parents of preterm children, less than 37 weeks gestation, face additional challenges as they transition to parenthood. Preterm birth frequently requires neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission and prolonged hospitalisation. Parent–infant separation and unfamiliar NICU environment, heavily reliant on specialised equipment and care procedures, increase parental stress and reduce caregiving opportunities to build confidence.6 7 Limited social support, combined with the restrictive nature of the NICU setting, places substantial physical, psychological, social and financial burdens on families that may persist beyond discharge.8 9

Supporting parental confidence is an essential component of neonatal care. NICU standards, competencies and recommended practices for Infant and Family Centred Developmental Care promote early and sustained parental involvement such as skin-to-skin contact, breastfeeding and participation in routine care activities such as diapering and soothing.10

Enhancing parental knowledge and engagement has been associated with reduced stress, improved confidence, greater satisfaction with caregiving and better developmental outcomes.11 Parent education in the NICU aims to increase caregiving confidence and preparedness for discharge.12 13

This systematic review was conducted to identify and synthesise studies that examined the impact of NICU-based parent education interventions on parenting confidence and self-efficacy in parents of preterm infants, as measured by parent-reported surveys. Although parental stress is closely related, the review focused on outcomes that healthcare professionals can directly influence through educational interventions.

Methods

Patients were not involved in any phase of this review study. As the study involves analysis of existing literature without any direct patient interaction, public involvement was not necessary.

The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines, and the protocol was registered on PROSPERO (ID Number: CRD42024524063).

In January 2024, the review team defined the research question using Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes (PICO) criteria. The review included studies of parents of preterm infants (P), that evaluated NICU-based or discharge-focused parent education interventions (I), with comparisons within or between groups (C) and outcomes measured using validated surveys assessing parental confidence or self-efficacy.

A fourth team member assessed the protocol for methodological accuracy and the free version of Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia; available at www.covidence.org) was used for initial study screening and data management.

Sources and search strategy

The literature search, based on the finalised PICO parameters, was initiated in March 2024. Database searches included PubMed/Medline, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Google Scholar and Web of Science. Search terms included ‘preterm’, ‘parents’, ‘mother’, ‘father’, ‘parent self-efficacy’, ‘parent confidence’, ‘parent education’, ‘NICU infants’, ‘NICU interventions’, ‘discharge planning’, ‘parent competence’, ‘premature infant’, ‘NICU graduates’, ‘neonatal intensive care unit’, ‘neonatal interventions’, ‘parenting interventions in NICU’, ‘parenting confidence survey’, ‘parent self-efficacy survey’ and combinations of these.

Study eligibility

Articles were eligible for inclusion if they were published in English, after 2013, involved preterm infants (<37 weeks gestation), delivered an intervention to parents during the NICU stay and used a validated survey to measure parental self-efficacy or confidence. Studies assessing parent outcomes at discharge or up to 3 months postdischarge were included if the intervention was initiated before discharge. Studies were excluded if they used qualitative or mixed-methods designs, implemented interventions solely after discharge, were systematic reviews or meta-analyses, or lacked full-text availability.

Study selection and data collection

Three team members independently searched the selected databases and uploaded eligible records to Covidence. Duplicate records were automatically removed by the software. Abstract and full-text screening were divided among the reviewers, with two serving as the primary screeners and the third acting as a tie breaker in cases of disagreement. Reasons for exclusion were documented independently by each reviewer within Covidence. Following the resolution of conflicts, the final set of included studies was reviewed and confirmed by the team.

Consensus was reached on all studies except one, in which the intervention was administered to one parent of the infant, while outcomes were assessed for the other parent who did not participate in the programme.

All 14 selected studies were read and critically evaluated independently by each of the three reviewers. Initial data extraction and risk assessments in Covidence were conducted using Covidence templates during the first review. Final data extraction and synthesis were completed using Microsoft Excel.

Analysis of interventions

To evaluate the content and reporting quality of the interventions, we applied guidance from the Best Evidence Medical Education (BEME) framework, classified outcomes using Kirkpatrick’s levels and completeness of intervention reporting was evaluated using the Template for intervention description and replication checklist.

Risk of bias assessment

Two team members primarily assessed study quality using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I- V.2) tool for observational studies and the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB-2) for randomised trials. The overall quality of evidence was then graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) group criteria (very low, low, moderate or high).

Results

The literature search identified 220 unique articles. Of these, 107 were screened in full text, and 14 studies met the PICO criteria for inclusion and data extraction. Study designs

Of the 14 included studies, four were randomised controlled trials (RCTs),1417 four were quasi-experimental studies with a two-group pre–post design,1821 two were cohort studies,22 23 two one group pre–post designs,24 25 one time lagged two group design26 and one was a quasi-RCT.27

Of the 14 studies, 4 (29%) reported unit-wide implementation of structured programmes such as Train-to-Home, Helping U Grow (HUG) and Family Integrated Care (FiCare/FiCare Plus)

Study locations

The studies were conducted in high and upper-middle income countries such as the USA (n=2), England (n=2), Canada (n=1), the Netherlands (n=1) and Singapore (n=1), Turkey (n=2), China (n=2), Iran (n=2) and Thailand (n=1).

Participant characteristics

Across all studies, there were 1674 participants. After excluding dropouts, 833 participants were assigned to intervention groups and 684 to control groups. One study included an additional supervision group of 15 participants. Six studies measured the impact of interventions on both parents, five exclusively addressed mothers’ outcomes, one study examined fathers and two studies focused on both parents and their infants.

Outcome measures used

Several well-established, self-reported surveys were used to assess parental self-efficacy and confidence. These instruments measure core aspects of parenting self-efficacy, including knowledge, confidence and competence in everyday caregiving activities and recognising and responding to their baby’s needs. All scales have moderate-to-high psychometrics and have been used across diverse settings, cultures and languages.

The Parenting Sense of Competence Scale (n=5) is a 16-item measure with two subscales, scored on a 6-point Likert scale (range 16-96). The Perceived Maternal Parenting Self-Efficacy Scale (n=6) includes 20 items, scored on a 4-point Likert scale (range 20–80). The Pharis Self-Confidence Scale (n=2) has 13 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (range 13–65). The Maternal Confidence Questionnaire (n=1) and Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale-Short Form (n=1) have 14 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale (range 14–70).

Higher scores on each of these measures indicate a higher level of self-efficacy or confidence.

Intervention characteristics

Foundations/theory

All studies were designed to strengthen the infant–parent dyad despite challenges associated with the NICU environment. Interventions were based on Bandura’s self-efficacy theory28 and principles of family centred care,29 both of which emphasise sharing information, active engagement, positive support and hands-on practice to enhance parent confidence.

Core topics/components

Although programme focus and intensity varied, common elements included education on feeding, recognising infant cues, daily caregiving activities (eg, diapering) and fostering parent–infant interaction. All programmes provided opportunities for parents to review educational materials, practise caregiving skills and develop confidence in interpreting their infant’s cues.

Infrastructure/materials used

Except for the NICU-2-Home app studies, all informational components were delivered in a NICU waiting room or designated area. All programmes engaged parents in bedside caregiving before discharge to practise and reinforce learning.

A variety of materials were used across the programmes. Four of the 14 studies incorporated virtual components, such as the NICU-2-HOME app, an online chat platform enabling parent-team communication after discharge and web-based video resources. One study (Mohammadi et al)17 used dolls and had specially designed garments to support Kangaroo Care training. Seven studies incorporated videos, handouts, manuals or workbooks for parents as part of the intervention.

Training was delivered through individual consultations, group sessions and bedside practice facilitated by various team members. Nurses delivered the intervention exclusively in four studies and were involved in all. Multidisciplinary participation, including dietitians and therapists, was reported in two studies.

Availability of programmes

Aligned with Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, NICU-2-HOME (https://www.angeleyehealth.com/solutions/nicu2home) is a smartphone-based programme designed to improve care coordination through tailored education, daily clinical updates, discharge planning tools and tracking features to empower parents, improve their confidence and enhance discharge preparedness. The programme is available as a smartphone application and has been integrated into the AngelEye Health platform which has many features including an embedded language translator.

COPE for Hope (COPE) was identified during data extraction; however, the association website was inactive at the time of our writing and information regarding its development, structure and current organisational affiliation could not be verified. COPE teaches NICU staff how to guide parents toward enhanced infant interactions and discharge preparation, which enhances parental self-efficacy and reduces stress.

FiCare is a structured model of neonatal care which views parents as active members of the NICU team and supports them to provide routine caregiving under professional supervision. The model combines psychosocial support, parent education, participation in daily care discussions and staff training to enhance parental self-efficacy and infant outcomes (https://familyintegratedcare.com/). There is no fixed cost for FiCare as it is a model of care rather than a commercially licensed programme. In published research, FiCare, like COPE, is generally implemented as part of a quality-improvement initiative or clinical study.

NICU-2-Home, COPE and FiCare have primarily been used within the USA where they were developed. Conversely, Train-to-Home was developed in the UK and has no standardised cost. The programme is freely available online (https://www.bristol.ac.uk/academic-child-health/research/research/maternal-health/prep/). HUG Your Baby (HUG) is a fee-based programme that provides structured parent education, teaching them to recognise and respond to their infant’s cues. Video-based materials and bedside coaching are key elements (https://www.hugyourbaby.org).

Among the remaining programmes reviewed, only Phiaching et al (2020) reported using publicly available resources and videos, with links given in the publication. The details of remaining self-designed programmes (n=4) were limited to the information described within the published article.

Staff training

Details regarding programme facilitator qualifications, experience or additional training were largely absent from the included studies. Only the four unit-wide implementation studies described staff training. However, no one described assessment of staff competence following training. In Ingram et al,21 nursing staff identified limited time for implementation as a barrier to the Train-to-Home programme.

Parent involvement/feedback (Kirkpatrick levels)

Level 1 (reaction): Five studies (including NICU-2-HOME, COPE and FiCare) reported incorporating parent feedback during development. Six others reported parent input, although its timing and integration into the programme design were not clearly described.

Levels 2 (learning): Level 2a measures change in participants’ attitudes, perceptions and confidence while level 2b measures knowledge acquisition. All studies included in this review used self-reported parent surveys to measure the impact of the intervention on parental confidence, without specific measurements of knowledge.

Level 3 (behaviour): Descriptions of hands-on parent engagement with their infant were not described across the studies. Although demonstration, supervision and supervised parent practice were likely provided, these components were not articulated and no studies formally assessed improvement in performance linked to improved confidence.

Level 4 (results): Four of the 14 studies (29%) evaluated readmission rates as a longer-term health outcome. All four studies reported lower readmission rates for the intervention group.

Intervention duration and outcome measures

Intervention duration and the timing of outcome measure data collection varied widely across studies. The shortest intervention was a 20 min HUG video. Eight studies specified durations ranging from a single 4 hour session to 4 weeks of online platforms access or multiday programme sessions. The COPE programme delivered scheduled education sessions at specific points during the NICU stay, such as 2 days after admission or one to 2 weeks before discharge. Six studies reported implementing the intervention throughout the infant’s NICU stay, including the HUG, Train-to-Home, NICU-2-HOME and FiCare programmes.

All studies describe the duration of the educational or information-delivery components of the interventions. None described the number of or frequency of parent–infant interactions, in which this information was applied prior to discharge.

The timing of data collection varied with respect to the duration of the infant’s NICU stay.19 For example, Garfield et al (2016)14 started the app use 2 weeks prior to discharge, and Gok et al (2021)18 began the intervention 1 week before discharge, whereas Kachoosangy et al (2020)15 and Ingram et al (2016)21 initiated intervention at admission. Similarly, data collection timing varies substantially across studies. Post-test assessments ranged from discharge to several weeks with postdischarge, with the shortest pre–post interval being 7 days in Phianching et al (2020).19 Details are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Routine/Usual care

Studies that included control or comparison groups provided limited details on usual care. In some cases, routine care included distribution of handouts or videos played in waiting rooms. Additional details are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Effect on parent confidence and self-efficacy

All included studies reported improvements in parent scores from baseline, irrespective of study design or intervention duration. Six of the 14 studies did not report a statistically significant difference between groups in postintervention scores (Ansari et al (2023),22 Veenendaal et al (2022),23 Garfield et al (2022),26 Gok et al (2021),18 Ingram et al (2016),21 Garfield et al (2016))14

Follow-up

Only four studies assessed parental confidence after discharge, with follow-up periods up to 30 days. In Garfield et al (2022),26 confidence initially improved but plateaued over time, possibly reflecting limitations in measurement tool sensitivity. In Phiaching et al (2022),19 the intervention improved fathers’ self-efficacy but not mothers’, potentially because mothers were already actively involved in breastfeeding and infant care. The Li et al (2022)27 study, which provided monthly support for 6 months postdischarge, demonstrated continued gains in confidence, suggesting that ongoing education and anticipatory guidance may support long-term outcomes.

Using RoB 2, only one of the four RCTs was rated as low risk of bias, while the other three were deemed to have some concerns. In Garfield et al (2016),14 the groups were not similar at baseline. Kachoosangy et al (2020)15 provided an overall value of significance in their tables, instead of specific group score changes, and Chen et al (2022)16 did not provide the score values themselves.

Similarly, using ROBINS-I V.2, 7 of the 10 non-randomised studies were rated as having some concerns, 2 moderate concerns and 1 as critical risk. These studies did not describe procedures to address bias due to confounding such as measurement of training outcomes of facilitators in implementation studies (Hunter et al, 2018)25 or selecting participants within the established inclusion-exclusion criteria (Shandra et al, 2018)24 or differences in staff perceptions between two data collection sites (Ansari et al, 2023).22

In several non-randomised studies, comparison groups were recruited from different locations or at different time points, resulting in variability in participant demographics and group sizes. Consequently, all non-randomised studies were considered at risk due to potential confounding factors.

Given the heterogeneity in outcome score reporting, intervention duration and intensity, and timing of data collection, meta-analysis was not appropriate. As a result, the overall quality of evidence, as assessed using GRADE (figure 3C), was low.

To enhance the accuracy of our reporting, we reviewed the BEME quality of evidence scale, Kirkpatrick’s levels of evaluation and the QUESTS (quality, utility, extent, strength, target and setting) criteria.30 Overall, studies demonstrated improvements in parent confidence scores from baseline. However, comparison and control groups also received routine medical team guidance, which may have supported parenting confidence separate from the interventions. All studies were classified as a Level 2 a on the Kirkpatrick scale. All studies demonstrated good clinical utility with the intended population and in appropriate settings. However, due to the predominance of studies conducted in resource-rich countries, limited reporting on facilitator training and insufficient detail to support replication in low-resource contexts, the overall certainty of evidence was rated as low.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review evaluating the impact of structured NICU-based educational programmes on parental confidence and self-efficacy. Overall, these programmes demonstrated positive effects, despite variations in content, duration, intensity or mode of delivery. Most interventions increased opportunities for parent–infant interactions under guided supervision, reinforcing that building parental confidence requires both education and experiential practice.

Parental confidence and self-efficacy are key predictors of infant development, influencing secure attachment, the quality of parent–child interactions and early cognitive and emotional growth.31 Parents of preterm infants may miss early opportunities for positive interaction and hands-on caregiving that typically foster confidence during the newborn period. Previous research suggests repeated, supported engagement in caregiving activities enhances parental confidence and self-efficacy among parents of infants with medical complexities.32 Most NICUs incorporate parent education as part of family-centred care and discharge planning to equip parents with the knowledge, skills and practice required to care for their infants both in the NICU and at home.33 34

Findings from this systematic review align with these observations. All educational interventions introduced during the NICU stay improved parental confidence and self-efficacy from baseline, regardless of delivery format or duration. Interventions that combined structured education with opportunities for guided practice such as bedside training, skin-to-skin care and interactive coaching were particularly effective. These findings emphasise the value of embedding parent education initiatives within routine NICU practice to strengthen parental competence and promote secure parent–infant relationships after discharge.

Parents of preterm infants are more likely to experience heightened stress and fear, which may negatively affect emotional well-being and disrupt the acquisition of parenting skills.35 The NICU team plays a critical role in recognising and responding to individual family needs and tailoring educational support accordingly.36 In all reviewed studies, allied health professionals such as nurses, therapists and lactation consultants primarily delivered education. In most settings, these professionals are responsible for family-centred care and provide coaching on essential caregiving practices such as feeding, positioning and pain management. International standards, such as the European Standards for Newborn Health and guidelines from the American Academy of Paediatrics, emphasise adequate staffing of trained nurses, therapists and lactation consultants to support parent education and facilitate a smoother transition home.37 38

Effective parent education should align with the infant’s clinical needs and developmental stage.39 The programmes in this review included core topics such as preterm growth patterns, feeding, infant safety and parent–infant interaction, all of which are essential for parent discharge preparedness.40 Principles of family-centred care emphasise the importance of active participation by parents in their infant’s care from the time of admission.41 42 Although all studies reported improved confidence and self-efficacy scores from baseline within intervention groups, six found no significant between-group differences compared with usual care. This may reflect the positive effect of informal education within usual care, variability in programme duration or intensity, or limited sample sizes. Importantly, even minimal structure support was associated with improved confidence, underscoring the value of consistent parental engagement. Significant improvements reported in two studies that offered full parental access suggest that parent presence and sustained involvement are critical elements for building confidence.

The longevity of intervention effects was not investigated. Parents’ and infants’ needs for information and support will change over time, especially following discharge. Therefore, NICU-based parent education programmes may benefit from including a postdischarge home component to sustain the increases in parental confidence. The four studies that used readmission rates as an outcome reported reduced hospital visits and readmissions, indicating a potential association between enhanced parental confidence and lower healthcare system utilisation.

Another key area for investigation is the effect of confidence-building programmes on long-term parental mental health outcomes, including post-traumatic stress disorder, related to the distress experienced during the NICU stay.43 In this review, two studies that included measures of parental stress, distress and anxiety as secondary outcomes did not report significant improvements with the interventions. These findings suggest educational programmes focused on knowledge and skills may need to provide additional psychological support to address broader parental mental health needs.

All studies included in this review were conducted in high-income or upper-middle-income countries. However, substantial inequities in healthcare access and parental engagement in NICU education and caregiving exist in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).44 45 Infection-control concerns are frequently cited as barriers to parental presence in the NICU, despite evidence suggesting that parent participation may reduce rates of infant infection and readmissions. Leveraging technology, such as mobile applications and telehealth-based education, along with investing in staff training, may help overcome some of these challenges and support parental empowerment and improved developmental outcomes in resource-limited settings. There is a need for replicable studies, those that include sufficiently described methods and samples, in different contexts.

Conclusion

Comprehensive parent education programmes can effectively enhance the confidence of parents caring for preterm infants. Interventions that facilitate parental access to their infant, teach recognition and appropriate response to infant cues, and provide opportunities for repeated guided practice during daily caregiving activities appear particularly impactful in strengthening confidence at discharge. However, evidence on the long-term sustainability of these effects remains limited, underscoring the need for follow-up studies assessing parental confidence and self-efficacy after discharge. Expanding structured, evidence-based education programmes in LMIC contexts may further promote equitable family-centred neonatal care and improve outcomes for preterm infants worldwide.
