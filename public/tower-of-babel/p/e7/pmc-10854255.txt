Estimation of Maximum Occlusal Bite Force of School-going Children in Different Dentition: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Niharika H Mutt, Manoj K Mallela George, Srinivas Nallanchakrava, Rebecca V Charlet, Hari Shanker Alampally, Dwitha Animireddy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBite force is an important indicator of the clinical state of mastication and its efficiency. Bite force measurement using a gnathodynamometer is a reliable method to assess the biomechanical properties of the masticatory system. It varies with different geographic populations and physiological and environmental factors.AimTo estimate the maximum occlusal bite force (MOBF) in school-going children of Hyderabad city at different dentition periods and to correlate with different variables.Materials and methodsA total of 392 school-going children in the age range of 3–13 years were selected following selection criteria (214 males and 178 females). They were divided on the basis of the dentition period. Demographic details along with general physical parameters such as height, weight, and maximum mouth opening (MMO) were recorded in a predesigned proforma. Maximum bite force was measured using a digital bite force instrument.ResultsMaximum occlusal bite force (MOBF) was 18.04 N in primary dentition of age 3–5 years, 47.64 N in mixed dentition of age 6–11, and 108.39 N in permanent dentition of age 12–13 years. The magnitude of bite force was observed to be directly proportional to age, height, weight, and dentition period. Males had greater bite force than females in all three dentitions. However, the difference was significant only in mixed dentition (p < 0.05). Of all the dentition bite force was significantly higher in permanent dentition.Bite force showed an inverse relation with body mass index (BMI) in primary and mixed dentition but a positive correlation was seen with permanent dentition. The mean MMO in males was slightly higher when compared to females. It increased with an increase in the dentition stage.ConclusionBite force is multifactorial. It has a significant correlation with physiologic and morphologic factors that influence the values of bite force.How to cite this articleMutt NH, Mallela George MK, Nallanchakrava S, et al. Introduction

The masticatory function can be described as the ability of an individual to break apart solid food. Understanding the state of every component is necessary for evaluating the masticatory efficiency of the stomatognathic system and the magnitudes of masticatory bite forces. Maximum occlusal bite force (MOBF), which is the consequence of the jaw elevator muscles’ action as altered by the craniofacial biomechanics, is a measure of the masticatory system's functioning status.1 Stronger bite power is suggested by studies to be the outcome of an improved masticatory system (Bakke 2006).

The amount of bite force varies greatly and is influenced by a wide range of parameters that are related to the anatomical, physiological, and geographical characteristics of subjects like age, gender, caries, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) in addition to craniofacial morphology. Additional factors influencing the bite force include the kind of recording devices used, the method used to measure it, the patient's position, the location of the sensor in the oral cavity, unilateral or bilateral measurements, and the size of the mouth opening during the measurements.2

There have been reports on a variety of techniques and tools for measuring bite forces. From several varieties of gnathodynamometers, lever-spring, manometer-spring, and lever, to micrometer devices and delicate electronic devices, the devices have developed.3

In dentistry, bit force determination has been widely used to evaluate the therapeutic effects of implants and prosthetic devices, to determine the effect on orofacial growth and development, and to provide reference values for epidemiological studies. It also helps to understand the mechanics of mastication. The utility of widely using biting force measurements in many dental specialties has been demonstrated by a number of research.2 But it became clear from doing a careful analysis of the pertinent literature that there weren't many studies assessing bite force in Hyderabad schoolchildren.

The purpose of the current study is to report on the maximum bite force among children at various dentition stages and investigate the relationship between this force and variables such as age, gender, height, weight, BMI, and MMO.

Materials and Methods

The present cross-sectional study was carried out between January 2021 and February 2022. The Institutional Ethics Committee granted ethical approval. Schools were chosen from different zones of Hyderabad and the required number of subjects was selected by stratified random sampling.

The sample size was estimated with a confidence level of 95% and the power of study was set at 80%. A total of 2002 children aged between 3 and 13 years were examined, of which 392 children who fulfilled the requirements for inclusion were chosen for the research as shown in Figure 1. Each participant's informed consent was acquired using a standard consent form that was available in both the local language and English.

General demographic details, physical characteristics (height, weight), myofunctional assessment (molar relation, MMO), stage of dentition, and MOBF (anterior, posterior right, and left) were recorded in a predesigned proforma.

Portable weight and height scales were used to record anthropometric measurements of height and weight. BMI was calculated using the formula (BMI = weight/height2).

Measurement of Maximum Occlusal Bite Force

Bite force was measured with a portable digital bite force instrument (gnathodynamometer) designed for pediatric use (Fig. 2). The individuals were sitting upright to ensure that the Frankfort plane was parallel to the floor before the bite force was recorded.

Bite fork instrument

To shield the subjects from infection, a disposable plastic tube covered the biting fork/gauge. OBF was assessed on the right, left, and anterior regions bilaterally, with a 15-second break in between bites. The subjects were told to bite the gauge three times as forcefully as they could. The MOBF for each side was determined by averaging the three readings for that side.

Discussion

Individual bite force has been measured in order to determine the prosthetic device's therapeutic efficacy and to comprehend the mechanism of mastication. This information is crucial for treatment planning, particularly when designing the prosthesis.4,5

After a review and search of the literature, it was found that no studies were conducted to determine the MOBF of children in the Hyderabad city population. Hence, a cross-sectional study was conducted to determine the MOBF of school-going children of Hyderabad city and to correlate it with different influencing variables. The present cross-sectional study would guide in obtaining a bite force reference value for future research.

Numerous bite force instruments are manufactured and commercially available with various designs to obtain accurate bite force values.6 However, they were used for research purposes rather than for clinical purposes. Hence, in the present study, a new customized bite force instrument with a calibration setup was prepared for pediatric use. It is easy to use, noninvasive, portable, has a digital display, is inexpensive, and has tiny load cells that are sufficient for measuring biting force in a clinical context. It also provides normal values and information about the variables that affect their variability.

It was shown that MOBF and the dentition stage were positively correlated (Table 1). This relationship may arise from improvements in masticatory effectiveness throughout the course of the various dentition stages, as well as the growth of the masticatory system and masticatory muscle. The change from the mixed to permanent dentition stage made the relationship noticeable.7 The outcomes corroborated the findings of Owais et al. and Sonnesen et al., who found a favorable correlation between MOBF and children's rising dental eruption phases.

At the age of nine, when the first molar's root growth is complete, occlusal force increases significantly.8 The results of this study verify that the passage from primary to permanent dentition is correlated with an increase in biting force that is acknowledged in relation to age. This result is consistent with a number of earlier investigations by Bonakdarchian et al., Braun et al., Kamegai et al., Usui et al., and Owais et al.1,9,12

However, when Braun et al. looked at bite force in adults between the ages of 25 and 41, they found no correlation between bite force and age.13 However, the population with permanent dentition in the current study was between the ages of 12 and 13. Children will have more muscle mass as they become older, which will give them a stronger bite force.14 According to Sonnesen et al., dental eruption through the various dentition stages causes a rise in bite force in developing youngsters between the ages of 7 and 12 years, allowing for a greater number of occlusal units and higher bite forces.7

In males, the average bite force was 19.5 N in primary, 53.5 N in mixed, and 107 N in permanent dentition. In females, it was 16 N in primary, 41 N in mixed, and 109 N in permanent dentition. It is evident that men had mean bite forces that were significantly larger than those of women, even though there was little variation in permanent dentition (Fig. 5). This discovery validates the findings of research by Serra et al., Su et al., Sathyanarayana et al., Olthoff et al., Sonnesen et al. and Bonakdarchian et al.11,15,17 According to a 2011 study by Mountain et al., boys between the ages of 3 and 6 had a stronger biting force than females, albeit this difference was not statistically significant.

Comparison of mean bite force with gender in different stages of dentition

Anatomical differences and the fact that males have more muscle mass than females contribute to gender disparities in bite force. Though not visible until puberty, male masseter muscle fibers have a larger diameter and more cross-sectional area than female masseter muscle fibers.2 Consequently, there is no statistically significant difference in bite force between males and females in the current study.

Males’ maximal bite power grows more quickly than females’ during the postpubertal era, according to a 1996 study by Braun et al. Larger biting forces in men may result from larger teeth and greater periodontal ligament areas, according to earlier research.2,18 Calderon et al found a statistically significant variation in bite force across genders solely in the molar area (Table 2).19

A positive correlation between MOBF and weight in primary, mixed, and permanent dentition was observed, but it was statistically significant only in permanent dentition. The current result concurs with the findings of Owais et al., who reported a positive relationship between bite force and body weight.1 In the same way, the studies by Rentes et al. and Mountain et al. revealed a slight positive correlation.20 Maximum bite force and weight were found to be significantly correlated by Pereira-Cenci et al. for both male and female individuals.21

Su et al. reported that in a sample of children aged 4–6, there was no relationship between body weight and bite force values. Such discrepancy between the findings can be due to racial differences or differences in technique while recording bite force.22

The current study found a small but favorable connection between bite force and height for both primary and permanent dentition. The results of Rentes et al. and Owais et al. concur with this one. However, Mountain et al. have found a strong association.

Abu Alhaija et al. examined bite force in adults and found a comparable association. According to Pereira-Cenci et al., there is a noteworthy association between bite force and males only.1,20,21,23,24

In the current study, the relationship between BMI and maximum biting force was shown to be statistically significant in permanent dentition but not in primary or mixed dentition. This is consistent with the findings of Koc et al., who found that in a sample of 34 people, the BMI variable did not demonstrate a significant correlation. A similar observation with nonsignificant results was reported by Mountain et al.24,25 On the other hand, biting force and BMI were found to positively correlate by Duarte et al.26

The mean MMO in males was slightly higher when compared to females (Table 3). Pearson's correlation coefficient signified a negative correlation between MOBF and MMO in primary dentition and a weak positive in mixed and permanent dentition. This discovery aligns with the findings of Su et al.

This finding deviates from a 2003 study conducted by Su et al. with children between the ages of 9 and 12. Young preschoolers’ masticatory muscles and jawbone are still developing. Regression analysis, on the contrary, revealed a positive relationship between biting force and maximal mouth opening in both mixed and permanent dentition on the right and left sides. This implies that the bite power is stronger the larger the mouth opening. A study conducted in 1986 by Fields et al. revealed a similar association in adults.27

In primary and mixed dentition, there was no discernible variation in mean values between the right and left posterior areas for bite forces of both genders at different ages (Table 1). There was a significant difference in the mean biting force between the left and right sides of the permanent dentition. One possible cause of this could have been the practice of chewing on one side. The results of the study by Tortopidis et al., which demonstrated that the anterior transducer (mean 286 N) registered the lowest forces and the bilateral posterior transducer (mean 580 N) the greatest, are consistent with this finding.28

During the measurement of MOBF, positioning of the bite fork in the oral cavity plays an important role in influencing the bite force values. In the current study posterior region showed more bite force when compared to the anterior region. Because of their wide periodontal regions, molar teeth resist more compression during clenching than anterior teeth. It has been reported that for younger patients, the biting fork should be positioned closest to the first premolar or deciduous molar.8

Because of this, occlusal bite force in the molar region was measured bilaterally in all dentitions in the current investigation.

The measurement device's precision has a direct impact on occlusal biting force readings. Because the biting element in many bite force measuring devices is made of a stiff substance, it might be challenging to measure bite force properly in younger children. A pair of occlusal bite forks with replaceable soft plastic sleeves were introduced in order to get around these restrictions. It is especially beneficial to use with children, as the digital display of the recorded bite force is exhibited. An occlusal bite force gauge was used in this investigation to record bite force in a secure and comfortable manner.

One of the limitations of the study was that it encountered certain behavioral issues, particularly when measuring the MOBF of children with primary and mixed dentition. Children had to repeat the test after opening their mouths to get the maximum measurement; some children became tired while recording bite force many times.

The design of the instrument and the subjects’ sensory feedback might have limited the willingness of children to exert maximum effort which could have influenced the values. Thus, further studies are needed to conduct with a larger sample size, comparing with different bite force instruments and also evaluating bite force for different populations, and different anthropological features and variables.
