Evaluating forest cover change and its impact on ecosystem services in Gargeda state forest western Ethiopia
Authors: Zelalem Telila, Abayineh Amare, Teferi Tolera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanges in land use and their effects on ecosystem services have become a global concern due to their implications for ecological integrity and human well-being. This study evaluated the impact of forest cover change on ecosystem services in Gargeda State Forest, western Ethiopia, over the past 30 years. Satellite imagery data from 1993 to 2023 was analyzed with ArcGIS to assess landscape dynamics. Ecosystem service values (ESV) are subjected to the benefit transfer approach based on valuation coefficients and household survey to support community perceptions. The results revealed a substantial loss in forest cover (− 110,214 ha) and water bodies (− 2064.87 ha), alongside increases in agroforestry (16.85%), farmland (11.94%), and settlements (1.76%). Shrubland and grassland exhibited fluctuating trends. Total ecosystem service value declined by 44.08%, from $414.81 million/ha/year in 1993 to $231.93 million/ha/year in 2023. The significant reduction observed between 1993 and 2003 by $122.94 million/ha/year). The estimated ecosystem functions, supporting services, showed the highest decline, by $90 million/ha/year and regulating services fell by $45.18 million/ha/year. Sensitivity analysis yielded coefficients below 1, confirming the reliability and robustness of the valuation approach. Local communities recognized the importance of remaining forest fragments, noting their provision of essential services such as food, raw materials, medicinal plants, and water supply. This major decline reflects the far-reaching impacts of land use changes over the past thirty years, indicating a reduced capacity of ecosystems to deliver vital services. Overall, the study highlights that the ongoing conversion of forest land into other uses threatens natural forests and contributes to declining environmental quality. These findings underline the need to incorporate ecosystem service values into land management policies to support sustainable development and conservation of forest across the region.Keywords: Agroforestry, Benefit transfer, Ecosystem service value, Forest, Land use changeSubject terms: Ecosystem services, Environmental impact, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Ecosystem services are the benefits that humans acquire from ecology, either directly or indirectly1,2. Human activities impact both nature and societies3. Population growth and unsustainable practices degrade environmental services and lead to severe global issues, primarily driven by various land use modifications4. Since 1950, around 68% of ecosystem services have been lost faster than they can regenerate5, due to agriculture expansion, deforestation, and urbanization6. Over these years, change in forest ecosystem has been much faster and wider than any other period in human history7.

Forest ecosystems, in particular, have experienced unprecedented change, requiring effective management to support biodiversity8. Over 30% of global forests provide ecosystem services, however 13 million hectares are lost each year due to agricultural demand5. According to Peprah9, deforestation and forest degradation threaten intact forest ecosystems, with both developed and developing nations, resulting in reduced biodiversity and environmental assets10,11. Specially in tropical regions leads to the potential extinction of up to 100 species per day12,13. Therefore, Balancing ecosystem service provision with human development is essential for sustainable forest conservation14.

Land use change significantly impacts the ability of biological systems to support human needs7. It alters ecosystems’ structure and function, leading to rapid farmland conversion for cash crops like coffee, settlements, and logging15,16. This conversion threatens global biodiversity and can drive species to extinction17. Halting deforestation is crucial to prevent biodiversity loss and the collapse of ecosystems, which could have catastrophic consequences for humanity10.

In sub-Saharan Africa, a growing population competes for scarce natural resources18. Ethiopian ecosystems face threats from logging, deforestation, and fragmentation, leading to declines in biodiversity and water shortages19,20. Overexploitation and land use changes diminish the ecological benefits forests provide, gradually harming those who rely on these resources for their well-being21,22. This degradation gradually harms individuals reliant on these natural resources for their well-being23,24.

Moreover, scientific investigation on the effects of land use change (LUC) on ecosystem services is increasing globally25. Hence, Studies show that LUC significantly reduces Ecosystem services and alters their interrelations25,26. Effective forest management requires spatially explicit evaluations of these services27. In Ethiopia, numerous studies have examined land use changes in both urban and rural areas28–30. Recent trends in agricultural expansion and coffee farming have led to significant LUC31. However there remains a critical gap in assessing how land cover changes impact the value of ecosystem services over time, especially using community-based perceptions and spatial benefit transfer approaches in protected forest areas. Forests provide essential services, including provisioning, regulation, cultural, and socioeconomic benefits, while contributing to poverty alleviation32,33.

The human-forest interaction is incredibly strong in Ethiopia34. Community perceptions provide valuable insights into the positive environmental impacts of forests, utilizing local knowledge to improve decision-making35. In Sub-Saharan Africa, rich local knowledge can inform forest management, especially in areas with limited data36,37. Despite the importance of these insights, few studies in Ethiopia integrate local perspectives with spatially quantified ecosystem service changes in protected forests, particularly in National Forest Priority Areas like Gargeda State Forest.

Additionally, while models like ARIES, MIMES, EVT, and InVEST require complex data and technical expertise, making them less accessible in resource-limited contexts2,38,39. Critically, these tools often fail to address how land cover changes influence the value of ecosystem services over time, particularly when incorporating community-based perceptions in protected forest areas. To fill this gap, the present study adopts a Benefit Transfer Approach based on coefficient values40. This approach is cost-effective, scalable, and rapid assessment without the need for complex datasets and technical proficiency41.

The Gargeda Forest, designated a National Forest Priority Area in 1983, aims to protect biodiversity and essential ecosystem services42. However, it faces significant pressures from development and poor management43. The lack of community involvement in decision-making further threatens these ecosystems44. Research on biological and environmental change has focused on land use, however conflicts between communities and services from protected areas still need improvement45,46.

Mapping multiple ecosystem services is essential for developing effective land use policies45. Various approaches evaluate ES across different ecosystem47–49. However, there are two appropriate methods to assess the values of a natural capital of forests. Primary benefit transfer method that estimate the monetary values of natural capital from policy site to study spot50 and the second method involves an InVEST model38. Each method has limitations in accurately reflecting site-specific values51,52. Assigning economic value to ecosystem services can raise awareness among decision-makers, the public, and scientists, promoting environmental sustainability53,54.

Overexploitation of forest resources can greatly diminish the provision of vital ecosystem services5. Despite their importance, these services have received limited research attention, particularly regarding statistical data and community perceptions. To support effective resource allocation and sustainable land management in Ethiopia, it’s crucial to monitor land use changes and their impacts on ecosystem service values. Therefore, this study aims to quantify the ecosystem services gained or lost in Gargeda State Forest due to land cover changes, using a spatial and temporal analysis framework.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the stunning Gargeda National Forest in the Kellem Wollega zone of western Ethiopia, about 652 km from Addis Ababa. Covering 302,072.50 hectares lies at coordinates 8°13’3” to 9°13’0” E and 34°13’0” to 34°53’3” N (Fig. 1). With temperatures ranging between 19 and 27 °C and annual rainfall of 800–1270 mm. Coffee is the main income source, with agriculture providing over 87% of employment. Most of the population (95.56%) lives in rural areas, but only 2.5% have electricity; many rely on firewood (75%) and charcoal (33%) for energy (ZAO, 2024). The varied altitudes create rich vegetation and supports diverse ecosystems including Afro alpine and moist evergreen Afro-montane forests55.

Land use classification techniques

Image classification

Researchers mapped and quantified land cover changes over a 30-year period (1993–2023) using remotely sensed satellite data and ArcGIS 10.7 software. The analysis utilized multi-spectral Landsat imagery, including Landsat 5 TM, Landsat 7 ETM+, Landsat 8 OLI, and the most recent Landsat 9 TIRS-2 (Table 1).The Landsat 9 Thermal Infrared Sensor-2 (TIRS-2) is particularly useful for monitoring deforestation and forest dynamics, offering a reliable and consistent record of forest changes56.

Measurement of ecosystem services

Ecosystem services valuation assignment

Current economic frameworks prioritize price-tagged commodities and services59; however, ecosystem services that enhance human well-being often lack monetary valuation60,61. This absence of measurement can lead to underestimations, complicating the determination of funds needed for sustainable resource management1. Even though measuring global ecosystem services is challenging, understanding their variations can inform policymakers about their impacts on human quality of life62. Various methods exist to evaluate ecosystem services, including the revealed preference method63, and the stated preference method, or contingent valuation, which gauges preferences through surveys64. The benefit transfer method estimates values at unstudied sites using data from existing valuation studies65. This method is especially useful in contexts where conducting primary valuation studies is time-consuming, costly, or logistically difficult. Benefit transfer method is popular due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness66. As valuation techniques evolve, they continue to provide critical insights that support evidence-based decisions for sustainable environmental management67. Therefore, this research employs the benefit transfer approach to evaluate ecosystem services for the policy site.

Biome equivalents for land use categories

This study examined various changes between biomes and land use categories (Table 3). According to Tolessa et al.68, rural settlements differ significantly from urban biomes described by Costanza et al.40. Rural communities provide superior ecosystem services by supplying organic waste materials to nearby gardens, as their permeable landscapes reduce erosion, unlike urban areas69. Despite the differences both provide shelter for people who utilize land for different practices Costanza et al.40, forests and shrub lands are not the same, shrubs not having as much canopy cover as forests, while do provide some ecosystem services comparable to those of Forests70. Tropical forest are used to interchange the natural forest areas in our situation since the western part of the forest is assumed to be humid Afromontane forests71. Furthermore, agricultural land that is used by small-scale farmers to produce crops was used to estimate the amount of cultivated land.

Perception of local communities

A total of 395 households from the Gargeda State Forest were randomly selected and interviewed across three districts and 11 local administrative units. Prior to the survey, field observations were conducted with community members and experts. The survey, administered in ‘Afaan Oromoo’ between December 2023 and January 2024, collected data on socio-demographic characteristics and perceptions of ecosystem services using pretested questionnaires. Data analysis was performed using STATA statistical software release 16 (https://www.stata.com), identifying significant predictors of perceived ecosystem services. A satisfaction index79 assessed farmer perceptions of forest services, using a three-point Likert scale, with results normalized to a percentage index.

Land use dynamics from 1993 to 2023

The estimation of land use change from 1993 to 2023 was conducted at ten-year intervals using various Landsat image with a resolution of 30 m (Fig. 2). The multi-temporal land cover maps reveal significant changes over this period. In 1993, extensive areas were covered by dense and intact forest. However, this dense forest has been notably diminished due to agricultural expansion, particularly for coffee cultivation, as well as illegal settlements.

Discussion

Change of land use 1993 to 2023

Over the past 30 years, the Gargeda state-owned forest has faced significant land use and cover changes. This study revealed a substantial net loss of forest land from 1993 to 2023, primarily driven by human activities like illegal settlements linked to population growth. Following the fall of the Derg government in 1991, increased migration intensified ecological degradation and deforestation. The collapse of the governance framework for forest resources raised demand for coffee farming, agricultural land, and housing. Figure 2 shows a notable transition from agroforestry systems in 1993 to forest cover in 2023, indicating a complex land use transformation. While the change appears straightforward, it is influenced by both ecological processes and socio-political factors, such as government-imposed land restrictions and reduced human activity that likely encouraged natural regeneration. Additionally, differences in satellite sensor capabilities between Landsat 5 and the more advanced Landsat 8 or 9 affect land cover interpretation, with earlier sensors prone to pixel mixing in transitional areas82. This may have obscured distinctions between agroforestry and fragmented forest patches in 1993. The 2023 data also reveal overlapping forest and settlement zones, reflecting ongoing land use dynamics. The extraction of forest products for daily needs, including charcoal and firewood, further worsened the situation. Deforestation accelerated due to shifting land use dynamics driven by economic growth, with expanding coffee production spurring agricultural expansion and settlement growth49. Rapid land conversion also degraded ecosystem services83.

Key drivers of land use change in Gargeda State Forest included land tenure policies, social unrest, and biophysical factors, reflecting broader Ethiopian trends of forest loss and agricultural expansion43,84. Population increase, a shift towards commercial crops and expanding settlements have resulted in the degradation of forestland convinced by different authors on different land use classes11,85. According to86,87, illegal logging was occurring in all parts of Ethiopia during the collapse Derg regime at 1991. This have also contributed to the first period (1991–2003) decreased forest cover in Gargeda State forest (Table 4).

Change of ecosystem service value

This study offers a comprehensive analysis of the ecosystem service values of Gargeda State Forest over the past 30 years. Using the benefit transfer method from Costanza, et al.40, it estimates changes in ecosystem services due to land use changes between 1993 and 2023. The method reveals significant declines in ecosystem services, consistent with prior research88. A sensitivity analysis validates the reliability of our ESV predictions. However, challenges arise in modeling agroforestry land use classes, as their yields depend on proxies lacking actual ecosystem service value, illustrating a limitation in Costanza et al.40 framework where some land use classes may be undervalued.

Ecosystem services and land use change are closely linked, significantly affecting human well-being89. The value of these services varies by land use type: agricultural lands excel in provisioning but lack cultural and regulatory functions90, whereas degraded forests provide cultural and regulatory benefits with limited provisioning91. Grasslands are strong in regulatory services but weak in supporting functions92. Land use change disrupts ecological processes like soil erosion and water cycling93. In the study area, converting forests and water bodies to coffee-based agroforestry and settlements has notably diminished ecosystem services, threatening environmental sustainability and biodiversity94.

Ecosystem service valuations, once focused on natural ecosystems, are now being applied to cropland and agroforestry95. These transformed landscapes have largely compensated for the loss of ecosystem service values from forests, providing significant income sources in Ethiopia96,97. Coffee-based agroforestry is particularly promising, as it may enhance ecosystem service flows through the integration of trees and crops97,98. However, the extent to which these agricultural advancements can offset forest service losses remains unclear. Coffee plantations offer cultural, regulatory, and ecological benefits99,100, while farmlands are crucial for food security and sustainable development. Farmers often retain key tree species to preserve ecosystem services101. Increased land use intensity alters vegetation cover, impacting hydrological processes and biogeochemical cycles102. Population growth rapidly transforms land into settlements, expanding farmland and disrupting water balance and surface flow, thereby affecting the interactions among water, soil, and vegetation that support ecosystem services103,104.

ESV function

The analysis indicates that ESV rank in the following order: provisioning, regulating, supporting, and cultural services (Table 7). Forest ecosystems provide essential services such as food, raw materials, medicine, and water, along with regulating services like climate control and disturbance regulation105. These functions are vital for global carbon sequestration and mitigating natural hazards like landslides and air pollution. Significant declines in provisioning services such as food production, water supply, and raw materials have been observed106,107. Haileslassie et al.108, noted that deforestation in the Ethiopian highlands has led to severe water shortages and quality issues. Reduced forest cover hampers water regulation, diminishing related services109,110. Conversely, there has been a slight increase in food production due to expanding agricultural land111. However, global trends show a decline in raw materials, consistent with the patterns of expanding agriculture through deforestation112. Notably, forests store more carbon in biomass and soils than is present in the atmosphere113.

Forest landscapes are home to most of the Earth’s species and provide essential goods and services to humanity114,115. However, regulatory functions like erosion control, disturbance regulation, and climate control have significantly declined. Degraded forests lose their ability to provide these critical services116. In Ethiopia, changes in land use have weakened forests’ capacity to retain carbon, leading to higher greenhouse gas concentrations117,118. This conclusion is supported by their findings and those of Tolessa et al.68 who noted a high rate of soil loss due to land degradation in Ethiopian forests which led to the drop in erosion control services. Related research in the area notes that with the loss of disturbance control services, ecosystem resilience to natural events like landslides and floods is lost119,120.

The most significant losses occurred in supporting services like pollination, waste treatment, nitrogen cycling, and soil formation, with nutrient cycling declining the most. This disrupts essential nutrient cycles in tropical ecosystems. Reduced waste treatment capabilities compromise the forest’s ability to filter pollutants116,121. Cultural services, including recreation and heritage, have also declined due to environmental degradation and biodiversity loss, negatively impacting tourism122. Woldemariam et al.123 highlight that land use changes in Ethiopia have harmed ecotourism, while the decline in cultural heritage services indicates a loss of cultural values tied to nature124.

Community perception

According to the study results, community members recognize the ecosystem service values associated with the remaining forest fragments. The finding corresponding to those associated with services in Brazil as reported by125 where the authors also discovered that forests play a crucial role in providing food and using resources for rural communities whose daily needs are met by these resources. Similarly, local people proximate forest areas place more value on ecosystem services that are seen as relevant, immediate, and visible like access to raw materials, medicine, and water because these services directly affect their quality of life126. Many local communities prioritize tangible services, and adjustments to these have clear effects. Additionally, the cultural and spiritual services reflect a growing recognition of non-material benefits over time127. These now encompass particulars like spiritual health, maintaining one’s cultural identity, and preserving customs learned through habitation. Change in results, which reflect the views of nearby people highlight the significance of maintaining conservation efforts and sustainable forest management.

Conservation and policy relevance

This study has several implications for forest management and policymaking. Ongoing forest cover loss necessitates community-based conservation initiatives and improved law enforcement. Reducing forest demand through sustainable land use and community involvement in governance is crucial128. Integrating economic assessments of ecosystem services into development strategies can shift policies from short-term land conversion gains to long-term forest preservation129. This demonstrates the economic value of ecosystem services, promoting environmentally sensitive decision-making130. Further research on maintaining and enhancing ecosystem service values in Gargeda State Forest is recommended, emphasizing longitudinal studies to track conservation impacts over time.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the impacts of land use dynamics on ecosystem services in Gargeda State Forest, which provides essential functions such as raw materials, carbon sequestration, water regulation, and biodiversity support. Using GIS technology and benefit transfer techniques, supplemented by key informant interviews to enhance reliability, the research aimed to provide comprehensive insights. While the benefit transfer approach offers valuable valuation information, its accuracy depends heavily on the quality and comparability of primary studies, requiring careful attention during data collection and processing. In addition, the use of Landsat satellite imagery, though cost-effective and widely applied, presents potential accuracy limitations due to its moderate spatial resolution, which may influence the precision of land cover classification. Advances in valuation methods and remote sensing technologies continue to provide critical insights for sustainable environmental management decisions.

The findings indicate a significant decline in forest ecosystem services due to land use changes, alongside increases in agroforestry and farmland areas and their associated service values. These practices contribute positively to food production, disturbance regulation, carbon sequestration, and pollination. Variations in service values among grassland and bushland highlight the complex interplay of social and environmental factors. However, the conversion of forest land to agroforestry, farmland, and settlements driven by population growth intensifies pressure on natural resources, threatening biodiversity and diminishing ecosystem service availability. This decline underscores the urgent need for sustainable agricultural practices to mitigate further environmental degradation.

The reduction in ecosystem service delivery reflects deteriorating environmental quality, emphasizing the necessity of stakeholder engagement in conservation efforts, especially for remaining forest patches. Integrating these findings into land management strategies is crucial for fostering sustainable development. This study thus serves as a valuable resource for academics, policymakers, and environmental experts committed to advancing sustainable initiatives in the region.

However, challenges such as the remoteness of the study area, limited road access, political instability, limited internet access, and financial constraints affected data collection, validation, and the resolution of land cover analysis. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable evidence and a foundation for policymakers, researchers, and environmental practitioners committed to advancing sustainable resource management in the region. Therefore, to enhance the accuracy and relevance of land cover analyses, future research should incorporate high-resolution satellite imagery alongside ground validation and socio-environmental data, enabling more nuanced and context-specific interpretations of remote sensing outcomes.
