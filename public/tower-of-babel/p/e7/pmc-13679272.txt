Mapping the interplay of intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits in children with and without autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Xin Wang, Shuo-Lin Pan, Li-Zi Lin, Yan Hu, Kang-You Li, Jin Jing, Xiu-Hong Li, Lei Shi, Ning Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits are psychological dimensions that vary continuously across the population. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) may reflect extreme expressions of these traits, yet their interdependencies and difference between typically developing (TD) children remain poorly understood.MethodsWe recruited 186 children with ASD and 364 TD children aged 6–12 years. Intelligence was assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children–Fourth Edition, while empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits were measured using validated Chinese versions of the Empathy Quotient, Systemizing Quotient, and Autism Spectrum Quotient. Undirected partial correlation networks and Bayesian networks were estimated separately for each group, adjusting for demographic covariates, including gender, parental education level, and household income.ResultsAcross groups, cognitive empathy consistently emerged as the central hub in both undirected and Bayesian network analyses. In ASD, autistic traits formed a densely interconnected cluster with limited cross-domain links, while in TD children they were more flexibly integrated with systemizing and other domains. Intelligence formed a cohesive yet relatively independent subnetwork, though in ASD verbal comprehension was directly predicted by social communication competence. The estimated networks demonstrated robust accuracy and acceptable stability.ConclusionThese findings reveal both shared and distinct patterns of network organization among intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits in children with and without ASD. Cognitive empathy may represent a potential target for future intervention research, while systemizing tendencies and cognitive strengths may also inform individualized support strategies in ASD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08411-4.

Introduction

Human cognition and behavior are shaped by multiple psychological dimensions, many of which are distributed continuously across the population rather than confined to categorical boundaries [1, 2]. Intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits exemplify this dimensional nature. Each construct captures a distinct but related domain of human functioning, yet all share the characteristic of varying along a spectrum that can be observed in both clinical and non-clinical populations [3–5].

This dimensional perspective is particularly relevant to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [6]. Rather than being qualitatively distinct from typical development, ASD may lie at the extreme of naturally distributed traits [7]. For instance, autistic traits measured by autism spectrum quotient (AQ) scores follow a quasi-normal distribution, with higher scores reflecting stronger features but not necessarily a diagnosis [8]. Similarly, both empathy and systemizing tendencies, measured via empathy quotient (EQ) and systemizing quotient (SQ), are present across all individuals but show sex differences and cognitive trade-offs [4]. Intelligence quotient (IQ), while historically studied as a measure of general cognitive ability, also spans a wide range, with individuals with ASD exhibiting intellectual disability, average ability, or exceptional intelligence [9, 10].

These shared features suggest that ASD is not a distinct categorical disorder but rather the extreme end of typical population distributions across cognitive and socio-cognitive dimensions. Understanding the interplay among IQ, EQ, SQ, and AQ in both typically developing (TD) children and those with ASD is essential for linking normative variation with clinical presentation. However, most research has examined these constructs in isolation or in pairs. For example, IQ–AQ studies show autistic traits span the full IQ spectrum, with higher AQ scores linked to both intellectual disability and exceptional ability [9, 10]. EQ–AQ studies consistently find negative associations, as reduced empathy scores correlate with greater autistic traits in clinical and non-clinical groups [11, 12]. SQ–AQ studies indicated stronger systemizing often accompanies higher autistic traits, supporting the view of ASD as an extreme form of typical systemizing tendencies [13, 14]. In contrast, IQ–EQ studies usually reveal weak or inconsistent relationships, suggesting that intelligence alone does not predict empathy, though specific components like verbal comprehension may aid social understanding [15, 16]. Finally, IQ–SQ studies show higher reasoning ability sometimes relates to elevated systemizing, particularly in science, technology, engineering and mathematics oriented populations [17, 18].

While these previous studies are informative, they overlook the fact that psychological dimensions rarely operate independently. Instead, these dimensions interact dynamically and may influence one another through moderation, mediation, compensation, or counterbalancing processes. For instance, intelligence can moderate the relationship between empathizing–systemizing profiles and autistic traits in children with ASD, with higher systemizing either buffering or exacerbating learning challenges depending on cognitive ability [19]. Likewise, empathy-related skills such as emotion perception may mediate the link between verbal intelligence and social adaptation in high-functioning ASD [20]. These findings underscore the need for an integrative framework that captures the interdependencies among IQ, EQ, SQ, and AQ rather than treating them as separate constructs.

Network analysis provides such a framework. By modeling psychological dimensions as nodes and their statistical relationships as edges, it reveals the interaction structure within complex systems [21]. Undirected networks highlight clusters of mutually reinforcing traits, while Bayesian networks further estimate potential directional influences [22, 23]. Yet, the structural dependencies among these dimensions, and how they may differ between ASD and TD populations, remain poorly understood.

This study adopts a network-based approach to map directional associations among IQ, EQ, SQ, and AQ in children with and without ASD. By examining both undirected and Bayesian networks, we aimed to capture patterns of co-variation as well as potential directional influences, offering a more nuanced view of how ASD reflects altered organization of socio-cognitive domains. Clarifying these relationships may also provide a theoretical basis for individualized educational interventions tailored to the cognitive profiles of children with ASD.

Methods

Participants

This study used baseline data from the Guangzhou Longitudinal Study of Children, an ongoing investigation into the developmental trajectories of children aged 6–12 years. Children with ASD were included if they had a prior diagnosis confirmed by both the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) and evaluation by an experienced clinician in a tertiary hospital. Two child psychiatrists further verified each case using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria. In addition, all children with ASD were required to be able to complete the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children–Fourth Edition (WISC-IV) assessment, indicating sufficient language comprehension and task participation abilities for study procedures.

TD children were recruited during the same period from mainstream schools and were matched to the ASD group by age range. TD children were excluded if they had a history of ASD or other neurodevelopmental or psychiatric disorders diagnosed by a psychiatrist, including learning disorders, dyslexia, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or emotional disorders. To further screen for ASD symptoms and intellectual disability, all TD children completed the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) and the WISC-IV. Children with an SRS total score < 60 and a full-scale intelligence quotient (FSIQ) ≥70 were eligible for inclusion. Across both groups, eligibility required being 6–12 years old, voluntary participation by the child and parents, and the absence of conditions that could influence assessment results, such as seizures, head trauma, cerebral palsy, other movement disorders, severe visual or hearing impairments, and known genetic or chromosomal abnormalities. Children with ASD were also excluded if they had clinically diagnosed attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder that could substantially affect cognitive or behavioral assessment. For families with more than one eligible child, only the eldest was included. Finally, 186 children with ASD and 364 TD children were included.

The study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee for Biomedical Research at Sun Yat-sen University and South China Normal University. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants before data collection.

Measures

Demographic information

Parents completed a study-specific questionnaire to provide demographic details. Collected information included the child’s age (in years), gender (male or female), and only-child status (yes or no). Parental characteristics were recorded, including maternal and paternal education level, categorized as lower (primary, secondary, or high school) or higher (university or above). Family socioeconomic status was assessed through monthly per capita household income, classified as <8,000 Renminbi (RMB) or ≥8,000 RMB.

Cognitive ability measurement

Children’s cognitive ability was assessed using the Chinese version of the WISC-IV, suitable for ages 6–16 [24]. The test provides index scores for verbal comprehension index (IQ_VCI), perceptual reasoning index (IQ_PRI), working memory index (IQ_WMI), processing speed index (IQ_PSI), and FSIQ. All assessments were administered by trained professionals in accordance with the standardized manual.

Empathy and systemizing measurement

Empathy and systemizing were measured using the Chinese versions of the Empathy Quotient Children’s Version (EQ-C) and the Systemizing Quotient Children’s Version (SQ-C), both completed by parents [25]. Together, these instruments include 45 items, with 23 items in the EQ-C and 22 items in the SQ-C. The EQ-C evaluates three dimensions of empathy: cognitive empathy (EQ_COG, understanding another person’s thoughts and feelings), social skills (EQ_SOC, ability to navigate social interactions), and affective empathy (EQ_AFF, sharing or responding emotionally to others’ feelings). The SQ-C assesses four domains of systemizing: technical systems (SQ_TEC, interest in structured activities, rules, routines, and mastering specific skills), abstract systems (SQ_ABS, interest in patterns, numbers, and understanding how systems or devices work), organizational systems (SQ_ORG, preference for order, arrangement, and noticing changes in the environment), and collectible systems (SQ_COL, interest in categorizing and collecting related objects, such as animals, vehicles, or cards). The EQ-C total score ranges from 0 to 46 and the SQ-C total score ranges from 0 to 44. Higher scores indicate stronger empathy or systemizing traits.

Autistic trait measurement

Autistic traits were assessed using the Chinese version of the Autism Spectrum Quotient Children’s Version (AQ-C) [26]. The AQ-C is a parent-report questionnaire designed for children aged 4 to 11 years and contains 30 items divided into five subscales, which are socialness (AQ_S), social communication competence (AQ_SCC), imagination (AQ_I), patterns (AQ_P), and attention switching (AQ_AS). Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (definitely agree) to 3 (definitely disagree). The total score ranges from 0 to 90, with higher scores reflecting more pronounced autistic traits.

Undirected network analysis

Undirected networks were estimated and visualized using the R package qgraph [27]. For each group, a Graphical Gaussian Model was estimated, representing each questionnaire subscale as a node and the connections between nodes as partial correlations after controlling for all other variables in the network [28]. Network regularization was performed using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) with Extended Bayesian Information Criterion model selection [29]. A hyperparameter value of γ = 0.25 was selected to balance sparsity and specificity [30]. This approach removed edges that could be better explained by other variables, resulting in a more interpretable network structure.

Four centrality indices were computed to evaluate the relative importance of each node within the regularized network: betweenness, closeness, strength, and expected influence [27]. Higher values on these measures indicate greater influence on the network’s structure and information flow. The accuracy and stability of each network were assessed using the bootnet package. Edge weight accuracy was estimated through a nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 1,000 samples, producing 95% confidence intervals, with narrower intervals indicating greater precision. Network stability was examined using a case-dropping bootstrap approach to calculate the correlation stability coefficient. Values above 0.25, and preferably above 0.50, were considered acceptable indicators of stability. Bootstrapped difference tests (α = 0.05, 1,000 samples) were conducted to assess whether edge weights or centrality indices significantly differed within each network.

To formally compare the ASD and TD networks, we conducted a network comparison test using the R package NetworkComparisonTest (version 2.2.1 [31]). This test evaluated differences in global strength, defined as the sum of the absolute edge weights across all node pairs; network structure invariance, calculated as the maximum difference between the edge weight matrices; and individual edge weights. Group differences were assessed using two-tailed permutation tests with 1,000 iterations, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Directed acyclic graph analysis

A Bayesian network consists of a directed acyclic graph (DAG) combined with a probability distribution, enabling the representation of conditional dependencies among variables [22]. In this approach, the DAG encodes relationships implied by conditional independence and expresses the joint probability distribution through the arrangement of nodes and edges. While causal conclusions cannot be drawn from observational data alone, DAGs can provide informative evidence about possible causal pathways, including both the strength and direction of associations [32]. Their use in psychological research has expanded in recent years [33].

Bayesian networks in this study were estimated using the R package bnlearn, which applies a hill-climbing search algorithm [34]. Network stability was assessed via 1,000 bootstrap resamples, with the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) computed for each [35]. An edge was retained if its direction was consistent in at least 85% of the bootstrap samples [36].

All computations were performed in R version 4.5.0.

Results

Participant characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants are summarized in Table 1. The sample comprised 550 children, including 186 with ASD and 364 TD children. The groups did not differ in age (ASD: 7.90 ± 1.45 years; TD: 7.87 ± 1.68 years; p = 0.823). However, significant differences were observed for gender, parental education, and household income (all p < 0.001). The ASD group included a higher proportion of boys (88.71% vs. 54.40%), and parents of children with ASD were more likely to report lower educational attainment and household income. Only-child status did not differ significantly (p = 0.063).

TD children outperformed children with ASD on all WISC-IV indices (all p < 0.001). Mean FSIQ was 112.62 (SD = 12.90) in the TD group and 89.26 (SD = 18.18) in the ASD group, with consistent differences across IQ_VCI, IQ_PRI, IQ_WMI, and IQ_PSI. Similarly, TD children scored higher on empathy (EQ: 27.18 ± 7.18 vs. 14.40 ± 6.41, p < 0.001) and systemizing (SQ: 19.15 ± 6.01 vs. 13.64 ± 5.32, p < 0.001) than children with ASD, with significant differences across all subscales. By contrast, autistic traits were more pronounced in the ASD group (AQ: 50.23 ± 11.65 vs. 28.60 ± 8.68, p < 0.001), with higher scores across all subscales except AQ_P dimension, which did not differ (p = 0.984).

Discussion

This study used both undirected and Bayesian network analyses to investigate the interplay among intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits in children with and without ASD. Across both approaches, empathy—particularly cognitive empathy—emerged as the most central feature in organizing socio-cognitive networks. In the ASD group, autistic traits clustered tightly together, with relatively limited links to empathy, systemizing, and intelligence, reflecting a more segregated network structure. By contrast, in TD children, autistic traits were more diffusely connected, particularly through the Patterns dimension, which showed stronger links to systemizing. Intelligence formed a cohesive cognitive subnetwork across both groups. However, cross-domain connections were more evident in the ASD network, where social communication competence occupied an upstream position relative to verbal comprehension, whereas intelligence remained largely independent of socio-cognitive domains in the TD network. These findings suggest that while ASD is characterized by a constrained and internally clustered socio-cognitive network, TD children show broader cross-domain integration, especially involving empathy and systemizing, with cognitive ability providing a stable but distinct foundation.

A consistent finding of this study was the central role of empathy, particularly cognitive empathy, across both groups. In both the undirected and Bayesian networks, cognitive empathy emerged as the most influential node, directly connecting with multiple domains and serving as a hub for information flow. This is in line with previous literature identifying empathy as a cornerstone of socio-cognitive development and adaptive functioning in children [37]. However, the pattern of empathy’s integration differed markedly between groups. In children with ASD, cognitive empathy showed limited cross-domain connections. In the directed network, it occupied an upstream position relative to autistic trait dimensions such as imagination and social communication competence. This finding resonates with prior research documenting selective deficits in cognitive empathy in ASD, particularly difficulties in perspective-taking and mentalizing [38, 39]. The narrower integration observed here may reflect a restricted capacity to recruit empathy as a compensatory mechanism in relation to other cognitive or behavioral traits. By contrast, in TD children, cognitive empathy occupied a broader central position. In the directed network, it directly preceded social skills, imagination, abstract systemizing, affective empathy, and social communication competence. This aligns with evidence that empathy facilitates flexible social adaptation and enables the combination of perspective-taking with rule-based reasoning in interpersonal contexts [40, 41]. Neuroimaging research provides one explanation for such inter-group differences that atypical activation and connectivity in brain regions subserving theory of mind and cognitive empathy (e.g., medial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction) in individuals with ASD [42, 43]. These alterations may limit the integration of empathy with other functional domains, contributing to the more circumscribed role we observed in the ASD network. These findings highlight cognitive empathy as a potentially important component of the ASD network and suggest possible implications for intervention. Previous training programs targeting theory of mind and empathy have already shown promise in improving social communication and adaptive outcomes in children with ASD [44, 45]. However, because our data are cross-sectional, the directional paths in the Bayesian network should not be interpreted as causal effects or as evidence of how the network would change after intervention [22]. Thus, while enhancing cognitive empathy may help strengthen specific pathways related to social communication, it remains unclear whether such enhancement would alter the broader network organization or shift the ASD network toward a pattern more closely resembling that observed in TD children. This possibility should be tested in future longitudinal and intervention-based network studies.

Autistic traits also showed divergent network patterns between groups. In children with ASD, these traits formed a tightly interconnected cluster, with the strongest links observed between imagination and social skills, attention switching and patterns, and attention switching and social communication competence. This organization suggests that difficulties in flexibility, imagination, and communication tend to reinforce one another, creating a closed system that restricts compensatory pathways. Such clustering is consistent with prior findings that core features of ASD often co-occur and mutually amplify, contributing to enduring socio-cognitive challenges [46, 47]. In contrast, autistic traits in TD children were less tightly clustered and showed broader cross-domain connections. Patterns were positively linked not only to other autistic trait dimensions but also to systemizing abilities, while attention switching was positively related to collectible systemizing. Unlike the ASD network, this pathway was not contingent on social communication competence, suggesting that repetitive tendencies are more closely tied to systemizing abilities rather than deficits in social cognition in TD children. This more open structure suggests that subclinical autistic traits can be integrated with cognitive styles in ways that support structured reasoning and problem-solving [48, 49]. The prominence of patterns as a hub aligns with accounts of enhanced perceptual and analytic processing, which may be advantageous outside the clinical range [50, 51].

The Bayesian networks further revealed differences in the directional organization of autistic traits. In children with ASD, social communication difficulties predicted limitations in attention switching, which in turn predicted patterns and systemizing tendencies. This organization indicates that restricted and repetitive behaviors may not arise in isolation but rather as downstream consequences of social communication impairments and cognitive inflexibility, consistent with prior findings that these features often co-occur and mutually reinforce one another [52]. Notably, the relationship between attention switching and patterns differed across groups. In the ASD network, attention switching predicted patterns, which is consistent with accounts emphasizing a detail-focused cognitive style in autism, such as weak central coherence and enhanced local perceptual processing [53]. This pathway may also be understood in relation to hyper-systemizing ability, which propose that heightened attention to fine-grained regularities can support rule detection and pattern-based processing in autism [54, 55]. By contrast, patterns predicted attention switching in TD children. This finding should be interpreted more cautiously, as direct evidence for this specific direction is limited. One possible interpretation is that, in typical development, attention to specific details may be more strongly guided by broader pattern-level organization, reflecting a more flexible integration of global and local information. More generally, the group difference is consistent with evidence that autistic and typical development may rely on different routes for integrating local and global information [53, 56]. Taken together, these findings suggest that autistic traits in children with ASD may form a more tightly coupled and potentially self-reinforcing system, whereas in TD children these traits may be more flexibly embedded within broader cognitive processes. This distinction highlights the need to target communication and cognitive flexibility in interventions for ASD, while also recognizing that autistic traits in the general population can serve as adaptive resources, scaffolding strengths in reasoning and analyzing.

Our findings indicate that intelligence formed a cohesive and relatively autonomous cognitive subnetwork in both groups. Verbal comprehension, perceptual reasoning, working memory, and processing speed were strongly interconnected, consistent with the view that intellectual abilities are organized as an integrated but domain-general cognitive system [57, 58]. This pattern also suggests that, although intelligence is often discussed in relation to autistic traits or socio-cognitive functioning, its internal organization may remain partly separable from these domains.

At the same time, the Bayesian networks revealed subtle but important group differences in the organization of intelligence. In children with ASD, social communication competence predicted verbal comprehension, suggesting that communication abilities may be closely positive linked to the development or expression of verbal reasoning [59]. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that language and communication profiles are important sources of cognitive heterogeneity in autism [60, 61]. In the ASD network, verbal comprehension further predicted working memory and perceptual reasoning, indicating that language-related abilities may serve as a key organizing node within the broader cognitive system. By contrast, in TD children, perceptual reasoning occupied a more prominent position, predicting verbal comprehension and working memory, with an indirect pathway to processing speed through working memory. These contrasting pathways suggest that the internal organization of cognitive abilities may differ between ASD and TD children. In ASD, verbal comprehension appeared more closely integrated with other cognitive domains, consistent with evidence that language and communication abilities contribute substantially to cognitive variability within autism [59, 62, 63]. In TD children, perceptual reasoning occupied a more central position, aligning with theories that view nonverbal reasoning as a foundation for broader cognitive development [64–66]. Together, these findings portray intelligence as a relatively stable cognitive core whose internal organization, and especially its connection with social communication, differs across developmental contexts. However, this relative independence should be interpreted cautiously, as intelligence was assessed using a performance-based cognitive measure, whereas empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits were assessed via parent-report questionnaires. Differences in assessment modality may therefore have contributed to the observed separation between cognitive and socio-cognitive domains. Future studies incorporating multiple assessment methods across all domains would help clarify the extent to which the observed network structure reflects substantive psychological relationships rather than measurement-related factors.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about developmental trajectories or causal relations among intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits. Although Bayesian network analysis provides insights into potential directional associations, longitudinal data are required to establish temporal ordering and clarify causal mechanisms. Second, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits were assessed solely through parent-report questionnaires. While these measures have demonstrated reliability and validity in Chinese samples, informant-based assessments are subject to bias arising from parental perception and interpretation of children’s behaviors. Future research would benefit from the inclusion of multiple informants and the use of direct behavioral or experimental tasks to provide a more comprehensive assessment of socio-cognitive functioning. Third, the Graphical LASSO network comparison revealed no significant structural differences between ASD and TD groups. This null result indicates that interpretations of group contrasts should be made cautiously, and future studies with larger samples may help detect subtler differences. Finally, although demographic variables were statistically controlled in the analyses, the ASD and TD groups were not individually matched on gender and FSIQ. In addition, the TD group demonstrated relatively high cognitive performance, with a mean FSIQ above the population average. Therefore, the TD sample may not fully represent the broader population of TD children, which may have influenced the observed group comparisons and network structures. Although retaining all eligible participants increased statistical power, future studies would benefit from recruiting age-, gender-, and FSIQ-matched comparison groups to provide a more rigorous evaluation of similarities and differences between ASD and TD children. Furthermore, although major neurodevelopmental and psychiatric comorbidities were excluded during recruitment, not all potential comorbid conditions were systematically assessed using standardized diagnostic instruments. Other unmeasured factors, such as medication use or differences in educational support, may also have influenced the observed network patterns. Addressing these factors in future studies will help strengthen the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Despite these limitations, the present study provides novel evidence on how intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits are organized within and across groups, offering a foundation for future longitudinal and interventional research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study mapped the organization of intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits in children with and without ASD. Cognitive empathy emerged as the central feature in both undirected and Bayesian networks. Autistic traits and systemizing formed distinct yet interrelated patterns, closely clustered in children with ASD but more flexibly integrated with socio-cognitive processes in TD children. Intelligence constituted a cohesive but relatively independent subnetwork, though it was more directly linked to social communication competence in children with ASD. These findings reveal both shared and distinct patterns of network organization among intelligence, empathy, systemizing, and autistic traits in children with and without ASD. Cognitive empathy emerged as a central feature across groups, while systemizing tendencies and cognitive abilities showed distinct patterns of integration across groups. Together, these findings may help inform future research on socio-cognitive functioning and intervention in ASD.
