Enhancing subharmonic response by controlling initial state of monodisperse microbubbles via a multi-gas core
Authors: Sihan Chen, Chang Lu, Hongyi Zhang, Alfred C.H. Yu, Peng Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The subharmonic response of microbubbles exposed to ultrasound has shown significant promise for nonlinear imaging and non-invasive blood pressure estimation. Elucidating the underlying mechanisms of subharmonic response of microbubbles is critical for advancing subharmonic-based ultrasound applications. Using a flow-focusing microfluidic device, we controllably generated monodisperse microbubbles (MDMBs) with lipid-packed shells and varying CO2 volume fractions (0%, 50%, and 80%) in multi-gas (CO2 and C3F8) cores, while maintaining consistent initial surface tension and shell viscoelasticity across MDMB population. Using the measured pressure-dependent attenuation spectra, pressure-dependent resonance-frequency curves were obtained to characterize the bubble state (i.e., elastic, elastic–buckling transition, buckling). With increasing CO2 volume fraction in the multi-gas core, the initial state of MDMBs after stabilization gradually transitioned from the elastic state to the elastic–buckling transition state. Correspondingly, the surface tension decreased and the shell elasticity increased, demonstrating that regulating the CO2 fraction in the gas core enables controllable tuning of the initial bubble state. Statistical analysis showed that MDMBs closer to buckling state exhibited a significantly lower (∼12% reduction) threshold peak negative pressure for subharmonic generation and a significantly higher (∼11 dB increment) subharmonic amplitude. The initial state approaching buckling state, achieved by increasing the CO2 volume fraction in the gas core of fabricated MDMBs, was therefore identified as the key mechanism governing the enhanced subharmonic response. These findings provide direct evidence supporting nonlinear bubble oscillation theory, elucidate subharmonic generation mechanisms, and establish a reliable method for tailoring MDMB states, thereby enhancing subharmonic responses for ultrasound-based medical applications.

Introduction

Microbubbles with lipid-encapsulated shells and gas-filled cores experience linear and nonlinear oscillations under the excitation of ultrasound waves, which can enhance linear (e.g., fundamental frequency) and nonlinear harmonic components (e.g., harmonics, ultra-harmonics, and subharmonics) in the scattering signals, thereby improving the contrast-to-tissue ratio of ultrasound imaging [1], [2]. Subharmonics at half the driving frequency are far stronger than those from tissue at the same incident pressure, and have received far less attention than the fundamental frequency or higher harmonics through biological tissue [3], [4]. Compared with the second harmonic, the subharmonic provides superior tissue signal suppression and a higher contrast–to–tissue ratio, improving image quality. Thus, using the subharmonic response of bubbles has received increasing interest for enhancing the sensitivity of ultrasound imaging [5], [6]. Moreover, due to the pressure-dependent nonlinear shell viscoelasticity and surface tension of the bubbles, bubble subharmonics have been found to be sensitive to changes in ambient overpressure, which enables cost-effective and non-invasive measurement of the local blood pressure in critical organs (e.g., cardiovascular pressure, portal hypertension, aortic pressure, and tumor interstitial fluid pressure) [7], [8]. To further advance the clinical applications of subharmonic-based imaging and pressure measurement, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms governing subharmonic generation and the development of novel approaches to enhance the subharmonic response are essential. Moreover, from a broader perspective, this also advances the mechanistic interpretation of other nonlinear oscillation behaviors (e.g., harmonics, ultra-harmonics) of bubbles.

Numerical and experimental studies have demonstrated that the occurrence and amplitude of subharmonic responses of bubbles exposed to ultrasound are dependent on multiple factors, including the acoustic excitation parameters (e.g., peak negative pressure (PNP), excitation frequency, pulse length, and pulse shape), the physical properties of microbubbles (e.g., filling gas and shell materials) and the interactions between bubbles and the viscoelasticity of surrounding medium [4], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. In terms of acoustic parameters, with increasing acoustic pressure, the amplitude of microbubble oscillation increases, transitioning from linear to nonlinear oscillation. When bubble oscillation reaches a sufficiently large amplitude, subharmonic components are generated in the scattering signal [15], [16]. Thus, there is a PNP threshold for triggering subharmonic occurrence [10], [17]. The threshold PNP for subharmonic occurrence is dependent on the excitation frequency f, with a larger deviation in f from the resonance frequency corresponding to a higher PNP threshold [10], [18]. Both numerical calculations and experimental measurements determined that an excitation frequency equal to twice the resonance frequency f0 (f/f0 = 2) minimizes the PNP threshold and maximizes the subharmonic amplitude under a fixed PNP [19]. Early numerical calculations also determined that the subharmonic amplitude increased as f/f0 increased within the range of 1.4–1.6, reaching a maximum when f/f0≈1.6, but gradually decreasing at higher f/f0[20]. A longer pulse length can provide more time for bubbles to rung-up, effectively enhancing the efficiency of non-linear oscillation and promoting the subharmonic occurrence [21]. In addition, the subharmonic amplitude is strongly affected by the pulse shape. A two-frequency compound pulse can enhance the subharmonic response, whereas a greater smoothness of the shaped pulses inhibits subharmonic emission [22]. The use of wide-bandwidth excitation, such as a chirp signal, rather than traditional sinusoidal excitation, is also found to enhance the amplitude of the subharmonic response[12]. In terms of the microbubble properties, the subharmonic emission of bubbles with different gas cores exhibits distinct temporal dynamics. This arises because the diffusion coefficient, which varies with gas type, affects the rate of Laplace–pressure–driven dissolution, resulting in different degrees of contraction [11], [23]. Early studies revealed that 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC)-based microbubbles exhibited a higher proportion of subharmonic emission at low excitation pressures (50–100 kPa), indicating a lower threshold for subharmonic generation than that of 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC)-based microbubbles [24]. Additionally, the shell viscoelasticity varies with ambient pressure, which also contributes to the variation in subharmonic emission with ambient pressure [18], [25].

In addition to establishing these empirical relationships between the subharmonic response and the aforementioned factors, the underlying mechanisms of nonlinear responses of bubbles, including harmonics and subharmonics generation, have been extensively investigated. Numerical calculations and experimental studies determined that nonlinear compression-only and expansion-only behaviors of bubble oscillation—characterized by a distinct bias toward compression over expansion amplitude or a distinct bias toward expansion over compression amplitude are always involved in the nonlinear responses of bubbles, particularly in subharmonic generation [26], [27], [28]. According to Marmottant’s theoretical model, depending on the phospholipid surface concentration on the bubble shell, lipid-coated bubbles exhibit three states with distinct shell viscoelasticities and surface tensions: buckling, elastic, and rupture [26]. The buckling state corresponds to a highly compressed bubble with over-saturated lipid concentration and a surface tension approaching 0 mN/m. The rupture state corresponds to a significantly expanded bubble with the lowest lipid surface concentration and a surface tension close to 72 mN/m [26], [29]. In the elastic state, the surface tension of bubbles with the regular lipid arrangement varies linearly with the radius (oscillation amplitude). Because the shell elasticity is described by the change of the effective surface tension with the bubble radius, as the bubble transitions from the elastic regime to the buckling or rupture regimes, there is a rapid change in the elasticity of the bubble shell from a large value (approximately maximum) to a small value (near zero) [16], [30], [31], [32]. Recently characterized surface-area-dependent elasticity curves validated that the shell elasticity decreases in an approximately linear manner with the radius in the elastic regime, but is abruptly reduced to a small value as the bubble reaches the buckling or rupture regimes [16], [31], [33]. Thus, when the initial bubble state is not sufficiently close to either the buckling or rupture regime, excitation at lower PNP results in oscillations whose compression and expansion amplitudes do not exceed the buckling or rupture points [33]. This indicates that microbubble’s oscillation remains entirely within the reversible, linear elastic regime, without the occurrence of nonlinear behavior and thus subharmonic emission. When the PNP reaches a critical threshold, the compression or expansion amplitude of the oscillating bubbles can exceed the buckling or rupture point. This causes an abrupt change in shell elasticity (from a large value to near zero) during the compression or expansion phase, producing asymmetric nonlinear oscillations, which manifest as increased compression amplitude in compression-only behavior and increased expansion amplitude in expansion-only behavior. This explains why subharmonic generation requires a specific acoustic pressure threshold [16], [28], [34]. In addition, when the excitation frequency approaches the resonance frequency, larger oscillation amplitudes can be induced, making it easier to exceed the buckling or rupture point and thereby facilitating nonlinear behavior and subharmonic emission [35]. Furthermore, the sensitivity of the subharmonic response to changes in ambient pressure arises primarily from the pressure-dependent nonlinear shell elasticity of the bubbles[18]. Under lower PNP, increasing the ambient pressure compresses the microbubbles and moves them closer to the buckling regime, thereby facilitating subharmonic generation [4], [9], [36].

Based on the above mechanistic analysis, the onset of subharmonic behavior of bubbles is primarily determined by the initial phospholipid surface concentration on the bubble shell, which relates to the initial state of the bubble (i.e., the proximity to the buckling or rupture point) [32], [37]. However, the conclusions from the above studies are largely derived from numerical simulations and indirect experimental measurements, and lack direct experimental validation. Particularly, the microbubbles used in these studies are almost exclusively polydisperse populations. As a result, bubbles within a population possess different lipid packing densities, leading to variations in shell viscoelasticity and surface tension and therefore different bubble states [37], [38], [39]. Thus, experimental validation of the above theoretical conclusions when employing polydisperse microbubbles is less convincing. Recently, significant progress has been made in monodisperse microbubble (MDMB) fabrication using microfluidics [2], [40], [41]. First, their narrow size distribution maximizes attenuation at a specific frequency, allowing accurate resonance frequency identification and precise bubble state determination. Importantly, by precisely controlling the pressure of the dispersed phase and the velocity of the continuous phase, the shell of every bubble in the MDMB population has approximately the same lipid packing density, ensuring that all bubbles are in the same state [40], [42]. Second, recent studies have proposed regulating the ambient pressure to achieve different surface dilation states (corresponding to different bubble states) of MDMBs, and then using pressure-dependent acoustic attenuation spectrum to characterize the state (i.e., buckling, elastic, and rupture) of MDMB populations [31], [32]. These new approaches provide the possibility for directly validating the relationship between the bubble state and the occurrence of bubble nonlinear responses. Importantly, it has been reported that other factors may influence the initial state of the harvested bubbles in the collection vial after stabilization. Such factors include the initial lipid packing density of the shell of stabilized MDMBs, the ratio of the pre-filled gas volume to the harvested MDMB solution volume, and the bubble concentration [31], [32]. Therefore, tuning these parameters may enable control over the initial bubble state, facilitate experimental elucidation of subharmonic mechanisms, and achieve controllable subharmonic generation in applications.

In this study, to achieve stabilized MDMBs with different initial states by leveraging the distinct solubilities of CO2 and C3F8, we used a flow-focusing microfluidic device to generate monodisperse microbubbles with varying CO2 volume fractions in multi-gas cores. Based on the pressure-dependent acoustic attenuation spectra, the initial states of different types of MDMBs were characterized to determine how bubble state changes with variations in the CO2 volume fraction in the multi-gas core. The corresponding surface tension and shell elasticity were statistically compared to determine the effectiveness of CO2 volume fraction in the gas core for tuning bubble states. Finally, using the measured subharmonic responses at the optimal excitation frequency, the threshold PNP and subharmonic amplitude across different MDMB states were statically compared to provide direct experiment validation for the mechanisms of subharmonic generation.

Materials and Methods

Generation of MDMBs with a multi-gas core

Fabrication of liposome suspension

Primary lipid DPPC (Lipoid, Germany) and lipid emulsifier 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DPPE-PEG2K) (Lipoid, Germany) were mixed at a molar ratio of 9:1 and dissolved in a solution of chloroform and methanol at a 2:1 vol ratio. A rotavapor (ZX98-1, Shanghai Luyi Industry & Trade Co., Ltd, China) operating at a fixed rotation rate was then used to evaporate the mixed phospholipid solution at 85 °C for 2 h, forming a thin film of lipid mixture. After redissolution in phosphate-buffered saline solution, the film was hydrated by ultrasonication at 60 °C for 1 h to obtain a clear liposome suspension.

Generation of monodisperse microbubbles

Fig. 1A illustrates the experimental setup for the controllable generation of MDMBs. MDMBs were fabricated in a flow-focusing microfluidic chip with three channels: two inlets for the inflow of the continuous and dispersed phases, and one outlet for the outflow of the newly formed MDMBs. During MDMB formation, the liposome solution of the continuous phase was driven by the C3F8 gas (Xingdao Technology Co., Ltd., China), the pressure of which was controlled by a pressure controller (Pressure Controller Light, Shanghai Pengzan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Its flow rate was maintained at 12.5–14.5 μL/min using a flow-sensor-based feedback controller (FSR3089, Shanghai Pengzan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). A pair of mass flow controllers (LFIX-2000-X, Sichuan Laifeng Fluid Equipment Mfg. Co., Ltd., China) was used to regulate the volume fraction (0%, 50%, and 80%) of CO2 (Shanghai Shenzhong Gas Co., Ltd., China) in the mixed C3F8 and CO2 gases of the dispersed phase. The pressure of the dispersed phase was controlled at 530–650 mbar using another pressure controller. Thus, the formed bubbles were filled with a multi-gas core of different volume fractions of C3F8 and CO2. The production rate of MDMBs generated was ∼ 90,000 bubbles/s. Fig. 1B shows an image of the newly formed MDMBs in the outlet channel of the microfluidics. The newly formed bubbles were collected in a 2-mL vial pre-filled with pure C3F8. Two vent needles were pre-inserted into the vial mouth and the vial bottom for bubble collection and to allow equilibration between the internal and external pressure, respectively. Calculation of the size distribution and PDI

The freshly harvested MDMBs were stabilized at 4 °C for 2 days. Then, 10 μL of MDMB suspension was gently transferred to a sealed container with a height of 170 μm. Images of the microbubbles were captured using a 40 × microscope objective (Fig. 1C1) and then binarized (Fig. 1C2). Thus, the area of each bubble was calculated from the total area of pixels (the area of a pixel was pre-calibrated as 0.33 × 0.33 µm2 using standard microspheres), thereby obtaining the microbubble radius (R). Approximately 1000 microbubbles in five microscopic images were imaged to calculate the bubble size distribution. Finally, the polydispersity index (PDI) was calculated as ∑(R-R¯)2/n/R¯, where R¯ and n are the mean radius and the number of the counted microbubbles, respectively [43], [44].

Measurement of pressure-dependent radius of MDMBs

To measure the radius of MDMBs under different ambient pressures, after a 2-day stabilization of the freshly collected MDMBs, 10 μL of bubble suspension was pipetted into a customized microscopic observation chamber with a 170-μm height and a 9-mm width. Two inlet ports on the top of the container were connected to a syringe pump (QHZS-001A1, Jiaozuo Yanhang Power Technology Co., Ltd., China) and a pressure gauge (MIK-Y290, Hangzhou Asmik Sensors Technology Co., Ltd., China) through a T-shaped tube (Fig. 2A). Before measurement, the inlet ports allowed the MDMB suspension to be pipetted into the chamber. During measurement, by reducing the air volume in the tube with a syringe pump, the ambient pressure within the container was continuously increased from 100 to ∼ 140 kPa over a 10-s pressurization period. Measurement of pressure-dependent acoustic attenuation spectrum

Regulation of ambient pressure

A customized container (20 × 20 × 10 mm3) was used to measure the acoustic attenuation of MDMBs under different ambient pressures. Its two sides were composed of acoustically transparent thin membranes with 1-mm thickness. Two inlet ports on the top of the container were connected to a syringe pump and a pressure gauge through a T-shaped tube. Before measurement, the inlet ports allowed the MDMB suspension to be pipetted into the pre-filled degassed saline solution in the container for dilution. During measurement, by reducing the air volume in the tube with a syringe pump, the ambient pressure within the container was continuously increased from 100 to ∼ 140 kPa over a 10-s period (Fig. 2B). A magnetic stirrer was placed on the bottom of the container to agitate the MDMB suspension, ensuring spatial uniformity of bubbles during measurement.

Transmission, receiving, and sampling of acoustic signals

A 5-MHz focused transducer (V309-SU, Olympus Corporation, USA) with a 50-mm focal length and a 13-mm diameter was used as the transmitting transducer. The excitation pulse sequence, which consisted of 35 pulses with frequencies varying from 1.1 to 4.5 MHz in steps of 0.1 MHz, a repetition frequency of 5 kHz, and a pulse length (PL) of 12 cycles, was generated by a D/A converter of the FPGA-controlled NI5781 module. The pulse sequence was then amplified by an RF power amplifier (E&I 16 2100 L, Electronics & Innovation Ltd., USA) with a 50-dB gain to drive the transmitting transducer. Before measurements, the pulse amplitude under each excitation frequency was calibrated to achieve a consistent PNP of 4.2 kPa in the focal region. Another 5-MHz planar transducer (Advanced Devices, Wakefield, MA) was spatially aligned with the transmitting transducer at a distance of 10 cm to receive the acoustic attenuation signals from the MDMBs. The received signals were first amplified by a pulser-receiver (5077PR, Olympus Corporation, USA) with 10-dB gain, and then digitized at a 100-MHz sampling rate by a A/D converter of the FPGA-controlled NI5781 before being transferred to the host computer. The rising edge of a square wave generated by the FPGA-controlled NI5781 was used to synchronously trigger the excitation pulse sequence, the signal sampling, and the syringe pump. Thus, over the 10-s pressurization period, 100 sweep excitation pulse sequences, corresponding to 100 different ambient pressures, were emitted, and the resulting 100 acoustic attenuation spectra were measured.

Calculation of the acoustic attenuation spectrum

Each sample of each type of MDMB was measured three times. After each measurement, the measuring container was flushed with degassed saline to eliminate residual bubbles. The measured attenuation coefficient, αexp, at a given excitation frequency was calculated as [31], [32]:(1)αexpf=10dlog10Psalf2Pbubf2where Psalf2 and Pbubf2 are the power spectrum amplitude from the control saline and MDMB solution, respectively, and d (=10 mm) is the acoustic path length through the bubbles.

Calculation of the shell elasticity and surface tension of MDMBs

The shell elasticity and surface tension of MDMBs can be obtained by fitting the theoretical pressure-dependent attenuation spectra, αtheory, to the experimentally measured attenuation spectra, αexp. αtheory can be expressed as [26], [45](2)αtheory=10ln(10)∑Rδtotδradn (R)Ωscsdwhere δtot and δrad are the total damping and radiation damping coefficients of a microbubble respectively, and n(R) represents the bubble-radius-dependent bubble concentration, which can be derived from the measured size distribution of MDMBs.

The scattering cross-section, Ωscs, can be expressed as [26], [45](4)Ωscs=4πR02((ω0/ω)2-1)2+δtot2where ω is the angular transmit frequency, and ω0 is the angular eigenfrequency of a single bubble, which is given by [26], [45](5)ω0=1ρLR023κP0+(3κ-1)2σ(R0)R0+4χR0R0 accounts for the initial bubble radius. ρL, κ, and P0 are the liquid density, the polytropic exponent of the filling gas, and the ambient pressure, respectively. χ represents the shell elasticity, and σ is the surface tension.

Thus, based on the measured and theoretical pressure-dependent attenuation spectra and the measured pressure-dependent size distribution of bubbles, the surface-area-dependent shell elasticity and surface tension could be obtained after multiple rounds of fitting [30], [31], [32]. The detailed calculations are presented as follows. At each ambient pressure in the elastic regime, the surface tension σ was set to an original value in the first fitting round. The values of χ and κs at each ambient pressure were varied until the root mean square error of αtheory and αexp reached minimum values. Then, according to the measured pressure-dependent radius of bubbles, χ were obtained as functions of the bubble surface area. The surface tension was then calculated by(6)σ(A) =∫0Aχ(A)AdAwhere A is the surface area of the bubble. σ(A) was set as the previous value in the next run. This procedure was repeated until the maximum difference between the set original value and final value of σ was less than 5 × 10−7. At this point, the accurate true value of σ was derived, and the fitting procedure was repeated once more to obtain the true values of χ. Finally, the surface-area-dependent elasticity and surface tension were determined.

Measurement of the subharmonic response

Transmission, receiving, and sampling of scattering signals

A customized container for subharmonic measurement (20 × 20 × 20 mm3) was placed in a water tank filled with degassed water, as shown in Fig. 3. Its four sides were composed of acoustically transparent thin membranes with 1-mm thickness. After stabilization for 2 days, the MDMBs were pipetted into the pre-filled degassed saline in the container through an inlet port on the top of the container. A magnetic stirrer was placed on the bottom of the container to agitate the MDMB suspension during measurements. To eliminate interfering reflections, an acoustic absorber (UA-1, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) was placed on one side of the water tank.Fig. 3Schematic representation of the experimental setup for subharmonic response measurement of monodisperse microbubbles.

Schematic representation of the experimental setup for subharmonic response measurement of monodisperse microbubbles.

A 3.5-MHz focused transducer (V382-SU, Olympus Corporation, USA) with a focal length of 50 mm and a diameter of 15 mm was used to emit the acoustic signals. It was driven by a pulse sequence signal, consisting of 50 pulses with a 30-cycle PL, a 10-Hz PRF, and the desired frequency and amplitude, which was generated by the D/A converter of the FPGA-controlled NI5781 module and amplified by a 50-dB RF amplifier. The relationships between the amplitudes of the excitation signal and the PNPs at different excitation frequencies (2–4 MHz frequency bandwidth at 0.2-MHz intervals) were pre-calibrated using a needle hydrophone. The scattered signals of the MDMBs were first received by another 5-MHz planar transducer (V309-SU, Olympus Corporation, USA) with a 15-mm diameter, positioned perpendicular to the transmitting transducer. The received signals were amplified by a pulse–receiver with 20-dB gain, digitized by a A/D converter with a 100-MHz sampling rate, and finally transferred to the computer. For each MDMB type, measurements were performed in triplicate under fixed acoustic parameters. After each measurement, the measuring container was flushed with degassed saline to remove residual bubbles.

Calculation of the subharmonic amplitude

The sampled time-domain signals were first processed via fast Fourier transform to obtain the frequency spectrum Vi(k) (wherei = 1, 2, …, 50 denotes the i-th sampling period and k is the k-th discrete frequency point). The power spectrum was then derived as Pik=Vik2. To isolate the subharmonic signal, the cumulative power was summed across all frequency points within a 0.1-MHz bandwidth centered on the subharmonic frequency fsub=f/2 (where f is the excitation frequency). The subharmonic amplitude, Ai, for the i-th sampling period was calculated as:(7)Ai=∑jPijwhere j indexes the corresponding frequency points within the aforementioned subharmonic bandwidth. Finally, the reported subharmonic amplitude, Asub, was obtained by [44](8)Asub=10log10Abub¯Asal¯where Asal¯ and Abub¯ are the mean subharmonic amplitudes measured in saline solution and MDMB suspension, respectively, averaged over 50 sampling periods under consistent acoustic excitation parameters.

According to previous conclusions, the state of MDMBs can be characterized using the pressure-dependent resonance frequency curve. As shown in Fig. 5B1–B3, three cakes (orange, red, green) marked the initial resonance frequency (corresponding to the initial state after stabilization for 2 days), the maximum, and the minimum resonance frequencies. With increasing ambient pressure, three dynamic regimes could be identified, a slowly rise between the initial and maximum resonance frequencies, a steep decrease between the maximum and minimum resonance frequencies, and a sharp increase after minimal resonance frequency. Based on the results in Fig. 6A and 6C, as the bubble size gradually decreased to the point at which rapid reduction of bubble size began (with slowly declining shell elasticity), resonance frequency reached a maximum, signifying that bubble entered the mechanically condensed state (red cake). As shell elasticity dropped to its minimum, resonance frequency reached a minimum, signifying that bubble entered the buckling state (green cake). Thus, three distinct regimes—the elastic regime (between the initial and maximal resonance frequencies), the elastic–buckling transition regime (between the maximum and minimum resonance frequencies), and the buckling regime (below the minimum resonance frequency) were identified. Thus, for MDMBs with 0% CO2 in their core, the initial state lay within the elastic regime. Starting from this point, a large overpressure (31.5 kPa) was required to drive MDMB transition to the final mechanically condensed state (elastic–buckling transition regime), and subsequently reach the buckling state (buckling regime). However, for MDMBs with 50% and 80% CO2 in their gas cores, their initial states were already close to, or had almost reached, the final mechanically condensed state (elastic–buckling transition regime). Tuned surface tension and shell elasticity of MDMBs with increasing CO2 volume fraction

Fig. 6A1–A3 shows the typical pressure-dependent normalized radius curves of different types of MDMBs during pressurization, respectively. According to previous reports, the point of slope abrupt change on the curve was characterized as the bubbles entering the buckling state, and the relative ambient pressure corresponding to this inflection point was defined as the buckling pressure. As shown in Fig. 6A1–A3, the obtained bucking pressures (27.3, 17.9, and 12.2 kPa) of MDMBs with 0%, 50%, and 80% CO2 in the pressure-dependent radius were approximately the same as those obtained from the pressure-dependent resonance frequency curves, showing a good consistence. Furthermore, for each MDMB type with distinct CO2 volume fractions, three vials of MDMBs were independently fabricated, and three samples were taken from each vial for measurements. Thus, a total of nine measurements were performed for each type of MDMB.

Marmottant’s linearized model is applied to calculate shell elasticity and surface tension under small-amplitude oscillation of bubble, the determined values (in the region from the initial state to the buckling state) are presented in Fig. 6B–C. Fig. 6B shows the surface tension of MDMBs with 0%, 50%, and 80% CO2 multi-gas cores as a function of the bubble relative surface area, A/A0, respectively. Similar change could be observed in these curves. Upon bubble compression, the surface tension gradually decreased to approximately zero with decreasing surface area. The initial surface tension (σ(A0)) at atmospheric pressure, decreased with increasing CO2 volume fraction. Fig. 6C depicts the surface-area-dependent shell elasticity of MDMBs with 0%, 50%, and 80% CO2, respectively. At 0% CO2, the shell elasticity of the compressed bubble initially increased, followed by a sharp decline. In contrast, at 80% CO2, the elasticity decreased monotonically with the decreasing relative surface area. These results show that the MDMBs with different CO2 volume fractions exhibit different initial surface tension and shell elasticity, corresponding to the different states in the pressure-dependent resonance frequency curves in Fig. 5B1–B3.

For different types of MDMBs, their respective initial area after 2-day stabilization was normalized to quantify the surface-area-dependent surface tension–elasticity curve. However, because the initial surface area differs among different types of MDMBs, their evolutionary trajectories (driven by overpressure) cannot be aligned on a common axis to infer the initial state of each type. Here, to further explain how the state of MDMBs with different CO2 volume fractions transitions, the surface area of buckling state was used to normalize the surface areas of all types of MDMBs during pressurization (the same flow rate of continuous phase and pressure of dispersed phase were used for the fabrication of different types of MDMB, resulting in the same lipid packing density on the shell in the freshly formed MDMBs. Therefore, the surface area of buckling state of different types of MDMBs was the same). The modified surface-area-dependent surface tension–elasticity curves are shown in Fig. 6D, E. The initial surface tensions and shell elasticities of different types of MDMBs resided at distinct positions on the evolutionary trajectories of surface tension or elasticity, as shown in Fig. 6D, E. With increasing CO2 volume fractions, both the initial surface tension and shell elasticity gradually approached the values at buckling state, showing that the initial state of MDMBs gradually transitioned to the buckling state with increasing CO2 volume fraction in multi-gas core. These results further show that regulating the volume fraction of CO2 in multi-gas core enabled the bubbles to achieve distinct initial states at atmospheric pressure, and the higher the CO2 volume fractions, the closer the initial state of MDMBs is to the buckling state.

The initial surface tension and shell elasticity of three MDMB types at atmospheric pressure are statistically compared in Fig. 6F, G, respectively. The initial surface tension significantly decreased with increasing CO2 (29.8, 19.9, and 13.5 mN/m for 0%, 50%, and 80% CO2, respectively), further confirming that this property could be effectively controlled by tuning the composition of the gas core. The initial shell elasticity (∼0.39 N/m) under 50% CO2 was significantly higher than that under 0% and 80% CO2 (∼0.33 and ∼ 0.36 N/m). This difference is consistent with the conclusions drawn based on the result in Fig. 5B, in which MDMBs with 50% CO2 had approximately reached the final mechanically condensed state, whereas those with 80% CO2 had entered the elastic–buckling transition regime and those with 0% CO2 were still in the elastic regime.

Discussion

Determination of the MDMB concentration and stirring duration for attenuation spectrum measurement

MDMBs of each type after stabilization for 2 days were added into the measuring container for acoustic attenuation spectrum measurement. According to previous reports, after entering a new environment, the stabilized MDMBs undergo further natural evolution driven by Laplace pressure as a result of changes in ambient conditions (i.e., from the collection vial to the measurement container), leading to a change of state [31], [46], [47]. Moreover, under the fixed environment of the measuring container, the final re-stabilized state of MDMBs is mainly dependent on the concentration of the suspension [31], [32]. To accurately compare the initial states of different MDMB types, it is crucial to ensure that each type of MDMB undergoes the same degree of Laplace-pressure-driven evolution in the measuring container. Thus, the diluted concentration of different types of MDMB in the measuring container must remain constant. Here, because the attenuation is approximately positively correlated with the bubble concentration [48], a maximum attenuation of ∼ 30 ± 5 dB/cm in the measured attenuation spectra at atmosphere pressure was used as a standard to regulate the volume of MDMBs pipetted from the collection vial. Typically, for each sample of each type of MDMB, ∼2 μL of MDMBs was pipetted into the measuring container to achieve ∼ 6 × 1010 m−3 concentration. Additionally, to ensure consistency between the initial states of MDMBs in the attenuation spectra and subharmonic measurements, the MDMB concentration must be the same in both measurement containers. Noted that the lower concentration n (∼ 6 × 1010 m−3) for all types of MDMBs was used, and the corresponding scattering cross-section Ωscs was ∼ 7 × 10-10 m2. Consequently, the product n2/3Ωscs＜0.02, which is significantly less than 1, confirming that higher order multiple scattering effects can be negligible [30]. This could effectively avoid multiple scattering from bubble-to-bubble interactions in the experiments, allowing the measured attenuation spectrum to approximate a linear sum of energy loss from individual scatterer and ensuring good consistency between the theoretical model and the experiments.

To determine the time required for MDMBs in the measuring container to reach a new stable state through Laplace-pressure-driven dissolution, the acoustic attenuation spectra were measured under atmospheric pressure and different overpressures for different stirring times (10, 20, 40, and 60 s) [31], [32]. As shown in Fig. 8, under atmospheric pressure, the resonance frequency of MDMBs shifted from 1.80 to 1.86 MHz when the stirring times was increased from 10 to 20 s, and subsequently remained an approximately constant for stirring times of 40 and 60 s. Moreover, under overpressure, the buckling pressure was 28 kPa after a stirring time of 10 s, and then reduced to a constant value of 20 kPa after 20, 40, and 60 s. Thus, stirring for 20 s ensures the re-stabilization of MDMBs in the measuring container, allowing accurate comparison of the initial state of MDMBs with different CO2 volume fractions in their cores.Fig. 8Effect of stirring duration on monodisperse microbubble (MDMB) states. Determination of the excitation frequency for subharmonics measurement

To investigate the effect of bubble state on the subharmonic threshold pressure, careful selection of acoustic excitation parameters is crucial. First, variations in excitation frequency give rise to different subharmonic amplitudes, which would confound analysis of the effects of bubble state on subharmonic response. Thus, an excitation frequency that drove all bubble types to their maximum amplitude was considered. Previous work determined that the amplitude of the subharmonic response of MDMBs is theoretically dependent on the ratio of the excitation frequency to the microbubble resonance frequency (f/f0), peaking when the excitation frequency is 1.6–2.0 times the resonance frequency [49]. The theoretical resonance frequency of the MDMBs used in this study is approximately 2.10 MHz (for MDMBs with mean radius of ∼ 2.31–2.50 µm and PDI of below 9%). However, we note that the PNP range used in the subharmonic measurements was generally above the 50-kPa threshold. Previous studies found that the resonance frequency decreases with increasing PNP, eventually reaching a constant value [44], [48], [50]. The reasons for this are as follows. Under lower PNP, bubble oscillation occurs in an elastic–dominated regime, where the oscillation amplitude is governed by shell elasticity. In contrast, under higher PNP, bubble oscillation enters an elasticity–free regime, where the oscillation amplitude is determined primarily by the bubble size rather than the shell properties [48], [51]. Thus, during subharmonic measurements, to determine the resonance frequency under the higher excitation PNP, the attenuation spectra of MDMBs with different volume fractions of CO2 were measured under different PNPs. As shown in Fig. 9A1, the resonance frequency decreased from ∼ 1.8 to ∼ 1.2 MHz when the PNP was increased from 4.2 to 60 kPa, and did not change at PNPs above 60 kPa (60–120 kPa). Triplicate experiments in Fig. 9A2 shows that the resonance frequency was almost constant under PNPs above 40 kPa. Thus, to ensure consistent excitation frequency conditions for each vial of MDMBs, the resonance frequency (fMR) at a PNP of 80 kPa was first characterized, and then an excitation frequency of f = 2 × fMR was selected for the subsequent subharmonic measurements (Note that 2.0 times the resonance frequency was chosen as the excitation frequency for experiments, though other multipliers within the 1.6–2.0 range (e.g., 1.6, 1.7, 1.8, 1.9) could also be used). Fig. 9B1–B3 shows the acoustic attenuation spectra of representative samples of each MDMB type excited at lower and higher PNPs (4.2 kPa and 80 kPa). At the higher PNP, fMR shifted from ∼ 1.7 to ∼ 1.1 MHz, ∼1.8 to ∼ 1.2 MHz, and ∼ 2.1 to ∼ 1.3 MHz for the representative samples of three types of MDMBs, respectively. Thus, the final optimized excitation frequencies for the subharmonic measurements therefore were 2.2, 2.4, and 2.6 MHz for MDMBs with CO2 volume fractions of 0%, 50% and 80%, respectively.Fig. 9Effect of excitation pressure on the acoustic attenuation of monodisperse microbubble (MDMB). Mechanism of tuning the initial state of MDMBs for enhancing the subharmonic response

We demonstrated that the composition of gases in the core influenced the degree of shrinkage and the initial state of microbubbles. Specifically, with increasing volume fraction of CO2 from 0% to 80%, the average radius of MDMBs after stabilization for 2 days decreased from 2.50 to 2.31 μm, corresponding to a decrease in surface tension from 30 to 13 mN/m. This is consistent with the previous reports [37], [52]. The equilibrium spreading pressure of freshly formed MDMBs is known to be less than 10 mN/m (interfacial tension > 60 mN/m), resulting in unavoidable Laplace-pressure-driven dissolution [30], [53]. The gas inside the MDMBs gradually diffuses into the surrounding aqueous environment, thereby mechanically compressing the lipid monolayer. Because the number of lipid molecules at the bubble surface remains constant, the lipid density on the shell gradually increases with a decrease in the bubble size [30], [53]. Theoretically, when the lipid monolayer reaches final mechanically condensed state with a (near) tensionless interface, the microbubble attains its final stable state with unchanged configuration [54], [55]. However, recent studies have reported that it is difficult for MDMBs to reach this state in a collection vial, with an intermediate state being observed even after stabilization for 2 days [31], [32]. Several factors—including the initial lipid packing density of the shell, the type of gas and its solubility, the ratio of pre-filled gas volume to the harvested MDMBs solution volume, and the bubble concentration—determine the extent to which the microbubble’s final state deviates from the fully mechanically condensed state.

In our studies, because both the dispersed-phase pressure and continuous-phase flow rate remained approximately constant for the fabrication of different MDMB type, the total number of lipid molecules adsorbed at the gas–liquid interface remained essentially constant across MDMBs with different CO2 volume fractions [42], [56]. When reaching the final mechanically condensed state, all MDMBs attained the same lipid packing density and size. However, the aqueous solubility of CO2 (∼0.8 mL/mL in water at 25 °C, 1 atm) is approximately 242 times higher than that of C3F8 (∼3.3 μL/mL in water at 25 °C, 1 atm) [37], [52]. During Laplace-pressure-driven dissolution, the CO2 inside the bubbles continually diffused into the surrounding solution and rapidly dissolved—remaining far from saturation—until fully depleted, resulting in compressed bubbles composed solely of C3F8 [37], [57]. The content of CO2 dominated the degree of bubble compression. With the increase in the volume fraction of CO2 in the core, more CO2 diffused into the solution, followed by fast dissolution, causing greater shell contraction and a smaller stabilized microbubble size. This corresponds to a state in which the microbubble approaches the mechanically condensed state more closely after 2 days of stabilization [31], [32], [37], [52], [57]. Our results also show that with increasing CO2 volume fraction from 0% to 50% and 80%, the initial state of the MDMBs transitions from the elastic state toward—and essentially reaches—the final mechanically condensed state, resulting in a decrease in the surface tension.

We note that the different types of MDMBs, after stabilization for 2 days, were directly transferred into the container and stirred for the same duration prior to subharmonic measurements. This helped ensure that all MDMB types underwent Laplace pressure-driven dissolution to an equivalent extent, thereby guaranteeing the accuracy of the measured relationship between bubble initial state and the subharmonic response. Our results show the dependence of the generation and amplitude of the subharmonic response on the initial state of MDMBs, and MDMBs approaching buckling state can effectively reduce the threshold PNP of subharmonic generation and enhance the subharmonic amplitude. This conclusion is consistent with previous theoretical numerical calculations, which determined that compression‑only behavior (i.e., subharmonic generation) occurs when the bubble radius approaches the buckling radius, and concluded that the change in effective surface tension is of fundamental importance for predicting bubble subharmonic behavior at low driving pressure amplitudes [4], [9], [16]. Moreover, via the measured size distribution, surface-area-dependent elasticity and surface-area-dependent surface tension of MDMBs with different CO2 volume fractions, we used Marmottant model to calculate the subharmonic response of different MDMB types and their relative oscillation amplitude under the excitation pulse (The calculation method is provided in the Supplementary Materials). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1B, with increasing CO2 volume fraction, the subharmonic amplitude gradually increased under the fixed PNP. Moreover, the decrease in expansion-to-compression ratio (E/C) with increasing CO2 volume fraction showed an intensification of compression-only behavior of bubbles (Supplementary Fig. 1A). These theoretical calculations are in good agreement with our experimental results. Thus, these experimental findings not only validate the accuracy and rationality of the Marmottant model for analyzing bubble nonlinear oscillations, but also demonstrate that using MDMBs with controllable initial state can provide reliable experimental data for revealing the mechanism of nonlinear responses of bubbles.

Further, we summarize the mechanism of subharmonic generation as follows. For different types of microbubbles with decreasing surface tension—i.e., whose initial states are progressively closer to the buckling state—as long as their initial states remain far from the buckling state, they will hardly cross the buckling state during the compression phase under low acoustic pressure excitation. Consequently, they will remain in the elastic regime and undergo linear oscillations with equal expansion and contraction amplitudes [50]. However, if their initial states approach buckling state, under a lower PNP excitation, bubble compression during oscillation more readily induced a transition from the elastic regime to the buckling regime, resulting in an abrupt change in shell elasticity and larger compression amplitude (i.e., compression-only behavior), causing subharmonic generation of MDMBs. Thus, on the one hand, the closer the initial state of a bubble is to the buckling state, the lower PNP required for the oscillating bubble to cross the buckling state during the compression phase, thereby making subharmonic generation more likely. On the other hand, for a given PNP, the closer the initial state of a bubble is to the buckling state, the greater the extent to which the oscillating bubble crosses the buckling state during the compression phase, and the more pronounced the abrupt change in shell elasticity, producing stronger subharmonics.

Potential bubble-based ultrasound medical applications

In addition to the bubble size, the shell viscoelasticity and surface tension contribute to the bubble’s acoustic response [58]. After formation, bubbles undergo Laplace pressure-driven dissolution, during which their state continuously evolves with the change of the shell viscoelasticity and surface tension [46]. Thus, the state of the bubble dictates its acoustic responsiveness, which, in turn, governs the efficiency of ultrasound imaging and therapy. In this study, we proposed a novel approach to control the state of MDMBs through tuning the volume fraction of CO2 in their multi-gas core. A higher volume faction of CO2 caused the MDMBs to approach the buckling state, thereby reducing the surface tension. This effectively lowered the PNP threshold for subharmonic generation and enhanced the amplitude of the subharmonic response. Such strategy can improve the contrast-to-tissue ratio in subharmonic-based ultrasound imaging and increase the sensitivity of subharmonic signals to changes in ambient pressure for noninvasive blood pressure estimation. Importantly, this approach represents a significant step toward tailoring next-generation microbubbles with programmable acoustic responses, enabling their optimization for ultrasound-based medical applications. For example, bubbles with lower buckling pressure—while remaining in the elastic regime far from the elastic–buckling transition regime—have higher stability, making them suitable for tracking microbubble trajectories within the vasculature, thereby enhancing the accuracy of ultrasound super–resolution imaging [32], [59]. Bubbles approaching the rupture regime can enhance the nonlinear response of expansion-only behavior, offering opportunities for the development of bubble-based ultrasound nonlinear imaging [31]. These promising applications will be validated in future research.

The limitations of the study

First, all the shell elasticity, viscosity and surface tension are nonlinear dependent on the surface-area of bubbles. At each ambient pressure, the theoretical attenuation spectrum was fitted to the measured attenuation spectrum to determine the shell viscoelasticity and surface tension. According to linearized theoretical model, only the surface area within elastic regime, where it varies linearly with ambient pressure, can be employed to derive both the shell viscoelasticity and the surface tension. Beyond the elastic regime, e.g., in the elastic-buckling and buckling regimes, the surface-area dependent shell viscoelasticity and surface tension couldn’t be derived. Thus, in the theoretical calculation, beyond elastic regime, the shell elasticity can be set to zero as it becomes very small, but appropriate values for the shell viscosity are lacking. This would not only introduce errors between theoretical calculations and experiments, but also preclude a detailed analysis of how changes in shell elasticity, viscosity, and surface tension individually contribute to the subharmonic response. Second, beside compression-only behavior of bubbles, expansion-only behavior of bubbles is considered as another potential mechanism for the nonlinear response (i.e., harmonics, ultra-harmonics and subharmonics generation). In our experiments, adding volume fractions of CO2 increased the bubble compression during 2-day stabilization period, bringing the bubbles closer to the buckling state and thereby facilitating the elucidation of the contribution of compression-only behavior to subharmonic generation. However, the pressure-dependent surface tension curves show that even bubbles with 0% CO2 exhibit a surface tension far lower than 72 mN/m, indicating that their initial state was far from the rupture state. Therefore, this strategy for regulating bubble state is not suitable for investigating expansion-only behavior of bubbles. Previous reports analyzed that the initial state of the stabilized bubble in the collection vial is dependent on many factors, including the lipid shell packing density of the initially formed bubbles, the pre-filled gas pressure in the collection vial, the ratio of pre-filled gas volume to the harvested MDMB volume in the collection vial, and the concentration and the stabilized time of MDMBs, and so on. Hence, in future studies, developing strategies to regulate the initial bubble state toward rupture will aid in elucidating expansion–only behavior of bubbles and its contribution to nonlinear responses. Third, from the point of ultrasound-induced bubble nonlinear oscillations, harmonics and ultra-harmonics are involved in the bubble nonlinear behaviors. Besides subharmonics, future studies require extending the present framework to address the generation of other nonlinear responses, the evolution of different harmonic components, and their dependence on the bubble state. This would significantly advance the development of bubble nonlinear oscillation theory. Further, although MDMBs with controllable state can effectively enhance subharmonic in vitro experiments, in the in vivo environment, factors such as blood flow and blood pressure may further alter the bubble state, thereby affecting subharmonic generation. Therefore, how to utilize MDMBs with controllable state to achieve strong subharmonic responses in vivo needs further investigation.

Conclusions

Using the multi-gas core (CO2 and C3F8) with different volume fractions can effectively modulate the degree of MDMB shrinkage after stabilization, therefore achieving controllable initial MDMB state. With increasing CO2 volume fraction, the initial state gradually transitioned from elastic to the final mechanically condensed state. This transition led to a decrease in the initial surface tension and the increase in the initial shell elasticity, enabling the controllable tuning of the bubble’s initial state for relevant applications. These findings establish a novel and feasible strategy for achieving controllable bubble state, which would offer new perspective on designing state-of-art microbubbles with tailored state for improving specific ultrasound imaging and therapy modalities. Further, we determined that MDMBs approaching buckling state exhibited a significantly lower threshold PNP for subharmonic generation and a significantly higher subharmonic amplitude. These experimental results were approximately consistent with the Marmottant’s theoretical calculations, therefore providing direct experimental evidence for the mechanism of subharmonic generation and consolidating the foundation of nonlinear oscillation theory of bubbles. More importantly, MDMB with controllable states can serve as a standard and controllable experiment model for further investigating the bubble nonlinear oscillation mechanisms and advancing ultrasound–based medical applications.
