Development of the SupersonicIEF Method for High‐Throughput Charge Variant Analysis
Authors: Will McElroy, Christopher D. Heger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe analysis of biopharmaceuticals for charge variants occurs from early‐stage samples through formulation and process‐development optimization. Higher throughput methods allow increased analysis of these samples to facilitate greater understanding of the samples and to better optimize their production and formulation. To enable higher throughput charge variant analysis, a new, rapid platform imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (icIEF) method was optimized to be two to three times faster than standard methods.

Introduction

A large and growing class of drugs is therapeutic biologics, including monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), fusion proteins, and even viruses. They are used for the treatment of cancer, autoimmune diseases, infectious diseases, and other conditions [1]. The successful application of therapeutic biologics is not only based on their potency and efficacy but also on avoiding manufacturing challenges in the final product. Protein aggregation, susceptibility to chemical degradation, and insufficient product stability are common issues related to chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) development. Analytical characterization plays a crucial role in the early stages of therapeutic biologic drug development [2]. By evaluating at the early stage, delays can be avoided during late‐stage development. Additionally, formulation and process development require extended characterization and benefit from fast quantitative platform methods [3, 4, 5, 6]. Addressing the need for a faster charge heterogeneity method that can provide accurate and reliable results could reduce the time and cost associated with biotherapeutic development.

A standard analytical technology for the charge heterogeneity characterization of therapeutic biologics is imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (icIEF) [7]. The introduction of icIEF considerably decreased sample run times 4–5‐fold by eliminating the mobilization required with standard single‐point detection icIEF instrumentation [8, 9]. Wide adoption of icIEF has shown the significant improvement the technique provides for charge heterogeneity analysis. icIEF leverages the isoelectric point (pI) of a protein and separates the protein inside a capillary by applying a voltage in the presence of ampholytes. The method is sensitive to high salt concentrations, and compression of the ampholyte gradient by salt can significantly shorten run times [10]. When salts are used for gradient compression, the current can increase excessively, leading to failed batches and shortened capillary life. To address this, we leveraged the Maurice instrument, which allows for both voltage ramping and a higher maximum focusing voltage (5000 V) to develop a new rapid icIEF method.

In this study, the development of a precise, versatile, and high‐throughput icIEF method is demonstrated. This method not only reduced sample run times 2–3‐fold compared to standard icIEF methods but also works for additional sample types. This was achieved through changes to the instrument method and ampholytic blocker concentration. The blockers [11] were employed to compress the ampholytic gradient, which accelerates the focusing process. The focusing rate was further increased by the addition of stepped voltage ramps and an increased final separation voltage while avoiding excess current. Together, this allowed significant shortening of each injection while achieving complete focusing of the protein charge isoforms. Furthermore, this SupersonicIEF method,1 was applied to a low‐concentration, high salt antibody sample to demonstrate its applicability to early‐development samples. This method should further enable the support of the ever‐growing activities required for the characterization, process development, and manufacturing of therapeutic proteins.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The USP mAbs were purchased from the USP (Rockville, MD, USA). Recombinant human PD‐L1‐Fc chimera (156‐B7) was from Bio‐Techne (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Belimumab (ICH5080) was purchased from ichorbio (Wayne, PA, USA). PlusOne Urea (17‐1319‐01) was purchased from GE Healthcare (Chicago, IL, USA). Iminodiacetic acid (IDA, ≥98.0%, 5678) and 10× PBS (P5493) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Overall, 1% methylcellulose (101876), Maurice pI markers 4.05 (046–029), 6.14 (046–031), 8.40 (046–033), and 10.17 (046–035), anolyte (102718), catholyte (042–338), arginine (042–691), SimpleSol Protein Solubilizer for icIEF (046–575), and 2 M Ammonium Acetate (046–580) were products of ProteinSimple. Pharmalyte 3–10 (17045601), 5–8 (17045301), and 8–10.5 (17045501) were purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA). All solutions were made using Millipore deionized water.

Methods

Maurice CE instrument (ProteinSimple) and icIEF cartridges (PS‐MC02‐C) were used for SupersonicIEF analysis. The instrument has two detectors that image the entire 5 cm capillary separation column: a UV absorption imaging detector operated at 280 nm and a UV fluorescence imaging detector with a 280 nm excitation wavelength and a 350 nm emission collection wavelength for protein native fluorescence. The IEF separation and detection were performed on a icIEF cartridge (5 cm length, 100 µm I.D., FC‐coated capillary) in the Maurice CE instrument.

SupersonicIEF Method

In the mAb icIEF experiments, USP mAbs 1–3 and Belimumab (1 mg/mL initial, 0.2 mg/mL final) were added to a master mix consisting of Pharmalyte 5–8 (0.5%) and Pharmalyte 8–10.5 (2%), Maurice icIEF pI markers 6.14 (0.6%) and 10.17 (0.6%), IDA (12.5 mM), arginine (12.5 mM),2 urea (2 M), and methyl cellulose (0.35%) final. For the low concentration experiment, the mAbs were first diluted to 1 mg/mL in PBS before diluting the samples to 0.2 mg/mL in H2O. These samples were then added to the same master mix to a final concentration of 0.04 mg/mL.

For the rhPD‐L1‐Fc experiment, rhPD‐L1‐Fc (2 mg/mL) was mixed with Pharmalyte 3–10 (1.9%) and Pharmalyte 5–8 (0.6%), Maurice icIEF pI markers 6.14 (0.6%) and 8.40 (0.6%), IDA (12.5 mM), arginine (12.5 mM), SimpleSol (40%), and methyl cellulose (0.35%) final. The final sample concentration was 0.4 mg/mL.

Electrophoresis for the SupersonicIEF method was performed by first applying a three‐step voltage ramp consisting of 1500 V for 0.3 min, 2500 V for 0.2 min, and 3000 V for 0.2 min, followed by a final focus voltage of 4300 V for 3.5 min. For native fluorescence detection, the exposure time was set to 10 s for standard samples and 60 s for low‐concentration samples.

icIEF Fractionation

USP mAb1 and mAb2 charge variants were fractionated as follows: Each mAb (2 mg/mL initial, 0.4 mg/mL final) was added to a master mix consisting of Pharmalyte 5–8 (0.5%) and Pharmalyte 8–10.5 (2%), Maurice icIEF pI markers 6.14 (0.6%) and 10.17 (0.6%), arginine (25 mM), urea (4 M), SimpleSol (10%), and methyl cellulose (0.35%) final and loaded onto MauriceFlex using a icIEF fractionation cartridge (PN PS‐MC02‐F). Charge variants were mobilized using 5 mM ammonium acetate.

For the icIEF fractionation method, a three‐step voltage ramp consisting of 250 V for 3 min, 500 V for 10 min, and 1000 V for 10 min, followed by a focus voltage of 1500 V for 25 min in an 8 cm length, 320 µm I.D. FC‐coated capillary, was used. The use of lower voltages was necessitated by the wider internal diameter of the preparative capillary. Then, charge variants were chemically mobilized in 5 mM ammonium acetate at 1100 V for 30 min in a 2 mL vial before 48 fractions were collected in 1 min intervals in a 96‐well plate.

Data were acquired using the Compass for iCE software (v4.0.0, San Jose, USA) and evaluated in JMP software (SAS Institute Cary, NC).

Results and Discussion

Initial development started with the evaluation of the effects of higher voltages on the current in the capillary. As the voltage increases, there is an increase in the current for the given conditions. Increasing voltage can lead to the current deviating from a linear increase with voltage, because heating results in decreased resistance in the running buffer [11]. To mitigate the effects of excessive current, the ampholyte concentration was lowered to 2.5% to provide a lower current while still in a robust concentration range to maintain the pH gradient within the capillary. Then an Ohm's law plot was made using increasing voltages on the Maurice ranging from 3000 to 5000 V, the maximum voltage that the instrument allows. The results showed that with 2.5% ampholyte, the current did not deviate from linearity with an RSq = 0.9983, showing that Joule heating is not a factor under these conditions (data not shown). Although this observation should be generally applicable, individual samples should be evaluated to determine if the increased current has any detrimental effects, especially if they are formulated in a high salt sample buffer.

The standard icIEF method uses a 1‐min initial voltage step to minimize the high initial currents. Additional voltage steps were added to the method to ramp the initial current up to the greater final voltages used in this method. The ramp was adjusted to three voltage steps consisting of 1500, 2500, and 3000 V, all for 0.2 min. This ramp was used for all conditions in a JMP (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA) d‐optimal design of experiment (DOE) that was conducted evaluating the final separation run time (3–5 min), voltage (3500–4500 V), and both acidic (IDA) and basic (arginine) blocker concentrations (5–15 mM) shown in Table 1. For all experiments in this work, the two blockers were used at equimolar concentrations. The USP mAb method with the three USP mAbs [13] was used as the basis for testing this new concept.

This figure has electropherograms displayed with the raw pixel units (not pI calibrated) to show the gradient changes induced by the blocker concentration on the pI markers’ relative positions. The compression of the gradient created by the higher blocker concentration is clearly shown by the smaller distance between the two pI markers. The compression decreases the distance between the peaks, but all conditions maintained sufficient resolution for robust integration of the mAb peaks. As the charged isoforms migrate to their pI, each specific isoform concentration increases, resulting in a higher and narrower peak at a specific pI. The integrated data were evaluated for peak width, height, and resolution among charge isoforms. For USP mAb1, low resolution between the first acidic peak and the main peak was observed for some conditions, so this feature became the most critical result to analyze further in Figure 2. The USP mAb1 main peak resolution model had a fit of RSq = 0.57 and RMSE of 0.0505, showing a reasonable fit. Both run voltage and time increased resolution for USP mAb1, whereas the concentration of blockers only increased resolution up to 10 mM. The greater distance between the peaks for USP mAb2 and USP mAb3 resulted in resolution being less predictive of changes to the profile. For these two samples, the main peak width was used for analysis, where a smaller peak width was the desirable response. The fit model for USP mAb2 main peak width showed a high RSq = 0.86 and a low RMSE = 0.002474. The prediction profiler showed no response from the blocker concentration and better response from increasing focusing time. The fit model for USP mAb3 main peak width showed a high RSq = 0.9 and a low RMSE = 0.0026. Like USP mAb2, focusing time showed the greatest response, though the top results with the lowest main peak widths (Conditions 3, 11, and 12) had comparable profiles. Final conditions were selected based on the increased current and gradient compression due to higher blocker concentration while balancing with focusing time with the goal for a SupersonicIEF platform method. The final conditions used for further studies included 2.5% ampholytes, 12.5 mM blockers, a voltage ramp of 1500, 2500, and 3000 V, all for 0.2 min, and a final focusing at 4300 V for 3.5 min.

To test the new method, the three USP mAbs were injected 32 times each for a 96‐injection batch. To shorten the overall batch time, the native fluorescence was limited to one 10‐s exposure. The full batch took 12.1 h to complete, which is approximately 2.5 times faster than a typical mAb method. The platform method aspect was further assured by testing an additional therapeutic antibody, Belimumab (0.25 mg/mL final), and a recombinant fusion protein, rhPD‐L1‐Fc. The SupersonicIEF icIEF profiles of Belimumab and rhPD‐L1‐Fc are similar to the profiles observed with the standard icIEF method (Figures 5 and 6). However, the comparison of SupersonicIEF and standard methods showed differences in sensitivity. Because the blockers compress the gradient, an increase the peak height is seen for samples with the same sample concentration. Concluding Remarks

Here we describe a new rapid icIEF method, called SupersonicIEF, which was achieved with minimal adjustment to current icIEF method parameters and reagent conditions. There is no fundamental change to the icIEF process, making the method broadly applicable as suggested by the variety of molecules tested in this study. The SupersonicIEF method requires no new reagents, consumables, or instrumentation. Additionally, the increased blocker concentrations minimize drift due to electro‐osmotic flow (EOF) and isotachophoresis (ITP) in the capillary, resulting in less shifting of the focused peaks during each injection and over the course of the batch. Furthermore, the decreased batch runtime minimizes any auto‐sampler stability issues that could be present.

The ability to use a platform method for charge heterogeneity analysis from low‐concentration early‐stage assessment samples through late stages of drug development should minimize method development time and provide better continuity of the integrated data. The capability to complete a 96‐well plate of samples overnight would be advantageous for formulation optimization or other high‐throughput analytical needs.

Currently, icIEF is a gold standard for charge heterogeneity analysis with fast method development and platform methods. This new SupersonicIEF method leverages the existing advantages of the Maurice, which include multiple voltage ramps and the ability to run at higher voltages overall. This method further accelerates the icIEF analysis with minimal changes to standard assay results.
