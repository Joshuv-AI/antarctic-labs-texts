Physical activity and metabolic syndrome in relation to incident dementia in UK biobank
Authors: Yiqun Li, Xiao Ren, Minglan Jiang, Pinni Yang, Yiming Jia, Lulu Sun, Ruirui Wang, Mengyao Shi, Xiaowei Zheng, Zhengbao Zhu, Yonghong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship among physical activity (PA), metabolic syndrome (MetS) and dementia based on UK Biobank.MethodsA total of 220,372 dementia-free participants over the age of 50 had available data for outcomes and exposures components were included. Four PA groups [High, Medium, Low and No Moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA)], two MetS groups (MetS and No MetS) and eight joint groups were categorized to explore the effects of PA level and MetS status on dementia outcomes. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to explore the associations between PA, MetS and new-onset dementia.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13 years, 4778 all-cause dementia [ACD, including 2997 Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and 960 vascular dementia (VD) events] were recorded. In multivariate models adjusting for genetic and biological factors, lifestyle factors and socioeconomic factors, participants with MetS had a 25% increase in the risk of dementia. In terms of PA, the incidence of dementia in individuals without MVPA increased by 46%. Cox proportional hazards regression and Kaplan-Meier analysis all showed that participants with MetS and No MVPA had remarkable highest risk in eight joint groups (for ACD, 1.43 (1.01–2.02); for AD, 1.61 (0.73–1.84); for VD, 3.14 (1.60–6.14), compared with the reference group of No MetS-High PA).ConclusionsThere is a synergistic correlation of MetS and PA level with dementia (ACD, AD and VD), which is significantly exacerbated in participants with MetS and No MVPA. Even low levels of PA may also effectively decrease the harmful associations between MetS and dementia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12986-025-01058-0.Keywords: Physical activity, Metabolic syndrome, Dementia, Alzheimer's disease, Vascular dementia, UK biobank

Introduction

Dementia remains a study hotspot of widespread concern in the field of public health. It is the fifth largest contributor to the global burden of disease, and consistently the chief cause of disability and dependence worldwide as well [1–3], with the most common subtypes——Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and vascular dementia (VD) [4]. And the total expenditure in 2022 for people age ≥ 65 with dementia spent on dementia healthcare, long-term care, and hospice services was estimated to be as high as $321 billion [2]. Given that, in the absence of curative pharmacological treatments, identifying modifiable risk factors is paramount. It was reported by epidemiological and simulation studies that interventions targeting the main modifiable risk and protective factors may delay or even stop the occurrence of dementia [1, 5]. In 2020 report of the Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, intervention, and care, keeping physically active in midlife and later life was recommend to protect against dementia [6].

With the development of society, the mechanized transportation and sedentary lifestyle of modern people have become increasingly common, which has led to the universality of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its rise of incidence worldwide [7]. The MetS, also called as syndrome X, the insulin resistance syndrome, and the deadly quartet, is defined by World Health Organization (WHO) as a cluster of abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia (raised triglycerides and lowered high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), hypertension and hyperglycemia. It is not only a single disease, but a sum of several metabolic risk factors. It has been proven to increase the risk of adverse health outcomes, covering cardiometabolic diseases, cognitive impairment and dementia [8–11]. Importantly, it has been reported that PA is also a cornerstone in the prevention and management of MetS itself [12]. As common related factors for dementia, although there is a wealth of research on the independent association between PA or MetS and dementia, existing literature has largely examined these factors in isolation, which may mask important interplay in their influence on dementia risk. Besides, investigating their joint association is crucial to identify high-risk subgroups, reveal the mechanism of action of potential biological pathways and inform targeted public health strategies.

Therefore, based on a large prospective cohort with long-term follow-up from UK Biobank, this study aims to verify the independent association of PA and MetS with all-cause dementia (ACD) and its common subtypes (AD and VD), and explore their joint associations to clarify whether the benefits of PA vary among individuals with different metabolic health conditions.

Methods

Study design and population

We used data from the UK Biobank, a population-based cohort study approved by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (MREC). It is a large-scale biomedical database and research resource containing de-identified genetic, lifestyle and health information and biological samples, covering half a million UK participants aged 40–69 years at baseline from the general population (5.5% response rate) [13] who consented to the use of their anonymised data and access to their national health-related hospital and death records. More information about the UK Biobank protocol is available online (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). Detailed information was also described elsewhere [14].

It is reported that the prevalence of dementia increases exponentially with age [15], and the majority of dementia patients are 65 years old or above [16]. However, the pathological changes in dementia (such as Aβ deposition) begin decades before clinical onset [6]. Given these, of all the participants in UK Biobank after dropped out during follow-up (N = 502394), to ensure that there were individuals at risk of developing dementia in the study population, we excluded 117,822 participants aged < 50 years [17], 41,708 with dementia at baseline or without data for dementia. Participants without data for PA (N = 72708) or all five MetS components (N = 49784) were also excluded. Finally, 220,372 participants were fitted into statistical analysis. Selection of our study participants in the UK Biobank was presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Exposures assessments

PA refers to any motor activity or body movement requiring muscle contraction and thus energy utilization [18, 19]. Its data were obtained during the baseline period using touchscreen questionnaires from the modified short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which assessed total physical activity (TPA), including walking, moderate, and vigorous PA performed over the last 7 days. We calculated total weekly MVPA volume (MET-minutes/week) by multiplying the standardised metabolic equivalent of task (MET) value of each activity (3.3 METs for walking, 4 for moderate and 8 for vigorous activities) by the number of minutes per week [20, 21]. All the participants were divided into four PA groups based on the grouping rules recommended by the IPAQ scoring protocol [20, 22–24]: (1) High (≥ 3000 MET-min/week); (2) Medium (600 ≤ PA < 3000 MET-min/week); (3) Low (0 < PA < 600 MET-min/week); (4) No moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) (0 MET-min/week from MVPA, regardless of total MET-min/week). This classification aligns with public health guidelines, where the threshold for “low” PA (< 600 MET-min/week) corresponds to activity levels below the minimum WHO recommendation for substantial health benefits [25]. To reflect the potential health benefits of insufficient PA compared to No MVPA, we have added another grouping method for PA: (1) Any MVPA; (2) No MVPA.

Based on the common definition agreed by both of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the American Heart Association/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (AHA/NHLBI) in 2009, the presence of any 3 of 5 the following risk factors constitutes a diagnosis of metabolic syndrome (MetS) [7]: (1) Abdominal obesity: elevated waist circumference (WC); (2) Elevated triglycerides; (3) Elevated blood pressure (BP); (4) Elevated blood glucose (BG); (5) Reduced indicates high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (Supplementary Table S1). Compared with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) has several advantages including greater convenience (fasting not required), greater preanalytical stability, and fewer day-to-day perturbations during stress, changes in nutrition, or illness [26]. Accordingly, HbA1c with a cut point of ≥ 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) was finally used as a proxy measure of glucose to represent elevated BG. In the light of status of MetS, two groups were categorized for later statistical analysis: (1) No MetS; (2) MetS.

Furthermore, to explore the combined effects of PA level and MetS status on dementia outcomes, we also categorized all participants into several joint groups based on PA and MetS groups: (1) No MetS, High PA (reference group); (2) No MetS, Medium PA; (3) No MetS, Low PA; (4) No MetS, No MVPA; (5) MetS, High PA; (6) MetS, Medium PA; (7) MetS, Low PA; (8)MetS, No MVPA.

Outcome assessments

The primary outcome in current study was new-onset al.l-cause dementia (ACD), and the secondary outcomes were new-onset Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or vascular dementia (VD), separately. Algorithmically defined outcomes (ADOs) were used to determine dementia outcome in UK Biobank [27]. Dementia diagnoses were ascertained using hospital inpatient records containing data on admissions and diagnoses obtained from the Hospital Episode Statistics for England, the Scottish Morbidity Record data for Scotland, and the Patient Episode Database for Wales. Additional cases were detected through linkage to death register data from the National Health Service Digital, National Health Service Central Register, and the National Records of Scotland. These data were algorithmically combined and categorized using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), to identify dementia. This methodology is the standard and validated approach for outcome ascertainment in large cohorts, as evidenced by its application in numerous high-impact publications using the UK Biobank [28–31]. The list of ICD codes used to ascertain dementia in the study was selected and validated by the UK Biobank outcome adjudication group and presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Covariates assessments

Following covariates were collected at baseline using a touch screen questionnaire, including age [< 55 years old (early middle age), 55–65 years old (the transition period from middle age to early retirement), and ≥ 65 years old (the high-incidence period of dementia corresponding to the traditional retirement age)], sex, ethnicity (White, Black and Other), education level [College or University degree, A levels/AS levels or equivalent, O levels/GCSEs or equivalent, CSEs or equivalent, NVQ or HND or HNC or equivalent, Other professional qualifications (e.g. nursing, teaching) and None of the above], household income in GBP, smoking status (Never, Previous, and Current), alcohol consumption status (Never, Previous, and Current). In addition, material socioeconomic deprivation was replaced by Townsend deprivation index (TDI), which was calculated based on the postcode of family address [32]. They provide a measure according to aggregated area socioeconomic deprivation data, consisting of car ownership, household overcrowding, owner occupation and unemployment. Higher TDI equate to higher levels of area-based socioeconomic deprivation [33]. The healthy diet scores were calculated according to the following rules [34, 35]: (1) Vegetable intake at least four tablespoons each day (median); (2) Fruit intake at least three pieces each day (median); (3) Fish intake at least twice each week (median); (4) Unprocessed red meat intake no more than twice each week (median); (5) Processed meat intake no more than twice each week (median). Each one point was given for each favorable diet factor, with the total healthy diet score ranging from 0 to 5. APOE-ε4 carrier status (“ε4 non-carrier”, “ε4 carrier”) was derived using the rs429358 and rs7412 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP), which were directly genotyped on the UK Biobank arrays [33, 36, 37].

Statistics and data analysis

Based on the grouping of exposure, we used descriptive statistics stratified by MetS status and PA level respectively to compare the baseline characteristics, in which continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percent).

To explore the independent and joint relation among PA, MetS and incident dementia, multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models were in use with the follow-up time as the underlying time scale. Except for separate grouping based on two exposure factors, overall sample was also further stratified into eight mutually exclusive groups for joint analyses. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were utilized to express results. Multiplicative and additive models were also put to use to analyze the interaction of PA and MetS with mortality risks for dementia. When analyzing the multiplicative interaction, PA, MetS, PA×MetS are simultaneously included in the logistic regression model. In the analysis of additive interaction, participants with Any MVPA and No MetS were used as reference groups, and the Odds ratio (OR), 95% CI and regression coefficient B of the other three groups were obtained using multivariate logistic regression. Measure of interaction on additive scale were calculated using the table prepared by Andersson et al., including the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) and the synergy index (S).

We also performed subgroup analyses for dementia outcomes, grouped by age, sex, ethnicity, education level, smoking status, drinking status and APOE-ε4 carrier status. To examine the robustness of our findings, as sensitivity analysis, we repeating the main analysis with (1) deleting participants suffering from dementia outcomes within 5 years; (2) deleting participants with APOE-ε4; (3) defining abdominal obesity using waist-to-hip ratio instead of WC (Waist-to-hip ratio ≥ 1.03 for male and ≥ 0.95 for female was defined as satisfying abdominal obesity [38].); (4) performing competing risk analyses using the Fine-Gray models that formally account for death as a competing event. In the 4th sensitivity analysis, we directly estimated the hazard of dementia in the presence of death as a competing event. And the outcome was defined as a composite variable with three states: (1) incident dementia (the event of interest), (2) death without dementia (the competing event), and (3) alive and free of dementia at the end of follow-up (censored). The follow-up time was calculated from the baseline assessment until the earliest of: dementia diagnosis, death, or the end of the study period. The same exposure variables (joint categories of MetS and PA) and sets of covariates (Models 1, 2, and 3) as in the primary analysis were used. The results of this competing risk analysis are presented as HRs with 95% CIs.

Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; Cary, NC, USA) and IBM SPSS (version 25.0; Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software.

Result

A total 220,372 dementia-free participants were recruited in 2006–2010, followed until death or the end of 2020 for the ascertainment of dementia and then included in our final analysis. Of these, 4778 respondents experienced ACD (2997 AD and 960 VD) during follow-up in which 57.43% were participants ≥ 65 years old. The majority of the participants (42.41%) had medium PA level, followed by having low PA level (25.63%). Only 17.54% of participants engaged in high PA level. There were 60,114 participants (27.28%) with MetS at baseline, of which 95.22% had elevated blood pressure and 77.93% had elevated HbA1c of MetS components. These with MetS also had lower PA level than participants with No MetS at baseline. Similarly, participants at higher PA level had lower proportion of MetS component. Detailed baseline characteristics of the study participants stratified by MetS status and PA level were presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S3, respectively.

In terms of the association among the three, the four joint groups of participants with MetS had a higher risk of dementia in multivariable-adjusted analyses, in which those without MVPA had the highest risk (for ACD, 1.77 (1.55–2.02); for AD, 1.57 (1.33–1.86); for VD, 2.92 (2.23–3.81), respectively). The HRs and 95% CI for each condition of exposure combinations compared with the referent group (No MetS-High PA) were illustrated in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S4. The Kaplan-Meier analysis also showed that participants with MetS and No MVPA had significantly lower survival probability rates of ACD, AD and VD (all log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2). In interaction analysis, results revealed that PA level and MetS status have a multiplicative interaction effect on dementia (for ACD, AD and VD, P < 0.0001). Further explored the additive interaction among them, in the multivariate models for ACD, RERI = 1.13, AP = 0.43 and S = 3.12, indicating that PA level and MetS status had a synergistic and additive interaction effect on the risk of dementia (Supplementary Table S5). This also meant the presence of No MVPA and MetS would significantly increase the risk of dementia (AD and VD).

In subgroup analysis (Supplementary Table S6, S7), among new cases of ACD (AD and VD), the largest proportion is older participants, especially in male. At each age or sex subgroup, for participants in the same MetS status, the risk of dementia almost shows a continuous upward trend as PA levels decrease. The results remained consistent with the main findings in sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S8, S9, S10, S11). Among them, when further limiting the study population to non-APOE-ε4-carrier, the joint association strength among PA level, MetS status and dementia incidence appeared a significant decrease (Supplementary Table S9). When death was accounted as a competing risk, although the HR value slightly decreased, all the associations that were significant in the main analysis still maintained statistical significance in the competitive risk analysis (Supplementary Table S11).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this should be the first study to simultaneously explore the direct correlation among PA, MetS and dementia (ACD, AD and VD). Therefore, this nationwide cohort study provided novel population-based evidence indicating a significant association among PA, MetS and dementia (ACD, AD and VD), whether independent or joint. And there is a synergistic action in the correlation among the three. Besides, we also observed that lower levels of PA especially No MVPA were remarkably associated with higher risk of dementia (ACD, AD and VD), approving the value of any MVPA targeting both MetS components and dementia.

High blood pressure, high BMI and high fasting plasma glucose, these three metabolic risks, were pointed out to be the top risk variables in 20 leading risk factors for years of life lost worldwide in 2040 by reference forecasts [38]. It is known that impairment of metabolism and insulin resistance are physiological conditions that favor the development of dementia [39], which were linked to MetS components. Clinical and epidemiological evidence indicates that MetS clusters such as hypertension, obesity, type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia promote the development of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), dementia and AD in several ways [40–44]. Thereafter, as an aggregation covering above three important risk factors, the concept of MetS has once again attracted widespread attention. In a cohort study based on more than 175,000 individuals aged ≥ 60 years, MetS was found to strongly associated with a 12% increased risk of dementia over 15 years [45]. Similar research findings were also found in a nationally representative cohort study of 4,106,590 individuals in Korea [46]. But there were also some studies found no statistically significant pooled association emerged between MetS and incident dementia [47, 48]. Given the contradictory results in the current research of this theme, we conducted this study based on a large cohort study with 13-year follow-up. Moreover, definitions related to the MetS are quite diverse actually. The waist circumference (WC) criteria of MetS definitions are frequently not used, which also means allowing risk factors for diseases and the absence of a common definition of MetS. Hence, a novel and stricter definition of MetS published on Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol in 2023 [38] has also been applied to our research in sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S10), and the results remained consistent with the main findings, which also verified the accuracy of this novel definition of MetS to a certain extent. And our study attained consistent findings that obtained positive results, proving MetS was significantly associated with a higher risk of dementia. In addition, we have also enriched the research results on the association between MetS and dementia subtypes (AD, VD) based on previous studies. Results were not only consistent with the overall trend of ACD, but also revealed that the correlation between VD (1.65 (1.44–1.90)) and MetS was stronger than that of AD (1.18 (1.09–1.28)), that was consistent with existing researches as well [46]. It was reported that nearly 1/5 or more of the adult population were affected by MetS with a secular increase in prevalence [19, 49]. Hence, it is imperative to propose practical and effective intervention measures.

Irene Sequi-Dominguez et al. revealed that health interventions aimed at promoting PA and healthy lifestyle changes had a strong positive effect on patients with MetS through a meta-analysis of nine studies, suggesting that PA may be effective tools for MetS management [50]. A meta-analysis based on a total of 17 studies including 64,353 participants and 11,271 incident cases revealed that the increase in PA levels can reduce the risk of MetS, providing a solid theoretical basis for preventing MetS through lifestyle interventions [51]. In terms of its mechanism, PA might improve MetS through reducing insulin resistance, increasing delivery of insulin to active muscles, increasing muscle glycogen synthesis under insulin stimulation, enhancing energy expenditure and maintaining a stable circadian rhythm and a healthy sleep pattern [13, 52–54]. PA is a cost-effective intervention that can both prevent and mitigate the impact of MetS. In fact, there is increasing evidence to support that PA is an effective, nonpharmacological alternative to clinical treatment, having outstanding performances in both neurological or neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic diseases, including dementia and MetS [6, 12, 55–59]. However, perhaps due to the requirement for sustained effort, the inadequate understanding of causal lifestyle factors, as well as a lack of health awareness, PA has not been fully utilized [60, 61]. By now, there is no effective medication to treat dementia as well, which highlights the key role of prevention. According to study evidence, physical activity (PA) could also change the trajectory in severe forms of dementia such as AD [18, 62]. A systematic review and meta-analysis, based on 2,154,818 participants at baseline, 74,700 ACD cases, 2848 AD cases, and 1423 VD cases during follow-up, revealed that PA was associated with lower incidence of ACD, AD and VD [63]. Related to our findings, participants in the lowest PA category (No MVPA) had highest risk of dementia (ACD, AD and VD). Compared with those in the highest PA level, those without MVPA had nearly 50% higher incident of ACD, almost 40% higher incident of AD and even 90% higher incident of VD. Only less than 600 MET-min/week of PA instead of no MVPA could also decrease dementia incidence by 36%.

Although PA can not only reduce the incidence of MetS, but also reduce that of dementia, which is also a possible adverse health outcome of MetS, there has been no research exploring the joint relationship among the three by now. Our results demonstrated that after adjusting for age and sex, individuals with MetS have a 27% higher incidence of ACD for individuals who are all at high PA level. For participant all with MetS, compared to those without MVPA, even if only low-level PA was carried out (< 600 MET-min/week) could also reduce the incidence of ACD by 46%, AD by 35% and VD by 146%. Furthermore, interaction analysis results in our study added to evidence that the combination of No MVPA and MetS was associated with a larger risk of dementia (ACD, AD and VD) than the sum of the estimated risk of No MVPA or MetS alone on an additive scale. Besides, our research found that in patients with MetS, high levels of PA can reduce their risk of dementia to a level similar to that of people without MetS but with low PA. Given that PA itself is also an effective means to improve MetS, this phenomenon may involve two pathways: one is the direct protective effect of PA on dementia [64–66]; the other is that PA indirectly reduces the risk of dementia by improving metabolic health (e.g., improving insulin resistance, regulating lipid metabolism, reducing inflammatory responses, and lowering blood pressure) [67, 68]. Therefore, the observed interaction may partly reflect the beneficial effect of PA through improvements in MetS during the follow-up period. This “risk offset” effect highlighted the particular importance of PA for people at high risk of metabolic abnormalities. It also provided insights into potential biological pathways and emphasizes the importance of including PA as part of the comprehensive management strategy for MetS patients.

Moreover, it could be found in sensitivity analysis that in the non-APOE-ε4-carrier, people with MetS but No MVPA were increased risk of ACD and VD, demonstrating that all PA level counts, even inadequate PA. Although the competitive risk analysis obtained slightly conservative risk estimates, the consistent conclusions drawn from both analytical methods further strengthened our main finding: regardless of metabolic status, higher levels of PA were associated with a lower risk of dementia, while individuals with both MetS and a lack of PA faced the highest risk of dementia. It was reported that fat oxidation (whether from circulating free fatty acids or muscle triglycerides) was significantly enhanced and the translocation of muscle glucose transporter-4 (GLUT-4) increased in any level of PA, supporting that at least any intensity of PA may have a beneficial effect [52]. Our findings suggested that PA as a modifiable protective lifestyle factor could be served as an effective target for both MetS and dementia prevention and treatment.

So far, numerous studies have detailed the association between PA and various health outcomes in the elderly, including the relation for chronic diseases prevention and risk reduction (all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk and CVD mortality, arterial stiffness, and cancer prevention and mortality), functional outcomes (musculoskeletal health, daily living ability disorders, functional limitations, healthy aging, quality of life, and risk of falling), and mental health outcomes (cognitive decline, AD, and incident depression) [4, 13, 53, 54, 69–72]. On the basis of existing research results, our study supplemented the evidence chain of the association between PA, MetS, and dementia, validated the potential value of insufficient PA compared to No MVPA, and also extended the association between PA and MetS to dementia and its subtypes (AD and VD) which were the adverse outcomes of MetS as well. As an activator of metabolism and a nonpharmacological treatment method with economic benefits, Public Health England produced a framework for embedding PA into the fabric of daily life aimed at preventing adverse health outcomes through PA interventions as early as 2014 [73]. It was reported that light intensity of PA has health benefits, suggesting that PA recommendations should advocate at least moderate desired physical intensity. However, for inactive people such as the most sedentary or low-fit people, the inclusion of light PA is highly recommended given that considerable benefits were likely to occur by engaging in modest activity levels (i.e., some exercise is better than none) [74–76]. Besides, the concept of MetS could also be helpful to inform disease risk prediction, by the identification of subphenotypes, such as people with metabolically unhealthy normal weight and metabolically healthy obesity [77]. It has practical implications for helping identify high-risk subgroups so as to develop targeted lifestyle and metabolic interventions. Therefore, it is necessary to enhance the understanding of the concept of MetS in general people and promote the application of its novel concept in the field of public health. Our results support the advocacy for a healthy lifestyle that concurrently target PA and MetS to enhance improvements in both exposures. Future studies with more sophisticated modeling and repeated device-based measurements of PA could shed further light on these bidirectional associations as well as the underlying explanatory mechanisms.

First of all, current study is a large prospective study based on detailed data from the UK Biobank, as well as the first study to explore the joint correlation among PA, MetS and dementia, with a large number of participants and extensive 13-year follow-up data, covering sociodemographic, medical, cognitive, and genetic data of over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years when recruited in 2006–2010 [14, 33]. This allowed us to conduct several sensitivity analyses so that the credibility of our findings enhanced. Major statistical analysis was conducted among participants aged ≥ 50 years at baseline, which covered the vast majority of individuals at risk of developing dementia. Secondly, while validating the relationship between PA level or MetS status and ACD were found in previous studies, our study also further explored their independent relationship with two common subtypes of dementia (AD, VD), as well as the joint association among the different states of the two exposure factors and dementia outcomes, expanding the research findings within this research topic. Thirdly, in order to explore the potential health benefits of any MVPA even insufficient PA compared with No MVPA, besides the traditional grouping method divided according to the IPAQ standard (low, medium, and high PA), another No MVPA group was further defined. As well, our research findings do demonstrate the necessity of low PA in comparison with NO MVPA, which has practical guiding significance. Last but not least, our combined approach examining MetS and PA jointly also helped to identify synergistic or antagonistic effects and reveal important public health information that may be concealed in separate analyses.

However, there are also some limitations to our study. First, in order to minimise the effects of reverse causalities and confounding, we have taken a series of measures, such as setting exclusion criteria for the study population and including covariates as comprehensive as possible. But residual confounding from unknown or unmeasured factors remains a possibility, and the observational nature of our study excludes a clear causal interpretation of the results. Second, the PA exposure and most covariates are self-reported, which might increase the possibility of recall bias. And we acknowledge that reliance on medical records may miss dementia cases with delayed diagnosis or those not seeking healthcare. These may potentially lead our study to underestimate the true degree of factor relevance. Nevertheless, dementia is a severe neurological disorder that typically necessitates medical intervention and thus is likely to be captured in hospital records. Third, all exposures and confounding factors were collected at recruitment and assumed to be relatively constant during follow-up, which means the changes that occurred during this period were not included in the analysis, that may have a slight impact on the results. It should be noted that the observed interaction may include both the direct effect of PA and the indirect effect brought about by the improvement of MetS. The limitation of this study also lies in the inability to distinguish between these two mechanisms. It is important to note that capturing the complexity of PA change over a lifetime is inherently challenging. While studies with accelerometer data are beginning to address this, our study focuses on establishing the fundamental role of baseline PA. This serves as a necessary foundation for future research to explore how dynamic PA patterns influence dementia risk. So further exploration is needed in the future to determine whether the changes of two exposure over time will have an impact on dementia outcomes. Finally, in some joint groups, the relatively small proportion of outcomes in AD or VD patients may affect our statistical power to detect differences in outcomes risk.

Conclusion

In this study based on 220,372 UK Biobank participants, we found that in individuals with MetS at baseline, maintaining a high level of PA is associated with a significantly reduced risk of dementia, and any level of PA could modify the association between MetS and dementia (ACD, AD and VD) where the coexistence of No MVPA and MetS contribute significantly to the excess dementia risk. Building upon the foundation of previous studies, our findings added to the sparse literature on the topic and suggest that even conducting PA that only meets the current low threshold (600 MET-minutes/week) of international PA standards can also effectively decrease most of the harmful associations between MetS and dementia.

In summary, conducting PA targeting the components of MetS has important preventive value for dementia, which could improve the quality of life furtherly.
