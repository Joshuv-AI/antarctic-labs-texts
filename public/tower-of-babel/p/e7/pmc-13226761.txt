Ligand decorated nanostructure carrier systems for targeted delivery of antimalarials
Authors: Kuldeep Singh, Ritesh Ranjha, Waseem Akram Malla, Priyanka Sharma, Mradul Mohan, Jyoti Das, Himmat Singh, Praveen Kumar Bharti, Anup R. Anvikar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimalarial chemotherapeutics, including artemisinin derivatives and their combination regimens, sustain clinical effectiveness against Plasmodium species, including resistant strains. However, these chemotherapeutics face challenges, including poor aqueous solubility and membrane permeability, a short elimination half-life, erratic oral bioavailability, and delayed parasite clearance. These formidable challenges have been widely addressed by developing new chemical entities, combining artemisinin-based combination therapies, exploring alternative routes of administration, or prolonging the dosing schedule. However, developmental costs, time, and translational obstacles that may exacerbate current treatment gaps. In recent years, nanostructured carrier systems (NSCs) have emerged as novel delivery platforms to overcome constraints of conventional delivery systems. NSCs such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles (NPs), metallic (Gold and ferrite) NPs, lipid-based NPs, nanoemulsions, self-emulsifying drug delivery systems, and micelles, can be further decorated employing ligands (Plasmodium recognizing antibodies, polymers, carbohydrates (glucose), aptamers, heparins) to recognize Plasmodium-infected red blood cells (piRBCs) specifically. This review provides in-depth insights into the ligand decorated nanostructured carrier systems (L-NSCs) capable of differentiating between piRBCs and normal RBCs based on specific ligand decoration, which are not only capable of targeting piRBCs but also the extracellular merozoite stage of Plasmodium. We have discussed in detail the ligands that recognize piRBCs, based on specific biophysical alterations, expression, and the transport of Plasmodium proteins on the surface of piRBCs. The RBCs are structurally simple, lacking organelles, with limited metabolic activity, and a uniform microenvironment, resulting in minimal differentiation between normal and piRBCs. Overexpression of Glucose transporter, appearance of P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein, and selective targeting to sialic acid residues on Glycophorin A on the cell membrane of piRBCs can act as potential recognition sites for L-NSCs. This review offers in-depth insight into emerging opportunities for selective recognition and targeted delivery of antimalarials to address challenges of poor pharmacokinetics, targeted delivery within piRBCs, and enhance the therapeutic efficiency of antimalarials against both susceptible and resistant Plasmodium strains that transmit human malaria.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04695-3.

Introduction

Malaria has been a formidable challenge since ancient times and remains a critical public health concern, particularly in tropical and sub-tropical regions [1–3]. In 2024, an estimated 282 million cases of malaria were reported, a substantial increase from the 249 million estimated malaria cases reported in 2022, along with 0.6 million deaths in 85 countries [4, 5]. Among antimalarials, quinolone derivatives (4-aminoquinolines & 8-aminquinolines), antifolates (sulfonamides and sulfones), naphthoquinones (atovaquone), and artemisinin and its derivatives (ARDs), either alone or in combination, are recommended as first-line therapy [6]. Artemisinin (a sesquiterpene lactone), recognized as the cornerstone of antimalarial drugs, was discovered in 1972; its structure was elucidated in 1979, and its active derivative, dihydroartemisinin, was developed by removing its carbonyl groups [7, 8]. In the 1980s, semisynthetic derivatives with improved solubility, such as artesunate (water-soluble) and artemether/arteether (oil-soluble), demonstrated enhanced antimalarial activity [9–11]. These derivatives have the potential to clear the parasites quickly, yet their short half-lives necessitate combination with a partner drug having a long half-life [12, 13]. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) have been recommended as the first-line treatments for both uncomplicated and multidrug-resistant P. falciparum malaria since the 1990s [14]. The combination of drugs provides mutual protection against the emergence of resistant clones, as the mutant strain remains susceptible to the partner drug with a longer half-life, thereby ensuring continued clinical effectiveness. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends ACTs, including artesunate-sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (ASP), artemether-lumefantrine (AL), dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine, artesunate-mefloquine, and artesunate-amodiaquine for the treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria, against chloroquine-resistant infections, and mixed Plasmodium infections [15–18]. A detailed description of the physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties of WHO-recommended ACTs is summarized in Table 1. The physicochemical properties of artemisinin derivatives and their partner drugs align with the Biopharmaceutical Classification System (BCS) classes II & IV, which are characterized by poor aqueous solubility and limited gastrointestinal permeability [19]. Further, the rapid development of resistance to the partner drug and subsequent failure of ACTs have become inevitable consequences. To extend the lifespan of ACTs, strategies, such as prolonging the dosing schedule [20], Triple artemisinin-based combination therapies (TACTs) [21–23], and alternative routes of drug administration, such as transdermal or microneedle array [24], have been investigated. To overcome these barriers, nanostructured carrier systems (NSCs) and next-generation ligand decorated nanostructured carrier systems (L-NSCs) have attracted considerable attention and emerged as a transformative approach to addressing both infectious and non-infectious diseases, with a recent notable rise in FDA-approved nano-therapeutics [25–27]. Specific targeting of antineoplastic agents, such as docetaxel delivered via PEGylated (targeting agent) doxorubicin in ferritin nanocages [28, 29] and RIPL peptide (IPLVVPLRRRRRRRRC)-conjugated (Targeting ligand) L-NSCs [30], has been achieved as an effective delivery system for ovarian cancer with reduced toxicity. Stimuli-responsive nanomaterials, Nanoplateletsomes [31], biomimetic liposomes [32], Dual targeting micelles [33], and nanoenzymes have also been investigated for targeted delivery and theranostics in gastrointestinal malignancies [34, 35]. The earlier reviews have primarily explored the NSCs in antimalarial drug delivery systems, lacking targeted delivery potential to piRBCs. The present review integrates mechanistic insights and ligand functionalization to enable stage-specific delivery of antimalarials. Additionally, the review highlights key translational challenges, including scalability and cost-effectiveness issues. Further, piRBCs and normal RBCs lack endocytic pathways and share a uniform microenvironment, which hinders differentiation for targeted drug delivery [36–38].

In light of the above facts, it is critical to identify specific targeting signatures on piRBCs arising from Plasmodium invasion and the upregulation of nutrient receptors to meet piRBCs’ high energy requirements. L-NSCs can bind specifically to these targets, enabling precise and effective delivery of antimalarial drugs to piRBCs. Recent reviews have highlighted the potential of nanotechnology, emphasizing passive distribution and reticuloendothelial uptake rather than specific recognition and delivery in piRBCs [39–41]. Researchers have recently focused their efforts on L-NSCs targeting piRBCs. The NSCs, including liposomes, nanoparticles, micelles, and nanoemulsions, are functionalized using antibodies, heparin, or chondroitin 4-sulfate, and ferrites [42, 43]. The ligand and piRBCs surface signatures interact selectively, enabling active targeting. Figure 1 illustrates the major classes of L-NSCs, including polymeric, lipidic, polymer–lipid hybrid, metallic, immunoliposomal, and self-microemulsifying drug delivery systems, which have been developed to overcome the limitations of conventional antimalarial therapy. NSCs enable passive accumulation in red blood cells and prolonged circulation, while L-NSCs achieve active targeting of Plasmodium-infected red blood cells [44]. Selective targeting can be achieved through knob-like protrusions, membrane fluidity, nutrient requirements, or expression of parasite-specific proteins on the surface of piRBCS [45]. Recent advances in the development of L-NSCs employing different targeting strategies have been extensively evaluated using in vitro and in vivo models, which are discussed in the following sections. This review provides in-depth insight into emerging opportunities for developing ligand-decorated NSCs, particularly the selection of ligands based on the mechanisms underlying Plasmodium-induced remodeling of piRBCs, which generates distinct surface signatures desirable for targeted antimalarial drug delivery.

Challenges in conventional ACT therapy

ACT formulations are associated with poor pharmacokinetic compatibility, a short half-life, and partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives, leading to delayed parasite clearance [46]. Early artemisinin resistance prevents ACTs from clearing the early ring stage of trophozoites, thereby delaying complete parasite clearance. Intra-parasitic heme-iron catalyzes the endoperoxide bridges of Artemisinin derivatives and produces free radicals that are essential for antimalarial activity [47]. However, mutations in the Kelch 13 propeller domain of P. falciparum reduce hemoglobin endocytosis, thereby slowing artemisinin activation [48–50]. The precise biological role of K13 and the specific mechanisms by which its mutations enhance survival responses to artemisinin derivatives remain unidentified [51]. Additionally, enhanced cell stress response and mitochondrial metabolism protect the mutated parasite from damage [52, 53]. It results in delayed parasite clearance, de novo resistance to partner drugs, and ultimately, therapeutic failure of ACTs. To address the shortcomings of current first-line antimalarial therapies, various approaches, including alterations in dosing schedules [54, 55], hybrid compounds [56, 57], combination therapy [58], chemical modifications [59], and the substitution of synthetic derivatives [60], are employed to improve solubility, enhance oral bioavailability, and deliver drugs to target tissues or cells [61, 62]. In one clinical trial, a short-course, higher-dose artesunate-sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine regimen (6 mg/kg/d for 7 days) showed no pharmacodynamic advantage, even in areas with rising artemisinin resistance [63, 64]. These strategies suffer from inherent limitations, including limited efficacy gains, suboptimal plasma drug concentrations, and emerging treatment resistance.

Existing NSC systems

Diverse nanotechnology-based platforms, including antimalarial-loaded liposomes, nanoparticles (polymeric, lipid-based, and metallic), self-microemulsifying delivery systems, microemulsions, and quantum dots, have been explored to enhance drug solubility and bioavailability [65–69]. The critical attributes of different NSCs are presented in Table 2. Liposomes are the preferred delivery choice due to their low toxicity, biodegradability, and biocompatibility, making them a promising platform for water-soluble and lipid-soluble antimalarial drugs [70]. However, the cost, the need for parental route, and stability make liposomes less preferable than other NSC forms. The co-artemether and lumefantrine-loaded liposomes have been explored for their rapid onset of action via the parenteral route and their reduced propensity to cause hemolysis. These liposomes have been reported to be safer and to lack hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity [71, 72, 73]. Antimalarial-loaded convetional NSCs have been developed to improve solubility and enhance oral bioavailability, thereby reducing the recommended dose up to 1/10th [74, 75]. Nanoparticles developed employing polymers, lipids, metals, and inorganic materials have also been shown to enhance circulation time and controlled release, as proven by both in vitro and in vivo studies. Poly (d, l-lactic-co-glycolic acid)-entrapped artesunate and artemisinin nanoparticles have shown superior parasite suppression than the free drugs [76]. Nanoparticles achieve higher drug-loading efficiency and exhibit a more pronounced antimalarial effect [77]. The antimalarial activity of Dihydroartemisinin entrapped solid lipid nanoparticles has been evidenced to be enhanced both in vitro (IC50 0.25 ng/ml) and in vivo (97.24% chemosuppression at 2 mg/kg/day. Similarly, artemisinin-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles have been shown to improve pharmacokinetic profiles, including enhanced half-life (4.03-fold), maximum retention time (3.27-fold), and AUC(0 − t) (2.91-fold). It resulted in the in vivo drug persistence and extended its antimalarial action [78].

Comparative evaluation of different nanostructured carrier systems in malaria

Lipid-based formulations, such as self-emulsifying drug delivery systems, nanoemulsions, microemulsions, and micelles, have also been extensively studied for the delivery of antimalarials, particularly ACTs. Artesunate is effective in treating Swiss mice infected with Plasmodium yoelii nigeriensis, even at a lower dosage for 5 days [79, 80]. In addition to conventional NSCs, several other delivery systems, such as quantum dots and solid dispersions, have also been explored. Efforts were also made using novel drug delivery systems. A novel solid dispersion formulation of Ganaplacide (KAF156) and lumefantrine has been reported to have therapeutic potential equivalent to the conventional artemether-lumefantrine regimen and to facilitate a single daily dose [81]. Artesunate-mefloquine-graphene quantum dots have been reported to exhibit enhanced effectiveness against cultured P. falciparum and reduced toxicity [82]. Table 2 examines the development of conventional NSCs, which demonstrate higher efficacy in both in vitro and preclinical studies. Artemisinin-loaded transferrin-conjugated NSCs have been used to deliver cargo to a brain cancer cell line, thereby supporting their role as an antimalarial and anticancer drug delivery system [83].

Recent studies have demonstrated significant improvements in the pharmacokinetic profiles of antimalarials when delivered through NSCs compared with conventional delivery systems [84–87]. Despite encouraging preclinical outcomes, these systems largely exhibit off-target drug exposure and limited selectivity for piRBCs, limiting their ability to fully address therapeutic failure and drug resistance. NSCs rely on passive targeting, which delivers the drug through natural biodistribution driven by enhanced permeability and retention due to pathological alteration. The passive targeting results in improved pharmacokinetic profiles, extended circulation times, and physicochemical interactions with piRBCs. Such challenges require a more effective approach to selectively target piRBCs.

Mechanistic insights into targeting Plasmodium-infected red blood cells

Targeting Plasmodium-infected red blood cells requires exploiting the significant structural, metabolic, and antigenic modifications of the erythrocyte membrane during the intraerythrocytic parasite cycle [88]. Some of the targeting receptors expressed on the epirBCs, such as PfEMR1 and RIFIN/STEVOR, have been explored. piRBCs undergo stage-dependent membrane modifications that provide parasite-specific docking sites for ligand-functionalized nanocarriers, unlike uninfected RBCs. The plasma membrane of RBCs contains approximately 20 prominent and 875 minor proteins, primarily involved in protein binding, signal transduction, catalysis, glycolysis, and antioxidant functions. These proteins are integral membrane proteins, extracellular proteins, and cytoskeletal proteins [89]. The normal RBC cytoskeleton comprises a framework of complex proteins like spectrin, actin, protein 4.1, ankyrin, and actin-associated proteins. proteins on the RBC. Glycophorins are membrane proteins and act as receptors for invading parasites, bacteria, and viruses. The Plasmodium parasite profoundly alters the structural and physiological processes of RBCs, enabling it to invade and grow within them [90]. Significant alterations in RBC morphology and rheological properties lead to increased rigidity and reduced deformability. P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) is expressed on the surface of piRBCs, where it directly binds to the knob-associated His-rich proteins [91, 92]. This interaction induces protrusion formation on the surface of late-stage parasite-infected red blood cells (piRBCs). The extracellular domains of PfEMP1 then engage with various host receptors, leading to the cytoadherence of infected piRBCs to endothelial cells [89, 93, 94]. The abundance of sialylated glycoproteins on the RBC membrane confers a negative charge, resulting in a negative zeta potential. It attracts the positively charged lipid or polymeric delivery systems. It enhances endothelial cell adhesiveness, including the vascular endothelium [93]. piRBCs can also expose membrane-bound phosphatidylserine (PS), and everted PS can be used as a specific marker to distinguish piRBCs from normal RBCs [95]. Other proteins, such as RIFIN/STEVOR (Mr ~ 37 kDa), which are expressed during the later stage and involved in antigenic variation, can target RBCs. Figure 2A depicts the receptors on normal RBCs and related ligands on Plasmodium parasites that can be explored for site-specific targeting of piRBCs. During intra-erythrocytic development, P. falciparum expresses knob structures and variant surface antigens on the surface of piRBCs (Fig. 2B). Further, overexpression of glucose transporter (GLUT1) has been explored for targeting piRBC. Glucose-functionalized L-NSCs can deliver antimalarials into piRBCs via the GLUT-mediated transporter [96]. In another approach. Sialoglycoprotein (Glycophorin A), which contains terminal sialic acid residues, was explored by developing sialic acid–binding peptides, lectins, or engineered antibodies functionalized on L-NSCs.

Selective piRBCs targeting approaches

Active targeting is design driven strategy that employ specific ligand-receptor approach to identify molecular receptor and deliver cargo in guided and selective manner. Ligand decorated NSCs involve active targeting strategy which specifically deliver drug to piRBCs. L-NSCs have functionbalized ligand (antibody, heparin, aptamer, sugar, small molecules) which is capable of differentiating between normal and piRBCs. The overexpression of receptors on piRBCs have been explored for targeting and delivery of antimalarials. Antibody modification

Liposomal nanocarriers have been functionalized with appropriately oriented monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies to selectively recognize Plasmodium-induced surface signatures, including PfEMP1 and BM1234 antigens, and host erythrocyte membrane proteins, such as glycophorin A and B, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Urban et al. (2010) reported that chloroquine could successfully remove 26.7% ± 1.8% of peripheral red blood cells at a dosage 10 times lower than its IC50 in a solution when decorated with immune liposomes [97]. The lumefantrine-loaded liposomes were functionalized using polyclonal antibodies to target piRBCs. In an in vitro culture study, these immunoliposomes showed 70% growth inhibition across all parasitic forms, thereby broadening their spectrum of activity. They can serve as advanced delivery systems for malaria therapeutics [98]. Immunoliposomes functionalized with glycophorin A have the potential to target both gametocytes and piRBCs. Liposomes targeted with glycophorin A as a targeting receptor inhibited parasite growth by 50% [99]. In a recent study, liposome-like self-assembly of dimeric artesunate glycerophosphocholine conjugates (DAGC) exhibited a higher in vivo half-life (approx. 10 h) than artesunate (1.5 h). This liposome-like self-assembly was explored for its efficacy against K13-mutant artemisinin-resistant malaria parasites, with a reduced IC50 value, representing outstanding in vitro antiplasmodial activity without causing hemolysis [100]. Overall, the developed immuniliposomes have shown improved efficacy at lower doses, targeting all stages of the Plasmodium parasite, including gametocytes and artemisinin-resistant Plasmodium parasites.

Polymer adhesion

Nanoparticles (NPs) developed using polymers (poly(amidoamines), or metallic (Gold), inorganic (Ferrite), and lipid-based materials have been investigated for their selective targeting potentials both in vitro and in vivo in mouse models. These nanoparticles have a higher drug-loading capacity, are stable, and exhibit the least toxicity.

The poly(amidoamine)-based nanoparticles, with a hydrodynamic diameter of 7 nm, exhibit a remarkable drug-carrying capacity and effectively inhibit the in vitro growth of P. falciparum (IC50 = 13.7 µM). Furthermore, these NPs have shown promising results in P. yoelii-infected mice in which their specific and subcellular targeting potential of polymeric nanoparticles was confirmed, and it was found that poly(amidoamines)- polymers preferentially adhere to piRBCs and target Plasmodium at the subcellular level [101].

Pluronic® polymer and 2,2-bis(hydroxymethyl)propionic acid (bis-MPA) dendritic derivatives have been studied for their antimalarial action against Plasmodium yoelii in an animal model and P. falciparum in vitro. In vitro, the IC50 of CQ and PQ was reduced by 3- and 4-fold, to 4.0 nm and 1.1 μm, respectively [102]. In another approach, polymeric nanoparticles loaded with dihydroartemisinin and coated with RBCs exhibited higher inhibition ratios. Key advantages include effective malaria treatment, prolonged residence time in the bloodstream, specific targeting of piRBCs, and biocompatibility [103].

The artemisinin-loaded, heparin-coated hollow mesoporous ferrite nanoparticles measuring 200 nm have been investigated for their specific targeting potential [104]. These nanoparticles specifically bind to piRBCs because hemozoin (Paramagnetic) is more concentrated during the parasite’s schizont stage (Fig. 4). The unique interaction between heparin and parasite-derived ligands further amplifies the inhibitory effect on malaria, with heparin-coated nanoparticles exhibiting greater blocking activity than free heparin [105]. Based on the higher nutrient uptake in piRBCs, human serum albumin (HSA) and Glucose were decorated onto NPs to enhance the delivery of antimalarials. The Artemether-loaded HSA nanoparticles were developed to actively absorb nutrients, piRBCs, for their intra-erythrocytic growth. The IC50 of Artemether-loaded human serum albumin nanoparticles developed to target piRBCs was reduced to 50% compared to free artemether [106]. In another approach, the ultra-small gold nanoparticles or nanoclusters were decorated with glucose and targeted to the cysteine-rich domains of merozoites or GLUT1 domain on piRBCs [107](Fig. 4). The lower IC50 value (27.4 ± 3.8 µM) of ultra-small gold nanoparticles compared to free ciprofloxacin (157.9 ± 58.8 µM) indicated potential for targeting piRBCs, which was further confirmed by CLSM imaging and flow cytometry [108]. Thus, NPs developed using a broader class of nanomaterials have shown significant potential for improving therapeutic efficacy, yet limited studies have been conducted in vivo animal models.

Biofilm coating

Nanoemulsions coated with a targeting ligand and micelles developed using supramagnetic nanoparticles, bearing glucose as the targeting ligand, have been evaluated for improved efficacy and complete parasite clearance. In a recent study, a novel nanotargeting drug delivery system (HEP@ART@NEs) was developed, with heparin on the outer coat and artemisinin as the core. Heparin showed multivalent interactions with piRBCs and the merozoite surface protein of the Plasmodium parasite in systemic circulation. Both in vitro and in vivo studies (in a rodent model infected with P. berghei ANKA) demonstrated enhanced survival and are recommended as a targeted delivery system for antimalarial therapy [109]. Glucose/quinine/SPIO micelles have also demonstrated precise tracking and effective treatment through selective targeting and a higher drug load within the Plasmodium parasite (Fig. 4).

Biomimetic-mediation

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are present in multiple body fluids, especially blood plasma, and range in size from 40 nm to 1000 nm. The vesicles contain host and parasite cell proteins and may play a role in intercellular communication [110, 111]. The presence of adhesion molecules and surface ligands enhances the inherent cell and tissue-targeting properties of EVs. EVs are now considered promising vehicles for delivering drugs to particular organs or cells and have been explored for malaria drug delivery. Naive and Plasmodium falciparum-infected RBCs have been utilized to isolate and characterize EVs. Drug delivery vehicles can use piRBC-EVs, which are more readily absorbed than RBC-EVs. When atovaquone and tafenoquine were injected into piRBC-EVs, they showed greater in vitro growth inhibition than their free-drug counterparts. Overall, atovaquone- and tafenoquine-loaded EVs suppressed P. falciparum growth more effectively, suggesting enhanced efficacy [112]. Despite their potential to target piRBCs, EVs encounter clinical translational challenges owing to their biological origin. It includes complex isolation processes, difficulties in large-scale production, and standardization. Additionally, concerns regarding the risk of parasitic biomolecule transfer, immunogenicity, and stability further limit their applicability.

Regulatory aspects

Ligand-decorated nanostructured carrier systems offer a promising avenue, yet their complexity in development, manufacturing, and evaluation poses significant challenges for scientific and regulatory purposes [113–115]. Figure 5 illustrates major regulatory guidelines from 2007 to 2025 and the approval of NSCs across different therapeutics classes since the 1950s. The early regulatory documents, comprising task force reports issued by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and later reflection papers on iron nanocolloidal formulations, were developed by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to expedite the approval of L-NSCs [116]. In India, “Guidelines for Evaluation of Nanopharmaceuticals in India” [117] and “Guidelines and Best Practices for Safe Handling of Nanomaterials in Research Laboratories and Industries” provide the safe handling and their evaluation for quality, safety, and efficacy, and are further specified in the Second Schedule of the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019 [118, 119]. L-NSCs are considered “Investigational New Drugs”, and regulatory aspects on safety, quality, and efficacy should be evaluated. It is also recommended that pharmacovigilance be conducted throughout the lifecycle of L-NSCs. Challenges in the development of L-NSCs

L-NSCs have demonstrated advantages in antimalarial therapy, improving therapeutic potential and safety profile, while reducing side effects. However, cost implications, scalability, accessibility, and large-scale production and adoption remain major hurdles, particularly in malaria-endemic impoverished countries [120]. The translational potential of L-NSCs has also been hindered by ligand stability and targeting efficiency due to heterogeneity in the expression of binding receptors on piRBCs. Addressing such obstacles, employing a targeted quality-driven, greener approach to production and cost-effectiveness in tackling resistant malaria should also be considered for overall management of malaria [121, 122]. In selective delivery of antimalarials to piRBCs, single ligand selection may be drastically limited by the antigenic diversity and the fact that Plasmodium-induced erythrocyte surface signatures are only expressed at certain stages. To date, the studies have been conducted in rodent malaria models that may not fully simulate human conditions such as cytoadherence, splenic clearance, or sequestration. Another major challenge with NSCs, such as formulation complexity, scale-up constraints, regulatory obstacles, and operational feasibility and stability in tropical ecosystems, must be addressed during formulation development.

Conclusion

Recent advances in targeted drug-delivery approaches represent the state of the art for developing NSCs to enhance drug accumulation in the desired site, thereby improving local drug concentration while minimizing systemic exposure to off-target tissues. Targeted NSCs preferentially engage piRBCs via parasite-induced membrane remodeling, altered permeability pathways, or selective nutrient uptake mechanisms, thereby overcoming intracellular delivery barriers, reducing therapeutic doses, and prolonging the clinical effectiveness of recommended antimalarial drugs particuarly ACTs. In the present review, the scope of NSCs and their mechanistic approaches in targeting piRBCs has been explored. Antimalarial-loaded NSCs have been explored for their ability to reduce IC50, increase AUC and elimination half-life, clear drug-resistant P. falciparum, possess gametocidal potential, and also target the extracellular stages of Plasmodium. It is important to select the ligand capable of differentiating between Plasmodium-infected and normal RBCs to maximize therapeutic efficacy, minimize toxicity, and mitigate the development of resistance in next-generation antimalarial drug delivery systems. NSCs will enhance safety and efficacy, mitigate ACT resistance, facilitate the development of conventional dosage forms, and protect the drug from degradation, thereby enabling site-specific drug delivery. NSCs can be an effective tool for treating human and simian malaria [123, 124]. However, biosafety-related challenges, particularly against extracellular vesicles, should be taken into account prior to their clinical application [125–127]. Overall, the recent effort to specifically recognize piRBCs employing site-specific ligands opens new avenues for targeting Plasmodium at the extracellular and intracellular stages. The targeted drug delivery systems can increase parasite exposure to lethal ACT doses for longer periods and reduce the emergence of drug resistance.

Way forward

L-NSCs have been evaluated as an intriguing antimalarial strategy that relies on rational ligand selection, stage-specific delivery, and mechanistic validation of piRBC selectivity. The scalability, operational feasibility, and regulatory compliance of L-NSCs are crucial for the successful transformation to the clinical stage. While challenges remain in the developmental phase of various types of L-NSCs, Immunoliposomes, nutrient-decorated NPs developed from metallic, polymeric, lipids, and inorganic materials, nanoemusions have shown advanced opportunities for researchers. More focus should be on the targeting moitieis capable of recognizing broady expressed and conserved region across multiple stages of piRBCs.
