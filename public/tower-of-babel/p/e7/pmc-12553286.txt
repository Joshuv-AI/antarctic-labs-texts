VaMiAnalyzer: an open source, Python-based application for analysis of 3D in vitro vasculogenic mimicry assays
Authors: Stephen P. G. Moore, Anqi Zou, Xinyu Zhang, Olivia Chika Jonathan, Deborah Lang, Chao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVasculogenic mimicry (VM) is the phenomenon whereby non-vascular tumor cells develop vascular-like structures. VM is linked to more aggressive tumor phenotypes including higher rates of metastasis and invasion and is potentially resistant to anti-angiogenic cancer therapies. VM is investigated in vitro using 3D assays with microscopy images capturing the resulting VM structures, including loops, branch points, and tubes. The standard method to quantify endpoint data is to count various structural features manually, which is time-consuming and open to bias. At present, no software solutions have been developed to specifically address the analysis and quantification of VM structures.ResultsTo address this limitation, we developed an open source, Python-based application, VaMiAnalyzer, allowing straightforward quantification of several VM structural features. The application follows a two-step approach that optionally corrects and enhances the raw input images and then analyzes and quantifies the VM features.ConclusionsVaMiAnalyzer is stand-alone software that allows automated measurement of VM structural features from phase-contrast microscopy images. It produces results that are strongly consistent with manual counts but in a significantly shorter time, allowing quick, non-biased analysis of VM from microscopy images.

Background

Tumors require a constant blood supply to bring nutrients and oxygen, as well as remove waste products. This is achieved through the process of angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels from a pre-existing vascular network, which is considered a hallmark of cancer [1]. In contrast, vasculogenic mimicry (VM) is the phenomenon where existing, non-vascular tumor cells can form their own tube/loop structures allowing transportation of blood throughout the tumor [2, 3]. Essentially, the tumor cells modify their expression profile to form endothelial cell-free (non-angiogenic) tube and loop structures [2, 4]. VM occurs in highly aggressive tumors and is correlated with increased metastasis and lower patient survival [5]. Furthermore, VM is resistant to anti-angiogenic therapies and provides a new route for cancer cells to escape therapeutic treatment and metastasize [6, 7].

A common method to investigate VM formation is to perform in vitro assays, where cancer cells are grown on a growth-factor reduced 3D matrix of collagen or fibrin, typically in the absence of nutrients and/or oxygen, within 96-well cell culture plates [8]. The resulting VM structures are imaged by phase-contrast or fluorescence microscopy. VM assays are based on tube formation or angiogenesis assays, which use endothelial cells grown on 3D matrix to investigate vascularization processes [9, 10]. To assess angiogenesis in vitro, several open source and commercial image analysis programs have been developed that can analyze and quantify vascular structures using parameters such as the number of branching points, tube number, and loop number (reviewed in Pereira, M. et al. [11]).

During VM image analysis, many researchers rely on manually counting VM structures from microscope images [12–19], a process that is both time-consuming and susceptible to unintentional bias. Some research groups have adopted commercial software designed for angiogenesis analysis, such as WimTube [20–23] (https://www.wimasis.com), a cloud-based analysis tool originally designed for tube formation assays, while others have utilized ImageJ plugins such as Angiognesis Analyzer (AA) [24–26] or AngioTool [27] to perform VM analysis. However, VM assays result in less uniform vascular structures compared to angiogenesis assays, due to VM originating from tumor cells that do not normally contribute to vascular development. Furthermore, tumor cells (unlike endothelial cells) continue to proliferate and form multilayered structures, leading to uneven focus in microscopy images. Due to these constraints, image analysis solutions designed for angiogenesis assays may work poorly on VM images and fail to adequately and successfully quantitate VM-specific characteristics. Despite this, no software solutions have been developed to specifically address the analysis and quantification of VM structures. To analyze VM quickly and accurately, new methodologies are needed. Here we describe an open source, Python-based image analysis program that can quickly analyze and quantify VM structures in microscope images using several parameters, without the potential addition of unintentional user bias.

Implementation

Image generation

Human melanoma cell line MEL537 (CVCL 8052) was cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS (Corning) without addition of antibiotics. Histological analysis labeling DNA with DAPI fluorochrome was used to verify lack of mycoplasma contamination.

VM assays were performed as previously described [19]. Briefly, 50 µL of growth factor reduced Matrigel® matrix (Corning) was added to the wells of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37°C for one hour to solidify. MEL537 tumor cells resuspended in DMEM without addition of FBS were added at 2 × 104 cells per well and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 24 h. Four images were captured from random positions in each well using an Olympus QColor 3 camera mounted to an Olympus CKX41 microscope system through a 10x objective.

The study employed a comprehensive image analysis approach to quantify VM structures from microscope images (Fig. 1). Raw images were saved as .TIF with the option for image correction due to lighting and uneven shading.

Image correction and enhancement

The 3D structures formed in VM assays can result in images losing focus or having an uneven background, leading to biased quantification based on partial images (Fig. 1). This can affect analysis through both manual measurements and existing software designed for angiogenesis assay analysis. Image correction is often necessary to ensure measurement quality and consistency. Each image underwent background and shading correction using the BaSiC algorithm [28], which effectively normalized illumination and reduced artifacts commonly present in microscopic images. This correction ensured that variations in image brightness and shading did not affect subsequent analyses. Additionally, mean and standard deviation values of the images were adjusted to target values to maintain uniformity across all samples. An optional local contrast enhancement was applied to some images to improve the visibility of fine details by adjusting the contrast within small regions of the image. This enhancement utilized Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to highlight subtle features that are crucial for accurate structure identification.

Structure detection and image binarization

This step involved a structure detection procedure; the corrected images were meticulously analyzed to identify and quantify VM tube structures. Prior to detection, each image underwent an intensity normalization process to reduce inter-sample variability in brightness and contrast. Specifically, the image was adjusted to a standardized appearance by linearly transforming its pixel intensities to match a target mean and standard deviation (mean = 95, std = 14). The desired values were estimated from a subset of high-quality reference images, selected for their representative tube structures and consistent illumination. The average pixel intensity statistics of these reference images were computed and used as the desired normalization parameters. Applying image normalization (adjusting to a uniform mean and standard deviation) helps reduce brightness and contrast variations caused by differences in acquisition conditions, thereby improving the consistency and robustness of subsequent structure detection.

After normalization, the image was converted to a binary format using a sliding window approach that employed the local variance method to optimize the detection of relevant structures. This method calculates the local variance of pixel intensities within a moving window to distinguish structure-rich regions from background extra-cellular matrix components. Specifically, a square sliding window (typically 20 × 20 pixels, adjustable between 10 × 10 and 50 × 50 based on image resolution and expected structure scale) was moved across the image with a configurable step size (default: 5 pixels; tunable between 3 and 25). For each window, the local standard deviation of pixel intensities was computed. If the standard deviation exceeded a predefined threshold (default: 8; tunable from 5 to 15), the corresponding region was flagged as potentially containing tube structures. These parameter values were selected and refined through empirical testing on a subset of annotated images, aiming to maximize sensitivity to true VM structures while reducing false positives.

Following the local variance thresholding, a binary mask was generated by setting pixels within high-variance regions to 1 (foreground) and others to 0 (background). To further refine the detection, a connected component analysis was applied to the binary mask, and only components exceeding a minimum area threshold (default: 3000 pixels; adjustable between 500 and 3000) were retained as valid VM structures. The area threshold can be adjusted to ensure biologically meaningful detection while minimizing the impact of noise.

Altogether, this approach enabled robust identification of VM tube structures based on localized intensity variation, while the adjustable parameters (window size, step size, standard deviation threshold, area threshold) allowed adaptation to varying image characteristics. By normalizing image intensity distributions to a consistent mean and standard deviation prior to detection, we reduced the influence of illumination differences and contrast variability across samples. This normalization step not only improved the consistency of detection but also enhanced the stability and generalizability of parameter settings, enabling the algorithm to maintain robust performance across diverse image conditions. This strategy ensured high sensitivity to true structures while minimizing false positives caused by background heterogeneity.

Graph construction and simplification

Once the binary images were generated, we constructed a graph based on the VM structure for subsequent quantitative analysis. Initially, binary images were skeletonized to highlight the tube structures, a process that extracted the medial axis of an object. Skeletonization effectively transforms the binary images into thin representations of the original structures, maintaining the topology and critical pathways of the vascular-like formations.

To create a detailed and accurate graph of these structures, a Depth-First Search (DFS) method was employed to traverse the skeleton. This traversal allowed us to systematically explore and map the entire network of vascular-like formations. During the DFS traversal, we identified and marked the key points within the skeleton, which were defined as nodes in the graph. These key points included two types of features:

Endpoints: skeleton pixels with only one neighbor (i.e., degree 1), typically corresponding to the termini of tubular branches.

Junctions (branch points): skeleton pixels with three or more neighbors (i.e., degree ≥ 3), representing intersections or bifurcations in the network.

Once key points were identified, edges were defined as the segments of the skeleton connecting these nodes. Each edge represented a continuous segment of the vascular-like skeleton between two nodes, effectively capturing the internal pathways within the network. By constructing the graph in this manner, we achieved a comprehensive representation of the tube structures, suitable for further analysis and quantification.

To simplify and refine the graph, we merged nodes that were in close proximity to each other, based on a predefined threshold distance. The default threshold distance for merging nodes was set to 5 pixels and is tunable within the range of 3 to 15 pixels, depending on the resolution and density of the vascular-like structures in the image. This merging process reduced the complexity of the graph by eliminating redundant nodes and consolidating the network into a more streamlined structure. Following node merging, small terminal branches were pruned. This step identifies terminal edges (i.e., those connected to nodes of degree 1) and removes any edge whose length is shorter than the given threshold. The default minimum terminal branch value was set to 200 pixels, with a tunable range of 100 to 300 pixels. This range was determined empirically based on human-annotated datasets, aiming to eliminate spurious short extensions without compromising valid microvascular-like structures.

Loops, branch points, and tube structure quantification

Through these steps—skeletonization, DFS traversal, node merging, and branch pruning—we constructed a clear and interpretable graph of the vascular-like structures. This graph provided a robust framework for subsequent quantitative analyses, ensuring that the key features of the vascular-like network were accurately represented and easily interpretable.

The final graph underwent detailed analysis to count loops, branch points, and tube structures, providing a comprehensive quantification of VM formation. This analysis involved identifying the number of loops, which are indicative of network complexity, as well as counting branch points that represent intersections within the tube network. Additionally, the total number of tube structures was quantified to assess the extent of VM within the samples.

Loops within the graph were identified using NetworkX’s minimum_cycle_basis function, which employs the algorithm of Kavitha et al. [29]. The algorithm finds the minimum cycle basis by iteratively considering all cycles formed by adding each edge to the shortest path between its endpoints and selecting the smallest set of independent cycles that form a basis for all cycles in the graph. The algorithm starts by calculating the shortest paths between all pairs of nodes. For each edge, it forms a cycle by combining the edge with the shortest path between its endpoints. The algorithm iteratively adds the shortest candidate cycle that is linearly independent of the previously selected cycles until a complete basis for the cycle space is obtained. The resulting loops were filtered to retain only those with at least two branch points, ensuring that only significant loops contributing to the tubular network’s complexity were counted.

Branches were identified by examining the degree of each node in the graph, with nodes having more than two edges considered as branches. To refine this identification, a distance-based merging parameter was used to merge nodes that were very close to each other, reducing redundancy and ensuring accurate representation. The distance threshold for merging was set to 50 and can be adjusted within the range of 20 to 100 based on image resolution and tube structure density. The algorithm checked the degree of each node and merged those within the specified distance to avoid over-counting closely spaced branches. This process enhanced the accuracy of branch point identification, providing a realistic depiction of the tubular network’s complexity.

The number of tube structures was determined by counting the edges in the pruned graph that exceeded a certain length threshold. This threshold was set to 120 and can be adjusted within the range of 50 to 200 depending on the specific requirements and characteristics of the images being analyzed. The threshold ensured that only significant tubular formations were considered. Each edge in the graph represents a tube segment between two nodes, and by setting a minimum length threshold, short and potentially insignificant segments were excluded from the count. The total number of tube structures was calculated by summing up these qualifying edges, providing a measure of the network’s extent.

The results were visualized and annotated to clearly display the identified structures and the statistical data regarding the number of loops, branches, and tubes. This visualization included annotated images showing the identified tube structures, branches, and loops, facilitating a clear understanding of VM formation in the analyzed images. The visualization process involved overlaying the identified graph structures on the original images, with distinct markers and color-coding for loops, branches, and tubes. Statistical summaries were also generated to provide a comprehensive view of the quantitative data. This provided a clear depiction of the vascular network’s complexity and spatial distribution within the samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2). Initial power analysis for a one-way ANOVA was used to estimate the optimal number of images needed to detect significant differences between three measurement methods with > 80% power at a p-value ≤ 0.05. This analysis indicated that a total of 36.7 images were required, which was rounded to 40 images.

However, assumption tests revealed that some data were not always normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk test; loops p = 0.0307 [Manual count], tubes p = 0.0368 [VaMiAnalyzer] for branches, normal distribution was met for all measurement methods). Additionally, the assumption of equal variances was not always met (Levene’s test; loops had equal variance, branches p = 1.665 × 10− 6, tubes p = 0.619 × 10− 5). Due to these violations of ANOVA assumptions, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was chosen as a more appropriate method.

To confirm that 40 images would still provide sufficient power for detecting significant differences using the Kruskal-Wallis test, a Monte Carlo simulation was performed with 10,000 iterations, for three measurement methods, and a significance level of p ≤ 0.05 using the observed mean and standard deviations [30]. Results indicated that the Kruskal-Wallis test with 40 images still achieved a power level of >80%.

The Kruskal-Wallis test was performed on all three parameters (loops, branches and tubes). For post-hoc comparison, Dunn’s test with Holm correction for multiple comparisons was then applied to all three parameters.

Results and discussion

VM assays are an in vitro method used to measure the formation of VM by aggressive cancer cells, and to test the impact that inhibition of target gene/s, or potential therapeutic compounds have on this phenomenon. Due to a lack of suitable VM-specific analysis tools, VM measurements are often implemented by manually counting structures or using software solutions developed for angiogenesis assays. We developed VaMiAnalyzer to enable the automated and unbiased quantification of VM structures from 3D VM assay images (Fig. 1). To test the functionality of VaMiAnalyzer we performed VM assays using MEL537 melanoma cells in standard 96-well plate based Matrigel® VM assays; images were taken from random points within several wells. Since VM assays are typically performed in 96-well plates and multiple images are taken per well [1], corner shadowing from the sides of the wells can occur (Fig. 1, picture 1). To counter this, image correction and enhancement of the raw input image by VaMiAnalyzer can be performed to produce uniform shading, brightness, and contrast across the entire image (Fig. 1, picture 2). However, if no shading is evident in images, the correction and enhancement feature may be omitted. VM structural analysis by VaMiAnalyzer measures and quantifies three key features: the number of loop structures, branching points, and tube structures (Fig. 1, picture 3). Quantified results are outputted as csv documents to the specified output folder with count data for each structural type per image, thereby allowing for easy averaging and statistical analysis. Several software solutions originally developed for analyzing angiogenesis assays have also been used for VM image analysis including the commercial cloud-based tube formation analysis tool WimTube [20–23] and the ImageJ plugin AA [24–26]. To compare the performance of these tools on VM image analysis, images (157.tif from data set is shown as an example, Fig. 2) were uploaded to VaMiAnalyzer, WimTube, and AA (version 1.0) [31] (Fig. 2A-C) and compared to manual counting results. All three software tools were operated using default settings. The output images from WimTube and AA remained in the original 8-bit color format (Fig. 2B, C), indicating that image preprocessing is not a feature of these programs. For image 157.tif, both manual counting and VaMiAnalzyer detected 5 loops in the image, whereas WimTube reported 8 loops, and AA reported 22 loops. For branches, manual counting reported 16, VaMiAnalyzer 14, WimTube 41, and AA 171, while manual counting reported 29 tubes, VaMiAnalyzer 33, WimTube 87, and AA reported 350. The substantial discrepancies in results between manual counting or VaMiAnalyzer with those of the WimTube and AA programs are likely due to the original design intent of these applications. Both WimTube and AA were developed for quantifying tube formation in angiogenesis assays using endothelial cells and may not accurately analyze images of highly proliferative tumor cells, which grow in a non-uniform manner. For example, WimTube identified multiple loops where only one loop existed and counted a loop within a solid cell mass (Fig. 2B, loop numbers 3,4,8). Likewise, AA detected several loop structures within a solid cell mass that should instead be classified as part of a tube (Fig. 2C, indicated by white arrows) and overestimated branches by 10X and tubes by 12X respectively, compared to manual counting.

For analysis with the WimTube tool, images were uploaded directly to the site since the tool does not provide any additional parameters or settings, and users simply submit their images and receive results. Additionally, there is no available documentation for the tool, so it is unknown how detection or quantification of the submitted images occurs. These results demonstrate that VaMiAnalyzer is an appropriate method for VM analysis with equivalent results to manual counts. Additionally, adoption of VaMiAnalyzer can save a substantial amount of time. Manual counting of the 40 VM images took an average of 12.1 min per image, whereas VaMiAnalyzer averaged 27.9 s per image (1.8 s average for correction, 26.1 s average for analysis) a reduction of over 11.8 min per image, or ~ 7.8 h for our total 40 image dataset. In short, VaMiAnalyzer provides a robust, easy to use, and automated solution for analysis of 3D VM assays from microscopy images and eliminates the potential for unintentional biasing of results.

Implementation details

VaMiAnalyzer was developed using Python version 3.9. It depends on functions from the Python packages OpenCV, NumPy, BaSiC, Matplotlib, Scikit-image, and NetworkX. The software is platform-independent and easy to install.

On a standard image (2048 width × 1536 height), the full processing pipeline, which includes binarization, graph construction, and quantification, takes approximately 2 s per image for correction and 26 s per image for analyzing on a CPU-only machine (Intel i9, 64GB RAM).

To ensure reproducibility, the source code, user manual, installation instructions, testing data, and tutorial are freely available at GitHub: https://github.com/CZCBLab/VaMiAnalyzer.

Conclusions

VaMiAnalyzer is a stand-alone, Python-based application designed to quantify several structural features that form during VM development in specialized 3D assays. Due to the nonuniformity of VM structural development by tumor cells, pre-existing software developed for similar but more uniform angiogenesis assays does not accurately quantify VM structures, with researchers regularly relying on manual counting instead. This user-friendly application allows automated measurement of VM structures from phase-contrast microscopy images, producing results comparable to manual counting in a significantly shorter time while eliminating the potential biasing of results.
