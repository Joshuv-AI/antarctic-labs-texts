TASER-Induced Femoral Neck Fracture Following Hip Resurfacing Arthroplasty in a Law Enforcement Officer
Authors: Amy Z. Lu, John Eschert, Anand Saluja, Katherine Mallett, Edwin P. Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Femoral neck fractures are a rare complication after hip resurfacing arthroplasty, often associated with technical error. Electrical discharge weapons, such as the Axon TASER 10, can cause violent involuntary muscle contractions. To our knowledge, no prior cases have reported a femoral neck fracture and implant migration due to TASER exposure in a resurfaced hip. We present the case of a 43-year-old law enforcement officer who sustained a femoral neck fracture and movement of his femoral implant following TASER discharge during training, resulting in impingement, shortening, and compensatory pain. The patient ultimately required conversion to total hip arthroplasty. This is the first known report of TASER-induced failure of a resurfacing implant, underscoring the potential for electrical injuries to affect joint prostheses in high-risk populations.

Introduction

Hip resurfacing arthroplasty (HRA) is a bone-preserving surgical option favored for younger, active patients with end-stage hip arthritis [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite its advantages, HRA carries an approximately 1–2% risk of femoral neck fracture, often attributed to technical factors, such as varus component positioning or femoral neck notching, as well as anatomical and geometric factors including head-neck ratio, neck-shaft angle, and hip axis length [[4], [5], [6]]. This complication is specific to HRA compared to total hip arthroplasty (THA) due to preservation of the femoral neck. Electronic control devices (ECDs), such as the Axon TASER 10, deliver rapid, high-voltage pulses capable of inducing involuntary skeletal muscle contractions [[7], [8], [9]]. Although musculoskeletal injuries after ECD exposure are rare, reported cases include femoral neck and vertebral compression fractures [[10], [11], [12], [13]] and myotendinous tendon injuries following stun gun exposure [14].

We present the first known case of a periprosthetic femoral neck fracture in a patient with a previously well-functioning HRA following TASER discharge, leading to varus implant migration and leg shortening, ultimately requiring conversion to THA.

Case History

A 43-year-old male law enforcement officer with no prior medical history underwent right HRA in June 2022 for osteoarthritis after failure of conservative management. Preoperative radiographs demonstrated right hip osteoarthritis with moderate-to-severe joint space narrowing and osteophyte formation (Fig. 1). The index HRA was performed using a posterolateral approach by the same surgeon who later completed the conversion THA. Intraoperatively, the femoral neck was noted to have adequate bone support for uncemented fixation, and a Biomet Magnum acetabular component (56-mm outer diameter/50-mm inner diameter) with a 50-mm Recap femoral resurfacing component (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) was implanted. He recovered uneventfully and resumed unrestricted physical activity, including training, hiking, and weightlifting. In February 2025, during a required training exercise, the patient was exposed to a 5-second discharge from an Axon TASER 10, with barbs contacting the back and right buttock. He experienced immediate, intense right hip pain and was unable to move. He also described a sensation of forced internal rotation of his right leg after the discharge ceased. Resultantly, the patient was unable to extend the hip to achieve a neutral standing position or bear weight, holding the extremity in a shortened, internally rotated posture. Radiographs from that day demonstrated varus migration of the femoral component and cortical irregularity at the femoral neck, consistent with a fracture, without evidence of prosthetic dislocation (Fig. 3). Given the clear radiographic evidence of femoral neck injury and implant migration, advanced imaging was not pursued, as it was not expected to alter the initial management strategy of protected weight-bearing. Following fracture healing but prior to revision surgery, the patient continued to experience substantial pain and functional limitation, with patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) demonstrating a Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score–Joint Replacement (HOOS-JR) of 49.86 and a visual analog scale (VAS) pain score of 8/10.Figure 3Post-TASER radiograph showing femoral component varus malposition with cortical irregularity.

Post-TASER radiograph showing femoral component varus malposition with cortical irregularity.

Initial management was nonoperative, including protected weight-bearing for 9 weeks before the patient progressed to full weight-bearing without an assistive device, indicating presumed healing of the femoral neck fracture. Nonoperative treatment was initially selected given the patient’s young age, apparent fracture stability, and the potential to preserve the existing hip resurfacing construct while allowing the fracture to heal. However, he continued to report persistent right groin and hip pain, exacerbated by rotation, perceived leg length discrepancy (LLD), with the right leg shorter than the left, and compensatory pain in the left hip and lower back. A shoe lift was required to offset the perceived LLD. As part of the preoperative evaluation prior to revision, laboratory studies were obtained to evaluate for occult infection. White blood cell count was within normal limits. Serologic markers for infection were negative, and joint aspiration was not performed. Serum cobalt and chromium levels were not obtained, as there was no clinical or radiographic concern for metallosis.

Given the symptoms and functional limitations, the decision was made to proceed with revision surgery to correct impingement and equalize leg length. The patient underwent anterior-based conversion to THA, consistent with the surgeon’s preferred approach for conversion of hip resurfacing to THA. This approach was selected to facilitate supine assessment of leg lengths and component positioning intraoperatively while allowing adequate exposure for femoral reconstruction and retention of the well-fixed acetabular component. The femoral component was extracted via a subcapital osteotomy, revealing a healed femoral neck fracture with callus formation. The joint fluid was clear, without signs of metallosis. A high-offset uncemented femoral stem with a dual mobility bearing was implanted. The acetabular component was retained. Postoperative radiographs confirmed restored alignment and leg lengths (Fig. 4).Figure 4Postoperative radiograph showing converted THA with restored alignment.

Postoperative radiograph showing converted THA with restored alignment.

At the 6-week postoperative visit, the patient reported improved range of motion, perceived restoration of leg length equality, and improved spinal alignment. He ambulated with a normal heel-toe gait and no limp. The surgical incision was well-healed, and hip range of motion was pain-free (0–100° flexion, 35° external rotation, 5° internal rotation, 40° abduction, 10° adduction) without instability. Mild groin discomfort was elicited with resisted straight leg raise, but muscle strength was full (5/5). Neurologic and vascular exams were normal. Radiographs confirmed well-positioned implants without complication. PROMs included a modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS) of 87, HOOS-JR of 80.55, VAS pain score of 2, and University of California–Los Angeles (UCLA) Activity Score of 10. He was cleared for walking as tolerated, swimming, and using a stationary bike.

At the 3-month postoperative visit, hip range of motion was improved compared to the prior visit (115° flexion, 40° external rotation, 10° internal rotation, 40° abduction, 10° adduction) and was pain-free. There was no LLD or tenderness of the thigh or groin. Importantly, he noted significant improvement in his back pain relative to the preoperative period.

At the 6-month postoperative visit, hip range of motion had further improved compared to the prior visit (120° flexion, 40° external rotation, 10° internal rotation, 40° abduction, 10° adduction). The patient reported slight discomfort during internal rotation and right leg extension assessments. PROMs included an mHHS of 97.9, HOOS-JR of 100, VAS score of 0, and UCLA Activity Score of 10. The patient was unable to run at the time of the visit but was working toward that goal. Overall, the patient reported continued improvement since the 3-month follow-up visit. Radiographs demonstrated unremarkable findings with appropriate implant component positioning (Fig. 5).Figure 5Six-month postoperative radiograph demonstrating unremarkable findings with appropriate implant alignment.

At the 8-month postoperative visit, the patient reported continued gradual recovery with mild residual right hip weakness and positional limitations during dynamic movement but improving endurance and mobility. He had resumed treadmill running, attended the gym daily, and performed modified lower extremity exercises, including squats and lunges, while remaining independent in activities of daily living. Mild lateral hip discomfort persisted with kneeling or floor-based activities, and he continued physical therapy for strength and mobility optimization. On examination, he ambulated without a visible limp, leg lengths were clinically equal, right hip strength was full (5/5) with resisted straight leg raise, and range of motion was pain-free (120° flexion, 45° external rotation, 10° internal rotation, 40° abduction, and 10° adduction). PROMs included an mHHS of 93.5, HOOS-JR of 92.34, VAS pain score of 2, and UCLA Activity Score of 10. Given his near pain-free function, restored biomechanics, and high activity level, he was cleared to gradually resume unrestricted activity, including return to full duty, with recommendation for continued annual radiographic surveillance.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a TASER-induced femoral neck fracture and implant failure following any joint arthroplasty, specifically HRA. Prior studies have attributed femoral neck fractures after HRA to technical factors such as varus component positioning and femoral neck notching, as well as anatomical and geometric parameters including head-neck ratio, neck-shaft angle, and hip axis length [[4], [5], [6]]. In this case, pre-TASER radiographs showed no evidence of femoral neck notching and demonstrated a well-integrated, stable hip resurfacing implant. Post-TASER films showed definite implant migration due to a subcapital femoral neck fracture. Intraoperative identification of fracture callus at the femoral neck supports the fracture as the initiating event leading to implant migration.

The Axon TASER 10 delivers high-voltage electrical pulses through 2 barbs, inducing intense involuntary muscle contractions across the torso and proximal limbs [[7], [8], [9]]. Although musculoskeletal complications after ECD exposure are rare, prior reports demonstrate that the magnitude of electrically induced forces can result in significant injuries even in healthy bone and soft tissue. Winslow et al. first described thoracic vertebral compression fractures directly resulting from TASER discharge, while Sloane et al. reported a similar thoracic compression fracture in a law enforcement officer after a routine 5-second TASER exposure during training, with the proposed mechanism likened to seizure-related axial loading injuries [10,11]. In addition to injuries caused by muscle contraction alone, Mangus et al. described severe craniofacial trauma—including a basilar skull fracture with intracranial hemorrhage requiring craniotomy, as well as comminuted nasal and orbital floor fracture—resulting from uncontrolled falls after TASER-induced neuromuscular incapacitation [13]. In this case, the resulting extreme isometric forces led to a femoral neck fracture. Prior reports have shown that the magnitude of these forces can cause joint dislocations, tendon ruptures, and fractures, even in healthy bone [[10], [11], [12],14,15].

A prior case reported bilateral subcapital femoral neck fractures in a young, fit security guard with no known bone disease after dual TASER exposure [10]. Our case adds to the literature by demonstrating how electrical energy can destabilize a mechanically integrated implant, likely through forceful contraction of the gluteus medius, minimus, iliopsoas, and short external rotators. This likely resulted in a subcapital fracture with subsequent varus malposition of the femoral component.

This case highlights the need to consider joint implant integrity in post-TASER evaluations, particularly in law enforcement or military populations with a higher likelihood of ECD exposure. Surgeons should maintain a high index of suspicion for femoral neck fractures, even in the absence of significant external trauma, when a patient with HRA presents with acute hip pain following electrical discharge.

Summary

TASER discharge can cause rare but clinically significant complications in patients with joint prostheses. This is the first reported case of periprosthetic femoral neck fracture and femoral component migration after HRA due to TASER exposure, ultimately causing limb shortening, impingement, and impaired biomechanics. This led to the decision for revision to THA. Increased awareness of this injury mechanism may guide evaluation and management in similar patient populations. Individuals required to undergo tasing as part of employment training should be made aware of this potential complication, particularly in the setting of HRA or THA.
