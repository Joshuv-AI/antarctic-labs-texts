Effect of postharvest melatonin and salicylic acid applications on quality characteristics and primary and secondary metabolite physiology of tomato fruits
Authors: Tansel Kaygısız, Fatih Sen, Muttalip Gundogdu, Shaghef Ejaz, Erdal Aglar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to investigate the effects of postharvest melatonin (ML) and salicylic acid (SA) applications on fruit quality and biochemical properties in tomatoes. The potential synergistic effects were investigated by applying these compounds to the ‘Depar’ tomato variety grown in Bolu in the 2024 production season. The tomato fruit that were mature and free from physical damage were selected and sterilized according to predetermined criteria. Afterwards, ML and SA solutions (1 mM) were sprayed on each fruit and then the fruit were stored at 5 ± 0.5 °C and 90 ± 5% relative humidity. Pomological and biochemical measurements and analyses were carried out on fruit stored for 24 days in three different periods (8th, 16th and 24th days).ResultsWith the extension of storage period, the increase in spoilage and ripening symptoms such as weight loss (8.53%), pH increase (5.01) and soluble solids content (SSC) accumulation (4.95%) was observed in tomatoes. In addition, the significant decreases occurred in malic acid, citric acid, tartaric acid, oxalic acid, vitamin C and phenolic compounds. However, SA and ML applications slowed down these negative changes, with the best results generally obtained in the SA + ML combination. This combination significantly limited the decreases in weight loss and chemical contents, delayed the ripening of tomatoes and maintained their nutritional value. Especially in early and middle storage periods (8th and 16th days of the cold storage), SA + ML applications gave the most effective results. However, some protective effects were reduced by the 24th day the cold storage.ConclusionSA and ML applications are recommended as an effective biopreservation strategy to extend the shelf life of tomatoes, prevent commercial value loss and maintain quality. This study demonstrates the potential of using bioactive compounds to preserve the quality and nutritional values of tomatoes after harvest.

Background

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), rich in biologically active compounds such as lycopene, β-carotene, vitamins C and E, and flavonoids, is an important fresh consumption product with high nutritional value for human health [1–3]. Due to their climacteric nature, tomatoes ripen and deteriorate quickly, making them highly perishable during post-harvest handling, especially in transport and storage [4, 5]. The short shelf life of tomatoes leads to economic losses and affects consumer satisfaction and food safety. While traditional methods like cold chain and controlled atmosphere packaging help extend shelf life, they have limitations including high cost, energy use, and complexity [6, 7]. Therefore, the use of natural and low-cost bioregulators that can be an alternative to these traditional techniques has attracted great interest in recent years. In this context, investigating the effectiveness of natural signaling molecules such as ML and SA in preserving post-harvest fruit quality is of great importance for sustainable and environmentally friendly agricultural practices.

ML is a biologically active indolamine compound naturally synthesized in plants and is a signaling molecule involved in the regulation of many physiological processes such as growth, development, aging and stress response. ML activates the plant's defense systems against environmental stress factors, preventing oxidative damage by directly neutralizing reactive oxygen species or increasing the expression of antioxidant genes. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, ML application increased the expression of CBF transcription factors and conferred tolerance to low temperatures. In addition, the important effects of ML include maintaining cell membrane stability, increasing phenolic compound production. and reducing stress damage in meristematic tissues [8]. ML applications delayed fruit senescence, activated antioxidant systems and preserved quality parameters in fruit species such as peach [9], strawberry [10], cherry [11], plum [12], banana [13], kiwi [14], mango [15] and pear [16]. It has also been reported that ML application in climacteric fruit causes a transient increase in ROS levels in the early storage stages, triggering antioxidant systems [17]. These effects make ML an environmentally friendly, non-toxic and effective postharvest application tool [18].

SA is a simple phenolic compound naturally found in plants and is a signaling molecule that plays an important role in the regulation of stress responses and physiological processes [19]. SA is involved in numerous biological functions in plants, such as seed germination, regulation of ethylene production, thermogenesis, and activation of systemic acquired resistance (SAR) mechanisms. It also increases the activity of defense enzymes such as peroxidase (POD) by enhancing both local and systemic defense responses against pathogens [20]. When applied in non-toxic doses to fruit and vegetables, SA reduces quality losses and increases durability [21]. SA applications delayed fruit softening, strengthened antioxidant systems and protected against cell wall degradation in many products such as tomato [22], tangerine [23], apricot [24], sweet cherry [25], grapefruit [26], blueberry [27], lemon [28], longan [29], pepper [30], banana and kiwifruit [31]. These effects are generally associated with the reduction of the activity of cell wall-degrading enzymes (PG, PME, CEL) [22, 24]. In addition, transcriptomic analyses in fruit such as jujube and pear have shown that SA application regulates the expression of genes related to antioxidant defense and polysaccharide metabolism [32–34]. The combined postharvest application of biologically active compounds such as melatonin and salicylic acid on the quality attributes and metabolic responses of tomato fruit has not been sufficiently investigated, and a comprehensive study is needed to reveal the potential synergistic effects of these two compounds. In this context, although the individual effects of both compounds have been thoroughly demonstrated, the lack of scientific data on determining the synergistic effects of their combined use clearly highlights the necessity of this study. This study aims to investigate the effects of ML and SA applied postharvest in tomato on fruit quality and biochemical properties and to reveal the potential synergistic role of these two compounds.

Material and method

Plant material and treatments

The plant material used in this study consisted of fruit of the tomato variety ‘Depar’ (Solanum lycopersicum L.) grown in an open field belonging to a commercial producer in Düzce (Türkiye) province during the 2024 production season. The fruit to be used in the study were selected from individuals that had reached vegetative maturity, had red outer peel colour, and had morphological characteristics specific to the variety (SSC: %4.07). Harvesting was carried out manually in the early morning hours when environmental stress factors were at their lowest. During harvest, the care was taken to preserve the physiological integrity of the fruit, the fruit that did not show any physical impact, cracks, rottenness or disease symptoms and were homogeneous in terms of shape, colour and size were carefully selected. The harvested tomatoes were placed in plastic crates in a way that would not cause any damage and were transported to the laboratory of the Department of Horticulture of the Faculty of Agriculture of Ege University and Bolu Abant Izzet Baysal University by a refrigerated transport vehicle. In the study, 1 mM concentration of ML and 1 mM concentration of SA solutions were prepared to be applied to tomato fruit. Melatonin (≥ 98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich) and salicylic acid (≥ 99% purity, Merck) used in the study were applied by dissolving in distilled water. The concentrations of melatonin (100 µM) and salicylic acid (2 mM) used in the study were selected considering the doses reported to be effective in preserving fruit quality in previous studies on different fruit species. In each solution application, the fruits were completely immersed in the respective solution for approximately 15 min. and then kept at room temperature (20–22 °C) for approximately 30 min to allow the chemical substance to penetrate the surface. At the end of this period, the fruit were stored in normal atmospheric conditions. The fruits in the control group in the experiment were treated only with pure distilled water using the same application method and were not exposed to any chemicals. The study was designed with three replications. 10 fruits were used for each replication in each measurement period, 30 fruits for one treatment, and a total of 30 × 4:120 fruits for each measurement period, for a total of 4 × 120:480 fruits. After the application, the fruit were stored in a cold storage at 5 ± 0.5 °C and 90 ± 5% relative humidity for 24 days. In order to determine the fruit quality and biochemical parameters during the storage period, analyses were carried out at three different times (8th, 16th and 24th days of storage) with 8-day intervals. Three fruit were taken from each replicate in each analysis period and evaluated.

Weight loss

During the study, the initial weight (Wi) of each fruit was carefully measured at the beginning of cold storage using a digital balance (Radwag, Poland) with a precision of 0.01 g. During the weight measurements, all fruit were carefully weighed and the accuracy of the measuring device was taken into account. The final weights (Wf) of the fruit were determined at the later stages of storage (on the 8th, 16th and 24th days). Weight loss was calculated by taking the difference between the weight at the beginning of each measurement period (Wi) and the weight after (Wf). This difference was proportional to the initial weight and the results were expressed as a percentage. Weight loss was used as an important parameter to evaluate the quality changes such as water loss and dehydration of the products. SSC, pH and TA

Three fruit selected from each replicate were first washed with distilled water and cleaned from external factors. Then, the fruit were homogenized with a blender (Promix HR2653, Philips, Turkey). The homogeneous mixture obtained was passed through cheesecloth to obtain the fruit juice filtrate. SSC was determined by measuring the juice with a digital refractometer (Atago PAL-1, USA) and the results were recorded as percentage (%). This analysis provides information about the taste and sugar content of the fruit. The pH value was measured directly with a pH meter and this value was used as an important parameter in determining the acidity of the juice. For titratable acidity (TA) measurement, 10 mL of distilled water was added to the fruit juice and then 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was added until the pH of the solution reached 8.2. The amount of NaOH consumed during the titration process was used to calculate titratable acidity and the results were expressed as % (malic acid).

Respiration rate

Respiration rate measurement was performed by placing three fruit selected from each replicate in airtight containers. After the containers were left to stand for 2 h, the amount of CO2 released from the atmosphere was measured with the Headspace Gas Analyzer GS3/L (Gasboard-3210Plus, Cubic Instrument, Chine). Respiration rate was calculated by observing the change in the amount of CO2 released into the atmosphere over time. Phenolic compounds

For the analysis of phenolic compounds, 10 g of homogeneously selected fresh fruit samples were taken and these samples were extracted in tubes with 25 mL of methyl alcohol for 6 h. The obtained extract was analyzed with a high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) device (Perkin-Elmer Series 200, Norwalk, USA). The HPLC system was equipped with a UV detector at 280 nm wavelength and the separation process was carried out on a Phenomenex Kromasil 100 A C18 (250 mm × 4.60 mm, 5 mm) column. The mobile phase was prepared as a mixture of water (A) and acetonitrile (B) containing 2.5% formic acid and the flow rate was determined as 1 mL/min and the injection volume as 20 L. The results were expressed as mg kg−1 according to the peak areas obtained [35].

Organic acids

Extraction of organic acids was carried out according to the method reported by Bevilacqua and Califano [36]. 10 g of fresh sample was taken into centrifuge tubes and 10 mL of 0.009 N H2SO2 solution was added. After homogenization, the samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min. The obtained supernatant was first passed through filter paper, then through 0.45 mm membrane filter and SEP-PAK C18 cartridge. Finally, the sample was injected into Agilent HPLC 1100 series instrument and separation of organic acids was performed on Aminex HPX-87 H (300 mm × 7.8 mm) column. Organic acids were detected at 214 nm and 280 nm wavelengths and the amount of acids in the solution was determined by HPLC data.

Vitamin C

The determination of vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid) content was performed following the procedure described by Cemeroğlu [37]. A 10 g portion of the fruit sample was homogenized with a 2.5% meta-phosphoric acid solution and then centrifuged. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm teflon membrane filter and subsequently analyzed using an HPLC system. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Phenomenex Luna C18 column (250 × 4.60 mm, 5 µm) maintained at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water acidified to pH 2.2 with sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), delivered at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. Detection was conducted using a diode-array detector (DAD) set at 254 nm.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained in the study were subjected to variance analysis (ANOVA). Differences between means were evaluated with Tukey's test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using the SAS Version 9.1 statistical software package (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Heatmap clustering analysis and principal component analysis were conducted with the Jmp Pro 17 statistical software package.

Results and discussion

Weight loss

In the study conducted on tomato fruit, it was observed that weight loss increased significantly as the storage period extended. The average weight loss, which was 4.31% on the eighth day, increased to 5.59% on the sixteenth day and 8.53% on the twenty-fourth day. These results indicate that the ongoing water loss in the fruit over time is reflected as weight loss. Considering the treatments, the highest weight loss of 7.41% was observed in the control group on the eighth day, whereas this rate decreased to 2.99% in salicylic acid-treated fruits, 4.84% in melatonin-treated ones, and 1.99% in the group where both compounds were applied together. On the sixteenth day, weight loss in the control group was 8.31%, while it was measured as 3.44% in the SA + ML treatment. At the end of the twenty-fourth day, the loss was 13.88% in the control group; this was reduced to 6.17% with SA, 7.77% with ML, and 6.32% with the combined SA + ML treatment. Statistical analyses supported these findings, revealing that both storage duration (p ≤ 0.01) and the interaction of treatment x time (p ≤ 0.001) had significant effects on weight loss. These data demonstrate that the combined use of SA and ML creates a synergistic effect in reducing water loss and thus contributes to the prevention of quality loss in tomatoes (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The weight loss in fruit occurs largely through evaporation of water through transpiration, depending on the surface-volume ratio of the fruit and environmental conditions [38]. This process causes significant economic losses and increases as the storage period extends [39]. The weight loss negatively affects the external appearance, texture and consumer perception of the fruit, shortening its shelf life [40]. However, previous studies have shown that these losses can be reduced with natural compounds such as ML and salicylic acid. It has been reported that ML increases the strength of the fruit surface and thus reduces water loss [41]. In the study conducted on figs by Kucuker et al. [42], it was shown that ML application reduced weight loss during storage and this effect varied depending on the ML dose. Similarly, previous study was demonstrated the weight loss-reducing effect of ML application on fruit such as pear [43], mango [44], banana [13] and peach [45]. ML prevents water loss by maintaining cell turgor [43] and delays signs of aging [46]. Mandal et al. [47] reported that the weight loss in ML-treated pineapple fruit was significantly lower than in the control group. SA is a phenolic compound naturally found in plants and plays a role in the regulation of physiological processes. It can reduce transpiration and thus water loss by slowing down metabolic activities in fruit during storage [48]. Application of SA preserved fruit quality by reducing water loss and spoilage in different fruit species such as banana, apricot, cherry, pomegranate, orange, grape [49–52]. This effect of SA is associated with the suppression of the activity of enzymes (PG, PME, CEL) that lead to cell wall degradation [22]. Sakaldas et al. [53] reported that SA significantly reduces water loss in blackberry and that the application concentration plays an important role in effectiveness. Respiration rate

A marked increase in the respiration rate of tomato fruit was observed as the storage period progressed. The value, which was 5.62 nmol CO₂/kg.h at harvest, reached 6.73 on day 8, 7.66 on day 16, and 8.82 nmol CO₂/kg.h on day 24. However, SA and ML treatments significantly suppressed this increase. On day 8, the respiration rate was 7.79 nmol CO₂/kg.h in the control group and 5.69 in the SA + ML treatment. Similarly, on day 16, these values were 9.17 (control) and 6.38 (SA + ML), and on day 24, they were 11.73 and 7.05, respectively. These findings indicate that especially the combined application of SA and ML slows respiration and thereby extends the shelf life of tomato fruit. Variance analyses also showed that both the storage period and treatments had statistically significant effects on respiration rate (*p ≤ 0.05, ***p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The mechanisms underlying these effects are supported by the findings obtained from previous studies. Sakaldas et al. [53] reported that SA reduces fruit respiration rate depending on concentration and this effect reaches its maximum level especially at 1.5 mM application. This effect of SA occurs by suppressing 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase and synthase enzymes involved in ethylene biosynthesis. This mechanism has been similarly demonstrated in products such as tomato [22], grape [54] and plum [55]. SA also delays senescence and cell wall degradation in fruit by activating defense enzymes and antioxidant systems [21]. These findings were similarly confirmed in fruit such as banana [22], pear [25] and papaya [56]. This effect of ML has been associated with the suppression of ethylene synthesis and regulation of antioxidant systems [32]. Zhai et al. [57] reported that ML reduces respiratory rate. However, the effect of ML may vary depending on the application dose. It has been stated that ML at high doses (e.g. 1000 μM) can trigger ethylene production and accelerate ripening by increasing endogenous ML levels [58]. This study reveals that SA and ML reduce respiratory rate most effectively when applied together. This synergistic effect can be evaluated as being related to both the suppression of ethylene biosynthesis and the reduction of oxidative stress. In addition, the strengthening of defense systems by SA [19] and the regulation of ethylene production and cell wall degradation by ML [44] constitute the basic biochemical mechanisms behind these results. Therefore, the combined use of SA and ML can be considered as an effective and applicable approach to extend the shelf life by reducing the respiration rate in perishable fruit such as tomatoes.

SSC

A significant increase in SSC was observed in tomato fruit during storage. The rate, which was 4.07% at harvest, increased to 4.95% at the end of day 24. This rise indicates continued ripening and accumulation of soluble sugars. However, SA and ML applications limited this increase. Especially the SA + ML combination kept SSC levels significantly lower compared to the control. On the eighth day, SSC was 4.92% in the control and 4.41% in the SA + ML group; this trend continued on day 24, reaching 4.74% in the same group. These findings suggest that SA and ML applications slowed down fruit ripening and controlled sugar accumulation. ANOVA results supported these observations; both storage time (p ≤ 0.01) and treatment x time interaction (*p ≤ 0.001) had significant effects on SSC (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The physiological basis of this effect is based on the natural increase in SSC by hydrolysis of insoluble polysaccharides during fruit ripening [59]. During storage, the degradation of cell wall components and water loss may lead to an increase in the soluble sugar content [60]. However, SA and ML slow down this process. Sakaldas et al. [53] reported that SA application reduced the increase in SSC in blackberry during storage and the effect was concentration dependent. This can be explained by the fact that SA reduces the ripening rate by suppressing ethylene production [55]. SA applications also slowed down SSC accumulation, delayed ripening and quality loss in tomato [22], grape [61], apricot [24] and many other fruit [51]. This effect is associated with a decrease in respiration rate and suppression of metabolic activity. Kucuker et al. [42] showed that the increase in SSC was less in ML-treated fruit in figs. In the study conducted on peach, lower SSC and higher acidity values were obtained in fruit treated with ML at the end of storage [54]. However, the effect of ML may vary depending on the concentration used and the fruit species. For example, Liu et al. [43] and Rastegar et al. [44] did not observe any significant changes in SSC and TA values in strawberry and mango fruit. The effect of ML applications in slowing down ripening can be explained by suppressing ethylene synthesis and related transcription factors by increasing endogenous ML levels [59]. In addition, the preservation of soluble sugar and TSS levels in ML-applied fruit has been attributed to the anti-aging and sugar metabolism regulating effect of ML [62]. Mandal et al. [47] reported that fruit treated with 8.0 mM ML maintained high sugar content even after 20 days of storage.

pH

A general increase in pH was observed during the storage of tomato fruit. The average pH, which was 4.48 at harvest, increased to 4.98 on day 8 and 5.21 on day 16, followed by a slight decrease to 5.01 on day 24. This indicates that the reduction in acidic compounds during ripening leads to a pH increase, although some acids might be reactivated in later stages. SA and ML treatments suppressed this increase, with the SA + ML combination consistently providing the lowest pH levels. On day 24, the pH was 5.27 in the control group but only 4.60 in the SA + ML group, indicating prolonged retention of the acidic nature of the fruit. Statistical analysis confirmed that both time and treatment effects were significant (***p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 1 and Table 1). This change in pH is closely related to the physiological and biochemical processes that occur during storage. Increasing pH values during storage are generally considered as a result of processes such as the metabolism of acids or the participation of organic acids in respiration [54]. However, this process can be modulated by applied exogenous substances. In particular, SA can suppress the pH increase by slowing down ripening and related biochemical changes. Sakaldas et al. [53]stated that SA application limited the pH increase in fruit during storage and the lowest pH values were reached with 1.5 mM SA application. In this study, similarly, SA and SA + ML applications allowed tomatoes to remain at lower pH levels. ML slows down the respiration rate and ethylene production and delays fruit senescence, which may limit pH changes [63]. Similar findings were also found in studies on banana [13], pear [16] and papaya [56]. However, some studies such as Liu et al. [43] and Rastegar et al. [44] reported that ML application had no significant effect on pH in species such as strawberry and mango. These contradictory results may be due to differences in application dose, time and fruit species [54]. In the study conducted by Kucuker et al. [54], it was stated that ML applications in peaches showed concentration-dependent effects on pH and pH values were measured higher at some doses. This suggests that the pH protection effect of ML is limited to the optimal dose and may have adverse effects at high doses. ML application may accelerate aging by increasing ethylene production at higher concentrations, while it produces positive results at low doses while SA appears to have a more stable and direct effect [59]. The effects of SA, such as delaying fruit softening, activating the antioxidant system and slowing down cell wall degradation [24], also indirectly contribute to pH maintenance. Transcriptome analyses in fruit such as jujube and pear have also revealed that SA application regulates antioxidant defense and carbohydrate metabolism [32].

Titratable acidity

A general declining trend in titratable acidity was observed in tomato fruit depending on the storage duration. The acidity level dropped from 0.65% at harvest to 0.49% on day 8, 0.41% on day 16, and 0.38% on day 24. The decrease was more rapid in the first 8 days, slowing down afterward. SA and ML applications slowed this decline to varying extents. While SA was more effective in maintaining acidity on days 8 and 16, ML provided the highest acidity level on day 24 (0.41%). Although the SA + ML combination was initially effective, it reached values similar to the control in the late period. Statistical analysis revealed that both storage duration and treatments significantly affected TA (**p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 1 and Table 1). This situation shows that both the natural course of ripening and the applications are determinants of acidity in the fruit. This decrease in TA during storage can be explained by the metabolism of organic acids in the fruit or their decomposition by the respiratory process [62]. However, SA and ML applications slow down this process and can preserve acidity for a longer time. Sakaldas et al. [53] reported that SA application preserved TA values at a higher level compared to the control group and this effect was especially evident at a concentration of 1.5 mM SA. In addition, it was emphasized that the effect of storage time was more dominant than SA application. This shows that the stabilizing effect of SA on TA levels decreases over time and storage time plays a greater role in TA loss. However, it is reported that SA delays TA loss by suppressing respiration and metabolic activities [55]. In some products such as grapes, storage time was more decisive than SA application and a decrease in TA was observed despite SA [60]. These findings suggest that fruit species and application parameters play a critical role in efficacy. Mandal et al. [47] reported that acidity can be maintained longer and sugar/carbohydrate content remains high in ML-applied fruit. This suggests that ML slows down the ripening process and provides a slower breakdown of organic acids. In addition, Wang et al. [62] stated that sugar content was better preserved in ML-applied fruit during storage. This balance between organic acids and sugar is considered one of the main factors affecting the taste profile. ML contributes to the preservation of both sugar and acid levels by providing this balance. These effects are due to the properties of ML such as delaying fruit senescence, slowing respiration and reducing microbial spoilage. In this context, it is an expected result that the TA decrease is more limited in fruit to which ML is applied.

Organic acids

Significant reductions were observed in malic, citric, oxalic, and tartaric acids as well as vitamin C content during storage. These compounds, measured at their highest levels at harvest, decreased substantially by day 24. For example, malic acid dropped from 38.93 to 25.20 mg/100 g, citric acid from 284.71 to 195.87 mg/100 g, oxalic acid from 4.33 to 3.11 mg/100 g, and tartaric acid from 32.83 to 21.06 mg/100 g. Similarly, vitamin C content declined from 19.15 to 12.01 mg/100 g. However, SA and ML treatments significantly slowed these losses. Especially the SA + ML combination preserved higher levels of malic, citric, and oxalic acids on days 16 and 24 and significantly improved vitamin C retention. On day 24, vitamin C in the SA + ML group was 13.63 mg/100 g, well above the control (8.99 mg/100 g). These results indicate that both time and the treatments had highly significant effects on these components (***p ≤ 0.001) (Table 2). These results are consistent with the literature showing that organic acid contents naturally decrease as the fruit ripens and that this decrease may negatively affect quality parameters such as taste, aroma and nutritional value of the fruit [64]. Kucuker et al. [54] reported that ML application in peaches maintained organic acid levels, especially during the storage period, and this effect varied depending on the ML concentration. Liu et al. [15] and Tijero et al. [11] similarly revealed that ML is effective in preserving organic acids during storage. This can be attributed to the slowing effect of ML on fruit metabolism and probably inhibiting organic acid destruction by suppressing the activity of some metabolic enzymes. SA applications were also effective in preserving organic acids, but this effect varied depending on the application concentration. In a study conducted by Sakaldas et al. [53], 1.5 mM SA application in blackberries preserved the highest organic acid levels, and it was also determined that SA application significantly affected organic acid levels with storage time. [65] and Kazemi et al. [66] stated that SA can preserve organic acid levels depending on the fruit species and storage conditions. Similarly, vitamin C loss increased with storage time, while SA and ML applications slowed down this loss. Haider et al. [67] reported that SA can reduce ascorbic acid oxidation by increasing ascorbate peroxidase activity, thus preserving vitamin C levels. Phenolic compounds

Significant decreases were found in all phenolic compounds evaluated in tomato fruit during storage. Compounds such as chlorogenic, gallic, catechin, p-coumaric, protocatechuic, caffeic, o-coumaric, quercetin, and ferulic acids, which were at their highest levels at harvest, declined over time. For instance, chlorogenic acid dropped from 24.85 to 14.85 mg/kg, gallic acid from 3.03 to 1.83 mg/kg, and protocatechuic acid from 4.13 to 2.78 mg/kg. These losses are thought to result from the oxidative degradation sensitivity of phenolic compounds. However, SA and ML treatments significantly slowed these reductions, with the SA + ML combination maintaining higher levels of many phenolic compounds. At the end of day 24, values such as 16.05 mg/kg for chlorogenic acid, 3.07 mg/kg for protocatechuic acid, and 4.74 mg/kg for quercetin were recorded in the SA + ML group. These findings suggest that SA and ML applications are effective in limiting the degradation of phenolic compounds during storage, and their combined use offers a stronger protective effect (Tables 3 and 4). Phenolic compounds are important secondary metabolites that affect quality characteristics such as firmness, colour, and aroma in fruit and vegetables, and also contribute to the protection of cell structure by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) [55]. It is natural for these compounds to decrease due to oxidative stress during storage; however, external bioregulators such as SA and ML have been effective in reducing these losses. Previous studies have reported that ML not only scavenges ROS in plants but also preserves phenolic contents by inducing the expression of antioxidant enzyme genes [46]. For example, Liu et al. [16], associated the fruit quality-enhancing effects of ML with an increase in phenols, flavonoids and other antioxidant components. In particular, Kucuker et al. [42] stated that phenolic compounds were maintained at higher levels during storage with ML application in figs and this effect was concentration dependent. Again, Wang et al. [46] revealed that ML prevented postharvest quality loss by increasing flavonoid contents. SA was similarly effective in preserving phenolic compounds during storage. Sakaldas et al. [53] reported that individual phenols, especially caffeic, chlorogenic, ferulic and protocatechuic acid, were preserved at higher levels with SA application in blackberry fruit. In the same study, it was emphasized that compounds such as gallic acid and o-coumaric acid also responded positively to SA application, but the effect may vary depending on the compound. In addition, SA has been shown to promote phenolic synthesis by increasing the enzymatic activity of the phenylpropanoid pathway (phenylalanine ammonium lyase, cinnamate 4-hydroxylase, 4-coumarate-coenzyme A ligase) in fruit such as oranges and grapes [48]. [50] reported that SA reduces the loss of phenolic compounds by regulating enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase and this effect was observed in different fruit such as lemon, pomegranate, blood orange. These defensive effects are also associated with the fundamental role of SA as a signaling molecule in plant physiology [19]. This suggests that these antioxidant substances may increase through common biosynthetic pathways or similar metabolic responses. Conclussion

In this study, the effects of postharvest ML and SA applications on fruit quality and biochemical properties were investigated in tomato. The findings show that SA and ML applications offer important biological preservation strategies to extend the shelf life of tomatoes and preserve fruit quality. Particularly, the combination of SA and ML significantly limited the deterioration symptoms such as weight loss and soluble solid accumulation by delaying the ripening process in tomatoes. Furthermore, these treatments slowed down the negative changes in the biochemical content of tomatoes (e.g. vitamin C, phenolic compounds and organic acids) and preserved the nutritional value of the fruit. It was observed that the SA + ML combination gave the most effective results in the early and middle storage periods (8th and 16th days), but these effects decreased by 24th day. These findings reveal that SA and ML are effective in extending the shelf life of tomatoes during storage, but their effects decrease after a certain period of time. In conclusion, this study demonstrated the effectiveness of SA and ML applications in preserving the postharvest quality of tomato and supported the use of these compounds as biopreservation strategies. SA and ML applications stand out as a promising approach to prevent commercial value loss and preserve the nutritional value of tomatoes. These findings may encourage the investigation of the use of similar biologically active compounds in different plant species, providing a potential basis for future research.
