Non‐HLA Autoantibodies Associate With a Positive Flow‐Crossmatch Pre‐ and Chronic Lung Allograft Dysfunction Post‐ Lung Transplant
Authors: Steven Hiho, Alex Paporakis, Sadiaa Rahman, Bronwyn Levvey, Mary Diviney, Gregory Snell, Lucy Sullivan, Glen Westall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibodies to HLA are a major cause of chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) following lung transplantation (LTx), however mounting evidence indicates non‐HLA autoantibodies may also play a role. LTx recipients may have increased autoantibodies related to their native disease, and these may impact both pre‐transplant assays and LTx outcomes. The flow cytometry crossmatch (FXM) assay is often used peri‐LTx to define immunological risk; however, the presence of autoantibodies in LTx recipients may associate with positivity in the FXM, and the clinical impact of this is unknown. In a retrospective, single‐center cohort, we investigate the association of pre‐transplant non‐HLA autoantibodies on the FXM and subsequent CLAD. The serum of 68 LTx recipients was retrospectively tested for 39 autoantibody targets pre‐ and 12‐months post‐transplant. A further cohort of waitlisted LTx recipients (n = 25) with HLA antibody‐negative FXM‐positive sera was evaluated for the presence of these same 39 autoantibodies to determine the association of autoantibodies and FXM reactivity. CLAD status and freedom from CLAD were defined and analysed for all recipients. MFI strengths in five autoantibody targets associated with FXM positivity when compared to the HLA donor‐specific and FXM negative patients. LTx recipients who were positive to ≥ 8 non‐HLA autoantibodies had an increased risk of CLAD (HR = 3.09 p < 0.01) and a decreased freedom from CLAD (p < 0.01). Recipients with increased positivity to non‐HLA autoantibodies pre‐LTx demonstrate increased risk of CLAD and decreased freedom from CLAD. Furthermore, the strength of these autoantibodies is associated with FXM positivity in the absence of HLA antibodies. Further research with larger cohorts is required to better understand which autoantibody targets are responsible for CLAD outcomes, and to define best cut‐offs to define reactivity.

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LTx) is a potential life‐saving option for patients with chronic end‐stage lung disease. However, outcomes following LTx remain poor when compared to other solid organ transplants, even with improved surgical and post‐transplant management [1]. Current median survival following LTx is only 7 years [2], with chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) being the primary cause of these poorer outcomes. Immune‐mediated damage to the lung allograft remains the major obstacle for successful LTx, with antibodies against the lung allograft being a potential driver of CLAD. HLA donor‐specific antibodies (DSA) are a recognised cause of immune‐mediated antibody damage, causing antibody‐mediated rejection (AMR) and an increased risk of CLAD [3, 4]. Recently, emerging evidence also suggests an important role of non‐HLA autoantibodies in allograft rejection [5, 6, 7].

Unlike HLA antibodies which develop through sensitisation of either transplant, pregnancy or transfusions, autoantibodies can develop pre‐LTx against lung tissue structural self‐antigens that become exposed through the damage associated with the native lung disease. The disease processes subsequently cause post‐translational modifications such as oxidation and citrullination to pulmonary peptides [8], with the resultant development of autoantibodies. Additionally, pulmonary disease can often demonstrate fibrotic‐associated damage leading to oxidative stress leading to inflammation and injury again with the subsequent development of autoantibodies [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated that 30% of waitlisted lung patients have pre‐existing non‐HLA autoantibodies, primarily in cystic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis patients [10, 11]. Although waitlisted recipients with pre‐formed autoantibodies towards AT1R [12, 13], Vimentin [14, 15], LG3 [16] and Collagen [17] demonstrate increased risk of rejection in kidney and liver transplantation, very few studies have investigated the role of autoantibodies specifically in LTx [6].

Many centers utilise flow cytometry to identify HLA antibodies (FXM) as part of the pre‐transplant assessment for transplantation. Although the FXM assay is a sensitive assay which is comparable to the single antigen bead (SAB) assay, it often results in positivity which cannot be explained by HLA antibodies [18]. Many refer to these reactions as ‘false’; however, it has long been speculative that the FXM positive results in the absence of HLA antibodies may be reacting to non‐HLA targets [19]. Although the clinical impact of these FXM positive (FXM+) DSA negative (DSA−) reactions is unknown, the incidence of these is often confusing and difficult to interpret for transplant clinicians.

Although the clinical relevance of non‐HLA autoantibodies in LTx is unknown, reports of increased autoantibodies in LTx patients pose challenges for clinicians in accurately determining transplant risk. Whether these non‐HLA autoantibodies are simply biomarkers of other mechanisms or contribute to the pathogenesis itself, the presence and impact of these autoantibodies on the pre‐transplant assessment need to be better defined.

Here, in this single‐center retrospective study, we investigate the impact of non‐HLA autoantibodies in the pre‐transplant assessment of LTx recipients. Primarily aiming to determine the association between non‐HLA autoantibodies and pre‐transplant FXM reactivity and the development of CLAD.

Methods

Cohort

This study involved a cohort of patients undergoing LTx at the Alfred Hospital, Melbourne between 2011 and 2018. All transplants were primary adult bilateral lung transplants.

Selection of cohort was based on having available stored pre‐ and 12 months post‐transplant serum, with complete local clinical follow‐up. After exclusions for single lung (38), follow‐up outside of centre (80) and no 12‐month serum (439) and sufficient pre‐transplant serum stored (39), a total of 68 LTx recipients were included in this study (Figure 1).

Cohort selection for non‐HLA autoantibody analysis: Of 664 primary lung transplants performed between 2011 and 2018, 626 were double lung recipients, and 546 had local follow‐up data available. Serum samples collected at 12 months post‐transplant were available for 107 recipients, of whom 68 had sufficient paired pre‐ and 12‐month serum stored for non‐HLA antibody testing.

The FXM group for this study was selected from a wider cohort of 265 FXM pairs [20], consisting of patient serum and their corresponding donor cells. From this wider cohort, the recipient serum where an FXM reaction that could not be explained by the presence of HLA DSA was included. For this study, 13 FXM+DSA− and 12 FXM−DSA− recipient sera were available to investigate the impact of non‐HLA autoantibodies on FXM reactivity.

Patients received a standard triple immunosuppressant regimen consisting of tacrolimus, azathioprine or mycophenolate, and prednisolone, with induction therapy with an IL‐2 antagonist reserved for patients with limited renal reserve. All cohort participants had clinical follow up until death, or the censor date of December 31, 2023.

This study was approved by the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (approval 478/19) and Australian Red Cross Lifeblood (approval 02022019). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and use of de‐identified data.

Non‐HLA Autoantibody Testing

Pre‐ and 12‐month post‐transplant serum samples were retrospectively evaluated for 39 non‐HLA autoantibodies (LABScreen Autoantibody, One Lambda, CA) according to manufacturer's protocols. Corrected Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used for all analyses.

Non‐HLA Antibody MFI Threshold Calculation

For all analysis investigating the association between non‐HLA autoantibody and CLAD and freedom from CLAD, the pre‐transplant corrected MFI for the No CLAD group plus one standard deviation (±SD) was used to define target positivity (Non‐HLA autoantibody MFI positivity thresholds used in study included in Table S1).

For the FXM+ group, the corrected MFI values were used for comparison between FXM+ and FXM− groups. This was to determine at which MFI levels, if any, correlated with an FXM reaction regardless of transplant outcomes or recipient disease status.

HLA Antibody Testing

Patient sera were routinely tested for HLA antibodies pre‐transplant by Luminex SABs (One Lambda Inc., Canoga Park) and all sera were treated with Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) to reduce the effect of interfering factors such as the prozone effect. For analysis, a MFI ≥ 1000 was used for assignment of HLA DSA positivity.

Flow Cytometry Crossmatch

All FXMs were initially performed as part of a wider study [20], where Halifaster FXM protocol was used for FXM assays as previously described [21]. The Halifaster FXM protocol is a rapid, standardised FXM offering optimised lymphocyte preparation, shortened incubation steps to reduce assay time and inter‐operator variability. The Halifaster protocol enables higher throughput and improved reproducibility. All recipient sera which demonstrated FXM positivity in the absence of HLA DSA were used for the FXM+DSA− group to investigate the role of non‐HLA autoantibodies on FXM reactivity. The FXM+ group was represented by six T‐cell FXM+ only, one was B‐cell FXM+ only and seven with both T‐ and B‐cell FXM+.

Clinical

The primary outcomes used for this study were CLAD and freedom from CLAD. CLAD was defined as a sustained irreversible loss of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) from baseline of ≥ 20%. Freedom from CLAD was the time between date of transplant and clinical diagnosis of CLAD [22]. CLAD adjudication was performed through review involving transplant physicians integrating clinical, spirometric, imaging and histopathologic data.

Statistical Analysis

Qualitative data was recorded as total number (n) and percentages of totals (%) whereas quantitative data reported as means with standard deviation (±SD). Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the association between non‐HLA autoantibody positivity and freedom from CLAD with Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (95% CI) reported to define risk. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and comparisons between groups (e.g., FXM+ vs. FXM−) were analysed using either independent t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test. Associations between CLAD (CLAD/No CLAD) and non‐HLA autoantibody positivity (neg/pos) were evaluated using Pearson's chi‐squared test, or Fisher's exact test where expected cell counts were < 5. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to determine association with number of positive autoantibody targets and Freedom from CLAD. In all models an α p‐value < 0.05 is considered significant. SPSS (v.28.0) was used for all statistical analysis.

Results

CLAD Cohort

The final cohort for the CLAD analysis consisted of 68 recipients, with 45 (65%) males with a mean age of 56 (±13) years. Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD) was the most common pre‐transplant diagnosis, and 27 (41%) had HLA antibodies identified (Table 1), with no transplant occurring in the presence of any pre‐tx DSA. HLA sensitisation increased post‐LTx with 38 (57%) developing HLA antibodies, whereby 7 (10%) developed class I only, 20 (29%) developed class II only and 11 (16%) developed both class I and class II DSA.

Non‐HLA Autoantibodies

The manufacturer provides numerous MFI thresholds to use for defining positivity using unsensitised healthy controls, these thresholds are determined to be positive for the 75th, 85th and 95th percentiles from these controls. In our study, these manufacturers' thresholds did not associate with any outcome in any analysis performed. Using our defined MFI thresholds for all 39 targets the median number of positive non‐HLA targets was 3 with IQR of 5 and ranges from 0 to 18 positive autoantibody targets (Figure 2).

Total number of positive pre‐transplant autoantibody targets in 68 lung transplant recipients. In the cohort of 68 lung patient recipients evaluated for 39 non‐HLA autoantibodies the ranges of total number of positive autoantibodies each recipient had was 0–18, where 10 recipients did not show positivity to at least one target, above the defined study MFI thresholds.

For the 12‐month post‐transplant samples, four non‐HLA autoantibody targets were associated with the development of CLAD. For the targets: CD36 (p < 0.05), AGT (p < 0.05), AURKA (p < 0.05) and LG3 (< 0.001), positivity was associated with the development of CLAD. Six autoantibody targets had no recipients demonstrate positivity within the study for the 12‐months post‐transplant testing.

Association of Disease Diagnosis and Autoantibodies

No significant association between native disease diagnosis and autoantibody positivity was seen for any of the 39 non‐HLA autoantibody targets when comparing both raw MFI values and positivity as defined in‐house (Non‐HLA autoantibody associations with native lung disease diagnoses included in Table S2).

Overall, higher Agrin, PTPRN, CXCL11 and Collagen I (ColI) and IV (ColIV) MFI values associated with a positive T‐cell FXM within this cohort. Discussion

Here, we have demonstrated that an increase in the number of pre‐LTx non‐HLA autoantibodies increases the risk of CLAD. Furthermore, we report that the presence of these non‐HLA autoantibodies can impact the reactivity in a flow cytometry assay in the absence of HLA antibodies. We have shown that recipients with a higher number of positive pre‐LTx autoantibody targets had significantly reduced freedom from CLAD, as recipients who were positive to ≥ 8 of the 39 autoantibodies developed CLAD earlier compared to recipients with ≤ 7 positive autoantibodies. This confirms previous work where increased autoantibodies are detrimental to lung transplant outcomes [6].

Although here we were unable to define which specific autoantibodies were associated most with the development of CLAD due to the small cohort, previous studies have indicated that some of these targets are associated with detrimental outcomes after transplantation, including Collagen type I, III and IV [23, 24, 25], ARHGDIB [26, 27], PECR [27] and fibronectin [28]. Furthermore, autoantibodies to vimentin [29] and α‐enolase [30] have been previously reported to associate with rejection following heart transplantation. Although it is unknown whether non‐HLA autoantibodies are a key driver of rejection following solid organ transplantation, or whether they are markers of other mechanisms and immunological processes which contribute to allograft damage, understanding that recipients with increased pre‐transplant autoantibodies are at higher risk of rejection may allow clinicians and treating teams to better manage and monitor to reduce this risk.

Here, we also report on the presence of some autoantibodies impacting the flow cytometry assay. Although it has been proposed that autoantibodies are the cause for ‘false’ reactivity in the flow cytometry, it is seldom reported. For many centers, this flow cytometry is utilised to identify HLA antibodies which may be damaging to transplant success by assessing risk of AMR. However, the FXM does not specifically identify HLA antibodies; rather, it can identify a variety of biological factors which may interact with cells. These reactions are often referred to as ‘false positive’ and reported as high as a 20% rate [18]. Recently, there has been a move within the transplant community to use a virtual assessment to better define the HLA antibodies pre‐transplant, and the occurrence of the positive FXM in the absence of any HLA antibodies has contributed to that push. Our findings here begin to answer what factors are eliciting those unexplained FXM reactivity.

We understand that the demographics seen here may not be representative of most centers, with a higher male (65%) and proportion of patients with a lung fibrosis diagnosis (62%); this could be due to selection bias towards a less HLA sensitised cohort. However, previous reports investigating non‐HLA autoantibodies have not seen an association between either sex or disease [6], so we believe the impact of this selection bias may be minimal. Another limitation we see is the low cohort size and potential selection bias within the scope of our selection for cohort. In this study, we looked at retrospectively sampled and stored serum from patients' post‐transplant. These patients may have required this additional sampling due to clinical suspicion of rejection, as was the protocol for a majority of the era of transplants at our centre. This could potentially skew the results of this study. We aim to broaden this cohort to include more routinely prospective pre‐ and post‐transplant sampling, which will negate this. Although we feel our findings contribute to the growing evidence that we need to look further into the impact of these non‐HLA autoantibodies, noting that HLA alone cannot explain the poorer outcomes following LTx.

One major concern the transplant community will need to address as we continue to investigate the role of autoantibodies in transplantation is the thresholds used to define positivity of these autoantibodies. Although kits have manufacturers' thresholds defined, often these are based on a surprisingly small number of healthy individuals and their autoantibody status. However, recipients awaiting transplantation have a variety of factors which contribute to increased autoantibody levels. Therefore, using a healthy baseline to define positivity may not be the best approach. Here, we define autoantibody positivity based off the mean plus one standard deviation from the No CLAD (stable) group. This provided a more accurate baseline of autoantibody levels to define positivity.

Establishing meaningful non‐HLA autoantibody thresholds for LTx requires validation in much larger, multi‐centre cohorts encompassing diverse native diseases and clinical outcomes. Such studies are essential to determine whether assay‐specific or population‐derived thresholds provide improved predictive value beyond manufacturer defined thresholds. Furthermore, it may be required to have separate thresholds for specific organs, as the same for kidney and liver may not be best suited to use on the thoracic organs. Moreover, studies could focus on measuring these autoantibodies in the graft to define the association more accurately with the presence of these and allograft rejection.

In this single‐centre retrospective study we have demonstrated that increased non‐HLA autoantibody positivity increases the risk of CLAD and that these autoantibodies impact the pre‐transplant flow crossmatch reactivity in the absence of HLA antibodies. Further studies are required to determine which specific autoantibodies contribute more to CLAD development and if these are markers of other mechanisms or play the primary role themselves. However, non‐HLA autoantibody testing can certainly be incorporated into the pre‐transplant work‐up of potential recipients awaiting LTx and provide additional information to clinical teams about true immunological risks. In turn, this enables clinical teams to personalise management post‐transplant.
