“Why did you choose that score?” – Associations between patient global assessment scores and mental health symptoms in patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Inna Powers, Sonja I. Ziniel, Jeanelle Sheeder, Jessica L. Bloom, Brian H. Pitts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatic disease significantly alters a child’s quality of life. Rheumatologists often apply a patient global assessment score (PGA) in real time to gauge quality of life, effectiveness of treatment, and disease activity for clinical and research purposes. Previous studies showed a discrepancy between the PGA and objective disease activity measures. This study explores patients’ self-reported reasons behind their PGA score, especially the role of anxiety and depression.MethodsThis is a single-center, cross-sectional, survey-based study. Seventy-one patients 12–18 years old with JIA, SLE or JDM completed a survey including the PGA, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ9) and Screen for Childhood Anxiety Related Disorders (SCARED). The associations of high (≥ 3) and low (< 3) PGA scores with mental health scores and reasons for selecting a PGA were analyzed using appropriate nonparametric tests.ResultsTwenty-seven participants (38.0%) had clinically significant anxiety symptoms and sixteen (22.5%) had moderate or worse depressive symptoms. Patients with high PGA scores were more likely to have clinically significant depressive (32.4% vs. 11.8%, p = 0.05) or anxiety symptoms (48.6% vs. 26.5%, p = 0.05) compared to patients with low PGA scores. Patients with either high depressive symptoms or anxiety symptoms were more likely to report trouble sleeping, feeling stressed about their disease, feeling anxious or sad/depressed, having to take too many medications, and an inability to participate in sports/activities as reasons for their PGA score compared to patients without high depressive or anxiety symptoms. In contrast, the degree of objective disease activity was only associated with the reason “inability to participate in sports”.ConclusionMental health likely influences how adolescents with rheumatic disease view the impact of their disease on their quality of life and functioning. This is important to consider when applying PGA scores in research studies and clinical care. The rheumatology community must develop efficient ways to better evaluate and treat potential factors mediating their patients’ overall health and functioning.

Introduction

Rheumatic diseases affect many aspects of adolescent lives and have the potential for acute and chronic complications. Physical complications may include pain, organ damage, disability, fatigue, disrupted sleep, side effects of medications, and changes in physical appearance [1]. Psychosocial challenges may include inability to participate in activities, loss of friendships, familial stress, fear of the unknown, and missed school days [1]. Some rheumatic diseases, especially SLE, can have neuropsychiatric manifestations including cognitive dysfunction, difficulties with attention and working memory [2, 3]. The treatment of rheumatic diseases can also be overwhelming with immunosuppressive agents (oral, injectable or intravenous forms), frequent doctor appointments and lab work.

One measure of rheumatic disease activity is the Patient Global Assessment, a visual 0–10 or 0-100 analog scale that asks patients “Considering all the ways your rheumatic condition affects you, rate how you’re doing?” This score is used in multiple validated patient-reported disease outcome measures in adult and pediatric rheumatology, such as the ACR (American College of Rheumatology)/EULAR (European League Against Rheumatism) Boolean remission criteria for rheumatoid arthritis. It is also included in the Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score (JADAS) and strongly affects the cutoffs for disease activity, especially clinically inactive disease [4]. Adult studies have shown that there are reasons unrelated to inflammation that can drive the patients’ global assessment higher than expected for their disease activity, such as concerns over emotional and mental health [5, 6]. In some adult and pediatric studies, the PGA did not consistently correlate with swollen joint counts, inflammatory markers, and other objective measures of disease activity [5, 7, 8]. This discrepancy is more pronounced with high PGA scores as low scores correspond better with disease remission, while high PGA scores are more likely associated with factors less directly related to active rheumatic disease [5, 7].

Understanding the reasons patients select their PGA scores might help address non-rheumatic contributors (like poor coping skills, social isolation, poor sleep hygiene) and comorbidities (anxiety, depression, and other mental illnesses) [9].

When considering the pediatric population, the burden of depression and anxiety on adolescents and young adults (AYA; less than 20 years old) is well documented. An estimate by a meta-analysis for point prevalence of mental health disorders in children and adolescents in North America is up to roughly 20% [10]. A large systematic review of the Global Health Data Exchange “Global Burden of Disease” tool showed a rise in mental health disorders over the past 20 years in AYAs globally, with anxiety disorders being most prevalent [11]. Additionally, AYA patients with chronic illnesses have higher prevalence of mental health disease than the baseline population. According to a systematic review, the prevalence of anxiety and depression is 13–57% and 15–59%, respectively, in JIA and SLE [12, 13]. This is similar to the prevalence of depression in adolescents with diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, and cystic fibrosis, which range from 15–20% [12, 14].

Mental health concerns may have significant implications for treating rheumatologic diseases. Early recognition and intervention for comorbid mental illness in rheumatology patients has been suggested to improve both medical and psychosocial long-term outcomes [15]. Prior research has suggested that patients with rheumatic conditions, like SLE, who have concurrent/untreated mental illness are at higher risk for poor disease control and poor compliance with treatment [16]. Still, there is a lack of standardized mental health screening in pediatric rheumatology clinics and the way in which mental health impacts a patient’s PGA score is unknown. As a result, symptoms of anxiety and depression may be misattributed to the impression of the patient’s overall rheumatic disease.

This study aims to assess depression/anxiety symptoms in a sample of pediatric rheumatology patients with JIA and SLE and to determine the degree in which these symptoms associate with their PGA scores. We also explore the reasons patients give for their chosen PGA score based on their objective disease activity. Lastly, we survey patients’ acceptance and comfort with mental health screening in the rheumatology clinic setting as well as the utility of the mental health materials provided.

Patients and methods

Participants

The study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board prior to recruitment. We used convenience sampling to recruit 12 to 18-year-old patients with JIA (all subtypes), JDM, or SLE in pediatric rheumatology continuity clinic between January 2023 and October 2023. Subjects had their diagnosis for at least three months, with the understanding that it may take longer for patients to adjust to a new rheumatic disease diagnosis, but to capture patients with JIA who are typically seen every three months in the first year from diagnosis, as recommended by the CARRA Consensus Treatment Plans. Exclusion criteria also included cognitive or communication deficits, or an inability to read/understand English to the degree necessary to fill out the questionnaire independently or read the materials. This was due to limited funding and resources in this study to translate all the printed and digital (Redcap) materials into multiple languages. Participants less than 18 years old who presented without a legal guardian were also excluded due to the inability to obtain written consent. Informed consent from all participants and their legal guardians was obtained in person at the end of their scheduled clinic visit. The PI reviewed upcoming clinic schedules for all providers to identify eligible patients. Out of 184 eligible patients, 91 were not approached due to PI’s unavailability, 93 were approached, and 71 were enrolled (Fig. 1). There were 52 patients with JIA and 18 patients with SLE. Only one patient with JDM meeting the inclusion criteria during the recruitment period was available. Due to lack of more JDM patients, comparative analyses of different disease types (JIA, SLE, and JDM) were not performed.

Patient survey

Participants received a printed survey consisting of the PGA, the question “Why did you choose that score?” in which they select all options that apply, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and Screen for Childhood Anxiety Related Disorders (SCARED). We used the PHQ9 to screen for symptoms of depression, a validated tool in adolescents that assesses symptoms over the previous 2 weeks [17]. Scores are categorized by severity into minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27) depression. In aligning with the American Academy of Pediatrics and prior research in adolescents, we chose a total score of ≥ 10 or a positive answer to item 9 (suicidal thoughts) as clinically significant cutoffs [18, 19]. We used the SCARED screener to evaluate symptoms of panic/somatic anxiety, generalized anxiety, separation anxiety, social anxiety, and school avoidance. A total score ≥ 25 indicated a positive screen for anxiety [20]. All survey data was entered into RedCap by the PI.

All patients, regardless of their screening result, received a folder of mental health related resources (Table 1). The PI went over each information sheet in the folder with the adolescent and parents, explained the potential usefulness of each, and encouraged them to explore the included apps and websites. As the “99 Coping Skills” sheet was reviewed, each participant was encouraged to identify coping skills they already use in times of stress. The PI had an informal discussion with the participants about their experience with their disease and attitudes toward mental health. Patients with a PHQ9 ≥ 10 or SCARED ≥ 25, were offered the option of having the PI contact the clinic social worker or Colorado Pediatric Psychiatry Consultation & Access Program navigator for further resources.

Clinical data

We obtained physician global assessment (PhGA) scores from the clinical note on the day of recruitment or via verbal inquiry to the primary rheumatologist. This data was important because the PhGA, a physician’s global estimate of disease activity, has been shown to correlate with the number of active joints, elevation of ESR/CRP, presence of fevers, rashes, serositis, etc. The PhGA is part of the Wallace criteria of clinically inactive disease in JIA subtypes (best possible score on the scale used, per physician’s discretion) [21]. Prior literature has used PhGA 0–1 as low disease activity [22, 23]. We used a cut-off of PhGA > 1 as active disease. Information about insurance, current medications, presence of any disease complications or organ damage, SLEDAI (Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index), joint count, and JDM DAS (Disease Activity Score) were all obtained from the medical record. The SLEDAI, joint count, and DAS are commonly used variables in research studies of rheumatic diseases and in clinical care to track disease activity. Active disease was defined as SLEDAI ≥ 4 for SLE patients [24–26], active joint count > 1 [4, 27] for JIA patients, or DAS > 3 for JDM patients [28], with scores below those thresholds defined as “low disease activity”. Disease complications encountered were joint erosions, contractures, macrophage activating syndrome, uveitis, kidney disease severe enough to require dialysis or transplant, and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage among others. Prior mental health diagnoses were self-reported by the study participants.

Safety protocols for suicidality

If a patient disclosed possible suicidal ideation, verbally or on the PHQ9, we used the Ask Suicide-Screening Questionnaire (ASQ) and the ASQ follow up, the Brief Suicide Safety Assessment, to further explore the frequency of suicidal thoughts, presence of a plan, access to means, prior suicidal attempts, and social supports [29]. Imminent risk would prompt us to contact the emergency department behavioral health social worker and to notify an accepting physician of patient transfer. Patients with passive suicidal ideation alone were strongly encouraged to complete a provided Safety Plan and identify trusted friends, family members, or health care providers should symptoms get worse. As stated in the informed consent, suicidality, passive or active, was disclosed to the parent(s) and the patient’s primary rheumatologist. The need for social work consultation for more long-term follow-up was discussed with the primary rheumatologist on a case-by-case basis.

Follow up

Four weeks after the initial screening and receipt of mental health resources, participants received a one-time RedCap survey link via text or email, based on their preference, with two automated reminders to fill out the survey. The follow-up questions asked whether patients used any of the mental health resources provided, if they found it helpful to have screening done by their rheumatologist, and other similar questions as seen in Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were used for patient demographics. Mann Whitney U tests were used to evaluate the relationship between high (≥ 3) and low (< 3) PGA scores and various ordinal patient variables – joint count (0–10), SLEDAI (0-105), PhGA (0–10), PHQ9 (0–27), and SCARED (0–82). Fischer’s exact test was used to evaluate correlations between the PGA and categorical or dichotomous variables (number of patients with active disease by joint count or SLEDAI, patients with PhGA > 1, presence or absence of disease complications, patients with clinically significant depression or anxiety by PHQ9 ≥ 10 or SCARED ≥ 25, and number of patients currently using steroids). The high vs. low PGA cutoff was based on our sample’s median and mean scores. A Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated to look at the relationship of the PGA to other variables. Chi-square tests were done to evaluate the relationship between patients’ self-selected reasons for their PGA score and presence of active disease, presence of any disease-related complications, PHQ9 ≥ 10, and SCARED ≥ 25. Nonparametric equality-of-medians test was used to compare the medians between follow-up survey responders and non-responders in Table 7. All p-values less than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. Feedback survey responses were reported in percentages. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software.

Results

Patient characteristics

There were no statistically significant differences between study participants and eligible non-participants with regard to age, sex, race, disease duration, disease activity scores, current medications, or history of a mental health diagnosis (Table 2). 75% of the participants were biologically female. The race/ethnicity distribution in our sample and the eligible population is also similar to the current US Census Bureau data for the state of Colorado. Median disease duration was 38 months. The median SLEDAI in patients with lupus was 2.00, and median joint count score was 1.63. Thirty-seven (52.1%) participants had at least one disease complication or history of organ damage, compared to 42 (37.2%) non-participants (p = 0.049).

Mental health screening results

The prevalence of a prior known mental health diagnosis in our study population compared to eligible non-participants was similar (38% vs. 42%, X2 = 0.319, p = 0.57).

The mean PGA score was 2.96 with a median of 2.00 and the mean PhGA score was 1.58 with a median of 2.00. The top three most frequently selected reasons for choosing a PGA score were pain (49.3%), fatigue (46.4%), and feeling anxious (43.6%). Thirty-one patients (43.7%) had either a PHQ ≥ 10 or SCARED ≥ 25, including twelve patients (16.9%) who had both. The median PHQ9 score among the 71 participants was 6 (3–9), which did not differ significantly across age. Sixteen patients (22.5%) had moderate to severe symptoms of depression. Four patients answered yes to PHQ9 question 9 asking about suicidality; each had a PHQ9 ≥ 10 and three of the four had a SCARED ≥ 25. Two of the four had a prior known diagnosis of a mental health disorder.

Twenty-seven (38%) patients had clinically significant symptoms of anxiety based on the total score. Some patients met the individual thresholds for different types of anxiety included in the screener without reaching a total SCARED ≥ 25. Nineteen (27%) patients met the threshold for panic/somatic symptoms, twenty-eight (39%) for generalized anxiety, fourteen (20%) for separation anxiety, nineteen (27%) or social anxiety, and twenty-three (32%) for anxiety related to school avoidance. There was no difference in anxiety type or the proportion meeting clinically significant anxiety threshold between patients in different age groups (all p ≥ 0.06).

Twenty-seven (38%) participants had a known prior history of a mental health diagnosis (self-disclosed). 14/27 (51.8%) were disclosed being on a medication to treat anxiety or depression. 16/27 (59.2%) reported having either a psychiatrist, counselor, or a therapist as part of their care. Of the twenty-seven, seventeen (62%) patients had either clinically significant anxiety or depression symptoms identified by the screeners. 9/17 (52.9%) were on medication for anxiety or depression. 10/17 (58.8%) had either a psychiatrist, counselor, or therapist.

Relationship between PGA scores and mental health symptoms

Patients with high PGA scores had significantly higher PHQ-9 scores (3-point difference, p = 0.01) and SCARED scores (9.5-point difference, p = 0.01) (Table 3). They were more likely to have clinically significant depressive (32.4% vs. 11.8%, p = 0.05) or anxiety symptoms (48.6% vs. 26.5%, p = 0.05) compared to patients with low PGA scores. Both the PHQ-9 and SCARED scores had statistically significant weak positive correlations with the PGA score (Table 4). Patients with clinically significant depressive symptoms had higher median PGA scores than those without (4.5 and 2 respectively, p = 0.009) (Table 5). Patients with clinically significant anxiety symptoms also had higher median PGA scores by 2 points, but this did not reach statistical significance with p = 0.114. In an exploratory analysis of the relationship with a higher PGA cutoff (PGA > 5) we saw that patients with very high PGA scores were more likely to have higher joint counts (p = 0.04), PhGA scores (p = < 0.001), and be on steroids currently (p = < 0.001).

Self-reported reasons for PGA scores

Patients with high disease activity (based on SLEDAI, active joint count, or DAS) were more likely to report swollen joints (44% vs. 19.6%, p = 0.029) and inability to participate in sports (52% vs. 21.7%, p = 0.009) as reasons for their PGA scores compared with patients with low disease activity. Patients with one or more disease complications were more likely to report feeling stressed about their disease (48.6% vs. 14.7%, p = 0.002) and inability to participate in sports (43.2% vs. 20.6%, p = 0.042) as reasons for their PGA scores compared to patients with no disease complications. Patients with clinically significant depressive or anxiety symptoms were more likely to report trouble sleeping, feeling stressed about their disease, feeling anxious, feeling sad/depressed, having to take too many medications, and inability to participate in sports/activities as reasons for their PGA score compared to patients without clinically significant depression or anxiety symptoms. Patients with clinically significant anxiety symptoms were also more likely to report pain, feeling sad/depressed, and changes in physical appearance as reasons for their PGA scores (Table 6).

Post screening surveys

Twenty-four of seventy-one participants (33.8%) completed the follow-up survey, with very positive feedback overall, (Fig. 2). There were no statistically significant differences between follow-up survey responders and non-responders with regard to age, sex, disease activity, history of a mental health diagnosis, PGA score, physician global score, PHQ9 and SCARED scores (Table 7). Most patients (82.6%) were somewhat to very comfortable talking to a doctor or therapist about their mood. Many patients felt that it was at least somewhat helpful to learn about their emotions (95.4%), to get resources about mental health (87%), and to talk about their emotions and mood (95.7%). Sixteen (66.7%) participants stated that they had read through the reading materials and handouts about mental health given by the PI. Two patients had started looking for a mental health therapist, while six had found one and/or had an appointment with a therapist within four weeks of study enrollment.

Participant feedback on mental health screening in rheumatology

Verbal feedback from participants

Quotes were documented from informal discussions with patients, siblings, and guardians about the rheumatologist’s role in mental health screening. One example of feedback from a patient was: “I wish I had this when I was younger. It took a long time to get diagnosed with my arthritis […] and that caused stress.” Patients also shared advice for others with similar diagnoses: “I want to tell other [teens], there will be ups and downs, but you’ll get through it.” Parents shared: “I want a doctor to tell these teenagers that this is normal because there’s a stigma around “Oh, you’re seeing a therapist”, “You guys should definitely ask these questions [about mental health]”, and “I think it’s great that you’re doing this”. As we were also looking into patient awareness of their symptoms, one patient who was asked “Were you surprised by your anxiety score?” said: “Actually, yeah!”

Patients and families also brought up several issues that were not addressed by the study. In regard to medical guilt and medical trauma, one patient who had a severe course of disease initially with a prolonged PICU stay said “My family got split up because I was sick and it’s my fault.” Another patient’s parent highlighted the importance of including the whole family in the discussion of impacts of a chronic disease: “The first year when she was diagnosed was hard on our family and the marriage. People don’t talk about that.” Several parents requested resources on grief over the loss of a normal life, one stating: “I want to see resources on – being able to not miss school, doing sports, and not missing out on social activities.”

Discussion

Relationships between PGA, mental health, and reason for score selection

Children with rheumatic disease face many challenges during their disease course that impact their quality of life. Our study showed that how children with JIA and SLE perceive those challenges and their impact differs whether they have significant levels of anxiety or depression. Like children with other chronic diseases, children with rheumatic disease have clinically significant symptoms of anxiety (38%) and depression (22.5%). This study showed that patients with higher PGA scores were more likely to have clinically significant depression and anxiety symptoms. This effect was not mediated by history of diagnosed mental health disease, but rather the degree of current symptoms. Interestingly, our study showed that the reasons patients selected their PGA score were different for children with high vs. low disease activity and the presence vs. absence of clinically significant anxiety or depression.

This study provides rheumatologists with valuable information about the patient experience of children with JIA and SLE. It suggests that patients with anxiety and depression choose their score based on symptoms less related to their rheumatic disease activity and that using this score to calculate a patient’s objective disease state may be misleading for both clinical care and research. It is critical to obtain more detailed information on what informs a patient’s score to avoid inappropriate interventions.

Interestingly, patients with high or low disease activity were equally as likely to select pain as a reason for their PGA score. Assessments of pain are important in every rheumatology clinic visit, as pain is often a high priority topic for patients and considered a “fifth vital sign” by some providers. Degree of pain is often very individualized and mediated by mental health and the brain-body axis. In our study, pain did not appear to consistently indicate active disease.

It is possible that suffering from symptoms of high anxiety aggravates the negative experience of lupus or arthritis. For example, patients with anxiety were more likely to select their PGA score based on pain, fatigue, and changes in physical appearance, in addition to the symptoms listed previously. However, only roughly a third of patients with a high anxiety score had clinically active disease, indicating that it is the presence of anxiety that makes patients likely to experience more negative symptoms related to the PGA, rather than active disease alone. Our data show that roughly half of patients with high anxiety symptoms lacked an established prior diagnosis of anxiety (or chose not to disclose it) indicating a potential critical gap in care.

Impact of inability to participate in activities and loss of normalcy

We saw in our results that inability to participate in sports and activities correlated with multiple variables including active disease, one or more disease complications, and clinically significant depression or anxiety symptoms. This was corroborated by the PI’s informal discussions and verbal feedback from families. Rheumatologists can do a better job of preparing patients and families to face the changes that may happen with a chronic disease diagnosis and how to maintain a sense of normalcy.

Parents of children with juvenile myositis have advocated for a variety of resources to be available for them and their children to address emotional health, including support groups, social media presence, educational materials, and mobile/media interventions [30]. After receiving some of these resources, feedback data suggest that the materials were well received, although a more detailed and focused follow up should be used in future studies to assess the effects of self-help materials.

Acceptance and attitudes about screening

Overall, the patient population was very receptive to participating in the study and answering these vulnerable, personal questions. Very few patients declined to participate due to disinterest. Typically, if the approached patients could not enroll, this was due to time restraints, an overwhelming or busy visit, or otherwise poor timing for the family. The verbal feedback we received from participants, as seen above, suggests that screening alone could be an important intervention and potentially put a name to symptoms that a patient may be experiencing but not fully understanding.

Rheumatologists have much more frequent follow-up visits with their patients and therefore, more frequent opportunities to identify and address mental health concerns, but this is not currently standard practice [31]. Treatment of rheumatic diseases includes immunosuppressive agents (oral, injectable or intravenous forms), frequent doctor appointments and lab work. In the first year of diagnosis, it is standard of care to follow up with patients with SLE and JDM about every 1–2 months and patients with JIA every 3–4 months [32–34]. In subsequent years, the timing is spaced out, however it still often exceeds the frequency of annual well-child visits done by a patient’s primary care doctor. Out of 119 pediatric rheumatologists surveyed recently, 92 (77%) agreed that routine screening for depression and anxiety should be done in pediatric rheumatology clinic [16, 35]. This is potential area of opportunity for developing pediatric rheumatologic care to further improve patient’s lives that aligns with patient and provider perspectives.

Limitations

Our study involved a small sample of the patient population and may not be generalizable to the national population of pediatric rheumatology patients. The race/ethnicity distribution in our sample and the eligible population is similar to the current US Census Bureau data for the state of Colorado and the predominantly female sample is consistent with previously reported literature showing stronger predilection of rheumatic conditions, especially JIA and SLE, in females [36]. Sub-analyses of patients with specific JIA subtypes or comparisons between rheumatic diseases were not performed due to sample sizes. Much of our patient sample had low disease activity (joint count < 1 or SLEDAI < 4). While this was representative of a larger eligible nonparticipant pool, the study results could be different if repeated in a sicker patient sample or those with higher disease activity. Patients who agreed to participate in the study could have prior knowledge, concern or interest in mental health care. It is unclear why the study population included a higher proportion of patients with disease complications. Lack of a free-text option for other symptoms contributing to the PGA could have led the participants to believe that the symptoms/reasons listed are the expected or only possibilities, however, we tried to make it clear in the instructions that this was not the case. Exclusion of patients who could not read/understand English is also a limitation as it is possible that the language barrier added an additional layer of complexity to their rheumatic disease.

Practice changes and future directions

We have shown that exploring the reasons behind a patient’s PGA score could be valuable in their care. In our clinic, in response to this study, providers reported asking patients about their chosen score and considered adding a follow up question exploring reasons for selecting a PGA score in the clinic note template. Mental health screening was completed on paper in this study, but automation and integration into the patients’ medical record is feasible and could allow for widespread screening and triage of all rheumatology patients. Future studies could look at how medical guilt or medical trauma affects the patients’ PGA score, and their perception of their rheumatic disease compared to those who had a disease course without significant events or hospitalizations. This study suggests that finding ways to evaluate and support mental health in the pediatric rheumatology setting may be equally as important as consistently evaluating and treating disease activity in helping youths with rheumatologic diseases thrive.
