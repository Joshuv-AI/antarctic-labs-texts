DSA Versus Noninvasive Imaging in Revascularization Referral for Recently Symptomatic Carotid Stenosis
Authors: Jane Khalife, Hamail Iqbal, Manisha Koneru, Zachary Padron, Joshua Vignolles‐Jeong, Alex Keister, Joshua Weinberg, Krystal Hunter, Renato Oliveira, Joshua Santucci, Ahmad Ballout, Hamza A Shaikh, Daniel A Tonetti, Pratit D Patel, Ajith J Thomas, Tudor G Jovin, Arsida Bajrami, Serdar Geyik, Shahid M Nimjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDCurrent revascularization guidelines for symptomatic carotid artery stenosis were established based on randomized studies where stenosis was assessed with digital subtraction angiography. Pursuing revascularization in clinical practice is typically based on noninvasive imaging. We aimed to assess whether discrepancies exist between these 2 diagnostic methods when patients with recently symptomatic carotid stenosis referred for carotid revascularization based on noninvasive imaging, subsequently underwent DSA.METHODSA retrospective study of patients presenting at 3 centers in the United States and Türkiye who routinely performed digitally subtracted angiography prior to revascularization between 2019 and 2024 was performed. Consecutive patients with acute ischemic events and concern for symptomatic ipsilateral carotid artery stenosis with moderate to severe stenosis on noninvasive imaging referred for digitally subtracted angiography were selected for inclusion. Digitally subtracted angiography was performed for intended carotid artery stenting in most cases as the first‐line treatment method; patients referred for carotid endarterectomy who underwent preprocedure digitally subtracted angiography were also included. Exclusion criteria included prior carotid endarterectomy or carotid artery stenting or the presence of intraluminal thrombus precluding immediate revascularization. The primary outcome was the rate of disagreement between noninvasive imaging and digitally subtracted angiography.RESULTSA total of 463 patients (65% males) were included, with a median age of 69 years. Disagreement regarding qualifying lesion severity for revascularization between noninvasive imaging and digitally subtracted angiography was found in 22.7% of patients. There was a significant difference in the proportion of immediate revascularization between the 2 cohorts (P<0.001). A total of 66/105 (63%) of those in the disagreement cohort were determined to have an etiology of stroke alternative to large artery atherosclerosis, requiring further diagnostic evaluation.CONCLUSIONThis study demonstrates that almost 1 in 4 patients referred for carotid revascularization based on noninvasive imaging are found to have insufficient stenosis on digitally subtracted angiography to support the decision to proceed with revascularization according to established stenosis severity thresholds.Keywords: carotid artery stenting, carotid disease, carotid revascularization, stenosis

Stroke carries a significant public health burden as it is the leading cause of adult disability and the fifth leading cause of death in the United States. 1 Extracranial carotid stenosis accounts for 8% to15% of new strokes and up to 15% to20% of all ischemic strokes. 2 , 3 In patients with symptomatic stenosis of 50% or more, seminal randomized trials have demonstrated the benefit of carotid endarterectomy (CEA) over medical therapy, resulting in lower rates of recurrent cerebral or ocular ischemic events than medical management. 4 , 5 Although carotid artery stenting (CAS) has not been compared against medical therapy in patients with symptomatic artery disease, recent randomized trials suggest it is equivalent to CEA. Specifically, carotid revascularization confers the highest benefit in the first 2 weeks from the index event, as evidenced by the number needed to treat of 5 with CEA to prevent 1 ipsilateral stroke in 5 years when patients were treated within 2 weeks of symptoms in those with stenosis >70% and ∼15 in those with stenosis 50% to 69%. 6

In most centers, detection of carotid stenosis and operative referral for revascularization is largely based on noninvasive imaging, including Doppler ultrasound (DUS) and computed tomography angiography (CTA). 7 However, both in NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial) and ECST (European Carotid Surgery Trial), the indication for revascularization was established based on results from digital subtraction angiography (DSA). 8 Accordingly, DSA has long served as the gold standard for verification of the severity of stenosis; discrepant results between DSA and noninvasive imaging modalities have been previously reported. 2 , 8 Poor concordance between CTA‐derived area stenosis calculations and DSA via the NASCET method is well known with CTA often indicating a higher degree of stenosis. 9 A single‐center study reported that nearly 1 in 5 patients with recent stroke due to ipsilateral carotid stenosis deemed to be candidates for revascularization based on CTA or MRA, DSA led to the institution of medical therapy only due to insufficiently severe stenosis. 12 We aim to perform a multicenter analysis of the ultimate management strategy and outcomes of patients who, based on noninvasive imaging, were deemed candidates for carotid revascularization and subsequently underwent DSA imaging.

Patients

Consecutive patients presenting with symptomatic carotid stenosis at 3 tertiary referral centers (2 comprehensive stroke centers in the United States between 2019 and 2022 and between 2022 and 2024 at a center in Türkiye) were retrospectively reviewed. At these centers, during the respective periods and as regular practice, the indication for any revascularization is established based on angiographic measurement of stenosis, with the intention to perform transfermoral CAS as the first choice. Qualifying patients who were deemed candidates for revascularization and who are poor candidates for CAS are then referred for CEA or transcarotid artery revascularization. Study inclusion criteria were (1) acute ocular or cerebral ischemic events; (2) CTA of the neck, DUS, or both showing moderate (50%–69%) to severe stenosis (70%‐99%) and/or complete occlusion; and (3) referral for revascularization by the treating vascular neurologist. Patients were excluded if they had prior ipsilateral CEA or CAS, or if there was the presence of intraluminal thrombus or a nonatherosclerotic etiology for carotid disease.

Data Collection

Demographic data were collected, including age, sex, race, and past medical history. The presence of cardiac abnormalities, such as a dilated left atrium and patent foramen ovale, was collected. Serum laboratory values were collected. Stroke characteristics collected included: recent cerebrovascular stroke or transient ischemic attack, laterality of stenosis, and etiology of stroke. The percentage of patients undergoing each type of noninvasive imaging evaluation was recorded. The degree of stenosis seen on either CTA, DUS, or DSA was measured using the NASCET criteria on 2‐dimensional imaging. High‐risk plaque features and the presence of calcifications were collected. The primary outcome was the percentage of patients undergoing revascularization based on catheter angiography confirmation of a qualifying stenosis for revascularization.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR); categorical data were reported as frequencies. Data were analyzed in the entire cohort, and then among subcohorts stratified based on whether noninvasive imaging‐based and DSA‐based stenosis measurements were mismatched (ie, CTA/DUS >50% versus DSA <50%; Figure 1) or in agreement (ie, CTA/DUS/DSA >50%). Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐test or nonparametric Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, and categorical variables were compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. Missing data were minimal and not imputed. Statistical significance was prespecified at 0.05. Analyses were conducted in JMP v18.0.0 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC).

In the stenosis agreement group (CTA = DSA), the majority of patients were found to have large artery atherosclerosis as the primary etiology of stroke (74.9%) (Table 1). A significantly greater proportion of patients with stenosis mismatch (CTA≠DSA) had a stroke etiology other than large artery atherosclerosis, requiring further evaluation (P<0.001). This was statistically significant for different etiologies, including cardioembolic (mismatch: 24.8% versus agreement: 8.4%; P<0.001) or other (mismatch: 22.9% versus agreement: 6.4%; P<0.001) stroke etiologies (Table 1).

Fewer than half of the patients were treated with CAS (41.7%; Table 2). There was a significant difference in the proportion of CAS in between the stenosis measurement mismatch and agreement cohorts (P<0.001; Table 2). Among patients with stenosis mismatch, only 1.9% underwent CAS, whereas 53.4% of patients with stenosis agreement underwent CAS (Table 2; Figure 2). Additional patients in the stenosis agreement group were treated with CEA or with transcarotid artery revascularization; however, these data were not collected. In the agreement group, the median stenosis on CTA was 80% (IQR 70–99) and 70% on DUS (IQR 60–85; Table 2). In the mismatch group, the median stenosis on CTA was 60% (IQR 50–69.5) and 64% on DUS (50–70; Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Our findings highlight a discrepancy concerning the accuracy of assessing stenosis. The overestimation of stenosis by noninvasive imaging may lead to potential overreferral to procedures performed below the standard threshold of 50% stenosis. Our main finding is that 22.7% of these patients do not qualify for intervention after undergoing DSA. Our experience expands upon a recent prospective study, which similarly showed nearly 1 in 5 patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis referred for interventional revascularization based on non‐invasive imaging actually harbored nonstenotic disease, resulting in medical management alone rather than revascularization. 10 The majority of patients in that series with nonstenotic disease (<50%) were found to harbor irregular plaques consistent with symptomatic nonstenotic carotid disease per established classification schema. 11 Furthermore, 6‐month follow‐up of these patients who were found to have nonqualifying lesions based on angiography and therefore were managed with medical therapy alone, demonstrated an event rate inconsistent with that of symptomatic angiographically proven stenosis, validating the medical therapy approach in lieu of revascularization. The results of the present larger, multicenter analysis support these findings.

Our results additionally show that the likelihood of disagreement in revascularization eligibility between the 2 modalities is more common in the lower range of stenosis on the pre‐DSA non‐invasive study. This is congruent with current literature, 12 , 13 which shows that when noninvasive imaging is showing moderate stenosis (50%‐69%), there remains uncertainty on whether the stenosis is >50% on DSA.

Our findings indicate that non‐invasive modalities should be interpreted thoughtfully in guiding final treatment decisions for recently symptomatic carotid stenosis, particularly in the moderate range. These findings are relevant for current clinical practice because patients are routinely referred for revascularization based on noninvasive imaging, with first‐line CEA or CAS depending on the treating center. 14 However, those referred for CEA do not typically undergo conventional angiography prior to surgery as routinely done in CAS, and consequently, the qualifying degree of stenosis remains unconfirmed, potentially resulting in unnecessary surgery in as high as 20% of patients. In that context, standardizing angiography prior to revascularization may reduce the number of interventions for which there is no evidence of benefit. 13 , 15 , 16 The tradeoff of increased procedural risk and cost of DSA may be justified given the higher incidence of stroke and myocardial infarction encountered with revascularization, especially in recently symptomatic patients. 17

Conventional angiography also increases the diagnostic certainty of the etiology of the stroke, guiding neurologists to perform additional investigations when the degree of carotid stenosis is insufficient to be the etiology of a stroke. In our data, only 37.1% of the patients in the mismatch group were found to have large artery atherosclerosis, presumably symptomatic nonstenotic carotid disease as the stroke etiology, with 24.8% found to have a cardioembolic source, prompting appropriate medical treatment regimens that are more effective in reducing recurrent stroke risk. As such, identifying the true etiology of the stroke is especially important to determine the appropriate secondary prevention strategy. 18 , 19 , 20

Evidence regarding the accuracy of measuring stenosis across imaging modalities is variable. 3 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Both hypertension and significant plaque calcification have been associated with overestimation of stenosis on CTA. 3 , 21 , 24 Highly calcified plaques create acoustic shadows that obscure the vessel lumen, preventing accurate measurement of stenosis or velocity. 21 , 26 , 27 Our analysis, however, showed that calcifications were not more prevalent in the mismatch group, possibly because our data did not stratify the degree of calcification where more severely calcified lesions would have potentially been found to have higher rates of mismatch (Table 2).

Limitations

Limitations of this study include factors inherent to the retrospective study design, including confounding. Furthermore, there may be variability in operator interpretation of imaging studies. Maximal stenosis on DSA was assessed by obtaining multiple 2‐dimensional views and using NASCET measuring techniques periprocedurally; however, this was performed by the operating neurointerventionalist in each case, reducing precision, thereby introducing variability in how angiography was carried out with potential underrepresentation of accurate > 50% stenosis with suboptimal angiographic views in those patients who were found to have nonqualifying stenosis. Although this study was multicenter, prospective studies using uniform DSA protocols comparing all noninvasive modalities, including DUS, CTA, and MRA, in larger cohorts will expand the generalizability of the results. Although our findings apply mainly to noninvasive imaging with CTA alone, we find that this is less likely to affect the study's conclusion, as patients in clinical practice are routinely referred based on 1 modality alone, with some studies showing CTA to be more accurate compared with DUS. 28 However, combining CTA and DUS results for referral could decrease the pre‐DSA discrepancy. Furthermore, anticoagulation status prior to presentation was not collected and could have influenced the stroke etiology given there is a high number of patients with comorbid atrial fibrillation. Additionally, this study does not address long‐term stroke rates in patients not subjected to revascularization to validate the decision not to intervene when prior studies have shown this to be the case. Future studies are needed to classify such patient‐related factors and assess the cost‐effectiveness and safety of noninvasive compared to invasive imaging modalities in evaluating carotid artery stenosis.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that about 1 in 4 patients referred for revascularization based on noninvasive imaging were found to have nonqualifying stenosis on DSA. Diagnostic angiography should be increasingly used in the evaluation and management of patients with recent symptomatic carotid artery disease with significant stenosis.
