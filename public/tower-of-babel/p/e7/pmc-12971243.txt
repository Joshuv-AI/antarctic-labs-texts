A Novel Dual‐Scope Method for Safe Endobronchial Ultrasound‐Guided Transbronchial Mediastinal Cryobiopsy
Authors: Mikito Suzuki, Kaito Yano, Tomohiro Imoto, Reiko Shimizu, Kazuo Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConventional endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy (EBUS‐TBMC) requires simultaneous withdrawal of the EBUS scope and cryoprobe to retrieve the specimen. This process complicates immediate haemostasis and frequently requires cumbersome intubation to ensure procedural safety. As airway haemorrhage can be fatal, a prompt response must always be ensured despite its rare occurrence. We have developed a novel dual‐scope method for EBUS‐TBMC. This technique involves the simultaneous transoral insertion of a thin bronchoscope for haemostasis alongside the EBUS scope for cryobiopsy without intubation. Immediate haemostasis under real‐time visualisation was performed using a thin bronchoscope remaining in the airway after withdrawal of the EBUS scope. This approach eliminates the ‘blind time’ experienced in conventional methods, significantly enhancing overall procedural safety. Herein, we report a case of methotrexate‐associated lymphoproliferative disorder (malignant lymphoma) that was successfully diagnosed using the dual‐scope method.

Introduction

Endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy (EBUS‐TBMC) is a novel biopsy method for hilar and mediastinal lesions. However, conventional EBUS‐TBMC requires simultaneous removal of the EBUS scope and cryoprobe to collect specimen, complicating immediate haemostasis and may necessitate intubation for sufficient safety [1].

Hanawa et al. developed a dual‐scope method as a novel haemostatic technique for transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) [2], involving simultaneous transoral therapeutic scope insertion for haemostasis with ultrathin/thin bronchoscope for cryobiopsy without intubation. Immediate haemostasis under real‐time visualisation is achieved with therapeutic bronchoscope in the airway immediately after scope withdrawal. We applied this established technique for TBLC to EBUS‐TBMC. We report a case of malignant lymphoma diagnosed using this method. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokyo Metropolitan Cancer and Infectious Diseases Center of Komagome Hospital (approval number: 3601).

Case Report

A woman in her 60s had a nocturnal fever and 1.5‐month history of weight loss. Her medical history included rheumatoid arthritis since her 30s, managed with prednisolone and tacrolimus. Subcutaneous methotrexate (MTX) (7.5 mg/week) was introduced 2 months prior. Laboratory tests revealed an elevated interleukin‐2 levels (3075 U/mL). Computed tomography demonstrated multiple mediastinal and intra‐peritoneal lymphadenopathies, suggesting malignant lymphoma; therefore, EBUS‐TBMC was planned for diagnosis (Figure 1a).

(A) Computed tomography demonstrates multiple swollen mediastinal lymph nodes. (B) Schematic of dual‐scope setup. The light source cable of the endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) scope is disconnected from the video system centre (CLV‐290; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), while the EBUS processor is connected (arrow). The thin bronchoscope (BF‐P290; Olympus) for haemostasis is then connected to the video system centre (arrowhead). This configuration allows the procedure to be completed using a single endoscopy tower. (C) Simultaneous insertion of the EBUS scope (arrow) and thin bronchoscope (arrowhead) into the airway. (D) Endoscopic views: The EBUS image is displayed on the main monitor, and the thin bronchoscope image on the sub‐display. The thin bronchoscope is inserted using the sub‐display, while the 1.1‐mm cryoprobe (arrow) is inserted into the station 4R lymph node.

The procedure targeted a station 4R lymph node (LN) using an EBUS scope (UC‐290FW; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). First, two passes of transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) were performed using a 22‐guage needle (NA‐U401SX; Olympus) to create an airway tract for the TBMC. Subsequently, the suction valve was removed, and a 1.1‐mm cryoprobe (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany) was inserted via this tract under ultrasound guidance. Direct cryobiopsy through the working channel was performed three times with a freezing time of 3–4 s each [3]. Finally, the dual‐scope method was employed. The light source cable of the EBUS scope was disconnected from the video system centre (CLV‐290; Olympus), and a thin bronchoscope (BF‐P290; Olympus) for haemostasis was connected to the EBUS processor (Figure 1b). The EBUS and thin bronchoscope images were set on the main and sub‐displays, respectively. While the first operator inserted the cryoprobe into the LN, the second operator transorally inserted the bronchoscope alongside the EBUS scope under sub‐display guidance; this manoeuvre took 54 s (Figure 1c,d). After a 9‐s freezing time, the scope, cryoprobe and specimen were withdrawn en bloc. Immediate haemostasis was achieved using the indwelling bronchoscope, with only mild bleeding observed. The total procedure duration was 22 min.

Pathological examination of the cryobiopsy specimens revealed atypical large lymphocytes with two or more nuclei, which were positive for CD30, and latent membrane protein 1, but negative for CD3 and CD20. Given the scarcity of background lymphocytes, a diagnosis of classical Hodgkin lymphoma (lymphocyte‐depleted sub‐type) was made (Figure 2). Based on the history of MTX exposure, the final diagnosis was MTX‐associated lymphoproliferative disorder (MTX‐LPD). MTX was immediately discontinued, and the patient is currently under observation in the outpatient department.

The pathological findings. The cryobiopsy specimens revealed atypical large lymphocytes with multi‐nucleated cells consistent with Reed‐Sternberg cells.

Discussion

Given that 66.7% of MTX‐LPD cases present with spontaneous regression, obtaining sufficient specimen volume is critical for accurate diagnosis [4]. We selected EBUS‐TBMC using the dual‐scope method owing to its higher diagnostic yield (89.7% vs. 27.6%) and sub‐classification ability for malignant lymphoma compared to TBNA [1].

The dual‐scope method is a novel approach to achieve haemostasis during cryobiopsy by allowing simultaneous insertion of two bronchoscopes without intubation, preventing the ‘blind time’ after EBUS scope removal with immediate haemostasis [2].

Severe bleeding in TBMC occurs in only 1%–2% of cases and is not life threatening [1]. This low rate is primarily because, unlike in TBLC, the bleeding originates from intervening intra‐nodal vessels, not major pulmonary vessels, which can be avoided via Doppler imaging. Additionally, bleeding drains only through a small airway tract. Haemostasis could be achieved in previous studies by rapidly reinserting the EBUS scope after specimen collection. However, as airway haemorrhage could be fatal, a prompt response should be achieved despite its rarity. As a fool‐proof measure against unpredictable haemorrhage, adding an indwelling thin bronchoscope enhanced safety, a goal achieved in approximately 1 min. Given this efficiency, we consider routine use acceptable, although the technique is particularly indicated during introductory phase, for hyper‐vascular lesions, or when TBNA causes visible bleeding.

We consider a thin bronchoscope the optimal choice for haemostasis using this dual‐scope method. Given the 2.2‐mm working channel of the EBUS scope, the thin bronchoscope's 2.0‐mm channel can offer comparable haemostasis. Furthermore, the EBUS scope has a large outer diameter; therefore, damage to the vocal cords with the insertion of two bronchoscopes should be considered. However, by using the thin bronchoscope, the combined outer diameter with the EBUS scope becomes 10.8 mm, which is sufficiently smaller than that of the 35 and 37 Fr double‐lumen intubation tubes (11.7 and 12.3 mm, respectively) used in thoracic surgery [5]. Consequently, using a therapeutic bronchoscope is physically tolerable in cases with a high bleeding risk. If necessary, a balloon catheter can also be positioned through a second scope.

We inserted a second bronchoscope under a sub‐display view, allowing simultaneous cryoprobe insertion under real‐time EBUS imaging, thereby ensuring time efficiency. This technique offers cost advantages when a single endoscopic tower is used.

We also employed a direct collection (DC) method to retrieve specimen through the working channel, enabling non‐intubated cryobiopsy with a freezing time of approximately 4 s without scope removal [3]. The retained position facilitated retrieving biopsies from the same area. We propose that combining DC and dual‐scope methods ensures safety and optimal specimen retrieval, requiring no specialised equipment beyond a second bronchoscope, but necessitating additional operator and procedural familiarity. Further case accumulation is required to evaluate its safety and efficiency.
