Short-term variations in summer temperatures and mental health-related emergency department visits in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Shaainee Ramnawaz, Eric Lavigne, Jura Augustinavicius, Scott Weichenthal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The acute effects of summer temperatures on mental health are not yet fully understood, particularly with respect to potential effect modifiers.Methods:We conducted a time-stratified case-crossover study to examine the association between daily variations in summer ambient and apparent temperatures and the risk of emergency department (ED) visits for mental health outcomes Ontario, Canada, from 2004 and 2019. Conditional logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per 5 °C increase in ambient or apparent temperature, adjusting for potential time-varying confounders including PM2.5, O3, and NO2. We also examined potential effect modification by social and material deprivation, as well as characteristics of the built environment.Results:Three-day mean ambient temperature was associated with an increased risk of ED visits for all mental health outcomes combined (OR = 1.022; 95% CI: 1.017, 1.027). This association persisted after adjustment for outdoor air pollutants (OR = 1.019; 95% CI: 1.014, 1.025). Three-day mean ambient temperatures were most strongly associated with disorders due to psychoactive substance use (OR = 1.043; 95% CI: 1.032, 1.054). Social deprivation modified the strength of observed associations (interaction P-value = 0.001), with 3-day mean ambient temperature associated with an increased risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes among those most socially deprived (OR = 1.032; 95% CI: 1.015, 1.049), but not among those least socially deprived (OR = 0.995; 95% CI: 0.962, 1.029).Conclusions:Our findings suggest that the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes is greater at higher summer temperatures, especially for disorders due to psychoactive substance use and among those most socially deprived.

What this study adds

Increasing evidence suggests that summer heat events are associated with increased risks of adverse mental health outcomes. In this study, we examined the relationship between daily variations in summer temperatures and emergency department visits for mental health outcomes in Ontario, Canada. Our findings suggest that short-term increases in summer temperatures increase the risk of hospitalization for mental health outcomes (particularly for disorders due to psychoactive substance use) and that these risks are greatest among those most socially deprived. Our findings highlight the need for community outreach during summer heat events to mitigate heat-related mental health risks among those lacking strong social support systems.

Introduction

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has found that the adverse impacts on mental health from climate change are increasing.1,2 As it stands, mental disorders already account for a meaningful proportion of global disability-adjusted life years,3 and, in Canada, the economic burden of mental health outcomes is estimated at more than 50 billion per year.4 As climate change impacts worsen globally, the additional annual societal costs of mental disorders attributed to climate change are projected to increase over 10-fold between 2030 and 2050, reaching $245 billion USD in 2050.5

Studies on temperature, including heat, and mental health outcomes make up the largest share of the climate change and mental health peer-reviewed literature globally, and heat is the second most studied exposure in relation to mental health in North America.6–9 While numerous studies have examined associations between ambient temperature and mental health-related hospital visits, there remains a high degree of variation in how studies assess temperature exposures, including assessment of absolute temperature, relative temperature, temperature extremes, and heatwave events.10–17 Within these broader categories, the assessment of ambient temperature versus apparent temperature (i.e., perceived temperature based on ambient temperature, relative humidity, and wind velocity) is less frequently examined.8,18 When classifying mental health outcomes according to ICD categories, some studies have grouped hospital visits related to any mental health outcomes into a single indicator, while others have focused on a specific type of disorder, which limits understanding and comparison of how temperature impacts specific mental health conditions.10,12,19

While the association between heat and mental health-related hospital visits has been widely studied, the mechanisms and factors that exacerbate or protect against these impacts remain poorly understood.8,9 In addition, few studies have specifically examined the association between apparent temperature and mental health-related emergency department (ED) visits over long periods of time and across multiple locations with diverse social deprivation characteristics.13,14,20 Similarly, only a handful of Canadian studies have examined potential modifiers of associations between temperature and mental health outcomes and apparent temperature has rarely been used as an exposure metric in these studies.11,12

In this study, we evaluated associations between ambient and apparent temperatures and the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes overall and by specific type in Ontario, Canada. Moreover, we examined potential effect modification by age, sex, socioeconomic status, and characteristics of the physical environment relevant to heat including tree canopy and building density. Both ambient and apparent temperature were examined to explore the impact of explicitly including the combined impacts of temperature and relative humidity in the apparent temperature metric as opposed to the standard approach of relying only on ambient temperature. This investigation builds on previous evidence using Canadian ED visits for mental health outcomes.11,21

Methods

Study design and location

We conducted a time-stratified case-crossover study22 in Ontario, Canada, to estimate associations between daily variations in summer ambient and apparent temperatures and the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes. We included all cases of ED visits for mental health outcomes registered between June 1 and September 30 during the years 2004–2019, defined based on ICD-10 codes (ICD-10, F00-F99) and excluding disorders of intellectual development (ICD-10 codes included: F00-F69; F80-F99) (Supplemental Table 1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380). We omitted disorders of intellectual development from the analysis owing to the small sample size. We identified three or four control periods for each case, matching on day of the week, month, and year. For example, if a case occurred on the second Friday in June 2005, we designated all other Fridays in June 2005 as control periods. We conducted analyses for all mental health outcomes combined (excluding disorders of intellectual development) as well as for each group of conditions listed in Table 1. We included individuals of all ages in our analyses.

Data collection

We obtained data on ED visits and demographic information from the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS). The NACRS database gathers deidentified ambulatory care data from participating facilities, ministries of health, and regional health authorities in Canada. In Ontario, all facilities are required to report data pertaining to ED visits to NACRS, where the main and other problems prompting a visit are described using ICD-10 codes.23 We limited our analyses to ED visits with mental health outcomes listed as the main problem. We derived environmental and neighborhood characteristics, including the percentage of tree canopy coverage (250 m buffer), the proportion of built areas (250 m buffer), and material and social deprivation indices for the residential locations of each case using data available from the Canadian Urban Environmental Health Research Consortium (CANUE).24 Tree canopy was defined as an area of vegetation of woody plants above 5 m in height. The data were derived from the Global Forest Cover Change (GFCC) Surface Reflectance product (GFCC30SR), which is based on enhanced Global Land Survey datasets. The Global Land Survey dataset is comprised of 30-m resolution pictures taken by Landsat 5 Thematic Mapper and Landsat 7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus in high-quality resolution. CANUE staff retrieved tree canopy cover data from Google Earth Engine for the years 2010 and 2015, extracted values (percent coverage) to postal codes and calculated summary measures (average percent coverage) within a buffer of 250 m from the centroid of postal codes. CANUE staff also produced a measure of building density for all Canadian postal codes in 2019. For any given postal code, built-up areas were calculated as the area of all building footprints (entire or partial) within a buffer of 250 m around the centroid coordinates of postal codes. The material deprivation index was determined from the average income of those between the ages of 25 and 64, the proportion of those aged 15 years and older who were unemployed, and the proportion of those aged 15 years and older without a high school diploma.25 The social deprivation index was calculated based on the proportion of single-parent families, the proportion of those aged 15 years and older who are separated, divorced, or widowed, and the proportion of those aged 15 years and older living alone.25 Material and social deprivation indices were assigned at the dissemination area (DA) level.25 DAs are small, relatively stable geographic unit with an average population of 400–700 persons based on data from the previous Census of Population Program. It is the smallest standard geographic area for which all census data are disseminated. The average size of a DA in Canada can vary significantly depending on the region. In urban areas, DAs are typically smaller, while in rural areas, they can be much larger due to lower population density. On average, a DA covers an area of about 8 km2.

We obtained daily ambient temperature and relative humidity data for each residential location using Daymet.26 The Daymet data provided information of water vapor pressure in the air. We calculated relative humidity based on Bolton’s equation27 that gives the maximum air water saturation based on the temperature. Daymet applies statistical modeling techniques to interpolate and extrapolate weather variables; temperature/apparent temperature exposures were assigned to the entire province of Ontario at 1 km2 spatial resolution. We extracted outdoor ozone (O3), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations from measurements collected as part of the National Air Pollution Surveillance program.28 We assigned daily air pollutant concentrations for PM2.5, NO2, and O3 to each case by averaging all measurements available from fixed-site monitors in a given city (most cities had a single monitor). If a city did not have a National Air Pollution Surveillance monitor, it was assigned values from the nearest monitor. We calculated apparent temperature using the weathermetrics29 package in R and adjusted all models for daily mean O3, NO2, and PM2.5 concentrations using the same lag-times as temperature in each model. Air pollutants were included as potential confounders given increasing evidence suggesting impacts of outdoor air pollution on the brain but models excluding air pollution were also examined. PM2.5 was moderately correlated with NO2 (r = 0.41) and O3 (r = 0.59) and O3 and NO2 and O3 were weakly correlated (r = −0.026). Correlations between ambient temperature and air pollutants were as follows: PM2.5 (r = 0.53), NO2 (r = 0.14), and O3 (r = 0.57).

Statistical analyses

We used conditional logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) describing the relationship between short-term variations in ambient and apparent temperatures and the risk of ED visits for all mental health outcomes combined (excluding disorders of intellectual development) and for each group of conditions. We examined associations between ambient and apparent temperatures and mental health outcomes using lag periods of 0–7 days as well as the 3-day mean (i.e., the mean temperature on the day of the event and the two preceding days). We did not adjust for relative humidity in models examining ambient temperature owing to recent evidence arguing against this approach.30 Instead, we considered apparent temperature, as this metric captures the combined impacts of ambient temperature and relative humidity. Our main analysis modeled ambient and apparent temperatures as linear terms rather than nonlinear terms because the case-crossover study design contrasts exposures within subjects within the same month and year, and thus, the range of exposures contrasted is often narrow.31 However, as sensitivity analyses, we examined additional models using natural cubic splines with 3 or 4 degrees of freedom (i.e., two or three internal equally spaced knots, respectively). Akaike information criterion values were examined for linear and natural spline models to compare model fit. Sensitivity analyses using natural spline models were only conducted for 3-day mean ambient temperatures because risk estimates were strongest for this time-period and the results for apparent temperature were nearly identical to those for ambient temperature. Exposure-response relationships were plotted using the predict() function in R with reference values equivalent to within-strata mean temperatures.

We examined potential effect modification by sex, age, material deprivation, social deprivation, tree canopy, and building density by examining associations across strata of these variables. For analyses examining effect modification by age, we categorized individuals into the following age groups based on previous literature: 17 years and younger, between 18 and 40 years, between 41 and 64 years, and 65 years and older.18,32,33 We divided material and social deprivation indices into five quintiles, the first one being the least materially or socially deprived. We split values for tree canopy and building density into three terciles. The material and social deprivation indices were not correlated (r = −0.0369) and capture different aspects of deprivation. The first tree canopy tercile represents the lowest tree canopy coverage. Similarly, the first building density tercile designates the lowest building density. We conducted formal evaluation of effect modification by including the appropriate product terms in conditional logistic regression models (i.e., product terms between temperature and the strata variables described above) and reporting the corresponding P-value for the interaction term. We conducted all analyses using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) version 4.2.334 and expressed all ORs per 5 °C increase in ambient or apparent temperature.

Results

In total, we included 1,107,614 ED visits for mental health outcomes (excluding disorders of intellectual development) in our analyses. Neurotic, stress-related, and somatoform disorders (32.3%), mental and behavioral disorders due to psychoactive substance use (27.4%), and mood disorders (21.3%) accounted for the highest percentage of the total number of ED visits (Supplemental Table 2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380). Temporal trends in mental health outcomes are shown in Supplemental Figure 1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380 and indicate that disorders due to psychoactive substance use increased over our study period.

Descriptive statistics for daily mean ambient temperature, apparent temperature, relative humidity, and O3, NO2, and PM2.5 concentrations are presented in Table 1. In Ontario, the daily mean ambient temperature during the summer months was 19 °C (SD = 3.9 °C) and ranged from 9.6 to 27 °C. ORs describing associations between 3-day mean ambient and apparent temperatures and the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes overall and for each group of conditions are shown in Figure 1. In general, similar associations were observed for ambient and apparent temperatures (Figure 1). Adjusting for mean outdoor concentrations of O3, NO2, and PM2.5 in ambient temperature and apparent temperature models did not meaningfully alter the results (Supplemental Figure 2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380 and Supplemental Tables 3–6; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380). Moreover, O3 (OR = 1.002; 95% CI: 0.999, 1.004), NO2 (OR = 1.007; 95% CI: 1.001, 1.013), and PM2.5 (OR = 1.004; 95% CI: 1.000, 1.009) were weakly associated with the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes overall.

In analyses conducted across age strata, a decreased risk was found among those aged 17 years and younger (OR: 0.916 per 5 °C increase in the 3-day mean ambient temperature; 95% CI: 0.899, 0.933). To examine this further, we conducted additional analyses separately for children (age <12 years: OR = 0.965; 95% CI: 0.915, 1.017) and adolescents (ages 12–17: OR = 0.909; 95% CI: 0.891, 0.927) and noted inverse associations among both groups. Regarding analyses performed across strata of sociodemographic characteristics, neighborhood-level social deprivation appeared to modify the strength of association between 3-day mean ambient/apparent temperature and risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes (interaction P-value = 0.001) with risks increasing as social deprivation increased (Figure 5 and Supplemental Table 7; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380). Discussion

In general, our findings suggest that increases in daily mean ambient and apparent summer temperatures are associated with an increased risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes particularly for disorders related to psychoactive substance use and among those experiencing the highest levels of social deprivation. However, risk estimates for ambient and apparent temperature were similar, and there was no clear advantage of using apparent temperature instead of ambient temperature in terms of identifying population health risks. Similar to previous studies, we found that the strength of associations between temperature and the risk of acute mental health outcomes was highest on the days immediately leading up to an ED visit (on lag 0–3) and decreased as the number of days between temperature exposure and an ED visit increased (i.e., with increasing lag-times).21,35–40 We identified a nonlinear association between ambient temperature and risk of ED visits for all mental health outcomes combined, disorders due to psychoactive substance use, and mood disorders. For mood disorders, ambient temperature was associated with decreased risk primarily below 20 °C, whereas at higher temperatures the inverse effect was attenuated. We also observed an inverse relationship between temperature and the risk of ED visits for behavioral and emotional disorders with onset occurring in childhood and adolescence.

The association between summer temperatures and the risk of ED visits for all mental health outcomes in our study replicates findings from Canadian studies using hospital attendance or admission data.8 For example, among residents of Toronto, Canada, daily mean temperatures at or above the 99th percentile in comparison to the 50th percentile was associated with an increased risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes (ICD-10, F00-F99)21 Using data from Alberta and Ontario, Canada, Lavigne et al11 also noted adverse effects of daily mean temperature at or above the 97.5th percentile relative to the percentile associated with minimal risk on the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes (ICD-10, F00-F99).

A range of biological, psychosocial, and behavioral mechanisms has been proposed as drivers of heat impacts on mental health. Potential biological mechanisms include impacts on thermoregulation, including neurologic or psychiatric symptoms of heat-related illness, exacerbation of thermoregulatory dysregulation associated with some mood disorders (e.g., major depressive disorder), and impacts on the brain, such as neuroinflammation, impacts to neural connectivity, and dysregulation of neurotransmitters (i.e., serotonin and dopamine).41 Psychotropic medications commonly prescribed to treat mental health symptoms can also alter thermoregulation and some drugs may be sensitive to heat, potentially decaying at higher temperatures.42 Potential psychosocial and behavioral mechanisms include adverse impacts related to sleep, fatigue, cognition, and mood (including irritability), violence and aggression, social isolation, and impacts to school and work conditions and livelihoods.43 In our study, we examined social and material deprivation as distinct (uncorrelated) candidate factors that were hypothesized to exacerbate the adverse impacts of heat on acute mental health outcomes. The strength of associations between temperature and the risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes overall increased with neighborhood-level social deprivation, but no clear evidence of effect modification was observed for neighborhood-level material deprivation. While the reasons for this are not entirely clear, one explanation for this finding may be that neighborhood-level measures of social deprivation are more strongly associated with individual-level social deprivation compared to neighborhood measures of material deprivation and individual-level material deprivation. Alternatively, it may be that social support is much more important than material wealth when it comes to preventing the adverse mental health impacts of heat. For example, while being wealthy provides the means to seek necessary medical care, in and of itself, it does not provide a social infrastructure, which seems to be most important to minimizing heat impacts on acute mental health outcomes. However, additional work is needed to verify this hypothesis. Nevertheless, the exacerbation of heat-related mental health risk in relation to higher levels of social deprivation observed in our study is consistent with existing evidence suggesting that social isolation is an important factor to consider in mitigating the adverse impacts of heat on mental health outcomes.43,44

Our findings further contribute to broader research demonstrating how social deprivation can exacerbate the impacts of heat on health. For example, a study carried out in British Columbia, Canada, found social deprivation to be a factor in the risk of death during the 2021 heat dome over the northwestern Pacific region.45 Reducing social isolation has also been a target of some strategies aimed to reduce mortality during heat wave events and vulnerability may be reduced by leveraging or building social connection,46 increasing social capital,47 and ensuring that early warning systems can incorporate a mechanism by which those that are most socially deprived are contacted before the heat event to prevent potential harm.

Our finding that ambient temperature was most strongly associated with an increased risk of ED visits for disorders due to psychoactive substance use corroborates findings from previous studies specific to ambient temperature and substance use-related ED visits in the United States.13,48 Hypothesized mechanisms include higher levels of substance use and substance use-related risky behavior while outdoors during warmer weather, increased levels of dehydration, and elevated toxicity and lethality of some psychoactive substances at high temperatures.41,48–50 These findings suggest an opportunity for the prevention and treatment of heat-related substance misuse during the summer months. Improved public health interventions in this area could serve to strengthen healthcare system preparedness and ultimately reduce morbidity during longer and hotter summers driven by climate change.51

Our findings suggest that the risk of ED visits for mood disorders is lower between 10 and 20 °C. While the reasons for this finding are not completely clear, one possibility is that people tend to move more comfortably outside and engage in behaviors that are protective of mental health when temperatures are in this range, such as physical activity and social contact outdoors.44,52,53 While not as apparent, a small increase or stabilization in risk is observed in the nonlinear association shown in Supplemental Figure 16; https://links.lww.com/EE/A380, which would be more consistent with other literature suggesting increased risk of ED visits for mood disorders at higher temperatures.54

In our study, temperature was also associated with a decreased risk of ED visits for behavioral and emotional disorders with onset occurring in childhood and adolescence among those 17 years of age and younger, which is inconsistent with past findings.54–57 Reasons for this are unclear, and we can only speculate as to why this observation occurred. One possibility might be that during hotter summer periods, children and adolescents may spend more time indoors where there may be increased parental or adult vigilance.58–60 This could have contributed to lower numbers of ED visits during the summer months, particularly for externalizing conditions such as conduct disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, but additional studies are needed to explore this question.

Our study had many advantages, including a large number of cases identified over 15 years through a mandatory reporting system and a case-crossover design to eliminate confounding by factors that do not vary over short periods of time-within subjects. However, it is important to note several limitations. First, the spatial resolution of temperature data was 1 km2, and thus, we could not capture small-scale spatial variations in temperatures that may exist in some areas. This likely contributed to exposure measurement error as it was not possible to capture local level variations in outdoor temperatures; however, this measurement error would likely have a Berkson-type structure (i.e., true local level temperatures with each 1-km2 grid varying around the measured 1-km2 value) and is not a likely explanation for our results. Second, our study relied on ambient outdoor temperature and did not assess indoor temperature, personal exposure to heat, or access to air conditioning. Third, our analysis likely only captured the most severe cases of mental health outcomes, namely those who sought care at an ED.61,62 Even among those severely affected by mental and behavioral conditions, some people may not have presented to the ED.61,62 Fourth, outcome misclassification is possible as the assignment of ICD codes was done in an ED setting rather than in a diagnostic psychiatric setting and a primary single code was assigned making it impossible to capture comorbidities which are common and for which care seeking may be more likely.63 Finally, our study design (i.e., comparison of outdoor temperatures across case/referent periods in the same month and year) limited our ability to examine nonlinear exposure-response relationships across broad temperature ranges and future studies are needed to thoroughly evaluate these relationships.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that increases in summer temperatures are associated with a heightened risk of ED visits for mental health outcomes in Ontario and particularly among those experiencing greater social deprivation. Further investigation of mechanisms driving these associations, including on the specific impacts of temperature on ED visits for disorders due to psychoactive substance, mood disorders, and behavioral and emotional disorders with onset occurring in childhood and adolescence, is needed.
