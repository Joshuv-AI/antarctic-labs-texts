Peristalsis of the gastric conduit post-esophagectomy: is it relevant? Detailed conduit analysis using dynamic magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Kaleb Lourensz, Anagi Wickremasinghe, Shalini Bigwood, Yazmin Johari, Paul Beech, Geoff Hebbard, Paul Burton, Wendy A Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe incidence of esophageal cancer resections is rising, and survivorship improving. Consequently, more patients are living longer with gastric conduits and enduring the associated morbidity. Gastric conduit shape and peristalsis are believed to mediate conduit function, gastrointestinal symptoms, and quality of life (QoL). However, the relationship between anatomical variations, peristaltic function, and conduit emptying remains largely undefined. This prospective observational study recruited patients with gastric conduits at least 2 years post-esophagectomy and controls with normal gastric anatomy. Dynamic and static MRI sequences were obtained following porridge ingestion. Nuclear scintigraphy conduit emptying studies were available for 17 out of 18 (94%) patients. QoL questionnaire data were collected. Eighteen patients with conduits and 12 controls were recruited. Peristalsis was measurable in 15 conduits. There was no difference in peristaltic wave frequency between conduits and controls (5 waves per 90 seconds in each group) or median Gastric Motility Index Interquartile Range (IQR) 35.45 mm2/second (17.3–47.5) vs 36.5 mm2/second (9.8–99.4) p = 0.61. Gastric emptying was significantly reduced in conduit patients (0.10% vs 15% p = 0.01). Acute angulation and conduits wider than 4 cm were associated with slower emptying (444 minutes vs 127 minutes, p = 0.025) and (286 minutes vs 76 minutes, p = 0.02), respectively. Delayed emptying demonstrated a significant positive correlation with reflux (rs0.614, p = 0.009) and increased gastrointestinal symptoms (rs0.553, p = 0.021). Peristalsis is present in most conduits, but it does not correlate with improved emptying. Anatomical features, particularly conduit angulation and width influence conduit function. These findings may guide further research into optimizing conduit formation and treating dysfunction.Keywords: clinical trial, dynamic magnetic resonance imaging, esophagectomy, gastric physiology, gastric conduit, peristalsis

INTRODUCTION

Physiological function of the gastric conduit following esophagectomy mediates gastro-intestinal symptoms and impacts quality of life (QoL).1 Esophageal cancer is increasingly prevalent2 and survivorship is increasing.3 This has resulted in a substantial increase in the number of person-years living with a gastric conduit and obligates a research focus on conduit function and resulting QoL parameters.

Gastric conduit peristaltic function and emptying are substantially altered following esophagectomy with gastric conduit reconstruction. Translocation of the stomach from the abdominal cavity to the thorax involves removal of the anti-reflux mechanisms normally present at the diaphragmatic hiatus.4 This also changes the extrinsic pressure on the stomach from the normal intra-abdominal pressure to a negative pressure environment in the thorax, causing an unfavorable pressure gradient for emptying.5 The vagus nerves are transected during a routine esophagectomy, and the resultant denervation is thought to affect peristalsis and impact normal pyloric relaxation.6,7 The net result of these anatomical and physiological changes is that many patients experience symptoms such as reflux (60%), dysphagia (25%), or dumping(50%).5,8 In addition to the impact on QoL, dysphagia is associated with increased weight loss 2 years post-esophagectomy and aspiration pneumonia both perioperatively and greater than 1 year post-operatively.9

The role of peristalsis in gastric conduit physiology is thought to be important but is incompletely understood. Manometric studies, measuring changes in intraluminal pressure of the conduit have demonstrated partial recovery of peristalsis at 1 and 2 years compared with 3-months post-esophagectomy,10 while others have demonstrated absent or minimal peristalsis of the tabularized gastric conduit.11–13 Scintigraphic conduit emptying studies have demonstrated the majority of patients experience delayed emptying (mean half time for the entire group was 241 ± 244 minutes), with severely delayed emptying in 37.5% of patients (mean gastric emptying half time of 422 minutes).14

Anatomical features of conduits can influence conduit emptying.15 Of note, it was found that tabularized gastric conduits (compared with whole stomach reconstruction) were associated with improved QoL and reduced rates of delayed gastric emptying. A study attempted to quantify this by manufacturing transparent tubes and measuring emptying times using fluids of different viscosity.16 They found wider tubes were associated with slower emptying, as the Bernoulli equation would suggest. The Bernoulli equation is reliant upon the assumptions that the flow is steady, the liquid has zero viscosity and is incompressible.17 The post-prandial stomach grinds and circulates chyme proximally and distally multiple times each minute, and intermittently empties through the pylorus. Given this, the applicability of this principle to gastric emptying appears unlikely.

Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive imaging technique that can visualize peristalsis, and has been performed on the native stomach to assess peristalsis for several decades.18,19 However, it has never before been used to assess the anatomy and function of the intrathoracic gastric conduit.

We hypothesized that peristaltic activity in the conduit would be reduced when compared with native stomachs and that the presence of ordered peristalsis would be an important factor determining emptying of the conduit.

We aimed to directly detect and measure peristalsis of the conduit using dynamic MRI to determine the presence, quality, and functional impact of peristalsis in the mature gastric conduit. Additionally, we aimed to identify anatomical features that may impact the function of the gastric conduit.

METHODS

Ethics approval for this prospective trial was obtained from the Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee no. 62/20 and was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12623000966695).

Participant selection

Post-esophagectomy patients were consecutively screened according to the inclusion criteria via the esophagogastric clinical audit database. Healthy controls with normal gastrointestinal anatomy and function were recruited via advertisement flyers. Those who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria were recruited. Informed written consent was obtained from each participant.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria at the time of recruitment were as follows: Age between 18 and 75 years, at least 24 months post-esophagectomy, no known local or distant recurrence, no previous major surgery, and no medications known to affect gastric motility.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with pre-existing diagnosed gastrointestinal dysmotility or contraindications for MRI were excluded. These contraindications included claustrophobia, inability to lie flat for an extended period or a known/suspected metallic foreign body.

Surgical technique

See Supplementary methods.

Quality of life

The RAND short-form 36 health survey20 was used to gather general QoL data, and specific validated gastrointestinal QoL questionnaires were used to identify symptoms of dysphagia, reflux, and dumping.21–23

MRI protocol

Each participant presented to the MRI department following a complete fast for at least 8 hours. Immediately prior to the supine MRI, the participant was given a semi solid porridge meal. This consisted of 24 g of rolled oats, 10.5 g of sugar, honey, and milk powder, mixed with 125 mL of water.

See Supplementary methods for further information on the MRI Protocol.

Quantitative analysis

The gastric conduit was examined in both an axial and sagittal plane for measurable peristalsis: when present, the amplitude and velocity was measured. The amplitude was calculated as the difference between the maximal and minimal peak of the peristaltic wave. (Fig. 1B). Velocity (mm/second) of the peristaltic wave was calculated by measuring the distance between the start and the end of the peristaltic wave and dividing it by the number of seconds elapsed. As previously described, the Gastric Motility Index (GMI) (mm2/second) was calculated by multiplying the velocity by the amplitude.24 Conduit angulation was recorded by measuring the angle of the distal part of the conduit (where is started angling anteriorly toward the diaphragmatic hiatus) in relation to a vertical line in a sagittal plane. Conduit width was measured in the distal half of the intrathoracic conduit. A conduit diameter of greater than or less than 4 cm was chosen to differentiate these two groups. Conduit and gastric volumes were calculated by tracing out the cross-sectional area of each transverse gastric slice (Fig. 1A) and multiplying it by the length of the conduit/stomach. Pyloric position relative to the diaphragmatic hiatus was estimated.

(C) Incoordinate peristalsis where the wave was only seen on one aspect of the conduit. (D) Coordinate peristalsis with waves seen on opposing aspects of the conduit.

Coordinated peristalsis was defined as peristaltic waves moving in harmonious motion in the cranio-caudal direction at two axially separated sections of the gastric wall. Incoordinate peristalsis was defined by the presence of a contraction at only one point of the imaged gastric wall (Fig. 1C,D).

Most patients previously had a nuclear scintigraphy conduit emptying study for clinical or research reasons. Where available, emptying half-times were extracted and used as an outcome measure of conduit emptying.

Statistical analysis

A two-sided p-value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Median and interquartile range were used to represent non-parametric continuous data and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman rank-order correlation coefficient was used to identify associations and directions of non-parametric data. Binary data was represented in whole numbers and percentage. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc, USA).

Participant Demographics

Three patients had complications graded as a Clavien-Dindo III or above.25 One patient had an ischemic tip of their conduit. This was managed with a return to theatre, and washout and drainage via thoracotomy. Another had a displaced feeding jejunostomy requiring a return to theatre, and the last had a splenic infarct and pleural effusion requiring percutaneous drainage.

All 30 MRI examinations were performed without complication and took approximately 30 minutes to complete. Anatomical delineation was excellent, allowing accurate measurement of peristaltic waves. All major anatomical structures were visualized with a high degree of confidence.

Several patterns of conduit shapes relating to the length, width, and placement within the mediastinum were identified (Fig. 2A–F). Some conduits demonstrated increased angulation in both a sagittal and coronal plane, resulting in a ‘reverse S’ configuration (Fig. 2C).

Conduit peristaltic features

Peristaltic waves allowing further analysis was observed in 15 (83%) conduits. There was no measurable peristalsis observed in in 3 (17%) conduits (Fig. 2).

There was a median of 5 (range 4–6) peristaltic waves in each dynamic frame of 90 seconds. The frequency did not change over the 20-minute dynamic scanning period. One patient had evidence of reverse peristalsis in the proximal conduit.

In seven conduits, peristaltic waves could be visualized but were incoordinate. Eight conduits demonstrated coordinated peristalsis. These were more evident in the distal aspect of the gastric wall. Peristaltic waves were observed to be more prominent on the greater curve aspect of the conduit. There was no difference in emptying between patients with coordinated vs uncoordinated peristalsis when measured as absolute volume reduction in milliliters (median (IQR) 0.27 (−24.3 to 22.7) vs 6.12 (−29.8 to 97.7) p = 0.27 or percentage reduction −0.02 (−7.3 to 4.3) vs 0.86 (−4.1 to 11.6) p = 0.69). Similarly, there was no significant difference in emptying between patients with measurable, and no measurable peristalsis when measured as absolute volume reduction in milliliters (median (IQR) 6.12 (−28.6 to 273.3) vs 58.60 (−160.2 to 101.7) p = 0.36 or percentage reduction 0.86 (−4.1 to 6.7) vs −11.94 (−23.4 to 24.4) p = 0.81).

Small peristaltic waves were occasionally visible in the fundus of the stomachs but rarely displayed coordinated peristalsis.

Strong antral peristaltic contractions were frequently seen, and despite the pyloric relaxing procedure, usually resulted in the retropulsion of chyme. Complete apposition of the conduit walls was rarely seen, except at the pylorus or where the conduit traversed the diaphragmatic hiatus.

Seventeen (94%) patients had a nuclear scintigraphy gastric emptying study with a median (IQR) half time of 160 (76–341) minutes.

Control peristaltic features

Coordinated peristaltic waves were observed in all 12 controls (Fig. 2). There was a median of 5 (range 3–6) peristaltic waves per 90 second block. The frequency of peristaltic waves did not change over the dynamic scanning period. Peristaltic waves were observed commencing proximal to the incisura angularis. Apposition of the gastric walls during peristalsis was generally observed to begin in the distal third of the stomach. Retropulsion of chyme was often seen in response to antral peristaltic contractions.

Comparison of peristaltic features between conduits and controls

The median frequency of peristaltic waves was identical for controls and conduits, at five waves per 90 seconds p = 0.56. The controls demonstrated consistent peristaltic waves, with a relatively constant amplitude and GMI over the scanning period. GMI was similar between the controls and cases at the beginning of the MRI, but by the end there was a non-significant trend toward increased GMI in the conduits vs controls (median (IQR) 46.4 mm2/second (15.8–69.1) vs 21.35 mm2/second (14.9–36.3) p = 0.20). Despite this, the controls had significantly greater emptying than the conduits when measured both as an absolute volume in milliliters (median (IQR) 75.5 (49.2–118.7) vs 0.75 (−36.1 to 79.4) p = 0.03) and percentage emptied (median (IQR) 15.1 (10.2–22.74) vs 0.1 (−9.4 to 7.9) p = 0.01) (Fig. 3).

Anatomical features of the gastric conduits

Pyloric position was within 1 cm of the diaphragmatic hiatus in 4 (22.2%) conduits. The remaining 14 conduit’s pylorus was a median 4.7 cm (range 1.2–11.5 cm) distal to the hiatus. The position of pylorus did not have a significant impact on the degree of angulation of the conduit or emptying.

Two clear groups emerged when measuring the angle of the conduit as it travelled through the thoracic cavity—examples seen in Fig. 2A,B,D. Thirteen (72%) patients had relatively straight conduits that were measured to have an obtuse angulation (>90°) in the sagittal plane to reach the diaphragmatic hiatus, with a median angle of 143°, range 133°–169°. The other five (28%) had conduits that extended to the posterior diaphragmatic recess, before acutely angling upward to the diaphragm, with a median angle of 59° (range 36°–76°). Conduits with acute angulation (<90°) showed a significant increase in median emptying half time compared with obtuse angulation (median emptying half time of 127 minutes (IQR 209–1364) vs 444 minutes, (IQR 73.75–295.5), p = .03) although there was no significant change in peristalsis or emptying over the MRI duration.

At the time of the MRI, nine of the conduits were wider than 4 cm, with a median width of 5.6 cm, range 4.5–7.2 cm. Nine of the conduits had a width of less than 4 cm and were considered ‘narrow’. They had a median width of 3 cm, range 2.5–3.7 cm. Of the narrow conduits, 6 (67%) demonstrated coordinated peristalsis, compared with only 2 (22%) of the wide conduits demonstrating coordinated peristalsis. Narrow conduits demonstrated a significantly faster emptying half-time than wide conduits median (IQR) 76 (71–245) minutes vs 286 (181–525) minutes p = 0.02 (Fig. 3).

All conduits in this study had a pyloric procedure: 9 (50%) had a pyloromyotomy and 9 (50%) had a pyloroplasty. There was no significant difference found in the volume or width of the conduit, peristalsis or emptying based upon the type of pyloric procedure.

Other anatomical features that were able to be visualized included: elongated blind pouch of the conduit ‘candy cane’ syndrome, redundancy of the conduit, prepyloric holdup of gastric contents, pre-hiatal holdup of gastric contents, dependent sumping in the posterior diaphragmatic recess (Fig. 2A–F).

Quality of life

Compared with the controls, patients with conduits had significantly increased gastrointestinal symptom scores for the categories of overall gastrointestinal QoL, dysphagia, reflux, and regurgitation using the Allen’s Questionnaire,21 as well as dumping symptoms using the Sigstad Questionnaire23 (Fig. 4).

There was a significant positive correlation between higher scintigraphy half times and reflux (rs 0.614, p = 0.009) and overall gastrointestinal QoL (rs 0.553, p = 0.021) using the Allen’s Questionnaire.21 There were no significant correlations between conduit emptying half-times and other measured gastrointestinal QoL scores.

The controls scored significantly higher than conduits in the SF-36 domains of general health, pain, and physical function.20 There was no difference in the other SF-36 categories (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

We have produced definitive evidence that most gastric conduits demonstrate measurable peristalsis. This has been a controversial topic in the literature with prior manometry studies concluding primary gastric conduit peristalsis either is not visible11–13 or does recover.10 Although the vagus nerves are routinely divided during esophagectomy leading to gastric denervation, intrinsic gastric pacemakers cells26 are likely the source of ongoing peristalsis in the gastric conduit. Three distinct categories of peristalsis were identified: conduits with a similar degree of peristalsis as controls, conduits displaying incoordinate peristalsis and conduits demonstrating no meaningful peristalsis. We did not identify any peristaltic variables that affected conduit emptying.

Anatomical variations of the gastric conduits were readily distinguishable upon review of the static MRI images. Acute angulation of the conduits in the sagittal plane was associated with a longer emptying half time using scintigraphy. Conduit formation that was wider than 4 cm was associated with a longer emptying half time using nuclear scintigraphy when compared with conduits <4 cm in width. Conduits that were almost entirely intrathoracic (where pylorus was within 2 cm of the esophageal hiatus) did not demonstrate any significant emptying changes compared with conduits where the pylorus extended >2 cm into the abdomen.

As expected, emptying of control stomachs was significantly faster than conduits. Frequency of peristaltic waves was equal between controls and conduits. All controls demonstrated coordinate emptying, compared with only half the conduits. The amplitude and velocity of the peristaltic waves between the controls and conduits were similar at the start of the MRI, but there was a non-significant trend toward increased amplitude of the conduit’s peristaltic waves by the end of the MRI. Despite this, controls appeared to not only have increased apposition of their opposing gastric walls during peristalsis, but it also commenced more proximally compared with the conduits. This may indicate the conduits have reduced muscular tone and subsequent widening of the antrum caused by vagal denervation or the pyloromyotomy/pyloroplasty. Although peristalsis did not appear to affect conduit emptying, it may still impact gastric emptying in the anatomically normal stomach.

Peristaltic motion appeared to induce intra-conduit movement of chyme. When observed, the result of this was retropulsion of chyme in the distal antrum, indicating functional closure of the pylorus despite the pyloric relaxing procedure. This retropulsion is an important part of trituration and is normal in controls.27 However, seeing this occur in conduits who had undergone pyloromyotomy or pyloroplasty was unexpected, and questions the longevity and efficacy of pyloric relaxing procedures. After the formation of straight, narrow conduits, adequate pyloric opening is the next logical point of hold up within the gastric conduit. Many studies have been performed to investigate the impact of pyloric relaxing procedures, with no convincing evidence that one procedure is better than another, or that a pyloric procedure is even necessary.15

Many experts have hypothesized that optimizing conduit function is a key factor in minimizing the impact to gastrointestinal QoL.28 Long-term studies have demonstrated that patient’s QoL 10 years post-esophagectomy continues to deteriorate, when compared with an age, sex, and comorbidity matched reference population.29 Our study demonstrated a correlation between improved overall gastrointestinal QoL and reflux with improved conduit function.

Nine of the patients had ‘negative’ emptying over the MRI duration—a slight increase in conduit volume. While this was unlikely to indicate a clinically significant increase in conduit size, it likely represents the portion of conduits that had no emptying. Increased post-prandial production of saliva and gastric juices, as well as the difference in conduit size during different phases of peristalsis may explain this finding.

We designed a prospective study to investigate a novel use of high-fidelity dynamic MRI to better understand the physiology and anatomy of the mature gastric conduit and compare it with normal gastric function in controls. Importantly, every patient recruited completed the procedure, there is no ionizing radiation, and it is entirely non-invasive. Our findings are a promising step to understanding what can be considered ‘normal physiology’ in the entirely abnormal anatomical state of the intrathoracic gastric conduit.

Patients were selected for this study according to the specified inclusion criteria. The fact that they survived for at least 2 years without recurrence and were willing to volunteer their time and undergo MRI scanning in the supine position, suggests that this cohort of patients had relatively straightforward operations and recovery. This introduces an element of unavoidable selection bias, as evidenced by the predominance of early-stage cancers. Therefore, these findings may not be translatable to patients who underwent a prolonged or complicated perioperative course.

Although dynamic MRI is probably the best available tool to determine conduit anatomy and peristalsis, this technology does have its limitations. Capturing dynamic images on MRI is only possible in a single plane at any time point. In a narrow conduit with angulation in a sagittal and coronal plane, peristalsis can only be assessed over a short segment. The size and position of a particular peristaltic wave can affect the volume calculation, and small variations in volume may reflect the degree of peristalsis during the image acquisition, rather than a true change. Prolonged MRI scans (60+ minutes) might provide a more comprehensive assessment of conduit function and emptying, particularly in the setting of patients with delayed conduit emptying. Cost, access, and variability of patient tolerance may be an impediment to this in many healthcare settings.

These techniques open further research opportunities and provides surgeons with some practical evidence that the ‘straight and narrow’ conduit will have the best chance of a satisfactory functional outcome. Promotility agents that are commonly used in the treatment of conduit dysfunction such as 5-HT4 agonists30 could be evaluated for efficacy in this context, and the impact of treatment based upon underlying peristaltic activity can be investigated. The heterogeneity of peristaltic activity and conduit function in these patients give credibility to the hypothesis that there are multiple underlying causes of impaired gastric conduit function, and dynamic MRI may be the ideal tool to identify these.

CONCLUSION

By utilizing imaging techniques that allow direct visualization of the gastric wall, we identified a new paradigm where peristalsis was not found to be influential in conduit emptying. We have demonstrated the correlation between specific anatomical features of the conduit and improved emptying: this includes obtuse conduit angulation and decreased conduit width. These findings provide data to help guide the esophageal surgeon on optimal conduit construction and improved gastrointestinal QoL for our patients. Future research focusing on efficacy of medical and surgical management on conduit failure will add to our understanding of conduit physiology. The technique of dynamic MRI appears to be a valuable tool for future research in this area.
