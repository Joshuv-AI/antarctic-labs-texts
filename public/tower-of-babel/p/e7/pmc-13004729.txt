Biomolecules Generated During Programmed Cell Death (PCD) Enhance the Capacity of Proliferating Ustilago maydis Cells to Overcome the Negative Impacts of Non-PCD Necromass
Authors: Stefan Stanovcic, Mira Milisavljevic, Natalija Azanjac, Snezana Kojic, Milorad Kojic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work builds upon our previous “repopulation upon shattering” (RUS) studies in the yeast-like microbe Ustilago maydis, focusing mainly on a deeper understanding of the suicidal type of death caused by self-generated hypoxia (SGH). The key findings are: (1) the massive loss of viability induced by SGH occurs via programmed cell death (PCD), (2) the PCD-released materials are more beneficial for the surviving cells than the nutrients liberated from cells that die through necrotic processes, (3) products of PCD can stimulate growth even in the presence of growth-suppressive necromass, (4) the beneficial effects of substances released through PCD extend to two other species within the family Ustilaginaceae, and (5) U. maydis exhibited a remarkable ability to withstand the negative effects of non-PCD necromass. The third and fifth points are of special note. The third indicates that PCD-derived materials are essential for critical initial regrowth in post-stress environments where there is a high presence of non-PCD necromass. As the revitalized population strengthens, it would correspondingly enhance the communal ability to exploit more challenging nutrients. The robust resilience of U. maydis survivors against non-PCD necromass could represent an important fitness determinant, mediating competitive success in mixed microbial communities where non-PCD necromass imposes selective pressure. Thus, while providing valuable insights, the study also underscores the need for further research, especially in understanding the molecular underpinnings of the RUS phenomenon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02736-z.Keywords: RUS, PCD, Regrowth, Necromass, Ustilago maydis

Introduction

In natural environments, most microorganisms are continually exposed to stressors of varying types and intensities that can suppress their growth or even threaten survival. To cope, microbes have evolved diverse protective mechanisms that sustain life under a wide spectrum of environmental threats. However, even the most sophisticated systems operate within certain limits. When stress exceeds repair capacity, homeostasis collapses, cells die, and populations decline [1]. Therefore, efficient restoration of population density after catastrophic stress and massive death is essential for microbial persistence in fluctuating environments. Understanding how microorganisms resist unfavorable conditions [2, 3] and how they recover from extensive mortality is critical for grasping their ecological dynamics.

Our previous studies on Ustilago maydis have been motivated by these considerations, leading to the concept of “repopulation upon starvation” (RUS) [4–7]. Using this yeast-like fungus as a model, we found that after massive killing, surviving cells can re-establish population density by exploiting intracellular compounds released from dead or dying cells. Namely, stress-damaged cells release nutrients sufficient to drive regrowth nearly to the original cell density [4]. Thus, RUS involves two coupled operations: a destructive one, marked by cell death and self-decomposition, and a constructive operation, centered on regrowth fueled by nutrient recycling [5].

While both operations serve the same purpose, they are carried out by cells in distinct physiological conditions, engaged in contrasting tasks. Consequently, the main questions related to these two aspects of RUS are informed by generally different theoretical and analytic concerns. The destructive side raises questions about how stress intensity determines the mode of cell death and the nutritive quality of released materials. The constructive aspect of RUS concerns the survivors’ ability to absorb and recycle these materials efficiently [4, 6]. Future RUS research should also be guided by eco-evolutionary considerations.

The present work focuses mainly on the destructive side of RUS. Prior research has shown that the nutritional quality of dead biomass (necromass) depends on the type of killing [8, 9], stress intensity [4], and the type of cell death [10]. Higher stress levels typically produce more toxic substrates and stronger growth inhibition during recovery [4]. Successful regrowth after such events therefore requires not only mechanisms for nutrient uptake but also for mitigating stress-induced toxicity.

When it comes to the mode of cell death, a rather common observation is that mild stress triggers programmed cell death (PCD), while severe stress causes necrosis [11–13]. Importantly, the mode of dying affects the nutritional outcome. In the unicellular green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, compounds released by PCD, were beneficial for conspecific cell growth, whereas materials liberated through non-PCD means, such as cell subjection to sonic waves, were inhibitory [10]. Moreover, these PCD benefits can be species-specific; substrates originating from one Chlamydomonas strain may suppress others [14]. Comparable results were reported for Ankistrodesmus densus [15].

These insights guided the present study and suggested new hypotheses to test. The immediate impetus, however, came from our finding that a pantothenate-auxotrophic strain of U. maydis (UCM350) could, in contrast to what was observed upon exposure to peroxide, successfully repopulate after suicidal death induced by self-generated hypoxia (SGH). This indicated that peroxide treatment depleted coenzyme A (CoA) resources, while SGH did not. The SGH-induced death was discovered recently [16]; it occurs in water under limited supply of oxygen, due to the U. maydis’ failure to halt its oxygen-consuming activity, causing hypoxia and accumulation of reactive oxygen species, ultimately resulting in severe loss of viability.

Guided by the above findings, we first asked whether nutrients released during suicidal death provide a growth advantage over those released from peroxide treated cells, and whether SGH-induced death involves a form of PCD; both proved true. Importantly, we then found that PCD-derived products can counteract the growth-inhibitory effects of non-PCD necromass. Lastly, we considered whether the beneficial effects of SGH-derived products might be species-specific.

Finally, we would propose redefining the RUS acronym. Initially meaning “regrowth under starvation,” it emphasized a closed system without external nutrient input. Yet, since nutrients arise internally, true starvation does not occur. Hence, we retain RUS but redefine it as “repopulation upon shattering,” more accurately capturing the link between cellular devastation and the survivors’ capacity to repopulate.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Growth Conditions

Standard procedures for cultivation and maintenance of U. maydis cells were performed as described previously [4, 16]. Unless stated otherwise, experiments used overnight cultures grown in YEPS medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% sucrose) at 30°C. The main strains were U. maydis UCM350 (pan1-1 nar1-6 a1 b1) and UIMG10 (nar1-6 a1 b1). The pan, nar, a1 and b1 denote auxotrophy for pantothenate, nitrate utilization deficiency, and mating type loci, respectively. UIMG10 is genetically identical to UCM350, except that the pan1-1 mutation is reverted to wild type, serving as a nominal control. The Pan1 (UMAG_10139) gene is located on chromosome 5 and encodes a putative pantoate-β-alanine ligase involved in pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis. Whole-genome sequencing (Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd.) identified a single T440→C transition in UCM350, converting Ser147 to Leu. Sporisorium reilianum f. sp. zeae, SRZ2_5 − 1, a2b2 and Sporisorium scitamineum I1, mat-2 strains (both kindly provided by Prof. Jan Schirawski, Friedrich Schiller University Jena, Germany) were propagated at 30°C (and maintained at 4°C) on YEPS-agar.

Stress Treatments

Cells were grown in YEPS to early stationary phase (approx. 2 × 108 cells per mL), harvested by centrifugation, washed 3 times in distilled water, resuspended in water at a concentration of 4 × 107 cells/mL and then subjected to various stresses. Oxidative stress was imposed by adding H2O2 in the presence of 10 mM Fe3+−sodium EDTA, catalyzing the Fenton reaction and generation of hydroxyl radicals. Samples were incubated 10 min at 30 °C with periodic mixing and washed three times with water.

For UV treatment, 8 mL of suspension was placed in 90 × 15 mm Petri dishes and irradiated with an Osram HNS30W G13 UVC lamp (36 cm-distance, intensity 18.4 mJ/cm², measured with UV Disc, UV-Technik International). Suspensions were agitated continuously using a MaxQ2000 seesaw shaker at 100 oscillations/min.

Heat stress was applied by incubating washed suspensions (10 mL in 100 mL flasks) at 45 °C for indicated times in a Memmert WB 22 water bath (120 rpm shaking). Samples were preincubated 15 min at 30°C and rapidly cooled on ice after treatment.

Self-generated hypoxia (SGH) was induced as described [16]. Briefly, washed overnight cultures were adjusted to 4 × 10⁸ cells/mL, dispensed into 1.5 mL microtubes with ~ 5 mm headspace, sealed with parafilm, and incubated 24 h at 30 °C. The aliquots were pooled and homogenized before further analysis.

After each treatment, aliquots were used to determine survival, while remaining samples were incubated at 30°C under agitation for ~ 3 days before plating for regrowth. Viability was measured by spot dilution assay. All experiments were run in duplicate and repeated at least three times.

Determination of Apoptosis/Necrosis

Apoptotic and necrotic cells in SGH- and peroxide-treated suspensions were detected using FITC Annexin V/PI dual staining (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with adjustments for fungal cell walls. Washed cells (4 × 10⁸/mL) were aliquoted into 1.5 mL tubes, sealed, and incubated at 30°C on an oscillating shaker. At defined time points, single-use aliquots were taken, cell walls destabilized with 40 mg/mL of lyzing enzymes (VinoTaste Pro, Novozymes) in 0.5X SCS buffer (0.5 M sorbitol, 10 mM 3-Na-citrate, pH5.8) at 30°C/2 min, and washed with PBS (1.37 M NaCl, 27 mM KCl, 100 mM Na₂HPO₄, 18 mM KH₂PO₄). Cells were resuspended in Annexin binding buffer (10 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CaCl₂, pH 7.4) at 1 × 10⁶/mL. Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and PI (2 µL, final 100 µg/mL) were added and incubated 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope. Two independent experiments were performed, 300 cells per sample counted. Statistical analysis used Tukey’s t-test (SPSS); p > 0.05 was considered not significant.

DNA Analysis

Molecular karyotype analysis was carried out by contour-clamped homogeneous electric field (CHEF) electrophoresis in 0.5× TBE buffer (45 mM Tris, 45 mM boric acid, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8) [17]. A 2015 Pulsafor unit (LKB Instruments) was used: 15 h at 60 s switch, then 11 h at 90 s switch. Protoplasts from 10⁹ cells were prepared with lysing enzymes (Sigma-Aldrich), embedded in agarose plugs, and digested with Proteinase K (30 °C, 24 h) in buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM EDTA, 1% sodium N-lauroylsarcosine, and 0.2% sodium deoxycholate.

Results

New Insights Into the Cellular Mediation of the Stressogenic Impact and the Factors that Might Contribute to the Variance of the Outcomes

After a massive (> 5-log) loss of viability induced by self-generated hypoxia (SGH), the auxotrophic U. maydis strain UCM350 (pan1-1 nar1-6) could almost fully restore its population upon re-aeration, showing that cells dying by this suicidal process release nutrients—including pantothenate and reduced nitrogen—sufficient to renew the population (Fig. 1A). In contrast, peroxide treatment caused a progressive failure of recovery: while 0.2–0.3% H2O2 allowed limited regrowth, higher concentrations (≥ 0.4%) abolished it. Since our previous work showed that UCM520 (nar1-1 met1-2) could restore its population after peroxide treatment [4]—indicating that the Nar1 gene does not play a significant role in this process—we wanted to examine whether the lack of recovery in UCM350 is in fact attributable to the mutation in the Pan1 gene. Indeed, pantothenate supplementation (10 µM) restored viability even after severe treatments (Fig. 1B). Moreover, while the 0.3% H2O2-threated cells (without pantothenate) formed colonies of highly variable size and altered morphology, in the pan-supplemented suspensions all treated cultures regrew back giving rise to colonies that were fairly uniform, indicative of accurate repair and efficient recovery (Fig. 1Ca). These observations obviously implied that the RUS phenotype was predominantly conferred by the pan1-1 mutation affecting pantoate-β-alanine ligase, an enzyme involved in CoA biosynthesis. Therefore, it was quite understandable that the phenotype could also be suppressed by supplementation with pantethine (Fig. 1B), a downstream metabolic intermediate in the production of CoA.

B A 72 h-recovery of cell population following peroxide treatment using increasing doses of H2O2 plus Fe3+ ions (Fenton reaction) without or with pan or pantethine supplementation. For analysis, ten-fold serial dilutions were spotted on YEPS agar medium and incubated at 30°C for 3 days. Two issues followed: whether pantothenate scarcity during recovery leads to genomic instability, and whether peroxide uniquely disrupts CoA resources due to its strong reactivity [18]. To test the first, we examined DNA karyotypes of colonies recovered from 0.3% H2O2 treatment either with or without pantothenate supplementation. CHEF analysis (Fig. 1Cb) revealed normal chromosomal patterns in isolates arising from supplemented cultures, but evident rearrangements in three out of four independent isolates arising from survivors cultivated without supplementary pan, suggesting that chromosome repair during recovery was inaccurate under these conditions. From this it is apparent that CoA is essential for accurate post-treatment repair and efficient cellular recovery. This result is consistent with previous observations in fission yeast where it was found that impaired CoA synthesis causes unstable chromosomal DNA [19].

The next objective was to address the question of whether the negative effect on CoA resources might specifically be caused only by peroxide treatment.

The Negative Impact on CoA Resources is Not Peculiar to Hydrogen Peroxide

To assess whether CoA depletion is specific to peroxide or a more general response to stress, we tested two additional environmental stressors of physical nature—UV irradiation and heat (45°C)—common in many ecological niches. UCM350 cells were exposed to increasing doses, and recovery was followed with or without pan-supplementation. As shown in Fig. 2, the dependence on external pantothenate increased proportionally with the intensity of UV and heat stress, mirroring the pattern seen with peroxide. Thus, this finding effectively ruled out the notion that the differences observed upon SGH- versus peroxide-treatment were peculiar to a drastic assault by hydrogen peroxide.

UCM350 cell suspensions (4 × 107/mL) were treated with increasing doses of UV irradiation or incubated at 45°C for increasing time intervals. Recovery was monitored every 24 h over a period of 3 days by spotting ten-fold serial dilutions on YEPS agar medium which was then incubated at 30°C for 3 days. Two further observations deserve mention. First, UV treatment produced a dose-dependent stimulation of regrowth—the higher the UV exposure, the faster the recovery. Second, brief heat exposure (5 min, 45°C) led to delayed cell death about 48 h post-treatment, independent of pantothenate supplementation, suggesting a mechanistically distinct death process from that induced by prolonged exposure. These observations add complexity warranting future study.

In summary, the stressogenic factors tested impact the quality of the released substrates in a different way, whereby peroxide, UV, and heat stresses all impair the nutrient value of released substrates by affecting CoA resources, whereas SGH does not. Although peroxide is a laboratory stressor, its effects appear to model natural stress processes well, reinforcing its ecological relevance. The data also confirm CoA’s essential role in accurate repair and efficient recovery. These conclusions raised a broader question central to the RUS framework—whether SGH-induced death preserves not only CoA resources but overall nutrient quality, thus yielding substrates more favorable for regrowth than those from peroxide-treated cells. The analyses that follow were designed to address these possibilities.

Suicidal, SGH-Induced Death Facilitates Production of Derivatives More Favorable to Growth

Assessing whether SGH-induced death preserves superior nutrient quality requires comparing not only supernatants but also whole cell suspensions, i.e., the total necromass that more realistically represents natural conditions. Previous findings showed that suspensions of peroxide-treated (PT) cells were, for unknown reasons, more inhibitory to growth than their corresponding supernatants [4], underscoring the ecological relevance of evaluating both substrates.

SGH suspensions were diluted tenfold to match the cell concentration of 0.5% PT samples (4 × 107/mL). Both supernatants (SN) and cell suspensions (CS) were inoculated with 103/mL of fresh U. maydis UIMG10 (Pan+) cells in pantothenate-supplemented medium (10 µM) and growth was examined over 48 h.

As seen in Fig. 3A, both SGH-CS and SGH-SN supported rapid proliferation—more than an order of magnitude greater than even the most favorable peroxide supernatant (0.5%) during the first 24 h, reaching their carrying capacity first, at ~ 32 h. The near-identical curves of SGH-CS and SGH-SN imply that total necromass imposes no additional growth stress. Conversely, PT-CS and PT-SN curves exhibited clear divergence, especially at higher peroxide concentrations (0.6%), confirming that peroxide-derived necromass contains inhibitory factors.

Since the PT-substrates were prepared at a density of 4 × 107 cells/mL, the initial (4 × 108 cells/mL) SGH samples were diluted 10X to equal the nutrient capacity of all substrates. Next, we examined whether SGH-induced death proceeds via a PCD pathway. Earlier work [16] showed SGH was accompanied by ROS accumulation and DNA laddering—typical but not definitive PCD markers [20]. We therefore analyzed phosphatidylserine externalization via annexin V staining combined with propidium iodide (PI). These data indicate that, as in other experimental systems including U. maydis [21], SGH-apoptotic cells progress sequentially from early to late apoptosis and finally to necrosis (Fig. 3Ba). By 20 h, annexin V-positive cells had also become PI-positive, a pattern confirmed by microscopic inspection (Fig. 3Bb, upper panel). In contrast, nearly all cells in the 0.5% PT suspension were necrotic (> 99% PI-positive).

In sum, the experiments have shown that under SGH-conditions U. maydis cells do execute PCD form of dying, that the substrates of cells that have undergone PCD are more favorable for growth than the nutrients from a non-PCD necromass, and that our results parallel those reported for C. reinhardtii [10]. A further point to note is that growth curves from SGH-CS and SGH-SN cultures coincide rather well, suggesting that the presence of total SGH-necromass does not impose additional stresses to the expanding population.

Products of PCD can Overcome the Growth-Suppressive Effects of Non-PCD Necromass

Microorganisms often face multiple simultaneous stressors that generate necromasses of varying composition and growth-promoting potential. We therefore examined whether substrates derived from PCD can counteract the inhibitory effects of non-PCD necromass. As far as we know, there are no reports on this issue at the moment.

Growth kinetics of U. maydis UIMG10 cells were followed in the presence of increasing densities of 0.7% peroxide-treated (PT) cells (2–10 × 10⁷ cells/mL) suspended in water, 10× diluted SGH (PCD) filtrates, or 0.5% PT-SN. All samples were inoculated with 10³ fresh cells and incubated at 30°C under agitation.

As shown in Fig. 4, PT necromass inhibited growth in a concentration-dependent manner. In the highest densities of PT cells suspended in water, even dying off of the inoculated cells occurred. Remarkably, supplementation with SGH-derived PCD substrates completely reversed this inhibition. After 24–48 h, all PCD-supplemented cultures displayed vigorous proliferation, surviving and expanding even when the live-to-dead cell ratio was as extreme as 1:10⁵. Although weaker, 0.5% PT filtrate also alleviated growth suppression to some extent, confirming its partial restorative potential. Consistent with previous observations (Fig. 3A), SGH-SN exhibited far superior growth-promoting capacity than peroxide-derived substrates, both in the absence and presence of necromass.

These findings demonstrate that compounds released from cells undergoing PCD strongly stimulate proliferation even in conditions dominated by growth-inhibitory, non-PCD necromass. From ecological perspective, this finding would imply that the dynamics of population recovery after massive death would critically depend on the fraction of cells dying via PCD.

Beneficial Effects of U. Maydis SGH Materials Extend to Related Species

Previous work on C. reinhardtii showed that materials released during PCD promoted conspecific growth but inhibited other Chlamydomonas species [14]. To determine whether SGH materials display a similar specificity, we examined their effects on two closely related smut fungi, Sporisorium reilianum and Sporisorium scitamineum. Cultures of each species were grown under three conditions: (i) 100× diluted YEPS medium (determined to have a carrying capacity equivalent to 10× diluted SGH-SN), (ii) 10× diluted SGH-SN, and (iii) a 1:1 mixture of both. Parallel U. maydis cultures were run as controls, and growth was monitored daily for three days at 30°C (Fig. S1).

Both Sporisorium species responded similarly to U. maydis: growth in SGH-SN matched that in diluted YEPS, and no inhibition was observed in any case. Thus, among these three confamilial species, SGH material exerted uniformly beneficial rather than antagonistic effects.

With this realization, we next examined whether SGH-material could mitigate the growth-inhibitory effects of PT cells on S. reilianum and S. scitamineum. Prior to this, it was necessary to determine the inherent sensitivity of these species to inhibition by 0.7% PT necromass. To this end, we assessed their ability to survive and proliferate at PT cell densities ranging from 1 × 107 to 4 × 107 over a three-day period, using equivalent inocula of U. maydis as a reference.

As shown in Fig. 5A, relative to U. maydis, both S. reilianum and S. scitamineum exhibited markedly poorer survival and growth when challenged with increasing densities of PT cells, indicating a heightened susceptibility of these microbes to the detrimental effects of 0.7% PT necromass. Nevertheless, supplementation with SGH-SN effectively improved the survival and growth of both Sporisorium species under the otherwise inhibitory conditions (Fig. 5B).

In summary, we found that the beneficial effects of U. maydis SGH materials extend to at least two other closely related species, although the degree of benefit somewhat varies. It is also important to note that, compared with its relatives, U. maydis displayed a markedly superior ability to withstand the inhibitory effects of PT cell necromass.

Discussion

RUS can be conceptualized as comprising two coupled processes: cellular self-degradation and nutrient recycling. Although distinct and executed by cells in different physiological states [5], they are linked through decomposition products that sustain regrowth of survivors. When degradation proceeds actively (autolytically), survivors benefit nutritionally from lysed cells without the metabolic cost of producing hydrolytic enzymes. However, both previous and present results indicate that the nutrient quality of released material depends on stress type, intensity, and mode of cell death.

The most favorable nutrients appear to arise from programmed cell death. This finding bears on the long-standing question of how PCD could evolve in unicellular organisms, where death of the cell equates to death of the individual, thus excluding direct selection for any trait, including PCD itself. Nonetheless, accumulating evidence [10, 22–24] suggests that PCD in unicellular life may be adaptive or altruistic in the sense that surviving cells profit from the beneficial biomolecules released by dying ones. In this sense, PCD exports fitness to kin or groupmates [25]. More recent work [26], integrating mathematical modeling with experimental observations, demonstrates that self-sacrifice by healthy cells cannot evolve. Instead, the evolution of PCD requires that unhealthy or damaged cells can sense their impending death and respond adaptively—by expediting death to reduce resource waste, processing intracellular components in a way to avoid necrotic toxicity, initiating autolysis to provide beneficial nutrients to the surviving relatives, or releasing anticompetitor compounds. Thus, PCD may represent not only an altruistic adaptation that provides nutritional benefits to surviving groupmates but also a mechanism that limits collateral damage to relatives.

These lines of reasoning can be applied to the phenomenon of SGH-induced death. Under SGH conditions, stressed cells appear to sense their demise and initiate PCD to release beneficial nutrients while avoiding necrotic events. Accordingly, both SGH cell suspensions and supernatants supported markedly greater growth than necrotic preparations (Fig. 3A). But what is particularly remarkable is the degree to which products of PCD can stimulate growth even in the presence of non-PCD necromass (Fig. 4), underscoring the robustness of PCD-derived benefits.

Several reasons make this finding particularly significant. Microorganisms in nature face diverse, often simultaneous stresses that vary in type and intensity, amplifying their effects and generating heterogeneity in physiological responses and survival. Numerous studies have shown that stress intensity determines the mode of cell death [11–13]: mild stress typically induces PCD, whereas severe stress triggers necrosis. Stress level also affects the composition and toxicity of compounds released during death [4]. These insights, together with the realistic assumption that environmental stresses are unevenly distributed, imply that microbial populations rarely experience uniform stress. Consequently, cells within the same population may die through different mechanisms, producing necromasses of varying composition and growth-promoting qualities.

The finding that PCD-derived substrates can mitigate the inhibitory effects of non-PCD necromass suggests that recovery after catastrophic damage would largely depend on the proportion of cells dying through PCD. Because growth efficiency in the presence of necromass correlates with the density of surviving cells [4, 8, 9], PCD-derived nutrients are crucial for initiating recovery following massive loss of viability. When such beneficial substrates are available, survivors can establish early growth even amid abundant necromass and later exploit less favorable substrates. Thus, from an ecological perspective, PCD would represent a crucial adaptation that enhances fitness, particularly during the early stages of population rebound. This view is broadly consistent with recent findings in a halotolerant microalgal strain, where elevated PCD, in response to hyperosmotic stress, was associated with faster regrowth and ultimately a higher population size [27].

Building on previous work, we examined whether SGH-released materials from U. maydis promote strictly conspecific growth by testing two related Ustilaginaceae species, S. reilianum and S. scitamineum. The results were negative; compounds released through PCD in U. maydis also supported the growth of both relatives and mitigated the effects of non-PCD necromass, although to differing degrees. This contrasts with C. reinhardtii [14] and A. densus [15], where PCD derivatives were conspecific. However, until further work is done assessing the relationship between phylogeny and the action of U. maydis’ PCD materials across a broader taxonomic scale interpretation of these differences would be premature. The most that we can say is that, unlike the aquatic algae, all three Ustilaginaceae species are terrestrial parasites likely shaped by different ecological constraints.

However, another important observation emerged from these comparisons. When challenged with increasing concentrations of PT cells, U. maydis displayed a far greater ability to withstand the inhibitory effects of non-PCD necromass than its relatives. Previous comparisons [6] showed that both U. maydis and Saccharomyces cerevisiae could recover from oxidative stress, but U. maydis exhibited faster and more extensive regrowth. This superiority stems not only from its efficient recycling of damaged materials but also from rapid autolysis and release of intracellular nutrients [6]. Thus, the present study adds another layer to this picture, a trait that could represent an important fitness determinant, mediating competitive success in mixed microbial communities where non-PCD necromass imposes selective pressure. Namely, successful competition under such conditions would depend not only on the capacity to exploit released molecules but also on the ability to avoid being intoxicated by them. Because this trait may carry significant ecological consequences, it will be of considerable interest to elucidate the mechanistic basis of this resilience. In conclusion, the results of this study both corroborated and expanded previous findings on PCD. By bringing new considerations in view, the study underscores the need for further explorations into both sides of RUS.
