Evaluation of post-treatment after wastewater stabilization ponds at municipal wastewater treatment plant
Authors: Szymon Kilian, Katarzyna Pawęska, Aleksandra Bawiec
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The primary objective of this study was to assess the operational conditions of the municipal wastewater treatment system. This system comprises a wastewater treatment plant that employs the activated sludge process, along with wastewater stabilization ponds as the third stage of treatment. The operating conditions of the wastewater stabilization ponds were observed during both normal operation and malfunction scenarios. The average values of the pollutant parameters at the inlet and outlet of the ponds did not differ significantly. However, an increase in the concentrations of total organic carbon (TOC) and phosphates was observed at the ponds’ outlet. Specifically, the TOC increased from 2.25 mg/dm3 (inlet) to 5.02 mg/dm3 (outlet), while phosphates rose from 0.34 mg/dm3 (inlet) to 3.02 mg/dm3 (outlet). The analyzed pollutants in the wastewater stabilization ponds samples were characterized by seasonal variability, particularly concerning TOC, dissolved oxygen, ammonium nitrogen, and phosphates. During malfunctions, the highest pollutant load (including activated sludge and precipitated phosphorus) was delivered to the first wastewater stabilization pond, inducing the phenomenon of internal loading, leading to increase in phosphates concentrations. To mitigate such issues, the main force should be focused on the proper operation of the wastewater stabilization ponds and wastewater treatment plants.

Introduction

One of the greatest challenges of our time is the ever-increasing water stress induced by the global climate crisis1–5. Water quantity is not the only one issue; the degradation of freshwater quality affects its availability for demanding sectors. In the EU, point sources of contamination are mostly controlled, but area and linear sources of pollution (e.g. cities, roads, agriculture) are still unresolved, and negative impacts on water quality and the spread of pollutants are still reported2,3,5. Therefore, there is a need to implement new solutions to mitigate water stress and improve water quality. The European Environmental Agency (EEA) indicates Natural Based Solutions (NBS) as recommended solutions to mitigate global warming water stress and reduce its impacts on the Earth’s biosphere4–6. In 2023, a new European Union regulation has come into effect7, this regulation allows water reuse from treated wastewater, for crop irrigation. The main goal of this regulation is to increase water reuse by EU members, while maintaining high safety standards7. Treated wastewater is considered to be an “endless”, reliable source of water, as it contains a number of organic material and biogenic compounds that could also help to limit the use of fertilizers. As the appropriate load of compounds necessary for plant growth can be supplied using treated wastewater8–10. The possibility of water reclamation from treated wastewater is indicated in many sectors, e.g., for irrigation (farmland and greenery), industry, municipal use, groundwater recharging, recreational and ecological uses. Their application for irrigation is mostly reported8–10.

One of the possible NBS consistent with the objectives of Regulation EU 2020/741 is Waste Stabilization Ponds (WSPs, or oxidation ponds). WSPs can be used to stabilise the quantity and quality of post-treatment effluent or as stand-alone Wastewater Treatment Plants (WWTPs), which have been shown to be effective in a wide range of regions (from warmer to colder regions)11–14. The treatment process in WSP depends on microorganisms (mainly bacteria and algae), the right load of BOD5 (depending on the type of WSP), the availability of light and dissolved oxygen (DO), and temperature13–17,22. WSPs can only be controlled by operators to some extent. While pond depth, BOD5 load to the ponds and pH can be controlled by a number of mechanical measures, DO concentrations can only be partially controlled by application of mechanical aeration systems. The temperature in these systems cannot be controlled. The population of microorganisms can be influenced by changing DO concentrations, pH levels and the C:N/P:N ratio of the effluent13–17. The treatment process in WSPs relies on intensive algal and bacterial biomass, due to their natural symbiotic relation. During the day, algae perform photosynthesis, aerating the upper layer of water column. Aerobic organisms and bacteria deplete that oxygen for oxygen-based decomposition of organic matter, producing carbon dioxide which is used by the algae during photosynthesis15–17. As a result, DO concentrations and pH varies in WSPs according to season (higher in summer and spring, lower in winter) and time of day (higher during the day, lower at night). Therefore, the BOD capacity and efficiency of WSPs is higher in summer16.

The main processes shaping the efficiency of these systems are nitrification, denitrification, sedimentation, bioaccumulation (by algae), aerobic decomposition of organic matter and fermentation. Aerobic processes (nitrification, aerobic decomposition of organic matter) take place in an upper layer of the water column that is aerated by wind action and algal photosynthesis. Suspended solids settle on the bottom of the pond and are decomposed by anaerobic processes (fermentation, denitrification). The contribution of anaerobic processes to wastewater treatment increases with the depth of the WSPs13–18. Wastewater stabilization ponds are widely described in the literature13–17. However, research on WSPs in Central Europe was mainly carried out over 30 years ago19,20, and no recent studies were found during the literature review. The efficiency of WSPs solutions under too high load of pollutants is also underreported across Europe21.

Based on the depth of the pond there are four main types of WSPs: high-rate, anaerobic, facultative and maturation ponds13,15,17,22. WSPs can be implemented using a single pond, although it is recommended to implement multiple ponds, in series or in parallel. Researchers report that multiple pond systems perform better, compared to single pond systems13,15,17.

WSPs can be implemented as WWTPs for small communities (1000–5000 person equivalents) using a multiple pond system. Another scenario in which WSPs can be used is as a tertiary treatment system implemented after existing conventional WWTPs, to further improve effluent quality. The use of WSPs in this scenario is in line with the requirements7 for treated wastewater reuse. In this case, the WSP will be the effluent receiver of the WWTP, which may lead to operational issues of the directly connected WSPs, as the malfunctions of the WWTP will influence the WSPs. One of the most popular conventional treatment technologies widely used in WWTPs is the Activated Sludge (AS) method23,24. This wastewater treatment method can be successfully combined with wastewater stabilization ponds as a third stage of treatment. Despite the popularity of the AS treatment, its common maintenance problems have been widely described. Sludge bulking (or foaming) is a phenomenon in which the AS microbial community becomes predominated by filamentous bacteria (or so-called foam-formers), causing water to be trapped in floc structures. Under these changes in sludge structure, their settling and mixing capabilities are compromised25–28. This reduces the treatment efficiency and causes sludge to flow out of the settling tanks as foamy sludge flocs rise. Sludge washout occurs mainly during heavy storm events, when the combined sewer system predominates the sewer system grid. Excessive flow of wastewater combined with storm water, for which the WWTP is not designed flows through the system, flushing out activated sludge and settled pollutant particles mixed with raw wastewater29,30. Although they are hydraulic solutions to bypass biological treatment31–33, many small and medium-sized WWTPs still don’t have them introduced.

When Activated Sludge (AS) treatment in Wastewater Treatment Plants (WWTPs) is combined with the Wastewater Stabilization Ponds (WSPs) described above, maintenance problems influence adversely ponds, as the uncontrolled load of organic and inorganic pollutants reaches ponds. AS discharged into the pond floats on its surface, blocking reaeration by wind action and sunlight needed by the algae population. Dissolved oxygen is depleted by the decomposition of organic matter from suspended solids and AS. The depletion of dissolved oxygen also disrupts nitrification, which occurrence is essential for the removal of ammonium nitrogen34. Due to anoxic conditions (changes in redox potential)35, inorganic pollutants accumulated in sediments may be subject to the flux between sediments and the treated medium due to the internal loading phenomenon36–38. This can lead to further degradation of effluent quality.

WSPs can be considered as a viable option for the reuse of treated wastewater or for the renaturation of treated wastewater prior to its final release into the environment. However, many of the existing WSPs may be operating under overload conditions due to WWTP malfunctions, and lack of good operating practices due to lack of funding. There is a lack of recent research on WSPs in the Central Europe region, leaving their state unknown. The operational issues described above, if unresolved can lead to degradation of effluent quality, far beyond malfunction periods, due to the accumulation of pollutants in the sediments of WSPs. Due to the ever-growing eutrophication, water scarcity and water reclamation potential, the authors decided to address this topic.

The scope of this paper is to assess the post-treatment performance of wastewater stabilization ponds combined with conventional wastewater treatment plant, based on zoned activated sludge method.

Materials and methods

The WWTP with WSP system is located in Sobótka, Poland (50° 53′ 55″N, 16° 44′ 40″E). Wastewater inflowing to the WWTP is municipal wastewater coming from small towns and villages, with a few industries scattered in different locations. The average daily (nominal) flow of the WWTP is 2500 m3/d and its maximum flow is 3000 m3/d. The person equivalent for this WWTP is 14,800 inhabitants. The WWTP operates on the basis of a zoned activated sludge process, with phosphorus removal based on an iron-based PIX application. The WSPs system serves as the third stage of treatment. Treated wastewater flows directly from the secondary clarifier to the first WSP and then through both ponds to the receiving water body. The area of the first pond is 0.47 ha and the area of the second pond is 1.35 ha, the depth of both ponds is 1.1 m. The approximate Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT) of this WSP system is 8 days. The wastewater velocity was different for WSP I and WSP II. The velocities were 0.01 m/s and 0.005 m/s respectively.

Sampling points were selected based on the flow path through the ponds and the inflow/outflow points of the WSPs. 20 sampling campaigns were conducted at monthly intervals during the period from 06.05.2021 to 11.03.2023. During the sampling campaign grab samples were collected from 10 sampling points shown in Fig. 1 into 1.5 L polyethylene containers and analysed in the Environmental Research Laboratory of the Institute of Environmental Engineering. Samples were collected each time at 9 a.m., samples were taken from the depth of 20 cm, source of temperature data is the Polish Institute of Meteorology and Water Management—National Research Institute. In the collected samples, the pollutant parameters of pH, conductivity, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, total organic carbon, nitrate nitrogen, nitrite nitrogen, ammonium nitrogen and phosphates were measured. The variability of pH was higher at WSP I (Supplementary Fig. S1—online). Turbidity varied greatly between WSPs and was higher at WSP I. The highest turbidity was in summer, but slightly higher turbidity could occur at other times of the year. In WSP II, turbidity was slightly higher in summer at sampling point no.6 (Supplementary Fig. S2—online). TOC is highest in summer and fall and lowest in winter and spring (Supplementary Fig. S3—online). Both turbidity and TOC show seasonal variability, especially in WSP I. Overall DO concentrations are lower in WSP I (Supplementary Fig. S5—online). The mean air temperature during the whole observation period was 9.82 °C in spring, 18.83 °C in summer, 10.97 °C, in fall and − 0.25 °C in winter.

Concentrations of ammonium nitrogen (NH4) in WSP I generally increase up to sampling point No.4, at the last sampling point (No.5) concentrations are lower (WSP I outflow), this trend occurred in all seasons. NH4 concentrations are lowest in fall (13.83 mg/dm3) and highest in summer (35.28 mg/dm3) at higher air temperatures. During spring and winter the concentrations show the highest variability in the whole concentration range. In WSP II, NH4 concentrations increase through the sampling points during spring and summer, with slightly lower concentrations at the last sampling point (No.9) in autumn and winter (Fig. 2). The lowest concentrations in WSP II are in fall (18.18 mg/dm3) and the highest in summer (30.55 mg/dm3). Similar to WSP I, the highest range of results is in spring and winter (Fig. 2).

Nitrite nitrogen (NO2) concentrations are generally higher in WSP I during all seasons (Fig. 3). The sampling point closest to the inlet (No.2) has the highest concentrations (spring 0.08 mg/dm3, summer 0.11 mg/dm3, fall 0.13 mg/dm3, winter 0.14 mg/dm3), which decrease as they flow through WSP I, especially during summer and winter (summer 0.03 mg/dm3, winter 0.06 mg/dm3). In WSP I NO2 concentrations are highest in winter (0.09 mg/dm3) and lowest in summer (0.03 mg/dm3). In WSP II the seasonal differences between sampling points are small. The highest NO2 concentrations in WSP II are in winter (0.07 mg/dm3) and the lowest in spring (0.03 mg/dm3) (Fig. 3).

The nitrate nitrogen (NO3) concentrations are characterised by a higher variability than the NO2 concentrations. NO3 concentrations are highest at sampling point No.2. In summer NO3 concentrations become lower as they flow through to the sampling point No.5, a similar pattern is visible during the summer (Fig. 4). In fall and winter there is a higher variability between sampling points. In WSP II NO3 concentrations show overall less variability between sampling points. The highest NO3 concentrations in WSP I are in winter (0.40 mg/dm3), the lowest in spring (0.32 mg/dm3). The highest NO3 concentrations in WSP II are in spring (0.29 mg/dm3), the lowest in summer (0.22 mg/dm3) (Fig. 4).

Phosphates (PO4) are the most important in terms of wastewater treatment, and eutrophication. They are one of the limiting factors for algal growth, as phosphates are scarce in natural conditions. The main sources of phosphates in wastewater are cleaning products, detergents, etc. It is therefore important to remove PO4 in wastewater treatment plants before the effluent is discharged. In the case of the WSPs described in this paper, the internal loading of phosphates from ponds is of most concern, as the effluent from the ponds is released into the environment.

PO4 concentrations are much higher in spring (WSP I 2.90 mg/dm3 and WSP II 3.19 mg/dm3) and summer (WSP I 5.15 mg/dm3 and WSP II 5.16 mg/dm3) in both WSP I and WSP II than in fall (WSP I 1.61 mg/dm3 and WSP II 2.78 mg/dm3) and winter (WSP I 1.61 mg/dm3 and WSP II 1.80 mg/dm3). In WSP I the concentrations increase in all seasons from sampling point No.2 to sampling point No.5. In WSP II the concentrations become slightly lower as they flow through the pond (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The operation of activated sludge (AS) WWTPs is based on the microbial population “fit for treatment” and the separation of the microbial sludge from the treated medium in the secondary settling tank24,25,40. It is crucial to maintain a proper balance of the microbial population of AS in WWTPs operating based on this technology. The lack of a proper balance of microbial communities can lead to a decrease in WWTP treatment efficiency and disturbances in wastewater treatment processes24. The shift towards filamentous bacteria in the microbial population of AS leads to changes in the structure of the activated sludge and thus its settling properties27,28,40. AS with a predominance of filamentous bacteria do not mix properly with the treated medium, resulting in reduced performance and discharge of AS with treated effluent as its settling properties are compromised.

The described operational problems were noted during the observation period. During the sampling campaigns, AS flowed into WSP I and covered its surface with a blanket-like structure to the extent of sampling points no.2 and no.3. The resulting structure settled down in the following days, and in seasons with higher temperatures and methanogenic bacterial activity, settled parts of the “activated sludge blanket” reappeared on the surface. AS spillage occurred several times during the period of this study due to lack of good settling properties and heavy storm events.

Pollutants released to the WSPs during malfunctions (AS, precipitated phosphorus, poorly treated/raw wastewater) accumulate in the sediments of the WSPs. Both nitrogen, organic carbon and phosphorus can be then re-delivered to the treated wastewater. The release of previously removed pollutants (mainly in the form of AS) is connected to surges in TOC concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S3—online) and higher conductivity and turbidity. Re-delivered and accumulated organic load under aerobic conditions (with high use of dissolved oxygen from the treated sewage) can be decomposed by aerobic microbial action, leading to creation of anaerobic conditions in the pond water column15,38,44.

Overall concentrations of ammonium nitrogen in WSPs are high, while TOC concentrations are generally low (Table 2). Thus, TOC can be considered as a limiting factor as it is required by nitrifying and denitrifying microbiota as part of biomass building, leading to limitation of nitrogen removal24,44. Algae growth can be limited by inadequate C/N and N/P ratios in ponds, as these play an important role in nutrient assimilation by algae and thus their reproduction. Furthermore, the lack of correct ratios can be a limiting factor for algal species diversity45. Microbial action, algae bioaccumulation are considered as the most impactful mechanisms of nitrogen and phosphorus removal in WSPs15. Therefore, the occurrence of limiting factors will have a negative impact on the performance ratios in WSPs. C/N ratio at the outlet of the studied WSPs was in the range of 0.402–0.001 (Table 2), while the best algal production can be achieved with C/N ratios of 3.7–8.745. Optimal N/P ratio for algae growth is in the range of 6.8–10, higher than optimal N/P ratio indicates phosphorus limitation and lower than optimal range indicates nitrogen limitation45. In the studied WSPs, N/P was in the range of 5.79–9.55 (Table 2), confirming TOC as a limiting factor. The data suggests that nitrification has a greater impact in WSP II. This is confirmed by the moderate negative correlation between NO2 and pH and the moderate positive correlation between NO2 and NO3 (the same correlation is significantly lower in WSP I). It is also visible that nitrification rates are limited by low TOC concentrations (moderate positive correlation between NO2 and TOC). The correlation between NH4 and pH is related to easier ammonification at higher pH17. As a result of this factor, nitrification rates may be higher in the predominantly anoxic WSP I, as the WSP I problem of lack of organic matter is less due to the inflow of activated sludge from the secondary clarifier. This provides organic matter essential for microbial life17,34,44.

Anaerobic conditions lead to methanogenic decomposition of organic matter and the occurrence of a phenomenon called internal loading15,38,44. Internal loading is a phenomenon that, under anaerobic conditions, leads to the “washing out” of long-term accumulated phosphorus (mainly) and nitrogen compounds under higher redox potential35. Internal loading is described in the literature, as the phenomenon with the greatest impact on phosphorus cycle in eutrophic ponds36. Recent studies show that internal loading of ammonia may be equally important37,38. Phosphorus in sediments can be found in organic and inorganic forms, inorganic forms can be immobilized with iron, aluminium or clay (during the occurrence of aerobic conditions/high redox potential conditions during fall and winter)35,36. Clay can be found on the bottom of WSPs, and iron-bound phosphorus can be found in ponds due to precipitation with ferric coagulant followed by its release. Immobilized phosphorus compounds can be transformed under altered redox conditions. Immobilized phosphates are protected from reductive dissolution by aerobic conditions35, and when anoxic conditions occur due to aerobic decomposition of organic matter, phosphates are mobilized and transported through the porewater/water interface into the water column. Moderate negative correlation between PO4/DO and moderate positive correlation between NH4/DO (Table 3) in WSP I confirms that internal loading is the primary source of phosphates in the ponds and occurs due to intense DO depletion by decomposition of organic material (mainly AS and untreated wastewater), this dependence is also confirmed by the correlation of PO4/Temperature (Table 3). The positive moderate correlation between PO4/Conductivity and PO4/Turbidity (Table 3) further confirms that iron/clay-bound phosphorus washed out of the WWTP and accumulated in sediments is the source of phosphorus pollution in WSPs. In both ponds there is a high negative correlation between DO and temperature (Table 3). The analysed data suggest that the internal loading phenomenon occurs mainly at the beginning of WSP I (sampling points no.2 and no.3), leading to degradation of the treated wastewater quality. No further treatment takes place in this pond due to low HRT and poor algal growth. Therefore, a deterioration in quality is observed.

The dynamics of these transformations are mainly related to the temperature and biological activity (moderate positive correlation between PO4 and temperature, high negative correlation between DO and temperature, and moderate positive correlation between NH4 and DO) (Table 3)13,15,18,35,36. It should be noted that wind plays an important role in WSPs by aerating the upper layer of the ponds and mixing bottom sediments with water, preventing them from settling completely. Under normal operating conditions, this ensures proper removal of nitrogen and organic matter. When WSP are overloaded additional mixing (despite good oxygen flux) rapidly depletes dissolved oxygen from the lower layers, leading to anoxic conditions13,15,17,18,36. However, the area around the WSPs in Sobótka is surrounded by trees, and the slopes of WSPs are steep. Therefore, factor of reaeration by wind action and mixing can be limited in studied WSPs. Low mixing by wind action would also explain low TOC concentrations in the upper layer of WSPs.

The function of the WSPs remains disturbed by the overflow of pollutants, which creates anoxic conditions in WSP I, to which AS and precipitated phosphorus flow and settle to the bottom of the pond. The anoxic conditions created by the intense decomposition of organic material derived from AS, leads to internal loading of phosphorus from precipitated phosphorus during chemical treatment (which can be flushed out during hydraulic overloads). Analyses carried out suggest that the primary source of nitrogen in WSPs is from inflow from WWTP, although the impact of ammonia internal loading is unknown, and requires further studies. Despite the degradation of the WSPs effluent quality in terms of NH4 and PO4, WSPs clarify the treated effluent in terms of turbidity due to their buffering capacity. However, if algae growth were to occur in these ponds, the case with turbidity would be different.

As the final effluent from WSPs is discharged into the environment, the degradation of quality, mainly in terms of phosphates, endangers regional aquatic life and allows eutrophication to occur.

However, reuse of treated wastewater from ponds could be viable option as the7 concerns BOD5, TSS, turbidity, presence of E. coli bacteria and parasites.

Conclusions

Wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) malfunctions (sludge bulking, sludge release to WSPs and release of precipitated phosphorus) strongly influence the investigated wastewater stabilisation ponds (WSPs). Ammonium nitrogen concentrations between WSP inlet and WSP outlet did not vary at the level of statistical significance. Nitrates and nitrites, varied from inlet to outlet, with a visible influence of WWTP malfunctions. Nevertheless, the changes in the concentrations of the forms of nitrogen are consistent with the studies on the seasonal intensity of nitrification and denitrification processes carried out by other researchers. The main factor influencing the changes in phosphate concentrations (and thus total phosphorus concentrations) in WSPs is the phenomenon of internal loading during the period of increased biological activity. It consumes the oxygen dissolved in the ponds and leads to the creation of anaerobic conditions, in which the process of washing out phosphorus compounds proceeds with greater intensity. The dependence of biological activity on temperature indicates the seasonality of the internal loading process. Total organic carbon (TOC) concentrations are low, which affects nitrification/denitrification rates and algal growth, and thus the performance of WSPs. Low TOC concentrations can be explained by the decomposition of organic material at the bottom of the ponds, combined with poor mixing by wind action. Wind action in this case is limited by trees surrounding the WSPs and by steep slopes of the WSPs. Poor mixing by wind action explains the overall low TOC concentrations in the ponds, the intensity of the internal loading occurring in the WSPs, and the lack of changes in ammonium nitrogen concentrations. WSPs act as buffers, levelling the outflow in relation to the quality and volume of the outflowing treated wastewater and reducing the turbidity from inlet to outlet.

Operators of WSPs should focus on maintaining proper depth of ponds, the extent of slopes should be properly managed to allow reaeration by wind action, trees should be limited in the area close to WSPs so that the pond area is not shaded and the reaeration by wind is not limited. The internal loading phenomenon can be mitigated by dredging, so operators of WSPs should carry out dredging frequently, especially after malfunctions of WWTPs, if the WSP serves as a tertiary treatment system. Aeration combined with carbon dioxide injection can prove to be helpful. In addition, the load of pollutants (BOD5, nutrients) should be controlled by the operators in order to limit the pollutant loads received by the ponds to their maximum self-cleaning capacity, focusing on their changes within seasons. To mitigate possible degradation of treated wastewater quality in WSPs, we recommend that quality monitoring of WSPs should be carried out frequently in addition to quality monitoring of WWTPs. Further studies are required to assess the extend of overloaded WSPs with occurrence of internal loading phenomenon.
