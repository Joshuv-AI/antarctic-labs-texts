Matrix stiffness induced CREB3L1 activation contributed to skin fibrosis through calcium influx triggered endoplasmic reticulum stress
Authors: Dongsheng Wen, Yeke Yu, Siyi He, Ya Gao, Chiakang Ho, Xinran Ye, Jiaming Sun, Yuxin Liu, Lu Huang, Yangdan Liu, Qingfeng Li, Yifan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkin fibrosis substantially contributes to morbidity and mortality. Fibrotic remodeling, characterized by accumulated and stiffened extracellular matrix, persistently exerts mechanical cues and consistently activates fibroblasts, implying biomechanics as a major driver of fibrosis. However, our understanding towards mechanisms of biomechanics induced fibrosis remained limited.MethodsIntegrated single-cell sequencing analysis was performed to reveal the atlas of fibrotic skin. ChIP sequencing was performed to reveal the binding sites of CREB3L1. Nanoindenter was used to identify the mechanical properties. Gel contraction assay, wound healing assay, and live cell imaging were conducted to assess the behavior of fibroblasts cultured on hydrogels of different rigidities. Bleomycin induced and mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis models were established on CREB3L1 knockdown and control mice.ResultsWe identified a mechanosensitive fibroblast cluster that exerts contraction and ECM deposition functions in fibrotic skin, and its transcriptional identity was maintained by CREB3L1. Elevated expression of CREB3L1 was confirmed in human and mouse fibrotic skin. Further analysis showed that CREB3L1 was required for fibroblast activation, including contraction, migration, and ECM accumulation. More importantly, we revealed that matrix stiffness could alter calcium homeostasis, causing calcium influx and endoplasmic reticulum stress. The ER stress state in turn triggered the cleavage of CREB3L1, releasing its luminal domain that function as a profibrotic transcription factor. Inhibiting CREB3L1 could alleviate skin fibrosis both in vivo and in vitro.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the molecular mechanism of CREB3L1 mechanosensitive activation in matrix stiffness induced skin fibrosis, and presents a promising therapeutic target for clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06347-2.

Introduction

The skin, a protective barrier against biological, chemical and physical assaults, is continuously subjected to external damage. A dysregulated repair response following injury leads to skin fibrosis. Although short-term fibrogenic responses can rapidly restore the structural integrity of the skin, chronic and progressive fibrotic remodeling fails to compensate for functional loss. The consequences of skin fibrosis with respect to morbidity and mortality are substantial. Nevertheless, current therapeutic interventions are far from satisfactory, and only a small number of clinical trials of antifibrotic compounds have demonstrated beneficial results [1], emphasizing the urgent need for development of novel strategies derived from advancing knowledge of skin fibrosis.

Regardless of the initiating cause, fibrosis is characterized by the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesized by fibroblasts [2]. Myofibroblasts, which are activated fibroblasts, combine ECM production and contractile ability to drive the formation of stiff, resistant scar tissue from the newly synthesized ECM. The mechanical properties of ECM, in turn, promote myofibroblast accumulation from precursor cells and maintain myofibroblast activation [3]. This positive feedback loop of myofibroblast persistence and tissue stiffening is among the essential factors that generate a fibrotic environment, which suggests that strategies designed to interfere with mechanosensitive signaling pathways are promising [4–6].

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of myofibroblasts plays an essential role in the processing of secretory proteins for the construction of a collagen-rich ECM [7, 8]. This function relies on a proper ER luminal calcium concentration. When cells are exposed to mechanical cues, calcium accumulates, which leads to the accumulation of misfolded and/or unfolded proteins [9], and the activation of three adaptive ER stress pathways: inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), protein kinase R (PKR)-like ER kinase (PERK), and activated transcription factor 6 (ATF6) [10, 11]. PERK undergoes autophosphorylation upon binding to misfolded proteins. This autophosphorylation inactivates eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) to mitigate translation, thus reducing the protein load [12]. Activated IRE1 excises a 26-nucleotide intron from X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1), changes the open reading frame of XBP1, and induces the expression of UPR target genes, including GRP78 [13]. ATF6 is a single-pass type II transmembrane protein whose cytosolic N-terminal domain functions as a basic-leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor. Under ER stress, ATF6 is transported to the Golgi apparatus and cleaved by site-1 protease (S1P) and site-2 protease (S2P). This cleavage releases cytosolic fragments that increase the transcription of multiple UPR target genes, including GRP78 and XBP1 [14].

Cyclic AMP response element binding protein 3-like 1 (CREB3L1, also known as OASIS), which is classified as an ATF/CREB family protein, participates in the transduction of ER stress and maintains intracellular homeostasis. The structure of CREB3L1 is strongly related to that of ATF6; both proteins contain a DNA-binding bZIP domain and a transmembrane domain [15]. Similar to the activation of ATF6, the N-terminal fragment of CREB3L1 is translocated to the nucleus following regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP) conducted by S1P and S2P [16], which makes it a membrane-anchored transcription factor during ER stress. CREB3L1 has been shown to engage in collagen synthesis, bone formation and osteoblast differentiation, thus contributing to osteogenesis [17]. Similarly, the aberrant expression of CREB3L1 has been observed in sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma, low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, breast cancer, thyroid carcinoma, and desmoid tumors [18–24], which indicates its role in promoting tumorigenesis. However, considering the shared mechanisms and similarities between cancer and fibrotic diseases, which are defined as ‘wounds that do not heal’ [25, 26], CREB3L1 might function in fibrogenesis. Nonetheless, the precise role of CREB3L1 in skin fibrosis, particularly the expression and activation of CREB3L1 under fibrotic conditions and the underlying molecular mechanisms, remains unexplored.

In this study, we demonstrated the correlation between aberrant CREB3L1 expression and matrix stiffness induced fibroblast activation. Mechanical loading provokes calcium influx and triggers ER stress. The accumulated misfolded proteins initiate the RIP of CREB3L1, which leads to the release and nuclear translocation of transcriptional fragments, thus causing fibroblast activation and fibrogenesis. Moreover, we generated CREB3L1 knockdown animal models and observed an ameliorated fibrotic phenotype compared with negative control mice. Our research revealed a novel strategy for interrupting the persistent fibroblast activation driven by biomechanics, and this strategy could be applied to fibrosis in various tissues and organs.

Materials and methods

Animals

8 weeks old male mice were used for the in vivo experiments. Preliminary quantitative analysis of CREB3L1 immunohistochemistry in normal and fibrotic mouse skin defined an effect size d = 2. Type I error rate (α) was set at 0.05, and power (1-β) was set at 0.9. The sample size was determined by A priori power analysis and two-tail t-test using G*Power (v3.1.9.6), yielding 7 mice per group. This sample size was applied to subsequent research in the study.

For the bleomycin induced skin fibrosis model, 100 µL bleomycin solution (1 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Catalog# HY-17565, MedChemExpress, USA) was intradermally injected into the dorsal skin every other day for 3 weeks following fur removal.

For the mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis model, a 15 mm full-thickness incision was made on the dorsal skin after fur removal, and a loading device was secured perpendicular to the line of incision. On post-operative day 3, the expansion screws were distracted by 2 mm and gradually adjusted to 4 mm over the subsequent 2 days. To compensate for the relaxation caused by natural elongation of skin, mechanical loading was reapplied every other day for 3 weeks.

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male mice only because male animals exhibited less variability in fibrotic phenotype. It is unknown whether the findings are relevant for female mice.

Primary human dermal fibroblast isolation and culturation

Primary human dermal fibroblasts were isolated from discarded foreskin tissue obtained from circumcisions on healthy male patients aged 8 to 18 years, with written informed consent. Adipose tissue and loose fascia of the foreskin were trimmed away and the tissue were cut into strips approximately 0.5 cm × 1.5 cm. The tissue strips were submerged in Dispase II solution (Catalog# 17105041, Gibco, USA) for 20 h at 4 °C. The epidermis was separated from the dermis. The remaining tissue was then transferred to 1,500 U/mL Collagenase IV solution (Catalog# 17104019, Gibco, USA) and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The collagenase digestion was centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was resuspended, counted viable cells per milliliter, seeded onto hydrogels, and incubated with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (D-MEM, Catalog# C11965500CP, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Catalog# 10437028, Gibco, USA) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic liquid (Catalog# 15240062, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Passages 3–6 primary fibroblasts were used for experiments.

Preparation of collagen-coated polyacrylamide hydrogels

The procedures manufacturing hydrogels were as previously described [27, 28]. In brief, a solution of acrylamide and bisacrylamide with initiator ammonium persulfate and catalyst tetramethylethylenediamine were allowed to polymerize in a vertical parallel glass mold with 0.75 mm gap. To prepare hydrogels with two different stiffness, the soft hydrogel solution was first poured into the parallel glass mold and hydrosealed with ethanol. After polymerization, the ethanol was removed, and stiff hydrogel solution was subsequently added and polymerized. Hydrogels were then conjugated with rat tail collagen type I (Catalog# 354236, Corning, USA) with crosslinker sulfo-SANPAH (Catalog# S917127, Macklin, China). Stiffness of hydrogels was defined by the concentrations of acrylamide and bisacrylamide, and was verified by Chiaro Nanoindenter (Optics Life 11, Netherlands). The hydrogels were disinfected with 75% ethanol and ultraviolet before used for cell culturation.

Collagen gel contraction assay

Cells were trypsin digested, resuspended in 1 mg/mL, pH = 7 collagen type I solution (Catalog# 354236, Corning, USA), and transferred into a 24-well plate. The collagen-cell mixture was allowed to polymerized for 30 min at 37 °C, and was gently detached from the well wall. Gels were scanned after 3 days using Bio 6000 scanner (Microtek, China). The area of contracted gel was analyzed using ImageJ (v1.54).

Wound healing assay

A 70 µL cell suspension at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/mL was seeded into each well of the 2-well Culture-Insert (Catalog# 80209, ibidi, Germany) and incubated for 24 h. The Culture-Insert was removed to allow cells migrating through the 500 μm cell-free gap between the two cell layers. The cells were incubated with D-MEM supplemented with 1% FBS, and were imaged every hour using the Livecyte label-free system (Phase Focus, UK). Phase contrast images of migrated cells were analyzed using ImageJ (v1.54) plugin Wound Healing Coherency Tool.

Live cell imaging

Cells were seeded into glass-bottomed 24-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well. Cells were imaged every 2 h using the Livecyte label-free system (Phase Focus, UK). Images were processed and analyzed using Phase Focus Dashboards (v3.7, Phase Focus, UK). Cell motility outputs were visualized using the Chemotaxis and Migration Tool (v2.0, ibidi, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy grids staining

HDFs cultured on soft and stiff hydrogels were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution, and harvested with cell scrapers. Collected cells were washed with phosphate buffer, embedded in 1% agarose, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and resin embedded. The resin blocks were cut into 70 nm sections using the EM UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica, Germany), and were collected onto the 200 meshes cuprum grids with formvar film. The cuprum grids were stained with 2% uranyl acetate saturated alcohol solution avoiding light and then with 2.6% lead citrate solution avoiding carbon dioxide. The grids were observed using an HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Western blotting

Cells were grown on soft or stiff hydrogels to confluence and treated with 50 µM N-acetyl-leucyl-leucyl-norleucinal (ALLN, Catalog# HY-18964, MedChemExpress, USA) for 2 h, 5 µM tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA, Catalog# HY-19696, MedChemExpress, USA) for 4 h, 1 µM thapsigargin (Catalog# MZ8006, MaoKang, China) for 4 h before harvest. Cells were lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Catalog# PB340, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on ice. Tissue samples were grinded in liquid nitrogen and lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail on ice. Lysates were centrifugated, and protein concentration of the supernatant was detected using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Catalog# BL521A, Biosharp, China). The lysates were separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The proteins were immunoblotted using the indicated antibodies and imaged using Touch Imager chemiluminescence imaging system (e-BLOT, China).

The primary antibodies were as follows: anti-CRBE3L1 (Catalog# 11235-2-AP, 1:500, Protein Tech), anti-GAPDH (Catalog# ab8245, 1:5000, Abcam), anti-βactin (Catalog# 66009-1-Ig, 1:50000, Protein Tech), anti-Collagen I (Catalog# ab260043, 1:1000, Abcam), anti-Collagen III (Catalog# 22734, 1:500, Protein Tech), anti-αSMA (Catalog# ab124964, 1:10000, Abcam), anti-Fibronectin (Catalog# ab2413, 1:2000, Abcam), anti-phospho-eIF2α (Catalog# AF1237, 1:1000, Beyotime), anti-CHOP (Catalog# A0221, 1:2000, Abclonal).

The secondary antibodies were as follows: goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP-linked (Catalog# ab6721, 1:10000, Abcam), anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked (Catalog# 7076, 1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cells were lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Catalog# PB340, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on ice. Lysates were centrifugated, and protein concentration of the supernatant was detected using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Catalog# BL521A, Biosharp, China). Hyaluronan concentration was quantified using a human hyaluronic acid ELISA kit (Catalog# R12359, Baiyi, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The limit of detection is 1.0 ng/mL.

Immunofluorescent staining

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, stained with indicated antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and coverslips were mounted with antifade reagent. Images were acquired using an Axio Imager 2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

The primary antibody used was: anti-CREB3L1 (Catalog# 11235-2-AP, 1:100, Protein Tech). The secondary antibodies used were: donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Alexa Fluor 488, Catalog# A21206, 1:500, Invitrogen), donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Alexa Fluor 555, Catalog# A31572, 1:500, Invitrogen).

The nuclear/cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio was quantified using ImageJ (v1.54) plugin Intensity Ratio Nuclei Cytoplasm Tool.

Free calcium imaging

Cells were cultured on soft or stiff hydrogels to confluence and treated with 5 µM GsMTx4 (Catalog# HY-P1410, MedChemExpress, USA) for 4 h, 1 mM 1,10-phenanthroline (Catalog# SG2020, Beyotime, China) for 4 h prior to Fluo-4 AM loading. The medium was removed and the cells were incubated with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) with 5 µM Fluo-4, AM (Catalog# 40704ES50, Yeasen, China), 0.04% Pluronic F-127 (Catalog# 60318ES60, Yeasen, China), and 1 mM Probenecid (Catalog# 55052ES76, Yeasen, China) at 37 °C for 60 min. The Fluo-4, AM loading solution was then removed and the cells were washed with HBSS. Images were captured using an LSM 900/Axio Observer 7 microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ (v1.54).

Histological imaging

Tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5 μm sections. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), picrosirius red, and Alcian blue. The H&E and Alcian blue stained sections were imaged using an SQS-40R slide scanning system (9Xbio, China). Picrosirius red staining were imaged using Axio Imager 2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a polarizing filter set. Visual directional analysis and distribution of orientations of collagens were performed on picrosirius red stained polarized images using ImageJ (v1.54) plugin OrientationJ (v2.0.7).

Nano-indentation

Tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, cryopreserved, and sliced into 50 μm sections. Sections were stained with H&E and analyzed using a Chiaro Nanoindenter (Optics Life 11, Netherlands) equipped with a probe of spring constant of k = 3.47 N/m, GEO factor (in air) = 2.75, spherical glass tip = 24 μm. The dermis was assessed using a 9 × 9 grid with a sub-grid length of 50 μm, and the center of each grid was determined as the measurement point. For each grid measurement point, adjacent contact points were also assessed. The average stiffness of three points represented the stiffness of the grid, and the average stiffness of all grids represented the stiffness of the region. For each skin sample, three sections were analyzed, and the results were averaged. The Hertzian contact model was applied for each load-indentation curve to determine the Young’s modulus. Collected data were analyzed using the software provided by the manufacturer (DataViewer v2.6.0).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing and analysis

HDFs cultured on soft and stiff hydrogels were crosslinked with formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to fragments. The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was conducted using Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit (Catalog# 26157, Thermo Scientific, USA) and CREB3L1 antibody (Catalog# sc-514635, Santa Cruz, USA) following manufacturer’s constructions. Library construction and sequencing were performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) following the NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit (300 cycles) protocol. Raw reads were quality controlled, aligned to human reference genome (UCSC hg19). Aligned reads were used for peak calling using Model-based Analysis for ChIP-Seq (MACS, v1.4.2 20120305). Peak annotation and visualization were performed using ChIPseeker (v1.44.0, R 4.2.3) and Integrative Genomics Viewer (v2.9.15).

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were integrated using the reciprocal PCA pipeline. The integrated dataset was identified with highly variable features, clustered, non-linear dimensional reduced using Seurat (v5.2.0, R 4.2.3) [29]. Cell type annotations were assigned using SingleR (v2.0.0, R 4.2.3). Single-Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) pipeline were conducted using pySCENIC (v0.12.1, Python 3.10.18) following the standard workflow [30].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0) and R (v4.2.3). Each experiment was replicated 3 independent times. Each in vivo experiment contained 7 independent mice. Student’s t-test was performed to examine the difference between 2 groups. Levene’s test was performed to test the equality of variances. One-way analysis of variance followed by a post hoc test (Tukey test) was used to compare multiple groups. Data were presented as means ± standard deviations. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic atlas revealed exclusive fibroblast clusters under fibrotic conditions

Skin fibrosis is a complicated biological process characterized by the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix. To elucidate the differences in cellular composition and molecular mechanisms between normal and fibrotic skin, we integrated and analyzed three single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets: normal skin tissue, hypertrophic scar tissue, and systemic sclerosis skin biopsy samples (Fig. S1a). Despite differences in etiology, hypertrophic scars and systemic sclerosis share similar pathological characteristics, such as an overabundant ECM and augmented matrix stiffness. After quality control, a total of 82,559 cells were obtained, subjected to Louvain algorithm clustering, and visualized in 2D by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Distinct clusters of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, T cells, pericytes, epithelial cells, monocytes, and nature killer (NK) cells were identified (Fig. 1a), and verified with known marker genes (Fig. 1b, S1b). The cell composition of the skin varies between normal and fibrotic conditions. Fibroblasts comprised 32% of all cells in normal skin tissues, whereas a notable increase in the proportion of fibroblasts (53%) was observed in fibrotic skin tissues (Fig. S1c).

The vital increase in the number of fibroblasts under mechanical cues suggests their significance in matrix stiffness induced skin fibrosis. We focused on the heterogeneity of fibroblasts under normal and fibrotic conditions and identified nine separate clusters (Fig. 1c, S1d). Interestingly, two specific clusters were present exclusively under fibrotic conditions, which suggests that they play a distinct role in promoting skin fibrosis induced by matrix stiffening. Further Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed their active response to mechanical stimuli and positive engagement in remodeling ECM (Fig. 1d). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis also revealed cytoskeletal deformation and the activation of mechanosensitive signaling pathways in response to mechanical cues (Fig. 1e).

Collectively, these exclusive fibroblast clusters presented under fibrotic conditions, displayed mechanosensitive features, and exhibited positive effects on the biomechanical response and ECM reorganization. This suggests that these clusters play crucial roles in promoting skin fibrosis progression. Hence, exploring the transcriptional landscape of these mechanosensitive clusters could help us understand their crucial role in fibrosis and reveal unique molecular mechanisms for further intervention.

Single-cell regulatory network identified CREB3L1 regulon as a driver for fibroblasts under fibrotic conditions

The transcriptional landscape is defined by an intermingled and complicated gene regulatory network, which is composed of transcription factors (TFs) and downstream genes. To explore the regulatory network that determines the identity of these distinct clusters under fibrotic conditions, we performed SCENIC analysis and identified 94 sets of regulons. These regulons, which are primarily selected by TFs with co-expressed genes, and whose recognition motifs are significantly enriched on the correct upstream regulators are retained (Fig. S2a). Among these, 22 regulons remained quiescent in normal skin but exhibited relatively high activity under fibrotic conditions (Fig. 2a). Cluster analysis revealed the JUN regulon and the CREB3L1 regulon as the most active regulons in matrix stiffness induced skin fibrosis (Fig. 2a). The comprehensive expression of JUN was detected among fibroblasts (Fig. 2b). Moreover, the expression of CREB3L1 was more restricted to unique clusters under fibrotic conditions (Fig. 2b), although its exclusive role remains vague. The enrichment of the Gene Ontology terms “extracellular matrix organization (0030198)” and “cellular response to mechanical stimulus (0071260)” was more consistent with CREB3L1 expression than with JUN expression (Fig. 2c). These findings indicate that CREB3L1 is among the most noteworthy regulons in the specific fibroblast clusters under fibrotic conditions, which emphasizes its potential role in promoting skin fibrogenesis upon matrix stiffening.

Histological investigations revealed dense, thick collagen fibers and augmented matrix stiffness in the human fibrotic dermis (Fig. 2d and e), and IHC staining revealed the elevated expression of CREB3L1 in fibrotic tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, we adopted a bleomycin induced skin fibrosis model and a mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis model (Fig. S2b - S2e), and obtained similar results (Fig. 2f and g). These results indicate a correlation between increased CREB3L1 expression and skin fibrosis.

To simulate normal and fibrotic skin in vitro, we used polyacrylamide hydrogels with different substrate rigidities according to our previously published methods (Fig. S2f) [27]. Western blotting revealed increased expression of both precursor (85 kDa) CREB3L1 and S1P cleaved (55 kDa) CREB3L1 in human dermal fibroblasts with matrix stiffening, whereas the expression of fully cleaved (50 kDa) CREB3L1 was slightly decreased (Fig. 2h, S2g). N-acetyl-leucyl-leucyl-norleucinal (ALLN) treatment, to prevent cleaved CREB3L1 degradation [31], increased the total amount of CREB3L1 detected, as well as the proportion of mature nuclear CREB3L1 (Fig. 2h).

Coincidentally, single-cell regulatory network analysis revealed that CREB3L1 is a crucial transcriptional driver of mechanosensitive fibroblasts in skin fibrosis. We further demonstrated the elevated expression of CREB3L1 in fibrotic tissues with augmented matrix stiffness, and we found that matrix stiffness alone could induce CREB3L1 expression and activation. These results indicate the significance of the mechanosensitive TF CREB3L1 in skin fibrosis.

CREB3L1 is required for matrix stiffness induced fibroblasts activation

The stiffness of the fibrotic ECM results in mechanical stress on fibroblasts and drives the quiescent fibroblasts into activated myofibroblasts [32]. These myofibroblasts are characterized by their contractile (‘myo’) ability and ECM production (‘fibro’) ability [2], which contribute to the formation of dense, stiff fibrotic tissue. We further investigated the influence of CREB3L1 depletion on matrix stiffness induced fibroblast activation.

Collagen gel contraction assays revealed a reduced contractile ability following CREB3L1 knockdown (Fig. 3a and b). Further immunoblotting revealed a significant downregulation in the protein expression of the myofibroblast marker α-SMA in response to the depletion of CREB3L1 compared with the negative control (Fig. 3c). In addition, we investigated the expression of crucial ECM proteins, including collagen types I and III and fibronectin, which increase the tensile strength of the matrix. Western blot assays revealed a notable decrease in ECM protein synthesis upon CREB3L1 knockdown (Fig. 3c). We also investigated the role of CREB3L1 in regulating the synthesis of extracellular polysaccharides, such as hyaluronan, which governs the hydration and viscoelasticity of the extracellular matrix. ELISA revealed an upregulation of hyaluronan expression in CREB3L1-depleted fibroblasts (Fig. 3d). These results collectively illustrated the contributing role of CREB3L1 in fibrotic ECM remodeling driven by matrix stiffness.

Cell migration is mechanosensitive. An individual migration strategy without stable cell-cell junctions is adopted by fibroblasts. Time-lapse phase contrast images revealed an increased migration ability of fibroblasts on a stiff matrix (Fig. S3a and Supplementary Movie 1). The depletion of CREB3L1 abolished the upregulated migration ability observed on stiff hydrogels (Fig. S3a and Supplementary Movie 2). Wound healing assays revealed a diminished migratory capacity of CREB3L1-depleted fibroblasts compared with that of negative control fibroblasts (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Movie 3). Tracking of single-fibroblast trajectory also revealed decreased displacement with CREB3L1 depletion (Fig. S3b).

These results demonstrate that CREB3L1 depletion could abolish the enhanced fibroblast contraction, migration, and ECM deposition capacity caused by substrate rigidity, which suggests that CREB3L1 is indispensable for matrix stiffness induced fibroblast activation.

Matrix stiffness activated CREB3L1 through calcium influx triggered ER stress

Inactive CREB3L1 is a single-pass membrane protein anchored to the ER, with its N-terminus facing the cytoplasm and its C-terminus facing the ER lumen (Fig. S2g). When ER homeostasis is disrupted, ER dysfunction leads to the accumulation of unfolded and misfolded proteins. Under ER stress, CREB3L1 proteins are transported to the Golgi apparatus, where they are cleaved to release their N-terminal domain. The N-terminal fragment is translocated into the nucleus to perform its transcriptional function [33]. Our western blot results revealed that matrix stiffness could increase CREB3L1 expression (Fig. 2h). Immunofluorescence staining further verified the alteration in subcellular localization. In fibroblasts cultured on soft hydrogels, the CREB3L1 protein was observed to be distributed around the nucleus, with a low immunofluorescence signal in the nucleus (Fig. 4a), which indicates that the quiescent CREB3L1 protein was stored in the ER. Upon substrate stiffening, the overall expression of the CREB3L1 protein was increased, and nuclear CREB3L1 protein expression dramatically increased (Fig. 4a), which suggests that CREB3L1 was transcriptionally activated under mechanical cues.

Normal ER function is highly dependent on a proper luminal calcium concentration, which is manipulated by mechanical cues [34, 35]. Calcium imaging revealed a greater calcium influx in fibroblasts cultured on stiff hydrogels than in those cultured on soft hydrogels (Fig. 4b). Blockage of mechanosensitive channels and stretch-activated cation channels by GsMTx4 significantly diminished the calcium influx caused by matrix stiffness, as well as the nuclear accumulation of CREB3L1 (Fig. 4b). Treatment with 1,10-phenanthroline, an S2P inhibitor, did not significantly influence calcium influx but successfully stopped the nuclear translocation of CREB3L1 (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, the immunofluorescence images of CREB3L1 in fibroblasts treated with 1,10-phenanthroline exhibited a high fluorescence signal around the nucleus (Fig. 4a), which indicated the S1P cleaved but not S2P cleaved CREB3L1 protein. This finding suggests that mechanical cues can trigger the proteolysis process of CREB3L1, whereas 1,10-phenanthroline treatment inhibits this process at S2P cleavage. These results illustrate that the calcium influx caused by matrix stiffness leads to the activation of CREB3L1.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis revealed expanded ER membranes under mechanical stress (Fig. 4c). Western blot assays revealed increased expression of ER stress marker C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) and UPR marker phospho-eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (p-eIF2α) when substrate rigidity increased (Fig. 4d). These results collectively demonstrate that matrix stiffness can increase the intracellular calcium concentration and trigger ER stress. To further investigate the role of ER stress in regulating CREB3L1, we applied tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), a known ER stress inhibitor [36, 37], and thapsigargin, an ER stress inducer [38], to fibroblasts cultivated on stiff hydrogels. Western blot analysis confirmed that TUDCA treatment partly abolished the ER stress caused by mechanical stress, whereas thapsigargin treatment had the opposite effect (Fig. 4d). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed that TUDCA successfully blocked CREB3L1 nuclear accumulation and activation (Fig. 4e).

Our results revealed that matrix stiffness induced CREB3L1 to be cleaved and activated through calcium influx triggered ER stress. Furthermore, the application of TUDCA to alleviate ER stress could prevent the nuclear accumulation of CREB3L1, which suggests the therapeutic potential of TUDCA in skin fibrosis treatment.

The transcriptional landscape of CREB3L1

To elucidate the transcriptional and epigenetic dynamics of activated CREB3L1 across fibroblasts on soft and stiff hydrogels, we inspected the sequence logo of CREB3L1 and subsequently mapped its genome-wide CREB3L1 binding sites using ChIP-Seq (Fig. 5a). The identified binding regions located 2000 bp upstream and downstream from transcription start sites (TSS) were retained for further analysis. We annotated 163 peaks located in the gene promoter region in the fibroblasts cultured on stiff hydrogels, whereas 116 peaks were annotated in the fibroblasts on soft substrates (Fig. 5b). In addition to the 57 shared peaks, CREB3L1 regulated the transcription of 106 unique genes in fibroblasts on stiff hydrogels, this number decreased to 59 in fibroblasts on soft hydrogels (Fig. 5c), which suggests that CREB3L1 has greater transcriptional activity when the matrix is stiffened. We next conducted GO analysis on the set of genes regulated by CREB3L1 in fibroblasts on stiff hydrogels. Biological process of GO indicated increased cell migration ability and enhanced ECM organization (Fig. 5c), which could contribute to skin fibrosis and was in accordance with our previous results. Cellular component of GO analysis revealed that the regulated genes were involved mainly in the plasma membrane and extracellular space, and molecular function analysis revealed dynamic cation binding and ECM construction functions (Fig. 5c). The combined pathway enrichment of KEGG and Reactome presented distinct upregulated signaling pathways related to cell pluripotency, spliceosome and ion channels (Fig. 5d). Interestingly, both the results of the GO analysis and the results of the pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated calcium transport capacity was regulated by CREB3L1, which suggests that CREB3L1 maintained the ER stress state under mechanical stimulus. We further investigated the genes identified by GO annotation and pathway enrichment. CREB3L1 binds the promoter regions of various genes including TRPV6 and CCR1, which disrupt calcium homeostasis [39, 40], and SENP2 and FOSL1, which promote fibrogenesis [41–44] (Fig. 5e).

Inhibiting CREB3L1 expression attenuated bleomycin and mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of interfering CREB3L1 in skin fibrosis, we constructed bleomycin induced skin fibrosis and mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis animal models (Fig. S4a, S4b), which are similar to the clinical manifestations of systemic sclerosis and hypertrophic scarring, respectively. Fibrotic skin induced by bleomycin and mechanical loading presented dense collagen deposition and a thickened dermis (Figs. 6a and b and 7a and b). However, the subcutaneous administration of lentivirus around the fibrotic area successfully diminished CREB3L1 expression (Figs. 6c and 7c, S4c), and partly alleviated the fibrotic phenotype (Figs. 6a and b and 7a and b). Nanoindentation analysis also confirmed the increased Young’s modulus of fibrotic skin tissue, and CREB3L1 knockdown softened the fibrotic tissue (Figs. 6d and 7d).

Our results revealed an upregulation of hyaluronan in CREB3L1-depleted fibroblasts (Fig. 3d). To investigate the expression of acidic polysaccharides in fibrotic skin with CREB3L1 knockdown, we performed Alcian blue staining. Under normal conditions, sparse blue dots were observed, possibly representing hyaluronan-containing structures (Fig. S4d). In bleomycin induced skin fibrosis, no Alcian blue signal was detected, whereas CREB3L1 knockdown could partly restore acidic polysaccharides synthesis (Fig. S4d), indicating the positive role of CREB3L1 knockdown in remodeling a physiological ECM architecture. Interestingly, no acidic polysaccharide signal was observed in normal scar or mechanical loading induced fibrotic skin, and CREB3L1 knockdown did not significantly restore acidic polysaccharide contents under mechanical loading (Fig. S4e). These divergent results between bleomycin and mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis models may reflect differences in the underlying etiology (inflammation versus stretch) and suggest a complex role of CREB3L1 in regulating polysaccharide synthesis in a context-dependent manner.

To better evaluate the ECM architecture, we performed picrosirius red staining to determine the composition and alignment of the collagen fibers. Under normal conditions, the collagen fibers present a basket-weave orientation that contributes to the flexibility and extensibility of the skin (Fig. 6e) [45]. With bleomycin treatment or mechanical loading, the fibrotic skin demonstrated a more parallel and less organized collagen structure (Figs. 6e and 7e), which led to stiffness and loss of skin function. CREB3L1 knockdown ameliorated the collagen fiber alignment and ensured physiological ECM remodeling (Figs. 6e and 7e). We further assigned pseudocolors to the collagen fibers according to their direction (Figs. 6f and 7f), and calculated the distribution of orientation. The results of the visual direction analysis supported the finding that the CREB3L1 depletion could alleviate the reorganization of the fibrotic ECM. In the normal dermis, the collagen fibers cross-linked to form a basket-weave network, whose orientation distribution displayed two peaks. In the bleomycin treated fibrotic dermis, only one significant peak was observed, which suggested parallel collagen fibers, whereas CREB3L1 depletion remodeled the aligned collagen fiber distribution (Fig. 6g). Similar outcomes were observed for mechanical loading induced skin fibrosis and CREB3L1 knockdown compared with normal scarring without mechanical load (Fig. 7g). In addition, it is interesting to note that owing to the incision without mechanical loading, the normal scar dermis exhibited a more parallel alignment than normal skin did. Furthermore, CREB3L1 knockdown partly ameliorated the ECM architecture but did not a fully restore to normal skin. Overall, our results highlight the effectiveness of blocking CREB3L1 activation in mitigating skin fibrosis.

Conclusion and discussion

In the present study, we explored the transcriptional atlas of normal versus fibrotic skin through an integrated analysis of single-cell sequencing data from normal skin, hypertrophic scar tissue, and systemic sclerotic skin. We identified unique fibroblast clusters that were specifically presented under fibrotic conditions. These fibroblast clusters exhibited mechanosensitive characteristics and actively participated in fibrotic tissue remodeling. Further transcriptional regulatory network analysis indicated that CREB3L1 was the most significant TF that promoted a profibrotic phenotype in these clusters. Our results verified the upregulation and activation of CREB3L1 in skin fibrosis, and demonstrated that therapeutic strategies inhibiting CREB3L1 could alleviate fibrosis progression both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, we demonstrated a possible molecular mechanism of CREB3L1 activation induced by matrix stiffness. The opening of ion channels driven by mechanical stimuli caused calcium influx and ER stress in fibroblasts, which in turn led to CREB3L1 cleavage and activation. Activated CREB3L1 translocates to the nucleus and functions transcriptionally to promote fibrosis.

Cells adopt their migration strategy based on the environment and biological function. In fibrosis, fibroblasts recognize the ECM conditions, polarize, and reorganize the cytoskeleton to generate a contractile force that drives cell migration. Characteristic strong stress fibers, clear polarization and robust focal adhesions define individual migration [46]. Collective migration is characterized by stable attachment to neighboring cells and the organization of leader and follower cells, which is exemplified by epithelial cells during wound closure [47]. Retrograde migration is an alternative mode of migration that involves the retrograde flow of the actin cytoskeleton, which generates retrograde shear forces and drives the cell body forward [48].

CREB3L1 was first identified in long-term cultured mouse astrocytes and was found to be transiently upregulated in embryos but remained low in adults [49], indicating its developmentally regulated expression. The upregulation of CREB3L1 protein expression was observed in gliotic events [49], whose pathological characteristics include fibrosis. CREB3L1 is also highly expressed in osteoblasts and directly binds to the unfolded protein response element-like sequence in the promoter region of COL1A1 [50]. CREB3L1 deficiency in mice led to severe osteopenia due to a lack of collagen type I [50], confirming its role in organizing the bone matrix. In various tumors, including those of the breast, bladder, and pancreas, CREB3L1 contributes to fibrosis in the tumor microenvironment and is associated with a poor prognosis [51]. Considering our results suggest that CREB3L1 promotes skin fibrosis, CREB3L1 might not only play a conserved and significant role in organ fibrosis but also be involved in other diseases with a fibrosis-like phenotype.

Fibroblasts are a group of cells that exhibit vast heterogeneity [52]. As technology advances, researchers are continuously identifying novel clusters and trying to map the enormous transcriptional landscape of fibroblasts. Myofibroblasts, which are activated fibroblasts that promote fibrosis progression, lack unique molecular markers. The widely acknowledged marker α-SMA is typically expressed in smooth muscle cells. Other myofibroblast markers, including latent TGFβ binding protein 1 (LTBP1), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and integrin α11β1 [53–55], are not unique to myofibroblasts. Our integrated analysis of normal and fibrotic skin revealed a specific mechanosensitive fibroblast cluster that exhibited elevated contractile and ECM production capacities, which suggests that it could represent the ‘myofibroblasts’ in our dataset. We further identified CREB3L1 as a signature TF that maintains the cell identity. Together with other markers, CREB3L1 can help us to distinguish myofibroblasts in fibrotic tissues and provide a more precise intervention strategy.

Our study focuses mainly on the activation of fibroblasts. The proposed inhibition of CREB3L1 could indeed block fibroblast activation and ameliorate skin fibrosis. However, the presence of an upstream ‘switch’ that controls differentiation into myofibroblasts remains unclear. Studies adopting pseudotime and cell trajectory analyses have identified mechanotransduction as the key to determining whether fibroblast transcriptional outcomes are profibrotic or regenerative [56, 57]. Determining the crucial midpoint that switches fibroblasts from a quiescent state to activated myofibroblasts could further elucidate the complex regulatory signaling network involved in fibrosis. Currently, therapeutic strategies targeting CREB3L1 could be effective, feasible, and commercially viable options.

CREB3L1 is activated by matrix stiffness through calcium influx triggered ER stress. Cyro-electron microscopy has revealed the three-dimensional structure of mechanically gated ion channels that explain calcium influx upon mechanical stimulation [58]. A recent study revealed that the ER membrane protein SLURP1 could preserve the calcium pump SERCA2b activity under mechanical stress, thus maintaining a high calcium level in the ER lumen [59]. These findings comprehensively illustrate that CREB3L1 activation is caused by alterations in the calcium concentration induced by matrix stiffness. ER is the primary pool for intracellular calcium storage. Under stress conditions, ER calcium homeostasis is disrupted, triggering calcium accumulation in the cytosol and mitochondria, which causes structural damage and functional disorders. This process, named calcium overload, increases the formation of mitochondria-associated membranes that facilitates excessive calcium influx into mitochondria [60] and eventually leads to mitochondrial damage and metabolic failure. Our results did not explore the dysfunction of organelles caused by calcium overload. However, the essential role of calcium overload in matrix stiffness induced skin fibrosis warrants further investigation.

Blocking CREB3L1 activation disrupts the mechanotransduction process, chemically protecting fibroblasts from external physical cues, and thus interrupting the positive feedback loop. Despite previous research, our understanding remains insufficient to support the development of selective inhibitors that interfere with the transcriptional activity of CREB3L1. Directly targeting CREB3L1 is challenging because of the lack of a defined binding pocket. Exploring the post-transcriptional modification landscape of CREB3L1 might provide promising therapeutic targets for clinical intervention. The PhosphoSitePlus database identified seven phosphorylation sites on CREB3L1, but all these sites were supported by fewer than three references, which makes the role of CREB3L1 phosphorylation unclear. The gaps in our knowledge regarding CREB3L1 phosphorylation, as well as other post-transcriptional modifications and the interactions with other binding partners that define the transcriptional activity, need further research, and could provide alternative targets for inhibiting CREB3L1 transcription [61].

In conclusion, our results highlight the essential role of CREB3L1 in skin fibrosis development. We demonstrate that CREB3L1 cleavage and activation are required for matrix stiffness induced fibroblast activation via a calcium influx induced ER stress mechanism. Our therapeutic interventions aimed at CREB3L1 inhibition have been shown to mitigate skin fibrosis. This study highlights the importance of CREB3L1 in skin fibrosis, and further application of our findings to other fibrotic diseases can accelerate translational research.
