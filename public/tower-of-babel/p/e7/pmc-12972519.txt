RNA modifications in peripheral blood are associated with acute coronary syndrome☆
Authors: Miron Sopic, Victoria Stopa, Jelena Munjas, Aleksandar Davidović, Luka Vukmirović, Dane Cvijanović, Marko Milanov, Fabio Martelli, François Bernardin, Nathalie Michelle Legrave, Yvan Devaux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) trigger inflammatory and immune reactions, yet whether this response is associated with changes in RNA modifications remains poorly understood. We investigated global epitranscriptomic patterns in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI, n = 118), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI, n = 22), unstable angina pectoris (UAP, n = 10), and stable angina pectoris (SAP, n = 152). Using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry, we quantified N1-methyladenosine (m1A), 2′-O-methyladenosine (2′-O-mA), N6-methyladenosine (m6A), N6,2′-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am), and pseudouridine (Ψ), together with their ratios to unmodified nucleotides.Levels of 2′-O-mA (P = 0.004), m6Am (P < 0.001), and Ψ (P = 0.021) were significantly decreased in STEMI compared with SAP. m6Am was also reduced in UAP (P = 0.037) and NSTEMI (P = 0.012), whereas m1A and m6A did not differ between groups. Among modification ratios, only the m6Am/A ratio declined across ACS subtypes (UAP P = 0.015; NSTEMI P = 0.032; STEMI P = 0.007), indicating relative cap-adjacent hypomethylation. Reduced m6Am correlated inversely with hsCRP (r = −0.19, P < 0.001) and hsTnI (r = −0.24, P < 0.001). In multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and hsCRP, m6Am remained independently associated with STEMI (OR = 0.14, 95% CI 0.02–0.81; P = 0.028).These findings demonstrate selective RNA modification changes in ACS and identify m6Am as an independent epitranscriptomic marker associated with myocardial infarction.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for an estimated 32% of all global deaths in 2022 [1], [2]. Within this broad category, ischemic heart disease, driven primarily by the progression and complications of atherosclerosis, represents the single largest contributor to CVD mortality [2]. The most severe clinical manifestations of ischemic heart disease are acute coronary syndromes (ACS), which include ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina pectoris (UAP). ACS arises when an atherosclerotic plaque becomes unstable exposing thrombogenic material that triggers platelet activation and thrombus formation. The resulting sudden reduction in coronary blood flow leads to myocardial ischemia of varying severity. Upon reperfusion, immune cells infiltrate the damaged area to facilitate healing, but this process can also drive excessive inflammation, fibrosis, and cardiac dysfunction. A deeper understanding of the immune response following ACS is therefore essential for risk stratification and the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

Epitranscriptomics is an emerging field of RNA biology that investigates chemical modifications of RNA and their roles in regulating gene expression, cellular function, and disease mechanisms. Although RNA modifications were first identified over 50 years ago, their significance has only recently gained attention with the advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies capable of mapping these modifications transcriptome-wide. To date, over 170 distinct types of RNA modifications have been identified, affecting both coding and non-coding RNAs. Among these, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most extensively studied and functionally characterized. M6A is a reversible modification that influences multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including pre-mRNA splicing, nuclear export, translation efficiency, and transcript stability [3]. The regulation of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is mediated by a dynamic interplay of “writers,” “erasers,” and “readers” [3]. The METTL3 complex (methyltransferase-like protein 3) deposits m6A marks, which are removed by demethylases such as fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) and alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase alkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), and recognized by YTH domain-containing proteins such as YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 1 (YTHDF1), YTHDF2, and YTH domain containing 1 (YTHDC1) to influence RNA processing and translation. Recent studies have demonstrated that m6A modifications play critical roles in immune cell development and function, including T cell homeostasis, macrophage activation, dendritic cell maturation, and the regulation of inflammatory responses [4]. For instance, METTL3-mediated m6A methylation has been shown to be essential for the programs CD4+ T-cell activation and effector T-cell differentiation [5], while FTO and ALKBH5 are involved in modulating cytokine expression and antiviral responses [6], [7].

In addition to m6A, other RNA modifications are emerging as potentially important regulators of immune function, although their roles are far less explored. 2′-O-methyladenosine (2′-O-mA) and N6,2′-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am), often located at the 5′ end of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) adjacent to the cap structure, are deposited by cap-specific methyltransferases such as phosphorylated CTD interacting factor 1 (PCIF1), which catalyzes methylation of adenosine at the first transcribed nucleotide [8]. These modifications influence mRNA stability and translation initiation by modulating interactions with cap-binding proteins and protecting transcripts from decapping [9], [10]. Pseudouridine (Ψ) is introduced through isomerization of uridine residues by pseudouridine synthases (PUS enzymes) [11]. This conversion enhances base stacking and RNA structural stability, affects translation fidelity and speed, and can reduce activation of innate immune sensors such as Toll-like receptors 7/8 (TLR7/8), thereby modulating inflammatory responses innate immune recognition [12]. N1-methyladenosine (m1A), found in both tRNA and mRNA, is installed by methyltransferase complexes including tRNA methyltransferase (TRMT) 6/61 A (cytosolic) and TRMT61B (mitochondrial) [13]. The addition of a methyl group at the N1 position alters base-pairing properties, influencing RNA secondary structure, which in turn can modulate translation efficiency and cellular stress responses [14].

To date, the link between RNA modifications in immune cells and ACS remain poorly understood. In this study, we aimed to analyse the global levels of selected RNA modifications m6A, 2′-O-mA, m6Am, m1A, and Ψ in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) of ACS patients using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC/MS).

Methods

This study enrolled 200 patients with ACS and 200 patients with stable angina pectoris (SAP) who were admitted to the Clinical Hospital Center Zvezdara (Belgrade, Serbia) between May 2022 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (approval no. 6/5/2022), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. After excluding samples with insufficient RNA yield or quality for downstream analyses, the final study cohort comprised 118 patients with STEMI, 22 with NSTEMI, 10 with UAP, and 152 with SAP. Since the aim of this study was to compare chronic and acute coronary artery disease, no healthy controls were enrolled and SAP patients were considered as the control group for this study.

Whole blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes 24 to 48 h after the acute event in case of ACS patients, or before elective coronary angiography in SAP patients. Samples were kept at 4 °C during transport, and PBMCs were isolated as previously described [15]. PBMCs were lysed in TRIzol reagent and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

Quantification of modified nucleotides was performed according to our previously published method [16]. Briefly, 250 ng of total RNA was first digested with Nuclease P1 to cleave RNA into nucleosides, followed by dephosphorylation with calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP). Twenty-five microliters of the obtained aliquot was subsequently mixed with 100 μL methanol containing 0.065 nmol d3-m6A as internal standard. After evaporation and resuspension in 40 μL water containing 20 mM ammonium formate, 1 μL extract was analyzed on a LC-MS system equipped with a Vanquish® Horizon UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap® Exploris480® mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Chromatographic separation was achieved as previously described [16]. The MS experiment was performed using electrospray ionization with polarity switching enabled. Source parameters were applied as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 40; aux gas flow rate, 8; sweep gas flow rate, 1; spray voltage, 3,5 kV (+) / 2,5 kV (−); capillary temperature, 350 °C; S-lense RF level, 50; aux gas heater temperature, 150 °C. The Orbitrap mass analyzer was operated at a resolving power of 120,000 (at 200 m/z) in full-scan mode (scan range: m/z 75–800; automatic gain control target set to 1e6 charges with an intensity threshold of 5e3, MS2 was perform at Orbitrap resolution of 15,000). Data from LC-MS measurements were acquired with the Thermo Xcalibur software (Version 4.7.69.37) and modified nucleotides were analyzed using TraceFinder (Version 5.1). Data were normalized to the internal standard, d3-m6A, for relative quantification.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As all continuous variables demonstrated non-normal distributions, non-parametric statistical methods were applied. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple groups. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant difference (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed using the Mann–Whitney U test to determine which groups differed. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation analyses were then performed to assess the relationships between modified nucleotides and a range of continuous clinical parameters, including age, body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, LVEF, hsTnI, and hsCRP (Fig. 4). Across all modified nucleotides, we observed positive correlations, indicating coordinated regulation of RNA modification patterns. The closest associations were seen among m6Am, 2′-O-mA, U, and Ψ. In particular, m6Am and 2′-O-mA showed one of the strongest relationships (r = 0.76, p < 0.001), suggesting shared regulatory pathways at the RNA cap structure. Several RNA modifications showed weak but significant associations with anthropometric and clinical data. U (r = 0.13, p = 0.019), Ψ (r = 0.12, p = 0.031), and m6Am (r = 0.12, p = 0.035) correlated positively with age. U (r = −0.19, p < 0.001), Ψ (r = −0.12, p = 0.032), m6Am (r = −0.19, p < 0.001), and 2′-O-mA (r = −0.17, p = 0.003) were inversely associated with hsCRP. m6Am (r = −0.24, p < 0.001), 2′-O-mA (r = −0.12, p = 0.030), and m6A (r = −0.12, p = 0.040) all correlated negatively with hsTnI. Due to the limited number of patients in the NSTEMI and UAP subgroups, subsequent analyses were focused on STEMI and SAP patients. We restricted further comparisons to variables and subgroups with adequate sample sizes to ensure sufficient statistical power. Thus, we next examined whether modified nucleotides or their ratio to unmodified nucleotides were influenced by sex, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or the use of statins, ACE inhibitors, β-blockers, diuretics, or oral antidiabetic medications. None of these factors had a significant effect on modified nucleotides or ratio levels in STEMI or SAP (data not shown).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining the relationship between RNA modifications in blood cells and ACS. In particular, no previous investigation has assessed global changes in m1A, 2′-O-mA, m6A, m6Am, and Ψ in the context of ACS. Our findings show that PBMCs from ACS patients display a distinct RNA modification profile, suggesting that epitranscriptomic marks may change upon acute immune activation or systemic stress responses that accompany plaque destabilization and myocardial injury.

Among known RNA modifications, m6A has been the most extensively studied across human diseases, including cardiovascular pathologies. Several studies have shown that m6A and its regulatory enzymes contribute to cardiac remodeling, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and overall heart function [17], [18], [19], [20]. However, much less is known about m6A levels in circulating blood cells during ACS. Our earlier pilot study indicated altered m6A levels in whole blood of post–MI patients with reduced LVEF [16]. In addition, bioinformatic analyses have demonstrated that 18 of 24 m6A regulators are differentially expressed in peripheral blood from STEMI patients compared with SAP [21]. Therefore, the present study extends past observations through the quantification of global m6A levels in PBMCs in a well-characterized cohort of ACS and SAP patients. In contrast to earlier studies demonstrating differential expression of m6A-regulating enzymes [21], our analysis did not reveal significant differences in global m6A levels between ACS and SAP. Importantly, this negative result does not exclude biologically meaningful alterations in m6A. Indeed, m6A deposition is highly transcript- and site-specific, and focal changes at individual nucleotide positions may not be detectable when measuring global modification levels with LC-MS. It is therefore plausible that ACS-related epitranscriptomic remodeling occurs at specific loci or within defined gene networks without producing measurable shifts in total m6A content. Future work applying single-nucleotide–resolution approaches, such as direct RNA sequencing, will be required to investigate such localized m6A signatures.

Compared with m6A, m1A has been far less explored in cardiovascular disease, and to date no studies have quantitatively assessed total m1A levels in blood from ACS patients. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that m1A may play a role in ischemic injury. A recent study by Chokkalla et al. [22] reported that cerebral ischemia leads to upregulation of the m1A demethylase ALKBH3 and a decrease in m1A levels at 24 h of reperfusion in a mouse model. In our cohort, m1A levels in PBMCs did not differ significantly between ACS and SAP patients. This finding supports the notion that m1A dynamics are highly context-dependent, influenced by cell type, tissue environment, and the nature of the injury. The mechanisms governing m1A deposition and removal may therefore vary substantially between neuronal tissue subjected to focal ischemia and circulating immune cells responding to coronary plaque rupture.

In contrast to m1A and m6A, our study revealed significant alterations in the levels of 2′-O-mA, m6Am, and Ψ. All three modified nucleotides were markedly decreased in STEMI compared with SAP, whereas m6Am was significantly reduced not only in STEMI but also in UAP and NSTEMI relative to SAP. Both 2′-O-mA and m6Am are positioned at or near the 5′ cap of mRNAs, where they contribute to transcript stability, RNA processing, and translational efficiency [4], [9], [23]. Their depletion in PBMCs may therefore indicate enhanced RNA turnover or impaired protein synthesis during acute coronary events. Ψ, which is enriched in multiple classes of non-coding RNAs and known to modulate innate immune sensing [24], may also participate in shaping the inflammatory response during acute ischemic injury. We additionally observed strong correlations among 2′-O-mA, m6Am, Ψ, and U, suggesting that these modifications may be governed by shared regulatory pathways, potentially linked to mRNA cap biology or broader RNA processing mechanisms. Importantly, multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated that reduced m6Am levels remained independently associated with STEMI after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and hsCRP, indicating that this association is not merely a consequence of systemic inflammatory burden or demographic imbalance. In contrast, the associations observed for 2′-O-mA and pseudouridine were attenuated after adjustment for hsCRP, suggesting that these modifications may be more closely linked to inflammatory activation during acute coronary events. These findings refine the interpretation of our results by indicating that m6Am represents the most robust and inflammation-independent epitranscriptomic alteration in ACS within this cohort.

To understand whether these changes reflected alterations in modification dynamics rather than absolute nucleotide abundance, we quantified the proportion of each modified nucleotide relative to its unmodified counterpart. Among all examined ratios, only the m6Am/A ratio showed a significant decrease in ACS subtypes compared with SAP, indicating that ACS is associated with relative hypomethylation at the m6Am position. The inverse correlations of these modifications with troponin I and hsCRP suggest that reduced RNA modification levels may reflect the severity of myocardial injury and the associated systemic inflammatory response.

Nevertheless, it is important to recognize that global quantification does not capture transcript- or site-specific changes, which may be functionally more relevant. Single-nucleotide–resolution methods could help identify specific transcripts or regulatory hotspots affected during ACS, but current direct RNA sequencing analytical pipelines do not reliably detect 2′-O-mA or m6Am, limiting our ability to resolve these signatures with existing tools.

This study has limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and we did not perform cell-type–specific or single-nucleotide resolution RNA analysis. Nonetheless, this is, to our knowledge, the first report to map RNA modification signatures in PBMCs after MI. These findings suggest that dynamic changes in RNA modifications accompany acute ischemic injury and systemic inflammation. A deeper mechanistic understanding of how these epitranscriptomic alterations influence immune cell function may uncover novel molecular pathways involved in post-MI healing and maladaptation. Ultimately, RNA modification profiling could inform new strategies for risk stratification, precision diagnostics, and the development of RNA-targeted therapies in cardiovascular disease.
