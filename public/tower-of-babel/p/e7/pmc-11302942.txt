Artificial Intelligence Machine Learning Algorithms Versus Standard Linear Demographic Analysis in Predicting Implant Size of Anatomic and Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty
Authors: Amir Boubekri, Michael Murphy, Michael Scheidt, Krishin Shivdasani, Joshua Anderson, Nickolas Garbis, Dane Salazar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Accurate and precise templating is paramount for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) to enhance preoperative planning, streamline surgery, and improve implant positioning. We aimed to evaluate the predictive potential of readily available patient demographic data in TSA and RSA implant sizing, independent of implant design.Methods:A total of 578 consecutive, primary, noncemented shoulder arthroplasty cases were retrospectively reviewed. Demographic variables and implant characteristics were recorded. Multivariate linear regressions were conducted to predict implant sizes using patient demographic variables.Results:Linear models accurately predict TSA implant sizes within 2 millimeters of humerus stem sizes 75.3% of the time, head diameter 82.1%, head height 82.1%, and RSA glenosphere diameter 77.6% of the time. Linear models predict glenoid implant sizes accurately 68.2% and polyethylene thickness 76.6% of the time and within one size 100% and 95.7% of the time, respectively.Conclusion:Linear models accurately predict shoulder arthroplasty implant sizes from demographic data. No significant statistical differences were observed between linear models and machine learning algorithms, although the analysis was underpowered. Future sufficiently powered studies are required for more robust assessment of machine learning models in predicting primary shoulder arthroplasty implant sizes based on patient demographics.

Accurate and precise templating plays a pivotal role for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) because it helps streamline preoperative surgical planning.1 Effective preoperative planning that involves advanced templating helps the surgeon understand unique patient morphology, recognize and anticipate potential surgical challenges, and achieve optimal implant positioning to minimize complications.1 Templating may contribute to improving operating room efficiency and may create opportunities for cost savings.2 In an environment centered around value-driven health care and consistently diminishing reimbursements year-over-year, it is imperative to deliver exceptional patient care regarding quality, timeliness, and cost effectiveness.3,4 Furthermore, radiographic templating with the use of a 25.4-millimeter metallic sphere calibrating marker and CT templating methods of shoulder arthroplasty implants have demonstrated variable accuracy, with prediction within one size ranging from 44% to 95%.5,6

There currently remains a lack of predictive models for TSA and RSA implant sizes. One study by Baumgarten et al evaluated the accuracy of three-dimensional CT scan software in combination with surgeon's judgment in predicting prosthetic utilization and concluded that implant sizes could be predicted with low accuracy (20% to 28%) in determining all prostheses used for surgery.7 Several previous studies have used demographic data to predict implant sizing for total hip and knee arthroplasty, but to the knowledge of the authors, no such study exists for predicting implant sizing for anatomic or reverse TSA.8-11

Unique design considerations in anatomic and reverse total shoulder prosthetic systems, such as onlay versus inlay humeral implants and adjustable neck-shaft angles, versions, and eccentric tapers, contribute to the difficulty in predicting implant sizing.12 TSA and RSA implants also lack a standard measurement convention across designs and across manufacturers, which limits the ability to quantify and predict implant sizing using a singular equation. Therefore, predictive models are often applied to a specific implant and may lose validity when applied more broadly.

The purpose of this study was to determine whether glenoid and humeral implant sizes for index TSA and RSA can be predicted based on patient demographic data alone. Second, this study assesses the difference between machine learning algorithms and standard linear statistical methods in predicting implant sizing for TSA and RSA.

Methods

After obtaining approval from the institutional review board, a consecutive series of 578 patients from a single institution were retrospectively identified within the period from January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2020, using current procedural terminology code 23472 to have undergone primary anatomic TSA or RSA. Analysis conducted was limited to the index surgery performed for each patient, eliminating those having received similar procedures for their contralateral shoulder. Cases involving revision TSA or revision RSA were excluded, as well as any subjects having cemented implants. This left 518 for analysis. A total of 107 subjects had electronic health records with partially missing implant data. In these cases, predictive analysis was conducted on available data, and these subjects were excluded from predictive analysis where implant information was missing. As an example, if a subject did not have a record of their polyethylene thickness but had a record of all other features, a predictive model was estimated for all implant features except their polyethylene thickness.

Demographic variables were collected for all patients by retrospective chart review including age, height, weight, sex, body mass index, self-reported race, and self-reported ethnicity. Implant characteristics were obtained by chart review including manufacturer, humerus stem size in millimeters (mm), and stem neck-shaft angle (degrees) for both TSA and RSA. For TSA only, head diameter (mm), glenoid implant height (mm), and anterior-posterior (AP) distance (mm) were obtained. For RSA only, baseplate diameter (mm), glenosphere diameter (mm), and polyethylene thickness (mm) were obtained. For polyethylene thickness and glenoid implant, ordinal sizes were also recorded, for example, S for small, M for medium, L for large, and XL for extra large.

This study involved implants from 12 noncemented press-fit systems: Arthrex Univers Apex, DJO Altivate, DJO Encore, Exactech Equinoxe, Exactech Equinoxe Reverse, Tornier Aequalis, Tornier Flex, Tornier Perform, Zimmer Bigliani/Flatow Shoulder System, Zimmer Biomet Comprehensive, Zimmer Biomet Comprehensive Reverse, and Zimmer Biomet Trabecular Metal Reverse (Table 1). Among these implants, there were 38 humerus size-design combinations and 19 glenoid size-design combinations in the study series.

Reporting on the machine learning models in this study is in accordance with the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) guidelines and the Guidelines for Developing and Reporting Machine Learning Models in Biomedical Research and recent call for improved methodological reporting and model presentation.13-15

Statistical Analysis and Machine Learning Model Development

A multivariate generalized linear regression was first conducted with stepwise backward selection criteria to eliminate any variables not identified as statistically predictive of the implant size (P > 0.10). Given the retrospective nature of this model development, no blinding was necessary. Next, the variables identified as statistically predictive of the respective implant feature were subsequently compared with 26 different machine learning models. These models included multivariate linear regression, multivariate linear regression with interactions, robust linear regression, stepwise linear regression, fine tree, medium tree, coarse tree, linear support vector machine (SVM), quadratic SVM, cubic SVM, fine Gaussian SVM, medium Gaussian SVM, coarse Gaussian SVM, boosted trees, bagged trees, squared exponential Gaussian process regression (GPR), matern 5/2 GPR, exponential GPR, rational quadratic GPR, neural network with 1 fully connected layer and a layer size of 10, medium neural network, wide neural network, bilayered neural network, trilayered neural network, SVM kernel, and least squares regression kernel. 15% of the available data (78 subjects) were selected at random to be used for test data, leaving 70% (363) for model training and 15% (77) for model validation. The same subjects were used for training, validation, and testing in all model development stages. Using the 15% randomly selected data for the test group, a paired t-test was used to compare the highest performing machine learning model with a multivariate linear regression model. The highest performing machine learning model was calculated using the smallest root mean square error achieved from the validation data from the implant size.

No assessment was conducted on the quality of the chosen implant size, in accordance with the standard in total joint arthroplasty literature.8-11 However, a post hoc analysis was conducted to assess any relationship between predicted size and complications requiring surgery including aseptic loosening, fracture, dislocation, infection, or continued pain. Postoperative complications were identified as any atraumatic procedure performed on the same surgical extremity. Patients were followed with an average follow-up of 2 years. Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS (IBM, Armonk) with a P-value of < 0.05 being considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the predictive value of readily available patient characteristics, namely their demographic variables, in predicting TSA implant sizes. The results of this study demonstrated that linear models may predict TSA implant sizes within 2 mm of the humerus stem size 75.3% of the time, head diameter 82.1%, head height 82.1%, and glenosphere diameter in reverse TSA 77.6% of the time. Linear models may predict TSA glenoid implant sizes accurately 68.2% and RSA polyethylene thickness 76.6% of the time and within one size 100% and 95.7% of the time, respectively.

It is well established that preoperative imaging offers essential information, not only for diagnosis but also for anticipation of surgical needs. Knowledge of a patient's unique anatomy or pathologic changes can improve surgical decision making and facilitate a streamlined surgery. However, preoperative radiographic templating, often a critical portion of total hip and knee arthroplasty, is not routinely relied on to determine implant positioning and size for anatomic TSA and RSA. One study used calibrated Grashey view radiographs to template Tornier Aequalis humeral implants and demonstrated low interobserver reliability and variable accuracy of preoperative templating for TSA implants.6 The authors also remarked that templating from an AP radiograph alone is not helpful because the sagittal plane of the humerus is typically narrower and can greatly affect implant size used in surgery. Multiple studies have also assessed the viability of CT and 3-dimensional reconstructions of the humerus and scapula for templating, with accuracy ranging from 44% to 99% in predicting within one size.5,16 The authors found that by using magnified templates provided from a manufacturer, there was substantial intraobserver reliability for head and stem size selection.5 Meanwhile, there has been a consistent push to maximize value in health care over the past several years, and several studies have examined modes for more efficient and affordable delivery of patient care.4,17

Demographic data reliably predicted implant sizing of both total knee and total hip arthroplasty, but the same for TSA has not yet been described.8-11 The implications of our findings not only are clinically relevant but also have notable potential effect on patient outcomes. The ability to reliably predict implant sizing for anatomic TSA and RSA with readily available demographic data may contribute to reducing the cost of care. The ability to predict implant sizes used intraoperatively with 95% to 100% accuracy may substantially reduce the required size of the trays, implant storage space, and in-house inventory needs. In high-volume and tertiary care centers, there is notable potential for improved value of care and increased operating room efficiency. Implant size predictions are relevant for nontertiary centers that may not have institutional trays and rely heavily on loaner trays. In addition to cost savings, reducing tray sizes has been associated with decreasing the risk of prosthetic joint infection.18

Although predicting TSA and RSA implant sizes from demographic data shows promise for clinical use, it is not meant to replace CT-guided templating. Instead, it serves to validate and augment standard templating efficiently and cost effectively, without additional radiation exposure. Patients with unique glenohumeral morphology and pathology require careful templating. Combining demographic predictions with CT-guided templating is advised. For those with distinct bone morphology or existing implants, accuracy may decrease with demographic predictions.

This study pioneers the use of demographic variables for TSA and RSA implant sizing but does not recommend relying solely on them. Demographic and CT-guided methods may overlook patient-specific factors, which often comprise a vital part of clinical decision making. These models offer a basic way to accommodate bony anomalies, validate templated sizes, and streamline shoulder arthroplasty procedures. The authors suggest that these models can improve inventory prediction accuracy and enhance patient outcomes.

This study has its limitations. First, the analysis used implants from only 12 noncemented press-fit implant systems. The application of the results of this study to more stems outside those used in this study requires additional investigation, including any cemented implants. The findings of this study are further limited by missing data, which may introduce bias and limit conclusive findings. We used statistical techniques to address this, but these methods are not a perfect substitute for a complete data set. In addition, per our post hoc analysis, our sample size was determined to be insufficient to detect a statistically significant difference in predictive capability between machine learning algorithms and linear regression models.

When comparing linear models with several machine learning models, there was no statistical difference. This conclusion likely stems from several aspects. Particularly, this study was limited to 518 subjects for data analysis. After randomly allocating into training, validation, and test groups, this left 78 for statistical comparison of standard statistical models with machine learning models. A post hoc analysis revealed the required sample size to identify a difference between machine learning models and standard linear regression statistics to be 635 subjects. Future studies of a larger sample size may support machine learning models to be of greater accuracy. Furthermore, while the discussion of the optimization techniques for each machine learning model is outside the scope of this article, the authors recognize there is a large degree of nuanced detail in each machine learning model that, when appropriately handled, may perform better than that observed in this study.

For future studies, researchers planning to develop a machine learning model should plan to have adequate sample size to ensure a sufficiently powered analysis. Future studies may also explore the specific subset of patients who deviate widely from predictions, assess complication rates, and perhaps use the deviation data to inform preoperative surgeon-patient consultation and to manage postoperative expectations.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrated that linear models predict TSA implant sizes with reliable accuracy. When comparing linear models with several machine learning models, there was no statistical difference, although this result may be due to a lack of power in this study. The use of demographic data models as a complementary method to confirm ideal implant sizing preoperatively, in addition to CT-guided templating, may prove beneficial to a comprehensive preoperative planning approach.
