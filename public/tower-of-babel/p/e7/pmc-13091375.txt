Effect of thermosonication on quality and shelf-life of sweet cherry following cold storage
Authors: Linghua Zuo, Yuzhuo Wen, Hongyue Li, Chenyun Zhang, Fuheng Hao, Jiaxuan Li, Yue Leng, Dayong Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sweet cherries are highly susceptible to quality deterioration during postharvest storage, resulting in significant economic losses. In this study, thermosonication (TS), an integrated preservation technique combining ultrasound (40 kHz, 480 W) with moderate heat, was used to enhance the storability of sweet cherries. The treatment at 40 °C for 15 min was identified as the most effective protocol. After 12 days of storage at 4 °C and 90% relative humidity, this treatment reduced the decay rate by 7.99% and weight loss by 0.34% compared to the control, while maintaining firmness at 1.14 times that of the control. TS significantly modulated enzymatic activities by reducing peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase activities by 35.41% and 33.32%, respectively, while increasing catalase activity by 33.69% relative to the control group. Cellular analysis confirmed the treatment induced only minimal damage to membrane integrity and cellular structure. Non-targeted metabolomics revealed that TS significantly (VIP > 1, P < 0.05) up-regulated 286 metabolites, including key flavonoids and isoflavonoids such as sakuranetin and diosmetin-7-O-β-D-glucoside. RT-qPCR confirmed the up-regulation of key structural genes (PaPAL, Pa4CL, PaCHS, and PaCAD) in the phenylpropanoid-flavonoid pathway. These changes enriched flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, elucidating the molecular mechanism of TS in enhancing antioxidant capacity. The results confirm TS as a scalable and eco-friendly technology for reducing postharvest losses of sweet cherries.

Introduction

Sweet cherries with brightly red color and novel taste are one of the most significant temperate fruits in the world market. They are rich in vitamin C, polyphenolics and other nutrients (Chezanoglou et al., 2024; Merkytė et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Therefore, sweet cherries are highly appreciated by customers owing to their organoleptic and nutritional quality (Blando & Oomah, 2019) and higher economic attractiveness. Global sweet cherry production reached 2.7 million tons in 2021 and has increased substantially by years (Osterman et al., 2024). However, sweet cherries are highly perishable, with postharvest losses estimated to reach up to 50% due to fungal decay and physiological deterioration during the supply chain (Mujtaba et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025). Given the high market value of sweet cherries, such a high loss rate implies that approximately 1.35 million tons of fruit could be wasted annually, representing a multi-billion-dollar economic loss to global industry. Therefore, delaying the quality deterioration of sweet cherries has become a crucial research focus.

Among different techniques, pre-cooling, modified atmosphere packaging, radiation and pulsed electric field have been commonly applied to lengthen the postharvest shelf life of sweet cherries (Mujtaba et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). The above methods each have advantages and disadvantages. For pre-cooling, fluid ice pre-cooling causes rotting and wilting on fruits, and both electrolyzed water and fluid ice pre-cooling make fruits more susceptible to microbial spoilage during storage (Tao et al., 2024). For modified atmosphere packaging, improving packaging efficiency is still a technical challenge (Li et al., 2024). Although radiation and pulsed electric field offer advantages such as speed and efficiency, they also need further development given cost and operability (Abbasi et al., 2021). Emerging electric field-based technologies, such as induced electric field treatment, have also demonstrated efficacy in inactivating heat-resistant bacterial spores in various food matrices (Xue et al., 2025), highlighting the ongoing innovation in non-thermal preservation methods. However, despite such technological advancements, the widespread industrial adoption of these techniques is often hindered by high energy consumption and discontinuous batch processing modes. In contrast, TS has emerged as a cost-effective and scalable alternative. Unlike other non-thermal methods, TS utilizes simple equipment and operates at moderate temperatures, offering a more energy-efficient and practical solution for postharvest preservation. Thus, applying TS to maintain the quality of sweet cherries warrants further exploration.

TS is an emerging and sustainable integrated technology that combines ultrasound with moderate heat in food processing applications (Ferreira et al., 2023; Moura et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). In this context, integrated implies a synergistic interaction where thermal and cavitation effects work simultaneously to mitigate multiple deterioration factors (e.g., endogenous enzymes and physiological decay) more effectively than individual treatments. In recent years, TS treatment has been widely applied to achieve comprehensive quality maintenance, which includes efficiently inactivating endogenous enzymes and delaying postharvest decay (Kalsi et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023). Moreover, TS confers a special advantage in maintaining the nutritional and sensory properties of foods without damaging bioactive components (Hu et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). Current studies focus more on the application of TS technology in fruit and vegetable juices for enzyme inactivation and sterilization. Recently, Nicolau-Lapeña et al. (2022) applied TS to strawberries at 35 or 130 kHz and 20, 50 or 55 °C, and found TS at 50 °C for 5 or 10 min, or at 55 °C for 5 min decreased Listeria innocua population, without causing major changes in strawberry quality. Alexandre et al. (2011) also demonstrated that TS could serve as an effective alternative to thermal blanching for red bell peppers and strawberries, achieving significant microbial inactivation while retaining quality attributes. Pinheiro et al. (2019) found that TS treatment (40 °C, 30 min) promoted a longer storage with consequent maintenance of tomato fruit quality. Although TS has shown promising results in robust fruits like tomatoes (Pinheiro et al., 2019), its direct application to delicate fresh produce remains largely unexplored and highly challenging. Sweet cherries present a unique physiological matrix. They are extremely perishable with a notably shorter storage life, yet they represent a massive global production with exceptionally high economic value (Blando & Oomah, 2019; Osterman et al., 2024). Their postharvest loss rate during the supply chain can reach up to 50% (Mujtaba et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025). Structurally, sweet cherries have relatively firm flesh covered by a highly fragile cuticle, leading to uncertainty regarding whether the intense acoustic cavitation and shear forces typical of TS treatments would cause irreversible microscopic cracking and surface pitting on the delicate cherry skin (Jiang et al., 2020). Applying TS to sweet cherries thus requires exploring a precise and unverified operational balance. The treatment intensity must be sufficient to penetrate the stem cavity and inhibit browning enzymes, yet mild enough to preserve epidermal integrity. The underlying cellular and metabolic responses of sweet cherries to such physical treatments remain unelucidated. Therefore, this study investigates how sweet cherries respond to TS treatment at both the cellular and metabolic levels.

Therefore, this study focuses on “Meizao” cherries, given the constraints imposed by postharvest quality deterioration of cherries during cold storage on industrial development, the limitations of the existing preservation technologies, and the potential of TS in fruit and vegetable preservation. A TS treatment system with different parameter combinations was constructed, including ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, power of 480 W, temperature of 40/50 °C, and duration of 10/15/25 min. Sensory quality indices (firmness, color, decay rate, etc.) and physiological and biochemical indices (total soluble solids, ascorbic acid, reducing sugars, and activities of peroxidase (POD), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), catalase (CAT), etc.) of cherries were measured during storage to clarify the regulatory effect of TS on the cold storage quality of cherries. With the aid of electrical impedance spectroscopy (combined with an equivalent circuit model) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the mechanism by which TS treatment affected cherry cell membrane permeability, cell wall integrity, and microstructure was revealed at the cellular level. Untargeted metabolomics (based on UPLC-MS) was employed to screen differential metabolites and analyze their variation patterns in key pathways such as flavonoid biosynthesis. Gene expression analysis was conducted using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) to validate the expression of key genes involved in these metabolic processes. Ultimately, this study was aimed to provide theoretical support and technical references for applying TS in postharvest preservation of cherries, contributing to reducing postharvest losses and enhancing product added value in the cherry industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sweet cherry “Meizao” fruits at the commercial maturity stage were harvested from an experimental orchard in Yantai, Shandong Province, China. The cherries were promptly transported to the laboratory at Jilin Agricultural University via cold chain logistics within 24 h of harvest. Only fruits exhibiting uniform color and size, and free from rot, cracking and mechanical damage were selected for the experiments.

TS processing

TS was performed using an ultrasonic bath (XM-P30H, Xiaomei, Suzhou, China) operating at 40 kHz and 480 W. The bath was filled with 6 L of distilled water. Based on preliminary trials and established thresholds (Nicolau-Lapeña et al., 2022; Pinheiro et al., 2019), temperatures were set at 40 and 50 °C for 10, 15, and 25 min to prevent thermal injury. For each batch, roughly 250 g of cherries were vacuum sealed in polyethylene bags and submerged in the bath. The post treatment fruit temperature was approximately 2 °C lower than the water. All samples were then immediately chilled in ice water, dried, and stored at 4 °C and 90% relative humidity for a 12 day assessment.

Physicochemical analysis

Weight loss

The weights of the cherries were assessed at various intervals (days 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12), and weight loss was calculated as a percentage of the initial weight (Dong & Wang, 2018).

Rotting rate

The decay rate of sweet cherries was assessed and scored according to the decay area of the fruit surface as follows: 0 (no decay), 1 (decay area < 1/3), 2 (1/3 ≤ decay area ≤ 2/3), and 3 (decay area > 2/3 of the surface). The results were expressed as the percentage distribution of each decay level (Colgecen & Aday, 2015).

Firmness

The hardness of cherries during storage was measured using a texture analyzer (TA. XT plus, Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, England) equipped with a P/2 probe. The test was performed with pre-test, test, and post-test speeds of 1.00 mm/s, and a penetration distance of 7 mm (Colgecen & Aday, 2015).

Color evaluation

The colors of the cherry samples were measured using a chroma meter (CM-2300D, Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan) at three random points on the equatorial region of each fruit. Colors were characterized by lightness (L*), chroma (C*), and hue angle (h°). The calculation formulas for C* and H° are as follows (Mujtaba et al., 2023):(1)C∗=a∗2+b∗2(2)h°=tan−1b∗/a∗

Total soluble solid (TSS) and pH analysis

Sweet cherries were mechanically disrupted using a mortar to facilitate extraction, and the homogenates were centrifuged at 4000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected to determine the TSS content using a digital refractometer (TDJ-32, Jingcheng Huatai, Beijing, China), and the results were expressed as a percentage (Mujtaba et al., 2023).

The pH of the supernatant was measured using a pH meter (STARTER-2C, Ohaus, Shanghai, China).

Ascorbic acid content

The ascorbic acid content in sweet cherries was determined using a spectrophotometer (UV-2002, Puxi, Beijing, China). Briefly, 5.0 g of a sweet cherry sample was homogenized with 5 mL of 1% HCl solution in an ice-cooled mortar. The mixture was transferred into a 25 mL volumetric flask and brought to volume with distilled water. The sample was then centrifuged at 5000×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected as a test solution. A 5 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 10 mL of 1% HCl in a 25 mL volumetric flask and diluted to volume with distilled water. The absorbance at 287 nm was measured. The ascorbic acid content was calculated as follows (Tsaniklidis et al., 2014):(3)Ascorbic acid content=c×Vtotal×Vtest×100V1×Wtotal×1000where c is the concentration obtained from the standard curve (μg/mL), Vtotal is the total post-homogenization volume (mL), Vtest is the total test sample volume (mL), V1 is the aliquot volume used for measurement (mL), and Wtotal is the total sample mass (g).

Reducing sugar content

The reducing sugar content in sweet cherries was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (Jo et al., 2008). A 1.0 g sample of sweet cherries was homogenized with distilled water in a mortar and transferred to a 10 mL test tube, which was then brought to volume. The tube was placed in a water bath at 80 °C for 30 min, then cooled and filtered. The residue was rinsed with 20 mL of distilled water, and the rinse solution was combined with the filtrate. The combined filtrate was diluted to volume in a 100 mL volumetric flask. Then 2.0 mL of the extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of a DNS reagent in a 10 mL test tube. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 5 min and then cooled. Absorbance at 540 nm was measured using the UV-2002 spectrophotometer.

Antioxidants analysis

Total phenolic content

The total phenolic content in sweet cherries was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. In brief, 2.0 g of a sweet cherry sample was homogenized with 6 mL of 80% methanol. The mixture was ultrasonicated in an ice bath for 30 min and centrifuged at 5000×g and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was diluted fivefold with deionized water. Then 100 μL of the extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After the mixture was vortexed for 5 min, 0.35 mL of a sodium carbonate solution (75 g·L−1) was added. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Absorbance at 765 nm was measured using the UV-2002 spectrophotometer (Mujtaba et al., 2023).

POD and PPO activities

The POD activity in sweet cherry tissues was assayed using a commercial kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) with modifications. Specifically, tissues were homogenized in an ice-cold saline solution at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3500×g and 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The reaction mixture was then prepared by combining the supernatant with the specified reagents according to the kit protocol. After incubation in a water bath at 37 °C for 30 min, the reaction was terminated by adding the termination reagent. The solution was centrifuged at 3500×g for 10 min, and the absorbance of 200 μL of the supernatant was measured at 420 nm (Tao et al., 2024).

The PPO activity was determined using a plant PPO assay kit (Yakeyan, Wuhan, China) by the microscopic method. Sweet cherry tissues were homogenized in an ice-cold extraction buffer at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio. The homogenate was centrifuged at 8000×g and 4 °C for 10 min to obtain crude enzyme extract. The reaction system was prepared by mixing the crude extract with the reagents as per the kit instructions. After incubation at 25 °C for 10 min, the reaction was stopped by boiling the mixture for 10 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 5000×g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant (200 μL) was measured at 410 nm (Tao et al., 2024).

CAT activity

The CAT activity in sweet cherry tissues was determined using a commercial assay kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, tissues were homogenized in an ice-cold saline solution at a 1:5 (w/v) ratio. The homogenate was centrifuged at 2500×g and 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. A reaction mixture was prepared by combining the supernatant with the kit reagents. After immediate vortexing, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 min. The reaction was terminated by adding ammonium molybdate, and absorbance at 405 nm was measured using a 200 μL aliquot of the mixture (Tao et al., 2024).

Bio-impedance measurement

Electrical impedance measurement system

The electrical properties of sweet cherry fruits under different treatment conditions were characterized via impedance spectroscopy using a testing fixture equipped with custom stainless steel needle electrodes. Measurements were performed across a frequency range of 10 Hz–2 MHz.

SEM

The microstructures of the samples were observed using an SEM device (SU8010, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The samples were mounted on silicon wafers with conductive carbon adhesive, subjected to critical-point drying using CO2, and finally sputter-coated with a gold layer for 60 s under vacuum (Xie et al., 2024).

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Plant materials and storage treatment

Sweet cherries were divided into three groups. Group 1 served as the control group and was analyzed immediately after selection (control-0d). Group 2 underwent TS treatment (40 °C for 15 min) following days of storage (TS-12d). Group 3 (control-12d) was stored for 12 days prior to analysis.

Sample extraction

The cherry samples were ground to powder in liquid nitrogen, and 25 mg of portions were transferred to cryogenic centrifuge tubes. An extraction solvent (1000 μL; methanol: acetonitrile: water, 2:2:1, v/v) containing isotope-labeled internal standards and homogenizing beads was added to each tube. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, homogenized through three cycles (35 Hz, 4 min each), and sonicated for 5 min in an ice-water bath.

The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min following incubation at −40 °C for 1 h. A 500 μL aliquot of the resulting supernatant was then recentrifuged under identical conditions, membrane-filtered, and transferred to injection vials for subsequent analysis. For quality control, a pooled sample was prepared by combining equal volumes of the supernatant from each individual sample (Yuan et al., 2023).

Liquid chromatograph mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm2, 2.6 μm). An aliquot (2 μL) was injected into the LC-MS system. Separation was achieved using a mobile phase consisting of 0.01% acetic acid in water (v/v; mobile phase A) and a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of isopropanol and acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The autosampler was maintained at 4 °C.

Mass spectrometry was performed using an ultra-high performance liquid chromatograph (UHPLC) (Vanquish, Thermo, Massachusetts, USA). The sheath gas flow was maintained at 50 arb, the auxiliary gas flow at 15 arb, and the capillary temperature at 320 °C. Full-scan and MS/MS resolutions were set at 60,000 and 15,000, respectively, and the stepped normalized collision energy was 20, 30 or 40. The spray voltage was maintained at 3.8 kV in the positive ion mode and − 3.4 kV in the negative ion mode (Yuan et al., 2023).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Sweet cherry fruit samples frozen at −80 °C were fully ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using a Column Plant Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed using 700.0 ng of qualified total RNA as a template with the First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Premix Kit (Catalog No. TSK314S, TSINGKE Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, core genes within the phenylpropanoid-flavonoid pathway (PaPAL, Pa4CL, PaCHS) and the key branch gene PaCAD were selected as targets for RT-qPCR analysis. Specific primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0, and sequence information was provided in supplementary Table S1. The RT-qPCR assays were performed using 2 × SG Fast qPCR Master Mix (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) in a LightCycler 480 II Real-Time PCR system (Roche, Switzerland). The amplification procedure was set as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing and extension at 59 °C for 25 s. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene, and the relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Zhang et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA), with post-hoc comparisons performed via LSD and Duncan's multiple range tests. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was indicated by distinct lowercase letters, and all values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least three replicate experiments.

Cherries are soft, juicy, and sugar-rich, and the primary cause of their postharvest decay is microbial infection (Jiang et al., 2020). No decay was observed in any group during the early storage period (Fig. 2B). However, on the 12th day of storage, the decay rates of the two thermal treatment groups and the control group were 6.89%, 8.67% and 10.89%, respectively, while the maximum decay rates of the TS groups were only 4%. At the end of storage, the decay rate of the TS40 °C-15 min group was 7.99% lower than that of the control group.

Firmness is a key index for consumers to evaluate the postharvest quality of fruits and vegetables. For the control group, the firmness of cherries was 382.51 g on day 0 of storage and decreased to 248.23 g after 12 days of storage, representing an overall decrease of 35.10% (Fig. 2C). In the late storage period, the firmness of the thermal treatment group and the control group decreased significantly, and both were lower than that of the TS40 °C-10 min and TS40 °C-15 min groups (27.02% and 25.85% decrease respectively).

Fruit color change is an important parameter reflecting ripeness and a key factor influencing consumer purchasing decisions. Among chromaticity indices, a higher L value indicates brighter color (Fig. 2D), a higher C value indicates higher color saturation (better quality) (Fig. 2E), and a lower H value indicates more severe browning (Fig. 2F). At the end of storage, the L values of the TS groups ranged from 16.87 to 17.55 (Fig. 2D), while the L values of the thermal treatment groups and the control group were 16.11, 16.07 and 15.52, respectively. This result indicates TS treatment slowed down the darkening of cherry brightness to a certain extent. Additionally, the C and H values of the TS groups were consistently significantly higher than those of the thermal treatment groups and the blank control group.

The internal pH of cherries is significantly and positively correlated with the spoilage process, meaning a higher pH corresponds to severer spoilage. The pHs of all groups increased during storage (Fig. 2G): the pHs of the thermal treatment groups and the control group increased from initially 4.49, 4.47 and 4.47 to 4.65, 4.73 and 4.67, respectively. In contrast, the pHs of the TS groups were significantly lower than those of the other three groups throughout the storage period. At the end of storage, the pH of the TS40 °C-15 min group was 4.57.

Nutritional indices

TSS is a key indicator of fruit ripeness and flavor quality. On the 3rd day of storage, the TSS contents of cherries in all groups were relatively high (Fig. 2H): the TSS content was 10.07% in the control group and ranged from 10.23% to 11.57% in the TS groups. No significant difference was observed among all groups in the first 6 days of storage. As storage progressed, the TSS contents of the TS groups surpassed those of the thermal treatment groups and the blank control group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the TSS contents of the TS groups were significantly higher than those of the latter two groups throughout the storage period (P < 0.05). On the 12th day of storage, the TSS content of the control group decreased to 8.07% (a 1.67% decrease compared to day 0), while that of the TS40 °C-15 min group decreased to 8.90% (only a 0.97% decrease compared to day 0).

Ascorbic acid content is a key factor for measuring the nutritional quality of fruits and vegetables, and generally declines with prolonged storage. (Fig. 2I). On the 9th day of storage, a large amount of ascorbic acid in cherries was lost, but the ascorbic acid contents of the TS groups were significantly higher than in the blank control group and the thermal treatment groups. On the 12th day of storage, the ascorbic acid contents of the TS groups remained significantly higher than in the latter two groups. Among these groups, the ascorbic acid content of the TS40 °C-15 min group peaked at 16.50 mg/100 g on day 3, and subsequently decreased to 1.46 mg/100 g on day 12. In contrast, the contents of the thermal treatment groups and the control group at this time were 1.30, 1.27 and 1.42 mg/100 g, respectively.

Reducing sugar content is one of the key parameters for evaluating cherry quality. During storage, a slower respiration rate leads to a slower decline in reducing sugar content (Fig. 3A). In the early storage period, the reducing sugar contents of the TS groups were maintained relatively high. On the 6th day of storage, the reducing sugar contents of all groups maximize: 2.04% in the TS40 °C-15 min group, while 1.86%, 1.87% and 1.62% in the thermal treatment groups and the control group, respectively. Phenolic compounds maintain the color stability of cherries during storage (e.g., delaying browning and reducing pigment degradation) by enhancing antioxidant capacity, thereby indirectly affecting color changes (Mujtaba et al., 2023). On the 6th day of storage, the total phenol contents of the thermal treatment groups and the control group all peaked at 0.75, 0.74 and 0.76 mg/g, respectively (Fig. 3B). On the 9th day of storage, the total phenol contents of the TS groups peaked and were significantly higher than those in the other three groups. This comparison indicates that TS treatment delayed the peak of total phenol content and maintained it at a relatively high level at the end of storage. Among these groups, the total phenol content of the TS40 °C-15 min group at the end of storage was 0.79 mg/g, while that of the control group was 0.61 mg/g.

Fruit enzyme activity indices

Enzyme activity is closely associated with quality changes in agricultural products, and abnormal enzyme activity can disturb the physiological metabolism of agricultural products. PPO and POD are the main enzymes involved in the browning of fruits and vegetables, while CAT is a key endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger in plants, which is indispensable during stress or senescence (Jiang et al., 2020).

During storage, the POD activity of the TS groups was significantly lower than that in the thermal treatment groups and the blank control group (Fig. 3C). At the end of storage, the POD activity of the TS40 °C-15 min group was 14.75%, 14.09% and 35.41% lower than in the other three groups, respectively. TS treatment delayed the increase in POD activity and maintained a relatively stable trend overall.

The PPO activity of cherries is generally increased as storage time progressed (Fig. 3D). On the 12th day of storage, the PPO activity of the TS40 °C-15 min group was 52.28 U/g, which was significantly lower than that in the thermal treatment groups and the control group (54.20, 64.08 and 78.40 U/g respectively) (P < 0.05). The PPO activity of the TS groups was significantly lower than that in the thermal treatment groups and the blank control group during the entire storage period.

The CAT activity of cherries generally increased as storage time progressed (Fig. 3E). At the end of storage, the CAT activity of the TS groups ranged from 41.57 to 57.10 U/g, while that of the thermal treatment groups and the control group was 26.40, 32.30 and 37.86 U/g, respectively. Additionally, the CAT activity of TS groups was mostly significantly higher than that in the thermal treatment groups and the blank control group during storage, demonstrating the efficacy of TS treatment in sustaining high antioxidant enzyme activity throughout the storage period.

Correlation heatmap analysis of quality indices

Cluster heatmap analysis was performed on the quality indices of cherries at 0 and 12 days of storage (Fig. 4A–D). As shown in the heatmaps, significant changes occurred in all quality-related indices of cherries in the control group after 12 days of storage. With prolonged storage, parameters including firmness, TSS, ascorbic acid, reducing sugar, and total phenol content decreased. After TS treatment, the decline in firmness was slowed, the color of cherries was successfully maintained, and the losses of TSS, ascorbic acid, reducing sugar, and total phenol content were mitigated, allowing the fruits to retain good quality after 12 days of storage. Meanwhile, TS treatment reduced the activity of POD and PPO and maintained the activity of CAT, enabling TS-treated cherries to exhibit strong antioxidant capacity. Discussion

Regulatory effect of TS on postharvest sensory and physicochemical quality of cherries

The main issues associated with postharvest quality deterioration of cherries are water loss, microbial infection, and enzymatic browning. TS treatment regulates quality through the synergy of multiple mechanisms. In terms of reducing weight loss rate (Fig. 2A), this difference was not significant during the first 6 days of storage. After 9 days of storage, the TS groups significantly inhibited water loss. The superior water retention observed in the treated fruit is attributed to TS, wherein the cavitation effect generates intense shockwaves and shear forces that disrupt the cell membranes of spoilage microorganisms, significantly reducing the microbial load. Simultaneously, the synergistic mild thermal treatment helps stabilize the tissue structure and minimizes intercellular spaces, thereby impeding moisture migration. These findings align with the observations of Roohi et al. (2025) and Pinheiro et al. (2019), who reported that TS treatment significantly reduced weight loss in fresh tomatoes and other fruit species, respectively, thereby confirming its broad efficacy for mitigating water loss in sweet cherries.

For decay rate (Fig. 2B), ultrasound induces cavitation effects through high-frequency sound waves, generating localized high temperature combined with high pressure and strong shear force. These effects directly damage the cell walls and membranes of microorganisms, leading to the leakage of cellular contents and even cell lysis and death (Javed et al., 2025). Similar electroporation-based microbial inactivation mechanisms have been reported in induced electric field treatments, which also disrupt cellular integrity and enhance germicidal efficacy in liquid foods (Zhang, Jin, et al., 2025; Zhang, Xu, et al., 2025). Bal and Torçuk (2020) found that the decay rate of cherries treated with ultrasound (10.8%) was lower than that of the control group (24.4%), which further confirms that ultrasound combined with thermal treatment significantly slowed down the increase in decay rate of cherry fruits. The “ultrasound + thermal treatment” combination in this study further highlights the synergistic advantage in antisepsis.

The maintenance of firmness is directly related to enzyme activity regulation, and the mechanism by which TS treatment affects fruit firmness is associated with the activities of polygalacturonase (PG) and pectin methylesterase (PME), both of which are linked to fruit softening (Chaudhary et al., 2025). TS treatment may alter the spatial structures of enzyme proteins, leading to changes in the conformation of the active center. In turn, this change inhibits enzyme activity, reduces pectin degradation, and maintains the integrity of cell walls and fruit firmness (Rathnakumar et al., 2023) The study by Zhang et al. (2024) demonstrated that TS treatment (20 + 40 + 60 kHz, 45 °C, 10 min) notably maintained the postharvest firmness of fresh-cut lotus roots. After 8 days of storage, the firmness of the treated samples was 25.80% higher than that of the control group, indicating that the treatment effectively suppressed the decline in hardness. Our results align with this finding, indicating that TS treatment effectively inhibits the decrease in cherry firmness (Fig. 2C).

In terms of colors, a study by Yildiz and Aadil (2022) demonstrated that high-intensity ultrasound treatment applied to fresh-cut mango for 15 min significantly delayed the decrease in lightness (L⁎ value) and the increase in total color difference (ΔE⁎) during 14 days of storage. Similarly, Li et al. (2025) reported that TS treatment markedly reduced color deterioration in tomato juice. The TS juice exhibited a notably low total color difference (ΔE) of 1.89 and a browning index of 0.082, indicating effective inhibition of pigment oxidation and degradation. These findings are consistent with our results, indicating that TS treatment plays an important role in maintaining the peel color of cherries (Fig. 2D–F). Specifically, the higher L*, C*, and h° values in the TS group indicate the retention of the bright red color characteristic of fresh fruit, whereas the control group exhibited senescent darkening. This is a typical postharvest phenomenon in sweet cherries where L* values reduce due to over-ripening and enzymatic browning (Quero-García et al., 2017). Our findings are in line with previous reports that ultrasound effectively delays this process in cherries (Bal & Torçuk, 2020). Such metabolic regulation is known to enhance the stability of anthocyanins and red pigments through antioxidant protection and co-pigmentation effects (Bal & Torçuk, 2020; Pinheiro et al., 2019). Therefore, the TS-maintained color metrics reflect the effective inhibition of senescence and browning rather than physical damage to the epidermis or pigment degradation.

Regarding the ability of TS treatment to maintain low pH (Fig. 2G). The study by Taiye Mustapha et al. (2020) demonstrated that the combined application of dual-frequency ultrasound (20/40 kHz) and low-concentration aqueous ozone (0.85 ± 0.2 mg/L) effectively delayed the increase in pH in cherry tomatoes. This phenomenon can be attributed to the cavitation effects induced by ultrasound, which enhance the mass transfer efficiency of ozone to the fruit surface. The treatment likely also suppressed the respiratory activity of the fruit, reducing the consumption of organic acids, while also inhibiting microbial proliferation, thereby preventing abnormal pH elevation. A lower pH environment further inhibits microbial proliferation (Jiang et al., 2020), forming a synergistic effect of physical antisepsis combined with chemical environment regulation. TS also presents an eco-friendly alternative to chemical fungicides. By utilizing physical mechanisms such as heat and cavitation rather than synthetic preservatives, TS avoids chemical residues while operating at lower temperatures than conventional thermal processing, thereby improving energy efficiency and reducing the environmental footprint of postharvest processing (Ferreira et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2020).

From an industrial perspective, the improvements observed in this study hold significant economic value. Although a reduction of 7.99% in the decay rate appears numerically modest, for high-value commodities like sweet cherries, this substantially reduces supply chain losses and prevents the cascading rejection of batches by retailers due to strict quality thresholds. Similarly, while the reduction of 0.34% in weight loss seems minor, it is critical for maintaining stem turgor. Since stem browning is the first visual sign of senescence perceivable by consumers, this slight moisture retention is decisive in maintaining the fruit's premium marketability and price point.

Retention mechanism of TS treatment on cherry nutrients

Postharvest nutrient loss in cherries is mainly caused by respiratory metabolism consumption and oxidative degradation. TS treatment achieves nutrient retention through two pathways: inhibition of respiratory metabolism and enhancement of antioxidation. For TSS (Fig. 2H) and reducing sugars (Fig. 3A), ultrasound treatment can inhibit the respiratory rate of fruits, thereby reducing the consumption of organic substrates (Mustapha & Zhou, 2021). Similarly, Pinheiro et al. (2019) found that TS treatment delayed the ripening process by inhibiting key enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism. Specifically, in sweet cherries, Bal and Torçuk (2020) confirmed that ultrasound treatment effectively maintained TSS and delayed senescence by suppressing respiration intensity and metabolic activity. These results are consistent with the higher TSS and reducing sugar retention rates observed in our TS groups, indicating that the inhibition of respiration and the delayed conversion of complex carbohydrates are the core mechanisms for nutrient preservation.

The retention of ascorbic acid depends on the synergy of antisepsis and antioxidation (Fig. 2I): TS treatment reduced the microbial consumption of vitamin C (VC) by microorganisms by lowering the decay rate, while maintaining high CAT activity (to scavenge ROS) to reduce VC oxidation. Similarly, the study by Yıldız and Yildiz (2025) demonstrated that high-intensity ultrasound (HIU) treatment significantly maintained the ascorbic acid content in strawberry fruit during storage. Alexandre et al. (2012) also found that ultrasound treatment maintained high anthocyanin and ascorbic acid contents in strawberries. These results suggest that TS serves as a robust method for minimizing ascorbic acid degradation, thereby underscoring its advantage in micronutrient protection.

The change in total phenol content (Fig. 3B) reflects the “bidirectional regulatory” effect of TS treatment. On one hand, the cavitation effect of ultrasound causes cell wall rupture, releasing phenols stored in cells (Zhang et al., 2024). On the other hand, the abiotic stress induced by ultrasound activates the phenylpropanoid pathway (Hong et al., 2025), promoting the de novo synthesis of phenols. Bal and Torçuk (2020) found that at the end of storage, the total phenol content of cherries treated with ultrasound was higher than that of the control group (116.3 vs. 109 mg/100 g). These reports align with our results, indicating that the TS groups can significantly preserve the total phenol content in cherry fruits. All these findings support this regulatory pathway. Notably, the increase in total phenol content not only improves the nutritional value of cherries but also delays color deterioration by enhancing antioxidant capacity (Mujtaba et al., 2023), forming a synergistic effect of nutrient retention and quality maintenance.

Enzyme activity and cell structure: Microscopic basis for quality regulation by TS treatment

Enzyme activity and cell structure are the microscopic cores of postharvest quality deterioration in cherries. TS treatment maintains quality through precise regulation of both factors. In terms of enzyme activity, POD (Fig. 3C) and PPO (Fig. 3D) are key enzymes for enzymatic browning (Jiang et al., 2020), and their activities were significantly inhibited by TS treatment. This may be because the cavitation effect and thermal effect of ultrasound jointly damaged the structure of the enzyme active center (Rathnakumar et al., 2023). Zhu et al. (2021) found that ultrasound (10 min) combined with 0.02% Portulaca oleracea extract treatment significantly inhibited potato POD activity, thereby delaying the enzymatic browning process. This demonstrates that ultrasound treatment plays a role in reducing POD activity. Ultrasound combined with thermal treatment in pear, apple, and strawberry purees was reported to inhibit PPO activity, prevent fruit and vegetable diseases, and improve fruit and vegetable quality (Silva & Sulaiman, 2022), indicating that TS can maintain cherry quality by inhibiting PPO activity.

The maintenance of CAT activity (Fig. 3E) aligns with prior findings: a study by Wang et al. (2024) demonstrated that treating minimally processed carrots with either 28 kHz, 300 W ultrasound for 5 min alone, or in combination with 1% ascorbic acid under mild heating conditions (50 °C), significantly enhanced CAT activity. The combined treatment of ultrasound and mildly heated ascorbic acid was the most effective, significantly mitigating the decline in enzyme activity during 5 days of storage at 4 °C. This finding is consistent with our results, indicating that ultrasound plays a positive role in regulating antioxidant enzyme activity.

Analysis of the fitting results (Fig. 5A and B) showed that ultrasound-thermal treatment had a certain impact on the integrity of cherry cell walls and cell membrane permeability, though the degree of impact was mild. Among them, the total impedance value of the TS50 °C-15 min group was the smallest, which means that in ultrasound-thermal treatment, excessively high temperature and excessively long treatment time had a more significant impact on cherry tissues.

Changes in cell structure and electrical impedance characteristics further revealed the mildness of the treatment. As indicated by the Cole-Cole plot of cherry tissues (Fig. 5C), ultrasound-thermal treatment had a certain impact on the structure of cell membranes, though the degree of impact was mild. In terms of the capacitance (C) (Fig. 5D): ultrasound-thermal treatment induced certain changes in cell membrane permeability, with the cell membrane being damaged but to a relatively small extent. Rs (Fig. 5E) and Rp (Fig. 5F) respectively indicated that possible damage to cell membranes or cell walls may have caused intracellular fluid to flow outward, resulting in changes in the permeability of cell membranes and cell walls.

SEM images (Fig. 6G and H) showed that in the early storage period, the pulp tissue cells of TS-12d cherries were in good condition with no obvious changes. As storage time progressed, the cell walls underwent changes but remained mild. This result was mutually corroborated by the results of electrical impedance spectroscopy.

Overall, TS treatment only caused slight damage to cell membranes (capacitance remained at the picofarad level) (Fig. 5C) and a small decrease in intracellular and extracellular fluid resistance (Fig. 5E and F). This slight damage did not disrupt the overall function of cells (Fig. 6G and H), but may have promoted the release of stress-resistant metabolites (e.g., total phenols). Guo et al. (2022) found that when carrots were treated with TS, the cell membrane capacitance was the lowest under three-frequency treatment at 70 °C but still remained at the nanofarad level. This finding indicates that TS treatment causes slight and minimal damage to cell membranes. This result aligns with our results, providing microscopic evidence for the safety and practicality of this treatment. Meanwhile, Imaizumi et al. (2017) proposed that extracellular fluid resistance is positively correlated with tissue porosity. This suggests that TS treatment may reduce water loss by expelling a small amount of internal gas to lower porosity, which is consistent with our weight loss data.

The upregulation of flavonoids and isoflavonoids is associated with the increased antioxidant enzyme activity and color retention observed in physicochemical analysis. Specifically, apigenin dimethyl ether, a derivative of apigenin, was significantly accumulated. Apigenin and its derivatives are potent antioxidants that can scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), similar to luteolin and kaempferol which are known for high antioxidant capacity (Liu et al., 2021; Sarker & Oba, 2020). This metabolomic finding supports the enzymatic data in (Fig. 3E), where CAT activity was maintained at high levels in the TS group. By providing a pool of non-enzymatic antioxidants (flavonoids) to complement the enzymatic system (CAT), TS treatment established a combined antioxidant system against oxidative stress.

The specific accumulation of sakuranetin provides a molecular basis for the reduced decay rate. Sakuranetin is a flavonoid phytoalexin known for its potent antifungal activity. Sudheeran et al. (2018) found that red fruits with high anthocyanin and flavonoid contents exhibited stronger tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses. Our results align with this established mechanism, as the significant upregulation of sakuranetin (Table 1) corresponds directly to the phenotypic improvement shown in (Fig. 2B), where TS treatment reduced the decay rate by 7.99%. This suggests that TS treatment potently induced the synthesis of defense compounds, promoting a metabolic shift towards disease resistance.

The biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and flavonoids is also closely tied to color maintenance. (Liu et al., 2023) indicated that these metabolites are critical for the color stability of fruits during storage. This agrees with our colorimetric data (Fig. 2D–F), where TS-treated fruits maintained higher L*, C*, and h° values, avoiding the senescent darkening observed in the control. The metabolic interconversion between these metabolites serves as the basis for maintaining physiological activities. Similar enhancements in bioactive compound extraction and immune activity have been reported in polysaccharides from Alocasia cucullata following freeze-thaw treatment (Gong et al., 2024), further supporting the role of physical treatments in modulating bioactivity in plant-based foods.

The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 8B–D) further clarified the systemic basis for these metabolic shifts. The differential metabolites were significantly enriched in Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and Flavonoid biosynthesis. These pathways play an active role in the fruit's defense against cold stress and pathogen attack. Secondary metabolites, particularly flavonoids and phenylpropanoids, play key roles in regulating fruit quality and resisting adverse stresses such as mechanical damage and extreme temperatures (Jacobo-Velázquez et al., 2015; Kaducová et al., 2019; Takshak & Agrawal, 2014; Thakur et al., 2019; Zaynab et al., 2018). By activating these pathways, TS treatment activated the fruit's defense system.

This stress-response mechanism is consistent with previous findings. For instance, Yang et al. (2023) reported that mechanical damage in apples induced the accumulation of diverse secondary metabolites (e.g., terpenoids, flavonoids, and lignans) as a defense response. Similarly, our results suggest that the cavitation and thermal effects of TS acted as a mild abiotic stress signal, triggering a comparable protective response in cherries. Zhang, Jin, et al. (2025) and Zhang, Xu, et al. (2025) also found that regulating secondary metabolism significantly promoted the accumulation of bioactive components (e.g., phenylpropanoids and flavonoids), thereby enhancing the ability of cherries to resist external stresses. In agreement with these studies, our metabolomic data confirms that TS treatment upregulated key flavonoids (e.g., apigenin dimethyl ether, sakuranetin), which functionally contributed to the maintained firmness and reduced decay observed in (Fig. 2B and C).

The specific enrichment in flavonoid biosynthesis explains the color retention. As plants activate defense systems in response to environmental changes (Kaducová et al., 2019), they simultaneously synthesize pigments and antioxidants. This dual function explains why TS-treated cherries not only resisted spoilage but also maintained better color, reflected in higher L*, C*, and h° values compared to the control.

These observations reveal a dual physicochemical mechanism of TS. Unlike previous studies on climacteric fruits like tomatoes (Pinheiro et al., 2019) or soft fruits like strawberries (Alexandre et al., 2011; Nicolau-Lapeña et al., 2022), where TS primarily serves as a sterilization tool, this study highlights a matrix-specific interaction for sweet cherries. The unique firm flesh and delicate cuticle structure requires a precise balance where TS induces a mild abiotic stress response, rather than acting solely through thermal inactivation. At the physical level, the moderate heat and cavitation effects of ultrasound induce subtle modifications in the electrical properties and structural integrity of the fruit tissue, which were objectively monitored and quantified using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (Fig. 5). This physiological modulation then triggers defense-related metabolic feedback at the chemical level, leading to the significant accumulation of antioxidant and antifungal compounds. This combined regulation markedly delayed senescence, inhibited decay, and maintained the commercial quality of sweet cherries. It should be noted that this study focused on the metabolic comparison at the conclusion of the storage period to highlight the cumulative effects of TS treatment on quality maintenance. While this snapshot approach effectively identified key biomarkers associated with senescence delay, future studies incorporating intermediate time points are recommended to elucidate the dynamic transitions of these metabolic pathways.

Transcriptional mechanism analysis of TS-regulated flavonoid biosynthesis in sweet cherry

To further validate the enrichment results of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway identified by non-targeted metabolomics, targeted verification of the transcriptional levels of key genes involved in the phenylpropanoid-flavonoid biosynthesis pathway was performed in this study using RT-qPCR. As shown in (Fig. 9A–D), TS treatment significantly transcriptionally activated four key genes in this pathway. Among them, PaCHS, the specific rate-limiting gene for flavonoid biosynthesis, exhibited the most prominent up-regulation, which was highly consistent with the existing research conclusion in the field of postharvest preservation of sweet cherry that “CHS is the core rate-limiting gene regulating the biosynthesis of flavonoids in fruits” (Liu et al., 2021).

The change trend of the expression of this gene was completely consistent with the up-regulation pattern of differential flavonoid metabolites and the significant enrichment result of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway identified by non-targeted metabolomics, which directly verified the reliability of the metabolomic data at the transcriptional level (Zhang, Jin, et al., 2025; Zhang, Xu, et al., 2025). The results of Pearson correlation analysis in (Fig. 9E) further confirmed the intrinsic correlation among the transcriptional activation of key genes in the phenylpropanoid-flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, the accumulation of flavonoids, and the maintenance of postharvest storage quality of sweet cherry. Under TS treatment, the expression levels of the key flavonoid biosynthesis genes PaPAL, Pa4CL, PaCHS, and PaCAD showed a moderate to significant negative correlation with decay rate and PPO activity. Among them, PaCHS, the rate-limiting gene for flavonoid biosynthesis, was significantly negatively correlated with PPO activity, Pearson correlation coefficient (r) = −0.71 (P < 0.05). These four genes exhibited a weak negative correlation trend with pH, weight loss, POD activity, and ascorbic acid content, with the overall r for negative correlation ranging from −0.71 to −0.048. In contrast, the expression levels of these aforementioned genes were highly and extremely significantly positively correlated with C* value, r ranging from 0.90 to 0.95 (P < 0.001). For total phenolic content, the expression levels of Pa4CL, PaPAL and PaCAD reached an extremely significant positive correlation, r ranging from 0.80 to 0.82 (P < 0.01), while the expression level of PaCHS showed a significant positive correlation, r = 0.74 (P < 0.05). These four genes presented a weak to moderate positive correlation trend with firmness, CAT activity, TSS, and L* value, with the overall r values for positive correlation ranging from 0.061 to 0.95.

This variation pattern was fully consistent with the underlying mechanism by which TS treatment delays postharvest quality deterioration of cherry fruits, and also matched the inherent physiological change characteristics of sweet cherry during postharvest cold storage. These results clarified that TS treatment could drive the biosynthesis and accumulation of downstream flavonoid antioxidants through transcriptional activation of key genes in the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, ultimately achieving the enhancement of antioxidant capacity, the maintenance of fruit quality integrity of sweet cherry, linking gene transcription to metabolite accumulation and physiological phenotype. At present, relevant studies on ultrasound-assisted preservation of sweet cherry are still focused on effect verification at the physiological phenotype level (Muzaffar et al., 2016). There is no report on the molecular mechanism of TS treatment's regulating the phenylpropanoid-flavonoid biosynthesis pathway in fruits, which fills this research gap in the field.

Conclusions

Ultrasound combined with thermal treatment significantly improved the storage quality of cherries, with the TS40 °C-15 min group demonstrating the optimal effect. Compared to the control group, this combined treatment significantly reduced the fruit decay rate by 7.99% and weight loss rate by 0.34%, maintained firmness at 1.14 times that of the control, preserved nutrients (e.g., total soluble solids and ascorbic acid) and delayed pH increase and color darkening. The treatment enhanced antioxidant capacity by inhibiting the activities of PPO and POD and maintaining the activity of CAT. Microstructural and impedance analyses revealed that the treatment had a mild impact on cell structure, causing only a slight decrease in cell membrane capacitance and intracellular/extracellular fluid resistance as well as slight folds in cell walls. Metabolomic analysis identified a total of 286 significant differential metabolites, which were enriched in pathways such as flavonoid biosynthesis. The results of RT-qPCR further confirmed that TS treatment significantly up-regulated the transcription levels of four key genes (PaPAL, Pa4CL, PaCHS, and PaCAD) in the phenylpropane-flavonoid biosynthesis pathway of sweet cherry fruits, among which the flavonoid biosynthesis rate-limiting gene PaCHS showed the most significant up-regulation. Pearson correlation analysis further verifies that the up-regulated expression of these genes was significantly correlated with flavonoid accumulation, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and maintained postharvest quality of cherry fruits. This finding elucidated the core molecular mechanism of TS treatment in promoting flavonoid accumulation and enhancing fruit antioxidant capacity at the transcriptional level. This treatment increased the contents of bioactive components (e.g., flavonoids and flavonoid-like compounds), improved the antioxidant capacity of cherry fruits, and thereby enhanced the ability of cherries to resist external stresses. In conclusion, ultrasound combined with thermal treatment safely and effectively extends the shelf life of cherries by gently regulating cell structure and transcriptional-metabolic pathways, providing a new approach for postharvest preservation of cherries. To promote the practical application of this technology, future research should focus on scaling up the technology for industrial applications, optimizing energy consumption for large-scale operations, and investigating consumer acceptance of TS-treated fruits.
