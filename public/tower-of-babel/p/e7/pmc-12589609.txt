Electrical impedance phase variation in relation to articulation manner
Authors: Seong Tak Woo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate variations in electrical impedance phase angle due to tongue-palate contact strength induced by different articulation manners. Unlike previous research focusing on pathological conditions, this study examined healthy participants, analyzing impedance phase variations across articulation manners (plain, tense, aspirated) and positions (alveolar, alveolar-post, velar). Tongue pressure, surface electromyography (sEMG), and acoustic features (F2 slope) were simultaneously recorded during articulation tasks, allowing a multidimensional analysis. Six healthy adults (four males and two females) performed consonant pronunciations designed to induce varying tongue tension. A custom-made flexible palatal sensor measured impedance characteristics, which were compared with tongue pressure and sEMG data. Additionally, acoustic analysis was conducted by examining the F2 slope and tongue–palatal contact duration. Results showed that increased tongue contact strength (TCS) led to higher impedance phase angles, elevated tongue pressure, and stronger sEMG signals. Acoustic analysis confirmed that stronger tongue contacts were associated with steeper F2 slopes and shorter contact durations. These findings suggest that the impedance phase may serve as an objective marker of tongue-palatal contact strength and articulation. Although limited by sample size, the findings of this study provide a basis for the development of diagnostic and rehabilitation devices for speech disorders.

Introduction

The tongue plays an important role in bodily functions, such as speaking, breathing, and swallowing. These tongue movements can be viewed as a series of voluntary and involuntary sensory movements that are finely and intricately coordinated. Information obtained from these tongue movements has been used to recognize defects such as pathological nervous system damage, degenerative diseases, and speech disorders due to aging. For example, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease cause problems in chewing and swallowing, and there may be problems in coordinating the tongue and pharyngeal muscles for the involuntary stage1,2. In addition, these diseases are accompanied by motor nerve abnormalities and cause language problems such as dysarthria, which is a failure to control properly the articulatory organs, and aphasia, which causes difficulties in speaking, reading, and writing due to problems in the language center caused by brain damage2. During pronunciation, the tongue controls the flow of air (during speaking) and creates various sounds by contacting other parts of the mouth. For this reason, the strength and position of the tongue must be accurate for clear and accurate pronunciation; in this context, the development of articulation exercises and rehabilitation devices related to this has been studied continuously3–6. The tongue’s motion is very specific when pronouncing words, and this position defines the relationship with other parts of the mouth, such as the molars, incisors, and palate. For example, consonants, such as /k/, /t∫/, and /t/, are pronounced differently depending on the position of the tongue and the part where the tongue meets in the mouth. The tongue is composed of several muscles, and these muscles move differently at the tip, middle, and root of the tongue to produce various sounds and pronunciations. Ultimately, the degree of tongue tension caused by the muscles can be considered an important indicator that expresses accurate pronunciation and subtle differences between words. Observing how the electrical impedance of the tongue muscles changes in relation to the strength or tension of the tongue can help diagnose speech disorders and can be utilized as an important indicator of pathological phenomena affecting the central nervous system7–10.

Many studies have observed changes in the electrical characteristics of the tongue and palate in relation to the intraoral environment. In particular, the perception threshold of the electrotactile feedback for the tongue and palate was observed, and the lumped-element model that characterizes the electrical impedance was examined11. In addition, a device that applies electrical stimulation to the tongue surface and measures it using surface electromyography was implemented to evaluate the motor function of the tongue12. In addition, the electrical impedance measurements obtained during tongue movement (tongue retraction) and vowel pronunciation (/a/, /i/, /u/), can be used in patient cases suffering from amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), motor neuron disorder, and central or peripheral trauma13. In this study, we focused on the phase angle of electrical impedance an electrophysiological characteristic observed due to tongue–palate contact. The impedance phase has been reported as a sensitive parameter for diagnosing and monitoring disease progression, such as in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and has been actively investigated in recent years14–16. Unlike previous investigations that primarily targeted pathological populations, we examined healthy speakers to characterize impedance phase variations across articulation manners (plain, tense, aspirated) and positions (alveolar, alveolar-post, velar). To provide a comprehensive understanding, impedance phase was measured simultaneously with tongue pressure, surface electromyography (sEMG), and acoustic formant dynamics (F2 slope), enabling an integrated analysis of physiological and acoustic characteristics of articulation.

Methods

Electrical impedance magnitude |Z| and phase measurements

The electrical impedance characteristics about the tongue contact strength (TCS) are based on the electrical impedance myography (EIM) method. EIM applies to a sinusoidal, alternating current (AC) stimulus in the frequency range of 10–100 kHz, subject to which the tongue skin tissue responds in the form of an AC voltage signal. Characteristic magnitude |Z| plots that are related to the underlying structure and properties of muscle can be generated as a function of frequency. Many studies have focused on the 50 kHz phase characteristics of muscle tissue. The frequency of 50 kHz is commonly used for most bioimpedance studies as biological tissue tends to be most reactive at or near this frequency and most commercially available devices emit electrical current at only this one frequency. Healthy tissue is most reactive at approximately 50 kHz19. That study observed a gradual decrease in the phase value following electrical stimulation in the muscle tissue of ALS patients at 50 kHz and confirmed a significant phase value difference compared with that of the normal group19,20. In other studies, the phase angle was identified as an indicator to distinguish the tongue function of ALS patients and healthy people using a four-electrode-based impedance analyzer15,16. Therefore, in this study, the AC of 100 µA at 50 kHz21–23, which is the most frequently used in single-frequency bio-impedance analysis, was selected and applied to the surface of the tongue, and the magnitude |Z| and phase angle were analyzed accordingly. Also, in this study, TCS was conceptually defined as the degree of tongue palate contact during articulation. It was not introduced as a separate numerical index but described the experimental conditions in which stronger tongue palate contact was elicited, thereby influencing the observed phase.

Acoustic characteristics in relation to articulation manner

Proper tongue contact position (TCP) and TCS are very important factors in pronunciation. For accurate speech execution, the correct positioning of the articulators must be achieved at the appropriate time, and this is possible only when the neuromuscular system operates with sufficient strength and endurance24. In addition, when the muscles involved in speech execution become fatigued, it becomes difficult to maintain the correct position, movement, and posture of the articulators. Some studies have reported that speech intelligibility and consonant accuracy of dysarthria are highly correlated with TCS25,26. These studies suggest that it is important to monitor the muscle strength and fatigue of the articulatory organs when evaluating the progression of the disease or the progress of treatment in patients with speech disorders due to neurological defects. Therefore, the impedance characteristics (phase values) studied herein can be considered important indicators for inferences on the stiffnesses of the tongue muscles due to the contact between the tongue and the palate. In particular, the experimental approach of pronouncing consonants in different TCPs (alveolar, alveolar-post, velar regions) and distinct consonants (plain, tense, aspirated consonants) in the same TCP to induce various degrees of tongue muscle tension is important in that it analyzes actual pronunciations rather than simply assessing TCS.

Formant features of articulation manner

TCS characteristics are closely related to the formant features (acoustic indicators). Formants are the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract and are essential in shaping the characteristics of different sounds (particularly vowels) during speech. The first formant (F1) is related to the height of the tongue27. For example, the lower the tongue position (for open vowels such as /a/), the higher the F1 frequencies, and vice versa (for closed vowels such as /i/). The second formant (F2) is related to the position of the anterior or posterior parts of the tongue27. Tongue positions closer to the front of the lip (such as the instances during the pronunciation of /i/) result in high F2 frequencies, and those toward the back of the lip (such as when pronouncing /u/) result in low F2 frequencies. The F2 slope changes almost in response to the back-and-forth movements of the tongue28,29. In addition, the slope of F2 is highly correlated with slope reduction and slow changes in tongue movement in speakers with articulation disorders30, which indicates that the duration time for the tongue to contact the palate and then separate again is correlated with the strength of the tongue. Specifically, when the movement and strength of the tongue are relatively slow and low during articulation, the change in F2 movement becomes longer and thinner. In this study, we focused on the change in the F2 slope to observe the tongue strength about the pronunciation of selected consonant syllables, that is, TCS. Considering previous studies, it can be expected that the F2 slope will increase, and the time required by the tongue to contact the palate will become shorter when pronunciation requires high-tongue strength.

Experimental method

Palatal sensor fabrication for electrical phase measurements

To measure the phase characteristics of the tongue about the pronunciation of selected consonants, a flexible printed circuit board was manufactured, which contained an Invisalign pseudo-palatal, type and electrodes. The pseudo-palatal is a custom-made oral appliance that fits against the palate roof of a user. The fabrication of the sensor was similar to that of a dental palate. First, an impression of the upper arch was obtained to adapt the palatal sensor to a hard palate, as shown in Fig. 1(A). To fabricate a pseudo-palatal, a dental impression of a research subject was obtained, and plaster was poured onto the impression to create a mold. Subsequently, the hardened tooth-shaped plaster was separated, and a thin plastic sheet of biocompatible material was adsorbed onto the tooth-shaped plaster to create a pseudo-palatal. The thickness of the fabricated pseudo-palatal was in the range of 80–100 μm. Additionally, a thin, flexible printed circuit board (thickness = 70 μm) containing 10 gold electrodes was attached to the pseudo-palatal after the implementation of some adjustments to ensure conformance to the shape and fit of the user’s pseudo-palatal, as shown in Fig. 1(B). The gold electrodes had a diameter of 2.0 mm, with an inter-electrode spacing of 2.5 mm. Four adjacent electrodes were sequentially connected to the four data lines of the impedance analyzer (SFB7, Impedimed, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) current source, sense lead 1, sense lead 2, and current sink. In the four-electrode method, the current source delivers the alternating current to the palatal surface, the current sink provides the return path, and the two sense leads measure the potential difference between the inner electrodes in contact with the palate. The connection order was fixed in this sequence for all measurements. As illustrated in Fig. 1(B), the electrodes set were employed across the observation region: electrodes 1–4 for the alveolar region, electrodes 4–7 for the alveolar–palatal region, and electrodes 7–10 for the velar region. For example, in the alveolar region, the current source was assigned to electrode 1, followed sequentially by sense lead 1, sense lead 2, and the current sink connected to electrodes 2–4. The same order was applied to electrodes 4–7 for the alveolar–palatal region and electrodes 7–10 for the velar region. An alternating current of 100 µA at 50 kHz was applied to the palatal sensor. Each time the participants produced the target monosyllabic words, the magnitude |Z| and phase were repeatedly measured and recorded. (The detailed experimental setup is described in Sections F. Pronunciation Materials and G. Testing Environment.)

Pressure and electromyography evaluations for comparative analyses

A balloon-type tongue pressure measurement device (IOPI, IOPI Medical, Woodinville, WA, USA) was used to compare the characteristics about the articulation positions and the tongue strength. Tongue pressure measurements were performed by six individuals. Typically, the IOPI was used to measure the pressure exerted by the tongue. It is extensively used to measure the strength and endurance of the tongue, and the strength of the lips31. It serves as an effective tool for the quantitative evaluation of the muscles associated with articulatory functions in the oral cavity32. This makes it particularly valuable for diagnosing speech and swallowing disorders33. The IOPI comprises a disposable probe, an injection tube as a connector, and a hard ring (bite block; length = 10.0 mm; thickness = 0.5 mm; diameter = 5.5 mm). During the experiment, participants were instructed to place the balloon in their oral cavities while sitting. Before they were instructed to articulate a selected monosyllabic sound, they positioned the balloon tube at the designated articulation position. Participants were asked to maintain this position while the measurer adjusted the probe and confirmed its correct placement. Before the experiment, participants performed the task three times (with a 5-min rest) and two preliminary exercises. They were then randomly arranged to measure the target syllable five times.

In addition, the electrical characteristics were quantified using the surface electromyography system (Vitalstim Plus, Chattanooga Group, Dallas, TX, USA) to observe the activation of the muscles around the tongue by the target monosyllabic sound. Typically, in studies conducted to observe the tongue movement and swallowing effect, the muscles around the omohyoid, mylohyoid, sternothyroid, and thyroid muscles are designated as the possible electrode locations, and the anterior surface of the neck is the placement region34–36. In addition, some studies reported that the most important anterior tongue movement was from left to right around the midline between the tip of the mandible and the center of the hyoid bone37. In surface electromyography (sEMG) experiments, the skin above the patient’s neck was first exposed, and alcohol (75%) was used to wipe the surface of electrodes to enhance their electrical conductivity. A bipolar surface electrode was placed horizontally over both sides of the patient’s submental muscles (mylohyoid, geniohyoid, anterior digastric), equidistant from the midline, and the reference electrode was secured under the clavicle. sEMG was measured using a Vitalstim Plus device with a selectable band-pass filter, heart rate filter with a bandwidth in the range of 100–370 Hz, heart rate filter turned on, and a 50 Hz notch filter, with a root-mean-square (RMS) range of 0.2– 2000 µV, and a sensitivity of 0.1 µV RMS.

Formant measurements and analysis

Formant measurements and analysis were performed using the Praat program (ver. 6.3.09, Boersma & Weenink, University of Amsterdam, Netherlands), a widely used tool in the field of phonetic science. The Praat program allows for the analysis, synthesis, and manipulation of speech sounds, providing specific functions for identifying and quantitatively analyzing formants, which are the resonant frequencies of speech. To account for differences in vocal tract length and oral structure between males and females, the maximum formant frequency was set to approximately 5000 Hz for males and 5500 Hz for females. Participants wore palatal plate sensors and pronounced the target monosyllabic words, during which their speech signals were recorded with a condenser microphone (PURER, Joytron, Republic of Korea) at a 22 kHz sampling rate and 352.8 kbps bitrate. The recorded data were subsequently processed for quantitative formant analysis.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, we used R Studio (version 4.4.2, Posit, Boston, MA, USA). First, we performed a multivariable linear regression analysis, treating pressure and sEMG data collected from each participant as data from independent variables, and phase as the dependent variable, to examine the relationships among pressure, sEMG, magnitude |Z|, and phase. Additionally, to analyze quantitatively the relationship among phase, pressure, and sEMG data in relation to tongue contact strength, as well as the relationship between F2 slope and contact duration (based on tongue contact strength), we performed the nonparametric statistical Friedman test. Post-hoc comparisons were subsequently conducted using Bonferroni correction.

Human recruitment

All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Daegu University (IRB No. 1040621-201907-HR-061-02). All research was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. No identifiable personal information is included in this manuscript. The study enrolled six healthy adults (four males and two females; mean age 33.2 years, range 25–40 years), who will hereinafter be individually expressed as S1 through S6 in Figs. 2 and 3(A), all native Korean speakers with middle-to-intermediate English proficiency. Participants had no history of speech, neurological, or psychiatric disorders and showed normal cognitive, auditory, and visual function as assessed by the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination. All participants volunteered without financial compensation. Personal data were protected under the oversight of the Daegu University Bioethics Committee.

Two types of experiments were conducted using the target consonants. First, magnitude |Z| and phase angle were obtained the plain, tense, and aspirated sounds at each articulation position. The target consonants were alveolar consonant /t/, post-alveolar consonant /t∫/, and velar consonant /k/. Second, to analyze the relationship between magnitude |Z|, phase, and formants, an acoustic index, the F2 slope, and the tongue-palate contact time were obtained. Testing environment

As shown in Fig. 2, each participant in the experiment wore a palatal sensor and pronounced the target consonant displayed on the screen three times. The process of presenting one syllable was implemented by setting the exposure time and visual elements for each slide using the E-studio program. The total time for presenting a single stimulus was 5 s, and it consisted of the following sequence: (1) fixation (500 ms), (2) blank (250 ms), (3) display of the target one syllable (1 s), (4) blank (250 ms), and (5) pronunciation (3 s). The target syllable was presented randomly using E-studio to prevent the participants from developing a learning bias. Before the experiment, the participants rested for 5–10 min and became familiar with the pronunciations by wearing the palatal sensor. The experiment was conducted under the supervision of an experienced speech therapist in a quiet environment with a constant temperature of about 26 °C in an air-conditioned laboratory, and background noise levels in the range of 25–30 dB sound pressure level.

Results

Comparative results in Phase, Pressure, and sEMG

The tongue pressure and sEMG amplitude exhibited a positive correlation with impedance phase, as shown in Fig. 3(A). Across all participants, increases in phase were consistently associated with increases in both tongue pressure and sEMG amplitude. Specifically, TCS was evaluated by integrating results from tongue pressure (IOPI), surface electromyography (sEMG, Vitalstim Plus), and phase characteristics. Specifically, stronger TCS was reflected when higher tongue pressure values, increased sEMG amplitudes, and distinct impedance phase shifts were simultaneously observed. For example, during the production of tense consonants, these measures consistently indicated stronger tongue palate contact. These findings were statistically confirmed through multivariable linear regression analysis, demonstrating that the phase is significant predictors both tongue pressure and sEMG during articulation tasks.

Figure 3(B) shows that tense consonant syllables at the alveolar, alveolar-post, and velar positions exhibited the highest phase, tongue pressure, and sEMG values among all target sounds. The experimental results consistently demonstrated that tense consonants produced the highest measurements across these modalities. Although the magnitude |Z| tended to decrease slightly during tense consonant production, it is challenging to draw definitive conclusions due to variability in the data compared to other measurement outcomes. Additionally, it was difficult to clearly distinguish differences between plain and aspirated consonants. The measured impedance primarily reflects the current path through biological tissue. Under stronger tongue–palate contact, the effective current conduction pathway expands across a larger tissue surface, thereby increasing the cross-sectional area available for conduction. This expansion reduces the resistance R. At the same time, the tissue’s capacitive and dielectric properties cause reactance Xc to decrease with closer contact. Together, these changes lower the overall magnitude |Z|. Importantly, as reported by similar studies38, the reduction in resistance R can is disproportionately larger than the reduction in reactance Xc, which leads to an increase in phase angle θ as Eq. (3). Similar observations have been reported in other studies, where magnitude |Z| at the tongue surface decreased during sustained tongue retraction and vowel articulation training for /a/, /i/, and /u/13.

As shown in Fig. 3(C), tense consonant syllables consistently exhibited the highest phase, tongue pressure, and sEMG amplitudes among the target sounds, indicating stronger tongue–palate contact and reflecting tongue strength characteristics. This interpretation is further supported by the acoustic analysis, where the F2 slope was observed to be steeper under conditions of stronger and faster tongue movements (Fig. 6). Consistent with previous reports30, these findings demonstrate that both physiological measures (pressure, sEMG, impedance) and acoustic parameters (F2 slope) converge to indicate enhanced tongue strength during tense consonant production. Statistical analysis confirmed these tendencies, as significant differences were observed among articulation types (p < .001).

Post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction revealed significant differences between tense and plain consonants across all measurement modalities — impedance phase, tongue pressure, and sEMG (all p < .0003), as well as magnitude |Z| (p < .0033). Similarly, significant differences between tense and aspirated consonants were observed in phase (p < .0003), pressure (p < .0033), and sEMG (p < .0167) measurements, although the degree of difference varied by modality. Additionally, a significant difference was noted between plain and aspirated consonants in sEMG measurements (p < .0167). However, magnitude |Z| showed a significant difference only between tense and plain consonants, with no significant difference observed between tense and aspirated consonants. This suggests that magnitude |Z| may be less sensitive than other parameters in reflecting changes in tongue strength associated with articulation manner.

The F2 slope was calculated as the change in F2 from the onset point (where the consonant begins) to the offset point (where the vowel begins). The slope size shows a steeper decline in TA (wherein the tongue strength is low) than those in PA and AA, where the tongue strength is high. Discussion

In this study, we observed the phase characteristics in relation to TCP and TCS based on electrophysiological analysis. In particular, the experimental approach used for consonants at different articulation sites (alveolar, alveolar-post, velar region) and distinct consonants at the same articulation site (plain, tense, aspirated consonants) to induce various tongue movements is of great importance in the proposed study.

Impedance phase feature in relation to articulation position

Based on the experimental results, greater tongue strength—defined in this study as higher tongue–palate contact strength (TCS) reflected by increased tongue pressure, sEMG amplitudes, and phase—was most evident in tense consonant production. Under these conditions, the acoustic analysis also revealed steeper F2 slopes, indicating that both physiological and acoustic measures consistently captured stronger articulation. The observed increase in phase can be explained by changes in both resistance R and reactance Xc. Under stronger tongue–palate contact, the effective current path expands across a larger tissue surface, which increases the conductive cross-sectional area and reduces the resistance R. At the same time, closer tissue contact also influences dielectric and capacitive properties, resulting in a reduction of reactance Xc. Importantly, as described in Eq. (3), the relative reduction in R is disproportionately larger than the reduction in Xc. This disproportion leads to an overall decrease in magnitude |Z| while simultaneously causing the phase angle to increase. These findings demonstrate that phase serves as a sensitive indicator of tongue strength during articulation.

Another issue we should pay attention to is the characteristics in relation to the articulation position. To enable comparison across measurement types, the phase, tongue pressure, and sEMG values were normalized within each articulation position. For each measurement type, the minimum and maximum values were scaled to 0 and 1, respectively, and the normalized values were then averaged across participants. The resulting normalized data are presented in Fig. 7. Increased phase, pressure, and sEMG deviations were observed in the alveolar region where the tongue tip mainly touched the palate compared with those observed in other regions. In addition, the deviations of each data tend to decrease as the velar region where the tongue body or lingual root touches the palate. This shows that the tongue’s strength is higher in the anterior than in the posterior region and is difficult to control. In a related study, it was reported that consonants pronounced in the alveolar region, such as /d/ and /t/, in TCP exhibited significant TCS and air pressure39. However, the data of the six participants obtained in this study are limited as they cannot be generalized because they were approached demographically. In particular, there are various individual differences, such as oral cavity volume and tongue size, depending on gender. A large-scale study conducted on 147 healthy adults (18–88 years old) observed the relationship between anterior and posterior tongue strength and palate shape, and found that individual palatal width, estimated tongue volume, and gender were significant predictors of tongue strength40. However, unlike previous studies, the adopted approach can be considered new in that it analyzed data measured in various ways, such as phase, pressure, and sEMG while considering the articulation manner that serves as the standard for pronunciation.

Formant features in relation to articulation position

Formant analysis is crucial for understanding the resonant properties of the vocal tract during speech production. Formants are the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract and are essential in shaping the characteristics of different sounds during speech. Specifically, the F2 is related to the position of the anterior or posterior parts of the tongue39,40. The F2 varies depending on whether the tongue, which obstructs the airflow in the vocal tract, is positioned in the anterior alveolar region or the posterior velar region. Tongue positions closer to the front of the lip (such as the instants associated with the pronunciations of /d/ and /t/) result in high F2 frequencies, and those toward the back of the lip (such as the instants associated with the pronunciations of /k/ and /g/) result in low F2 frequencies. As shown in Fig. 5(B), the change in F2 is caused by velar constriction (such as the instants associated with the pronunciations of /kα/, /k*α/, and /khα/). Conversely, there were no changes in F1 and F3 in relation to the anterior or posterior positions of the tongue. Typically, F1 is closely related to the height of the tongue, and F3 is closely related to pharyngeal constriction27.

Limitations of the presented study

This study is associated with several limitations. First, the electrical impedance characteristics about TCS were observed for the decrease in |Z| and the increase in phase, as in the experimental results of Fig. 3(B). On the other hand, in the statistical analysis of Fig. 3(C), the magnitude |Z| showed relatively small differences. This study also focused primarily on phase variation rather than magnitude |Z|, which aligns with previous research that emphasized phase characteristics over absolute magnitude |Z| values.

The main objective of this study was to first establish the relationship between articulation-related tongue contact patterns and the phase of impedance during speech production, both in healthy individuals and patients with articulation disorders. However, it is important to note that a deeper understanding of tissue properties requires multifrequency analysis and Cole–Cole modeling. Bioimpedance research including multifrequency approaches still faces significant limitations. This is mainly because the electrical properties of biological tissues, such as subcutaneous fat and muscle, and particularly the fine structural changes at the cellular level, are highly frequency-dependent and variable22,41. Moreover, ongoing other research are attempting to elucidate the complex frequency-dependent behavior of biological tissues23. Therefore, future studies should incorporate multifaceted analyses, including multifrequency sweeps and Cole–Cole plot interpretations, to better understand TCS variations due to different articulation manners.

Second, owing to the small sample size of only six subjects, our study lacks the statistical power to perform variance analysis between groups. Thus, future studies should include a broader demographic to enable statistical approaches and interpretations that can validate the findings across various age groups and patient conditions.

Third, the ultimate goal is to develop a rehabilitation training device for real-time TCS measurement and feedback to the user. To this end, a novel sensor is required for the detection of magnitude |Z| and phase. This study is associated with limitations in that it measured and analyzed the data of the experimental participants using a conventional commercial impedance meter. Commercially available integrated circuits (ICs) with bioimpedance measurement functions provide an opportunity to develop portable devices. Most ICs operate at similar frequency ranges (64–131 kHz) but use different electrode types (bipolar or tetrapolar) and stimulus signal shapes (sinusoidal, trapezoidal, square)42. In particular, AD5933 and MAX3000x are the most extensively utilized bioimpedance ICs. The AD5933 has been available since 2005 and has been the most investigated bioimpedance IC43–45. The MAX3000x is being used in research on respiration and electrocardiographic monitoring, but it may be suitable for implementing wearable devices owing to its smallest size (8 mm2) among the currently released bioimpedance ICs46. In future studies, it will be required to standardize magnitude |Z| and phase through additional experimentally acquired data from participants and develop a rehabilitation training device capable of TCS feedback using a dedicated bioimpedance IC.

Conclusion

In this study, we observed the electrical characteristics (impedance phase angle) in relation to tongue strength and articulation manner due to the contact between the tongue and palate in general pronunciation test cases. Experiments on consonants at different articulation positions and distinct consonants at the same articulation position used to induce various degrees of tongue muscle tension by the articulation manner were found to be very meaningful. As a result of the experiment, it was confirmed that the higher the TCS, the lower the magnitude |Z| and the higher the phase angle. Comparisons were performed using the standard tongue muscle measurement device, IOPI, and sEMG. In addition, the appropriateness of the proposed approach was confirmed by analyzing the formant components. In the formant experiments, a high correlation between TCS and F2 slope was observed that confirmed that the proposed TCS analysis approach is an objectively valid index. Despite the study’s limitations (outlined in the discussion), the magnitude |Z| and phase characteristics in relation to articulation manner suggest that they can be utilized in the development of portable devices for the rehabilitation training of patients with structural and functional tongue problems that impede swallowing and speaking ability, and patients with dysarthria with speech pathological abnormalities.
