Oral Acute Toxicity and Genotoxicity of Heteroctenus junceus Scorpion Venom
Authors: Alexis Díaz‐García, Jenny Laura Ruiz‐Fuentes, Sophia Mejias Medina, Nicolas Zapata Guajardo, Christian Ross Skarneo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe study evaluates the elemental composition, oral acute toxicity, genotoxicity, and mutagenic potential of Heteroctenus junceus scorpion venom.MethodsThe elemental composition was determined by atomic absorption spectrophotometry and HPLC‐MS/MS. The acute oral toxicity test was assessed in mice at 300 and 2000 mg/kg. The in vivo micronucleus test was conducted in mice treated with three scorpion venom doses (10, 100, and 1000 mg/kg). Meanwhile, the in vitro micronucleus test was conducted in TK6 cells treated with three scorpion venom doses (1.25, 2.5, and 5 mg/mL). The Ames test followed the OECD guidelines, and various scorpion venom concentrations (0.16, 0.32, 0.63, 1.25, 2.5, 5 mg/mL) were used.ResultsThe elemental analysis of scorpion venom reveals the lowest amino acid and elemental composition levels compared to other species. An LD50 value greater than 2000 mg/kg was obtained in the oral acute toxicity test with transient pathophysiological effects in the liver, lung, and kidney at 300 and 2000 mg/kg. The in vitro micronucleus test showed that the negative control and scorpion venom concentrations did not induce micronuclei in TK6 cells. Meanwhile, the in vivo micronucleus test showed an increase in the frequency of micronucleated polychromatic erythrocytes and a dose‐dependent decrease in erythrocyte count only at doses higher than 100 mg/kg. The Ames reverse mutation test did not show an increase in revertant colonies in the scorpion venom‐treated groups compared to the negative control.Conclusions H. junceus venom has a low toxicological potential.Keywords: acute oral toxicity, Ames test, Heteroctenus junceus scorpion venom, micronucleus test

Introduction

Scorpion venoms contain a complex mixture of peptides, proteins, enzymes, and other bioactive molecules, which contribute to the toxicity of the venom. Scorpion venom components have been described to include water, metals, oligopeptides, amino acids, and ions. In addition, the presence of mucoproteins, mucopolysaccharides, hyaluronidase, phospholipase, serotonin, histamine, biogenic amines, and unidentified substances has been described (Delgado‐Prudencio et al. 2022, Gwee et al. 2002, Possani et al. 1999, Pucca et al. 2015, Rodriguez de la Vega and Possani 2004). Scorpion venoms can induce clinical manifestations in the human body, such as neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, inflammation in organs, hematological abnormalities, vasculitis, metabolic disturbances, reduced blood flow in circulation, and others (Alhelail et al. 2024, Duzzi et al. 2021). Researchers conduct investigations to obtain evidence about how scorpion venom disrupts normal physiological functions and affects targeted organs and biochemical parameters in the body (Jiang et al. 2021, Pourkhalili et al. 2015). All the data contribute to identifying the toxic potential of these species, which is useful to reduce the impact of the envenomation process and potentially uncover new therapeutic opportunities.

Heteroctenus junceus (formerly Rhopalurus junceus), also popularly known as the Cuban blue scorpion, is an endemic species found in Cuba belonging to the Buthidae family. This scorpion has gained attention due to its potential medicinal properties. Researchers have investigated the biological activities of H. junceus venom, including its effects on cancer cell viability and its in vivo antitumor properties, with some promising results (Díaz‐García et al. 2019, Diaz‐Garcia et al. 2013).

Some toxicological studies have been conducted to understand the mechanisms of toxicity of this scorpion venom. The safety of aqueous solution of H. junceus scorpion venom in short‐term oral toxicity in MRI mice receiving 100 mg/kg/day for 28 days and subchronic toxicity in mice administered with 0.1, 10, and 100 mg/kg by oral route for 90 days was carried out (Lagarto et al. 2020). In both experiments, body weight change was observed without additional evidence of a significant adverse effect. In addition, teratogenic potential was evaluated, and there were no signs of embryofetal toxicity. These first experimental pieces of evidence provide data about the reduced toxicological potential of this scorpion venom. Herein, we provide elemental analysis of the scorpion venom and their potential relation to safety data by evaluating the acute oral toxicity and genotoxicity of H. junceus scorpion venom.

Material and Methods

Obtaining the Sample

Scorpion venom was obtained from the LifEscozul company. Adult scorpions kept alive under bioterium conditions, fed once a week and left unmilked for at least 21 days, were used in the study. The scorpions were milked individually by electrical stimulation of the scorpion telson. The venom from one hundred scorpions was pooled in a 5 mL glass flask, lyophilized for preservation, and sent to the laboratory. Once in the laboratory, the scorpion venom was restored by adding double‐distilled water and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. The soluble components were obtained, filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter, and stored at −20°C until used. The protein content was determined by the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit.

Elemental Analysis

The elemental composition of the venom was analyzed by mixing the venom dilution (1/100) in buffer solution (0.2%, v/v, nitric acid and 0.05% v/v, Triton X in water).

The scanning and quantitative multi‐element analysis of scorpion venom was performed using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS). The organic matrix was destroyed by dry incineration in a muffle furnace at 4500C for 8 h. The obtained ash was dissolved in HNO3 and analyzed. The metal determination included Calcium, Copper, Magnesium, Potassium, and Sodium. Phosphorus was determined by spectrophotometry on an ashed sample portion by complexing with the molybdovanadate reagent. Zn was determined by atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS) after generation of metal hydrides (Official Methods of Analysis of AOAC INTERNATIONAL 2023). A total of three readings were collected with three replicates per sample.

Determination of Amino Acids Using High‐performance Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS)

The identification of amino acids in the venom of H. junceus was accomplished by using the high‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS/MS) technology. The column temperature was carefully controlled at 30°C. The injection volume was 1 mL, while the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The mass spectrometer was used in positive and negative ionization multiple reaction monitoring modes.

Acute Oral Toxicity Test

The acute oral toxicity test was performed in accordance with the Good Laboratory Practice standards for non‐clinical laboratory studies ENV/MC/CHEM(98)17, and the acute oral toxicity guideline (OECD 423), described by the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD). Female mice (8–12 weeks old) were randomly selected and housed in cages, weighed, and tail‐tagged for individual identification. Five days before treatment, animals were allowed to acclimate to the laboratory conditions. Animals were allowed to drink water and food ad libitum throughout the experiment, were maintained on a 12‐h light/dark cycle, and were housed at a room temperature of approximately 22°C with a constant humidity of 30%–70%. All mice were kept fasting for 12 h before treatment. Mice were divided into two groups, which received 300 mg/kg (n = 6) and 2000 mg/kg (n = 6), respectively. All groups were administered orally using an intragastric tube. Following the administration, animals were monitored exhaustively within the first 4 h after the treatment period, and daily for 14 days. Body weight was examined at 0, 7, and 14 days. Clinical signs of mice were monitored and recorded every day for 14 days. Finally, all animals were anesthetized and euthanized, and complete gross postmortem examinations were performed on all animals in the test.

Determination of Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity

Cell Culture

The human lymphoblastoid TK6 cell line was used in the study. The cells were initially maintained at 1×105 cells/mL in RPMI‐1640 medium (GIBCO, Invitrogen Corp., CA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated horse serum (GIBCO, Invitrogen Corp., CA, USA), 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, in a 100 mm culture plate at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity.

Cytotoxicity was evaluated by cell viability and trypan blue assay. To this end, TK6 cells were seeded into a 96‐well plate at a density of 1×104 cells/well, and various scorpion venom concentrations were added (0, 0.18, 0.36, 1.25, 2.5, 5 mg/mL). The 96‐well plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cell viability counting in the Neubauer chamber was employed to measure the cytotoxic effects, calculate the relative population doubling (RPD), and the relative increase in cell count (RICC) as recommended (OCDE 487).

In Vitro Mammalian Erythrocyte Micronucleus Test

At least three scorpion venom concentrations were selected for the micronucleus test, based on the cytotoxicity results from previous experiments. TK6 cells were seeded at 1×104 cells/well and received either short‐term (6 h) or continuous (24 h) treatment with negative control (PBS), scorpion venom concentrations (1.25, 2.5, 5 mg/mL), and positive controls (4‐Nitroquinoline‐N Oxide (100 nM), Cyclophosphamide (100 nM), colchicine (100 nM)) individually. After short‐term treatment, cells were washed and cultured in a fresh medium for 18 h before slide preparation. After continuous treatment, slides were prepared immediately.

Cells were examined for any change in the colour of the culture medium, cell lysis, or precipitate. Then, cells were harvested, washed twice with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The pellet was suspended in hypotonic KCl solution (0.075 M) and gently mixed by inversion. Then, an aliquot of ice‐cold fixative (methanol/glacial acetic acid: 25:1) was added and gently mixed. A drop of the suspension was placed on a clean glass slide, air‐dried, and stained with Giemsa solution. The slides were covered with coverslips, and the cells were examined at ×400 magnification under an Optical Microscope. Three technical replicates were used, and 103 cells were analysed for micronuclei in each group. The frequency of micronucleated cells (%) was determined. 4‐Nitroquinoline‐N‐Oxide (CAS No: 56‐57‐5), Cyclophosphamide (CAS No: 50‐18‐0), and Colchicine (CAS No: 64‐86‐8) were used as clastogenic without metabolic activation, clastogenic with metabolic activation, and aneugenic positive controls, respectively.

Metabolic Activation

The post‐mitochondrial fraction (S9) from rat liver supplemented with cofactors at a concentration of 3% was used as an exogenous metabolic activation system (Xenometrix AG, Switzerland).

In Vivo Mammalian Erythrocyte Micronucleus Test

The in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test was conducted following the Good Laboratory Practice regulations for nonclinical laboratory studies ENV/MC/CHEM(98)17 (OECD 474), outlined by the OECD. The CF1 mice (8–12 weeks old) were randomly selected and housed in cages, weighed, and marked on the tail for individual identification. Five days before treatment, animals were allowed to acclimate to the laboratory conditions. The food consisted of a commercial pelleted rodent diet (Labdiet 5000 Prolab RMH 3000) and was analysed for microbiological and chemical contaminants. Animals drink water and food ad libitum throughout the experiment, except before dosing. Mice were maintained on a 12‐hour light/dark cycle at a room temperature of approximately 22°C with a constant humidity of 30%–70%.

Ten animals were included in each treated group: Cyclophosphamide monohydrate [CAS:9055‐19‐2] (positive control), distilled water (negative control), H. junceus venom groups (HVJ): HJV1(10 mg/kg), HJV2 (100 mg/kg), and HJV3(1000 mg/kg). The scorpion venom groups were administered orally using an intragastric tube. Positive and negative controls were included using the same treatment schedule as the test substance. Clinical signs, including general conditions and analysis of skin, eyes, nose, oral cavity, abdominal region, and external genitalia of all groups, were recorded after dosing, 4 h later, and once a day until the end of the experiment, when the animals were sacrificed with CO2 gas (AVMA 2020).

Substances were administered, and 36–48 h later, peripheral blood cells (200 µL) were obtained from the tail vein of each animal. Peripheral blood from each animal was spread individually on a glass slide and fixed with 100% methanol for 3 min. Finally, the slides were stained with Giemsa solution for 15 min, washed, and dried at room temperature. The slides were primarily coded before the study and examined randomly and blindly by an expert using a brightfield microscope. For each animal, around 6000 erythrocytes (TE) (micronucleated polychromatic erythrocytes [PCE] + normochromatic erythrocytes [NCE]) were counted. The ratio PCE/TE was calculated. The historical data from the negative control data establishes the normal values in the experiments.

Bacterial Reverse Mutation Test (Ames Test)

The in vitro Ames test (TG471) followed the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) guidelines for Testing Chemicals (OECD, 1997). Six bacterial strains (Salmonella typhimurium TA1537, TA1535, TA100, TA98, and E. coli WP2 uvrA and Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA (pKM101)) were used in the study. A preliminary cytotoxicity test was performed with some strains to determine the upper and lower limits of scorpion venom concentration. The Ames fluctuation assay was performed in 384‐well plates, and all plating was done in triplicate (Bridges 1980). The final scorpion venom concentrations (0.16, 0.32, 0.63, 1.25, 2.5, 5 mg/mL) were exposed for 90 min. All tests were in the presence and absence of the post‐mitochondrial fraction (S9) prepared from the livers of Sprague‐Dawley male rats, treated with the enzyme‐inducing agent Aroclor 1254. The strains were used at a final concentration of 107 bacteria/mL, and the plates were incubated for 48 h at 37°C. The S9 mix was composed as follows: 0.2 M phosphate buffer pH 7.4, 1 M glucose‐6‐phosphate solution, 0.1 M NADP solution (40 µL), 0.4 M MgCl2.6H2O solution, 1.65 M KCl solution, and post‐mitochondrial S9 fraction. Distilled water was used as a negative control, and different positive controls were used depending on the strain used (Table 1). Revertant strains were identified based on the pH changes in the plate wells, and the average of revertant strains was calculated for each group. A result is considered positive for a mutagenic effect if the number of revertant wells in a treated group is at least twice that of the number of revertant strains in the negative control.

Statistical Analysis

In acute oral toxicity, differences in body weight in the scorpion venom‐treated groups at 0, 7, and 14 days were statistically analysed using the t‐test. Statistically significant differences in the frequency of micronucleated cells between the positive‐ and scorpion venom‐treated groups and the PBS‐treated group were evaluated. The analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 version 9.0.0.

Oral Acute Toxicity

The acute oral toxicity of H. junceus scorpion venom was evaluated in female mice. No mortality occurred in any of the administered groups during the test period. Furthermore, no clinical signs associated with the scorpion venom treatment were observed in the 300 mg/kg groups. Meanwhile, in the second group (2000 mg/kg), clinical signs such as mild to moderate dyspnea, mild to moderate ataxia, mild to moderate tremors, and mild to moderate circular movements on their axis were observed. These clinical signs were observed from 4 h until Day 2 of the observation period (Table 4).

Histopathology Analysis

Histopathological analysis of mice treated with 300 mg/kg and 2000 mg/kg showed major organs affected (Table 5). Lung histology was evaluated with H&E‐stained tissue sections. The animals treated with 300 mg/kg showed mild to moderate pulmonary congestion, increased alveolar wall thickness, moderate‐grade oedema, and mild‐grade haemorrhage, which suggests mild or initial pulmonary toxicity. In addition, many inflammatory cells were observed in the pulmonary microvasculature. Liver samples showed moderate congestion, swelling, vacuolization, nuclear activation of hepatocytes and Kupffer cells consistent with liver toxicity. Similarly, the kidney samples presented moderate congestion and swelling. The seriousness of tissue lesions was graded equally in each treated group.

Discussion

H. junceus scorpion venom is widely used in traditional medicine to treat some illnesses. Therefore, the study of the elemental composition and possible toxic effects of H. junceus scorpion venom could be useful in the safe use of this venom (Lagarto et al. 2020).

In this study, we selected the oral route for administering the H. junceus venom because, in traditional medicine, this venom is typically consumed mixed with drinking water. Additionally, previous sub‐chronic toxicological studies using the oral route did not reveal significant adverse effects, indicating it as a potentially safe route of administration (Lagarto et al. 2020). In general, the scorpion venom contains a variety of peptide toxins and enzymes that induce pathological effects associated with the sting. The enzymes include phospholipases, hyaluronidase, and proteases, among others, that provoke damage at various levels and the spread of peptide toxins throughout the body (Delgado‐Prudencio et al. 2022, Boghozian et al. 2021, Mendoza‐Tobar et al. 2024). In particular, the H. junceus venom does not contain some of these enzymes (Diaz‐Garcia et al. 2015). However, the presence of hyaluronidase has been identified in some studies (Diaz‐Garcia et al. 2015, Rodriguez‐Ravelo et al. 2013). Furthermore, various studies have demonstrated significant antitumor effects in in vivo experimental models, administered orally (Diaz‐Garcia et al. 2019, Díaz‐García et al. 2019, Yglesias‐Rivera et al. 2023). Therefore, the oral route could promote the degradation of venom enzymes in the gastrointestinal tract, reducing their potential adverse effects while preserving their pharmacological activities.

In our work, the amino acid concentration was very low compared to other studies (Boghozian et al. 2021). Particularly, almost all the amino acids in the sample show similar levels. This behaviour can be due mainly to the extraction methodology of H. junceus venom. The extraction of H. junceus scorpion venom has been standardised to occur when components show the highest levels, so at this extraction time, the amounts of amino acids required to generate the venom components should be minimal (Díaz‐García et al. 2019). This is true if we consider that the production and storage of venom components in glands is a costly process involving a significant increase in energetic and metabolic rate in scorpion venoms (Nisani et al. 2012, Nisani et al. 2007).

Recent comparative studies conducted with three different species of scorpions show differences in the proportions of amino acids in each species (Boghozian et al. 2021). Lysine, leucine, and arginine were found in the highest proportions in these venoms. The predominance of basic and neutral amino acids over acidic ones correlates with the basic characteristic of most scorpion toxins (Abdollahnia et al. 2024, Caliskan et al. 2006, Daoudi et al. 2021, Xu et al. 2014). The highest levels of these amino acids are in contrast with those observed in our study. These differences between the studies could be due, at least, to the extraction time. In our case, the venom is completely regenerated, while in the other case, the venom could still be regenerating new proteins and toxins.

Based on the elemental composition determined in H. junceus venom, calcium and sodium showed lower levels compared to the elemental scan analysis carried out in Androctonus bicolor, Androctonus crassicauda, and Leiurus quinquestriatus scorpion venom species, which found the higher sodium and calcium levels in their samples (Al‐Asmari et al. 2016). Similarly, differences were observed in potassium, magnesium, and copper concentration, with the lowest levels in our study (Al‐Asmari et al. 2016). Some reports show the association between high levels of minerals and systemic venom toxicity. For instance, high levels of potassium have been related to cell depolarisation, contributing to local pain and toxicity (Inceoglu et al. 2003). Besides, increased concentration of cofactors in gland venom, like zinc, calcium, and magnesium, promotes high enzymatic activity, contributing to the diffusion of toxins throughout the body (Ortiz et al. 2014). The presence of phospholipases has been widely reported in toxic scorpions like Heterometrus sp, Pandinus imperator, Anuroctonus phaiodactylus, Mesobuthus tamulus, and Centruroides sp, among others (Delgado‐Prudencio et al. 2022, Mendoza‐Tobar et al. 2024). The presence of enzymes in H. junceus venom is scarce, with two reports showing only negligible hyaluronidase activity (Diaz‐Garcia et al. 2015, Rodriguez‐Ravelo et al. 2013). The low enzymatic activity in H. junceus venom, coinciding with very low levels of enzyme cofactors, could add evidence of the low enzyme‐based toxicity of this species.

In oral acute toxicity tests, different doses of H. junceus were orally administered to mice. In the study, the highest OECD‐recommended dose was included (2000 mg/kg) (OECD 2002), and no deaths occurred during the 14 days of evaluation. The high LD50 value and the transient clinical signs are evidence suggesting low toxicity of this natural extract. (van Berlo et al. 2022).

The rapid onset of symptoms observed in animals treated with scorpion venom could be correlated with the rapid distribution of venom components in the body. (Díaz‐García et al. 2019). The absence of significant morphological tissue alterations in organs can be explained by the potential low affinity of venom components to the main organs analysed. Furthermore, the gastrointestinal tract can help reduce toxic effects because it represents an aggressive environment (acidity, enzymes) for the proteins and peptides present in the venom. (Hosseini et al. 2017, Yan et al. 2024). All these hypotheses agree with the transient disappearance of signs and symptoms in the animals and reversible functional damage induced by scorpion venom rather than structural tissue damage.

In the present study, histopathological analysis revealed that animals treated with H. junceus scorpion venom are affected in the main organs analysed (lung, kidney, and liver). The histological changes in the liver included cytoplasmic vacuolation and Kupffer cell activation. Cytoplasmic vacuolization of hepatocytes can be related to excessive glycogen accumulation within cells, observed after mild acute and subacute liver injury. Some reports suggest it is an alternative cellular adaptation to resist further insult (Nayak et al. 1996) and might involve the senescence process of hepatocytes as a consequence of liver injury (Aravinthan et al. 2012). The liver injury caused by the effects of scorpion venoms involves functional impairment of the liver, structural changes related to congestion, and an increase in hepatic enzymes (El Hidan et al. 2016). Usually, Kupffer cells activate to protect the liver from toxin‐induced injury and are involved in hepatocyte proliferation, as part of the self‐regeneration process of liver tissue (Tan et al. 2016). The lungs were also subjected to histopathological analysis, revealing morphological alterations like congestion, oedema, and atelectasis. Clinical and experimental reports coincide that scorpion envenomation causes pulmonary oedema as part of acute lung injury (Mesquita et al. 2002), as observed in our study. Scorpion venoms are one of the most powerful stimulants of the autonomic nervous system. The action on the autonomic system includes adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), provoking acute lung injury and pulmonary oedema. Pulmonary oedema is a consequence, in part, of increased pulmonary vascular permeability induced by vasoactive substances such as leukotrienes and prostaglandins released in the presence of the venom. Furthermore, decreased alveolar fluid clearance as part of sodium‐potassium ATPase downregulation contributes to lung injury. (Bompelli et al. 2018, Comellas et al. 2003, Razi and Malekanrad 2008, Zayerzadeh et al. 2010). The kidney was also affected after scorpion venom treatment. The kidneys represent the main route of venom excretion from the body. Thus, the components of scorpion venom, regardless of the species involved, induce histopathological alterations in renal tissues that involve degenerative changes with disorganised glomeruli and necrotic tubules post‐envenomation. (Heidarpour et al. 2012, Saidani et al. 2018).

Additionally, scorpion venom evokes common patterns in acute renal injury related to ischemia induced by intense renal vasoconstriction as part of the renal congestion, due to toxins acting on vascular ion channels. Moreover, the presence of venom enzymes contributes to the release of proinflammatory cytokines and vasoactive mediators, which increase inflammatory reactions during acute kidney injury. (Saidani et al. 2018, Angsanakul and Sitprija 2013, de Sousa Alves et al. 2005, Matkivska et al. 2023).

The pathophysiological effects induced by scorpion venoms are exerted by stinging the victims or, in laboratory experiments, by injection (Caliskan et al. 2006, Daoudi et al. 2021, Xu et al. 2014). The oral route used in the study may potentially help mitigate damage to affected organs based on the few transient adverse effects observed. It is worth mentioning that the adverse effects observed in the study occurred at significantly higher doses compared to the reported pharmacological doses. (Yglesias‐Rivera et al. 2023).

The assessment of micronucleus induction in vitro and in vivo is the core of a battery of genotoxicity tests recommended by regulatory agencies worldwide as part of product safety assessment. TK6 cells are highly sensitive for the reliable and accurate detection of compounds with known aneugenic and clastogenic activity, meeting the criteria recommended by OECD TG 487. (Sobol et al. 2012). Therefore, the absence of micronuclei in the TK6 cell line after treatment with scorpion venom demonstrates the low genotoxic potential of the natural extract.

Unlike in vitro assays, the in vivo micronucleus experiment shows a significant frequency of micronucleated cells at doses above 100 mg/kg of venom. However, the increased frequency of micronuclei appears to be due to a decrease in total erythrocyte count, rather than an increase in the proportion of micronucleated cells. Therefore, we hypothesise that the transit of scorpion venom through the gastrointestinal tract activates some components responsible for the decrease in erythrocyte count. Among the scorpion venom components, enzymes may be responsible, at least in part, for the hemolysis of erythrocytes (Diaz‐Garcia et al. 2015, García‐Gómez et al. 2011). Some authors have reported the presence of enzymes in scorpion venom species (Mendoza‐Tobar et al. 2024, Chaubey 2010, Delgado‐Prudencio et al. 2022, Wesam and Naglaa 2014). Particularly, phospholipase A2 (PLA2) has been reported in scorpion venoms (García‐Gómez et al. 2011, Krayem and Gargouri 2020). This enzyme is a powerful hemolytic agent that converts lecithin to lysolecithin, which in turn contributes to the osmotic fragility of red blood cells. Likewise, the presence of some peptides from scorpion venom that act on the membranes of red blood cells, inducing hemolysis, must be considered. (Borchani et al. 2011). It is noteworthy that the hemolytic activity of H. junceus venom occurs at significantly higher doses starting from 100 mg/kg compared to the doses inducing anticancer activity in vivo (12.5 mg/kg) orally (Yglesias‐Rivera et al. 2023, Díaz‐García et al. 2019).

The Ames test is regarded as a sensitive and specific assay for predicting mutagenic rodent carcinogens due to the use of amino‐acid‐requiring strains of S. typhimurium and E. coli to detect point mutations, which involve substitution, addition, or deletion of one or a few DNA base pairs (OECD 2020). Our experimental conditions indicate that H. junceus was unable to reverse the strain's phenotypes because it did not induce base pair substitution and/or open reading frame shifting. Although the presence of the postmitochondrial fraction of rat liver S9 does not alter the results and confirms the absence of mutagenic effects, the Ames test is effective in detecting mutagenicity, as observed in the experiments and previously reported for the positive controls used in the study (Rainer et al. 2021, Vijay et al. 2018).

Conclusion

In vitro and in vivo toxicological testing indicate that H. junceus has a low toxicological potential. In clinically relevant experimental settings, in vivo preclinical studies with doses below 100 mg/kg should be considered to ensure optimal therapeutic outcomes with minimal toxicological effects in pharmacological experiments. Furthermore, the elimination of potential enzymatic toxicity should be explored, potentially expanding the therapeutic window of this extract in future in vivo studies. Strategies that aim to modify the natural extract or identify specific active components to reduce and/or eliminate the identified toxic effects should be considered.
