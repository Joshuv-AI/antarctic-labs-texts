Percutaneous retrieval of a fractured coronary catheter: A case report
Authors: Chengli Tao, Aixia Zhou, Shuang Wu, Xiaohui Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntravascular angiographic catheter fracture is an extremely rare but critical complication of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Conventional retrieval strategies (e.g., surgical intervention, snares) may prolong procedures and raise peri-procedural complication risks. Herein, we report a case of successful retrieval of a fractured catheter using an innovative technique.Case presentationA 59-year-old female with 7-year hypertension was admitted for 3-month recurrent exertional angina. Right radial coronary angiography was performed, during which a TIG catheter fractured ∼8 cm proximal to the ulnar-radial bifurcation in the right brachial artery. Initial attempts to retrieve it via a Runthrough NS guidewire failed.To retrieve the fragment, we sequentially advanced JR3.5, 6FBL3.0, and 6FJR3.5 guiding catheters via the right radial arterial sheath to the proximal stump, then tried advancing a Cross-IT XTR guidewire along them—this maneuver remained unsuccessful.Subsequently, an inferior vena cava (IVC) filter retriever via the right radial sheath failed to snare the proximal stump. We then constructed a custom snare by looping a Runthrough NS guidewire at an extension catheter tip, and retrieved the fragment via balloon-assisted counter-traction.Postoperatively, the patient was stable without complications. Dual antiplatelet therapy and follow-up were initiated, with no adverse events at 1-month follow-up.ConclusionThis minimally invasive technique obviates surgery, shows promise for intravascular foreign body management, and provides insights for adapting to failed conventional strategies in complex cases.

Introduction

Coronary angiography, the gold standard for coronary artery disease diagnosis, is widely used globally with excellent safety. The transradial approach is increasingly preferred in clinical practice due to benefits like improved patient comfort, shorter recovery, and reduced bleeding risk [1].

Guidewire fracture and entrapment is an extremely rare yet clinically impactful complication of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), with a reported incidence of approximately 0.1 %–0.2 % in the literature [2]. Despite its low occurrence, this complication poses considerable hazards. Catheter-based retrieval strategies are generally prioritized over surgical extraction, with commonly employed techniques including stent jailing, snare loop retrieval, wire tangling, microcatheter support and balloon inflation [3]. Failed catheter-based retrieval attempts often necessitate more invasive interventions.

We herein present our clinical experience regarding the successful retrieval of a fractured angiographic catheter fragment using a balloon-assisted counter-traction technique in accordance with the CARE (Case Report) reporting checklist.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old female with 7-year hypertension (BP stably controlled on medication) had 3-month recurrent exertional angina (Canadian Cardiovascular Society [CCS] class II): retrosternal tightness, dyspnea, left shoulder/arm numbness, relieved by rest or vasodilators. ECG showed non-specific T-wave changes; troponin was normal. She received medication after admission and was scheduled for coronary angiography.

Routine disinfection/draping and right radial artery puncture under local anesthesia were performed, with anticoagulation using 100 U/kg unfractionated heparin (UFH) and nitroglycerin 200 μg. A 6F arterial sheath (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted, and a TIG multipurpose catheter (APT Medical, Xiangyang, China) was advanced to the aortic sinus via a J-tip guidewire (Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT, USA) for selective left coronary angiography. Despite repeated attempts at adjustment, left coronary ostial access could not be achieved, likely due to the presence of coronary artery spasm. Therefore, right coronary angiography was performed for diagnostic purposes, which revealed mild 10–20 % stenosis and TIMI 3 flow.

Subsequent repositioning attempts still failed to reach the left coronary ostium. Fluoroscopy confirmed the TIG catheter had fractured ∼8 cm proximal to the right brachial artery's ulnar-radial bifurcation (Fig. 1). After confirming no vascular injury, repeated initial attempts to advance a Runthrough NS guidewire (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) through the fractured catheter lumen for retrieval were unsuccessful. To retrieve the fractured segment, we first advanced a JR3.5 guiding catheter (JR3.5GC, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) via the right radial sheath to the proximal stump in the right brachial artery, then tried advancing a Cross-IT XTR guidewire (Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) along it into the stump lumen—this failed. Sequential replacement with 6FBL3.0 guiding catheter (6FBL3.0GC, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) and 6FJR3.5 guiding catheter (6FJR3.5GC, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) also failed to advance the guidewire into the stump.

Subsequently, an IVC filter retriever (Becton Dickinson Bard, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) advanced via the right radial sheath failed to snare the fractured catheter stump. Thus, a homemade snare was made by looping a Runthrough NS guidewire at an extension catheter tip; a Mave 2.0 × 15 mm balloon (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) inflated to 12ATM shaped the loop (Fig. 2).Fig. 2A homemade snare was constructed by looping a Runthrough NS guidewire around the tip of an extension catheter, with the snare loop indicated by the arrow.Fig. 2

The homemade snare was advanced to the fracture site, and after careful manipulation, the stump was grasped (Video 1). An XTR guidewire was advanced to the aortic arch, then the snare was withdrawn. A Firefighter 1.0 × 10 mm balloon (MicroPort Scientific Corporation, Shanghai, China) inflated to 20 ATM dilated the vessel, then a Mave 2.0 × 20 mm balloon (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) inflated to 20 ATM facilitated catheter removal via balloon-assisted counter-traction under fluoroscopy (Fig. 3).

Postoperatively, the patient was hemodynamically stable with no complications; 1-month follow-up ultrasound of right brachial/radial arteries showed no abnormalities.Fig. 3A Mave 2.0 × 20 mm balloon inflated to 20 ATM facilitated catheter removal via balloon-assisted counter-traction.Fig. 3

Discussion

Although catheter fracture is uncommon in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), it can lead to severe consequences. Fractured fragments may occlude coronary arteries or cerebral vessels (inducing myocardial infarction or stroke), trigger thrombosis, cause vascular injury (e.g., dissection, perforation), or pose a potential infection risk [[4], [5], [6]]. Such cases typically require reintervention or surgical intervention. Given the widespread use of coronary angiography, even though the incidence of its complications is relatively low, such complications should not be overlooked—underscoring the need for clinicians to maintain a high level of vigilance.

Coronary catheter fracture has diverse and complex etiologies. First, catheter materials have an inherent vulnerability to torsional or bending stress, as well as high-pressure expansion—factors that can thereby increase the risk of fracture [6]. Second, anatomical factors—such as abnormal coronary ostia and tortuous or calcified vessels—complicate catheter manipulation, thereby elevating fracture risk [7]. Third, forced catheter withdrawal (e.g., when encountering resistance or entrapment by vascular spasm or calcified plaques) is another key contributor [1]. In the present case, vascular spasm, complex vascular anatomy, and repeated attempts to access the left coronary ostium likely collectively increased the risk of catheter fracture.

Upon detection of intraprocedural catheter fracture, emergency management protocols should be initiated promptly. The first step involves using fluoroscopy to accurately locate the fractured fragments—while simultaneously documenting their size and shape—and comprehensively assess for vascular injuries, such as contrast extravasation.

Percutaneous retrieval is typically the first-line approach, with three commonly used techniques: 1) Snare capture, where a snare is percutaneously advanced to ensnare the fragment before careful withdrawal [3,8]; 2) Dual-wire entanglement, in which two guidewires of different calibers stabilize the fragment's distal end via entanglement or hooking [9]; 3) Balloon-assisted retrieval involves advancing a small balloon catheter along a guidewire to the fractured fragment, followed by partial inflation of the balloon to anchor the fragment and prevent its migration with blood flow [10]. In emergency scenarios, operators may flexibly combine these techniques to enhance retrieval success rates [6]; if percutaneous retrieval attempts fail, urgent surgical intervention should be promptly performed.

Here, initial attempts to retrieve the fractured catheter using standard guidewires or snares failed, attributed to guidewire-catheter mismatch, vascular occlusion induced by the fractured fragment, and aggravated vascular spasm. We innovatively shaped a Runthrough NS guidewire into a snare loop at the tip of an extension catheter, which was secured by inflating a Mave 2.0 × 15 mm balloon to 12 atmospheres (atm). This custom-made retrieval device—used under fluoroscopic guidance and strict force control—precisely captured the fractured fragment, which was then successfully retrieved via balloon-assisted traction. Postoperative ultrasound examination confirmed the absence of residual fragments and ruled out vascular complications, thereby obviating the need for unnecessary surgical intervention.

Conclusion

A comprehensive understanding of coronary catheter fracture's etiologies and hazards, along with formulating personalized retrieval strategies for specific clinical scenarios, is clinically significant for clinicians; our experience shows custom-made retrieval devices are a viable option for complex catheter-related complications, helping avoid unnecessary surgery and optimize care. Notably, the custom snare loop in this case was temporarily fabricated to address an emergency—while developed primarily from clinicians' experience, it lacked scientific measurements and precise design, so more in-depth research will be needed to expand such devices' clinical application.
