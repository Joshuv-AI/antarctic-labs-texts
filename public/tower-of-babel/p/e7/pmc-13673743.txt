Writing about the disrupted self: a randomized controlled trial of a structured online expressive writing intervention for bereaved Chinese adults
Authors: Junlong Zheng, Dongpeng Yao, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisruption and restoration of the self are theorized to be central to bereavement adjustment, yet previous interventions rarely address self-disruption directly. This study examined whether an online expressive writing intervention targeting self-disruption could alleviate grief symptoms in bereaved adults.MethodsIn a two-arm randomized controlled trial, 66 bereaved participants were randomly assigned to an intervention group (n = 35) or a waitlist control group (n = 31). The intervention consisted of six sessions (one psychoeducational session and five structured expressive writing sessions) over three weeks, focusing on four aspects of self-disruption, namely: Loss of Self-Direction, Self-Derailment, Self-Devaluation, and Disconnection of Relational-Self. Grief severity was assessed using the PG-13-R at baseline and post-intervention, with a six-month follow-up for the intervention group.ResultsCompared with the waitlist control group, the intervention group showed a significantly greater reduction in grief severity (Cohen’s d = − 0.93). Within the intervention group, the improvement remained stable at the six-month follow-up.ConclusionOnline expressive writing targeting self-disruption in bereaved adults may provide an effective, low-cost, and scalable intervention for grief. However, in the absence of a validated measure of self-disruption, whether the intervention exerts its effects through changes in the self remains untested.Trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration Number ChiCTR2600120661, Date 18.03.2026).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02210-0.

Background

Throughout the lifespan, individuals encounter various forms of loss, among which the death of a loved one is particularly profound. Bereaved individuals constitute a substantial population. According to United Nations data, approximately 61.7 million deaths occurred globally in 2023 [1]. Estimating an average of nine bereaved individuals per death [2], the affected population worldwide exceeds 500 million. The loss of a loved one frequently precipitates a range of adverse physical and psychological outcomes [3]. Physiologically, bereavement is associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, cortisol dysregulation, and increased overall mortality [3–6]. Psychologically, approximately 10% of bereaved individuals develop prolonged grief disorder (PGD) [7], which is characterized by intense yearning for the deceased, preoccupation with thoughts or memories of the lost loved one, and accompanying emotional distress, meaninglessness, and profound loneliness [8]. Furthermore, bereaved individuals show increased susceptibility to depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and sleep disturbances, along with an elevated risk of suicide [4, 9–12].

Research suggests that following the death of a loved one, the bereaved individual’s sense of self is often disrupted or impaired, and restoration of the self constitutes a key task in grief adaptation. For example, the cognitive attachment model proposes that when a bereaved individual’s identity is largely constructed around the deceased, the risk of developing prolonged grief disorder increases. A central task during bereavement is therefore to revise one’s identity to incorporate the reality of the loss, allowing the development of new goals and roles [13]. The meaning reconstruction model similarly posits that bereavement disrupts an individual’s existing meaning structures and that rebuilding a coherent meaning system involves identity change, that is, developing new aspects of the self or new roles to regain a sense of meaning in life [14]. The cognitive-behavioral model highlights negative self-related beliefs, such as self-blame and guilt, as important maintaining factors of complicated grief [15]. In addition, Worden’s theory of mourning tasks suggests that a key task of mourning is to develop a new sense of self in a world without the deceased [16]. Consistent with these theoretical accounts, a recent qualitative study drawing on clinicians’ perspectives also identified self-related disturbances, such as identity disruption and low self-esteem, as clinically relevant risk factors for PGD [17]. Moreover, recent network analyses indicate that self-related symptoms occupy central positions in grief symptom networks, including role confusion [18–20] and the feeling that a part of oneself has died [21].

Taken together, both theoretical and empirical evidence highlight the disruption and restoration of the self as central processes in grief. However, existing grief interventions have predominantly been based on cognitive models [15, 22]. In a meta-analysis of online grief interventions, the included studies were exclusively based on cognitive behavioral therapy [23]. Such interventions typically focus on correcting catastrophic interpretations of the loss and avoidance behaviors through techniques such as cognitive restructuring and exposure, while paying relatively little attention to disruptions and restoration of the bereaved self. The feasibility of targeting the bereaved self as a focus of intervention therefore remains to be explored.

Internet-based interventions have emerged as a promising approach for delivering mental health services. Compared to traditional face-to-face modalities, they offer lower barriers to access, greater scalability, flexibility in time and location, as well as enhanced privacy and anonymity [24]. These advantages are particularly relevant in the Chinese context. China has a remarkably large bereaved population—with 10.93 million recorded deaths in 2024 [25] and an estimated nine bereaved individuals per death [2], the number of bereaved individuals approached 100 million in 2024 alone. Among this population, the prevalence of prolonged grief disorder (PGD) is 8.9%, and the prevalence of prolonged grief (PG) symptoms is 32.4% [26], indicating a substantial demand for mental health services. However, there is currently a severe shortage of mental health professionals in China [27], and only a small proportion have received systematic training in grief therapy and counseling [28]. Bereaved individuals also face additional barriers, including geographical restrictions, limited financial resources, and social stigma [28]. In this context, internet-based interventions represent a particularly viable strategy for delivering accessible grief support in China [29]. Meta-analytic evidence has demonstrated that online interventions exert a significant therapeutic effect on grief (g = 0.54) [24]. However, research on internet-based grief interventions in China remains scarce [30, 31], highlighting an urgent need for further empirical investigation.

A form of intervention that is particularly well-suited to online delivery is expressive writing (EW). Originating from the “written emotional disclosure” paradigm proposed by Pennebaker, EW involves having individuals express their deepest thoughts and emotions surrounding a major stressful or traumatic event during relatively short, consecutive writing sessions [32]. It is a low-cost, relatively simple, and easily implementable intervention [33]. Expressive writing is inherently compatible with internet-based interventions: given its self-directed nature, it does not rely on frequent, immediate face-to-face interactions, rendering it naturally conducive to web-based implementation. Furthermore, the enhanced anonymity and privacy characteristic of online interventions can effectively reduce participants’ psychological defenses and apprehensions regarding self-disclosure [34], thereby increasing their level of engagement and the depth of their emotional expression. However, a recent meta-analysis indicated that previous EW interventions for bereaved individuals yielded an overall small effect size (g = 0.388), and that structured writing with explicit themes and targets produced superior outcomes compared to unstructured approaches [35]. This finding suggests that, to maximize therapeutic benefits, internet-based EW interventions for bereaved individuals should adopt a structured format with clearly defined thematic guidance.

Based on this rationale, the present study aimed to examine the effectiveness of an online expressive writing intervention targeting the bereaved self. From an intervention perspective, this study explores the feasibility of targeting the bereaved self as a focus of grief intervention, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of the role of the self in the process of grief adaptation. Moreover, the online expressive writing format used in this study is simple, low-cost, and highly accessible, offering a potentially scalable approach for expanding the reach and accessibility of grief intervention services.

Methods

Design

This study employed a two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) design (intervention group vs. waitlist control group) conducted online. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were assigned to either the immediate intervention group or the waitlist control group using simple randomization. Randomization was performed by the second author using a computer-generated random number sequence created in Microsoft Excel. Due to the nature of the intervention, allocation concealment was not implemented. However, participants were not explicitly informed of the group allocation or the study hypotheses. Instead, the study was presented as a two-phase intervention program, and participants were informed only of the phase to which they were assigned.

Upon completion of the baseline assessment (T0), the intervention group received the intervention immediately. Given the extended 6-month follow-up period of this study, and out of ethical considerations, the waitlist group was provided with the intervention immediately after the intervention group completed their post-test, rather than withholding the treatment until the completion of the follow-up assessment. Consequently, by the follow-up phase, both groups had received the intervention, precluding between-group comparisons. Therefore, follow-up data were collected solely from the intervention group.

All participants completed online questionnaires prior to the intervention (T0) and either immediately post-intervention or following a three-week waiting period (T1). Furthermore, the intervention group completed an additional follow-up questionnaire at 6 months post-baseline (T2). Data from the T0, T1, and T2 time points were included and analyzed in this study.

Recruitment

Participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged over 18 years; (2) having experienced the death of a close relative more than one month prior to enrollment; (3) not simultaneously participating in other intervention studies; (4) not diagnosed with any other psychiatric disorders; and (5) not taking medications for psychiatric conditions. The exclusion criteria were: (1) inability to read Chinese; (2) inability to use the internet normally; and (3) lack of writing ability. Participants were recruited between December 2023 and February 2024. Recruitment posters were disseminated through several online platforms, including WeChat public accounts, bereavement mutual-support groups on Xiaohongshu, bereavement communities on Douban, and the online platform Missing Starry Sky. Following the principle of voluntary participation, bereaved individuals who were interested in the study scanned the QR code on the recruitment poster to register. After reading and signing the informed consent form, participants completed and submitted the baseline questionnaire. Subsequently, the researchers screened the applicants according to the aforementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria. Prior to data analysis, all demographic information was anonymized to protect participants’ privacy. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Renmin University of China before data collection, including the primary outcome and analysis plan (Ethics Approval No. 23–079). The trial was subsequently registered retrospectively with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2600120661).

Intervention

The intervention was a structured, self-guided online expressive writing program consisting of six sessions delivered over three weeks. It was developed based on relevant literature on self-concept and grief adaptation [36–43], combined with the research team’s prior intervention experience and consideration of the cultural characteristics of self-construal in the Chinese context. Based on this framework, the intervention was organized into four thematic components, each designed to address a theoretically derived domain of self-disruption.

Loss of Self-Direction. This component was designed to address difficulties in future orientation and life direction after bereavement. Participants were guided to reflect on how the loss had affected their goals, plans, and sense of purpose. Writing tasks encouraged them to identify personally meaningful short-term and long-term goals, consider possible future life pathways, and develop concrete action steps. The writing tasks emphasized reflection on future direction and continuity between present experiences and future aspirations.

Self-Derailment. This component was designed to address perceived disruptions in self-continuity and identity. Participants were asked to reflect on who they were before the loss and how they perceive themselves currently. Writing exercises invited them to identify perceived changes and continuities in the self, reflect on the relationship between past and present identities, and construct a coherent personal narrative. The task was intended to encourage reflection on self-continuity and identity change following bereavement.

Self-Devaluation. This component was designed to address negative self-evaluation, including self-blame, guilt, and reduced self-worth. Participants were guided to identify critical self-beliefs and emotionally distressing self-related thoughts. Writing tasks encouraged cognitive reframing, self-compassion, and recognition of personal strengths and coping abilities. The writing tasks were intended to encourage a more balanced and supportive reflection on the self.

Disconnection of Relational-Self. This component was conceptualized as a culturally informed domain of self-disruption within the Chinese context. It was designed to address perceived disruptions in interpersonal connectedness and the relational aspect of the self. In the Chinese cultural context, the self is commonly conceptualized as interdependent and embedded within social relationships, rather than as an independent, isolated entity [44, 45]. From this perspective, close relationships constitute an integral part of the self. Accordingly, disruptions in significant relationships following bereavement may reflect not only interpersonal changes but also disturbances in the structure of the self. Based on this understanding, this component was included as a domain of self-disruption. Participants were guided to reflect on changes in their relationships and sense of connection with others following the loss. Writing tasks encouraged them to identify available social resources, consider possible ways of reconnecting with others, and explore meaningful interpersonal roles. The writing tasks were intended to encourage reflection on social resources, interpersonal roles, and possible pathways toward interpersonal reconnection.

Based on these four themes, six intervention sessions were designed (see Table 1). Detailed instructions for each session are provided in Supplementary Material 1. The first session consisted of psychoeducation delivered via video, providing participants with foundational knowledge about grief and addressing common misconceptions regarding grief recovery. The remaining sessions were conducted as structured expressive writing tasks corresponding to the four themes. In the final session, participants completed an integrative writing exercise in which they reflected on previous sessions and wrote a future-oriented narrative to integrate reflections across the four themes. The intervention was delivered over a three-week period, with two sessions per week. Each expressive-writing session lasted approximately 20–30 min. All procedures were conducted via the WeChat platform. Participants who consented to the study signed an informed consent form after being briefed on the study procedures. For each writing session, participants read the provided instructions and completed the writing task accordingly. Completed writings were submitted to the researcher either as a photograph of handwritten text or as a Word document, with submissions due within one day. Reminder messages were sent at 20:00 on the day of the session, and at 10:00 and 20:00 the following day if the submission had not yet been received.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power. Based on previous studies [46–49], the effect size was set at a medium level (f = 0.25), with statistical power of 0.80 and a significance level of α = 0.05. The power analysis was conducted based on repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) [48].

Two primary statistical analyses were planned in the present study. First, for the 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA examining pre–post changes between two groups, the estimated minimum total sample size required was 34 participants. Second, for the one-way repeated-measures ANOVA with three time points within the intervention group, the estimated required sample size for the intervention group was 28 participants, corresponding to a total sample size of 56 participants. Considering both analytical approaches, the larger sample size requirement was adopted as the minimum sample size for the study, resulting in a required total sample of 56 participants.

Measures

(1) Outcome Measure: The primary outcome, prolonged grief, was assessed using the Chinese version of the Prolonged Grief Disorder-13-Revised (PG-13-R) scale. The original PG-13-R consists of 13 items [50], and the present study used its validated Chinese version [51]. Ten items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (completely), yielding a total score between 10 and 50. A total score of ≥ 30, together with self-reported functional impairment and a time since loss of more than 12 months, indicates a high risk of meeting diagnostic criteria for prolonged grief disorder (PGD). In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for the scale at T0 was 0.888.

(2) Demographic and Loss-Related Characteristics: Participants provided demographic and loss-related information via a self-report questionnaire. The assessed variables included gender, age, educational level, marital status, religious beliefs, time since loss (in months), relationship to the deceased, cause of death, and perceived closeness to the deceased. Perceived closeness to the deceased was assessed using the Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (IOS; [52]), a single-item pictorial measure in which participants select one of seven pairs of increasingly overlapping circles representing the self and the deceased. Greater overlap indicates greater perceived closeness to the deceased, with scores ranging from 1 to 7.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention on prolonged grief (PG), linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were employed. The analysis comprised two parts: a two-group pre–post analysis and a three-time-point analysis (pre-test, post-test, and follow-up) conducted exclusively for the intervention group.

(1) Two-Group Pre–Post Analysis: An LMM was constructed with PG as the dependent variable. Group (intervention vs. waitlist control) and time (pre-test vs. post-test) were included as fixed effects, and participant ID was specified as a random intercept. During model building, the inclusion of a random slope and the group × time interaction term was evaluated, and only terms that significantly improved model fit were retained in the final model. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d. Following methodological recommendations [53] and prior research [48], Cohen’s d was computed by dividing the unstandardized regression coefficient of the group × time interaction by the pooled standard deviation of baseline scores. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional thresholds (0.2 = small, 0.5 = moderate, ≥ 0.8 = large). Baseline differences between groups were examined using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Variables showing significant baseline differences were included as covariates in subsequent models.

(2) Three-Time-Point Analysis for the Intervention Group: An LMM was constructed with PG as the dependent variable, time (pre-test, post-test, follow-up) as a fixed effect, and participant ID as a random intercept. Model fit was compared between treating time as a continuous variable and using dummy coding, and the better-fitting model was selected. The inclusion of a random slope was also evaluated and retained only if it improved model fit. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated by dividing the unstandardized regression coefficient for time by the standard deviation of baseline scores. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, with the same interpretation criteria for effect sizes. All analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Missing data were handled using maximum likelihood estimation. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 and SPSS version 29.0.

Results

Sample characteristics

Of 108 individuals who registered for the study, 42 did not proceed to participation because they did not respond (n = 40) or reported time conflicts (n = 2). The remaining 66 participants provided written informed consent, completed the baseline assessment (T0), and were randomized to the intervention group (n = 35) or the waitlist control group (n = 31). At post-test (T1), outcome data were available for 20 participants in the intervention group and 22 participants in the waitlist control group; 15 and 9 participants, respectively, did not complete the post-test assessment. All 20 intervention-group participants who completed the post-test assessment were retained at the 6-month follow-up (T2). Reasons for attrition are shown in the corresponding boxes. All 66 randomized participants were included in the intention-to-treat analyses.

Sample characteristics at T0 (N = 66)

At post-test (T1), outcome data were available for 20 participants in the intervention group and 22 participants in the waitlist control group; 24 participants (36.4%) did not complete the post-test assessment, including 15 participants in the intervention group and 9 in the waitlist control group. Attrition was descriptively higher in the intervention group (42.9%) than in the waitlist control group (29.0%), although this difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.310).

At baseline, the intervention and waitlist control groups did not differ significantly in demographic characteristics. However, baseline grief severity was significantly lower in the intervention group (M = 36.43) than in the waitlist control group (M = 41.03), t = − 2.43, p = 0.018. Completers and non-completers did not differ significantly on any measured baseline variable (all ps > 0.10; see Supplementary Material 2). In addition, Little’s MCAR test was non-significant, χ² (4) = 1.44, p = 0.837. In logistic regression analyses predicting dropout, neither treatment condition (OR = 2.41, 95% CI [0.81, 7.77], p = 0.123) nor baseline grief severity (OR = 1.53, 95% CI [0.88, 2.78], p = 0.143) was a statistically significant predictor.

At follow-up (T2), data were obtained from all 20 intervention-group participants who completed the post-test assessment.

Intervention effects (Pre- and Post-Test)

Given the significant baseline difference in grief levels between the intervention and waitlist control groups, baseline grief scores were included as a covariate in the analysis.

Baseline grief scores were mean-centered and entered into a linear mixed-effects model. The model did not converge when random slopes were included; therefore, following the principle of model simplification [54], only a random intercept was retained. The inclusion of the Time × Group interaction significantly improved model fit (Δχ² (1) = 20.82, p < 0.001), and this interaction term was retained in the final model.

As shown in Table 3, a significant Time × Group interaction effect was observed (B = − 7.11, p < 0.001), indicating that the intervention group exhibited a significantly greater reduction in grief levels at post-test compared with the waitlist control group. The corresponding effect size, calculated using the baseline standard deviation, was Cohen’s d = − 0.93, indicating a large effect. The main effect of baseline grief (mean-centered) was also significant (B = 0.85, p < 0.001), suggesting that higher baseline grief levels were associated with higher grief levels at subsequent assessments.

Intervention effects within the intervention group (Pre-test, Post-test, Follow-up)

Time was first entered into the linear mixed-effects model as a continuous variable and then as dummy-coded variables to compare model fit between the two approaches. When treated as a continuous variable, time was assigned values based on the measurement intervals: pre-test = 0, post-test (three weeks) = 0.7, and follow-up (six months) = 6. When dummy-coded, two dummy variables were created to represent the post-test and follow-up, with pre-test as the reference category. Model comparison indicated a better fit for the dummy-coded model (ΔAIC = 3.98); therefore, this specification was adopted for subsequent analyses.

In this model, participant ID was included as a random intercept. The inclusion of random slopes for the time dummy variables did not significantly improve model fit, as indicated by a likelihood ratio test (Δχ² (2) = 2.44, p = 0.296); therefore, only the random intercept was retained in the final model.

As shown in Table 4, a significant main effect of time was observed. Compared with the baseline reference, scores at post-test were significantly lower than at pre-test (B = − 7.24, p < 0.001), and scores at follow-up were also significantly lower than at pre-test (B = − 7.14, p < 0.001). Effect size analyses indicated that the effect size from pre-test to post-test was Cohen’s d = − 0.97, and from pre-test to follow-up was Cohen’s d = − 0.96, both representing large effects.

To further compare differences between follow-up and other time points, the model was refitted with follow-up as the reference category. The results showed that pre-test scores were significantly higher than follow-up scores (B = 7.14, p < 0.001), with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.96. There was no significant difference between post-test and follow-up scores (B = − 0.10, p = 0.955), with an effect size of Cohen’s d = − 0.01.

Discussion

The current study conducted a three-week online intervention based on expressive writing, specifically targeting the self among bereaved individuals. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that, compared to a waitlist control group, the self-targeted online expressive writing intervention yielded significant improvements with large effect sizes in the grief levels of bereaved individuals at post-treatment. Furthermore, follow-up assessments of the intervention group indicated that these therapeutic effects were stably maintained at six months. In conclusion, the findings suggest that self-targeted online expressive writing might be an effective approach for alleviating grief in bereaved individuals.

The effect size observed in the present study was substantial, exceeding the overall effect of online grief interventions reported in meta-analyses (SMD = -0.30, 95% CI [-0.40, -0.19]) [55] and the average effect size of expressive writing interventions for bereaved individuals (Hedges’ g = 0.39, 95% CI [0.19, 0.59]) [35]. The relatively large effect size may be considered in relation to two features of the intervention design. First, disruption and restoration of the self occupy a central position in the process of grief adaptation, as articulated in prior theoretical models [13–16] and empirical research [18–21]. Second, the large effect size may also be related to the structured nature of the writing protocol. In a recent meta-analysis of expressive writing interventions for grief, Yao et al. [35] found that structured writing yielded a substantially higher effect size (Hedges’ g = 0.583) compared to emotional disclosure (Hedges’ g = 0.137) and themed writing (Hedges’ g = 0.121), suggesting that what participants write about is more important than the act of writing per se. In the present study, rather than simply inviting participants to freely disclose their emotions, the intervention provided clearly defined structural themes—Loss of Self-Direction, Self-Derailment, Self-Devaluation, and Disconnection of Relational-Self—that guided participants to systematically confront specific dimensions of their impaired self. Compared to unstructured emotional disclosure paradigms [56, 57], such structured guidance may have enabled more focused cognitive and emotional processing of grief-relevant content.

In terms of intervention costs, compared to therapist-administered online interventions [58–60], self-guided online courses with therapist feedback [47, 48, 61], and self-help online writing interventions involving therapist feedback [62–64], the present study requires minimal professional input, thereby substantially reducing implementation costs and lowering barriers to large-scale dissemination.

At the 6-month follow-up, results indicated a significant improvement in grief levels in the intervention group compared to baseline, with no significant difference observed between post-treatment and follow-up assessments (using follow-up as the reference: pre-treatment vs. follow-up, B = 7.144, p < 0.001; post-treatment vs. follow-up, B = − 0.10, p = 0.955). This finding suggests that the improvements observed within the intervention group remained stable over the six-month period. This pattern is consistent with the sustained follow-up effects reported in previous online grief interventions [47, 48, 61, 65] and a recent meta-analysis [55]. A plausible explanation for this enduring effect is that, rather than facilitating mere emotional catharsis, the structured, self-targeted writing themes in the present study may have assisted bereaved individuals in repairing and reconstructing their impaired self-concept. Such a process of self-repair and identity reconstruction—in which the reality of the loss is integrated into a coherent life narrative and disrupted self-cognitions are gradually revised—may yield durable, cross-temporal benefits.

Compared with mainstream cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) approaches, the present intervention shares overlaps in both content and technique, yet differs in its underlying conceptualization of the therapeutic target. On the one hand, several themes addressed in this study—such as Self-Devaluation, Loss of Self-Direction, and Self-Derailment—map onto core constructs within cognitive-behavioral models of grief, including impaired autobiographical memory integration and negative self-beliefs [15]. Likewise, expressive writing may function, at least in part, as a form of emotional exposure. On the other hand, the present intervention departs from CBT in a critical way: rather than targeting discrete cognitive or behavioral components, it conceptualizes self-disruption as a higher-order, structural disturbance of the self. In cognitive-behavioral frameworks, prolonged grief is primarily understood as being maintained by maladaptive information processing and avoidance, with interventions aimed at modifying specific beliefs or reducing avoidance behaviors. In contrast, the self-disruption framework adopted here emphasizes a more global breakdown of the self-system, spanning temporal continuity (past–present–future integration), evaluative processes (self-worth and self-definition), and relational embeddedness (the social and interpersonal self). Accordingly, the hypothesized mechanism of change in the present intervention is not limited to correcting dysfunctional cognitions or reducing avoidance. Instead, the intervention was designed to facilitate the reorganization of the individual’s self-narrative and identity structure, with the aim of restoring coherence, continuity, and meaning.

A notable limitation is the relatively high attrition rate at post-treatment: 24 of 66 participants (36.4%) did not complete the assessment, including 15 of 35 (42.9%) in the intervention group, which exceeds the 22.63% average reported for online grief interventions [55]. All primary analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle and used maximum-likelihood estimation in linear mixed-effects models, which incorporates all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption. In addition, post-hoc analyses found no statistically significant associations between dropout and treatment condition, measured baseline characteristics, or baseline grief severity (Results), providing some reassurance that attrition was not systematically related to observed variables. However, the modest sample size limited power to detect predictors of dropout, and the analyses could assess only measured variables; thus, unmeasured determinants of dropout and a missing-not-at-random mechanism cannot be excluded. Accordingly, given the extent of missing outcome data, the estimated intervention effect should still be interpreted cautiously. The pattern and magnitude of attrition may partly reflect the unguided, home-based format of the intervention. In expressive-writing RCTs for grief, therapist-guided interventions have reported attrition rates of approximately 7–22% [60, 62, 64, 66, 67], whereas unguided home-based interventions have reported rates of 35–56% [61, 68, 69], a range that includes the present intervention. Among the 15 intervention-group participants who discontinued, five ceased responding, seven did not submit the writing tasks on time, and only three reported feeling too overwhelmed by distress to continue writing. This pattern is consistent with evidence that self-guided interventions tend to have higher dropout rates [70] and that the absence of feedback and individualized support may contribute to disengagement [71, 72]. Although expressive writing can involve a temporary increase in distress [73, 74], guided formats allow participants to receive acknowledgment, reassurance, and support in processing difficult emotions [75, 76]. In an unguided format, by contrast, participants may need to manage both the emotional demands of the writing tasks and practical difficulties in sustaining engagement on their own. Future studies should examine whether low-intensity support strategies can improve retention while preserving the accessibility and low cost of self-guided delivery. One possibility is minimal automated feedback, such as brief empathic responses or tailored encouragement generated by large language models, as illustrated by Kishimoto et al. [77]. Such approaches should be evaluated carefully for their effects on engagement, safety, and clinical outcomes. At present, this format may be most appropriate for bereaved individuals who are willing to engage in self-guided writing and have sufficient time and emotional resources to do so. Individuals experiencing more severe symptoms or difficulty sustaining engagement may benefit from more structured, guided, or professionally supported formats.

Beyond attrition, several limitations remain. First, because no comprehensive and psychometrically validated instrument is currently available to capture the multidimensional nature of self-disruption in bereavement, we were unable to assess this construct directly or to conduct mediation analyses testing whether reductions in grief were mediated by changes in self-disruption; we are developing the Self-Disruption in Bereavement Scale to address this gap. Second, the waitlist control did not account for nonspecific influences, including treatment expectancy, intervention-related attention, and repeated writing. Future trials should use an active writing control condition matched for writing dose, reminder contact, and treatment rationale to determine whether the self-related content confers benefits beyond writing itself. Moreover, because the waitlist group was treated after the post-test, the 6-month follow-up was uncontrolled, and the durability of gains should be regarded as preliminary. Third, to limit questionnaire burden we did not assess depression or PTSD, which often co-occur with grief [48, 61, 62], reducing the comprehensiveness of the findings. Fourth, the sample was predominantly female (86%), highly educated, largely non-religious, and mostly bereaved of a parent—a profile consistent with prior expressive-writing RCTs for grief [60–62] and likely reflecting women’s greater help-seeking and the literacy demands of writing-based interventions, with the low religiosity mirroring the broader Chinese population. The small sample also precluded moderation analyses, so we could not test whether efficacy varies by baseline grief, gender, education, religion, or relationship to, and closeness with, the deceased—relevant given that different losses (e.g., of a parent, spouse, or child) may not be phenomenologically equivalent—questions that larger, more heterogeneous samples should address, advancing cross-cultural research on grief. Fifth, the small sample led randomization to yield significantly lower baseline grief in the intervention group; although this was adjusted for as a covariate, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, and the resulting estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution. Sixth, the trial was registered retrospectively; although the primary outcome and analysis plan had been pre-specified in the ethics-approved protocol, retrospective registration cannot fully exclude selective outcome reporting. Future trials in this line of work should be prospectively registered before recruitment begins. Seventh, the culturally grounded “Disconnection of Relational-Self” dimension requires cross-cultural validation.

Conclusion

The present study provides preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of a structured, self-guided online expressive writing intervention designed around self-related difficulties in bereaved Chinese adults. Compared with the waitlist control group, participants who received the intervention showed a significant reduction in prolonged grief symptoms, and within the intervention group these improvements remained stable at the 6-month follow-up. From a theoretical perspective, the findings provide initial interventional support for the value of organizing grief intervention around self-related difficulties, thereby extending existing theoretical accounts that emphasize self-disruption in grief adaptation. From a practical perspective, this intervention offers a potentially accessible, cost-effective, and scalable approach to grief support. Future studies with larger samples, active control conditions, and validated measures of self-disruption are needed to examine whether changes in self-disruption mediate intervention effects and to further clarify for whom and under what conditions this approach is most beneficial.
