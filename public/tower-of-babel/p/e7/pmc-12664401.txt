Evaluation of Plasmodium falciparum PSSP17 essentiality in human-to-mosquito transmission and its prospective candidacy as a biomarker
Authors: B. López-Gutiérrez, N. Hathaway, L. Alonso-Palomares, V.O. Nyasembe, M. Kato, T. Hamerly, J.B. Parr, J.J. Juliano, M. Harbers, R.R. Dinglasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryReliance on detecting the Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 (PfHRP2) biomarker by current rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) is compromised by the emergence of pfhrp2/3 deletion strains that evade detection and treatment. Additional biomarkers can arguably expand the diagnostic toolbox, but these new biomarkers must be essential for parasite development to avoid similar outcomes to PfHRP2. Recently, Plasmodium sexual stage protein 17 (PSSP17; PF3D7_1218800) was identified in saliva from children with subclinical infections, offering a non-invasive modality for malaria diagnosis. However, the essentiality of PSSP17 has not been determined. We show that pssp17 is dispensable in P. falciparum asexual stages but is important for onward transmission by mosquitoes. Importantly, the 19,280 publicly available P. falciparum genomes did not reveal naturally occurring deletions. Thus, should Δpssp17 mutants arise, mosquitoes cannot effectively transmit them. These data suggest that the further consideration of PSSP17 as an RDT biomarker for non-invasive, saliva-based sensitive RDTs is warranted.

Introduction

Since 2015, reductions in the malaria disease burden have plateaued, suggesting that our current tools for malaria control and prevention are insufficient. Novel enabling technologies are needed for the identification and treatment of the subclinical reservoir of malaria parasites. These undetected parasites can infect mosquitoes and drive local transmission despite extensive control measures.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 The difficulty in detecting and quantifying these carriers with low parasite densities using field-deployable tools hinders our quantification of the infectious reservoir (i.e., size of the parasitized reservoir population). The majority of the RDTs used for the diagnosis of malaria infections target PfHRP2. However, these diagnostics are further challenged by the emergence of natural parasite deletions of the phrp2 and pfhrp3 (pfhrp2/3) genes among parasites circulating in the Horn of Africa that escape detection by widely used blood-based RDTs that are designed to detect the PfHRP2 antigen in blood.8,9,10,11,12,13,14 Although alternative RDTs are available, such as those detecting the parasite lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH), they exhibit a lower sensitivity.15

It is well known that the sexual parasite stage responsible for transmission from humans to mosquitoes via P. falciparum gametocytes does not cause classical symptoms of the disease. While some asymptomatic infections are detectable by microscopy, submicroscopic infections with either or both asexual stages and gametocytes do not result in clinical patency. Consequently, these asymptomatic individuals do not present at a clinic nor receive antimalarial drugs to clear the infection.2,3,6,8,12 However, “asymptomatic infection,” may be a misnomer, considering growing evidence for cognitive and subpatent physiological impacts.16 Plasmodium sexual stage protein 17 (PSSP17; PlasmoDB: PF3D7_1218800), which is expressed and secreted by female P. falciparum gametocytes, was detected in the saliva of asymptomatic children from Cameroon and in symptomatic individuals (including adults) in the clinic in Sierra Leone.17,18 PSSP17 (PlasmoDB: PF3D7_1218800) was originally identified as PSOP17, i.e., a Plasmodium secreted ookinete protein; however, considering its protein expression in female gametocytes, this compelled a revision of the gene annotation. Its predicted expression in Plasmodium ookinetes inside the mosquito midgut suggests its role in midgut invasion before the establishment of sporogony.19 Contradicting data suggest that pssp17 is essential, as knockouts could not be recovered in the P. berghei murine malaria model.19 In P. falciparum, the gene is predicted to be dispensable during asexual development,20 but its essentiality for the mosquito or sporogonic stages remained untested. Considering growing efforts to identify the asymptomatic and mosquito-infectious parasite reservoir,21 we proposed that PSSP17 may be a useful biomarker for detecting both subclinical and clinical malaria infections.18 However, given concerns surrounding pfhrp2/3 deletions capable of diagnostic escape, addressing the question of PSSP17’s essentiality is critical to evaluating its candidacy as a diagnostic biomarker. We generated replicate Δpfpssp17 P. falciparum clones in the gametocyte-competent, mosquito-infectious NF54 isolate and characterized their phenotype at both the asexual blood stage and mosquito stages of the parasite life cycle to estimate the long-term utility of PSSP17 as a malaria rapid diagnostic test biomarker.

Results

Analyses of Plasmodium falciparum whole-genome sequence datasets from field samples did not reveal Plasmodium sexual stage protein 17 gene deletions but identified major and minor variants

PlasmoDB contains variation information on pssp17, but the data are limited to 286 strains (https://plasmodb.org/plasmo/app/record/gene/PF3D7_1218800). To ensure analysis of any possible variation within the gene, we explored whole-genome sequence (WGS) data from 19,280 P. falciparum parasites collected across the globe to identify examples of naturally occurring deletions of the pssp17 locus (Figure 1A). Full gene alleles of pssp17 were called using the de Bruijn graph assembly of all publicly available (as of January 2024) WGS data from P. falciparum isolates (Table S1). Out of these samples, 13,272 (1,615 unique genomic sequences with 66 unique translations) full-length translated alleles were called. None of the alleles had any premature stop codons. The median per-base read coverage was 83x, with no samples having zero coverage. Two major alleles were detected, with the major allele matching the P. falciparum 3D7 strain allele (Figure 1A) and the second domain allele matching lab isolates V1-S and D6. There was only one amino acid change detected above 1% within the global population, R194K, detected in 36.4% of the global population. There were 10 other low frequency AA changes that occurred in at least 5 alleles, which had the following allele count and global frequencies: L3I (n6, 0.045%), T18I (n = 30, 0.022%), H19Q (n = 20, 0.015%), T64S (n = 6, 0.045%), K83N (n = 5, 0.038%), E111K (n = 9, 0.067%), M201I (n = 5, 0.037%), I237V (n = 6, 0.052%), N294D (n = 103, 0.8%), and L320I (n = 9, 0.078%). Most alleles were single amino changes from the major allele (Figures 1B and 1C). The locations of these positions within a 3D structure predicted by AlphaFold are shown in Figure 1D and are distributed across the protein. The two major alleles are found in all four major geographical regions: Africa, Asia, Oceania, and South America. The dominant global allele is predominantly found in Africa, Oceania, and South America, while the R194K allele is dominant in Asia (Figure 1A). Oceania and South America have additional geographically unique alleles. The Plasmodium sexual stage protein 17 gene is non-essential for asexual parasite development but is involved in female gametocyte development

Attempts to delete the ortholog of pfpssp17 in P. berghei were unsuccessful, suggesting that this gene is essential for asexual parasite development.19 However, more recently, a genome-wide transposon-mediated mutation study suggested that pssp17 is not essential for P. falciparum20 and further indicated its importance at the mosquito transmission stages, i.e., gametocytes and ookinetes. We produced two pssp17-null mutants in the NF54 mosquito infection-capable strain of P. falciparum using CRISPR/Cas9 technology22 and characterized their viability during the asexual, sexual, and transmission stages.

We observed that Δpfpssp17 clones produced viable asexual parasites, with no apparent differences in parasitemia (Figure 2E). However, following the initiation of gametocytogenesis, we observed marked reductions in the total gametocyte output (Figure 2F). To evaluate successful Δpssp17 gametocyte transmission to mosquitoes, we performed standard membrane feeding assays (SMFA, Figure 3A) in replicate An. gambiae (KEELE) cohorts. Across all three replicate infections, we observed an average reduction of 77% in midgut oocyst infection (proportion of infected mosquitoes) at 8 days post-infectious bloodmeal (dipb) for Δpssp17 10F and an average reduction of 89% for Δpssp17 4D as compared to wildtype NF54 controls (Figures 3B–3D). We observed a 100% reduction in the median midgut oocyst infection intensity for both Δpssp17 10F and 4D. The observed breakthrough of a handful of Δpssp17 oocysts was unexpected, considering the reductions in overall gametocyte output. We produced polyclonal mouse antisera against PSSP17 and showed via SMFA that the protein is not involved in P. falciparum NF54 WT ookinete binding to the An. gambiae midgut (Figure S1), suggesting that the effect of the deletion is primarily on gametocyte development. However, to determine if the “breakthrough oocysts” that we observed would produce sporozoites, we conducted five replicate SMFAs to ensure the expected variability observed in the oocyst study was captured. We supplemented the 10% sucrose solution with 0.05% p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) to support sporozoite production26 and increase mosquito survivorship27,28,29,30,31 to day 14 post-feeding. Salivary gland sporozoite infection prevalence (proportion of infected mosquitoes) was scored using P. falciparum 18S rRNA gene endpoint PCR (Figure 3E). Discussion

Ideally, a candidate RDT biomarker must have three optimal characteristics with respect to the parasite protein biomarker: (a) low mutability of the capture or detection antibody epitope or silencing mutations that do not change the amino acid sequence of the recognized epitope, (b) gene stability with no recorded occurrences of gene deletions arising across all existing natural parasite populations that have been sequenced to date, and (c) a low likelihood of deletion mutations expanding within the parasite population due to the essentiality of the protein biomarker for either asexual development and/or mosquito transmission.

Our data provide confidence that, unlike what has been described for pfhrp2/3, with the natural occurrence of pfhrp2/3 null mutants in South America and the Horn of Africa, to date, there is no recorded existence, much less spread of pssp17 deletion parasites. Four natural mutations were identified for what we determined to be a well-conserved gene across the globe. The most frequently observed mutation, R194K, is proximal but is not present in either the capture or detection antibody epitopes that were developed as part of the effort to develop a PSSP17 RDT (WO2016/205585).18 While a change in the size, charge, hydrophobicity, or side chain of amino acids of a monoclonal antibody epitope can result in a loss of antibody specificity and/or avidity, these data suggest that these mutations will not affect the design of the PSSP17 RDT.

However, we were surprised by the data indicating that Δpssp17 gametocytes provided to An. gambiae produced oocysts, albeit few per mosquito. It is possible that although PSSP17 was predicted to be important for ookinete-to-oocyst transition,19 it may not be a major mediator of midgut cell invasion. Other pathways for mosquito midgut invasion32 have been described for a clonal parasite line (P. berghei ANKA 2.34). These data suggest that Plasmodium produces ookinete subclones in the mosquito midgut that can rely on several different parasite ligand-mosquito receptor interactions to support invasion, and that in the absence of PSSP17 by gene deletion or via protein masking by anti-PSSP17 antibodies, ookinete-to-oocyst transition can proceed unabated. It has also been recently shown that not all P. falciparum oocysts will produce sporozoites.33,34 On average, a single P. falciparum oocyst produces ∼1,250 sporozoites33 and studies have estimated that as little as 6–10 sporozoites can likely initiate infection 32% of the time (including through simple probing without blood ingestion), while mosquitoes with >1,000 salivary gland sporozoites, essentially with at least one or more oocysts, have a higher likelihood of 78% for successful infection.35,36,37 However, a recent study revisiting the minimum parasite burden in mosquitoes needed to establish mammalian infection found that oocyst burden, and thus salivary gland sporozoite burden, are highly correlated with infection success,38 and the number of “breakthrough” oocysts observed was quite low. Although the oocyst intensities reported herein are comparable to natural low-intensity infections capable of driving malaria transmission,39 the gametocytemias used to feed mosquitoes in this study are considerably higher than those typically present in the peripheral blood of naturally infected individuals. While our findings strongly suggest that parasites lacking pssp17 transmit less efficiently, the likelihood of natural transmission cannot be accurately predicted based solely on oocyst or sporozoite burden in this experimental model. Moreover, the laboratory-adapted strain used in this study may differ from natural circulating parasites. Future studies investigating the precise functional role of PSSP17 during gametocytogenesis could help validate our conclusions concerning its candidacy as one of the several robust biomarkers for parasite carriage. However, taken together, should a mutation arise that allows parasite populations to escape PSSP17 detection, the transmission of these parasites will probably be considerably less effective, supporting the potential long-term utility of the PSSP17 protein as one of ultimately several saliva-based biomarkers of both clinical and subclinical infection.18

Considering the contemporary emergence of diagnostic-evasive strains of P. falciparum, the need for a test with a different biomarker to assure reliable case detection cannot be stressed. Novel pLDH-based RDTs may meet this need in the near term40; however, only a few RDTs can reach a limit of detection <100 parasites/μL blood,41 resulting in false negatives. Although it is unclear if a PSSP17-based RDT will be developed, as there are other abundant parasite antigens identified in saliva that are present in asexual and gametocyte stages,18 an RDT that uses saliva to identify a parasite-derived protein biomarker, with sensitivity for submicroscopic, PCR-positive infections,18 can theoretically enhance the community’s adjunctive surveillance capabilities at any point-of-need (port, clinic, or school) with a non-invasive test. A field deployable saliva-based RDT that is more acceptable to the community, especially in children,42 can be similarly used as an adjunctive diagnostic in reactive case detection (RACD) activities, wherein parasite-positive individuals are treated with artemisinin combined therapy (ACT). However, it should be noted that gametocytes are less affected by ACTs,43 and recent treatment is a predictor for gametocyte carriage.44 Importantly, gametocyte carriage and density have been shown to vary by age and across transmission intensities, and light microscopy can miss as much as 40% of gametocyte carriers.44 So, there may indeed be added value for a gametocyte biomarker in specific situations. Irrespective of the parasite biomarker in saliva, the prevailing stumbling block is the reluctance of the community to consider saliva as an alternative biofluid to blood for malaria diagnostics, regardless of the advantages of the non-invasive approach. Expanding this approach to include additional candidate biomarkers and multiple parasite and vector strains in future studies will be essential to broaden the translational potential of this strategy. Regardless, our data indicate that any new malaria biomarker under consideration should undergo early-stage genetic and phenotypic characterization to build the case for continued translational product development.

Limitations of the study

While our findings provide valuable insight into the essentiality of pssp17 (PlasmoDB: PF3D7_1218800), we acknowledge the limitations of using a single strain (NF54) and that laboratory-adapted strains often accumulate mutations that may not reflect the genetic and phenotypic diversity of naturally circulating parasites. While a multi-strain approach would be ideal, to date, the limited availability of laboratory-adapted strains that are capable of producing viable gametocytes, which are infectious to mosquitoes, remains a major experimental constraint in the field.

Experimental model and study participant details

Parasite culture

The human blood used for culturing P. falciparum NF54 strain parasites (authenticated by ATCC through BEI resources; MRA-1000) and for mosquito infections was collected from a pool of pre-screened, anonymous donors obtained commercially from LifeSouth Blood Bank, making informed consent not applicable.

Parasites were cultured with washed O+ human erythrocytes (provided by Lifesouth Community Blood Center) in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated human O+ serum (provided by Interstate Blood Bank), 0.3 g/L L-glutamine, 5 g/L hypoxanthine, 25 mM HEPES, 2.1 g/L NaHCO3, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 μg/mL streptomycin. Parasites were incubated at 37°C under hypoxic conditions using the candle jar method. Parasite growth was monitored by observing Giemsa-stained thin blood smears under the microscope and cultures were diluted with fresh uninfected erythrocytes to maintain a parasitemia ≤10%.

Gametocyte cultures were initiated from Δpssp17 clones and parental NF54 parasites at 0.5% asexual parasitemia and medium was replaced daily until day 17. Gametocyte development was characterized on day 16 by observing Giemsa-stained thin smears under the microscope.

Mosquito colony

Anopheles gambiae (KEELE), an outbred laboratory colony was used in all experiments. These mosquitoes were provided directly from Keele University (P. Eggleston and H. Hurd). Adult mosquitoes were maintained at a temperature of 27°C with a relative humidity of 75–80% and a 12-h light/dark photoperiod. Larvae were maintained on Koi fish food pellets and adults were offered 10% sugar solution ad libitum.

Method details

In silico analysis

We analyzed 19,280 publicly available (as of January 2024, refer to Supplemental Data References) Illumina whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from worldwide P. falciparum isolates. Local gene length assembly was performed by using standard de Bruijn graph assembly using PathWeaver (https://github.com/nickjhathaway/PathWeaver) on reads that mapped to the pssp17 gene (genomic location Pf3D7_12_v3 738,356 to 740,255) to construct full alleles across the whole gene. The final alleles were then translated and compared to 3D7 to calculate amino acid changes. The geographical regions of origin of the downloaded samples were used to calculate the within-continent frequencies of amino acid changes. The protein 3D structure of pssp17 was determined by Alphafold45 and downloaded from the Alphafold protein structure database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/Q8I5L9).46 The structure was then visualized with UCSF ChimeraX47 to mark the locations of the residuals of interest within the 3D structure.

Plasmid generation

To enhance transfection efficiency, parasites were transfected with two versions of the pDC2-Cas9-hDHFRyFCU plasmid48 (designated A and B; see Table S2), each encoding a distinct sgRNA targeting one of the two homology regions used for donor DNA integration via homologous recombination. sgRNAs were designed using the Eukaryotic Pathogen CRISPR Guide RNA/DNA Design Tool (EuPaGDT)49 to maximize efficiency and minimize off-target activity. Primers p1 and p2 (see Table S3), which contained homology arms to the pDC2 backbone for In-Fusion seamless cloning, were annealed in vitro and cloned into BbsI-digested pDC2-Cas9-hDHFRyFCU. The resulting plasmids were transformed into Stellar competent cells and screened for the presence of the correct sgRNA sequence by Sanger sequencing.

Two homology regions adjacent to the sgRNA target sites (positions −310 bp to +89 bp and +1899 bp to +2505 bp relative to the pssp17 start codon) were amplified from genomic DNA of the parental NF54 WT P. falciparum strain using primers with homology arms to the pUC19 backbone (primers p3 and p4; see Table S3). The purified PCR products were then cloned into linearized pUC19 via In-Fusion cloning. The resulting plasmids were transformed into Stellar competent cells, screened for the presence of the homology arms by restriction digestion, and the correct sequence was further verified by Sanger sequencing.

Parasite transfection

Cultures with ≥5% ring-stage parasitemia were transfected by electroporation50 with 60 μg of ScaI-linearized pUC19-PSSP17 plasmid and 60 μg each of circular pDC2-Cas9-hDHFRyFCU-PSSP17-A and -B plasmids, resuspended in incomplete Cytomix (120 mM KCl, 0.15 mM CaCl2, 2 mM EGTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM K2HPO4, and 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.6). Plasmids were first resuspended in 30 μL of TE buffer and then diluted with 370 μL of incomplete Cytomix before adding ring stage-synchronized, packed infected red blood cells. Electroporation was carried out in a 0.2 cm-gap cuvette using a Gene Pulser Xcell Electroporation System (Bio-Rad) with a single pulse (310 V, 950 μF).

Following electroporation, parasites were cultured in medium supplemented with 10 nM WR99210 for five consecutive days to select for transfectants. After this initial selection, the culture medium was replaced three times per week, and cultures were diluted 1:2 once a week. Parasite growth was monitored by Giemsa-stained thin blood smears, and successful gene editing was confirmed by diagnostic PCR using primers p5 and p6. Edited parasite populations were synchronized with sorbitol and maintained at low parasitemias under shaking conditions to minimize multiple infections per red blood cell. Subsequently, parasites were diluted to a final concentration of 0.5 parasites per well in 96-well plates, and clonal lines were expanded from single-parasite populations.

Multiplication rate assay

Wild-type and knockout parasites were synchronized by two consecutive rounds of sorbitol treatment, seeded at 0.5% ring-stage parasitemia, and monitored over 48 h. A total of 2,000 erythrocytes were counted per sample.

Standard membrane feeding assay

On day 17, gametocyte cultures were adjusted to 1% stage V gametocytemia by adding fresh, uninfected red blood cells and resuspended in prewarmed, heat-inactivated human serum and O+ erythrocytes at 50% hematocrit. To prevent premature gametocyte activation, the infective blood was maintained at 37°C at all times and then introduced into glass, water-jacketed membrane feeders preheated to 37°C. Female mosquitoes aged 6–8 days were allowed to feed for 20 min. Fully engorged mosquitoes were separated and maintained on 10% sucrose solution for 14 days. For midgut oocyst assessment, a subset of mosquitoes was collected 8 days post-infectious bloodmeal, and midguts were stained with 0.1% mercurochrome in water and examined in 1X PBS to determine oocyst numbers.33,51

Salivary gland sporozoite infection prevalence

The heads and thoraxes of adult mosquitoes were dissected and transferred to tubes containing DNAzol. Tissue was mechanically homogenized on ice for 5 min using a disposable pestle. Glycogen was added as a co-precipitant during DNA ethanol precipitation. Purified DNA was resuspended in nuclease-free water and used for Plasmodium falciparum 18S rRNA endpoint PCR with the primers 5′-GGCGTGGAGCTTGCGGCTTAATTT and 5′-GGCAGGGACGTAGTCAGCACAATCT. PCR amplification was performed using the QuantaBio AccuStart II kit (Cat. 95142-800) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min (1 cycle), followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 64°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 min, with a final hold at 4°C. PCR products were resolved on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with GelGreen and visualized using a Li-COR Odyssey Fc Imager (LICORbio).

Production of polyclonal IgG against PSSP17 for membrane feeding assays

We used polyclonal antibodies that were previously generated in outbred Swiss Webster mice (22–24 g) that were immunized with 5 μg/mL of purified recombinant PSSP17.17 The mice were primed s.c. and boosted i.p. with the same dose every two weeks. The mice were exsanguinated two weeks following the final dosing to collect serum IgG. For the An. gambiae (KEELE) infection assays (described above), purified polyclonal anti-PSSP17 total IgG concentrations of 0.5 and 1 mg/mL were evaluated. All previous mouse immunization studies were performed according to the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) Animal Care and Use Committee-approved protocol (MO12H232).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Three biological replicates were performed to assess the asexual blood-stage multiplication rates, gametocyte development, and male-to-female ratios of Δpssp17 clones compared with their NF54 wild-type parental line. Five biological replicates were conducted to evaluate the ability of Δpssp17 sporozoites to reach the mosquito salivary glands (measured as P. falciparum 18S rRNA endpoint PCR positivity at 14-dpib). Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Three biological replicate standard membrane feeding assays (SMFA) were performed to assess the ability of Δpssp17 clones to form oocysts on the mosquito midgut. Each data point in an SMFA figure represents the number of oocysts for each individual mosquito. Statistical analyses were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with Dunn’s multiple comparison test for repeated measures, with significance defined as p < 0.001.
