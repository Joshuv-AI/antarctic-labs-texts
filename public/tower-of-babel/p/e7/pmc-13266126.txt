Elucidation of sugar metabolic profiles and transcriptional regulatory networks during the developmental stages of sweet corn kernels
Authors: Haiying Feng, Jianghua Guo, Cuirong Xu, Ziheng Li, Zhenhua Hu, Jin Zhu, Wenkang Chen, Fazhan Qiu, Changcheng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySweet corn is highly valued by consumers due to its desirable taste, which is largely influenced by sugar metabolism within the kernels. Despite its importance, the genetic and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying sugar metabolism in sweet corn remain inadequately characterized. In this study, targeted profiling of sugar metabolism was conducted at three critical developmental stages of sweet corn kernels-the blister, milky, and waxy stages-using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Simultaneously, the direct RNA sequencing was employed to explore the complexity of transcriptional regulation throughout kernel development at the transcriptomic level, facilitating the construction of a regulatory network for sugar metabolism-related genes. The results indicated that variations in polyadenylation tail length may modulate sugar metabolism during kernel development by influencing transcript abundance. This study elucidates potential transcriptional regulatory pathways governing sugar metabolites in sweet corn kernels and provides foundational data to support precision breeding strategies.Subject areas: Plant biochemistry, Plant bioinformatics, Plant biology, Plant systematics, Plant development, Agricultural plant products

Introduction

Sweet corn (Zea mays var. rugosa Bonaf.), a distinctive variety of maize and an important crop both as a vegetable and fruit, is extensively cultivated worldwide.1 It is highly valued by consumers for its superior flavor and rich nutritional value, and is predominantly consumed either fresh or processed into canned products and other derivatives.2 The sweet corn phenotype is predominantly determined by recessive mutant genes expressed within the endosperm, including su1, su2, sh1, sh2, sh4, bt1, bt2, and se. When these genes are present in a homozygous state, they encode inactive starch synthase enzymes, thereby inhibiting starch biosynthesis and leading to a marked accumulation of sugars.3,4,5,6,7,8,9 Based on the specific mutant alleles involved, sweet corn is categorized into normal-sweet (su1 su2), super-sweet (sh1, sh2, sh4), and sugar-enhanced corn types (su1se).10,11,12,13,14,15 Currently, the super-sweet variety, governed by the endosperm mutant gene sh2, predominates the commercial market.16 High-quality and complete reference genomes for super-sweet corn inbred lines Ia453-sh217 and RC18 have been successfully generated through de novo genome assembly.

Sugar metabolites represent fundamental and indispensable constituents of living organisms. Beyond their contribution to sweetness, these metabolites serve as critical molecular entities essential for life and participate in a wide array of physiological processes in plants, including growth and development, hormone regulation, photosynthesis, circadian rhythm regulation, and responses to abiotic stressors.19,20,21,22 Recent advancements in metabolomics have facilitated high-throughput, comprehensive profiling of the complex metabolic landscapes in crop species.23 Despite considerable efforts to characterize sugar metabolites in sweet corn, the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying their metabolism remain inadequately understood.24,25,26 In recent years, direct RNA sequencing (DRS) technology, leveraging third-generation nanopore sequencing platforms, has exhibited notable advantages in detecting novel transcript isoforms, assessing polyadenylation tail lengths, and predicting methylation modification sites. In the present study, we utilized gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to identify 27 sugar metabolites across various developmental stages of sweet corn kernels. Simultaneously, transcriptomic analyses using DRS technology were conducted to elucidate gene expression dynamics at three developmental stages and to pinpoint key candidate genes implicated in the regulatory networks of sugar metabolism. This investigation substantially enhances our comprehension of the transcriptional regulatory frameworks governing sugar metabolism and provides a foundation for the molecular breeding of sweet corn.

Results

Fluctuations in the concentrations of sugar metabolites

To elucidate the changes in sugar metabolism throughout the developmental stages of sweet corn kernels, targeted quantification of sugar metabolites was performed on kernel tissues collected at 14 days (blister stage), 21 days (milky stage), and 28 days (waxy stage) post-pollination. A total of 27 sugar metabolites were identified, including 21 monosaccharides, 5 disaccharides, and 1 trisaccharide (Table S1). Data variability was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV), defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of the raw data. Notably, more than 80% of the detected metabolites across the three developmental stages exhibited CV values below 0.2, indicating high stability and reproducibility of the experimental data (Figure 1A). Subsequently, hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was employed to evaluate the accumulation patterns of these metabolites across nine samples. The results demonstrated strong reproducibility among the three biological replicates at each developmental stage. Moreover, metabolite accumulation profiles at 21 and 28 days post-pollination were relatively similar, whereas those at 14 days post-pollination showed distinct differences (Figure 1B). To identify differential metabolites between groups, orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted for pairwise and multi-group comparisons. The OPLS-DA score plot revealed clear separation among the three developmental stages, with tight clustering of replicates within each group (Figure 1C). Model performance was evaluated using three predictive parameters: Q2, R2X, and R2Y. In the multi-group comparison model (DAP14 vs. DAP21 vs. DAP28), these parameters reached values of 0.935, 0.91, and 0.997, respectively. Values approaching 1 for these metrics, combined with low p values, indicate a robust and reliable model (Figure 1D). Collectively, these findings confirm the accuracy and reliability of the sugar metabolite quantification data. Subsequently, a more comprehensive analysis was performed on the sugar metabolites exhibiting significant differential expression across various sample groups. For pairwise comparisons, metabolites with a fold change of ≥2 or ≤0.5 were deemed significantly different. In the case of multiple group comparisons, metabolites were considered significantly different if they had a variable importance in projection (VIP) score exceeding 1 and a p value below 0.05. Notably, the differential metabolites identified throughout the entire kernel development period (DAP14 versus DAP28) largely overlapped with those observed during the early developmental stage (DAP14 versus DAP21), predominantly consisting of 11 sugar metabolites. In contrast, only two sugar metabolites exhibited significant changes during the later developmental stage (DAP14 versus DAP28), while the remaining nine sugars that showed significant alterations in the early stage did not display notable changes thereafter. These results suggest that the regulation of most sugar metabolic pathways primarily occurs between the blister and milky stages, whereas the transition from the milky to waxy stage has a relatively limited impact on sugar metabolite levels (Figure 1E). Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the identified differential metabolites revealed their principal involvement in amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, galactose metabolism, and starch and sucrose metabolism (Figure 1F).

Sweetness serves as a critical sensory attribute for assessing the eating quality of sweet corn, predominantly influenced by the concentration of sugar metabolites, particularly soluble sugars, within the kernels. These soluble sugars primarily comprise glucose, fructose, maltose, and sucrose, which are interconvertible through enzymatic catalysis.27,28 Among the various developmental stages of sweet corn kernels, sucrose emerges as the most abundant soluble sugar (Figure 1G), exhibiting an average concentration of 260 mg/g. Sucrose is closely linked to the perception of sweetness and maintains high levels throughout kernel development, peaking during the milky stage. This substantial accumulation at the milky stage ensures the fulfillment of primary taste requirements at harvest. As a disaccharide, sucrose can be hydrolyzed into glucose and fructose; however, the concentrations of these monosaccharides do not exhibit a correlated relationship, a phenomenon supported by previous research.29 Glucose (Figure 1H) and fructose (Figure 1I) are present at moderate concentrations, with the average glucose content (61.157 mg/g) approximately double that of fructose (31.581 mg/g). Both sugars are found at higher levels during the early kernel developmental stages, measuring 119.088 mg/g and 65.497 mg/g, respectively. Their concentrations decline markedly as the kernels mature, reaching 41.968 mg/g for glucose and 19.354 mg/g for fructose at the milky stage. In other sweet corn cultivars, such as Zhetian 11, glucose and fructose levels are relatively low during early kernel development and tend to stabilize during the mid to late developmental phases. Conversely, in Xuetian 7401, glucose and fructose concentrations initially increase, peaking at 18 days post-pollination, before subsequently decreasing. In both cultivars, sucrose content demonstrates a decreasing trend following 14 days post-pollination.25 These findings suggest that distinct sweet corn varieties possess unique taste profiles attributable to variations in soluble sugar composition. A comprehensive investigation into the regulatory mechanisms governing sugar metabolism may facilitate targeted modulation of individual sugar contents and ratios, thereby optimizing sweetness characteristics. Maltose is the least abundant soluble sugar (Figure 1J), with an average concentration of merely 0.272 mg/g. Its concentration peaks during the milky stage; however, this elevation does not reach statistical significance when compared to the other developmental stages. Similarly, D-sorbitol (Figure 1K), classified as a soluble solid, attains its highest accumulation during the milky stage, thereby directly affecting textural attributes such as tenderness and crispness in sweet corn. The milky stage is generally regarded as the optimal harvest period for sweet corn. These findings suggest that the dynamic fluctuations of these five sugars are intricately associated with the sensory qualities of sweet corn. Consequently, refining the sampling time frame for kernel tissue around the milky stage may facilitate a more precise determination of the ideal harvest period.

Further statistical analyses identified sixteen additional differential sugar metabolites beyond the commonly studied soluble sugars glucose and D-fructose (Tables S2–S5). Seven sugar metabolites, including L-fucose, D-xylulose, D-ribono-1,4-lactone, D-ribose, D-arabinitol, L-rhamnose, and inositol (Figures S1A–S1G), were significantly downregulated throughout the entire kernel development period. Eight sugar metabolites, including D-arabinose, D-xylose, 2-acetamido-2-deoxy-D-glucopyranose, D-mannose, D-galactose, cellobiose, raffinose, and D-mannose-6-phosphate sodium salt (Figures S1H–S1O), exhibited significant downregulation during the early stages of kernel development but remained relatively unchanged in later stages. Six sugar metabolites, including D-galacturonic acid, phenylglucoside, D-glucuronic acid, barium D-ribose-5-phosphate, trehalose, and levoglucosan (Figures S1P–S1U), maintained relative stability throughout kernel development. Notably, D-galacturonic acid showed a slight decreasing trend at the milky stage, whereas the other five sugars demonstrated modest increases. It is posited that these six sugars may also contribute to the flavor profile of sweet corn at harvest. Remarkably, xylitol (Figure S1V) was the sole sugar metabolite to exhibit a significant increasing trend throughout kernel development. As a low-calorie sweetener, xylitol not only aids in weight management but also confers health benefits, including the reduction of dental caries risk, alleviation of constipation, and enhancement of bone mineral density.30

Dynamic transcriptomic characteristics analysis

To investigate the genetic and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, we utilized DRS technology to profile the transcriptome of sweet corn kernels at three distinct developmental stages: the blister, milky, and waxy stages. A total of nine sequencing libraries were constructed, and raw reads with a quality score above 10 were selected for downstream analyses. After stringent quality control procedures, approximately 63 gigabases of data comprising 57.59 million reads were retained. The number of reads per sample ranged from 5.62 million to 8.21 million, with average read lengths between 986 and 1,241 nucleotides, and a maximum read length of 459,746 nucleotides. The mean read quality score was approximately 20 (Tables S6). These results confirm the successful generation of high-quality sequencing data via DRS, establishing a solid basis for subsequent analyses. The quality-filtered reads were aligned to the maize reference genome (https://download.maizegdb.org/Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0/Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0.fa.gz) using minimap2 software, achieving alignment rates between 95.32% and 97.01% (Tables S7). Consensus transcript sequences were assembled using FLAIR software (Tables S8). Following deduplication, the transcripts exhibited an N50 length of 2,351bp (Figure S2A). Comparative analysis of the non-redundant transcripts against annotated genomic transcripts was conducted using gffcompare, which identified 21,926 novel transcripts. Among these, transcripts exhibiting intron retention represented the largest category, totaling 10,159 (Figure S2B). Functional annotation indicated that these novel transcripts are primarily involved in critical cellular metabolic pathways, including glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, amino acid metabolism, terpenoid and polyketide metabolism, cofactor and vitamin metabolism, and lipid metabolism (Figure S2C). Collectively, these findings provide a comprehensive resource to advance the understanding of transcriptional regulatory mechanisms governing sweet corn kernel growth and development.

In the present investigation, a comprehensive identification of 48,036 genes and 94,465 expressed transcripts was conducted across nine sweet corn kernel samples encompassing distinct developmental stages. Of these, 39,756 genes corresponded to previously annotated maize genes, whereas 8,280 represented novel gene identifications (Figure 2A). The majority of genes were associated with a single transcript isoform; however, approximately 38% of genes exhibited multiple transcripts arising from diverse alternative splicing events (Figure 2B). To elucidate the genomic distribution of transcripts, a density map was constructed utilizing the Circlize R package, differentiating between positive and negative DNA strands with a default window size of 500 kb. The transcripts demonstrated an even distribution across all ten chromosomes and were implicated in various cellular processes (Figure 2C). Seven predominant types of alternative splicing events were characterized: alternative 3′ splice site, alternative 5′ splice site, alternative first exon, alternative last exon, mutually exclusive exon, retained intron, and exon skipping. Quantitative analysis across all sweet corn kernel tissues revealed that retained intron events were the most prevalent, accounting for 43.48% of splicing occurrences, whereas mutually exclusive exons were the least frequent (Figure 2D). Additionally, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), which lack protein-coding potential but are integral to epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional regulation, were identified through the application of three computational prediction tools: CNCI,31 CPC2,32 and PLEK.33 A total of 2,771 lncRNAs were consistently predicted by all three methodologies (Figure 2E). Subsequent analyses indicated that transcript (Figure 2F) and gene (Figure S3A) expression levels, as determined by DRS, remained relatively stable across the developmental stages of sweet corn kernels. Moreover, the expression density distributions for both transcripts (Figure 2G) and genes (Figure S3B) deviated from a standard normal distribution. Throughout the entire developmental timeline, a substantial number of differentially expressed transcripts (DETs) (8,333) and genes (6,579) were identified. Notably, the early stage of kernel development exhibited 4,396 DETs and 3,633 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), whereas the late developmental stage showed the lowest numbers, with 530 DETs and 500 DEGs. These results collectively suggest that cellular metabolic activity is particularly elevated during the initial phases of kernel development and remains significant throughout the developmental progression (Figures 2H, S3C, and S4).

mRNA poly(A) tail length changes

DRS facilitates accurate and efficient detection of variations in mRNA polyadenylation tail length.34 Utilizing the Dorado software to estimate transcript poly(A) tail lengths, we observed a consistent distribution of poly(A) tail lengths across nine samples, with mean values ranging from 68.97 to 94.77 bp (Figure 3A). Examination of the association between median poly(A) tail length and gene expression levels revealed a significant negative correlation in sweet corn kernel tissues at multiple developmental stages (Figures 3B and S5). This observation aligns with prior findings reported in bovine adipocytes35 and rice.36 To identify transcripts exhibiting differential poly(A) tail lengths across the three developmental stages, pairwise comparisons were conducted using thresholds of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute median difference (|Diff_median|) exceeding 15. Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these differentially polyadenylated transcripts demonstrated predominant enrichment in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and amino acid biosynthesis pathways. Notably, the amino acid biosynthetic pathways implicated included those for valine, leucine, isoleucine, cysteine, methionine, and arginine (Figure 3C). Given that amino acids directly influence the taste and flavor profile of sweet corn,37 these findings bear functional significance. Furthermore, metabolic processes related to carbohydrates and amino acids were predominantly active during the early stages of kernel development (Figure S6). Collectively, these results suggest that the modulation of poly(A) tail length may regulate carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism during sweet corn kernel development by influencing transcript abundance.

In this study, we identified four known key genes integral to the regulation of kernel sugar metabolism: Zm00001eb176800 (bt2), Zm00001eb228530 (sbe1), Zm00001eb279740 (sugary2), and Zm00001eb37-4090 (sh1). These genes exhibited high expression levels throughout the developmental stages of sweet corn kernels. The bt2 gene encodes the small subunit of ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase). Maize bt2 mutant exhibits reduced starch content, a marked increase in soluble sugar concentration at maturity, and notably impaired the development of the basal endosperm transfer cell layer.38 The sbe1 gene encodes starch branching enzyme 1, which primarily catalyzes the formation of α-1,6-glycosidic linkages in starch molecules. Mutation of sbe1 in maize results in decreased digestibility of endosperm starch granules by pancreatic α-amylase, alterations in the branching patterns of amylopectin and amylose, and reduced seed germination rates.39 The sugary2 gene encodes starch synthase IIa (SSIIa). Mutation of sugary2 in maize leads to an increased proportion of short glucan chains within amylopectin and a concomitant reduction in intermediate-length chains.40 The sh1 gene encodes sucrose synthase, a critical enzyme involved in carbon allocation, osmotic potential regulation, starch biosynthesis, and seed development.41 Loss-of-function of SH1 causes a significant decrease in sucrose synthase activity and starch accumulation, resulting in a characteristic shrunken kernel phenotype.42 For polyadenylation analysis, transcripts exhibiting the highest read counts were selected across various developmental stages of sweet corn kernels. Our results revealed a significant inverse correlation between the expression levels of these four genes and their poly(A) tail lengths (Figure S7). This observation implies that modifications in poly(A) tail length may indirectly influence the transcriptional regulation of key sugar metabolism genes, thereby modulating sugar metabolic pathways within sweet corn kernels. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying sugar metabolism in maize kernels.

Poly(A) sites in eukaryotic organisms are typically organized into clusters. Conventionally, all poly(A) sites located within a 24-nucleotide window are defined as a poly(A) cluster (PAC). In the tissues of sweet corn kernels, PACs are predominantly localized within the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), accounting for up to 90% of the total (Figure 3D). Furthermore, the majority of genes possess only one or two PACs (Figure 3E). During kernel development, the number of genes exhibiting shortened 3′ UTRs significantly exceeds those with lengthened 3′ UTRs, with these genes primarily associated with the early developmental stages of the kernel (Figures 3F, S8A, and S8C). Changes in 3′ UTR length are closely linked to RNA stability, gene expression regulation, and protein translation efficiency. Notably, this study observed that both elongation and shortening of the 3′ UTR corresponded with increased gene expression levels throughout kernel development, representing a particularly intriguing finding (Figures 3G, S8B, and S8D). In both animal and plant systems, the sequences proximal to the poly(A) site can be categorized into three distinct elements: the far-upstream element (FUE), the near-upstream element (NUE), and the cleavage element (CE). The polyadenylation factor CPSF directs the site of poly(A) tail addition by recognizing the NUE element. Consequently, the NUE element is integral to transcript processing and maturation. In plants, the predominant polyadenylation signal within the NUE element is the hexanucleotide sequence AAUAAA. Our analysis of NUE signals associated with 3′UTR lengthening events during kernel development revealed that the frequency of the highly conserved AAUAAA signal was notably low. Instead, the majority of signals comprised alternative sequence motifs or single-nucleotide variants of the canonical AAUAAA sequence (Figure 3H). Collectively, this study provides a high-quality dataset that enables a comprehensive assessment of transcript expression levels, structural variations, and regulatory modifications throughout sweet corn kernel development.

Integrated analysis of differentially expressed metabolites and differentially expressed genes

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA), based on multivariate statistical dimension reduction theory, was employed to further investigate the relationships between differential transcripts and metabolites across each comparison group (Figure 4A). Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to visualize the fold changes of transcripts and metabolites with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.99, represented in a nine-quadrant plot delineated by black dashed lines marking quadrants 1 through 9 (Figure 4B). The majority of correlated transcripts and metabolites were concentrated in the lower-left quadrant. These results indicate that DETs and sugar metabolites exhibit strong correlations throughout the overall kernel development of sweet corn, implying a potential regulatory causal relationship. A more detailed, stage-specific analysis revealed that DETs exerted a greater influence on sugar metabolites predominantly during the early stages of kernel development (Figures S9A and S9B), whereas their impact was comparatively diminished during the later stages of kernel development (Figures S9D and S9E). Subsequently, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using differentially expressed metabolites and transcripts. Focusing on pathways enriched in both omics datasets, it was observed that the pathways enriched throughout the entire kernel development period of sweet corn closely corresponded to those identified during the early developmental stage. Differential sugar metabolites showed significant enrichment in amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (p < 0.05), while differential transcripts were significantly enriched in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (p < 0.01), general metabolic pathways (p < 0.01), pentose and glucuronate interconversions (p < 0.05), the pentose phosphate pathway (p < 0.05), and starch and sucrose metabolism (p < 0.01) (Figures 4C and S9C). Notably, a markedly reduced number of pathways were enriched during the late kernel development stage. However, these pathways represented a subset of those enriched in the early stage. This finding further suggests that the majority of metabolic activities are largely completed during the early phases of kernel development, with comparatively limited changes occurring in the later stages (Figure S9F).

Through an integrated analysis of differentially expressed metabolites and genes, three pivotal candidate genes-Zm00001eb090260 (XYL4), Zm00001eb111020 (xylA), and Zm00001eb141560 (HK)-were identified as potentially instrumental in regulating sugar metabolism. The expression levels of XYL4 and xylA exhibited the strongest correlations with xylose concentrations, with correlation coefficients of 0.925 and 0.933, respectively. XYL4 encodes xylan 1,4-beta-xylosidase, an enzyme responsible for catalyzing the hydrolysis of 1,4-β-D-xylan into D-xylose.43,44 During the development of sweet corn kernels, a decline in XYL4 expression level (Figure 5B) corresponded with a decreasing trend in D-xylose content (Figure S1I). As a metabolic product, xylose can be transformed through chemical or biotechnological methods into various industrially significant compounds, including xylitol, ethanol, furfural, lactic acid, and butyric acid.45 The substrate xylan constitutes a major structural component of cellulose. Therefore, targeted modification of the XYL4 gene via gene-editing technologies may enhance the eating quality of sweet corn by modulating cellulose content within the pericarp. The xylA gene encodes D-xylose isomerase, an intracellular enzyme that facilitates the reversible isomerization between D-xylose and D-xylulose. During kernel development, an upregulation of xylA expression level (Figure 5C) promotes this isomerization, which is reflected in a concomitant reduction in D-xylose levels (Figure S1I) and a declining trend in D-xylulose content (Figure S1B). Industrially, xylA plays a critical role in the enzymatic production of high-fructose syrup by catalyzing the conversion of D-glucose to D-fructose.46 Given that fructose possesses a higher sweetness than glucose and is abundant in super-sweet corn varieties, genetic modification of xylA holds potential for the development of superior sweet corn cultivars. Regarding the HK gene, its expression demonstrated the highest correlations with the sugar metabolites D-mannose (0.981), D-fructose (0.979), and D-glucose (0.975). HK encodes hexokinase 4, an enzyme that phosphorylates these sugars to yield D-mannose-6-phosphate, D-fructose-6-phosphate, and D-glucose-6-phosphate, respectively. This phosphorylation constitutes the initial step of glycolysis and is subject to multifactorial regulation.47 In the present study, an increase in HK expression levels (Figure 5D) was associated with a marked decrease in the concentrations of its substrates (Figures S1K, S1I, and S1H), suggesting an accelerated phosphorylation process.

Discussion

The transcriptional process within an organism is a crucial mode of gene expression and participates in various regulatory life processes.48 In maize, conventional RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) technology has been predominantly employed to elucidate transcriptional regulatory mechanisms,49 facilitating investigations into several agronomic traits such as kernel oil content,50 carotenoid and tocochromanol biosynthesis,51 and glycolysis.52 In contrast to traditional RNA-Seq, DRS technology, which utilizes the nanopore-based third-generation sequencing platform, obviates the need for reverse transcription and PCR amplification of RNA molecules. This approach enables the accurate preservation of the native features of RNA sequences. As individual RNA molecules translocate through a nanoscale pore, distinct ionic current signatures generated by different nucleotides and their modified bases allow for precise sequence determination, concurrently providing insights into mRNA modifications, alternative splicing events, and polyadenylation status.53 This technology has been successfully applied across a diverse range of species, including viruses,54 yeast,55 nematodes,56 honeybees,57 humans,58 cattle,59 Arabidopsis thaliana,60 and rice.36 The present study represents the inaugural application of DRS in maize. The poly(A) tail is integral to the regulation of gene expression, contributing to mRNA processing, maturation, transport, stability, and translation efficiency.61 While the average poly(A) tail length is approximately 200 nucleotides in mammals62 and around 70 nucleotides in yeast,63 our findings indicate that sweet corn kernels exhibit an average poly(A) tail length of approximately 80 nucleotides. Although DRS methods have revealed substantial variations in poly(A) tail lengths across developmental stages in nematodes,56 honeybees,57 and bovine adipocytes,35 our analysis detected no significant differences in poly(A) tail length among different developmental stages of sweet corn kernels. These observations imply potential distinctions in the dynamics of poly(A) tail length regulation between plant and animal systems.

Recent advancements in high-throughput omics technologies have facilitated the generation of extensive datasets. Although conventional single-omics analyses can elucidate certain genetic information and metabolic pathways, they frequently prove insufficient for comprehensively characterizing complex biological processes and regulatory networks.64 In this study, we employed DRS in conjunction with targeted sugar metabolism and utilized an integrative multi-omics analytical approach to examine the dynamics of sugar accumulation and distribution throughout kernel development, thereby identifying a critical regulatory gene network governing sugar metabolic pathways (Figure 5A). Variations in sugar metabolite concentrations appear to be driven by corresponding alterations in the transcriptional activity of upstream or downstream regulatory genes, with several differentially expressed genes exhibiting strong correlations with sugar metabolite levels across distinct developmental stages. Furthermore, we identified four additional candidate genes-Zm00001eb056490 (lpa3), Zm00001eb049310 (VTC4), Zm00001eb334460 (bglB), and Zm00001eb00100 (FUK)-which, despite lacking strong correlations with sugar metabolite content, are likely implicated in the regulation of sugar metabolism in sweet corn kernels. Prior studies have characterized lpa3 as a low phytic acid mutant in maize, resulting from a Mu transposon insertion. lpa3 encodes 1D-myo-inositol 3-kinase and is predominantly expressed in the maize embryo.65 Our findings indicate that elevated lpa3 expression during early kernel development (Figure 5E) corresponds with a decline in inositol content (Figure S1G). As the most upstream regulatory gene in the phytic acid biosynthesis pathway, lpa3 directly influences phytic acid accumulation, a known anti-nutritional factor that chelates essential metal ions in humans and monogastric animals, thereby reducing their bioavailability. Consequently, decreasing phytic acid levels in maize kernels holds potential for enhancing nutritional quality. However, such reductions often incur adverse effects, including diminished seed germination rates and reduced kernel weight.66 In this context, precise genome editing of specific genes to lower phytic acid content without eliciting significant pleiotropic detriments represents a pivotal objective in maize breeding programs.67 The gene VTC4 encodes inositol-phosphate phosphatase. In Arabidopsis thaliana, vtc4 knockout mutants exhibit reduced inositol levels, disrupting normal inositol biosynthesis.68 In sweet corn kernels, decreased VTC4 expression during early development (Figure 5F) contributed to lowered inositol content (Figure S1G), although this change was not statistically significant. The bglB gene encodes beta-glucosidase, an enzyme catalyzing the conversion of cellobiose to D-glucose.69 Notably, increased bglB expression level (Figure 5G) was associated with a significant reduction in D-glucose content (Figure 1H). Lastly, FUK encodes fucokinase, the sole enzyme responsible for phosphorylating L-fucose to fucose-1-phosphate, a precursor in GDP-fucose biosynthesis.70 Elevated FUK expression level during early kernel development (Figure 5H) correlated with a marked decrease in L-fucose levels (Figure S1A). The identification of these candidate genes offers valuable genetic resources for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms underlying sugar metabolism in sweet corn and for advancing the development of improved germplasm.

Despite the identification of several critical regulatory genes implicated in sugar metabolism, the exact mechanisms underlying their interactions and functional impacts remain inadequately characterized. This study presents a thorough transcriptomic analysis of differentially expressed genes across multiple stages of kernel development. Furthermore, it explores the association between these genes and sugar metabolism, thereby clarifying their biological functions from a transcriptomic standpoint. The results provide a theoretical basis for further investigations into the nutritional quality of sweet corn and support the breeding of improved cultivars.

Limitations of the study

In this study, using DRS based on the latest Oxford Nanopore Technology, we revealed potential transcriptional regulatory pathways associated with sugar metabolism during the development of sweet corn kernels. However, we were unable to perform further functional validation of the identified candidate genes through genetic manipulation approaches such as overexpression or CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology. The non-PCR amplification method employed in this study presents certain difficulties in detecting low-abundance transcripts and accurately measuring poly(A) tail lengths. Furthermore, while our findings suggest that variations in poly(A) tail length may modulate sugar metabolism by influencing transcript abundance, this conclusion is currently based solely on correlational analysis and lacks more direct experimental validation.

Method details

Plant materials and sampling

The inbred line T639 was developed from the elite super sweet corn cultivar “Jinyinsu2” through successive generations of self-pollination and rigorous selection, and it is categorized as a super sweet corn inbred line. Cultivation of T639 was conducted at the Southern Breeding Base of the Wuhan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, situated in Sanya, Hainan Province. All plants underwent self-pollination, with precise documentation of pollination dates. Kernel tissues were collected at 14, 21, and 28 days post-pollination, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80°C for downstream analyses, including quantification of sugar metabolites and RNA extraction. To ensure experimental robustness, three biological replicates were obtained for each sampling time point.

Sample preparation and extraction

Stock solutions of 27 sugar metabolite standards were prepared by dissolving each compound in methanol (MeOH) at a concentration of 2 mg/mL. The internal standard, [2H2]-L-threonine, was similarly dissolved in MeOH at a concentration of 1000 μg/mL. All standard solutions were stored at −20°C. Methanol was procured from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), while all metabolite standards were obtained from TCI (Shanghai), IsoReag (Shanghai), and CNW (Shanghai Anpel). Freeze-dried samples were finely ground using a mixer mill (MM 400, Retsch). Subsequently, 20 mg of the powdered material was dissolved in 500 μL of a solvent mixture composed of methanol, isopropanol, and water in a volumetric ratio of 3:3:2. The solution was vortexed and then subjected to sonication for 30 min. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 5 min, 50 μL of the supernatant was combined with the internal standard. The resulting mixture was evaporated under a nitrogen gas stream and subsequently freeze-dried using a lyophilizer. Derivatization was performed by sequentially adding 100 μL of methoxyamine hydrochloride solution in pyridine (15 mg/mL) and 100 μL of BSTFA. Pipette 50 μL of the derivatization solution and dilute it with n-hexane to 1 mL. Filter the solution through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and transfer it into an amber sample vial for GC-MS analysis.84,85

GC-MS analysis

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis was employed to quantify sugar metabolites using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph coupled with a 5977B mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was conducted on a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm×0.25 μm). Helium was utilized as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. Sample injection (1 μL) was performed in split mode with a split ratio of 5:1. The oven temperature program commenced at 160°C, maintained for 1 min, followed by an increase to 200°C at a rate of 6°C per minute. Subsequently, the temperature was ramped to 270°C at 10°C per minute, then to 300°C at 5°C per minute, and finally to 320°C at 20°C per minute, where it was held for 5.5 min. Data acquisition was performed in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. The transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole temperatures were set at 280°C, 230°C, and 150°C, respectively. Electron impact ionization was conducted at 70 eV.86,87

Library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from sweet corn kernels at 14, 21, and 28 days after pollination using the R6834 Total RNA Kit I, with three biological replicates prepared for each developmental stage. A total of 25 μg of RNA, meeting quality control standards, was used for enrichment, reverse transcription, purification, and ligation of sequencing adapters. The resulting libraries from all nine samples were loaded onto an FLO-PRO004RA sequencing flow cell and sequenced on a PromethION platform for 48 to 72 h.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analysis of sugar metabolite-related data

Hierarchical cluster analysis was performed using the pheatmap package in R (Figure 1B). Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis was conducted with the OPLSR. Anal function from the MetaboAnalystR package (Figure 1C). Metabolites showing significant differences between groups were identified based on log2FC and Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores. Metabolite annotation was carried out using the KEGG compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) and the KEGG pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Subsequently, these data were subjected to metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) to elucidate relevant metabolic pathways (Figure 1F).

Processing of DRS raw data

Base calling of the raw pod5/fast5 files was performed using Dorado software, converting the data into FASTQ format sequence files. Subsequent data filtering was applied based on the average quality score (Q score) of the sequencing reads, with a threshold set at Q ≥ 10; reads meeting this criterion were retained as high-quality clean reads. These clean reads were then aligned to the maize reference genome using minimap2,73 and the alignment results were summarized with samtools.74 Consensus transcript identification was performed using Flair software,75 while transcriptome assembly and redundancy removal from the alignment data were achieved using StringTie76 (Figure S2A). To compare the DRS-derived transcripts with existing genome annotations and to identify novel transcripts, the gffcompare77 tool was employed (Figure S2B). Functional annotation of the newly identified transcripts was carried out using the KEGG Orthologs database (Figure S2C). Finally, alternative splicing events were analyzed using SUPPA2 software78 (Figure 2D).

Poly(A) site and length analysis

Quantifypoly(A) was used to identify and annotate poly(A) sites from reads aligned to the reference genome.79 Changes in 3′ UTR length were statistically analyzed using Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient (R value) and significance (p-value). A change was defined as '3' UTR lengthening’ when R > 0 and p < 0.05, and as '3' UTR shortening’ when R < 0 and p < 0.05 (Figures 3F and 3G). For the statistical analysis of the poly(A) signal AAUAAA and its variants in the NUE conserved region, the Chi-squared test was used to determine significant differences in poly(A) signal usage between proximal and distal conserved sites (Figure 3H). The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to analyze significant differences in poly(A) tail length. Transcripts with a median poly(A) tail length difference greater than 15 between two comparison groups and a false discovery rate (FDR) value less than 0.05 were defined as significantly differential poly(A) tail lengths.

Identification of differentially expressed genes and transcripts

Gene and transcript expression levels were quantified using Salmon,80 with transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) as the metric for expression measurement (Figures 2F and S3A). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 software package.81 Fold change values were calculated as the ratio of base mean expression values between two comparison groups. p-values were obtained using a negative binomial (NB) model and were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to produce q-values.88 Genes and transcripts exhibiting |log2 FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value <0.05 were considered differentially expressed (Figures 2H and S3C).

Comprehensive analysis integrating metabolomic and transcriptomic datasets

Canonical Correlation Analysis was performed on the differential sugar metabolites and transcripts across each comparison group using the CCA (R) 1.2.1 package82 (Figures 4A, S9A, and S9D). Pearson correlation coefficients between transcripts and sugar metabolites were calculated with the cor function in R. Subsequently, fold changes of elements exhibiting correlation coefficients greater than 0.99 within each differential comparison group were visualized using nine-quadrant plots (Figures 4B, S9B and S9E).
