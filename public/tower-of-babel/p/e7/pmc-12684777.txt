Safety and Feasibility of YC‐6 in Patients With Large Hemispheric Infarction: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Huiqing Hou, Baoyu Feng, Dawei Zang, Zixiao Li, Yilong Wang, Xingquan Zhao, Yongjun Wang, Shuya Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant cerebral edema is a critical cause of poor outcomes in large hemispheric infarction and novel neuroprotective agents are urgently needed. We aimed to assess the safety and preliminarily explored the feasibility of YC‐6 (5α‐androst‐3β,5,6β‐triol for injection) in large hemispheric infarction.MethodsThis study was a prospective, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, proof‐of‐concept trial. Patients with large hemispheric infarction in middle cerebral artery within 12 hours of symptom onset were enrolled. Patients were assigned to receive either 300 mg YC‐6 or placebo twice a day for 10 days, randomly. The primary safety outcomes were serious adverse events and all‐cause death. The primary efficacy outcome was the distribution of the modified Rankin Scale score at 90 days.ResultsThe incidence of serious adverse events (52.2% and 43.5%) and all‐cause death (39.1% and 40.0%) within 90 days were similar between YC‐6 and placebo group. There was no statistical difference between the groups in the distribution of modified Rankin Scale score (common odds ratio 0.73, 95% CI: 0.25 to 2.18). The proportion of a modified Rankin Scale score of 0 to 4 was 56.5% in YC‐6 group compared with 35.0% in placebo group (P=0.16). In addition, YC‐6 treatment numerically alleviated edema volume at 72 hours and 10 days (RD −15.02, 95% CI: −40.94 to 10.90; RD −10.35, 95% CI: −46.69 to 25.98) and decreased midline shift at 10 days (RD −0.6, 95% CI: −4.17 to 2.97).ConclusionsThis study preliminarily explored the safety and feasibility of YC‐6 in patients with large hemispheric infarction. Based on this trial, the phase 2 trial of YC‐6 was worthy of anticipation.Registration http://www.chinadrugtrials.org.cn; Unique identifier: CTR20192127; http://www.chictr.org.cn; Unique identifier: ChiCTR2400088887.Keywords: cerebral edema, large hemispheric infarction, neuroprotectionSubject Categories: Ischemia, Clinical Studies, Ischemic Stroke, Neuroprotectants, Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke

The middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarction, a typical subtype of large hemispheric infarction, often leads to severe cerebral edema related with subsequent death or worse outcome. 1 , 2 , 3 Although decompressive craniectomy has been demonstrated to be effective to reduce mortality and severe disability, 4 the clinical application is limited due to its invasiveness and optimal interventional time, especially surgical complication. 5 Recent clinical studies have indicated that intravenous glibenclamide potentially reduced cerebral edema in patients with large hemispheric infarction. 6 , 7 Preclinical studies also found many compounds to reduce cerebral edema. 8 , 9 Safe and feasible strategies with multi‐target mechanism may greatly mitigate edema in patients with large hemispheric infarction.

YC‐6, a steroid compound 5α‐androst‐3β,5,6β‐triol, is a novel agent in antiedema and neuroprotection. Preclinical studies have showed that YC‐6 targeted several pathways to impact neuroprotective efficacy, as glutamate‐medicated mechanisms, mitochondrial disfunction, and inflammatory. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Especially, YC‐6 reduced brain water content by suppressing glutamate‐induced excitotoxicity. 12

In this proof‐of‐concept clinical trial, we aimed to explore the safety and feasibility of YC‐6 in patients with large hemispheric infarction.

METHODS

Data Availability and Sharing

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design

This was a double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled proof‐of‐concept study evaluating the safety and feasibility of YC‐6 in patients with acute ischemic stroke. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal proxies at enrollment. The trial was conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the Declaration of Helsinki and was supervised by an independent data and safety monitoring committee. The trial was reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Tiantan Hospital and each study center. Clinical trial registration: http://www.chinadrugtrials.org.cn. Unique identifier: CTR20192127; http://www.chictr.org.cn. Unique identifier: ChiCTR2400088887 (for a supplementary registration for the publication of this article in English).

Participants

The enrollment started on November 10, 2021 (first subject informed consent form signing date) and was terminated on February 23, 2024 (last subject follow‐up completion date). The study enrolled 46 patients aged 18 to 85 years from 9 centers in China who diagnosed as ischemic stroke within 12 hours of symptom onset. The participating centers were listed in Table S1. Inclusion criteria were acute ischemic stroke (infarcted area >1/2 of MCA in computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging or >2/3 MCA in computed tomography perfusion imaging, and also the occlusion of internal carotid artery or M1 segment of MCA in computed tomography angiography or magnetic resonance angiography). Additional inclusion criteria were the baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score ranged from 15 to 25 and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was less than 2 before onset. Patients were excluded if they had received or intended to received decompressive craniectomy; vascular recanalization was successfully performed (NIHSS decreased more than 4 or mRS was less than or equal to 1 after thrombolysis, and modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction more than or equal to 2b after thrombectomy) before the study drug treatment; symptomatic cerebral infarction was present in both cerebral hemispheres; clinical signs of cerebral hernia was present (1 or both sides of the pupil dilated, fixed, brain stem reflex disappeared); history of severe kidney diseases, severe liver diseases, malignant tumors, or other critical diseases; Females were in pregnancy or lactating; or the investigators considered other conditions that was not appropriate in this study. Protocol deviations were summarized in Table S2.

Randomization and Masking

Eligible participants underwent 1:1 randomization to receive either intravenous YC‐6 (Guangzhou Cellprotek Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China; 50 mg/vial) administered at 300 mg or matching placebo (hydroxypropyl betadex injection; same manufacturer, 1 g/vial) administered at 6 g. The intervention protocol mandated treatment initiation within 12 hours of symptom onset. The dosage of YC‐6 was determined based on phase 1 clinical trial data showing 300 mg as the maximum tolerated dose. The randomization was conducted by an interactive Web response system (Randomization and Trial Supply Management eBalance, version 5.X; Zhejiang Taimei Medical Technology Co, Ltd, China). The randomization code was kept in a highly secure digital storage system with strict access controls. Only authorized personnel could access the randomization code, and it was encrypted to prevent unauthorized viewing or modification. The treatment was masked to the involved personnel (including the sponsor, investigators, and participants). Both treatment groups had the same drug vials in appearance.

Procedures

The bolus of the study drug was 30 mL (300 mg or 6 g), dissolved into 100 mL saline. The intravenous infusion was administered for 40 minutes twice a day continuously for 10 days. The maximum tolerated dose was based on the phase I trial. YC‐6 and placebo were stored and transported at room temperature between 10 °C and 30 °C in a sealed container. Drugs and procedures for neuroprotective purposes (including edaravone, preparation containing edaravone, butylphthalide, mild hypothermia etc.) were prohibited within 14 days after signing the informed consent. All other treatments followed the standard of management for ischemic stroke.

Clinical assessments were conducted at each site by trained and certified doctors who were unaware of the trial group assignments. The NIHSS score was evaluated at baseline and 10 days. The mRS score, Barthel index, and European Quality of Life‐5 Dimensions (EQ‐5D) at 30 and 90 days. All the assessment were in person or by telephone (when face‐to‐face interview was unavailable).

Magnetic resonance imaging scan was obtained at baseline (defined as results of imaging examination within 24 hours), 72 hours, and 10 days. Images were obtained on a 3.0 T system (including diffusion‐weighted imaging, fluid attenuated inversion recovery, 2D‐T1w, 2D‐T2w, susceptibility weighted imaging, TOF‐MRA), and interpreted with 3D Slicer software which reported in previous studies. 14 , 15 All magnetic resonance imaging scans were transferred to Tiantan Neuroimaging Center of Excellence, China National Clinical Research Center for Neurological Diseases and analyzed by 2 professional physicians who were blinded to the assignment of treatment groups and clinical outcomes. Cerebral edema was evaluated by the change of midline shift, lesion volume, and edema volume at 72 hours and 10 days from baseline.

Outcomes

The primary safety outcomes were serious adverse events (SAEs) and all‐cause death within 90 days. The secondary safety outcomes were adverse events (AEs), treatment emergent AEs, drug withdrawal for any reason and drug withdrawal for AEs. SAEs and AEs were categorized according to the standard terminology.

The primary efficacy outcome was the distribution of mRS scores at 90 days. The secondary efficacy outcomes were the proportion of an mRS score of 0 to 4 at 90 days, NIHSS change of 10 days after treatment from the baseline, the proportion of an mRS score of 0 to 2 at 30 and 90 days, Barthel index score at 30 and 90 days, EQ‐5D score at 30 and 90 days, and the proportion of patients who had decompressive craniectomy indications within 90 days. The exploratory outcomes were the changes of midline shift, lesion volume, and edema volume at 72 hours and 10 days of symptom onset from baseline.

Statistical Analysis

This study was a preliminary exploratory clinical trial, and no sample size was calculated. All statistical analyses were preplanned. Efficacy analyses were performed in the full analysis set and per‐protocol set. The full analysis set included all randomly assigned participants who received at least 1 dose of the study drug, while the per‐protocol set comprised participants who completed the study according to the protocol without major protocol deviations. Categorical variables were presented as counts and proportions and compared using Chi‐square test or Fisher exact test between the YC‐6 group and placebo group. Continuous variables were summarized as means with SDs, or medians with interquartile range, and compared using t‐tests or rank sum tests. For ordinary mRS score at 90 days, an ordinal analysis was conducted to compare the distribution between groups, with common odds ratio and corresponding 95% CI calculated. For dichotomous efficacy outcomes, the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel Chi‐squared test adjusting for the site effect was used for comparison of end points between groups, with risk difference (RD) and related 95% CI calculated. For continuous efficacy outcomes, analysis of covariance or Wilcoxon rank test was used to compare end points between groups, and RD and related 95% CI were calculated.

Safety analyses were performed on the safety set, which included patients who received at least 1 dose of the study medication and had at least 1 post‐treatment safety assessment. For safety outcomes, counts and proportions were calculated for both the YC‐6 and placebo groups. For all‐cause mortality, log‐rank test was conducted to compare differences between groups, and for other safety outcomes, Chi‐square test or Fisher exact test were used.

All statistical analyses were performed by SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). All P values were reported as 2‐sided, and P<0.05 was set to be statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patients’ Characteristics

From November 2021 to February 2024, a total of 49 patients were enrolled, and 46 patients were randomized (Figure 1). Patients’ characteristics were almost similar between the YC‐6 group and placebo group (Table 1). The median of NIHSS in the 2 groups was 18 (IQR, 16–21) and 17 (IQR, 16–20). Five patients in the YC‐6 group and 1 patient in the placebo group were treated with intravenous thrombolysis. No patients were administrated with decompressive craniectomy.

Safety Outcomes

No significant differences were observed in SAEs (52.2% and 43.5%) and all‐cause death (39.1% and 40.0%) within 90 days between the 2 groups (Table 2). There were no significant differences in the secondary safety outcomes. A list of AEs and SAEs was provided in Tables S3 and S4. The incidence of infection and infestation complications was 56.5% in the YC‐6 group and 39.1% in the placebo group, which mainly included pulmonary infection and urinary tract infection. Six of 23 (26.1%) in the YC‐6 group and 2 of 23 (8.7%) in the placebo group withdrew the experimental drug due to AEs (not related to experimental drug), while 1 of 23 (4.3%) in the YC‐6 group withdrew the experimental drug due to a transfer to another department.

Efficacy Outcomes

Three patients in the placebo group were missing an mRS score at 90 days. There was no difference between the YC‐6 and placebo groups in the distribution of mRS score (common odds ratio 0.73, 95% CI: 0.25 to 2.18) (Table 1; Figure 2). In the secondary efficacy outcomes, the proportion of an mRS score of 0 to 4 was 56.5% in the YC‐6 group compared with 35.0% in the placebo group at 90 days (P=0.16). No significant differences were investigated in all other functional outcomes and proportion of patients with decompressive craniectomy indications in the 2 groups (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Our most important finding is that YC‐6 treatment is well tolerated in patients with large cerebral infarction in this proof‐of‐concept study. In addition, the YC‐6 group numerically increased the proportion of patients who had an mRS of 0 to 4 at 90 days, decreased midline shift at 10 days, and alleviated edema volume at 72 hours and 10 days compared with the placebo group. This result inspires a further clinical trial to explore the safety and efficacy YC‐6 with appropriate dosage in patients with large cerebral infarction.

Although the clinical benefit of neuroprotective therapy encounters challenges, it is still regarded as a promising treatment for improving the prognosis of stroke. New neuroprotective agents that target multiple pathways of the ischemic cascade should be taken seriously if they placed robust neuroprotective effects in preclinical models. 16 YC‐6, a novel synthesized compound, was confirmed to target multiple aspects in mechanism of neuroprotection in preclinical studies. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Additionally, YC‐6 could reduce cerebral edema and is more suitable for treating severe edema in patients with large hemispheric infarction. 12 This study enrolled stroke patients within 12 hours of onset, involved more than 50% of the MCA, and confirmed occlusion in internal carotid artery or MCA M1 segment. This preliminary study explored the safety profiles of YC‐6 at a dose of 300 mg twice daily. We will further explore the optimal dosage and expand the sample size to confirm the safety and efficacy of YC‐6 in patients with malignant middle cerebral artery infarction.

No statistical difference in all AEs was observed during the follow‐up between the YC‐6 and placebo groups. There was no significant impact of YC‐6 on blood glucose level in this study. The incidence of infections and infestations complications was common in patients with large hemispheric infarction. The primary contributing factors were associated with severe clinical conditions and impaired consciousness. Given that this study was a proof‐of‐concept clinical trial with a limited sample size, the data should be interpreted as preliminary and indicative. Although this present study did not meet the primary efficacy end point of evaluating a significant difference in the distribution of 90‐day mRS score between the 2 groups, YC‐6 treatment numerically improved the proportion of mRS score of 0 to 4 at 90 days and alleviated cerebral edema at 72 hours and 10 days. Our findings could be explained by several mechanisms mediated by neuroprotective effects of YC‐6, which might be closely related with development of edema. Firstly, preclinical studies showed that YC‐6 reduced vasogenic edema and brain injury by suppressing glutamate‐mediated excitotoxicity through promoting the excessive influx of intracellular calcium through the N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate receptor. 12 Previous studies also indicated that neuronal death of basal cortex and cerebral edema of early brain injury could be attenuated by inhibiting glutamate‐mediated excitotoxicity. 17 In addition, YC‐6 could mitigate intracellular acidification and neuronal injury resulting from glutamate‐induced lactate accumulation through AMPK‐mediated neuronal reprogramming in ischemic stroke model. 13 Secondly, YC‐6 could prevent neuronal viability, mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production and oxidative stress in primary cultured cortical neurons exposed to hypoxia followed by reoxygenation. 10 Consistent with this context, there might be a causative correlation between cytotoxic edema and mitochondrial damage or oxidative stress. 18 , 19 , 20 Thirdly, YC‐6 might have a neuroprotective effect in neuroinflammation related diseases by downregulating tumor necrosis factor‐α‐induced inflammatory response. 11 Tumor necrosis factor‐α receptor inhibitor played a protective role against neurological impairment and cerebral edema in rat stroke model. 21 Moreover, therapeutic target of tumor necrosis factor‐α pathway significantly decreased brain edema after acute ischemic stroke. 22 , 23

Our results revealed that a further clinical trial should be designed with optimal enrolled stroke patients, considering stroke severity and time window. On the other hand, the data of image indicators missed because of death caused brain hernia also induced deviation of our research results. Therefore, we aimed to further verify the effect of neuroprotection and antiedema of YC‐6 treatment in more suitable patients in a larger clinical trial.

This study had some limitations. The first limitation is this proof‐of‐concept study provided limited data because of the small sample size of enrolled patients. The second limitation is that only 1 patient reached the criteria of decompressive craniectomy indications during the follow‐up in each group. The selection bias of the subjects might limit the representativeness of the patients with large cerebral infarction. The third limitation is that the exploratory indicators might not be the most advanced, which limited image calculation methods. Finally, patients in the trial were almost all Han Chinese with large cerebral infarction, and caution is needed when extrapolating our findings to other ethnic populations and other stroke subtypes.

CONCLUSIONS

YC‐6 treatment was safe and feasible in patients with large cerebral infarction. Further studies are needed to provide more evidence on the safety and efficacy of YC‐6 in patients with large cerebral infarction.
