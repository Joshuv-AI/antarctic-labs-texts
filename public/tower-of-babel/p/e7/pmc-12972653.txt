Observations of the developmental stages of Coquillettidia perturbans (Diptera: Culicidae) with special reference to the pupal trumpets
Authors: Nicholas Benton, Fiona F Hunter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Coquillettidia perturbans (Walker) is a North American mosquito species of medical and veterinary importance that has been implicated as a vector of eastern equine encephalitis virus. Larval and pupal C. perturbans possess highly specialized respiratory appendages that enable them to respire from air vesicles in the hollow roots and shoots of emergent aquatic vegetation, which means that the larval and pupal stages have remained largely understudied. Field-collected C. perturbans larvae, pupae, and adults were studied under laboratory conditions in an attempt to establish a laboratory colony. Blood-fed adults successfully laid egg rafts in containers provisioned with water hyacinth plants (Pontederia crassipes). Larvae and pupae were maintained on hydroponically grown wheat seedlings (Triticum sp.). Images and descriptions of egg rafts, larvae, and pupae are provided with special reference to hatch success, larval instar traits and pupal trumpet morphology.

Introduction

Coquillettidia perturbans (Walker) (Diptera: Culicidae), commonly known as the “cattail mosquito” extends from southern Canada to northern Mexico (Johnson et al. 2018). This species belongs to a unique group of mosquitoes (tribe Mansoniini Belkin) which possess modified respiratory larval siphons and pupal trumpets for obtaining oxygen from aquatic plant roots and shoots. These modifications enable the larvae and pupae of C. perturbans to reside in deep, permanent freshwater habitats, including marsh edges and overgrown pools with dense aquatic vegetation. C. perturbans use a variety of aquatic plants for respiration, such as water sedges (Carex spp.), water hyacinth (Pontederia crassipes Solms), and cattails (Typha spp.) (Lewis and Bennett 1980, Lounibos and Escher 1983, Slaff and Haefner 1985, Morris et al. 1990). All larval instars and pupae obtain oxygen by piercing into plant tissue, which indicates that the modifications of the larval siphon and pupal trumpets are vital for C. perturbans survival. While thorough descriptions of the larval siphon exist (Bosak and Crans 2002), the pupal stage of C. perturbans has yet to be described in detail.

C. perturbans is a species of growing medical and veterinary importance in North America, being incriminated as a bridge vector for eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV) in multiple states (Crans and Schultz 1986, Crans et al. 1986, Nasci et al. 1993, Moncayo and Edman 1999, Cupp et al. 2003, Sherwood et al. 2020). C. perturbans is considered to be an opportunistic blood-feeder, feeding on various avian and mammalian hosts (Apperson et al. 2002, Molaei et al. 2008, Watts et al. 2009). Despite its potential medical and veterinary importance, the unique adaptations of larval and pupal stages have made it difficult to study and control the aquatic stages of C. perturbans. The current study was initiated in an attempt to establish a laboratory colony of C. perturbans and along the way, previously undocumented aspects of egg raft hatching success, larval instar traits and pupal trumpet morphology were observed.

Materials and Methods

Field Collection, Laboratory Methods, and Colony Maintenance

Adult C. perturbans were collected using two methods. CDC miniature light traps (John W. Hock Company, Gainesville, Florida, United States), baited with CO2 and UV light, were set in St. Catharines, ON and a stormwater management pond in Toronto, ON. Supplemental sweep-netting at dusk was also conducted on public roads near Lake Gibson in Thorold, ON.

The adults were housed in 32 × 32 × 32 cm BugDorm cages (BugDorm, Faichung, Taiwan) provisioned with 10% sucrose ad libitum. Prior to blood-feeding, female mosquitoes were starved for 24 h. Mosquitoes were fed using a method revised from the artificial feeding method provided in Jové et al. (2020). Cages containing 20 field-collected females were fed using a Hemotek 6W1 artificial feeding system with 1 mL reservoirs (Hemotek, Blackburn, United Kingdom) with a blood-meal comprised of 2 mL of sterile sheep blood (Rockland Immunochemicals, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States) and 20 uL of 20 mM ATP. In preliminary experiments, one cage was fed using the collagen membrane provided with the Hemotek system (MEM5) and 4 cages were fed using a feeding membrane made from mouse skin plucked of hair. Sugar meals were reintroduced immediately after blood-feeding. Three days post-blood-feeding, a 1.2 L container of dechlorinated tap water provisioned with a small P. crassipes plant was provided for oviposition. This method was modified from methods used by Laurence and Smith (1958) in a related taxon, Mansonia africana (Taeniorhynchus africanus).

Larvae, Pupae Collection and Maintenance

From March to June 2023, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th larval instars and pupae were collected from cattails (Typha sp.) pulled from a ∼1 to 3 m, densely vegetated pond near Lake Gibson in Thorold, ON. To collect immatures, cattail roots were submerged in a water-filled 3 L bucket and shaken vigorously, based on a technique used to sample C. perturbans by Lounibos and Escher (1983). Pupae collected in June were transported to the lab while still attached to the Typha spp. roots so that roots could be sliced with a scalpel to reveal the inner air ­vesicles and pupal trumpets inside the roots for imaging.

There is a paucity of information on C. perturbans egg raft shape, size, and quality. After examining a variety of egg rafts laid in captivity, we wanted to ascertain which types of egg rafts should be used when trying to establish a colony. Thus, egg rafts were collected, assigned to one of five grades (as described in Fig. 1) and placed into individual 1.2 L plastic containers filled with dechlorinated water to determine hatching rates. The containers were kept in an enclosed greenhouse at 24 to 26 °C and 12 h Light:12 h Dark photoperiod. A small number of first larval instars was collected for imaging and the rest were reared to later larval instars.

Larval C. perturbans were given living plant substrates for attachment. Due to their rapid growth and ease of maintenance, wheat seedlings (Triticum sp.) were grown hydroponically using a method modified from Guille (1973) and Johnson and Russell (2019). Aluminum window screen sheets (17 × 15 cm) were used to hold wheat seeds barely submerged below the water line, which allowed roots to grow through the screen and into the water column. Seedlings needed to be changed every 5 to 7 d to minimize root rot. Larvae were fed 0.1 L per 1.2 L container daily of a diet made of 1.25 g crushed dog biscuits and 5 pellets of specific pathogen free mouse (Mus musculus) dung infused into 150 mL of dechlorinated tap water and left in the greenhouse for 3 to 5 d to encourage bacterial growth. Most wild-caught 3rd and 4th larval instars were reared to pupation to observe attachment to the wheat seedling roots. Larval attachment was passively observed when dislodging larvae during wheat seedling changes and container cleaning.

Larvae were killed in boiling water then placed in 70% ethanol to preserve coloration and structural features. Preserved larvae and live pupae were photographed using a Keyence digital microscope. SEM photographs of pupal trumpets were captured using a HITACHI (model SU5000) scanning electron microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Hatch rates of the different egg raft grades were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test with significance values adjusted using Bonferroni correction. All Statistical analysis was performed in R (version 4.3.1).

Results and Discussion

The collagen membrane supplied with the Hemotek system resulted in no mosquitoes taking a bloodmeal. However, for the 4 cages in which hairless mouse skin was used, the number of females that blood-fed per cage ranged from 9/20 to 12/20 for an average of 11 ± 1.1 (SD). In subsequent attempts to blood-feed, only hairless mouse skin was used.

Complete egg rafts were oval or teardrop-shaped and contained approximately 150 to 200 eggs. Most rafts were observed floating on the surface of the water although some egg rafts were observed along the edge of the plastic oviposition containers (Fig. 2A) or underneath the leaves of P. crassipes (Fig. 2B). Eggs were already melanized when observed (Fig. 2C).

Throughout the study a total of 65 egg rafts were collected from wild-caught or laboratory reared adults. Of these, only 4 egg rafts failed to hatch over the course of 14 d, including those in grade F. Eggs hatched into 1st larval instars, on average, in 3.8 ± 1.4 (SD) days with a range of 2 to 8 d. Fig. 3 shows the number of hatched 1st larval instars recovered per egg raft grade. Eggs from grade F egg rafts did not hatch and were excluded from further analyses. The hatch rates of grade A and grade B egg rafts were not different from one another, nor were the hatch rates of grade C and grade D egg rafts. Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction showed that hatch rates of grade A egg rafts were significantly higher than grade C egg rafts (adjusted P = 0.002) and grade D egg rafts (adjusted P < 0.001). Similarly, hatch rates of grade B egg rafts were significantly higher than grade C egg rafts (adjusted P = 0.017) and grade D egg rafts (adjusted P = 0.001).

Immediately upon hatching, the 1st larval instar larvae were almost completely transparent (Fig. 2D) and remained transparent until molting into the 2nd larval instar. As larvae matured, they would slowly become cream-colored. The 1st larval instars measured less than a millimeter in length and the larval saddle was entirely transparent. The 2nd larval instars were larger with fully cream-colored bodies, although the larval saddle remained pale. The 3rd larval instars showed a further increase in size and the introduction of darker pigmentation on the thoracic region and tan-colored larval saddles. Finally, the 4th larval instars were the largest at approximately 1 cm. Laboratory-reared 4th larval instars were completely cream-colored. In contrast, 4th larval instars collected from the wild were often cream-colored with green splotches on the thorax or they were completely green and, on rare occasions, they were so dark that their thoraces and abdomens appeared black. This suggests that C. perturbans larval coloration may depend on conditions in their natural habitat, such as their diet. Wild larval diets consist of algae and chlorophyll-containing microbes (Merritt et al. 1990). Further research might consider the effects that larval coloration has on survival and predation in the wild. Larval filter-feeding observations were compatible with descriptions provided by Merritt et al. (1990). Larval attachment appeared to have grown stronger as the larval instars progressed, as observed by Lounibos and Escher (1983). Larvae would almost immediately detach when disturbed when 1st or 2nd instars, but would often require vigorous shaking or dislodging with forceps when 3rd and 4th instars. However, the plants used in the laboratory were not those from which wild-collected larvae were sourced. Wild-collected 3rd and 4th larval instars detached from and then re-attached at a different location on the wheat seedling roots on a fairly regular basis (every 2 to 6 h). Presumably, this is because the air vesicles in the wheat roots are not as large as those found in aquatic plants such as Typha sp., thus requiring the larvae to find new attachment sites. It would be beneficial to determine if, and how often, wild C. perturbans larvae reposition themselves to obtain oxygen when on their native host plants.

Pupal Morphology and Behavior

The modified pupal trumpets (Figs. 4 and 5A) contain multiple unique structures not seen in standard mosquito pupal trumpets (Fig. 5B). The main trumpet structure, also known as the dorsal trunk, was elongated, tapering into a thinner trumpet neck. At the apex of the pupal trumpet lies the trumpet tip, an arrowhead-shaped structure comprised of numerous looped segments that are tightly connected to form the neck and tip of the trumpet (Fig. 5B and C).

In the laboratory, all C. perturbans pupae were observed attached to the vascular tissue of the wheat seedlings. Both pupal trumpets were observed anchored into a single opening (Fig. 6A and B), with the trumpet tips fully embedded in the vascular tissue (Fig. 6C and D). Pupae would remain in the same location throughout development. When dislodged, most pupae would remain motionless. The few that did move were able to move their pupal trumpets freely. Some movements appeared to be from gyrations of the tail and body, but pupae were also able to move the trumpets independently of one another. Detached pupae would move in slow deliberate movements rather than the fast tumbling motions seen in pupae of other mosquito species. Pupae would routinely arch backwards while attempting to reposition detached pupal trumpets. Despite giving pupae opportunities to reattach to wheat roots or coarse polystyrene, none was able to reattach. Fig. 6E shows pupal trumpets that are not attached to plant material, and Fig. 6F shows a detached pupa that had recently molted from a 4th larval instar.

We determined that grades A and B eggs rafts had the best hatch rates (Fig. 3) and recommend that anyone attempting to start a C. perturbans colony use eggs rafts that are as complete as possible (ie comprised of more than 100 eggs). We found that the greatest challenge for establishing a C. perturbans colony was that 4th larval instars reared from eggs would not pupate, indicating that diapause in northern populations of C. perturbans is obligatory.

The pupal stage of C. perturbans incorporates many unique morphological and behavioral adaptations to gaining its oxygen from the underwater roots and shoots of aquatic plants. It appears that pupae place their trumpet tips inside the hole created by the 4th larval instar larval siphon, as observed by Laurence (1960) in Mansonia uniformis. The trumpet tips have been found to possess structures that further anchor the trumpets into the attachment substrate. Pupal trumpet tips were never observed on exuviae, and trumpet tips were the most frequent break-off point when attempting to remove pupae from roots. This suggests that the trumpet tips break off and allow the pupae to float to the surface before the adults emerge.

Understanding the basic biology of the immature stages of this species may lead to the identification of new methods for interfering with larval siphon attachment and/or pupal trumpet tip insertion as a novel approach to controlling C. perturbans populations.
