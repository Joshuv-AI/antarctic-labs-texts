Assessing training needs of hand tendon injuries management in Saudi residents: valid and reliable tool
Authors: Ahmed M Sonbol, Arwa Abulnaja, Asail A Alammar, Lana Alshawwa, Hind I Fallatah, Ali H Alyami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand tendon injuries have substantial implications for individuals and communities, necessitating accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. These aspects are typically integral components of orthopedics and plastic surgery residency training in numerous countries. This study aims to assess the need for additional educational interventions among residents in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA 16-item questionnaire assessed hand tendon management topics, with content validity established using Lawshe’s method. A cross-sectional study included 231 participants, analyzing variables through descriptive statistics, reliability analysis (Cronbach’s Alpha), and statistical tests (Chi-square, t-tests, ANOVA) to examine relationships, compare means, and identify predictors (p < 0.05).ResultsThe majority of respondents were board residents (81.4%, n = 188) specializing in orthopedics (90.9%, n = 210). Findings revealed positive outcomes with above-average mean ratings across all aspects of hand tendon injury management topics. Overall needs were evaluated using a hundred-point mean score, yielding a mean of 78.61 (SD = 19.5) indicating a generally favorable needs in this area. Among the topic categories, basic sciences received the highest mean score (mean = 81.85, SD = 21.3), while secondary flexor tendon surgery had the lowest mean score (mean = 73.48, SD = 25.8). Significant correlations were observed among all sub-categories (p < 0.001) indicating construct validity. Reliability analysis showed high Cronbach’s alpha values, indicating strong internal consistency across different categories.ConclusionsFurther training interventions are crucial for effective hand tendon injury management among residents in Saudi Arabia, with a focus on comprehensive knowledge acquisition rather than solely skill development.Keywords: Hand injuries, Tendon injury, Residency and internship, Simulation training, Educational needs assessment, Competency-based education

Background

Tendon injuries in the hand and wrist are prevalent, constituting a significant proportion of cases encountered in the emergency department, ranging from 6.6–20%[1, 2]. In Islamic countries, a surge of injuries related to the slaughter and processing of meat occurs during the three days of festivities following the Hajj ceremony, with tendon lacerations accounting for 11% of these cases [3, 4]. The implications of untreated or improperly treated tendon injuries are substantial for both patients and communities [5, 6]. Optimal outcomes in tendon repair are attributed to the meticulous and skilled technique of the surgeon [7].

A comprehensive needs assessment study was carried out in Denmark using the Delphi method, involving 95 experts in orthopedic surgery training. Among the 33 procedures deemed essential for general orthopedic surgeon competencies in a simulation-based curriculum, tendon repair techniques ranked eighth [8]. Moreover, 61 plastic surgery program directors emphasized the importance of mastering the zone II flexor tendon repair with a multistrand technique for graduating plastic surgery residents [9]. Consequently, the proficiency in tendon repair is considered crucial in the training programs of plastic surgery and orthopedics residencies [9, 10]. Recognizing the importance of this skill, the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS) has incorporated tendon repair into the curriculum of orthopedic and plastic surgery Saudi boards [11, 12].

Adequately assessing trainees’ learning needs locally is vital for effective planning, monitoring, and enhancement of the educational process, ensuring the relevance of educational activities and consistency in training [13, 14]. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the necessity for additional educational interventions in the training of management of hand tendon injuries among orthopedic and plastic surgery residents in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Tool development and content validation

To develop the initial questionnaire for assessing hand tendon management, the main topics were derived from Tang’s book “Tendon Surgery of the Hand“ [15]. Each item in the questionnaire utilized a 5-point Likert scale to evaluate participants’ perceptions of the relevance to residency training and the need for further educational intervention to enhance knowledge or skills. The scale ranged from Strongly Disagree (score of 0) to Strongly Agree (score of 4). Scores were calculated using a simple additive method and converted to a hundred-point scale. The questionnaire encompassed five sections: basic science, primary flexor tendon repair, secondary flexor tendon surgery, extensor tendon repair and reconstruction, and rehabilitation.

Content validity was established using Lawshe’s method, which involved determining the content validity ratio (CVR) for individual items [16] and the content validity index (CVI) for the overall questionnaire [17]. A panel of nine experts reviewed the questionnaire, including one plastic surgeon and four orthopedic surgeons specialized in upper extremity and hand surgery, of them, two had degree in medical education, and four hand and upper extremity surgery fellows. Based on the evaluation by the experts, a critical CVR of 0.778 was recommended for each item [18]. Consequently, items with CVR scores below this threshold were eliminated, resulting in an improved CVI of 0.911 (compared to the initial CVI of 0.767), indicating excellent content validity of the tool [19]. The final version of the questionnaire comprised 16 items.

Implementation and data collection

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Unit of Biomedical Ethics at the Faculty of Medicine in King Abdulaziz University, with reference number 54 − 23. An electronic form of the questionnaire was developed utilizing Google Forms as the platform for data collection. Orthopedic and plastic surgeons, along with residents from multiple hospitals in the three major cities of Saudi Arabia (Riyadh, Jeddah, and Makkah), were personally approached and requested to independently complete the questionnaire using a provided barcode. Strict adherence to privacy and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, as no identifying data were collected. The purpose and objectives of the study were explicitly communicated to all potential participants through a cover letter prior to their enrollment. By choosing to complete the questionnaire, participants indicated their voluntary consent to participate in the study. The survey instrument comprised two main sections: the first section encompassed demographics, training and practice patterns, and experience in hand tendon injury management, while the second section consisted of the developed needs assessment questionnaire focusing on the need for hand tendon injury management training.

Statistical analysis

The data collected in this study were analyzed and visualized using IBM SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) and GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics were employed to characterize the study variables, presenting categorical and nominal variables as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations.

We suggest a leaning towards agreement on a 5-point Likert scale when the average score centers above the midpoint (i.e., 2 on a 0 to 4 scale). If the average score surpasses this midpoint in all items, it represents the main finding of the study. Reliability Analysis was conducted using Cronbach’s Alpha to assess the properties of measurement scales and the items comprising those scales, as well as the average inter-item correlation.

For statistical comparisons, the Chi-square test was used to examine the relationship between categorical variables. Independent t-tests and One-way ANOVA, followed by the Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test, were utilized to compare means between two groups and more than two groups, respectively, assuming a normal distribution. In cases where the assumption of normality was violated, Welch’s t-test and Games Howell post hoc test were employed as alternatives to the LSD test for comparing two group means and multiple groups, respectively. Binary outcome variables were considered as dependent variables, and General Linear Model Univariate Analysis was employed to identify significant predictors using the Main Effect model. The conventional p-value threshold of < 0.05 was used to reject the null hypothesis.

Participants displayed a notable demand for knowledge across various domains. In the basic sciences segment, there was a clear need for understanding anatomical hand tendon features, nutrition and healing of tendons, and biomechanics of core and peripheral tendon repairs. Within primary flexor tendon repair, participants highlighted the importance of comprehending indications, tendon sheath and pulleys, repair techniques, special considerations, re-rupture management, traumatic avulsions, and outcomes assessment. Secondary flexor tendon surgery underscored the significance of knowledge in tenolysis procedures. Extensor tendon repair and reconstruction emphasized primary management indications per zone, sagittal band injuries, and reconstruction techniques. Lastly, in rehabilitation, residents emphasized the importance of knowledge in rehabilitating both flexor and extensor tendon injuries.

Notably, the basic sciences segment obtained the highest mean score (mean = 81.85, SD = 21.3), while secondary flexor tendon surgery received the lowest mean score (mean = 73.48, SD = 25.8).

Overall, favorable above-average mean scores were obtained for all categories, with the highest mean score for the knowledge needs in hand tendon anatomy topics (mean = 3.39, SD = 1.0) and the lowest for secondary flexor tendon surgery (mean = 2.94, SD = 1.0).

The knowledge needs in hand tendon injury management topics were assessed using a hundred-point scale. The results indicated an overall mean score of 78.61 (SD = 19.5), with the highest mean score observed in the basic sciences category (mean = 81.85, SD = 21.3), and the lowest mean score in secondary flexor tendon surgery (mean = 73.48, SD = 25.8). The other categories included primary flexor repair (mean = 78.85, SD = 20.9), extensor tendon repair and reconstruction (mean = 76.55, SD = 21.9), and rehabilitation (mean = 78.52, SD = 22.8).

The reliability statistics demonstrate the internal consistency of the scoring tool used to assess knowledge needs in hand tendon injury topics among residents, with favorable Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.884 for basic sciences (n = 3), 0.921 for primary flexor tendon repair (N = 7), 0.822 for secondary flexor tendon repair (N = 3), 0.841 for extensor tendon repair and reconstruction (N = 2), and 0.957 for the overall score (N = 16).

The construct validity and correlation between the needs for each sub-category of hand tendon injury topics indicated a significant correlation among all sub-categories (p < 0.001) according to a two-tailed t-test at a 0.01 level of significance. These positive values for all categories suggest the employed test’s strong validity or ability in measuring meaningful relationship between the hand tendon injury topic scores.

Specifically, participants specializing in plastic surgery had significantly higher mean scores in all hand tendon injury topic categories compared to those specializing in orthopedic surgery, indicating a greater need for knowledge in these areas among plastic surgeons. Additionally, respondents who had operated on and followed fewer than 5 cases had significantly lower mean scores in all hand tendon injury topic categories compared to those with more than 5 cases of experience. Furthermore, only the mean score for primary flexor tendon repair showed a significant difference based on the role of the residents (p = 0.048). Specifically, board graduates and other attending physicians reported a significantly higher mean score (84.61 ± 18.2) compared to current board residents (77.57 ± 21.3). Interestingly, the overall score for the need in hand tendon injury topics demonstrated significant differences based on the residents’ specialized training in hand tendon injuries (p = 0.008). Participants with specialized training reported significantly higher mean overall scores (81.31 ± 17.8) compared to those without training (74.30 ± 21.3), indicating a better understanding of the need for knowledge in these hand tendon injury topics among those with specialized training. No significant associations were observed in the mean scores based on whether the residents were junior or senior (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide supporting evidence that there is a need for further educational intervention to train residents in managing hand tendon injuries in orthopedics and plastic surgery in Saudi Arabia. This study provides a valid and reliable tool to assess the need for further hand tendon injury management training in postgraduate education.

To address this, we recommend that medical educators in postgraduate education for orthopedic and plastic surgery develop comprehensive training courses that focus on teaching the fundamental principles of hand tendon injuries management, rather than solely relying on theater experience or self-reading by residents. These courses should cover various aspects, including basic sciences, principles of primary flexor tendon repairs and special considerations, an overview of secondary tendon repair, principles of extensor tendon repair, and postoperative rehabilitation. The courses should utilize a variety of teaching methods, such as lectures, case scenarios, simulations, and group discussions. Furthermore, it is important to assess the knowledge and skills of residents in both short-term and long-term evaluations. Currently, there is no similar specialized course available in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, we encourage educators to actively participate in the design of such courses, aligning with the country’s vision for 2030, which emphasizes independent learning [20].

Tendon repair is a technically demanding skill that is better taught outside the operating room (OR) in a safe environment to allow the residents to practice their mistakes. It is later refined in the theater as advised by Sirisena et al. [21]. Unfortunately, this is against our current situation where many of our surveyed physicians built their skill in hand tendon injuries based on clinical experience.

Ingraham et al. [22] suggest that simulation outside the OR is a less stressful environment, allowing the trainee to focus on developing fine motor skills and practice. In their educational model, Bari et al. [23] proposed a practical tutorial to teach zone II flexor tendon repair on cadaveric fresh frozen tendons. There was a significant improvement in the residents’ technique that was even maintained over time.

More cost-effective simulators were studied by Vinnicombe et al. [10] and Sirisena et al. [21]especially silicone sealant, which was found to be the most realistic synthetic simulator as per Musbahi et al. [24]. In fact, those articles and most of the literature focused on skill acquisition training, simulation, or techniques comparison rather than a whole integrated curriculum, including basic science, clinical practice, and technical skills development. Our findings, in fact, showed the need for better understanding of basic sciences more than the surgical skill.

In the United States, similar to Saudi Arabia, the management of hand tendon injuries is considered a crucial clinical competency in the hand surgery milestones for orthopedic and plastic surgery residents, as outlined by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education [25]. Our findings also indicate a positive correlation between experience and the recognition of the need for specialized training among residents.

Our tool, originally designed for use in Saudi Arabia, exhibits versatility that transcends national borders, making it suitable for regions with comparable training settings. Emphasizing its adaptability and potential for global integration, we underscore that this tool is both valid and reliable, highlighting its significance in a variety of educational settings beyond the local context.

The study has several limitations, primarily stemming from its cross-sectional design. This design restricts the ability to establish causality due to the lack of temporal sequence and introduces the potential for recall bias in self-reported data. Furthermore, the study cannot examine changes over time and may be subject to selection bias resulting from the convenience sampling of participants. A further limitation arises from the potential for bias based on specialty and profession, given the overrepresentation of orthopedic specialists and the underrepresentation of plastic surgery respondents. This discrepancy can be attributed to the disparity in the annual number of trainees within each specialty in Saudi Arabia, with 379 trainees in orthopedics compared to 28 over the past four years, as reported by the SCFHS [26]. Additionally, a higher proportion of board residents participated compared to other attending physicians, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader professional audience.

Conclusions

The management of hand tendon injuries holds significant importance within the curriculum of orthopedics and plastic surgery residency programs. Relying solely on theater experience and self-readings is insufficient to adequately address the complexity of this area. The findings from a needs assessment conducted in Saudi Arabia indicate a clear demand among trainees for additional specialized training in this domain. Such training should encompass not only skill acquisition but also encompass a comprehensive understanding of basic sciences, clinical judgment, and principles of postoperative rehabilitation for various types of hand tendon injuries. In order to effectively address these learning objectives, educators are strongly encouraged to develop diverse learning opportunities, incorporating innovative teaching strategies such as simulation.
