Role of lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) in anterior cruciate ligament-injured athletes
Authors: Ghislain Aminake, Ashraf Hantouly, Matthew Free, Piero Agostinone, Alan Getgood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) has re-emerged as a significant adjunct to anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), particularly in high-level athletes who are at a heightened risk for re-injury and graft failure. This review synthesizes recent literature examining the biomechanical and clinical outcomes associated with LET in the context of ACLR in high-level athletes. Biomechanical studies indicate that LET can enhance knee stability by providing additional restraint against anterior tibial translation and rotatory instability, thereby reducing rotational laxity and offloading stress on the intra-articular graft. Cadaveric and in vivo studies suggest that LET can reduce forces applied to the graft and improve knee kinematics, particularly in the setting of concomitant injury to the anterolateral complex. Furthermore, recent clinical research has shown that incorporating LET during ACLR surgery is associated with lower rates of graft failure and improved functional outcomes, including high rates of return to pre-injury level of sport among high-level athletes. However, the literature also highlights the need for careful patient selection and consideration of potential implant-related complications, as well as the risk of lateral knee pain. Recent evidence indicates that LET does not increase the risk of lateral compartment osteoarthritis when modern surgical techniques and appropriate graft tensioning are employed. Overall, while LET shows promise in improving the outcomes of ACLR surgery in high-level athletes, further high-quality research is warranted to establish standardized protocols and long-term efficacy.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is a devastating injury with debilitating long-term consequences, particularly for athletes. It has been estimated that there are around 250,000 ACL injuries annually in the United States alone (1,2). The implications of ACL rupture extend beyond the ligament itself, often involving concomitant damage to other soft tissue structures, such as the anterolateral complex (ALC), which plays a critical role in rotatory stability.

A growing body of literature has revived interest in the ALC due to its role in stabilizing residual rotational laxity following ACL reconstruction (ACLR) (3,4), a factor closely linked to surgical outcomes and long-term joint health. Persistent anterolateral instability has been associated with graft failure following isolated ACLR (3-5), prompting a shift in surgical approaches. Consequently, many authors have proposed lateral extra-articular procedures (LEAPs), including lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) and anterolateral ligament reconstruction (ALLR), as adjunctive techniques to reinforce ACLR, thereby reducing ACL graft failure and improving postoperative stability (5).

Moreover, it has been reported that LET decreases ACL graft stress forces by approximately 43% (6). By offloading strain from the ACL graft, LET not only improves mechanical stability but also enhances biological integration. The impact extends beyond biomechanical alterations, influencing histological changes at the ACL graft level. The mitigation of excessive stress creates an environment appropriate for ligamentization and incorporation, potentially accelerating graft maturation and reducing the risk of failure (7).

Given the high physical and psychological demands placed on high-level athletes, optimizing stability and minimizing the risk of re-injury is critical for their ability to return to pre-injury performance levels. LET offers a potential advantage in this population by providing additional rotational control and reducing graft failure rates (5,8,9). As research continues to evolve, refining patient selection criteria and surgical techniques will be essential in maximizing the benefits of LET, particularly for high-level athletes who require the highest level of performance and durability from their reconstructed knee.

Evolution of the understanding of the ALC

The origins of the “ALC” concept trace back to Paul Segond’s observations in 1879 when he described a “pearly, resistant band” on the lateral aspect of the knee. He correlated its avulsion, now known as the Segond fracture, with rotatory instability (10).

In 1958, Kaplan provided the first detailed description of the layers of the iliotibial band (ITB) and its attachment (11,12). He also identified Kaplan’s fibers, which connect the deep layers of the ITB to the femur. Despite these insights, the ALC was not thoroughly investigated and remained underappreciated throughout most of the 20th century, as research efforts focused predominantly on the ACL.

Building on Kaplan’s work, Terry et al. expanded the understanding of the ITB’s function and emphasized on the its role in anterolateral stability. They suggested that the structural integrity of the ITB is crucial for normal knee function as it is considered the real anterolateral ligament (ALL) of the knee (13).

Interest in the ALC resurfaced again when Hughston et al. described the lateral capsular structures’ role in rotational knee stability and pivot shift (14). This was followed by several studies, including those by Vieira and Vincent, which further detailed the anatomy and function of the ALC (15,16).

However, it was not until Claes and Helito that the ALL was formally reintroduced into orthopedic literature (17,18). Their work identified the ALL as a distinct anatomical structure with unique histological features and highlighted its potential role in residual instability following ACLR. This was followed by the work of Herbst et al., who described the layers of the ALC in detail (19).

Parallel to this, several anatomical, clinical and biomechanical studies have continued to explore and investigate the contribution of the ALC to knee stability and patients’ outcomes, reinforcing the concept that this complex plays a crucial role in rotational control and stability, particularly in ACL-deficient knees (20-24).

Biomechanics and rationale

Several cadaveric studies have explored the role of ALC injury in anterolateral rotatory instability (ALRI), particularly in cases of ACL deficiency. Kittl et al. compared the effects of sectioning the ALL and ITB with and without cutting the ACL (23). They found that the ITB is the primary restraint against tibial internal rotation and simulated pivot shift, while the ALL has only a minimal role in controlling knee rotation.

Conversely, Sonnery-Cottet et al. argued that the ALL plays a significant role in restraining internal rotation and pivot shift (4). They observed that simultaneous sectioning of the ALL, ACL, and ITB has a synergistic detrimental effect on knee rotatory stability.

Despite multiple studies investigating this issue, it remains unclear whether the ITB or the ALL plays the primary role. However, the involvement of both structures is thought to contribute to significant ALRI, and a high-grade pivot shift may be expected in cases of concurrent ALC and ACL tears (24,25).

Based on this understanding, anterolateral extra-articular augmentations, both LET and ALLR are increasingly considered supplementary procedures to standard ACLR. These techniques aim to reduce the incidence of post-surgical residual rotatory laxity and intra-articular ACL graft failure (26).

Multiple cadaveric studies have demonstrated that isolated ACLR does not fully restore native knee stability when ALC structures are also damaged (24). Additionally, concurrent anterolateral procedures help offload forces within the reconstructed ACL graft (27,28). However, less evidence is available regarding differences among various lateral tenodesis or anterolateral reconstruction techniques. Inderhaug et al. found that ACLR combined with LET effectively restores native biomechanics in combined injuries, whereas ACLR and ALLR do not fully restore tibial anterior translation and internal rotation (29). In contrast, another study reported that ALLR and LET combined with ACLR were functionally equivalent in restoring knee kinematics (30). Differences in surgical techniques, fixation methods, and robotic testing protocols, including specimen age, applied forces, and simulated movements, affect the reproducibility of cadaveric data (24).

Clinical evidence aligns with findings from cadaveric studies. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomized controlled trials found that LEAP provides superior rotatory stability of the pivot shift phenomenon and Lachman test results while significantly reducing graft failure rates compared to isolated primary ACLR (31). A similar conclusion was drawn from a recent meta-analysis on revision ACLR. Grassi et al. reported that the relative risk of re-failure decreases significantly when a combined LEAP is performed, without increasing the risk of surgical complications (32).

Currently, the primary concern regarding LEAP is the potential increased risk of lateral knee compartment osteoarthritis. This risk is thought to arise because LEAP may excessively restrict tibial internal rotation, thereby increasing the load on lateral knee cartilage (33-35).

Some in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated a significant reduction in tibial internal rotation following LEAP (24,35). However, this finding is not consistently reported in the literature (36). Inderhaug et al. suggested the degree of internal rotation restraint might depend more on graft tensioning at the time of fixation rather than the reconstruction itself (37). Chiba et al. reported no additional effect of LET on tibial rotation at both 6 months and 12 months after ACLR surgery (38).

Moreover, the assertion that tibial rotation restraint directly leads to cartilage degeneration remains speculative in the absence of strong clinical evidence. Although early studies on isolated LET procedures reported a high incidence of knee osteoarthritis, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they were based on outdated surgical techniques and rehabilitation protocols (26). In contrast, most recent studies indicate that LEAP does not increase the risk of lateral compartment osteoarthritis in the medium to long term, suggesting that these procedures are safe in this regard (39-42).

Current concepts

For high-level athletes, the primary goal for both the athlete and surgeon is to restore knee stability, enable the athlete to return to their pre-injury performance level, and do so as quickly as possible while minimizing the risk of re-injury. In recent years, adding a LEAP to ACLR has gained popularity among surgeons taking care of high-level athletes as a strategy to help achieve these goals. Return to sport (RTS)

RTS is a key part of an athlete’s recovery following ACLR. For high-level athletes, returning to their pre-injury performance level is not only a professional goal but is often an integral part of their identity and career.

Studies on RTS in high-level athletes who have undergone ACLR with LEAP have found return to pre-injury competitive level rates ranging from 85.7% to 100%, as shown in Table 2 (43-50). However, all of these studies are retrospective in nature without randomization. In one of the largest studies on this topic, Borque et al. found that 88% of 117 professional athletes who had undergone combined ACLR and LEAP returned to pre-injury performance levels. The majority of these athletes were professional soccer or rugby players (43). In this study, graft failure was defined as having a revision surgery, potentially missing out on athletes who were able to manage with a failed graft. In a study on professional soccer players, Balendra et al. reported that 96% of athletes returned to their pre-injury competitive level after ACLR with LET (44). Return to pre-injury level in this study was defined as returning to the same league regardless of the athlete’s performance. Similarly, Guzzini et al. found that 100% of female Italian national and international footballers returned to their professional level following ACLR with LET (45). Only 16 athletes were included in this cohort without randomization. Rosenstiel et al. studied 70 professional athletes, mostly soccer players, who underwent ACLR with ALLR, and found that 85.7% returned to their pre-injury competitive level within 1 year, while 22.5% of the original population had retired at the time of follow-up (46). A study of French national ski team athletes revealed that 96.8% returned to international-level skiing following ACLR with ALLR (47). Additionally, Borque et al. reported that 92% of professional rugby players returned to the same level after ACLR with LET (48). One study examined outcomes of revision ACLR with LEAP in professional athletes. Alessio-Mazzola et al. found that 91.7% of professional soccer players who had hamstrings or bone-patella tendon-bone (BPTB) revision ACLR with a McIntosh-modified Coker Arnold LET returned to their pre-injury level of competition (49).

RTS time

For high-level athletes, prolonged time away from competition can be a threat to the athlete’s career. In professional athletes following ACLR with LEAP, studies report mean RTS time frames between 7.9 and 10.4 months, as shown in Table 2 (43,44,46-49).

Balendra et al. studied 232 professional soccer players and found that athletes who underwent ACLR with LET had a mean return to professional competition time of 10.4 months, compared to 11.1 months for those who had ACLR without LET (44). In a study of professional skiers, Guy et al. reported that 61.3% of athletes who underwent ACLR plus LET returned to international competition in less than 10 months, compared to 58% of athletes who had ACLR without LET (47). Rosenstiel et al. found a mean RTS of 7.9 months among professional athletes following hamstring ACLR with ALLR, with soccer and rugby being the most common sports (46). Borque et al. reported that professional rugby players returned at a mean of 9.6 months following ACLR with LET (48). For revision ACLR with LET, Alessio-Mazzola et al. reported a mean RTS of 9.2 months among 24 professional soccer players (49).

Graft failure

For high-level athletes, reinjury following ACLR can have career-ending consequences. A recent systematic review found that only 13–69% of athletes return to pre-injury level of sport after revision ACLR (51). As a result, minimizing the risk of re-injury is crucial for high-level athletes.

Studies examining combined ACLR with LEAP in professional athletes have reported re-injury rates ranging from 2% to 6.5% as presented in (Table 2). In the largest study of combined ACLR-LEAP in professional athletes, Hopper et al. found that athletes who had ACLR combined with an ALLR or modified Lemaire LET had a significantly lower failure rate (6.0%) compared to those who had ACLR alone (15.5%) at a mean 8.3 years follow-up (50). Another large study by Borque et al. found a significantly lower rate of graft failure among professional athletes, mostly soccer and rugby players, who had undergone combined ACLR with a modified Lemaire LET, compared to those who had isolated ACLR (3.4% vs. 9.5%) (43). Similarly, Guy et al. found a significantly lower graft failure rate in professional skiers who had combined ACLR and LEAP compared to those who had ACLR alone (6.5% vs. 34%) (47). In a study of 125 professional rugby players, those who had ACLR with LET had a lower rate of graft failure compared to those who had an isolated ACLR (5.5% vs. 8.0%) (48). Rosenstiel et al. reported on 70 professional athletes who received hamstring ACLR with ALLR, and at a mean follow-up of 3.9 years, reported a 5.7% graft failure rate (46). In their study of professional footballers, Balendra et al. examined results of ACLR with various graft types both with and without modified Lemaire LET and found that the combination of BPTB ACLR with LET had the lowest graft failure rate at 2%, although this was not statistically significant (44).

These findings collectively demonstrate that ACLR with LEAP in professional athletes achieves high rates of return to pre-injury performance with relatively low failure rates across various sports. The consistency of these outcomes across different surgical techniques (LET vs. ALLR) and graft types suggests that the addition of LEAPs provides robust protection for high-level athletes.

Career length

Only one study has examined the impact of adding LEAP to ACLR on career length in high-level athletes. In their study of 125 professional rugby players, Borque et al. found that adding LEAP to ACLR significantly increased career length. Five years after ACLR, 88% of athletes who had the addition of a LEAP were still playing, compared to 70% of those who underwent isolated ACLR (P<0.05) (48).

Complications

While LET has demonstrated favorable outcomes, potential complications must be considered. Reported complications include lateral knee pain, typically transient and responsive to conservative management, occurring in approximately 5–10% of cases. Knee stiffness, though rare when appropriate rehabilitation protocols are followed, has been reported in 2–3% of patients. Hardware-related complications, including irritation from staples or suture anchors, occur in approximately 3–5% of cases and may require removal (52,53). Importantly, recent evidence demonstrates that LET does not increase the risk of lateral compartment osteoarthritis when performed with contemporary techniques and appropriate graft tensioning (54). Overall complication rates for combined ACLR with LET remain comparable to isolated ACLR, with most complications being minor and manageable.

Senior author approach

The senior author utilizes an individualized and tailored approach to ACLR, particularly in high-level athletes. No single strategy is universally appropriate for all patients; instead, surgical decisions must take into consideration each individual’s sport-specific demands, knee morphological characteristics, and the risk profile for re-injury.

Risk stratification plays a central role when considering the addition of an LET to ACLR. Certain patient profiles, such as individuals under the age of 25 years, those with generalized ligamentous laxity, knee hyperextension greater than 10 degrees, posterior tibial slope greater than 10 degrees, participation in pivoting or contact sports, pivot shift grade ≥2, or undergoing revision ACLR, are associated with a significantly higher risk of graft failure (26,44). For these high-risk groups, current evidence increasingly supports the use of LET as an adjunct procedure, demonstrating improved rotatory stability and reduced graft failure rates. It is worth mentioning that the benefits of LET in these populations outweigh the potential risks, particularly when surgical technique and patient selection are optimized, though definitive evidence from large randomized trials across all ACL graft types is still pending.

The modified Lemaire technique is the senior author’s preferred LET technique, with the ITB graft passed deep to the fibular collateral ligament (FCL) and graft fixation achieved by either staples or suture anchors while the knee is positioned at 60 degrees of flexion and neutral tibial rotation as illustrated in Figure 1 (55).

The STABILITY-1 study, a multicenter randomized controlled trial of 618 young active patients, demonstrated that adding a modified Lemaire LET to hamstring ACLR significantly reduced the rate of ACL graft failure at 2 years (5). Despite this growing body of evidence, there remains a lack of clarity regarding the connection between graft type and the need for LET. The STABILITY-2 study, another multicenter randomized controlled trial, was specifically designed to address this knowledge gap. With 1,272 participants enrolled across North America and Europe, the trial is evaluating outcomes of primary ACLR using different grafts (patella tendon or quadriceps tendon autograft) with and without LET (ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT03935750). The results are anticipated to provide robust evidence to guide the decision-making process, helping to refine graft selection and define which patients are most likely to benefit from LET in the context of primary ACLR.

Conclusions

LET appears to be an effective adjunct to ACLR surgery in high-level athletes, improving rotatory stability and reducing graft failure without increasing the risk of lateral compartment osteoarthritis when performed with contemporary techniques. High rates of RTS at the pre-injury level and low re-injury rates have been consistently reported across multiple cohorts. Individuals with recognized risk factors for graft failure appear to benefit most. Still, further high-quality studies are expected to refine indications and optimize surgical decision-making.
