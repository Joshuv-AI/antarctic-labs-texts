Clinical Impact of Candida auris in Otology: Prevalence and Surgical Outcomes From a Single-Center Study in Korea
Authors: Jae Sang Han, Dong-Gun Lee, In Young Yoo, Yeon-Joon Park, Shi Nae Park, Kyoung Ho Park, Jae-Hyun Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCandida auris is an emerging pathogenic yeast of increasing global concern. This study aimed to explore recent epidemiological trends in C. auris at a single center and evaluate its impact on otologic surgery outcomes.MethodsAn epidemiological review was conducted for patients with positive fungal culture results at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital between January 2018 and December 2023. Medical records of patients with C. auris were retrospectively analyzed, focusing on surgical outcomes and comparing them with those of patients infected with other fungi.ResultsOf 3,430 fungal cultures from ear discharge, C. auris was identified in 104 cases (3.0%). Among 89 patients with C. auris isolated from ear discharge, 53 (59.6%) had mixed infections, with Staphylococcus species being the most common co-pathogen. Of 207 patients with fungal infections who underwent otologic surgery, 39 had C. auris. The postoperative complication rate was significantly higher in the C. auris group (34.2%) compared to that with other fungal infections (16.1%) (P = 0.011).ConclusionC. auris is widely distributed in the community and is particularly prevalent in patients requiring surgical treatment. Its presence in otologic surgery cases is linked to a higher risk of postoperative complications. Enhanced vigilance and proactive management by otolaryngologists are crucial to minimize nosocomial transmission and improving surgical outcomes.Keywords: Candida, Candida auris, Otologic Surgical Procedures, Complications

INTRODUCTION

Candida auris is an emerging pathogenic yeast of growing global concern.1 Initially identified in 2009 from ear discharge from a patient in Japan, C. auris has rapidly become a major nosocomial pathogen worldwide.2,3,4 In the United States, the spread of C. auris has been particularly rapid, prompting the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to begin tracking it as a national notifiable infectious disease in 2016.5,6

Four key characteristics contribute to the potential danger of C. auris. First, it exhibits multidrug resistance, being resistant to all three main classes of antifungal drugs, including azoles, echinocandins, and polyenes. Second, while it rarely causes significant issues in healthy individuals, it can lead to invasive infections in immunocompromised patients. Third, C. auris can persistently colonize human skin and abiotic surfaces. Fourth, it is challenging to isolate, particularly when employing conventional techniques.7 These characteristics necessitate significant precautions in tertiary care institutions with a high number of immunocompromised patients.

The ear remains the most common site of C. auris detection, consistent with the origin of its name. A single-center study in South Korea found C. auris isolated from ear discharge in all 79 examined patients, suggesting that otolaryngology clinics could be at significant risk for nosocomial outbreaks.8 However, most research on C. auris has been conducted in the fields of laboratory medicine or internal medicine, with relatively little attention from otolaryngologists.

Given that the otolaryngology department is often the primary point of care for patients with C. auris, our study aimed to investigate recent epidemiological trends of C. auris at our center and assess its impact on the outcomes of otologic surgery.

METHODS

Participants and clinical data collection

This retrospective medical record review examined patients with C. auris at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital from January 2018 to December 2023. We collected comprehensive clinical data including patient demographics, specimen details, infection source, otoscopic findings, surgical history, temporal bone CT, and treatment outcomes.

Patients undergoing otologic surgery during the study period were selected for surgical outcome evaluation. A control group was established comprising patients who had otologic surgery with positive preoperative fungal culture results during the same timeframe. We retrospectively reviewed medical records of both groups, focusing on chief complaints, surgical techniques, operated side, and postoperative outcomes.

The participant enrollment scheme is shown in Fig. 1.

Participant enrollment scheme. A total of 3,430 fungal isolates from ear discharge were collected between 2018 and 2023 for analysis of epidemiological trends and demographic characteristics. Surgical outcomes were comparatively evaluated in 206 patients who underwent surgical treatment.

Fungal culture

The tympanic membrane and external auditory canal were examined under microscopy or oto-endoscopy, and cultures were obtained from the ear discharge. Sterile gloves and instruments were used throughout the procedure. Clinical specimens were inoculated onto three agar media: Blood Agar Plate (BAP, Sinyang Diagnostics, Seoul, Korea), Chocolate Agar (Choco, Asan Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Korea), and MacConkey Agar (MAC, Asan Pharmaceutical). Plates were incubated at 35°C under aerobic conditions—BAP and MAC for 2 days, Choco for 5 days. Any microbial growth was identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry with the ASTA MicroIDSys system (MALDI-TOF MS, ASTA Corp., Suwon, Korea) and CoreDB version 1.29.00 reference database, employing an on-plate method for sample preparation and analysis.

Statistical analysis

Epidemiological and demographic data were analyzed using mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and percentages for categorical variables. A two-tailed unpaired t-test was used to compare continuous variables. Surgical outcomes were analyzed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, with data distribution normality assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital (KC24RISI0322). Due to the retrospective nature of the medical record review, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

RESULTS

General epidemiological trends of C. auris in ear cultures

Between January 2018 and December 2023, C. auris was identified in a total of 104 isolates from 89 patients through fungal culture from ear discharges. During the study period, a total of 3,430 fungal cultures from ear discharge were performed, with C. auris identified in 104 cases, yielding a prevalence rate of 3.0%. The annual positive rate of C. auris among ear discharge fungal cultures is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Annual prevalence of C. auris among fungal cultures from ear discharge from 2018 to 2023. Bars represent the total number of fungal cultures per year, with the portion positive for C. auris highlighted in orange. The number and percentage of C. auris cases are labelled above each bar.

Demographic characteristics of patients with C. auris isolated from ear discharge

Table 1 summarizes demographic data from 89 patients with C. auris isolated from ear discharge. Among these, 53 patients (59.6%) had mixed infections, with additional pathogens identified in the initial cultures, including 46 bacterial and 11 fungal co-pathogens. The most commonly identified bacterial co-pathogen was Staphylococcus species (n = 28), followed by Corynebacterium species (n = 9), Pseudomonas species (n = 6), and other bacteria (n = 4), comprising two cases of Brevibacterium luteolum, one of Acinetobacter baumannii, and one of Klebsiella aerogenes. All cases of fungal co-infection involved Aspergillus species: A. niger (5 cases), A. terrus (4 cases), and A. flavus (2 cases) (Fig. 3A).

Of the 89 patients, 39 (43.8%) underwent surgery, while 31 (34.8%) received local treatment including dressing and application of topical antifungal ointments. The remaining patients were lost to follow-up. Among the 31 patients who received local treatment, 12 (38.7%) showed improvement in symptoms, whereas 19 (61.3%) experienced no improvement or had a recurrence during the follow-up period.

Follow-up ear discharge cultures were performed for 29 patients, with a mean ± SD interval of 7.1 ± 7.6 months (range: 2 weeks to 25 months). Of these, 12 patients (41.4%) had persistent growth of C. auris, while 17 patients (58.6%) showed no further growth of C. auris. Nine patients (31.0%) had growth of other pathogens without the presence of C. auris, including four cases of Staphylococcus species, one case of Corynebacterium species, one non-fermenting gram-negative bacillus, and three cases involving other Candida species (one C. haemulonii and two C. parapsilosis) (Fig. 3B).

Between January 2018 and December 2023, of the 89 patients diagnosed with C. auris, 22 (24.7%) were lost to follow-up, 31 (34.8%) received medical treatment, and 39 (43.8%) underwent surgical intervention. All patients in the medical treatment group (100%) received regular local dressing with antifungal ointment application and were prescribed otic drops. Within this group, 21 patients (67.7%) were instructed to perform home vinegar irrigation, using a 1:2 solution of vinegar and normal saline to irrigate the external auditory canal for 15 minutes daily. Among the 31 medically treated patients, 15 (48.4%) showed clinical improvement. Specifically, of the 21 patients who received both vinegar irrigation and local dressing, 11 (52.4%) demonstrated improvement, while 4 of the 10 patients (40%) who received only regular dressing showed improvement. However, statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in treatment outcomes based on vinegar irrigation use (P = 0.519).

Surgical outcomes in patients with C. auris

To evaluate the surgical outcomes of the 39 patients who underwent surgery, we included additional patients with non-C. auris fungal infections who had undergone ear surgery as a comparison group. A total of 207 patients (207 ears) with fungal infections underwent otologic surgery between January 2018 and December 2023 at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital. Of these, 39 patients had C. auris growth, while 168 patients had other fungal growths identified in their ear discharge cultures. After excluding one patient from the C. auris group due to follow-up loss before 6 months postoperatively, 206 patients were included in the analysis. Among these patients, 95 (46.1%) demonstrated growth of Candida species (C. parapsilosis, 43; C. auris, 38; C. orthopsilosis, 5; C. ciferrii, 4; C. haemulonii, 2; C. albicans, 1; C. guilliermondii, 1; C. tropicalis, 1), 95 (46.1%) showed growth of Aspergillus species (A. niger, 45; A. terreus, 27; A. flavus, 7; A. fumigatus, 5; A. sydowii, 4; A. versicolor, 3; A. candidus, 1; A. glaucus, 1; unidentified, 2), 6 (2.9%) exhibited mixed growth of Candida and Aspergillus, and 10 (4.9%) had other fungi (Malassezia species, 5; Trichophyton species, 2; T. rubrum, 1; Absidia species, 1; Cladosporium species, 1; Kodamaea ohmeri, 1) (Fig. 4A).

The C. auris group (n = 38) was compared to the other fungal group (n = 168). A history of prior otologic surgery was more common in the C. auris group (P = 0.005). The postoperative complication rate was significantly higher in the C. auris group (34.2%) compared to the other fungal group (16.1%) (P = 0.011). In primary surgeries, the complication rate was significantly higher in the C. auris group (42.9%) than in the other fungal group (16.9%) (P = 0.006). However, no significant difference was observed in revision surgeries (P = 0.337) (Fig. 4B). When comparing the C. auris group with the other Candida species group (n = 57), a history of prior otologic surgery was also more common in the C. auris group (P = 0.008). The overall complication rate for other Candida species was 12.3%, which was significantly lower than that of the C. auris group (P = 0.010). In primary surgeries, the C. auris group exhibited a significantly higher complication rate (P =0.007); however, no statistically significant difference was found in revision surgeries (P = 0.330) (Fig. 4C).

The onset of postoperative complications occurred significantly earlier in the C. auris group (2.8 ± 3.7 months) compared to the other Candida species group (6.9 ± 3.7 months, P = 0.017), but not when compared to the other fungal group (4.1 ± 5.0 months, P = 0.298). Additionally, diabetes mellitus was significantly less prevalent in the C. auris group compared to both the other fungal group (P = 0.030) and the other Candida species group (P = 0.005). No significant differences were observed for other underlying diseases (P > 0.05). Detailed data are presented in Table 2.

Representative cases of C. auris patients

Case 1

A 50-year-old female patient presented with otorrhea and a history of breast cancer for which she was undergoing hormone therapy postoperatively. Initial cultures isolated P. aeruginosa, with no detection of C. auris. After undergoing Type I tympanoplasty, the patient experienced reperforation and persistent otorrhea. Repeated cultures at this stage identified C. auris for the first time. Subsequent revision surgery, including canal wall up mastoidectomy with tympanoplasty, failed to resolve otorrhea, necessitating canal wall down mastoidectomy. However, symptoms persisted, along with worsening hearing, leading to subtotal petrosectomy. Despite this, postauricular wound dehiscence with otorrhea continued. Consequently, this patient required the wound to remain open with regular irrigation for over two years (Fig. 5A).

Representative cases. (A) A patient with central tympanic membrane perforation initially presented with P. aeruginosa infection. After the first surgery, persistent otorrhea developed, and C. auris was subsequently isolated. Multiple revisional surgeries failed to control otorrhea and wound infection. (B) A patient with persistent otorrhea following mastoidectomy was found to have C. auris in the initial culture. After local treatment of the mastoidectomized cavity, C. auris was no longer isolated. Revisional mastoidectomy with mastoid obliteration was performed, resulting in a successful outcome without recurrent infection or otorrhea.

Case 2

A 66-year-old woman sought treatment for persistent right ear otorrhea following a previous canal wall down mastoidectomy for middle ear cholesteatoma 15 years earlier. Oto-endoscopy revealed severe otorrhea in the mastoidectomized cavity and the initial fungal culture confirmed C. auris without co-infection.

After one month of frequent ear dressing and vinegar irrigation, active otorrhea was controlled, and follow-up cultures at two and four months showed no fungal growth. Revisional canal wall mastoidectomy and tympanoplasty with mastoid obliteration was performed, resulting in a successful surgical outcome without recurrent otorrhea during the four-month follow-up period (Fig. 5B).

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrated the prevalence of C. auris in otorrhea and emphasized its widespread presence in the community. C. auris emerged as the second most common Candida species among patients undergoing otologic surgery, following C. parapsilosis. Notably, patients with preoperative C. auris infections were observed to have a higher rate of postoperative complications.

Since its first identification in 2009, C. auris has spread globally, with cases reported in over 45 countries.4,9 Although national surveillance data are lacking in South Korea, a previous single-center study from 2016–2018 found 111 C. auris isolates from 79 patients, representing 0.9% of Candida species in clinical samples, with 107 isolates from ear cultures.8 Our research examined C. auris cases in ear cultures from 2018 to 2023, observing a progression in positivity rates from 3.0% in 2018 to 4.6% in 2023, indicating a substantial and concerning increase in the pathogen’s prevalence.

Notably, among 207 patients who had positive fungal cultures prior to otologic surgery, C. auris was the third most frequently isolated organism, following A. niger (21.7%, n = 45) and C. parapsilosis (20.8%, n = 43), accounting for 18.4% (n = 38) of cases. This observation contrasts with prior findings, where only 3.0% of fungi isolated from the ear were C. auris, suggesting a higher prevalence of C. auris in cases requiring surgical intervention. Interestingly, in our cohort, patients with C. auris isolated prior to surgery were more likely to have a history of otologic surgery compared to those with other fungal infections. Although the exact reasons remain unclear, two potential explanations can be proposed. First, chronic conditions such as otitis media might lead to repeated clinic visits and potential nosocomial transmission of C. auris via colonized instruments like otoscopes. Second, the multidrug-resistant nature of C. auris might contribute to persistent, uncontrolled inflammation, which may lead to a clinical course that ultimately requires surgical intervention. Further research is needed to elucidate these findings.

Genomic studies have shown that C. auris appeared concurrently on several continents, leading to the formation of six distinct clades. Initially, four primary clades were recognized: Clade I (South Asia), Clade II (East Asia), Clade III (South Africa), and Clade IV (South America). More recently, two additional clades have been identified: Clade V (originating in Iran) and Clade VI (linked to Singapore).10,11 South Korea, like other East Asian countries, is predominantly associated with Clade II.12,13 While our study did not include genomic analysis, it is reasonable to assume that Clade II is the most prevalent in our population. Clade II has been shown to have lower virulence.14 Clinically, Clades I, III, and IV are primarily associated with hospital outbreaks and invasive infections, while Clades II and V are more commonly linked to ear colonization or infections.15 Furthermore, most Clade II strains are susceptible to fluconazole and other antifungal agents.14 In this study, patients with C. auris infections associated with poor otologic outcomes. Fortunately, postoperative complications associated with C. auris were mostly localized, such as recurrent otorrhea or reperforation, without progression to candidemia. This is presumed to be attributable to the low virulence of Clade II strains.14

This study provides an important perspective on C. auris infections from the standpoint of otolaryngologists, who frequently encounter ear-related manifestations of this pathogen. While the existing literature primarily focuses on nosocomial infection control and diagnostic challenges, limited awareness among otolaryngologists regarding C. auris could facilitate its spread. When patients present with ear discharge, it is essential to perform fungal cultures capable of identifying specific strains. If C. auris is identified, it is crucial to implement measures to prevent nosocomial transmission. These measures include isolating the patient, enforcing strict hand hygiene protocols, and thoroughly disinfecting medical equipment and environmental surfaces using a disinfectant approved by the Environmental Protection Agency specifically for C. auris, as recommended by the CDC.16 C. auris can persist on human skin and in the environment, facilitating its spread within healthcare settings. Fortunately, in our study, C. auris isolated from the ear did not cause life-threatening infections; however, given that a case of skull base osteomyelitis has been reported in an elderly immunocompromised patient,17 otolaryngologists should remain highly cautious about C. auris transmission. In cases of chronic otitis media patients with C. auris requiring surgical treatment, meticulous infection should be implemented beforehand. Surgery should be performed only after complete eradication of C. auris. Furthermore, since postoperative complications occur relatively early, careful monitoring is necessary to detect complications such as wound infections.

We presented two surgical cases of C. auris infection. The first case represented our most severe postoperative experience with C. auris infection. At the time of the surgery, we were unaware of the critical importance of managing C. auris. Following this adverse experience, we established a protocol to confirm the elimination of C. auris through preoperative culture testing. The second case highlights that surgery can be successful when C. auris is thoroughly controlled. This study did not directly investigate this topic, and further research is warranted to evaluate surgical outcomes in patients where C. auris has been completely eradicated prior to surgery.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to analyze the outcomes of otologic surgeries complicated by C. auris infections. Our findings suggest that eliminating C. auris prior to surgery may help reduce the risk of postoperative complications. Since C. auris detected in the ear is typically a superficial infection, initial management should focus on meticulous dressing and the application of topical antifungal agents.18 Additionally, vinegar irrigation can aid in normalizing the pH of the external auditory canal, thereby supporting the treatment of otomycosis.19,20 However, in our study, the success rate of local treatment was only 38.7%. Therefore, further research to develop more effective strategies for eradicating C. auris is needed.

Despite the relevance of our study, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, this study has limitations due to its single-center retrospective analysis design, which may have introduced selection bias or missing data that could affect the results. Therefore, further prospective studies are needed. Secondly, we did not perform clade-specific analyses and antifungal susceptibility tests of C. auris isolates. Although there were not sufficient data to include in this study, genetic analysis performed on a few cases revealed that all belonged to Clade II (data not shown). Since Clade II is predominant in our region, it can be assumed that most of the findings in our study reflect the characteristics of C. auris Clade II. Additionally, there was a limitation that antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed. Therefore, future studies incorporating genetic analysis and antifungal susceptibility testing will be necessary. Thirdly, the detection of C. auris prior to surgery does not necessarily indicate its presence at the time of the operation. It is possible for appropriate preoperative treatment to eradicate C. auris before surgery, which could lead to underestimation of postoperative complications attributable to C. auris infections.

This study examines the current status of C. auris from the perspective of otolaryngologists and its impact on otologic surgery. C. auris appears to be widespread in the community and is particularly prevalent among patients requiring surgical intervention. Although preoperative cultures identifying C. auris did not frequently result in severe complications, the incidence of postoperative complications was higher compared to other fungal species. Otolaryngologists, who are often the first to encounter patients with C. auris infections and play a critical role in preventing nosocomial transmission, should maintain a high level of vigilance. Prior to surgery, it is essential to eradicate C. auris to minimize the risk of postoperative complications and prevent the spread of this pathogen within healthcare settings.
