Deep Learning–Enhanced Biomarker Interpretation on Cytology Cell Blocks
Authors: Rong Xia, Nickolas G. Littlefield, Christopher Y. Park, Riyue Bao, Joan Cangiarella, Aylin Simsir, Qiangqiang Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cytology cell block specimens are essential for diagnosing patients with advanced-stage malignancy and often represents the only available strategy for therapeutic biomarker evaluation. The use of cell blocks preserves tumor cells, captures high-grade or metastatic populations, and retains meaningful microenvironmental context, making them well suited for IHC analysis. With the rapid expansion of computational pathology, deep learning–assisted biomarker interpretation in cell blocks is emerging as a promising frontier for improving reproducibility, reducing interobserver variability, and enabling quantitative assessment of spatial tumor–immune interactions. Because many treatment-defining biomarkers are routinely assessed on cytology cell blocks, this mini-review highlights artificial intelligence–based applications for PD-L1, HER2, ER/PR, Ki-67, ALK/ROS1, BRAF V600E, and p16 markers that directly inform decisions about immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and hormone therapy. Also reviewed are emerging predictive models that convey biomarker status directly from morphology, extending the utility of artificial intelligence beyond conventional IHC interpretation. Finally, spatial pathobiology–related opportunities afforded by cell block preparations are discussed, and future directions for integrating artificial intelligence–enabled analysis into cytology workflows are outlined. Together, these advances position cytology cell blocks as an important platform for computational biomarker interpretation and morphology-driven precision oncology.

Cytology cell block preparations have become an essential component of modern oncologic diagnostics, allowing pathologists to evaluate clinically actionable biomarkers from minimally invasive procedures such as fine-needle aspiration and fluid collections. Compared with conventional smears, cell blocks preserve cohesive clusters, stromal elements, and limited three-dimensional architecture, enabling immunohistochemistry (IHC) assessment that often parallels that of small biopsy samples.1 Biomarkers commonly assessed on cell blocks—including programmed cell death protein ligand (PD-L1; reported as tumor percentage score [TPS] and combined positive score [CPS]), Ki-67, human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER)-2, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and emerging therapeutic targets such as claudin-18.2—direct treatment decisions across lung, breast, gastrointestinal, and metastatic malignancies. Multiple studies have shown that IHC analysis of PD-L1 using cell blocks is feasible and reliable for clinical decision making.2,3

The interpretation of many biomarkers is inherently spatial, requiring accurate identification of tumor cells, immune infiltrates, and supporting stromal components. PD-L1 CPS depends on quantifying PD-L1 expression in both tumor cells and PD-L1–positive tumor–infiltrating lymphocytes, underlining the relevance of spatial context. Recent deep-learning (DL) approaches have demonstrated promising performance for PD-L1 evaluation in both histologic examination and cytology. For example, Han et al4 developed an integrated multi–artificial intelligence (AI) model pipeline for automated PD-L1 CPS scoring in gastric cancer, demonstrating strong concordance with pathologist consensus and highlighting the value of AI in resolving complex tumor–immune interfaces.

Emerging data specifically support the use of digital and AI-assisted PD-L1 scoring in cytology cell blocks. PD-L1 scoring in cell block specimens has shown high agreement between digital and conventional glass slide review.5 AI-assisted PD-L1 scoring in non–small cell lung carcinoma cell block specimens has shown strong comparability to pathologist interpretation, supporting its feasibility in diagnostic workflows.6 These findings highlight the potential for DL in standardizing biomarker interpretation; reducing observer variability; and extracting spatial features such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte density, tumor–immune proximity, and intratumor heterogeneity—elements highly relevant to spatial pathobiology.

Together, these developments position cytology cell blocks as a promising and underutilized platform for AI-enabled biomarker analysis. Cytology Cell Blocks as a Platform for Biomarker Analysis

Cell blocks are frequently used to evaluate clinically significant biomarkers across a wide range of malignancies. In solid tumor cytology, several therapeutic and predictive markers have been validated on cell block preparations, including PD-L1 (TPS and CPS), HER2, ER, PR, Ki-67, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK; clones D5F3 and 5A4), reactive oxygen species (ROS)-1, B-Raf V600E (VE1), and p16.7,8 These markers are routinely applied in lung carcinoma, breast carcinoma, gynecologic tumors, and metastatic disease when tissue biopsy is limited, and they support critical decisions involving immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and hormone therapy.

Because cell blocks often represent the only available diagnostic material in advanced-stage disease, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of both biomarker evaluation and lineage confirmation is crucial for optimal patient management.

From a spatial perspective, cell blocks offer distinct analytic advantages. The presence of intact tumor clusters allows for the reliable evaluation of membranous biomarkers such as HER2, while associated immune cells and stromal elements provide critical context for PD-L1 CPS scoring and for quantifying tumor–immune interactions.20 Furthermore, cell blocks frequently capture heterogeneous tumor populations, enabling the assessment of intratumoral variation in biomarker expression—an aspect increasingly recognized as relevant to therapeutic response.21 The ability to evaluate biomarker heterogeneity within the cytologic specimens is particularly important in effusion cytology, where the tumor population often represents the higher-grade or metastatic component of the disease, providing a window into the biologically aggressive fraction of the tumor and potentially revealing biomarker expression patterns not fully reflected in the primary lesion. These spatial features generate a strong foundation for computational analysis, supplying the structural cues necessary for tumor and immune cell segmentation and for downstream DL-based biomarker quantification.

Despite their utility, cytology cell blocks also present technical challenges. Variable cellularity, fixation differences, scant material, and occasional processing artifacts can affect IHC staining and scoring. Standardization across institutions remains limited, with differences in cell block preparation methods influencing antigen preservation and staining quality. These factors underline the need for careful validation and, when integrating AI tools, robust model training that reflects the diversity of cytology preparations encountered in practice.

Overall, the architectural preservation and spatial information inherent to cytology cell blocks make them a strong platform for biomarker analysis and a compelling target for AI-driven spatial pathobiology.

Artificial Intelligence in Biomarker Interpretation—Core Technical Foundations

AI-based approaches, specifically deep neural networks, have advanced rapidly in digital pathology, and many of the underlying principles translate well to cell block cytology. Unlike conventional computer vision approaches, DL approaches allow models to learn discriminative morphologic and spatial features directly from image data, a capability particularly relevant for biomarker evaluation, where the accurate identification of tumor cells, immune cells, and staining patterns is essential.

Cell Segmentation and Classifications

Accurate biomarker interpretation begins with accurately identifying (ie, segmenting) and subtyping (ie, classifying) the cell populations. Cytology cell blocks present unique challenges due to three-dimensional clusters, overlapping cells, inflammatory backgrounds, and occasional degenerative changes. Convolutional neural network (CNN)–based image segmentation models—such as U-Net, Mask R-CNN,22,23 and other encoder–decoder architectures—have demonstrated strong performance in delineating tumor cells and distinguishing them from immune cells, stromal fragments, and debris.24 These models help to standardize the cell populations used for biomarker quantification and reduce the variability introduced by background elements.

Recent advances in computational pathology have substantially improved the accuracy and robustness of cell segmentation and classification across diverse imaging modalities. In circulating tumor cell analysis, Stevens et al25 demonstrated that the DL-based StarDist algorithm yields markedly superior single-cell delineation in both whole blood and diagnostic leukapheresis samples, outperforming the CellSearch and ACCEPT pipelines26, 27, 28 by resolving closely apposed cells and preventing merged or oversized segmentations that lead to undercounting of circulating tumor cells. In whole-slide images used for cervical cytology, the U-Net, Vision Transformer,29 and Detectron230 frameworks were benchmarked by Mosquera-Zamudio et al,31 indicating that these DL-based approaches achieved high Dice scores, intersection over union, and panoptic quality scores, with robust detection even in suboptimal imaging conditions, thereby supporting their utility in nucleus-level instance segmentation for digital cytology. Complementing these advances in segmentation, MAPS—a computationally efficient AI framework that performs cell type annotation from highly multiplexed images with pathologist-level accuracy, surpassing existing methods including, but not limited to, ASTIR32 and CellSighter,33 across the MIBI and CODEX data sets—was introduced by Shaban et al.34 Collectively, these studies highlight the critical role of advanced AI pipelines in enabling precise nuclear segmentation, reliable single-cell phenotype assignment, and scalable cell type classification in digital pathology using whole-slide images.

Self-Supervised Learning and Foundation Models in Addressing Limitations in Annotation

Annotated cytology data sets are limited compared with those used in histologic examination, making self-supervised learning an attractive strategy. Self-supervised learning enables models to learn representations from large collections of unlabeled cytology or histologic examination tiles before being fine-tuned for biomarker tasks.35 Large foundation models trained across diverse pathology data sets have demonstrated improved generalizability and reduced annotation requirements.36, 37 Although most foundation models have been developed using histologic examination, early evidence indicates that their learned morphologic representations can be transferred to cytology biomarker tasks, with promising results.38

Multitask and Multimodal Learning Approaches

In the interpretation of biomarkers, particularly PD-L1, both staining intensity and spatial relationships between tumor and immune cells are critical. Multitask DL models can perform tumor segmentation, immune cell detection, and biomarker scoring simultaneously, improving efficiency and internal consistency.6 Multimodal approaches that integrate imaging with clinical or molecular data (eg, mutational profiles or therapy response) offer opportunities to enhance the predictive accuracy of biomarkers, especially where tumor heterogeneity is prominent.

Spatial Feature Extraction

DL enables the quantification of spatial relationships highly relevant to biomarker interpretation. Spatial metrics such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte density, tumor–immune proximity, cluster architecture, and intralesion variability can be extracted directly from cell block images.39, 40, 41 These features align directly with emerging concepts in spatial pathobiology and provide mechanistic insights into tumor–immune dynamics, particularly in the context of PD-L1, Ki-67, and other treatment-relevant biomarkers evaluated on cytology specimens.

Beyond conventional spatial metrics, recent frameworks have demonstrated that AI can extract granular, biologically meaningful spatial features at both the cellular and imaging tile levels. In the Cellular Features Based Interpretable Network (CellFiNet), nuclei segmentation and phenotype classification enable the computation of geometric and spatial distribution features, including, but not limited to, phenotype-specific cell densities and graph-based features, which capture local clustering, tumor–immune spatial organization, and microarchitectural heterogeneity. These features were among the strongest slide-level predictions of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma cell-of-origin subtypes.42 Complementing CellFiNet—the biomarker detection system proposed by Mosinska and colleagues43 in the patent—the systems and method for biomarker detection extract cell-level and tile-level embeddings directly from whole-slide images using DL models, and then aggregate those embeddings to quantify spatially resolved histologic patterns relevant for biomarker detection (ie, c-myc prediction). Together, these approaches indicate that modern DL frameworks can capture fine-grained, spatially structured relationships within tissue, extending from individual nuclei to complex, multicellular neighborhoods, and thereby enhance mechanistic interpretation of biomarker expression in pathobiology.

Deep-Learning Applications in Biomarker Interpretation on Cytology Cell Block Specimens

DL approaches have increasingly been explored to assist with biomarker interpretation on cytology cell block specimens, particularly for biomarkers that require precise identification of tumor cells, immune cells, or membranous and nuclear staining patterns. Although the evidence base is still emerging, several key biomarkers—most notably PD-L1, HER2, ER/PR, Ki-67, and select molecular surrogates—have been the focus of recent computational studies.

PD-L1 TPS and CPS

Among all therapeutic biomarkers, PD-L1 has received the greatest attention in DL research. Accurate PD-L1 TPS scoring requires the correct identification of PD-L1–positive tumor cells, while the identification of PD-L1–positive immune cells within the tumor microenvironment is required for CPS scoring. DL systems developed for PD-L1 analysis typically include tumor segmentation modules, immune cell classification networks, and slide-level scoring algorithms that aim to standardize assessment and reduce observer variability.

Several AI-based methods have demonstrated strong concordance with pathologist scoring in histologic examination, and this paradigm is now extending into cytology. Multimodel integration strategies have achieved high agreement with pathologist CPS scoring, highlighting the value of combining tumor–immune segmentation, intensity quantification, and spatial analysis as a unified pipeline.4 Digital cytology studies evaluating PD-L1 on cell block material have similarly shown high comparability between AI-assisted scoring and pathologist interpretation, supporting the feasibility of computational approaches in this setting.5,6 These tools may be particularly valuable for specimens containing scant tumor populations or mixed inflammatory backgrounds, where manual interpretation can be challenging.

Beyond immunotherapy, DL applications are expanding to other predictive and lineage-specific markers essential for comprehensive cytologic evaluation. Recent DL models are moving beyond isolated PD-L1 scoring to incorporate lineage-specific classification, addressing the challenge of distinguishing tumor cells from reactive mimics (eg, macrophages or mesothelial cells). In non–small cell lung carcinoma, for instance, DL algorithms have demonstrated the ability to distinguish phenotypes of adenocarcinoma from those of squamous cell carcinoma directly from hematoxylin and eosin morphology—effectively acting as a morphologic surrogate for TTF-1 and p40 immunostains.44 Integrating these lineage-prediction capabilities into PD-L1 workflows ensures that the tumor proportion is derived from the correct neoplastic population, thereby reducing false positives and providing a more comprehensive diagnostic profile from limited cell block material.

Moreover, complementing these morphologic approaches, emerging laboratory workflows are evaluating dual-staining protocols (eg, combining lineage markers and PD-L1 on a single slide) to cell block sections. In this context, DL offers a distinct advantage by digitally unmixing overlapping chromogens—separating nuclear lineage markers from membranous PD-L1 signal—to enable simultaneous, accurate scoring of tumor type and biomarker status from a single, limited specimen (M. Sharaf, C.Y.P., R.X., unpublished observations, 2025).

HER2

HER2 evaluation on cell blocks requires the accurate assessment of membrane staining, which can be subtle and heterogeneous in small cytology specimens. DL approaches designed for HER2 analysis in histologic examination—particularly models optimized for membrane detection and differentiation between complete and incomplete staining—have shown promise in improving consistency and sensitivity in borderline cases.45

Although published AI studies on HER2 in cytology cell blocks remain limited, the technical foundation from histologic examination–based models provides a strong basis for future development. AI-based membrane detection may help to resolve variability in HER2 scoring caused by cell fixation differences, cluster overlap, and sampling heterogeneity often seen in cytology.

ER and PR

DL models for nuclear biomarker interpretation—including ER and PR—have focused on robust nuclear segmentation, detection of staining intensity, and estimation of tumor cell percentage scores. While most published models are based on histology sections, the principles of nuclear quantification and intensity scoring translate directly to cell block specimens, where nuclear morphology is generally well preserved.46 AI-assisted ER/PR scoring has the potential to standardize evaluation, particularly in metastatic contexts.

Ki-67

DL systems are particularly well suited to Ki-67 quantification, which historically has required manual counting or the estimation of thousands of nuclei. Convolutional neural network–based nuclear segmentation models, combined with the automated detection of Ki-67–positive nuclei, have achieved high agreement with pathologist-derived indices in histology and cytology preparations.47,48 These methods can improve reproducibility in neuroendocrine tumors, breast carcinoma, and other malignancies in which the proliferation index has prognostic and therapeutic relevance.

ALK, ROS1, and BRAF V600E Surrogate IHC Analysis

AI applications for mutation-surrogate IHC analysis (eg, ALK clone D5F3 or 5A4, ROS1, and BRAF V600E VE1) are less developed but remain an area of active interest. These biomarkers rely on specific staining patterns—strong granular cytoplasmic staining for ALK, cytoplasmic staining for ROS1, and distinct nuclear/cytoplasmic reactivity for BRAF V600E—that could benefit from automated detection and intensity quantification. Early studies of the use of DL in histologic examination suggest that it can support the identification of positive cases and reduce false positives, providing a foundation for future cytology-focused applications.49

p16

AI-based p16 interpretation is still at an early stage; however, DL methods for nuclear and cytoplasmic pattern recognition may support automated assessment in human papillomavirus–associated lesions. Cytology cell blocks in gynecologic and head and neck malignancies provide suitable material for computational evaluation, particularly in distinguishing block-positive from heterogeneous or partial staining patterns.50

In addition to assisting with IHC interpretation, the use of machine learning approaches for predicting biomarker or mutation status directly from morphology is increasingly being explored. This includes early studies attempting to infer p16 status—a surrogate for human papillomavirus–driven oncogenesis—based solely on cytomorphologic features using DL classifiers.50

Overall, the applications of DL in biomarker interpretation on cytology cell blocks are most advanced for PD-L1; emerging for Ki-67 and nuclear markers; and still developing for HER2, ALK/ROS1, and BRAF V600E. These methods will strengthen reproducibility, support spatially informed scoring, and serve as a foundation for AI-enabled spatial pathobiology.

Spatial Pathobiology and the Future of AI-Assisted Biomarker Analysis in Cytology Cell Blocks

Cell blocks provide a compact yet biologically meaningful microenvironment that supports spatially informed biomarker evaluation. Although they do not preserve the full anatomic architecture of surgical biopsy specimens, cell blocks consistently capture cohesive tumor clusters intermixed immune cells and stromal fragments that together generate a micro-architecture sufficient for spatial analysis. These features allow DL models to quantify the relationships between tumor cells, immune infiltrates, and biomarker expression patterns in ways that extend beyond conventional IHC-based scoring.

AI-based PD-L1 assessment exemplifies this potential. DL systems can simultaneously segment tumor and immune cells, measure PD-L1 expression, and compute spatial metrics such as tumor–immune proximity or immune-excluded patterns—features that correlate with biological behavior and treatment response.41 Such analyses are particularly informative in effusion-derived cell blocks, where the tumor population often represents the high-grade or metastatic component, providing insight into biomarker heterogeneity that may not be captured in the primary tumor. Similar opportunities exist for Ki-67, where spatial gradients of proliferation may reflect clonal dynamics within metastatic deposits.

Emerging predictive modeling represents a natural extension of these capabilities. Beyond IHC interpretation, machine learning models trained on histologic examination have shown that biomarker status—such as BRAF mutation, microsatellite instability, or immune activation signatures—can be inferred directly from morphology.51 In cytology, early studies suggest that DL may identify morphologic correlates of p16 positivity, an established surrogate for human papillomavirus–driven oncogenesis, even without immunostaining.52 Although still preliminary, such models highlight the possibility that cell blocks could support morphology-derived biomarker prediction, expanding utility in situations where material is limited or exhausted.

Clinical translation requires rigorous validation aligned with College of American Pathologists/Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CAP/CLIA) standards for digital pathology, including the demonstration of reproducibility across staining platforms, fixation protocols, cellularity levels, and scanner systems. Variability inherent in cytology preparations—particularly effusion cell blocks—poses challenges to model generalizability, while algorithm transparency and oversight remain essential for clinical acceptance. Advances in self-supervised learning, foundation models, and multimodal architectures may help to overcome these barriers by reducing dependence on large-scale annotated data sets and improving robustness across institutions.

In summary, cytology cell blocks provide an accessible platform for AI-enabled biomarker analysis and spatial pathobiology. DL can improve scoring consistency and extract meaningful spatial features, even from small specimens. As predictive models advance, cytology may support both biomarker interpretation and morphology-based biomarker prediction, extending computational precision oncology to settings where cytology is the primary diagnostic strategy.
