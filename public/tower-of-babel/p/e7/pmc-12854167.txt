A review of inflammatory bowel diseases in humans and pets: treatment strategies
Authors: Seyyed Meysam Abtahi Froushani, Sayyed Jafar Hasani, Milad Ghaderi, Leila Mahmoudzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) encompasses a group of chronic inflammatory conditions that primarily impact the gastrointestinal system. While ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease are the principal manifestations in humans, animals frequently exhibit lymphocytic-plasmacytic enteritis/colitis and eosinophilic enteritis/colitis. Growing evidence suggests a complex interplay among genetic predisposition, gut microbiota imbalance and abnormal immune responses to intestinal microbes in susceptible individuals. This intricate involvement results in remarkably similar clinical presentations across species. Patients often experience symptoms such as diarrhea, vomiting, weight loss and anemia. Extraintestinal manifestations including uveitis, skin rash and arthritis may also occur. Endoscopy and biopsy typically serve as the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis and differentiating it from other gastrointestinal disorders in humans and animals. The treatment approach generally focuses on managing disease activity through immunosuppressive medications such as glucocorticoids, administered at appropriate dosages. However, the precise cause of IBD remains a topic of ongoing research. With the emergence of additional treatment options like herbal compounds and fecal microbiota transplantation, which have demonstrated effectiveness in restoring gut health in IBD patients, there is optimism for novel therapeutic strategies. Ultimately, conclusion is that chronic gastrointestinal conditions like IBD are complex in both human and veterinary medicine. These diseases share numerous common pathophysiological features, yet, diagnostic and treatment challenges continue to exist.Key Words: Crohn's disease, Humans, Inflammatory bowel disease, Microbiota, Pet

Introduction

Chronic intestinal disorders (CIDs) are long-lasting digestive issues persisting beyond three weeks, with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) as a critical subset. The IBD typically presents as Crohn's disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC), usually onset between 20 - 40 years, though possible at any age.1 The CD involves widespread inflammation throughout the digestive tract causing symptoms such as diarrhea and weight loss, potentially leading to complications like strictures and abscesses. In contrast, UC affects the rectum and colon, marked by surface inflammation and bloody stools.2 The IBD origins involve genetic predisposition, gut microbiome imbalance, immune system dysfunction and environmental elements. In veterinary practice, chronic enteropathy (CE) is the preferred term over IBD, encompassing unexplained intestinal conditions with inflammatory cell presence.3 Canine IBD exhibits greater variety than human IBD. The CE classification is based on treatment outcomes and results from interactions between host genetics, gut environment and immune responses.4 Symptoms vary by affected area, large intestine CE may cause constipation or dyschezia, while small intestine CE often involves diarrhea and weight loss. Certain dog breeds show higher susceptibility to CE.5 The CE treatment in dogs and cats includes dietary modifications like elimination and hydrolyzed diets alongside anti-inflammatory drugs and antibiotics. Complementary and alternative therapies, including probiotics, prebiotics and various natural supplements are increasingly recognized for managing chronic gut disorders in human and veterinary medicine.6 This review explores the similarities and differences in IBD between humans and dogs as well as cats, and strategies for managing them.

Genetic.

Genetic predisposition plays a significant role in IBD. Familial risk in humans is tenfold higher, highlighting a solid genetic component. Both genes and environmental factors influence IBD prevalence within a population and the degree of familial clustering. The relative contribution of these factors may vary between low- and high-incidence regions. Ethnicity also seems to influence risk, with Caucasians and individuals of Jewish descent exhibiting higher susceptibility. Interestingly, this risk appears to be modulated by lifestyle factors. People emigrating from low-prevalence regions to high-prevalence areas, like England, show an increased risk of developing IBD.7 Genome-wide association studies have identified specific genetic regions linked to chronic intestinal disorders. When influenced by environmental factors, these regions are thought to play a role in an abnormal immune response. This immune dysregulation affects the immune system innate and adaptive components. The innate component involves a compromised mucosal barrier function and impaired microbial recognition. On the other hand, the adaptive component shows an imbalance between regulatory T cells which suppress immune responses and effector T cells, which initiate immune attacks. Together, these dysfunctions contribute to reduced gut microbiota diversity.8 The IBD exhibits a genetic susceptibility component. Notably, the Nucleotide-binding Oligomerization Domain 2 (NOD2) mutation demonstrates a well-defined association with CD in Western populations. Interestingly, this association appears less prominent in East Asian populations. Despite these discrepancies, familial aggregation remains similar across Asian and Western populations. The IBD-associated genes involve mucosal immunity, including barrier function and microbial recognition.9 Similar trends are observed in companion animals. Certain dog breeds, like Soft-coated Wheaten Terriers and German Shepherds, appear to be predisposed to IBD, suggesting a genetic influence.10 Lending further credence to this concept of population-specific genetic influences, recent investigations have identified specific polymorphisms within Toll-like receptor (TLR) genes that are associated with canine IBD. Feline IBD can manifest in cats of any age or breed. However, a predisposition has been observed in middle-aged cats and certain Asian breeds such as Siamese.11

Stress.

Emerging evidence suggests that stress may be a potential risk factor for IBD and its associated symptoms, impacting both humans and companion animals such as cats and dogs. While inflammation often accompanies symptoms in IBD cases, some individuals experience symptoms without active inflammation. Studies suggest stress may contribute to this phenomenon. For example, a high level of perceived stress in humans correlates with an increased risk of symptoms. Supporting the potential role of stress in IBD, a study investigating chronic idiopathic large-bowel diarrhea in dogs identified a significant association. The study found that 37.80 % of the diagnosed dogs exhibited abnormal personality traits, suggesting a possible link to behavioral responses to environmental stressors.12,13

Smoking and drinking alcohol.

Both active and passive smoking are important risk factors for CD. Nevertheless, neither nicotine nor carbon monoxide are the causative agents. In contrast, smoking exhibits a protective effect in UC.8,14 Recent research suggests a potential link between alcohol consumption and increased risk of relapse in IBD. This concern stems from growing evidence that alcohol can alter the gut microbiome, weaken the intestinal barrier and activate the immune system, all of which are known contributors to IBD flares.15 One well-established mechanism involves the disruption of the epithelial barrier by ethanol. Alcohol weakens the physical and immunological defenses of intestinal epithelial cells and gut-associated lymphoid tissue as demonstrated in various studies. This mucosal injury caused by alcohol impairs nutrient absorption and increases gut permeability, a critical factor in IBD pathogenesis.16 Bacteria, including potentially harmful proinflammatory strains and bacterial toxins (endotoxins). Exposure to endotoxins has been linked to transient endotoxemia which triggers the production of proinflammatory mediators like tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL) -1 and IL-6. These elevated cytokine levels can lead to mucosal ulcerations, damage to the colonic epithelium and formation of crypt microabscesses, ultimately resulting in an IBD flare-up.17

Diet.

Nutritional elements are becoming more acknowledged as possible factors in the onset of IBD in human and animal groups. Research on humans has shown that eating habits can influence the risk of IBD. Consuming high amounts of total fats, polyunsaturated fatty acids, omega-6 fatty acids and meat seems linked to a higher risk of IBD. On the other hand, a diet rich in vegetables is connected to a lower risk of UC. At the same time, the intake of fiber and fruits is associated with a decreased risk of CD.18 Investigating specialized diets such as elemental, semi-elemental and polymeric formulas has been therapeutic in pediatric CD. Although the exact mechanisms behind their effectiveness are still not completely understood, these diets have shown significant clinical advantages in reducing disease activity and related symptoms.19

Research indicated that the use of dexpanthenol (D-panthenol, provitamin B5), the sole active form of pantothenol, in conjunction with prednisolone significantly reduced the severity of IBD, surpassing the effects of either treatment administered independently. Furthermore, it was suggested that the combination therapy involving dexpanthenol might enhance intestinal healing, as the synergistic effect of the medications could lead to a decrease in overall protein levels.20

Immunobiology and the microbiota.

A delicate equilibrium exists among the gut microbiome, intestinal barrier and immune system, wherein any disturbance to one component can disrupt the others. Modifications to the gut microbiome can alter gut permeability, intestinal epithelial cell differentiation and the immune response. The microbiome composition varies across species, including humans, dogs and horses.21 In humans, the stomach, duodenum and proximal jejunum are primarily inhabited by aerobic microorganisms including Streptococci, Lactobacilli and occasional Candida species. The microbial composition in the distal ileum resembles that of the colon more closely than the upper small intestine with an anaerobic predominance. Key colonic organisms found in humans encompass Clostridium species, Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium. As a result, the firmicutes and proteobacteria phyla are prevalent in the human duodenum. In contrast, firmicutes and bacteroidetes dominate in the distal colon.22 Unlike humans, canine gastrointestinal tracts harbor a significantly more abundant and diverse microflora. This microflora exhibit regional variations with enterobacteriales bacteria predominating in the small intestine. Conversely, the small intestine and colon share populations of Clostridiales, Fusobacteriales, Bacteroidales, and Lactobacillales bacteria. Notably, Fusobacteria constitutes a major bacterial group in dogs, unlike in humans, horses and chickens.23 The IBD arises from a dysregulation in the complex interplay between the host and its commensal microbiota. This disruption appears to lead to an immunodeficient state rather than a classical autoimmune disorder.24 Although specific pathogens have been linked to the development of IBD, a causative role for any single agent has yet to be definitively established. Instead, accumulating evidence suggests that microbial antigens, typically encountered within the intestinal lumen, may trigger gut inflammation.25

Ghorbani et al., demonstrated that Lactobacillus acidophilus, as a probiotic, can alleviate the symptoms of UC in rat models. In this study, the clinical manifestations and pathological damage caused by acid were significantly reduced in rats treated with L. acidophilus compared to those that did not receive the treatment. Improvements in clinical signs and findings from immunohistochemistry and chemical assays supported this. Notable alterations were observed in the expression of various microRNAs (miR) including miR-1, miR-99, miR-Let7d, and miR-155, as well as in mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and interferon-gamma. The authors suggested that the therapeutic application of probiotics effectively ameliorated UC by significantly altering the levels of miR-1, miR-99, miR-Let7d and miR-155.26

The dysbiosis associated with IBD can arise due to various factors such as host genotype affecting the composition of the microbiota and infections, antibiotics, drugs and diet. Disturbing this regulatory equilibrium can trigger the activation of leukocytes, resulting in heightened production of inflammatory molecules and the development of chronic intestinal inflammation. In the case of dogs and cats, the enteric flora plays a significant role in the etiology of IBD with cats exhibiting increased levels of mucosal-associated enterobacteriaceae and dogs showing a positive correlation between Clostridiaceae clones and the severity of clinical symptoms.27 The T helper (Th) cell responses are essential, as Th1 responses are triggered against intracellular pathogens, Th2 promotes atopy, immunoglobulin E and eosinophil/mast cell activation, and Th17 promotes local tissue destruction. Regulatory T cells help maintain an immunosuppressive environment in the intestinal mucosa. In humans with active IBD, there is a notable infiltration of innate (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer T cells) and adaptive (B and T cells) immune cells as well as an imbalance between effector and regulatory T cells. Similarly, increased levels of IL-17 mRNA and Toll-like receptor 4 expression in horses affected by chronic active simple proctitis suggest the potential involvement of Th17 cells in equine IBD.28

Epidemiology.

On a global scale, there is a notable increase in the incidence and prevalence of IBD, with comparable rates observed in both Europe and North America. Estimations indicate that around 0.60% of the Canadian population is affected by IBD, with UC prevailing more frequently than CD.7 In the United States, over 2 million individuals are affected by IBD; in Japan, the number is 0.20 million.29 The highest occurrence of IBD generally transpires between the ages of 15 and 30, although the condition can develop at any stage of life. Notably, IBD affects both genders equally. While not directly fatal, IBD is associated with a slight reduction in life expectancy. In 2010, it contributed to an estimated 34,000 deaths globally.30 While IBD can affect any breed, specific canine populations exhibit a genetic predisposition. Boxers and Dobermans appear more susceptible to eosinophilic gastrointestinal eosinophilia, while German Shepherds are predisposed to eosinophilic gastrointestinal eosinophilia and lymphocytic-plasmacytic enteropathy (LPE). Basenjis can develop a particularly severe form of LPE known as immune proliferative small intestinal disease. Similarly, Shar-Peis are at increased risk for a severe form of LPE characterized by hypo-proteinemia and deficient serum cobalamin levels.31 In felines, Siamese cats and potentially other purebreds like Persians and Himalayans may be predisposed to LPE. Additionally, some cats may experience a concurrent presentation of IBD, lymphocytic cholangitis and chronic pancreatitis, a condition known as triaditis.32 There is no apparent gender bias associated with IBD, with both dogs and cats developing the disease most frequently in middle age. Early, intermittent signs may sometimes be observed, however, intestinal inflammation diagnosed in animals younger than 6 months might not represent true IBD but rather indicate the presence of an underlying, undiagnosed cause.31

Pathogenesis.

Research on animals has shown that the presence of bacteria significantly impacts the development of IBD. For example, mice prone to IBD, like IL-10 knockout mice, do not exhibit the condition when raised in an environment free of germs.33 Moreover, it is widely acknowledged that individuals with IBD have a significantly reduced diversity of commensal microbiota compared to healthy individuals. This reduction mainly affects the firmicutes and bacteroidetes phyla. Furthermore, infections have been linked to the onset and worsening of IBD.34 Evidence suggests that macrophages in CD have an impaired acute inflammatory response. This dysfunction is believed to contribute to defective bacterial clearance, potentially perpetuating inflammation within the gut. In contrast to CD, UC appears to be characterized by an exaggerated macrophage response towards gut bacteria. Granulomas, immune cell aggregates, further underscore this difference in CD, however, not typically observed in UC.35 The CD is primarily identified by the prevalence of Th1 and Th17 cells, which encourage local tissue damage. In contrast, UC is believed to be driven by Th2 cells. Emerging evidence indicates that natural killer T cells produce Th2 cytokines such as IL-13, impairing epithelial cell function.36 Hence, UC might be primarily characterized as a superficial disorder affecting the epithelial tissue.

Clinical signs.

The IBD is a group of chronic gastrointestinal disorders affecting humans, dogs and cats with unique features in each species. In humans, IBD primarily presents as CD and UC. While sharing symptoms like chronic diarrhea and abdominal pain, CD and UC differ in anatomical distribution and disease characteristics.37 The CD involves transmural inflammation throughout the gastrointestinal tract with skip lesions. At the same time, UC is limited to continuous colon and rectum inflammation. The CD can lead to complications like strictures and fistulas. In contrast, UC inflammation is confined to the gut inner lining.38 Both diseases follow a relapsing-remitting pattern and can have extraintestinal manifestations. The IBD involved patients also face increased risks of thrombosis and autoimmune hemolytic anemia.39 In dogs, IBD presents with gastrointestinal symptoms affecting both small and large intestines without the distinct CD and UC categorizations seen in humans. Canine IBD can lead to protein-losing enteropathy and thromboembolism. Extraintestinal manifestations, though less common, may include skin conditions and polyarthritis.40 The absence of a definitive inflammatory response or cytokine profile in dogs complicates understanding the disease mechanism. Feline IBD has unique characteristics due to cat anatomical differences as obligate carnivores. The proximity of pancreatic and biliary systems often results in concurrent conditions like cholangitis or triaditis.41 Feline IBD affects both small and large intestines, with non-specific signs such as weight loss and vomiting. Extraintestinal manifestations may involve kidneys and skin. Like human and canine IBD, feline IBD follows a cyclical course of remission and exacerbation.42 Managing IBD across species is challenging due to patient discomfort, social impacts and treatment complexities. Understanding species-specific IBD characteristics is crucial for accurate diagnosis and adequate human and veterinary medicine management. Future research should focus on elucidating IBD mechanisms in different species to develop more targeted and effective treatments.

Diagnosis.

Accurate differentiation between IBD and other intestinal disorders is essential to ensure appropriate treatment. In humans, alternative diagnoses may include infectious colitis (bacterial, viral, parasitic), microscopic colitis (whose etiology remains unknown), celiac disease (linked to gluten sensitivity), irritable bowel syndrome (characterized by the absence of inflammation), ischemic bowel disease (resulting from reduced blood flow), diverticulitis (inflammation of pouches in the colon) and cancer (such as colorectal cancer or lymphoma). Meanwhile, in dogs and cats, potential alternative diagnoses encompass dietary intolerance/allergies (related to food sensitivity), parasitic infections (including roundworms, hookworms, etc.), bacterial infections (such as Salmonella or Escherichia coli), protozoal infections (like Giardia), antibiotic-associated diarrhea, metabolic/ endocrine diseases (involving issues with the liver, kidneys, or thyroid), lymphoma and inflammation of gastrointestinal diverticula. Additionally, age and breed considerations can impact the diagnostic process. Young animals with IBD may present congenital issues, while certain dog breeds may be predisposed to specific types of IBD. Diagnosis typically entails a comprehensive evaluation by a veterinarian or physician including symptom assessment, various tests (such as blood work, stool analysis, imaging studies and endoscopy with biopsy) and excluding other potential causes. This thorough approach facilitates an accurate diagnosis of IBD and enables the implementation of appropriate treatment strategies.43 Endoscopy is considered the gold standard for diagnosing IBD in all patients. Diagnosing IBD can be a complex undertaking. However, colonoscopy has emerged as the most effective tool for definitive diagnosis. The diagnostic workup for IBD typically begins with a multi- faceted approach. Initial investigations often involve a complete blood count to assess for anemia, a potential marker of chronic inflammation or blood loss. Additionally, a fecal occult blood test is employed to detect microscopic blood in the stool, which may indicate mucosal injury. If these initial tests warrant further investigation, endoscopic procedures are frequently used for definitive diagnosis.43 In cases of severe symptoms, radiography and computerized tomography may also be utilized to rule out perforation. Histopathological examination of mucosal biopsies remains the cornerstone in differentiating UC from CD. Additionally, depending on the clinical presentation, a comprehensive diagnostic workup may incorporate urinalysis, stool culture, liver function tests and electrolyte panel assessment.44

Treatment.

While recognizing the significant role played by gut microbiota in the development of IBD is growing, therapeutic approaches have primarily focused on immunosuppression rather than strategies to restore a healthy microbial community. The treatment options for IBD include making lifestyle adjustments if there are identifiable factors contributing to the disease, medical interventions to manage symptoms and surgical procedures. In cases of human IBD, medications with anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties are commonly used. For example, 5-aminosalicylates address active UC, while corticosteroids are administered to induce remission in both CD and UC. However, it is essential to note that corticosteroids are not sufficient for maintaining remission and carry significant long-term risks.13 The efficacy of antibiotics such as metronidazole and tylosin in inducing remission in active UC and CD and preventing relapse in inactive CD lends credence to the concept that targeted modulation of gut microbiota can influence IBD activity.45 It is advisable to consider harmless treatments with minimal side effects yet demonstrating high efficacy. For instance, research has shown that the application of non-adherent bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, as well as mesenchymal stem cells pulsed with 17 beta-estradiol, leads to a reduction in symptoms of UC in Wistar rats. This effect is mediated by the decrease of inflammation and the establishment of an anti-inflammatory state, which is associated with a significant reduction in lipid peroxidation, neutrophil infiltration and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the colonic tissue (Tabel 1).46,47

Monoclonal antibodies targeting inflammatory cytokines, particularly anti-TNF-α agents, demonstrate efficacy in CD yet are ineffective in many individuals and entail substantial costs, with around 10.00 % of initial responders experiencing a loss of response or intolerance annually.48 Nutritional interventions such as iron supplementation for anemia and enteral nutrition for inducing remission in active disease, potentially affect the gut microbiome, epithelial barrier and immune response.

Conversely, dietary management forms the cornerstone of IBD treatment in canines and felines. Implementing a novel diet such as one comprising new protein and carbohydrate sources or a hydrolyzed protein diet is recommended. An affirmative reaction to alterations in diet indicates a potential case of food-responsive entero-pathy, encompassing conditions such as food intolerance and allergy.49 Antimicrobial therapy involving metro-nidazole and tylosin, possessing both immunomodulatory and antibacterial properties, is commonly employed in dogs and cats. Positive outcomes from such treatments are antibiotic-responsive diarrhea or tylosin-responsive diarrhea.50 Corticosteroids represent the primary immunosuppressants prescribed for canine IBD, although side effects are prevalent, necessitating combination therapy or alternative options like cyclosporine.51 In feline IBD, glucocorticoids, alone or in conjunction with antibacterial therapy, are most frequently utilized. While probiotics appear to influence gastrointestinal flora in IBD-afflicted dogs, their clinical efficacy remains unproven. Limited data exists regarding fecal transplantation which has demonstrated potential in treating Clostridium difficile infections but demands prolonged treatment for IBD due to the persistence of intestinal dysbiosis (Fig. 2).52

The limitations inherent in traditional medications for treating intestinal disorders have prompted an exploration of alternative approaches. Among these, functional foods and nutraceuticals, designed explicitly for their health- enhancing properties are emerging as promising human and veterinary medicine options. Nutraceuticals refer to food or supplements enriched with bioactive ingredients to augment their pharmacological properties. These ingredients often encompass phytochemicals and non-nutritive plant compounds found in fruits and vegetables renowned for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory attributes. Research indicates a higher prevalence of complementary and alternative medicine usage involving these natural ingredients among individuals with human IBD compared to healthy counterparts. Moreover, studies showcasing such botanical antioxidant and anti-inflammatory attributes provide optimism for managing chronic intestinal disorders.53

Curcumin, derived from turmeric, stands out for its robust antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, showing potential for ameliorating colitis. Human trials involving UC patients suggest its efficacy in sustaining remission with promising indications also found in veterinary studies focusing on canine intestinal ailments.54 Aloe vera, renowned for its various bioactive compounds boasting antioxidant, antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects has garnered attention. While research underscores its potential benefits in UC, the literature lacks specific veterinary investigations into its application in CE.55 Boswellia serrata, commonly known as frankincense, offers another avenue with its gum resin containing active anti-inflammatory components. Human trials highlight its role in maintaining UC remission, while veterinary studies hint at its potential as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent in dogs.56

One of the notable plants recognized for its efficacy in treating UC is aqueous cinnamon extract. Research indicates that both treatments can lead to a reduction in the cumulative disease score. Furthermore, the findings revealed that cinnamon treatment enhanced the total antioxidant capacity of colonic specimens from colitis-induced rats more effectively than prednisolone treatment. Additionally, myeloperoxidase and nitric oxide levels were significantly down-regulated in the colons of rats treated with cinnamon, surpassing the effects observed in the prednisolone group. These findings suggest that cinnamon, a form of herbal medicine, presents a promising approach to alleviating inflammation in a rat model of UC. It is reasonable to attribute some of the advantageous effects of cinnamon extract to its inherent antioxidant properties and direct anti-inflammatory effects. The aqueous tarragon extract exhibits effects similar to cinnamon extract, improving disease symptoms through a comparable mechanism. These findings suggest that utilizing locally available, cost-effective and side- effect-free medicinal plants is preferable to the use of expensive and potentially harmful medications such as prednisolone and sulfasalazine. This approach not only enhances the well-being of patients but is also likely to be more favorably received by pet owners.57,58

The alleviation of UC-related symptoms is similarly observed with the hydro-alcoholic extract of Dracocephalum moldavica, commonly known as Baderashboo in the local dialect. This fragrant plant is native to the Azerbaijani regions of Iran, where its essence is collected during spring. The extract from this plant demonstrates a notable reduction in TNF-α, IL-6, and interferon-gamma cytokines compared to prednisolone. Additionally, levels of myeloperoxidase and nitric oxide as an inflammation indicator enzyme assay were significantly lower in the colons of rats treated with this extract, exceeding the effects seen in the prednisolone group. These results indicate that D. moldavica extract holds considerable promise as a herbal treatment for reducing inflammation in rat models of UC. The beneficial effects of D. moldavica extract are attributed to its inherent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties.59

Several other nutraceuticals, such as wheatgrass juice, seeds of Plantago spp., thymol, bilberries (Vaccinium myrtillus), green tea (Camellia sinensis), citrus fruits and pomegranate demonstrate promising benefits based on human studies warranting further exploration in veterinary medicine.60,61

Conclusion and future perspective.

The IBD is a condition that impacts not only humans but also dogs and cats, prompting researchers to investigate common disease mechanisms that are shaped by both anatomical and genetic factors. Dogs are an effective model for studying human IBD, as they exhibit comparable immunological deficiencies and disruptions in their microbiota. Unlike humans, that categorize IBD into distinct forms such as UC and CD, companion animals frequently display inflammation affecting both the small and large intestines simultaneously. In the case of cats, their IBD presents unique characteristics, influenced by their carnivorous diet and susceptibility to inflammation affecting the liver, pancreas and intestines concurrently. These species-specific differences necessitate tailored treatment strategies that focus on the specific sites of inflammation. While immunosuppressive medications continue to be the conventional treatment for IBD, there is a growing interest in alternative therapies including dietary modifications, nutritional supplements and fecal microbiota transplantation which are being explored for human and veterinary applications. The study of IBD in dogs offers valuable insights into potential treatments for human IBD and vice versa, highlighting the possibility of developing innovative therapeutic approaches that transcend species boundaries. Nonetheless, many currently available medications demonstrate limited effectiveness and safety, emphasizing the potential benefits of adopting a comparative research approach to enhance treatment options for humans and animals suffering from this complex disease.
