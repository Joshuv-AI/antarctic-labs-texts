Neddylation-dependent CUL3-KLHL12 E3 ligase drives microglial oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in traumatic brain injury by targeting GCLM for degradation
Authors: Yu Ni, Yuan Liu, Xincheng Zhang, Yimin Huang, Huayu Kang, Chenxuan Yu, Zhengqiao Jiang, Yuxuan Xiong, Ting Lei, Kai Shu, Chao Gan, Huaqiu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicroglia-mediated neuroinflammation and oxidative stress are pivotal drivers of secondary injury following traumatic brain injury (TBI). While neddylation governs essential cellular functions, its specific contribution to microglial activation and TBI pathology remains poorly understood.MethodsWe integrated bulk microglial RNA sequencing profiles with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from TBI mouse brains. To assess therapeutic potential, we employed a controlled cortical impact mouse model and treated animals with the neddylation inhibitor MLN4924. The role of microglia was validated using microglia-depleted mice. Mechanistically, a combinatorial approach utilizing AlphaFold 3 molecular docking predictions, quantitative proteomics, and immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry was performed to identify molecular targets.ResultsWe revealed a specific and robust up-regulation of neddylation exclusively within microglial clusters. Pharmacological inhibition of neddylation using MLN4924 significantly ameliorated neurological deficits, attenuated brain edema, and preserved blood-brain barrier integrity. Crucially, these neuroprotective benefits were abrogated in microglia-depleted mice, pinpointing microglia as the primary cellular target. We identified the glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM) as a novel substrate of the CUL3–KLHL12 E3 ligase complex. MLN4924 inhibits CUL3 neddylation, thereby impeding the CUL3–KLHL12-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of GCLM. Consequently, GCLM stabilization restores intracellular glutathione synthesis, effectively scavenging reactive oxygen species and mitigating neuroinflammation.ConclusionsOur findings characterize the Neddylation-CUL3–KLHL12-GCLM axis as a critical regulator of microglial redox homeostasis and highlight this pathway as a promising therapeutic target for TBI intervention.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08559-3.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a formidable global health challenge, characterized by high mortality rates and persistent long-term disabilities [1]. Despite advancements in neurosurgical interventions and critical care, the clinical prognosis for TBI patients has not seen commensurate improvements, largely due to the intricate heterogeneity of its pathophysiology [1, 2]. The initial mechanical insult triggers a protracted and complex cascade of secondary injuries that can persist for days or even weeks. As the primary damage is instantaneous and irreversible, the therapeutic window effectively relies on mitigating these secondary sequelae [2, 3]. Within this pathological landscape, the synergistic interplay between fulminant neuroinflammation and oxidative stress has emerged as a central driver of neuronal apoptosis, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disintegration, and neurological dysfunction [3–5]. Consequently, deciphering the molecular checkpoints that govern the inflammatory microenvironment and redox homeostasis is imperative for developing neuroprotective strategies capable of arresting this deleterious cascade.

As the resident immune sentinels of the central nervous system, microglia orchestrate the delicate balance between tissue repair and secondary damage following TBI [3, 6]. In the acute phase, microglia undergo rapid activation; while a controlled response facilitates debris clearance, sustained and aberrant pro-inflammatory activation precipitates a “respiratory burst,” releasing a deluge of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α [4, 5, 7]. This oxidative surge not only inflicts collateral damage on neurons and endothelial cells but also compromises BBB integrity, thereby recruiting peripheral immune cells and establishing a self-amplifying “inflammation-oxidation” loop [4, 8]. To counteract such insults, cellular survival depends heavily on the glutathione (GSH) antioxidant system [9]. Emerging evidence indicates that the depletion of microglial antioxidant capacity post-TBI is critically linked to the rapid loss of the glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM), rendering cells vulnerable to ROS accumulation [6]. However, the upstream post-translational modification (PTM) machinery that dictates the stability and turnover of GCLM in the TBI microenvironment remains poorly defined.

Protein stability is rigorously controlled by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), a process often regulated by upstream modifications such as neddylation. Neddylation involves the covalent attachment of the ubiquitin-like protein NEDD8 to substrate proteins via an E1–E2–E3 enzymatic cascade, a mechanism essential for activating the Cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase (CRL) family [10, 11]. The neddylation of the Cullin scaffold induces a conformational rearrangement that relieves autoinhibition, thereby licensing the ligase for substrate ubiquitination and subsequent degradation [11]. Pharmacological blockade of this pathway using the NAE inhibitor MLN4924 (Pevonedistat) has demonstrated efficacy in suppressing aberrant CRL activity and has shown neuroprotective potential in models of ischemic stroke and neurodegeneration [11]. Yet, the specific role of neddylation in modulating microglial oxidative stress following TBI, and the precise E3 ligase complexes involved, have not been fully elucidated.

Among the diverse CRL family, Cullin 3 (CUL3) is structurally unique in its ability to bypass traditional adaptor proteins, binding directly to BTB domain-containing adaptors to form functional E3 ligase complexes with exquisite substrate specificity [10, 12]. Recent structural and functional analyses have highlighted the versatility of CUL3 complexes, such as CUL3–KCTD10 and CUL3–LZTR1, in regulating critical cellular processes ranging from transcription-replication conflicts to immune cell activation [13–15]. Moreover, CUL3 has been implicated in the regulation of NRF2-mediated antioxidant responses, suggesting a potential link between CUL3-dependent ubiquitination and redox homeostasis [10, 16, 17]. Given the tight regulation of antioxidant proteins by the UPS, we hypothesized that a specific CUL3-based complex might serve as the critical nexus linking neddylation signaling to GCLM stability. Specifically, we posit that TBI-induced hyperactivation of the neddylation pathway promotes the assembly of the CUL3–KLHL12 complex, which targets GCLM for degradation, thereby impairing GSH synthesis and exacerbating neuroinflammation.

In this study, we re-analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to map the dynamic cellular landscape of the cortex following TBI, revealing a robust and specific upregulation of the neddylation in microglia [3, 18]. Integrating a controlled cortical impact mouse model with pharmacological inhibition (MLN4924) and genetic manipulation, we validated the therapeutic efficacy of targeting neddylation to mitigate secondary injury. Furthermore, using immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS), we identified the CUL3–KLHL12 E3 ligase complex as the key effector mediating GCLM ubiquitination. Our findings delineate a novel Neddylation-CUL3–KLHL12-GCLM axis that regulates microglial redox homeostasis, providing a compelling preclinical rationale for repurposing MLN4924 to restore endogenous antioxidant defense and improve outcomes in TBI.

Materials and methods

Integrated analysis of single-cell and bulk transcriptomics

Publicly available transcriptomic datasets were acquired from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), comprising two single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets derived from the mouse cortex (GSE180862) and hippocampus (GSE247339), alongside a bulk RNA-sequencing dataset of sorted microglia (GSE253476) utilized for cross-validation. Processing, integration, and dimensionality reduction of scRNA-seq data were executed using the Seurat R package (v4.0). Following rigorous quality control and normalization, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was employed to visualize the landscape of major cell clusters, which were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression. To delineate microglial heterogeneity, the microglia cluster was extracted and subjected to re-clustering, facilitating the identification of distinct subclusters and the assessment of their distribution across experimental conditions to pinpoint TBI-associated populations. Marker genes characterizing these TBI-specific microglial subclusters were determined using the FindMarkers function with a threshold of |log2FoldChange| >0.25 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. In parallel, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the bulk RNA-seq dataset distinguishing the TBI group from the Sham group were identified based on criteria of |log2FoldChange| >1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Finally, to establish a robust core gene signature associated with acute TBI, an intersection analysis was performed on upregulated genes derived from the cortical microglia subcluster, the hippocampal microglia subcluster, and the sorted bulk microglia dataset. Overlapping targets, including Nedd8, were visualized using Venn diagrams.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, 17–22 g) were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. Mice were housed in a controlled environment at 22 ± 3 °C with 60% relative humidity under a 12-h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to standard rodent food and water in the Experimental Animal Center of Tongji Science Building at Tongji Hospital. All experimental protocols and procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval no. TJH 202,201,049).

Controlled Cortical Impact (CCI) Model

The TBI model was established using a controlled cortical impact device (YHCI99; Wuhan Yihong Technology, Wuhan, China). Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for induction and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane. A 3-mm2 craniotomy was performed over the left parietal cortex (1.0 mm posterior to bregma, 1.0 mm lateral to the midline), ensuring the dura mater remained intact. The injury was induced using the following parameters: impact speed 3.5 m/s, deformation depth 1.0 mm, and dwell time 400 ms. Sham mice underwent the same craniotomy procedure without the impact. Following the procedure, the scalp incision was sutured, and body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C using a homeothermic heating blanket until recovery from anesthesia.

Animal sampling and tissue preparation

At the designated experimental endpoints (primarily day 3 post-TBI), mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (180 mg/kg) and xylazine (30 mg/kg). For histological assessment, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), after which brains were post-fixed for 24 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. For biochemical analysis and microglia sorting, mice were perfused with PBS only; ipsilateral cortical tissues were either rapidly dissected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for protein extraction, or dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a Gentle Enzymatic Dissociation Kit (RWD Life Science) and labeled with CD45/CD11b antibodies for subsequent flow cytometry isolation.

Modified neurological Severity score

Neurological deficits were evaluated using the Modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS) as previously described [19]. Neurological function was graded on a scale of 0–18 (0 = normal, 18 = maximal deficit) assessing motor, sensory, reflex, and balance tasks.

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using the rotarod test at a fixed velocity(ZH600-B, Zhenghua Biological Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd., China). Mice were placed on the rotating rod maintained at a constant speed of 10 rpm, and the latency to fall was quantified

Behavioral assessments

Behavioral tests were recorded and analyzed using the XR-XM101 behavioral analysis system (XinRuan, Shanghai, China).

Morris Water Maze (MWM)

Spatial learning and reference memory were evaluated using the Morris Water Maze (MWM) paradigm, as previously described. The apparatus consisted of a circular pool (120 cm in diameter, 50 cm in depth) filled with water rendered opaque and maintained at a temperature of 20–22 °C. A submerged escape platform (6 cm in diameter; 1 cm below the water surface) was fixed within the southwest quadrant. During the acquisition phase, mice were released from semi-randomized starting positions across the four quadrants, and the time required to locate the platform (escape latency) was quantified. Trials were terminated when the mouse mounted the platform and remained for 10 s. If the platform was not located within a 60 s cutoff, the animal was manually guided to the target and allowed a 15 s consolidation period. To assess memory retention, a probe trial was conducted 24 hours after the final training session (Day 7) with the platform removed. Swimming trajectories were captured via an overhead video system and analyzed using DigBehv software (Jiliang Software Technology Co., Shanghai, China).

Barnes Maze

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the Barnes Maze paradigm. The apparatus consisted of an elevated circular platform (90 cm in diameter) featuring 16 equidistant holes along the perimeter. Visual spatial cues were positioned surrounding the maze and maintained constant throughout the experiment to serve as navigational references. A dark escape box was affixed beneath one designated target hole. During the acquisition phase (4 consecutive days), mice were initially habituated in the escape box for 1 min. Subsequently, animals were placed in a cylindrical start chamber at the maze center. After 10 s, the chamber was removed, and bright illumination (400 lux) was activated to serve as an aversive stimulus. Mice were allowed a maximum of 3 min to locate and enter the escape box. Upon entry, the light was extinguished, and the hole was covered to facilitate a 15 s consolidation period. To evaluate memory retention, a probe trial was conducted 24 hours after the final training session. The escape box was removed, and mice were permitted to freely explore the platform for 3 min. Trajectories were tracked and analyzed using the TopScan system (Clever Sys Inc., VA, USA).

Open Field Test (OFT)

Spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were evaluated using the Open Field Test (OFT). The apparatus consisted of a square arena (50 × 50 cm) enclosed by opaque walls. Mice were individually placed in the center of the field and allowed to freely explore for a duration of 5 min. To eliminate olfactory cues between trials, the arena was thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol. Behavioral kinematics were recorded using a video tracking system (XinRuan, Shanghai, China). The total distance traveled was quantified as a measure of general locomotor activity, while the time spent in the center zone (defined as the central 50% of the arena) served as an inverse index of anxiety, reflecting the animal’s thigmotactic behavior.

Novel Object Recognition (NOR)

Recognition memory was evaluated using the Novel Object Recognition (NOR) paradigm. The test was conducted in an open-field arena (50 × 50 cm). During the familiarization phase, mice were allowed to explore two identical objects placed symmetrically for 10 min. Exploration was strictly defined as sniffing or directing the nose toward the object at a distance of <2 cm; sitting on or leaning against the object was not considered exploration. After a 24-hour retention interval, the test phase was performed to assess long-term memory. One of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object of a different shape and texture but similar volume. Mice were allowed to explore both objects for 5 min. To avoid olfactory cues and side bias, the arena and objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials, and the positions of the novel and familiar objects were counterbalanced across animals. The Discrimination Index (DI) was calculated to quantify preferential exploration using the following formula: DI= (Tnovel - Tfamiliar)/ (Tnovel + Tfamiliar). Where Tnovel and Tfamiliar represent the time spent exploring the novel and familiar objects, respectively. Mice with a total exploration time of less than 10 s during the test phase were excluded from the analysis to ensure data validity.

Brain water content assessment

Brain edema was evaluated at 3 days post-TBI. The ipsilateral hemisphere was weighed immediately after removal (wet weight) and again after drying at 100 °C for 24 h (dry weight). Brain water content was calculated as: [(wet weight – dry weight) / wet weight] × 100%.

Evaluation of blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability

BBB integrity was assessed on day 3 post-TBI using the Evans Blue (EB) extravasation assay. Mice received an intravenous injection of 0.5% EB (2 mL/kg). After 1 h of circulation, animals were perfused transcardially with PBS to remove intravascular dye. For quantification, brains were dissected, weighed, and homogenized in PBS. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (4 °C) for 5 min. Following overnight incubation, supernatants were treated with 50% trichloroacetic acid to precipitate proteins, and absorbance was measured at 610 nm using a spectrophotometer. EB concentration was calculated based on a standard curve normalized to tissue weight. For macroscopic visualization, brains were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h and sectioned into 2–3 mm coronal slices for imaging.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, TGF-β1, and IL-10 in cell culture supernatants and brain tissue homogenates were quantified using ELISA kits (Abclonal, Wuhan, China). For in vitro samples, culture media were collected 24 h post-treatment; for in vivo samples, 30 mg of brain tissue was homogenized in 500 μL of DMEM on ice, filtered through a 40 μm mesh, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min to obtain the supernatant. The assay was performed by washing the plates three times with 350 μL of washing buffer (40 s per wash), followed by incubating 100 μL of samples or standards at 37 °C for 2 h. After three washes, 100 μL of biotinylated antibody working solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by the addition of 100 μL of streptavidin-HRP solution for 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, 100 μL of TMB substrate was added and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15–20 min; the reaction was terminated with 50 μL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Nissl body staining

Brain sections underwent standard deparaffinization and rehydration processes. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with Nissl staining solution (G1036, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 10 min at ambient temperature. Following three rinses with distilled water, the sections were dehydrated using two cycles of 95% ethanol (2 min per cycle). Finally, the samples were cleared in xylene for 5 min and mounted with neutral resin for histological analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

Wild-type (WT) and Gclm-knockout (GCLM-KO) BV2 microglial cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, G4515, Servicebio) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141C, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (G4003, Servicebio) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

For experimental assays, a sequential treatment protocol was adopted. Cells were pre-treated with GSH-EE (5 mM) or vehicle at 0 h. To induce an inflammatory response, Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, HY-D1056, MCE, China; 500 ng/mL) was added to the culture medium at the 30-min time point. Subsequently, cells were treated with MLN4924 (600 nM) at 1 h. Samples were harvested at specific intervals based on the downstream analysis: 6 h for ROS detection, 12 h for mRNA quantification, and 24 h for cytokine analysis (ELISA), migration, and phagocytosis assays.

Cell transfection and genetic modulation

Transient transfection of BV2 cells was performed at 60–70% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Genetic manipulations included CUL3 silencing via small interfering RNAs (siRNAs; RIBOBIO, Guangzhou, China), GCLM knockout using specific sgRNA plasmids (Tsingke Biological Technology, Beijing, China), and overexpression or ubiquitination assays utilizing pcDNA3.1(+)-based constructs (CUL3-HA, Ubiquitin-FLAG, and K48R/K63R mutants; BioSune, Shanghai, China). Transfection complexes were prepared in Opti-MEM, incubated with cells for 6 h, and replaced with complete medium; cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent immunoblotting or immunoprecipitation analyses. Target sequences for siRNAs and sgRNAs are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Transwell migration assay

To evaluate microglial mobility, BV2 cells (5 × 104/chamber) were seeded into the upper compartments of Transwell inserts (8-μm pore size; Corning, USA) containing serum-free medium. The lower compartments were filled with 10% FBS-supplemented medium serving as a chemoattractant. Following a 24-h incubation with LPS (500 ng/mL) in the presence or absence of MLN4924 (600 nM), cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were mechanically removed. The migrated cells on the bottom surface were subjected to 4% paraformaldehyde fixation (30 min) and Crystal Violet staining (G1014, Servicebio; 30 min). Migration rates were determined by averaging cell counts from four randomly selected microscopic fields per sample.

Flow cytometric analysis of ROS and Phagocytosis

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from fresh peri-contusional cortical tissues or cultured BV2 cells. Tissues were mechanically dissociated and enzymatically digested with Collagenase IV and DNase I at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by filtration through a 70-µm cell strainer.

ROS quantification

Cells were incubated with the Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Fluorometric Assay Kit (Red) (E-BC-F005, Elabscience) at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. After three washes with PBS, intracellular ROS levels were determined by measuring fluorescence intensity in the PE channel.

Ex vivo phagocytosis assay

Tissue-derived single-cell suspensions were incubated with pHrodo™ Red Zymosan BioParticles™ (0.5 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in complete medium at 37 °C for 2 h. This probe emits fluorescence specifically within the acidic phagosomal environment. Following incubation, cells were washed and surface-stained with fluorophore-conjugated anti-CD11b and anti-CD45 antibodies to identify microglia. Data acquisition was performed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Microglial populations were gated based on surface markers, and phagocytic activity was quantified as the percentage of PE-positive cells (pHrodo-high) using FlowJo v10.8.1 software.

Determination of brain GSH content

Brain tissue samples were collected at day 3 after TBI and rapidly processed on ice. Briefly, brain tissues from the indicated region were homogenized in cold PBS, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g at 4 °C to remove tissue debris. The supernatants were collected for GSH measurement. Protein concentrations in the same samples were determined using a standard protein assay, and GSH levels were normalized to total protein content.

Reduced GSH content was quantified using the Glutathione (GSH) Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reagents were equilibrated to room temperature before use, and Reagent A was mixed thoroughly before the assay. Samples, calibrators, and working reagents were added to a clear flat-bottom 96-well plate. The assay is based on the reaction of GSH with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), producing a yellow-colored product. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader. GSH concentrations were calculated from the standard curve generated using the supplied calibrator and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Detection of intracellular GSH using GSH tracker probe

Intracellular GSH levels were assessed using GSH Tracker Probe CPDSA (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-D3004). Briefly, WT BV2 and GCLM-KO BV2 cells were treated with LPS and/or MLN4924 as indicated. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with CPDSA at a final concentration of 20 μM for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cells were then washed with PBS to remove excess probe, and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst. Fluorescence images were captured using identical acquisition settings, with CPDSA detected at an excitation wavelength of 635 nm and an emission range of 655–755 nm. CPDSA fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ and normalized to the number of Hoechst-positive cells. Results were expressed as CPDSA fluorescence per cell.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from peri-contusional cortex tissues or cultured BV2 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (G2007, Servicebio) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (PMSF). Lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 13,000× g for 30 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (G2026, Servicebio). Equal amounts of protein (10–20 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE (6–12% gels) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (MilliporeSigma, Germany). After blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PSD95, Occludin, Synaptophysin (SYP), Claudin-5, CUL3, GCLM, NAE1, NEDD8, and Ubiquitin. β-actin or α-tubulin served as loading controls. Detailed information regarding antibody clones, dilutions, and sources is listed in Supplementary Table 1. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence system (Beyotime, China) and quantified via ImageJ 1.8.0 software.

Cycloheximide chase assay

GCLM protein stability was assessed by cycloheximide chase assay. Briefly, BV2 cells were transfected with si-NC, si-CUL3, si-KLHL12, CUL3 overexpression plasmid, or si-KLHL12 together with CUL3 overexpression plasmid. After transfection, cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-12320) at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL to block new protein synthesis. Cells were collected at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h after CHX treatment and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting with antibodies against GCLM and β-actin. GCLM band intensity was quantified using ImageJ, normalized to β-actin, and expressed relative to the corresponding 0 h level. Protein degradation kinetics were compared among groups.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from peri-contusional cortical tissues or BV2 cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a KAIAO K5800 spectrophotometer (China). mRNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (R223-01, Vazyme, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 1 Plus system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q341-02, Vazyme) to evaluate the expression of target genes (Cul1–5), inflammatory cytokines (Il6, Il1b, Tnf, Nos2), and phenotypic markers (Arg1, Mrc1). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis (60 °C to 95 °C) was conducted to verify amplification specificity. Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to the internal control (β-actin or Gapdh) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method, where Δ Cq = Cq (target gene) - Cq (control) and ΔΔ Cq = Δ Cq (experimental) - Δ Cq (control). The fold change in gene expression of the experimental group was compared with the control group. The sequences of all primers used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded brain sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in boiling Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Sections were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting Iba1, CD68, or NEDD8. Specific information on the antibodies used is available in Supplementary Table 1. Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using an Olympus CKX53 microscope, and data were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Duolink proximity ligation assay (PLA)

To visualize in situ protein-protein interactions and ubiquitination levels, the Duolink In Situ Red Starter Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was employed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, BV2 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and blocked with the provided blocking solution. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific pairs of primary antibodies raised in different species: (1) anti-GCLM and anti-Ubiquitin to detect ubiquitination levels; (2) anti-KLHL12 and anti-GCLM; and (3) anti-CUL3 and anti-KLHL12 to assess direct protein interactions. Subsequently, PLA probes (anti-rabbit PLUS and anti-mouse MINUS) were added, followed by ligation and amplification steps to generate red fluorescent puncta indicative of positive interactions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000).

Integrative Proteomic characterization and network Analysis

Data processing and differential expression analysis

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using MaxQuant software (v1.6.17.0) for peptide identification and Label-Free Quantification (LFQ). Searches were performed against the Mouse UniProt reference proteome. Standard filtering criteria were applied to exclude potential contaminants and reverse decoy sequences. To ensure robust quantification, proteins were retained only if valid LFQ intensities were detected in at least one experimental group (n = 3 biological replicates). Missing values were imputed from a normal distribution (width = 0.3, down-shift = 1.8) to simulate low-abundance signals near the detection limit. Differential expression analysis was subsequently performed using the limma R package. Differentially Expressed Proteins (DEPs) were identified based on a stringent threshold of p < 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 (log2FC ≥ 1 or ≤−1).

Functional annotation and network construction

Hierarchical clustering analysis and heatmap visualization were conducted in R (v3.2.0) to delineate expression signatures across the Sham, TBI, and TBI+MLN4924 cohorts. Volcano plots were generated to visualize the statistical significance relative to the magnitude of expression change. To elucidate the biological implications of the DEPs, Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was utilized to identify coordinated pathway alterations independent of specific fold-change cutoffs. Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) networks were constructed based on the STRING database and visualized using Cytoscape software. Finally, to validate Neddylation targets, Venn diagram analysis was employed to identify the intersection between the upregulated proteome profile and the NEDD8-interacting candidates identified via IP-MS.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Sample extraction was performed by transferring samples to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes, followed by the addition of 600 μL pre-cooled methanol: water (4:1, v/v) containing a mixture of internal standards. Samples were dispersed via pipetting and subjected to ice-bath sonication (1620 W, 10 min; 6 s on/4 s off). Extraction was further promoted by incubation in an ice-water bath for 20 min, followed by overnight protein precipitation at − 40 °C. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), 400 μL of the supernatant was transferred to LC-MS vials and dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen or vacuum. The dried residues were reconstituted in 300 μL of methanol:water (1:4, v/v), vortexed for 1 min, sonicated for 10 min, and incubated again at − 40 °C overnight. Following a final centrifugation step (12,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C), 150 μL of the supernatant was collected for analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all study samples. Chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection were conducted using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus system coupled to a Thermo QE ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem high-resolution mass spectrometer (Waters/Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Protein structure prediction and molecular interface analysis

To elucidate the structural basis of the ternary complex formation among CUL3, KLHL12, and GCLM, protein structure prediction was performed using the AlphaFold3 AI system. Full-length amino acid sequences for mouse CUL3 (UniProt ID: Q9JLV5), KLHL12 (UniProt ID: Q8BZM0), and GCLM (UniProt ID: O09172) were retrieved from the UniProt database. The predicted multimers were ranked based on the interface predicted template modeling score (ipTM) and predicted template modeling score (pTM). The top-ranked model of the CUL3–KLHL12-GCLM trimeric complex was selected for downstream analysis. Structural visualization and detailed characterization of the binding interfaces—including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic contacts—were conducted using PyMOL (v3.1.5.1; Schrödinger, LLC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed prior to hypothesis testing. For normally distributed data, comparisons were made using paired or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, or one-way/two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for multiple-group comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To determine whether TBI-associated transcriptional remodeling was accompanied by altered multicellular communication, we performed CellChat analysis to infer ligand–receptor-mediated signaling networks. Network-level analysis showed that TBI altered both the number and strength of interactions among major CNS cell types compared with sham controls in cortex, indicating broad remodeling of the TBI microenvironment (Supplementary Figure 1E). Global ligand–receptor profiling further revealed that these communication changes involved multiple functional modules, including immune/inflammatory signaling, growth factor pathways, ECM–integrin interactions, cell adhesion, metabolic signaling, and phagocytosis-related pathways (Supplementary Figure 2A). We then focused on microglia-derived outgoing signals, given the presence of a TBI-24 h-specific microglial subcluster. TBI-24 h microglia displayed enhanced communication with astrocytes, endothelial cells, neurons, vascular smooth muscle cells, and oligodendroglial lineage cells (Fig. 1E). These interactions prominently involved injury-associated APP, GALECTIN, MIF, PSAP, and TNF pathways, suggesting that acute TBI promotes microglia-centered rewiring of intercellular communication within the TBI microenvironment.

To elucidate the key molecular drivers orchestrating this pathological phenotype, we performed an intersection analysis of upregulated genes across the cortical, hippocampal, and sorted microglia datasets. This convergent screening strategy prioritized 157 core genes, among which Nedd8—the apex activator of the neddylation cascade—emerged as a top-ranked candidate (Fig. 1F, G). These multi-omics data implicate the activation of the neddylation pathway as a conserved and specific signature of the microglial response to acute traumatic injury.

To mechanistically dissect the functional relevance of this pathway, we utilized the BV2 microglial cell line to establish an in vitro model of neuroinflammation via LPS stimulation. We first confirmed that pharmacological inhibition of NAE using MLN4924 [23] did not compromise cell viability at the selected concentration (Fig. 1H, I). Crucially, MLN4924 treatment effectively ameliorated the LPS-induced activated phenotype, evidenced by a marked suppression of both aberrant migratory capacity (Fig. 1J, K) and hyper-phagocytic activity (Fig. 1L, M). Furthermore, blockade of the neddylation cascade potently abrogated the inflammatory transcriptional signature, resulting in significantly downregulated expression of Il1b, Il6, and Tnf (Fig. 1N–P). Collectively, these data identify the neddylation pathway as a pivotal regulator of the pro-inflammatory microglial state associated with TBI pathology.

MLN4924 attenuates neurological deficits and preserves blood-brain barrier integrity following TBI

To evaluate the translational potential of neddylation inhibition, we administered MLN4924 (60 mg/kg, i.p.) to mice immediately following CCI (Fig. 2A). This dosing regimen was selected on the basis of previous preclinical studies demonstrating that MLN4924 at the same dose effectively suppresses neddylation activity and confers therapeutic benefit in diverse pathological settings [23–25], and we performed an additional tolerability assessment in parallel. Over the observation period, MLN4924-treated mice exhibited body weight trajectories and daily food intake comparable to those of vehicle-treated controls, with no statistically significant differences between groups (Supplementary Figure 3A, B). Gross pathological and vascular assessments confirmed that the treatment significantly ameliorated TBI-induced cortical tissue loss and cerebral edema (Fig. 2B–E). Furthermore, MLN4924 preserved blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, as evidenced by a marked reduction in Evans Blue extravasation (Fig. 2F, G). To interrogate the cellular basis of this protection, we examined the neurovascular unit components. MLN4924 effectively mitigated neuronal loss (NeuN+) and maintained myelin architecture (MBP+) as showed in Figs. 2I–2K. Western blotting further revealed the restoration of key tight junction proteins, Occludin and Claudin-5, which were otherwise depleted in the injured cortex (Figs. 2L–2P) [26]. Crucially, we hypothesized that this structural preservation was mechanistically linked to the suppression of microglial over-activation. To this end, we performed fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate microglia. After debris exclusion and singlet selection, CD11b+CD45+ microglia were gated for downstream functional analysis (Supplementary Figure 4A, B). Flow cytometry analysis of the sorted microglial population showed that MLN4924 treatment significantly decreased the PE-A+ fraction, indicating reduced erythrophagocytic capacity after TBI (Figs. 2Q, 2R). This functional suppression was substantiated by immunofluorescence analysis: while TBI induced robust expression of the lysosomal marker CD68 and its extensive colocalization with Iba1 [25], MLN4924 treatment significantly attenuated CD68 levels and reduced the spatial overlap between CD68 and Iba1 (Figs. 2S–2V), indicating inhibited phagocytic activity. Consistent with this, microglia in the treated group shifted from an activated amoeboid state back to a ramified, surveillance-like morphology (Supplementary Figure 5A–D).

Finally, these histological and cellular improvements culminated in comprehensive long-term functional recovery. Beyond the acute phase, MLN4924 significantly preserved motor coordination on the Rotarod (Fig. 2W). Extended evaluation showed that the treatment alleviated anxiety-like behavior and improved locomotor activity (Supplementary Figure 5E–J). Moreover, MLN4924 enhanced spatial memory in the Morris Water Maze, as reflected by representative search trajectories (Fig. 2X), shortened escape latencies, and increased time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 2Y). Collectively, these data demonstrate that MLN4924 confers robust neuroprotection by preserving BBB integrity and curbing excessive microglial phagocytosis.

This structural preservation translated into broad functional recovery. In the Rotarod test, microglia-depleted mice exhibited preserved motor coordination comparable to the MLN4924 group (Fig. 3L). Crucially, to definitively confirm this dependency, we assessed whether MLN4924 could confer additional benefits in a microglia-deficient environment. We reasoned that if MLN4924 exerted direct protective effects on neurons or the vasculature, the combined treatment (TBI+PLX5622+MLN4924) should offer superior protection compared to depletion alone. However, quantitative analysis revealed no significant additive effects across histological, molecular, or behavioral readouts (Fig. 3B–S). The observation that MLN4924 failed to further enhance neuroprotection when microglia were absent indicates that its therapeutic efficacy is not driven by direct action on neurons or endothelial cells, but rather depends on modulating microglial function. Collectively, these data establish microglia as the essential cellular conduit through which MLN4924 exerts its neuroprotective effects against TBI.

To identify the specific molecular scaffold underlying this activation, we performed anti-NEDD8 immunoprecipitation. This analysis revealed a distinct enrichment of conjugated proteins migrating at approximately 90 kDa (Fig. 4G), a molecular weight consistent with members of the Cullin family, the canonical substrates of neddylation. To distinguish which specific Cullin isoform was involved, we systematically screened the expression profiles of Cul1 through Cul5 at the mRNA level in vitro. This screen identified Cul3 as the most profoundly induced isoform (Supplementary Figure 7E–J). Importantly, this induction pattern was recapitulated at the protein level in the injured cortex in vivo, where MLN4924 treatment effectively reversed the aberrant elevation of CUL3 (Fig. 4H, Supplementary Figure 7K-P). These data implicate a dysregulated CUL3-dependent complex as the primary pathological driver.

To map the downstream effectors of this CUL3 axis, we performed quantitative proteomics comparing Sham, TBI, and TBI+MLN4924 groups. Quantitative proteomics revealed that TBI induced a marked inflammatory proteomic signature compared with Sham controls, with upregulated proteins enriched in leukocyte migration, innate immune activation, NF-κB signaling, lysosomal/endosomal compartments, and phagocytic vesicle-related pathways (Supplementary Figure 7Q, R). We then compared TBI + MLN4924 mice with TBI mice to determine how neddylation inhibition reshaped this injury-associated proteome. MLN4924 treatment reduced multiple inflammatory mediators and increased proteins associated with redox regulation and ubiquitin-dependent turnover, including GCLM and KLHL12 (Fig. 4I). Consistently, enrichment analysis of MLN4924-upregulated proteins highlighted ubiquitin–proteasome and CRL-related pathways, such as protein polyubiquitination, ubiquitin ligase complex, cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase complex, and ubiquitin-like protein transferase activity (Fig. 4J; Supplementary Figure 7S). These findings prompted us to focus on CRL-dependent protein turnover as a potential mechanism underlying MLN4924-mediated protection. Heatmap analysis revealed coordinated alterations in multiple proteins involved in neddylation, ubiquitination, proteasomal degradation, and stress-response regulation between TBI and TBI + MLN4924 groups (Fig. 4K). Among these molecules, GCLM was consistently upregulated following MLN4924 treatment, suggesting that it may serve as a downstream effector of neddylation blockade.

Based on the principle of substrate trapping, we reasoned that CRL substrates would accumulate after MLN4924-mediated inhibition of neddylation-dependent protein turnover. We therefore intersected proteins upregulated in the TBI + MLN4924 group with the NEDD8-interacting proteome identified by Co-IP/MS, yielding four candidate proteins: SQOR, KLHL12, GCLM, and NDUFS1 (Fig. 4L). Among these, GCLM, a key regulator of glutathione biosynthesis, and KLHL12, a CUL3 substrate adaptor, were prioritized for further investigation [28, 29]. Protein–protein interaction analysis further placed them within a CUL3-centered neddylation/CRL network containing NEDD8, NAE1, UBA3, RBX1, and CUL3 (Fig. 4M). To further examine the Cullin specificity of GCLM regulation, we performed a Cullin-family knockdown screen in BV2 microglia. Among the tested Cullin members, CUL3 silencing produced the most pronounced increase in GCLM protein levels, whereas knockdown of CUL1, CUL2, CUL4A, CUL4B, or CUL5 had comparatively limited effects (Supplementary Figure 8A, B). These results support CUL3 as the predominant Cullin family member involved in GCLM regulation and nominate the CUL3–KLHL12–GCLM axis as a candidate mechanism linking neddylation-dependent CRL activity to impaired antioxidant homeostasis after TBI.

We next determined whether GCLM is regulated through ubiquitin–proteasome-dependent turnover. Before functional analysis, we first validated the efficiency of CUL3 loss- and gain-of-function manipulations in BV2 microglia. Three independent siRNAs effectively reduced CUL3 expression, whereas CUL3 overexpression markedly increased its protein abundance (Supplementary Figure 9A–D). Using these validated approaches, in situ PLA with antibodies against GCLM and ubiquitin showed that CUL3 knockdown reduced, whereas CUL3 overexpression enhanced, GCLM-associated ubiquitination signals (Supplementary Figure 9E). Consistently, immunoblotting confirmed that CUL3 depletion increased GCLM abundance, whereas CUL3 overexpression reduced GCLM levels (Supplementary Figure 9F–H). We further validated KLHL12 knockdown efficiency and found that KLHL12 depletion did not substantially affect CUL3 expression but markedly increased GCLM protein levels (Supplementary Figure 9I–L). These data suggested that CUL3 and its adaptor KLHL12 negatively regulate GCLM abundance.

We then directly examined whether GCLM undergoes proteasomal degradation. Proteasome inhibition with MG132 induced accumulation of ubiquitinated GCLM, indicating that GCLM is subjected to ubiquitin–proteasome-dependent turnover (Fig. 5F). Cycloheximide-chase assays further showed that CUL3 or KLHL12 knockdown prolonged GCLM stability, whereas CUL3 overexpression accelerated GCLM degradation; notably, KLHL12 depletion attenuated the destabilizing effect of CUL3 overexpression (Fig. 5G). Immunoprecipitation-based ubiquitination assays confirmed that CUL3 silencing reduced, whereas CUL3 overexpression enhanced, GCLM polyubiquitination (Fig. 5H, I). KLHL12 depletion similarly decreased GCLM ubiquitination (Fig. 5J). Together, these results demonstrate that CUL3, together with its substrate adaptor KLHL12, promotes ubiquitination-dependent proteasomal degradation of GCLM.

Mechanistically, ubiquitin-mutant analysis revealed that GCLM ubiquitination was dependent on K48-linked, but not K63-linked, polyubiquitin chains (Fig. 5K). Site-mapping experiments further identified Lys263 as the major ubiquitin acceptor site required for GCLM ubiquitination (Fig. 5L). These results establish that the CUL3–KLHL12 E3 ligase complex targets GCLM for K48-linked polyubiquitination at Lys263 and subsequent proteasomal degradation (Fig. 5M).

Finally, because MLN4924 was administered systemically, we examined whether this mechanism was operative within microglia in vivo. In injured cortical tissue, TBI increased NEDD8-conjugated proteins and CUL3 expression while reducing GCLM abundance, and these alterations were reversed by MLN4924 treatment (Supplementary Figure 10A). More importantly, in FACS-sorted microglia from the ipsilateral cortex after TBI, TBI similarly increased NEDD8-conjugated proteins and CUL3/KLHL12 expression, accompanied by reduced GCLM abundance. MLN4924 reversed these alterations within the purified microglial population (Supplementary Figure 10B).

Together with the BV2 microglial gain- and loss-of-function data, these findings support a microglia-intrinsic neddylation–CUL3–KLHL12–GCLM mechanism contributing to MLN4924-mediated restoration of redox homeostasis after TBI.

To verify that this redox-inflammatory regulation occurs within microglia in vivo, we analyzed FACS-sorted microglia from TBI cortex. MLN4924 reduced Nos2 and Tnf expression, restored Arg1 and Mrc1 expression, and decreased ROS-associated fluorescence in purified microglia from TBI mice (Supplementary Figure 12A, B). These data support a microglia-intrinsic GCLM–GSH-dependent mechanism. Functionally, MLN4924 improved sensorimotor and cognitive outcomes after TBI, whereas BSO abolished these benefits and exogenous GSH reinstated them (Fig. 6K–P; Supplementary Figure 13A–D). Together, these findings establish that restoration of GCLM-dependent glutathione biosynthesis is essential for MLN4924-mediated suppression of microglial inflammation and neurological recovery after TBI.

Discussion

The therapeutic stalemate in traumatic brain injury (TBI) management largely stems from the complexity of secondary injury cascades, where the entanglement of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress creates a self-perpetuating cycle of tissue damage [30–33]. While antioxidant therapies targeting the Nrf2 transcriptional axis have long been proposed, their clinical translation has been hindered by a lack of understanding regarding the post-translational checkpoints that govern the stability of antioxidant enzymes [34–37]. Our study bridges this gap by delineating a precise Neddylation-CUL3–KLHL12-GCLM axis. We demonstrate that the hyper-neddylation state in microglia is not merely a bystander effect but a central driver that collapses cellular redox defense by targeting the rate-limiting enzyme GCLM for ubiquitin-mediated degradation. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for repurposing the neddylation inhibitor MLN4924 to restore redox homeostasis.

The application of scRNA-seq in our study allowed us to resolve a critical ambiguity in the field: the cellular specificity of neddylation following TBI. Recent investigations have highlighted the pathological relevance of this pathway in TBI. For instance, Zhang et al. (2025) recently demonstrated that neddylation aggravates neuronal apoptosis and promotes neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation [38], a finding supported by other studies linking protein modification to neuroinflammation [39–41]. While acknowledging these multicellular contributions, our transcriptomic landscape reveals that microglia exhibit the most robust upregulation of neddylation. Consistently, both sorted microglial RNA-seq and FACS-based analyses confirmed enhanced NEDD8 conjugation, CUL3/KLHL12 induction, GCLM loss, ROS accumulation, and inflammatory polarization within purified microglia after TBI, all of which were reversed by MLN4924. Moreover, microglial depletion largely phenocopied the protective effect of MLN4924 and blunted its additional benefit, supporting a microglia-dominant mechanism. Although our data support a microglia-dominant mechanism, potential contributions from neurons, endothelial cells, astrocytes, and infiltrating immune cells cannot be completely excluded and should be investigated in future cell-type-specific studies.

A central conceptual advance of this study is the identification of the CUL3–KLHL12 complex as the specific effector machinery driving this process. While therapeutic targeting of the ubiquitin-proteasome system is often limited by the risk of off-target toxicity, the modular architecture of Cullin-RING ligases (CRLs)—particularly CUL3—offers a strategy to circumvent this challenge, as their specificity is strictly governed by distinct adaptor proteins [34, 42]. Crucially, accumulating evidence suggests that KLHL12 modulation is metabolically benign. For instance, Zhao et al. (2025) recently demonstrated that intestinal KLHL12 is dispensable for lipid absorption and chylomicron metabolism [43], a finding consistent with other reports indicating that targeting specific KLHL proteins causes minimal systemic perturbation [15, 44–47]. By defining KLHL12 as a critical regulator of central nervous system redox homeostasis, our findings not only expand its functional repertoire but also highlight its potential as a precise therapeutic target with a superior safety profile compared to broad-spectrum proteasome inhibitors. Importantly, we do not infer that GCLM is the sole downstream substrate responsible for all CUL3- or MLN4924-dependent effects. Given the broad impact of neddylation blockade on multiple Cullin ligases, additional CUL3 substrates, including NRF2-related regulators or other redox- and inflammation-associated proteins, may also contribute. Nevertheless, substrate-trapping proteomics, Cullin-family screening, KLHL12 loss-of-function, and disruption of the GCLM-GSH axis collectively support GCLM as a predominant and functionally validated mediator in this context.

Mechanistically, the targeted degradation of GCLM represents a ‘double hit’ to the post-traumatic neural parenchyma, converging on uncontrolled oxidative stress and microglial-mediated neuroinflammation. Although enhancing GCLM transcription via Nrf2 activators，like Soloxolone derivatives [48–50], remains a popular therapeutic strategy, our findings indicate that such efforts may be counteracted by post-translational instability. We observed that hyper-active neddylation promotes rapid GCLM turnover, rendering transcriptional induction insufficient to maintain functional protein levels. This loss of GCLM leaves microglia vulnerable, unable to contain the surge in ROS. Crucially, pharmacological blockade of neddylation using MLN4924 stabilized GCLM and broke this pathological feed-forward loop. This mechanism was supported by both loss- and gain-of-function evidence: MLN4924 failed to suppress ROS accumulation and inflammatory activation in Gclm-deficient microglia, whereas cell-permeable GSH restored the anti-inflammatory phenotype; conversely, in vivo inhibition of GSH synthesis by BSO abolished MLN4924-mediated protection, which was reinstated by exogenous GSH supplementation. This implies that during the acute window of TBI, therapeutic efficacy hinges on preserving proteostasis rather than merely boosting gene transcription, as the latter cannot compensate for the rapid degradation of the antioxidant machinery.

From a translational standpoint, our data support the repurposing of MLN4924 (Pevonedistat) for acute TBI intervention, yet this strategy necessitates a cautious approach. While MLN4924 is a potent investigational agent with a defined pharmacokinetic profile, its broad mechanism of action poses inherent risks to fundamental cellular machinery. Notably, Jia et al. (2025) recently revealed that MLN4924-like agents can trigger the degradation of RNA Polymerase II and elicit DNA damage responses [51]. This concern is amplified by the critical role of CUL3 in maintaining genomic stability. As highlighted by Kloeber et al. (2025), cells rely on intricate sensors like the CUL3 adaptor KCTD10 to resolve co-directional transcription-replication conflicts [13]. Consequently, systemic disruption of cullin-dependent pathways could theoretically exacerbate replication stress, particularly in proliferating cell populations such as the reactive glia required for scar formation [52]. To circumvent these liabilities, future therapeutic strategies should prioritize spatiotemporally restricted regimens—such as short-term dosing or nanoparticle-mediated delivery—to confine MLN4924 activity to the lesion site, thereby maximizing neuroprotection while preserving global DNA replication and transcriptional fidelity.
