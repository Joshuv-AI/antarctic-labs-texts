Development of a theory-based provider-focused training to improve HPV vaccine recommendations for young adults
Authors: Heather N. Owens, Coralia Vázquez-Otero, Mariana Arevalo, Joyce Cui, Melody Chavez, Melinda Leigh Maconi, Carley Geiss, Kea Turner, Susan T. Vadaparampil, Veronica Barrios-Monroy, Alissa Pena, Junmin Whiting, Shannon M. Christy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the United States (US), only 47% of young adults (18–26 y old) had initiated the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine in 2022. Provider recommendation is the strongest predictor of HPV vaccination; however, limited studies have explored methods for training providers on making HPV vaccine recommendations to young adults. The purpose of this study was to obtain provider feedback on education materials for effective HPV vaccination recommendations to patients ages 18–26. Guided by the Theory of Planned Behavior and Health Belief Model, an HPV vaccine training was developed for primary care providers. Fifteen US primary care providers were recruited in 2022 for semi-structured interviews. Participants reviewed and provided feedback on the draft training. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Overall, feedback on the education materials was favorable. Participants noted learning new and useful information and appreciated the diversity of people throughout the training. General feedback included flexibility in the training format and timing due to busy schedules and providing incentives to encourage participation. There were mixed feelings toward participating in a role-play activity. Recommendations included using clear language and opportunities to make the training more persuasive. Future research is needed to refine the intervention based on provider suggestions and test the effectiveness of the intervention for improving provider recommendations for young adults.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV)-related cancers can be prevented through vaccination.1 The HPV vaccine is recommended for routine vaccination for adolescents ages 11–12 and can be given starting at age 9. Catch-up vaccination is recommended for those ages 15–26 and approved for use through age 45 through shared decision-making with their healthcare provider.1 In the United States (US), catch-up HPV vaccination rates are low among young adults (18–26 y old), with only 47.4% receiving at least one dose of the series in 2022.2 While many factors contribute to vaccination, provider recommendation is the strongest predictor of HPV vaccine initiation and completion.3,4 Unfortunately, provider HPV recommendation for young adults is suboptimal. Indeed, a previous study found that out of 223 young adults ages 18–26, only 53% had received a recommendation for the HPV vaccine; notably, young adults who received a recommendation were 35 times more likely to initiate the series.5

Training healthcare providers on how to effectively recommend the HPV vaccine has proven successful in increasing adolescent HPV vaccination rates. Prior provider interventions have utilized strategies to address provider misconceptions, improve patient-provider communication, and enhance consistency in the delivery of a strong provider recommendation. These efforts have demonstrated improvements in HPV vaccine uptake and completion via techniques such as communication skills training (i.e., the Announcement Approach Training), audit and feedback, and quality improvement initiatives.6–8 However, these prior provider training efforts have largely focused on providers caring for adolescent patients as opposed to young adults. While these approaches have been rigorously evaluated for adolescent HPV vaccination, there has been limited study of what training methods may be effective for training providers to address HPV vaccination among young adults.9,10 Two interventions evaluating provider-focused training for providers seeing adolescents and young adults reported that while there were small, non-significant increases among adolescents10 and increases among females 11–12 y old and males 13–17 y old,9 HPV vaccination did not improve among young adults after both interventions.9,10 Research noting differences between young adults and parents of adolescents found that messaging influences vaccine intentions differently between the two groups. Messaging that increased parental HPV vaccine confidence decreased vaccine confidence among young adults.11 Therefore, the goal of this current study was to develop a theory-informed training that considers barriers and facilitators providers may encounter when recommending the HPV vaccine for young adults. The study aimed to obtain provider feedback on the delivery, timing, and content of an HPV catch-up vaccination recommendation training designed for primary care providers who provide care to patients ages 18–26.

Materials and methods

Overview

This study was part of a larger study consisting of the following three components: (1) a quantitative survey with young and mid-age adults living in the US to examine factors associated with HPV vaccine uptake and intentions,12 (2) qualitative interviews with 15 young US adults to evaluate HPV vaccine education materials for 18–26-y-olds,13 and (3) qualitative interviews with US primary care healthcare providers (data presented herein).14

Development of educational materials

The research team developed draft training materials using information sources from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the American Cancer Society (ACS), among others.15–23 The slide presentation was designed with assistance from an instructional designer and included tables/figures with rates of HPV-related cancers and disparities, images depicting low- and high-risk HPV infections, and additional images and graphics to increase the visual appeal of the intervention. As the training was developed for US providers and the nonavalent vaccine is the only HPV vaccine currently administered in the US, the materials largely focused on the nonavalent vaccine. The materials did include a timeline of Food and Drug Administration approvals of the HPV vaccines, including earlier versions of the bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines. The initial content of the slide presentation included: (1) background information on HPV, HPV-related cancers, and the HPV vaccine and dosing schedule, and disparities in HPV infections and vaccination rates; (2) the reduction in HPV and HPV-related health concerns since age-eligible individuals began receiving the HPV vaccine; (3) safety of the HPV vaccine, potential side effects, and who should not get the vaccine (e.g., pregnant individuals should wait until they are no longer pregnant); (4) HPV vaccination for young adults and adults age 27–45; (5) screening for HPV-related cancers; (6) cost of the HPV vaccine; (7) clinic strategies to improve HPV vaccine rates through system-, provider-, and patient-level strategies; and (8) strategies for making an effective vaccination recommendation. Messages in each section were mapped to theoretical constructs from the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and Health Belief Model (HBM).24,25 The training was designed to be completed in 60 minutes. Potential delivery methods envisioned included self-guided online module or delivered by a clinician educator. The draft materials also included an opportunity for discussion and a role-playing activity to practice making strong HPV vaccine recommendations.

Participant recruitment and data collection

Healthcare providers (e.g., physicians, nurses, or physician assistants) across the US who deliver care to young adults were recruited for qualitative interviews using flyers distributed via e-mail (i.e., an existing contact list of clinicians who had had prior contact with the institution, contact list of public-facing e-mails from institutions/providers throughout the US), social media, and/or primary care organizations, clinics, and directly to providers at a local academic health system. The inclusion criteria included (1) being aged 21 y or older; (2) able to read, write, and understand English; (3) providing primary care, family medicine, and/or OB/GYN care to adults patients ages 18–64; (4) having access to the internet and a computer and/or a working phone; and (5) being willing to have the interview audio recorded. After eligibility screening, consent, and enrollment, participants were sent an email with the draft training materials and a brief demographic questionnaire (i.e., sociodemographic and clinical role characteristics, HPV vaccine practices). Of the 976 providers emailed, 22 replied and 15 were eligible and consented for the study.

The interview guide elicited participants’ feedback on the draft materials in areas such as content, layout, comprehension, and response efficacy. Initially, the qualitative team conducted ten interviews, then the research team made modifications to the materials (i.e., slide presentation) based on feedback and mapped new messages/content to the guiding behavioral theories. Subsequently, five additional interviews were conducted to receive further feedback. The interview guide remained the same for both rounds of interviews. Interviews were conducted until saturation was reached for the total sample (i.e., no new themes emerged), which was tracked through post-interview assessments and team meetings during data collection. This sample size is consistent with recommendations in the literature to ensure identification of all relevant themes.26–28 Interviews were conducted in 2022 via phone or Zoom by two female PhD-level qualitative research specialists (MC and MM) with graduate training and experience in qualitative research methods, who were unknown to participants. The first set of interviews ranged from 22 to 86 minutes (M = 45) and the second set of interviews ranged from 50 to 128 minutes (M = 68). Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed. Participants received a $50 gift card for their participation. Study procedures were reviewed by the Moffitt Cancer Center’s Scientific Review Committee (MCC#20853) and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of record (Advarra; Pro00047536) and determined to be exempt from IRB oversight. COREQ guidelines informed qualitative methodological choices, processes, and reporting.29

Data analysis

Sociodemographics obtained from the brief questionnaire were summarized to describe participant characteristics with frequencies, proportions, means, ranges, and standard deviations using Stata software, version 13. Qualitative analysis was completed after each round of interviews. Analyses were based on principles of applied thematic analysis.30 CFIR domains31 guided the codebook development, which included a combination of a priori codes and inductive codes identified throughout data collection, data review, and regularly held analysis meetings. Qualitative analysts trained in CFIR construct coding guidelines (MM and MC) independently coded transcripts using NVivo 12 Plus qualitative software. Following coding findings were organized by CFIR domains, themes, and subthemes. Qualitative analysts met regularly for debriefings throughout the analysis and writing process to ensure agreement, consistency, and rigor. Findings from both rounds of interviews were synthesized to create the final report presented in this study.

Overall, providers were receptive to the training materials, describing the training as “excellent,” “clear,” “useful,” and “effective.” One participant emphasized that they “wish everybody would take it.” Moreover, participants described the training as including clinically useful, comprehensive, and accurate information that was appropriate for the intended audience.

Educational appeal

The majority of participants expressed learning new and useful information about HPV and the vaccine from the training, which was unprompted feedback. For instance, this reaction occurred upon seeing a slide that discussed that oropharyngeal cancers are now the most common HPV-related cancer type in the US (surpassing cervical cancer incidence). In addition to learning new things, participants noted that materials provided useful refreshers and reminders, including the multiple HPV strains covered by the vaccine and vaccine administration/number of doses depending on the patient’s age at first dose.

Participants appreciated the diversity of people represented in the images throughout the training presentation slides, such as those of different races/ethnicities, ages, and genders. Moreover, some participants described how the slides challenged common assumptions related to age, relationship status, sexual orientation, gender, and religion.

Recommendations to improve materials

Participants had a variety of recommendations to improve materials, including modifying some of the language, adding information for clarity, and including new information to enhance topics covered. They also suggested ways to effectively appeal to provider audiences, enhance uptake and engagement, support greater clinical effectiveness, and strengthen visual appeal. Lastly, participants provided layout design suggestions.

While participants found the materials clear overall, they gave input on ways to clarify the information shared through changes to verbiage. Participants recommended modifying language to clarify, add context, and emphasize strategies for clinicians to effectively and sensitively engage with patients without making assumptions about risk behaviors. Moreover, participants suggested adding additional information to slides to further clarify content. For instance, including specific information about the lack of an HPV screening test for males and the observed disparities in cancer rates and access to screening. Participants described that adding additional information to some slides would be useful when considering clinical practices and assisting patients (e.g., working with pharmacies to streamline the administration process for those clinics that do not stock the HPV vaccine).

While information shared was largely well-received, some participants described opportunities to make the information more persuasive, easier to reference, and increase uptake and engagement with providers. Overall, the duration of the training seemed to be appropriate, although some participants stressed that “anything longer than that is gonna be a challenge” and “it’s always better if we could do a little shorter.” Further, some participants commented on the number of slides feeling overwhelming, potentially dissuading clinicians from reviewing all of them. Recommendations to reduce this potential barrier included dividing the training into two parts, as well as having a presenter share the information rather than relying on independent learning (review of the slides). Some participants also suggested providing incentives for completion.

Some participants suggested modifying the visuals to better reflect content, more accurately represent healthcare practices, and more effectively appeal to provider audiences. For example, there were mixed opinions about a slide showing two photos of genital warts. Two participants agreed that this image was useful and served as “a reminder if we haven’t seen genital warts in awhile.”

The training included a role-play activity at the conclusion. Participants had mixed feelings toward participating in this type of activity. Positive feedback included: “If I had seen this at a conference or someone was presenting this, then this would be good,” and “I think it’s always good to practice saying, so that salesmanship part of it is, I think, a good interactive action.” Others described potential barriers, such as colleagues being annoyed by this type of activity and watching the training alone, to participating, and suggested using a video clip to demonstrate effective and ineffective examples of communication.

Lastly, participants provided minor organization and flow suggestions, such as changes in slide order, editing for redundancy, combining slides, breaking up bullet points with too much information, removing white space, and bolding or underlining important points as needed.

Feedback on training format and delivery

Participants recommended flexibility in the clinical training format and timing, such as a recorded webinar accessible on-demand, to successfully reach healthcare providers with busy schedules. Additionally, some participants suggested providing information in multiple formats, such as videos, to reach people with different learning styles. When discussing strategies for clinician training engagement, many participants suggested continuing education (e.g., CME) credits and/or some external reward, such as a bonus, offering meals, or monetary incentives (or equivalents such as gift cards) to incentivize training attendance/participation. Lastly, participants, particularly those preferring in-person training, indicated that having tangible materials (e.g., one pager to hang up) as a reminder of what was learned and discussed during the training would be helpful.

Discussion

The study aimed to obtain provider feedback on the delivery, timing, and content of an HPV catch-up vaccination recommendation training designed for primary care providers. HPV vaccination among young adults is suboptimal and the consistency and quality of HPV vaccine provider recommendations for young adults varies.2,5 Thus, training and educational opportunities should be developed for providers to effectively recommend the HPV vaccine among young adults and improve vaccination among this at-risk age cohort. The majority of providers reported serving college students. When discussing the HPV vaccine with patients, providers reported using a variety of strategies; the most reported strategy was patient-focused handouts, followed by a printed checklist of talking points. While these strategies might include evidence-based information, they are lacking the communication strategies proven to be effective in increasing vaccination uptake. Research shows that recommendations, primarily strong and presumptive recommendations, have the greatest impact on HPV vaccination.32

To promote successful uptake of HPV vaccine training among providers, participants suggested strategies such as providing incentives for providers to participate (e.g., monetary incentives or continuing education credits) and flexibility in the delivery format (e.g., recorded webinar) and timing of training to accommodate busy schedules. Through a partnership with the ACS Vaccinate Adolescents against Cancers program, federally qualified health centers implemented a train-the-trainer program and provided Maintenance of Certification and CME credits for participating providers.33 Of those who claimed credits and completed the evaluation survey, 90% reported changes to their practice based on the training, and 81% reported improved communication skills.33 Additionally, HPV vaccine initiation and completion rates increased.33 Similar strategies could be used to incentivize providers’ participation in trainings for HPV vaccine recommendations for young adults. Long, scheduled trainings that must be done synchronously may prevent some providers from attending, despite their interest in the topic.

When asked to provide feedback on educational materials, providers noted specific things they liked and also made suggestions for improvement. First, the training provided new information and served as a refresher about HPV. The providers also appreciated the diversity of people represented in the materials. This representation was intentional, as it shows the different groups of individuals who should receive the HPV vaccine. It is important that providers are aware of the benefits of vaccination and recommend the vaccine to age-eligible individuals, regardless of race/ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, or other sociodemographic characteristics.

The potential implications of this study are multilevel. HPV vaccination is important for young adults due to the increased risk of exposure to HPV, and provider recommendations are most effective for vaccine uptake among this population.3 A recent study shows that only 45% of college providers routinely screen female patients for their HPV vaccine status.34 Most of the participants in the current study provide care to college/university students (73%), so the feedback received can potentially impact the vaccine recommendations students receive. As there are noted differences between young adults and parents of adolescents and the factors that influence vaccination,11 it is important that those providing care to young adults are adequately prepared to provide education on the vaccine, answer these questions, and recommend the vaccine. The training developed in the current study begins to address a gap in available training for providers focused on the young adult population. Overall, participants had positive feedback on the training, which could increase their willingness to complete the training. At the clinic level, internal policies and procedures can be created to promote the training among their providers, including providing incentives for completion and flexible training formats, as recommended by the study participants. Future research steps will need to include formal intervention testing of the educational materials to evaluate provider engagement and effectiveness in discussing the HPV vaccine with young adult patients. This will include evaluating the need to develop it in different languages for US providers and the need for booster trainings. In future research, the efficacy of such provider training initiatives plus student-focused education initiatives and campus campaigns could be tested.

Strengths and limitations

Potential limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Most of the participants were female and non-Hispanic White, which may not be representative of the population. Moreover, while there was diversity in the clinic roles of the participants, there could be nuances of the settings in which providers work that were not collected in the current study, including the socioeconomic background of their patients and how that potentially impacts of HPV vaccination. We did not directly ask the participants about their practice setting, which could provide important context for interpreting the findings of the study. Future research should examine the setting in which a provider works to determine if there are differences in the reception of the training. Another limitation of the study are potential biases in the study sample and responses. The participants may differ from others that did not elect to participate in the interview in their HPV vaccine attitudes and practices. Additionally, their feedback on the educational materials may be impacted by social desirability.

Nonetheless, to our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and gather formative provider feedback on theory-based training materials for primary care providers recommending the HPV vaccine to young adults. Further, this approach overcomes limitations of prior research, including the use of health behavior theory. The training materials that were presented to participants were developed using reliable sources (i.e., CDC and ACS) and an instructional designer. In a review of HPV vaccine web-based educational interventions for providers, none of the 21 included studies stated that the intervention was guided by a theory or model.35

Providers’ insights facilitated the improvement of training materials’ feasibility and acceptability to promote HPV vaccine recommendation to young adults to be utilized in a future intervention study. Providers overall were receptive to the training and offered suggestions for refining the training in the future, such as areas of content, visuals, and linguistics, and promoted ways to increase provider engagement. Findings will inform the development of an intervention to address the multilevel factors to HPV vaccine uptake among young adults, including provider-focused educational materials.
