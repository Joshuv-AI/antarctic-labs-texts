Validation of a Briefer Anxiety Screening Tool for Use in Pediatric Cardiology Clinics
Authors: Sidra Ahmad, Keneisha Sinclair-McBride, Erica H. Lee, Ioanna Barkas, Kimberlee Gauvreau, Jane W. Newburger, David R. DeMaso, Susan F. Saleeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety disorders are common in pediatric heart disease (PHD), including congenital, arrhythmic, and acquired heart disease. The standard screening tool, Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED), is too lengthy to complete in a busy pediatric cardiology clinic.ObjectivesThis study assessed the concurrent validity of a briefer tool, Pediatric-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pediatric Anxiety (PROMIS-Anx), relative to SCARED in children with PHD.MethodsChildren, aged 7.5 to <18 years, with PHD and their caregivers both completed PROMIS-Anx and SCARED between January 2020 and August 2022 during cardiology clinic visits. Association of PROMIS-Anx and SCARED scores were analyzed using Spearman correlation coefficient (rs), and a cut point was developed for PROMIS-Anx in children and their caregivers using Youden index.ResultsAmong 144 child/caregiver pairs, anxiety was present in 36.0% of children by SCARED. Scores on SCARED and PROMIS-Anx were higher in females, adolescents, and those with known behavioral health disorders, but were not associated with cardiac disease severity, race, or ethnicity. SCARED and PROMIS-Anx scores were highly correlated in children (rs = 0.76, P < 0.001), and slightly less so in caregivers (rs = 0.58, P < 0.001), both reaching statistical significance. A PROMIS-Anx score of >30 was identified as a threshold for significant anxiety symptoms based on SCARED.ConclusionsPROMIS-Anx scores correlate with SCARED across the range of scores, particularly when completed by the child. Routine screening for anxiety in cardiology clinic, with a brief tool such as PROMIS-Anx, can promote earlier identification and treatment of anxiety in children with PHD.

Children with pediatric heart disease (PHD), including congenital, arrhythmic, and acquired heart disease, experience high rates of anxiety, reported between 15%% to 33%, higher than children without heart disease in the United States (∼9%).1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Concurrent with anxiety, patients with PHD have increased rates of other psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions, including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, learning disabilities, and others, with rates of aforementioned conditions increasing into adulthood.2, 3, 4 Elevated anxiety rates occur in children with PHD across a range of disease complexity, and have been associated with worse health outcomes and increased resource utilization.5,7,8 As medical and procedural outcomes continue to improve, mental health and developmental concerns have become major factors impacting quality of life for patients with PHD and their families.9,10 The American Heart Association released a recent call to action for congenital heart programs to integrate psychological support into the care of all cardiac patients, ensuring earlier detection and management of co-occurring mental health disease across the lifespan. Cardiology clinics are the medical home for many patients with PHD and the ideal setting for initial screening of commonly associated mental health conditions.11,12

This study expands on our previous work, in which screening for anxiety in cardiology clinic was deemed feasible and found favorable by most caregivers.13 The high demand and fast pace of the cardiology clinic requires a brief and accessible screening tool for anxiety to effectively facilitate identification of patients with PHD in need of referral for further assessment and intervention. In the current study, we aimed to assess the concurrent validity of a concise anxiety measure from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) by comparison with the longer and more commonly used Screen for Child Anxiety Related Disorders (SCARED) in our PHD patients (Central Illustration).14, 15, 16 Although PROMIS anxiety measures have been validated in the general pediatric population, their performance specifically in children with PHD has not been characterized. We examined the correlation between SCARED and PROMIS scores measured for the same cohort of patients, and evaluated the performance of PROMIS as a screening tool for elevated anxiety symptoms necessitating further assessment.Central IllustrationConcurrent Validity of a Rapid Anxiety Screening Tool for Pediatric Cardiology ClinicsAnxiety is common among pediatric cardiology patients yet is underidentified partially due to time constraints and lengthy assessments. Concurrent Validity of a Rapid Anxiety Screening Tool for Pediatric Cardiology Clinics

Anxiety is common among pediatric cardiology patients yet is underidentified partially due to time constraints and lengthy assessments. The study evaluated the concurrent validity of PROMIS-Anx compared with SCARED. Findings support the use of a brief screening approach to potentially enable earlier anxiety identification and referral during routine cardiology care. PROMIS-Anx = Pediatric-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pediatric GenPop v3.0; SCARED = Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders.

Methods

Between January 2020 and August 2022, children and their caregivers presenting to an ambulatory pediatric cardiology for routine care were enrolled to independently complete both child and caregiver versions of the SCARED and PROMIS anxiety screening tools. Inclusion criteria for the children included: 1) ages 7.5 to <18 years at enrollment; 2) a history of congenital, arrhythmic, and/or acquired heart disease (collectively referred to as PHD); and 3) ≥3 prior cardiology clinic visits. Children who had undergone recent cardiac catheterization or surgery within 3 months of their clinic visit were excluded. Caregivers and their children were respectively consented and assented for participation and encouraged to individually complete a caregiver and child SCARED and PROMIS form. Both tools are designed for child completion as young as age 8, although a research assistant (RA) or caregiver provided assistance to younger children in need of support. The RA was trained in the use of the tools and tabulated results, which were then reviewed with the cardiology providers. Results were shared with families by either the cardiologist or the RA, at the discretion of the provider. The RA shared resources on mental health with all families approached, irrespective of enrollment or scores.

A comparison of the SCARED and PROMIS tools are shown in Table 1. PROMIS has 2 validated measures of anxiety in the general pediatric population; an 8-item paper survey (used in our prior study) and a computer adaptive test (Pediatric Bank GenPop v3.0 [PROMIS-Anx]).13,19,20 Previous experience using the shorter PROMIS tool was notable for a particular item (1 of 8) often left unanswered, impacting overall score. In review with clinical psychologists (K.S.M. and E.H.L.), the PROMIS-Anx tool with all questions included was considered more comparable to SCARED. The primary cardiac diagnosis was classified as mild, moderate, or severe through chart review and in accordance with the 2025 American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guidelines.21 Additional cardiac diseases, not included within the guidelines, were grouped as: mild, supraventricular tachycardia and Kawasaki disease (without coronary artery aneurysms); moderate, ventricular tachycardia, long QT syndrome, Marfan syndrome, unspecified connective tissue disease with moderate or severe aortic dilation, cardiac tumor requiring resection, myocarditis, and pacemaker; or severe, primary pulmonary hypertension and heart transplant (S.F.S.). The electronic medical record was queried for patient demographics, age at cardiac diagnosis, length of cardiac follow-up, number of lifetime cardiac surgeries and/or catheterizations, and the number of noncardiac subspecialists per patient.

Behavioral health diagnoses (BHDs), considered only if coded in the medical record, were categorized as: 1) anxiety disorder; 2) nonanxiety BHD; and 3) anxiety disorder plus, which indicated an anxiety disorder and at least 1 additional nonanxiety BHD. Anxiety disorders included generalized, separation, social, phobia, panic, and/or adjustment disorder with anxiety. Nonanxiety BHDs included mood disorders (eg, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and disruptive mood dysregulation disorder), as well as adjustment, conduct, obsessive-compulsive, eating, gender dysphoria, autism spectrum, attention-deficit/hyperactivity, developmental delay, learning disability, and stereotyped movement disorders. These diagnoses were medically confirmed via electronic medical record entry and billing codes by their primary and/or consulting hospital physicians. The institutional review board approved this study (IRB-P00031697).

Statistical analysis

Categorical demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized using frequencies and percentages, and continuous characteristics using medians and 25th and 75th percentiles (Q1, Q3). Study enrollment was interrupted due to the COVID-19 pandemic, and differences in those enrolled before and during the pandemic were compared using Fisher exact and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for categorical and continuous characteristics, respectively. SCARED and PROMIS-Anx scores were approximately normally distributed based on visual inspection of histograms and were summarized using means and SDs. Item responses were imputed as the median value for subjects missing <10% of the items on a questionnaire, and these imputations were used to recalculate the total scores. Complete case analyses were also performed, including only patients without any missing survey items. SCARED and PROMIS-Anx scores were compared among patient subgroups using the two-sample t-test or one-way analysis of variance, with separate analyses for child-reported and caregiver-reported scores; proportions of patients with SCARED scores ≥25 (indicative of anxiety) were compared using Fisher exact test. To verify that higher PROMIS-Anx scores were found in patients who had higher SCARED scores, associations between SCARED and PROMIS-Anx scores were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients (rs). We first considered a cut point for PROMIS-Anx based on maximization of Youden index for the receiver operator characteristic curve. We also considered the value on the child-reported score which identified the same proportion of patients as the established SCARED cut point of ≥25. Sensitivity, specificity, and percentages of false negative and false positive results were calculated for this PROMIS-Anx cut point, using SCARED ≥25 as the gold standard; these metrics are reported with 95% CIs. All analyses were performed in Stata Statistical Software (version 18; StataCorp, 2023).

Results

A total of 144 children and their caregivers participated in the study with demographics and clinical characteristics shown in Table 3. No significant differences were seen in demographics, clinical characteristics, known BHDs, or survey scores between patients enrolled before or during the pandemic. Most children had moderate or severe heart disease, with predominantly conotruncal lesions (n = 26), left-sided obstructive lesions (n = 23), and cardiomyopathy (n = 12) in the moderate category, and single ventricle heart disease (n = 23) and heart transplant (n = 6) in the severe category. More than 40% of patients had a known BHD, most commonly anxiety in 25.0% of all patients. Completion rates for the different survey tools are outlined in Table 4, with mothers completing the majority of caregiver questionnaires (67.6%). Receiver operating characteristic curve analyses determined a child PROMIS-Anx cut point of ≥25 with a sensitivity of 95.9% (86.0%, 99.5%), specificity of 63.6% (52.7%, 73.6%), and false negative rate of 4.1% (0.5%, 14.0%), although it yielded a false positive rate of 36.4% (26.4%, 47.3%). In addition, there were a disproportionately high number of positive screens (57.7%) when compared to SCARED (36.0%). A Child PROMIS-Anx score >30 identified 36.0% of patients as having elevated anxiety symptoms, the same proportion identified by Child SCARED ≥25. Performance of this Child PROMIS-Anx cut point, using Child SCARED ≥25 as the standard, yielded a sensitivity of 69.4% (54.6%, 81.7%), specificity of 81.8% (72.2%, 89.2%), false negative rate of 30.6% (18.3%, 45.4%), and false positive rate of 18.2% (10.8%, 27.8%). The same cut point was applied to Caregiver PROMIS-Anx, with 23.7% of caregivers reporting a PROMIS-Anx score >30 in their child. Performance of this Caregiver PROMIS-Anx cut point, using SCARED ≥25 as the standard, yielded a sensitivity of 75.0% (53.3%, 90.2%), specificity of 87.0% (79.4%, 92.5%), false negative rate of 25.0% (9.8%, 46.7%), and false positive rate of 13.0% (7.5%, 20.6%).

Discussion

The rising prevalence of mental health problems among children in the United States has highlighted anxiety disorders as a particular area of concern.1,22 Children with chronic medical conditions, such as heart disease, face heightened rates of anxiety.2,23,24 In our study, over one-third of children with PHD scored within the clinically significant anxiety range as measured by SCARED, with girls and adolescents disproportionately affected. Anxiety in youth is strongly correlated with anxiety and depressive disorders in adulthood, suicidality, and substance misuse.25 There is an urgent need for routine mental health screening and support in pediatric cardiology clinics, particularly for families relying on their subspecialists as their medical home.9,11, 12, 13,26 A simple and rapid anxiety screening tool, suitable for busy cardiology clinics, could enable timely identification of anxiety and spur earlier referral and interventions for a greater number of children with cardiac disease. Our study is the first to evaluate the concurrent validity of the PROMIS-Anx tool compared to SCARED, specifically in pediatric cardiology patients, and to determine a score that signifies anxiety symptoms in need for further mental health evaluation and support.

We observed a correlation between PROMIS-Anx and SCARED across the range of scores in PHD patients, particularly when the child self-reported symptoms. PROMIS-Anx was completed at a higher rate than SCARED for both children and caregivers, likely due to its shorter length with less associated survey fatigue. By RA observation, the PROMIS-Anx survey was completed in no more than 1 to 2 minutes compared to ∼10 minutes for SCARED. Our analysis determined a cutoff score of >30 on PROMIS-Anx in PHD patients to correlate with clinically significant anxiety on SCARED for both children and caregivers reporting on their children. Although the false negative rate is high, simply performing the screening in cardiology clinic opened the door for discussion of mental health and normalized referral for support based on the tool or patient/caregiver request.

Our findings emphasize the importance of having pediatric patients complete screening tools in clinic vs relying solely on caregiver reports. Caregivers generally reported fewer anxiety symptoms than their children. Discrepancies between informants is a common phenomenon when assessing child and adolescent behavioral health concerns, although parent-proxy–reported outcomes are more commonly used clinically.27,28 Children, particularly adolescents, may be less likely to share the extent of their anxiety concerns with their caregivers, illustrating the need for reliable anxiety self-reporting tools. We are currently piloting collecting patient-reported outcomes in cardiology clinic; the mental health and quality of life measures are completed by children and adolescents unless unable. From use of PROMIS-Anx in our pilot, we are also finding ∼33% of respondents screen positive with a threshold of >30, concordant with the current study and known anxiety rates in PHD patients.2, 3, 4, 5, 6

As noted previously, anxiety in childhood is associated with persistence of mental health conditions into adulthood.25 In patients with PHD, this persistence has important downstream consequences, as mental and physical health are closely intertwined. Large studies demonstrate that adults with congenital heart disease (CHD) and known mental health disorders experience longer lengths of stay than those without psychiatric diagnoses.29,30 Beyond health care utilization, adults with CHD and mental health disorders exhibit worse biologic markers of cardiovascular inflammation and heart failure.31 In the same study, even after adjustment for CHD complexity, major arrhythmias, and other factors, depression in adult patients with CHD was associated with a 3-fold increase in all-cause mortality. Routine anxiety screening in children with PHD presents a critical opportunity to intervene early and potentially alter the trajectory of cardiac health.

A nimble anxiety screening tool, validated in our pediatric cardiac patient population, brings us 1 step closer to an integrated behavioral health model as used by other subspecialty groups managing chronic childhood disease.26 For example, psychosocial care standards, including use of PROMIS instruments, have been implemented for pediatrics oncologists in the care of all children with cancer and their families.32, 33, 34 Cystic fibrosis guidelines recommend a dedicated mental health coordinator embedded within the child’s core care team to assist with annual screening for anxiety and depression beginning at age 12, plus screening caregivers when indicated.35 The time has arrived for pediatric cardiology subspecialty to embrace mental health screening as a routine part of cardiology care to improve the psychosocial and physical functioning of our children and families.34,36, 37, 38

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. This study was conducted with a convenience sample which may not represent the full distribution of cardiac diseases or the full range of anxiety severity in our typical cardiology clinic. Clinical data were collected through chart review and may underestimate BHD rates. In addition, BHDs were obtained from the medical record, including from the problem list, and may have been applied by nonbehavioral health clinicians. In this study, the PROMIS-Anx screening tool was compared to a standardly used screening tool, although not to physician interview, the true gold standard.

Conclusions

PROMIS-Anx is a brief anxiety questionnaire that correlates with SCARED in a population of children with diverse cardiac disease. Its use as a screening tool in ambulatory pediatric cardiology may increase detection of anxiety and potentially allow for earlier referral and intervention. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the findings in our study.
