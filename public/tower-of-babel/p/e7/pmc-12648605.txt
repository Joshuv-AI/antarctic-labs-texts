Venous thromboembolism in obese hormonal contraceptive users: a large national database study
Authors: Divyaswathi Citla-Sridhar, Madhvi Rajpurkar, Lakshmi Srivaths, Bethany Samuelson Bannow, Rachel P. Rosovsky, Nancy Sokkary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) among obese and nonobese females aged 10 to 44 years prescribed hormonal therapy (HT) and compare VTE rates between different HT types in a large national database.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using TriNETX data, which includes electronic health records and pharmaceutical claims from 81 million patients across 56 US tertiary care hospitals. Females prescribed HT between 2000 and 2022 were stratified by body mass index. VTE incidence was identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth Revision, codes and anticoagulant prescriptions. Propensity score matching controlled for confounders.ResultsOf 4,020,467 females, 34% (1,370,481) were obese. VTE incidence was higher in obese HT users (0.99%) than nonobese users (0.51%; P < .01). Obese users of levonorgestrel + ethinyl estradiol (EE) had a VTE rate of 0.55% vs 0.33% in nonobese users (P = .01, relative risk [RR] = 1.63). Similarly, obese users of norethindrone + EE (0.44% vs 0.24%; P < .01; RR = 1.8) and norgestimate + EE (0.44% vs 0.22%; P < .01; RR = 1.97) had higher VTE rates. No significant differences were found for progesterone-only HT users.ConclusionIn this large study comparing rates of VTE among obese and nonobese females on HT, a significantly higher risk of VTE was observed among obese females using certain HTs, particularly EE with levonorgestrel, norethindrone, and norgestimate. These results highlight the importance of considering obesity as a key risk factor when prescribing HT, particularly for females with higher body mass index.

Introduction

Obesity and hormonal therapy (HT) are independent risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE), with an odds ratio of 1.08 and 4.03, respectively [1]. Obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2, is a rising phenomenon worldwide as the rate has more than doubled since 1990 [2]. Nearly 40% of adults in the United States are obese, and specifically, the prevalence among females aged 20 to 39 years is 36.5% [3]. Moreover, prevalence of childhood obesity in the United States is among the highest in the world and continuing to increase [3,4].

Obesity is thought to increase VTE risk via several mechanisms including inflammation, impairment of fibrinolytic activity, increased production of clotting factors, and venous stasis [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Previous studies have found that the VTE risk in obese individuals is 2.5 times higher than that of their nonobese peers, and the impact of obesity on risk was greater among females under age 40 years [9].

In the United States, nearly all females might use a form of hormonal contraception at some point in their life. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported that, between 2017 and 2019, 65.3% of US females aged 15 to 49 years were using contraception [10,11]. Among hormonal contraceptive users, the combined hormonal contraceptives (CHCs) remain the most commonly used [10]. The overall VTE risk with CHC is approximately 2-fold higher than an individual’s baseline risk, with a further increase depending on the type of progesterone, dose of estrogen, and route of administration. Estrogen also leads to an increase in thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation by increasing various coagulation proteins and decreasing the levels of anticoagulant proteins [12].

To date some case-control and cohort studies have demonstrated an increased VTE risk among obese contraceptive users to a varying degree [13,14]. However, these data do not include adolescents and were derived from populations with relatively low rates of obesity. Additionally, most data come from countries that have different formulations of hormonal contraception not used in the United States.

This study aimed to assess and compare the VTE risk among obese females, age 10 to 44 years, who were prescribed HT, with nonobese HT users, using a national electronic-medical record database. Using a large national database allowed the inclusion of females from diverse ethnic, economic, and geographic background.

Methods

Study database

This retrospective cohort study used population-level data from TriNETX, LLC, a comprehensive administrative database aggregating longitudinal inpatient and outpatient data from 56 US tertiary care hospitals, encompassing over 81 million unique patients. The database includes standardized electronic health records and pharmaceutical claims data from 2000 to 2022, enabling a detailed analysis of health care outcomes across a broad, diverse population. Due to the de-identified nature of the dataset, institutional review board approval was not required.

Patient selection

The study population comprised those identified as female in the medical record aged 10 to 44 years with a new prescription for any HT recorded between January 1, 2000, and December 30, 2022. Prescriptions were identified by specific pharmaceutical claims and procedural codes. Recorded BMI data was used to categorize individuals as obese or nonobese according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines. We defined obesity status based on age-specific criteria. For individuals older than 18 years, nonobese status was defined as a BMI of >18.5 and <30 kg/m2, while obesity was defined as BMI of ≥30 kg/m2. For individuals aged 18 years or younger, nonobese status was defined as a BMI between the 5th and <95th percentile for age and sex, and obesity was defined as a BMI at or above the 95th percentile.

HT users were defined as individuals who used either progesterone only or CHCs. Progesterone-only methods included norethindrone (NTE), drospirenone, etnogestrel subdermal implant, levonorgestrel intrauterine devices, and medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA)intramuscular (IM) injections. CHCs were categorized into oral, topical (transdermal patch), and vaginal forms (contraceptive ring). Oral CHCs included combinations such as levonorgestrel + ethinyl estradiol (EE), drospirenone + EE, desogestrel + EE, ethynodiol + EE, NTE + EE, norgestrel + EE, norgestimate + EE, and estetrol + drospirenone. Topical CHCs included levonorgestrel + EE and norelgestromin + EE, while vaginal forms included segesterone + EE and etonogestrel + EE. Nonusers were individuals who were not prescribed any HT during the study period. Only HT methods with a specified route of administration were included in the main analyses, resulting in a more specific, however smaller, cohort. This study exclusively examined HT formulations containing EE in combination with a progestin or progestin-only preparations. To assure the study population reflected individuals using hormone therapy primarily for contraception or menstrual symptom control, menopausal hormone therapies including formulations with conjugated estradiol were excluded to avoid confounding as conjugated estrogens and postmenopausal individuals have different thrombotic risk profiles.

Individuals with a history of venous thromboembolism (VTE) or documented pregnancy were excluded from the analysis. To minimize misclassification of exposure, individuals who had prescriptions for more than 1 type of HT during the one-year observation period were excluded. This approach allowed us to attribute outcomes to a single contraceptive method within the defined timeframe.

Complete data on all contraceptive routes, including unspecified routes, are presented in the Supplementary Tables 1–4. However, a substantial portion of hormone contraceptive users did not have a specified route.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the incidence of VTE among HT users, stratified by BMI classification. VTE events were defined by the presence of one or more International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-Ninth or ICD-Tenth Revision codes for VTE diagnosis, along with a single prescription for an anticoagulant within 1 year of the hormonal prescription.

Propensity score (PS) matching was applied to control for age and race to reduce confounding, enabling a more accurate comparison between obese and nonobese HT users. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, and comparisons between groups were made using chi-squared or Fisher exact test. Relative risks (RRs) were calculated to estimate VTE risk, with statistical significance defined as a P value of <.05.

To protect patient privacy and per TriNETX policy, any outcome with fewer than 10 patients was reported as not available. Outcomes where the value was <10 were excluded from statistical analysis.

Results

Demographics

The initial database search identified 4,020,467 females aged 10 to 44 years with available BMI data. Of these, 34% (1,370,480) were obese and 66% (2,649,987) were nonobese. Among obese users, the majority (97.5%) were between 22 and 44 years old, with only 2.45% in the 10 to 21 age group. Similarly, most nonobese users (95.4%) were aged 22 to 44 years.

In terms of racial distribution, White individuals comprised the largest proportion in both groups (49.3% of obese users and 50.51% of nonobese users), followed by Black individuals (23.05% and 24.56%, respectively). Asian and American Indian representation was relatively low, with Asians accounting for 2.2% of obese users and 2.41% of nonobese users, while American Indians made up 0.46% and 0.43%, respectively. The other category constituted 25% of obese users and 22.09% of nonobese users. Additionally, Hispanic/Latino individuals represented 12.8% of obese hormone users and 14% of nonobese users.

VTE incidence

The incidence of VTE among obese HT users was 0.99% and, in nonobese users, was 0.51%, with a statistically significant difference (P < .0001). Discussion

This study provides important insights into the relationship between obesity and the risk of VTE in females using HT. Our findings indicate that obese females (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) who are prescribed HT have a significantly higher risk of VTE than their nonobese counterparts. Specifically, the VTE rates were notably higher in obese users of levonorgestrel + EE, NTE + EE, and norgestimate + EE oral contraceptives. The remainder CHC combinations had small numbers and could not be compared, likely because they are not as commonly used. These results highlight the increased vulnerability of obese females to VTE when using certain types of HTs and underscore the need for careful consideration of VTE risk when prescribing hormones.

Both obesity and hormonal contraception have independently been shown to double an individual’s baseline risk of VTE [9,12]. Several studies have consistently demonstrated a strong association between increasing BMI and higher risk of deep venous thrombosis. This is particularly evident in overweight (BMI = 25-29.9), moderately obese (BMI = 30-34.9), and severely obese (BMI = 35-39.9) individuals when compared with those with normal weight [[15], [16], [17]].

In a large Mendelian randomization study conducted by Klovaite et al. [15], which analyzed data from 87,574 individuals, a strong observational association between increasing BMI and a higher risk of deep venous thrombosis was observed, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.3 for overweight individuals to 3.4 for severely obese individuals. The study also revealed that obese users of HT had a 4-fold increased risk (95% CI, 1.1-15.0) of VTE compared with normal-weight HT users. This is consistent with the findings from our study, which showed that obese users of certain HTs, such as levonorgestrel + EE, NTE + EE, and norgestimate + EE, had a significantly higher VTE risk than their nonobese counterparts.

The study by Sidney et al. [18] further supports our findings, showing a strong association between HT use and increased VTE risk, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 4.07 (95% CI, 2.77-6.00). In this study, the risk was significantly higher among obese individuals, and interaction testing showed a P value of .01, indicating a strong interaction between obesity and HT use in increasing VTE risk.

Suchon et al. [19], as part of the PILl Genetic RIsk Monitoring (PILGRIM) study, also identified obesity as a significant risk factor for VTE among females using HT. Their study of 968 females with a first VTE and 874 controls found that females with a BMI greater than 35 kg/m2 had an OR of 3.46 (95% CI, 1.81-7.03) for developing VTE than females with a lower BMI. This is in line with our results, which found that the risk of VTE was notably higher in obese females using HT.

Nightingale et al. [20], in a pooled analysis using combined data from the GPRD and MediPlus databases, demonstrated that HT users with a BMI of 25 to 29.9 kg/m2 had a 1.4-fold increased risk of VTE (OR, 1.4; 95% CI, 1.0-2.0), those with a BMI of 30 to 34.9 kg/m2 had a 1.8-fold increased risk (OR, 1.8; 95% CI, 1.1-2.9), and those with a BMI of ≥35 kg/m2 had a 3.1-fold increased risk (OR, 3.1; 95% CI, 1.6-5.8), compared with HT users with a BMI of 20 to 24.9 kg/m2. These findings are consistent with the increased VTE risk observed in our study, particularly for females with a BMI of ≥30 kg/m2 who used HTs, and further emphasize the heightened risk associated with obesity in this population.

It is important to note that not all prior analyses accounted for potential confounders using PS matching. In contrast, our study used PS to minimize confounding and ensure a more robust comparison of VTE risk among obese and nonobese HT users. Our study is also novel in that it included adolescents and a racially diverse patient population, which was not represented to the same extent in Nightingale et al. and other prior studies [19,20]. Unlike prior studies that examined regionally limited populations—such as a cohort exclusively composed of White Danish individuals [15], patients from Kaiser Permanente in Northern and Southern California [18], and individuals in England [20]—this study used a large, nationally representative database in the United States. Our study not only gives strength to the current literature findings but also, and importantly, enhances the existing literature by including PS matching and a wider scope of HT users.

Progesterone-only methods

In contrast to the findings for HTs, progesterone-only contraceptive methods generally carry a lower risk of VTE. However, concerns remain regarding certain progesterone-based formulations, such as IM MPA and high-dose NTE acetate [21,22]. Our study did show a trend toward a higher risk of VTE among obese users of 5-mg NTE, although this trend did not reach statistical significance. Notably, a similar trend was not observed with IM MPA. This lack of significant association with IM MPA may be attributed to the lower incidence of VTE in this group or preferences in patients with obesity to avoid IM MPA given risk of weight gain, which could have resulted in insufficient statistical power.

Importantly, progesterone-only contraceptive methods evaluated in this study did not show a significant effect on the risk of VTE based on BMI. This finding suggests that progesterone-only methods may offer a safer alternative for obese females, as they do not appear to carry the same elevated VTE risk as HTs, especially among females with higher BMI. This result aligns with current clinical guidelines that indicate progesterone-only contraceptives pose fewer thromboembolic risks than combined methods [23].

However, our data showed numerically higher VTE rates in both obese and nonobese users among 5-mg NTE users than some estrogen-containing combined methods. Partial in vivo conversion of high-dose NTE to EE may contribute to an increased thrombotic risk, as demonstrated in prior studies dose and type of progesterone may affect risk of thrombosis. However, it is also possible that the patient populations prescribed high-dose NTE acetate have underlying comorbidities that increase their baseline VTE risk and preventing use of estrogen, which we were unable to fully adjust for in this dataset.

Clinical implications

The 2024 US Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use classifies obesity as a category 2 condition for CHCs, meaning that the benefits generally outweigh the risks, although clinical judgment is necessary. For progestin-only pills, obesity is categorized as category 1, indicating no restrictions for use [23]. Our findings support these guidelines, suggesting that while obesity does increase the risk of VTE among CHC users, progesterone-only contraceptives may provide a safer option for obese females, particularly in those with higher BMI.

Given the elevated VTE risk associated with HT use in obese females, clinicians should carefully evaluate individual risk factors such as BMI, age, smoking history, and genetic predispositions (eg, factor V Leiden mutation or a strong family history) before prescribing HT. Additionally, patients should be informed about the potential risks and benefits of various contraceptive methods, including progesterone-only options and be involved in shared decision-making to ensure the safest choice for their reproductive health.

Pregnancy implications

While the increased risk of VTE associated with HT use in obese females is notable, the risks of unintended pregnancy must also be carefully considered. Pregnancy in itself elevates the risk of VTE by 4 to 5 times and remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality not only in the United States but also worldwide [24,25]. Additionally, studies indicate that obese individuals face an even greater risk of VTE during pregnancy [26]. As such, clinicians should offer individualized contraceptive counseling, weighing the heightened VTE risk with HTs against the potential risks of unintended pregnancy and alternative contraceptive options.

Public health implications

This study adds to the growing body of literature examining the risk of VTE among obese contraceptive users, particularly within the US population, which has higher obesity rates than those of other developed nations. By including adolescent females (age 10 years and older) in the analysis, this study provides valuable information about VTE risk in younger obese contraceptive users—a group that has been underrepresented in previous research. Moreover, the use of a large national database enabled the inclusion of a broad and diverse population, improving generalizability and accuracy. This approach was particularly advantageous as database studies allow for the capture of large numbers of underrepresented populations, making it feasible to assess VTE risk across different demographic groups in a real-world setting.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, the reliance on administrative and claims data introduces potential biases due to incomplete or inaccurately recorded clinical information. For instance, BMI values, which were used to classify individuals as obese or nonobese, may be underreported or inaccurately recorded. The observational nature of the data limits the ability to establish causality, and there is the potential for improper coding, which could lead to overdocumentation of thromboembolic events. To mitigate this, only VTE codes linked to an anticoagulant prescription were included. Additionally, a significant proportion of hormonal prescriptions did not specify the route of administration, which led to the exclusion of these records from the analyses, potentially reducing the cohort size. Another limitation of using electronic health record data is that prescription records do not necessarily reflect actual adherence to HT. Discontinuation due to side effects or patient preference may alter the real-world risk of VTE, a factor that future prospective studies should consider. We could also not further stratify obesity categories in this study, as doing so resulted in smaller sample sizes that limited the ability to make meaningful statistical comparison. BMI values may be subject to misclassification due to potential inaccuracies in height and weight measurements or a temporal disconnect between BMI recording and the date of hormone therapy prescription. We did not exclude patients who underwent surgical procedures during the study period, as capturing all relevant surgical events would have required inclusion of numerous ICD procedure codes across multiple specialties. This was beyond the scope of our study and may have introduced residual confounding, as surgery is an independent risk factor for VTE. We were also unable to assess discontinuation of HT, as the dataset does not consistently capture prescription refills or patient adherence. This may have introduced exposure misclassification affecting the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, the use of a large, diverse database such as TriNETX is a significant strength as this represents various ethnicities, economic backgrounds, and geographic locations across the United States, enhancing the generalizability of the findings not observed in previous studies. In addition, this study included patients as young as 10 years, data on adolescents has not been included in other studies. Furthermore, the longitudinal nature of the database, spanning over 2 decades, allowed for an in-depth analysis of hormonal contraceptive use and VTE events over time. PS matching was also used to control for confounding variables such as age and race, which strengthens the validity of our findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the higher risk of VTE among obese vs non obese females using HT, particularly with oral contraceptives containing levonorgestrel + EE, NTE + EE, and norgestimate + EE. While progesterone-only contraceptives were not associated with a significant increase in VTE risk, they may offer a safer alternative for obese females. These findings emphasize the need for personalized contraceptive counseling and to include BMI as a significant risk factor to ensure the safest options for females at risk for thromboembolic events.
