Resilience as a mediator of the relationship between perceived social support and public health emergency-related post-traumatic growth among medical staff
Authors: Wenning Fu, Ruiting Cai, Zongju Chen, Keran Qiu, Yi He, Jing Wang, Li Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs central participants in Public Health Emergency (PHE), medical staffs may face positive changes, such as post-traumatic growth, while being traumatised. However, previous research remains deficient in factors influencing Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG). The aim of this paper is to examine how resilience mediates the relationship between perceived social support and PTG among medical staff.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted from March to June 2023 among 730 healthcare workers at two tertiary-grade hospitals in China, utilizing random sampling. We employed descriptive statistics, univariate analyses, and structural equation modeling (SEM) to explore the mediating role of psychological resilience among these healthcare professionals.ResultsThe mean PTG score among medical staff was 60.48 (SD = 24.49). The direct impact of perceived social support on PTG was significant (0.218, 95% CI [0.083, 0.361], P < 0.001), constituting 49.98% of the total effect. Moreover, the indirect impact of perceived social support on PTG via resilience was also significant (0.218, 95% CI [0.119, 0.320], P < 0.001), accounting for 50.02% of the total effect.ConclusionThese findings underscore the importance of enhancing perceived social support and resilience in fostering post-traumatic growth among healthcare workers facing public-health emergencies. It suggests that interventions should focus on increasing perceived social support and resilience to foster positive psychological changes and improve the quality of healthcare services.

Introduction

Public Health Emergency (PHE) is a formal proclamation made by the World Health Organization (WHO) in response to an exceptional event. It refers to an exceptional incident recognized as posing a public health threat to other nations due to the global transmission of disease and possibly necessitating a coordinated international reaction [1]. During Public Health Emergencies, factors such as physical fatigue caused by prolonged consecutive shifts [2], frequent emotional distress due to intermittent shortages of critical personal protective equipment and patient deaths [3], and disruption of normal social support systems resulting from prolonged separation from family members due to isolation protocols all exacerbate the psychological burden on frontline teams [4, 5]. This can potentially adversely affect their health and functioning [6].

Although PHE emergency statuses are gradually phased out through collective human efforts [7], the psychological challenges healthcare workers face while treating patients remain unresolved and inadequately addressed [8]. However, some healthcare professionals may experience positive psychological transformations during PHEs, such as post-traumatic growth (PTG) [9, 10]. Post-traumatic growth refers to the positive psychological changes experienced by individuals after encountering traumatic events, including alterations in self-perception, interpersonal relationships, and life philosophy [11]. Investigating these positive changes in PTG can promote reflection and comprehension of their experiences, thereby mitigating the negative impacts of trauma [12].

The factors that influence the development of PTG are complex and multifaceted, one of the most studied factors is perceived social support. Perceived social support refers to individuals’ subjective perception of being cared for, supported, and valued by others [13]. It has been discovered that individuals benefit from perceived social support, including that from family, colleagues, and the organization [14, 15]. Perceived social support can provide substantial social interactions that could directly lead to positive changes, such as expressing emotions and giving feedback. Previous research had found significant positive correlation between perceived social support and PTG [16–18], and a favorable indirect influence on PTG was found in one study [19], however the type of trauma, culture, age, and sample size may affect this association [20]. Perceived social support not only directly promotes post-traumatic growth [21], but also exerts an indirect effect [22] through other factors such as resilience, coping styles, and self-efficacy. Therefore, it is of great significance to analyze the relationship between perceived social support and post-traumatic growth in depth.

Another factor that may play a role in PTG is resilience, it is an adaptation in the face of adversity [23], it can be defined as the preservation of mental health or rapid recovery following periods of exposure to stressors [24], which may be brought on by potentially traumatic events, difficult circumstances, a crucial life stage, or physical disease [25]. Resilience was found to be a protective factor that engaged in the PTG of severe illness [26], and have closely related to PTG [27]. Resilience can enable healthcare workers to cope effectively with trauma-related stressors, which enables people to thrive and gain experience in the face of hardship [28].

Certain investigations have revealed that perceived social support could intertwine with additional elements, like resilience, to impact PTG. Wu’s research [29] highlighted that resilience acted as a mediator in the connection between perceived social support and PTG among primary caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia. This implies that the constructive impact of perceived social support on PTG was more pronounced for individuals with lower resilience levels compared to those with higher resilience levels. Hou’s study [30] found that resilience mediates the impact of perceived social support on healthcare workers’ mental health to some extent, and is moderated by age. Given that PTG is one of the positive changes in mental health, we wanted to verify whether psychological resilience also mediates the effect of perceived social support on PTG.

This study is based on the social support buffering model, which proposes that perceived social support can buffer the adverse effects of stress and foster positive psychological outcomes [31]. Furthermore, the Post-traumatic Growth Theory suggests that individuals may undergo positive changes following traumatic events, through cognitive processing and the utilization of resources [32]. Combining these two theories, we propose that: H1: Perceived social support has a positive direct effect on PTG among medical staff. H2: Perceived social support has a positive indirect effect on PTG through resilience among medical staff.

Our study examines the impact of perceived social support and psychological resilience on PTG among healthcare workers. It underscores the significance of perceived social support and psychological resilience in fostering PTG and calls on policymakers to establish supportive environments and provide resources for healthcare workers.

Methods

Design and participants

The study recruited 730 healthcare workers from two general hospitals in Hubei Province using a random sampling method in 2023. The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software [33]. For a mediation analysis with three variables (perceived social support, resilience, PTG), the effect size (f²) was set to 0.15 (medium effect), α = 0.05, and power (1-β) = 0.95. The calculated minimum sample size was 680. Considering a 10% invalid questionnaire rate, we distributed 877 questionnaires, and the final valid sample size (730) met the requirement.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age over 18 years old; (2) obtained a physician or nurse qualification certificate; (3) voluntarily participated in this study. The exclusion criteria were (1) psychological problems, which refers to having a clinical diagnosis of mental disorders (e.g., anxiety disorder, depression) confirmed by a psychiatrist, or scoring above the clinical cutoff on the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) during the preliminary screening; (2) uncooperative, which refers to participants who refused to complete the questionnaire, provided incomplete responses (missing more than 10% of items), or were deemed to have random responses (e.g., all items rated the same score). We conducted an online survey using a web-based platform (“SurveyStar”, provided by Changsha Ranxing Science and Technology, China) that ensured the anonymity and confidentiality of the respondents. Before taking the survey, the participants provided their electronic informed consent by selecting “yes” from two options on the consent page. The survey system also implemented logic checks to verify the quality and validity of the responses. The data from the completed questionnaires were automatically entered into a data file and double-checked by two independent researchers. We prevented multiple responses from the same IP address by restricting one response per question per device.

Measurements

General demographic information

We designed an online questionnaire based on the research objectives and collected the demographic data of the respondents, including age, gender, marital status, length of employment, education level, and occupation type.

Chinese post-traumatic growth inventory (C-PTGI)

The study employed the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) developed by Tedeschi and Calhoun [34], a scale designed to gauge the extent of PTG in individuals who have undergone significant crises. The Chinese iteration of PTGI was introduced by Wang, demonstrating a total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.874 [35]. The Chinese version, C-PTGI, operates on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (no change) to 5 (significant change). The scale includes 21 items, covering five dimensions of positive psychological changes or growth following trauma: life perception, personal power, new possibilities, relationships with others, and self-transformation. Elevated scores correspond to heightened levels of post-traumatic growth. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.983, indicating an excellent interpretation.

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Perceived social support (MSPSS)

The study used the Chinese version of the 12-item Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Perceived social support (MSPSS) [36], consisting of three dimensions from family, friends, and others, to measure perceived level of perceived social support of participants. The MSPSS is a Likert 7-point scale, ranging from 0 (Strongly Disagree) to 6 (Strong Agree), with higher mean scores on the dimensions indicating a higher level of support [37]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in this study was 0.986, interpreting excellent.

The 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10)

The study used the Chinese version of the CD-RISC-10, translated by Wang [38] in 2010, to measure psychological resilience during stress. The Chinese version, which encompasses 10 items, exhibits good psychometric properties [39], Employing a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always), it encompasses three dimensions: tenacity, strength, and optimism, wherein higher scores signify heightened psychological resilience [38]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was even higher, at 0.983, indicating an excellent interpretation.

Ethical statement

The research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines [40]. Prior to completing the questionnaire, participants provided informed consent by acknowledging and electronically signing the consent form. The study maintained data integrity and confidentiality, allotting each participant a numerical identifier for absolute anonymity. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Wuhan, China.

Data analysis

We used SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 26.0 for data analysis. Out of 877 distributed questionnaires, we excluded the incomplete and illogical ones and retained 730 valid questionnaires, with an effective rate of 83.2%. Missing values were handled using the multiple imputation method, which is a robust method for dealing with small amounts of missing data [41]. We performed descriptive statistics and correlation analysis to describe the sample and examine the relationships among the variables. We employed structural equation models to examine the mediating influence of psychological resilience on the relationship between perceived social support and PTG. Model fit indices were not reported because the mediation model was saturated (just-identified), rendering global fit statistics meaningless. Besides, we utilized a two-way bootstrapping approach, generating 5,000 bootstrapped samples at a 95% confidence interval (95% CI) to assess the significance of the standardized coefficient (β). Significance of mediation or direct effect was considered when the 95% CI for β did not encompass zero (P < 0.05). The predictor was perceived social support, the mediator was resilience, and PTG served as the outcome variable.

Discussion

This study constructed a mediating model to explore the relationship between perceived social support, resilience, and public health emergency-related PTG among medical staff. We selected medical staff from Hubei Province, China’s PHE epicenter, to obtain more representative results. Our results showed that perceived social support not only directly promoted PTG but also indirectly influenced PTG through resilience, with resilience playing a partial mediating role. These findings confirm our initial research hypothesis and provide new insights into the psychological mechanism of PTG among medical staff in public health emergencies.

In our study, the PTG score of medical staff was 60.48 ± 24.49, which was lower than the score of frontline nurses in a previous study (M = 70.53) [42]. As the PHE persisted, the positive emotions of frontline medical staff, such as a sense of achievement from saving patients and pride in professional dedication, were increasingly diminished due to prolonged fatigue and repeated stress. This resulted in a significant decline in their post-traumatic growth (PTG) levels during the normalization stage of pandemic prevention and control [43]. We found that younger (M = 65.39) and less experienced medical staff (M = 65.35) tended to have higher PTG scores, which was inconsistent with previous studies [43, 44]. A possible reason is that younger medical staff were more willing to accept life changes and could get help and support from more sources (such as different types of online platforms). Moreover, less experienced and non-clinical medical staff might have lower risk exposure during PHE [45], resulting in less psychological stress than clinical workers, and thus could recover and grow faster from the trauma.

By investigating correlations and mediation effects, we have substantiated the substantial impact of perceived social support on PTG and the mediating function of resilience within this connection. Our results indicated a favorable correlation between perceived social support and PTG among medical staff. This is consistent with previous research [19] that highlights the importance of perceived social support in promoting positive psychological outcomes in individuals who have experienced traumatic events. Perceived social support from colleagues, family, and friends in healthcare settings mitigates the stress experienced by medical staff during public health emergencies [44]. As the most fundamental form of social support, family support provides healthcare professionals with emotional security, reduces anxiety caused by emergency work, and further enhances their psychological resilience [46]. Other social supports, such as colleagues and organizations, strengthen healthcare workers’ professional identity and problem-solving coping abilities, offering practical coping strategies and work assistance during emergencies [47]. By providing emotional validation and coping resources, it plays a beneficial role in coping with stressors, which may facilitate the process of PTG [48]. These results validate the role of perceived social support in the social support buffering model [31] in promoting positive psychological outcomes, while emphasizing that creating a supportive work environment for healthcare workers can contribute to improving their well-being and enhancing the quality of medical services.

Moreover, our study established resilience as a substantial mediator in the association between perceived social support and PTG. Previous studies have demonstrated that resilience serves as a protective factor for healthcare workers’ mental health [49, 50], playing a pivotal role in their swift recovery from PHEs [51]. This finding refines the theoretical connotation of the Post-traumatic Growth Theory [32], revealing the specific psychological mechanism by which social support resources partially act on PTG through individual resilience in the medical staff group [29]. Resilience serves as a critical psychological resource for healthcare workers to process traumatic events cognitively and achieve post-traumatic growth [46, 52]. Perceived social support can enhance this resource, thereby indirectly promoting post-traumatic growth. It is emphasized that the arrangement of psychological counseling service should be considered when building a work support environment for medical staff.

In the context of partial mediation, other variables such as self-disclosure may contribute to the remaining direct effect of perceived social support on PTG. For instance, medical staff who are more inclined to share their emotional experiences with others may more effectively utilize social support to directly promote PTG [53], beyond the mediating role of resilience [54, 55]. Furthermore, research indicates that there is a bidirectional and mutually reinforcing relationship between PTG and intentional behavior [56, 57]. Differences in coping styles further complicate these relationships: positive coping styles enhance the positive correlation between psychological resilience and PTG by encouraging proactive stress management [58]. However, Li et al.‘s [59] study indicates that PTG can positively predict resilience development, while resilience does not predict PTG development, with post-traumatic growth mediating the relationship between nurses’ resilience development. Since these variables were not included, our model may underestimate the complexity of the pathway. Future research could explore a multi-mediation model that incorporates self-disclosure, purposeful rumination, and coping styles to comprehensively analyze this mechanism.

Our research demonstrates that healthcare professionals can actively utilize social support resources while enhancing their personal psychological resilience. This enables them to more effectively cope with the inherent challenges and pressures of frontline work and promotes personal transformation and development [60].Based on this finding, we recommend establishing a multidimensional and sustainable support system to enhance the overall resilience of health systems in responding to public health emergencies. This includes: (1) Constructing a hierarchical psychological support system that encompasses prevention, intervention, and follow-up [61]. Introducing digital psychological assessment tools to enable dynamic monitoring and precise intervention for high-risk groups. (2) Establishing a humanized scheduling and occupational health management mechanism—developing an intelligent workload dynamic monitoring system [62] for high-exposure departments (e.g., emergency, ICU), implementing flexible shift rotation with risk grading. (3) Launching peer support programs [63] where experienced medical staff share resilience-building strategies with colleagues. (4) Perfecting a comprehensive organizational support guarantee system [64]—providing financial and material incentives, such as hazard allowances and protective equipment upgrades, to enhance perceived organizational support.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study that merit consideration. Firstly, the utilization of a cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causal relationships, and future studies should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to establish the temporal and causal relationships among the variables. Secondly, the generalis ability of the conclusions may be limited by the fact that only areas with a high prevalence of outbreaks were selected by this study. Random or stratified sampling are needed to enhance the external validity and diversity of the sample. Thirdly, this study did not consider rumination, self-disclosure, and coping styles, which may affect the experimental results.

This study has both theoretical and practical implications. First, it enriches the literature on the psychosocial well-being of medical staff in traumatic events by examining the positive psychological changes they can experience when facing public health challenges. Second, we propose a mediated model to elucidate how perceived social support and resilience promote PTG in healthcare workers, providing empirical evidence. Third, it suggests that improving perceived social support and psychological resilience can enhance the well-being of medical staff and the quality of care provided. Fourth, our findings could assist policymakers in creating supportive environments and resources for healthcare workers to address the challenges of future public health emergencies.

Conclusion

To sum up, our study offers empirical evidence affirming the favorable influence of perceived social support on PTG within medical staff, where resilience acts as a mediating factor. These outcomes imply that medical staff might encounter favorable psychological transformations or growth while navigating the challenges of PHE. Additionally, perceived social support and resilience emerge as crucial factors in facilitating this process. Given these insights, psychosocial interventions become imperative for medical staff, promoting constructive coping mechanisms and fostering personal growth in the face of demanding circumstances. Our study underscore the importance of fostering a supportive environment and enhancing resilience among medical staff. Future endeavors in both research and practical applications should delve deeper into and bolster the factors that contribute to fostering PTG among medical staff in PHE.
