Efficacy and Safety of a Novel Filtering Function to Reduce Radiation Exposure During Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Nobuhiro Suzuki, Hidehiro Iwakawa, Toshiharu Shinohara, Ryosuke Kato, Hiyu Wakabayashi, Ryota Kaimori, Ken Terata, Hiroyuki Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to assess the effectiveness and safety of a new filtering feature (Spot ROITM, Canon Medical Systems, Japan) in reducing radiation exposure during radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) for atrial fibrillation (AF).Methods and ResultsThe study involved 2 steps: (1) an ex vivo experiment to evaluate the radiation reduction of Spot ROITM (SROI); (2) a clinical study with 69 consecutive patients undergoing initial AF-RFCA, divided into conventional collimation (CC) and SROI groups. All procedures used 3-dimensional electroanatomical mapping guidance. In the ex vivo model, SROI reduced radiation exposure by 66.7%. Clinically, all predefined procedures were successful, with similar complication rates between groups (CC vs. SROI, 3.2% vs. 2.6%; P=0.88). The dose–area product for patients (CC vs. SROI: 17.5 [12.8–25.4] vs. 5.6 [3.6–9.8] Gy · cm2, P<0.001) and scattered radiation at the operator’s chest level (13.0 [10.3–18.5] vs. 3.0 [1.8–5.3] µSv, P<0.001) were significantly lower with SROI. Procedural time (150 [125–167] vs. 150 [120–183] min, P=0.89), fluoroscopy time (13.6 [10.7–18.9] vs. 12.6 [9.6–15.2] min, P=0.09), and short- to intermediate-term recurrence rates were comparable between groups.ConclusionsSROI reduced the radiation exposure of both patients and operators without compromising procedural success, complication rates or recurrence rates.

Central Figure

Catheter ablation (CA) for atrial fibrillation (AF) is an effective strategy to reduce symptoms, and may also lower the risk of developing heart failure.1–3 Recent advances in procedural technologies have enabled AF ablation with less reliance on fluoroscopy in certain settings,4,5 but further strategies to minimize radiation exposure remain an important focus to improve the safety of both patients and operators.

A novel filtering functionality, Spot Region of Interest (Spot ROITM; Alphenix, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan) (hereafter referred to as SROI), has recently been introduced as a dose-reduction tool. Although its efficacy has been investigated in neurointerventional procedures and, more recently, reported in a case of percutaneous coronary intervention,6,7 its usefulness in CA for AF has not yet been evaluated.

We performed this study was to determine whether SROI reduces the radiation exposure of both patients and operators without affecting the rates of success, complication or recurrence.

Methods

Study Overview

The study had 2 steps. Step 1: preclinical study to assess the radiation-reduction effect of the SROI. Step 2: clinical study involving patients undergoing initial radiofrequency CA (RFCA) for AF. The overall timeline is shown in Figure 1. Notably, we retrospectively enrolled AF-RFCA cases performed with conventional collimation (CC), while cases using the SROI were prospectively included after completing Step 1. Details of SROI

The SROI technology has 2 main components. (1) A novel collimator design incorporating a 0.7-mm copper filter with a fixed square aperture, which enables full-dose fluoroscopic exposure within the ROI while reducing the dose to surrounding regions by 65–85% through enhanced beam attenuation (Figure 2A). (2) The SROI filter can be freely repositioned within the selected field of view (FOV) along both the x- and y-axes (Supplementary Movie). This allows the operator to adjust the ROI to the anatomical area of interest without repositioning the patient’s table. The filter can be moved either under fluoroscopic guidance or virtually using the last image hold, providing flexible and efficient ROI targeting throughout the procedure. Step 1: Ex Vivo Evaluation of the Fluoroscopic Dose-Reduction Effect of SROI

An ex vivo experiment using single-plane C-arm angiography equipment (Alphenix, Canon Medical Systems) and a thoracic phantom model (Kyoto Kagaku, Tokyo, Japan) was assembled to assess the dose-reduction effect of SROI (Figure 2B). The Alphenix system is equipped with a 30×30-cm a-Si flat panel detector (TFP1200C/A2.OEM; Canon Medical Systems) with a pixel size of 194 µm. In total, 47 dosimeters were evenly placed on the surface of the phantom.

We randomly selected fluoroscopic data, including table height, C-arm angle, fluoroscopy duration, and FOV size, from RFCA procedures for patients with AF performed without SROI. Fluoroscopy was carried out with an 8-inch FOV at a frame rate of 7.5 pulses/s. These settings were then replicated in the ex vivo experiment, with and without SROI.

The entrance surface dose (ESD) at each dosimeter was measured using an optically stimulated luminescence dosimeter (Landauer, Glenwood, IL, USA), and overall dose-reduction rates were calculated for conditions with and without SROI. Additionally, 2 dosimeters were placed at heights of 100 cm and 150 cm above the floor and 50 cm from the center of the table to measure scattered radiation and estimate operator exposure.

Step 2: Verification of the Efficacy and Safety of SROI During AF-RFCA

Study Population and Protocol We included consecutive patients who underwent initial AF-RFCA between August 2023 and June 2025. Patients were excluded if they had congenital heart disease, heart failure classified as New York Heart Association Class III or IV, a prior ablation procedure for AF, or were younger than 18 years. The cohort was chronologically divided into 2 groups: patients who underwent AF-RFCA with CC between August 2023 and December 2023 (CC group), and those treated with SROI between January 2024 and June 2025 (SROI group).

The primary endpoints were the success rates of pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) and adjunctive procedures such as cavo-tricuspid isthmus ablation, the procedure time, fluoroscopy time, radiation exposure dose, complication rate, and recurrence rate. In a subset of 24 cases (CC group, n=11; SROI group, n=13), operator radiation exposure was also measured by attaching dosimeters to the chest and abdomen outside the lead protector (Figure 2C). Notably, under-table lead curtains and a lead shield were consistently used to reduce operator exposure during all procedures (Figure 2D).

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of Akita University (Approval Number: 3180) and registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (Registration Number: UMIN000057737). Written informed consent was given by all participants in the SROI group. In the CC group, informed consent was obtained through an opt-out method, in accordance with national ethical guidelines.

Ablation Procedure

Pre- and postprocedural oral anticoagulation was required for at least 3 weeks before and 3 months after CA. All antiarrhythmic drugs except amiodarone were discontinued at least 4–5 half-lives before the procedure; amiodarone was discontinued at 8 weeks prior. All procedures were performed under moderate to deep conscious sedation and with 1 of 2 operators. A multipole electrode catheter was inserted via either the cubital or internal jugular vein and positioned in the coronary sinus under fluoroscopic guidance.8,9 All RFCA procedures were guided by 3-dimensional electroanatomical mapping (3D-EAM) systems, either CARTO 3 (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA, USA) or EnSite (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA). The PVs were mapped after transseptal catheterization, and PVI was performed with a point-by-point approach.

For CARTO 3, RF ablation was delivered with a Thermocool STTM SF catheter (Biosense Webster) at 30–40 W, targeting an ablation index of 350 for the posterior wall and the region above the esophagus, and 50 W with an ablation index of 500 for other regions.10 For EnSite, a TactiFlexTM catheter (Abbott) was used with a standard setting of 40 W for 20 s; in the posterior wall and the region above the esophagus, applications were titrated to 40 W for 10 s.11 An esophageal temperature probe was inserted nasally, and RF delivery was immediately stopped if the esophageal temperature reached 39℃. Real-time contact force was monitored and maintained at 5–15 g, with an inter-lesion distance <5 mm.

The procedural endpoint was confirmation of bidirectional block in each PV, assessed after a 30-min waiting period with intravenous isoproterenol infusion and/or adenosine to test for dormant conduction. If acute PV reconnection or dormant conduction was observed, gap mapping and additional RF applications were performed until bidirectional block was achieved in all PVs.

Follow-up

A standard 12-lead ECG and 24-hour Holter monitoring were routinely performed at 1 week, and at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after hospital discharge, as well as in response to any recurrence of symptoms. Follow-up was extended as long as clinically feasible through outpatient visits and telephone interviews.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as median with interquartile range (25–75th percentile). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. For group comparisons, parametric data were analyzed using Student’s t-test, and nonparametric data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were compared with either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Based on the Step 1 findings, the sample size for Step 2 was estimated assuming a 66.7% reduction with the use of SROI and using the mean and standard deviation of radiation exposure during RFCA under the CC setting. With an α error of 0.05 and study power of 80% (β=0.20), the estimated sample size was >30 patients in each of the CC and SROI groups. Freedom from atrial arrhythmia recurrence was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and recurrence-free survival in the 2 groups was compared using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. All analyses were performed using StatFlex software version 7.1 (Artech, Osaka, Japan).

Results

Step 1: SROI in the Phantom Model

Figure 3A shows a heat map of radiation exposure in the phantom model with and without SROI filtering, demonstrating a clear reduction in exposure when SROI was applied. Figure 3B presents the ESD measured at each dosimeter placed on the phantom. Overall, the ESD was significantly lower with than without SROI (1.0 [0.5–4.3] vs. 3.0 [1.3–15.6] mGy, respectively; P<0.001), corresponding to a reduction in the median dose of approximately 66.7%. In the operator exposure model, SROI use resulted in a 33.5% reduction at 150 cm height (with vs. without SROI: 284 vs. 427 mSv) and a 34.2% reduction at 100 cm height (312 vs. 473 mSv). Procedural Outcomes

Successful PVI was achieved in all patients, and only 2 periprocedural complications occurred: 1 case of pericarditis in the CC group and 1 groin hematoma in the SROI group (CC vs. SROI, 3.2% vs. 2.6%; P=0.88). The pericarditis was managed conservatively with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and pericardiocentesis was not required.

Table 2 summarizes the procedural outcomes for the 2 groups. The dose–area product (DAP) was significantly lower in the SROI group than in the CC group (5.6 [3.6–9.8] vs. 17.5 [12.8–25.4] Gy · cm2, P<0.001), corresponding to a 68.0% median reduction in the fluoroscopic dose (Figure 4A). By contrast, the procedural time did not differ between groups (CC vs. SROI: 150 [125–167] vs. 150 [120–183] min, P=0.89), nor did the fluoroscopy time (13.6 [10.7–18.9] vs. 12.6 [9.6–15.2] min, P=0.09) (Figure 4B). DAP reduction in the SROI group was consistent across all procedural phases, while the fluoroscopy time in each step was similar in both groups (Table 2). AF Recurrence After RFCA Between CC and SROI

During a median follow-up of 348 [96–629] days, AF recurrence was observed in 5 patients (16.1%) in the SROI group, and 5 patients (13.2%) in the CC group. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in recurrence rates between groups (Figure 6). Discussion

Main Findings

In this study we evaluated the efficacy and safety of a novel filtering function for AF-RFCA. Our key findings are listed below.

(1) Procedural success was achieved in all cases, with comparable complication rates in the CC and SROI groups.

(2) Patient radiation exposure was significantly lower in the SROI group, with a median reduction of 68.0%.

(3) Scattered radiation exposure of the operators was also reduced with SROI compared to CC, particularly at chest level.

(4) Short- to intermediate-term AF recurrence rates were similar in both groups.

Risk of Radiation Exposure and Efficacy of SROI

Although angiography equipment is indispensable for performing CA, the long-term risks of radiation are not negligible.12–14 Cumulative doses >100 mSv have been associated with an approximately 1% increase in lifetime cancer risk above baseline.12 In addition, patients who have undergone >3 cardiac catheterization procedures have been reported to face 1.8-fold higher mortality rate from lung cancer than patients who have undergone only 1 procedure.15 Minimizing patient radiation exposure is therefore essential to reduce preventable long-term health complications.

For operators, the risk of scattered radiation has also become of increasing concern. For cardiac catheterization operators, head radiation exposure is significantly greater on the left side,16 and intracranial tumors have been reported to occur more frequently in the left hemisphere.17 Moreover, interventional physicians have been reported to have a 3-fold increased risk of cataracts.18 Thus, reducing radiation exposure is critical for the health of both patients and interventionists.

In this context, we evaluated the efficacy of SROI, a novel filtering function, in reducing radiation during RFCA, and demonstrated that it significantly reduced the fluoroscopic dose to both patients and operators. Regarding patient radiation exposure, using the SROI reduced fluoroscopy exposure by 68% compared with a standard collimation setting, with similar fluoroscopic times. Although the difference in fluoroscopy time did not reach statistical significance, it tended to be shorter in the SROI group, which had RFCA performed after the CC group. Therefore, this study cannot completely exclude historical bias. However, the study demonstrated significantly lower radiation exposure for both patients and operators, highlighting that the differences in radiation exposure between the groups were more substantial than the nonsignificant difference in fluoroscopy time. These results suggested that the slight reduction in fluoroscopy time was not the sole factor behind the significant decrease in radiation exposure. Interestingly, abdominal scattered radiation exposure for the operators did not differ significantly between the SROI and CC groups, whereas exposure at the chest level decreased in the SROI group compared to the CC group. The relatively close distance from the X-ray tube to the abdomen, compared with the chest, may explain these findings.

Continued Need for Fluoroscopic Guidance as a Safety Measure

Although advances in 3D-EAM systems have reduced fluoroscopy use during AF ablation, real-time fluoroscopy remains essential in many cases, particularly when anatomical variability or patient movement compromises the accuracy of the 3D-EAM systems.19,20 Moreover, zero-fluoroscopy procedures often require high operator expertise and stable procedural conditions, which may not always be achievable in routine practice.4,21,22 Tzeis et al. reported that fluoroless ablation could not be achieved in 37% of patients; minimal rescue fluoroscopy was necessary to confirm catheter location and assess potential complications.23

Because SROI provides targeted fluoroscopic visualization without requiring major modifications to conventional systems, it offers a practical approach to reducing radiation exposure while maintaining procedural safety and flexibility.6 Consistent with this, our study showed that SROI use did not affect procedural or fluoroscopy times, or complication and recurrence rates.

Clinical Implications

Although this study focused exclusively on AF-RFCA to ensure protocol consistency, the utility of SROI may extend to other energy sources, including pulsed field ablation. Although the use of pulsed field ablation for AF is rapidly increasing,24–26 longer fluoroscopy times have been reported compared with RFCA.27 Further studies are therefore warranted to evaluate the efficacy of SROI in reducing radiation during pulsed field ablation. In addition, the seamless integration of SROI with existing fluoroscopic systems may position it as a versatile and scalable tool for broader application across CA procedures for other arrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia and supraventricular tachycardia, in which radiation exposure remains a clinical concern.

Study Limitations

First, it was a single-center, non-randomized, historical-control study, which could have introduced selection, observer, and historical biases. Nevertheless, the patients’ characteristics were similar in the SROI and CC groups. Second, the sample size was relatively small; however, the effective sample size was estimated from the ex vivo analysis. Finally, because the SROI function is available only on the Alphenix Evolve Edition X-ray system and cannot be used with other equipment, the generalizability of our findings may be limited.

Conclusions

The use of SROI reduced the radiation exposure of both patients and operators without compromising procedural success or complication and recurrence rates.
