Evaluation of the relationship between the J-CTO score and rentrop classification in patients with chronic coronary syndrome and chronic total occlusion
Authors: Ecem Gürses, Gamze Yeter Arslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe J-CTO score is a well-established tool for evaluating lesion complexity and predicting procedural success in chronic total occlusion (CTO) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The extent of coronary collateral circulation, typically graded using the Rentrop classification, is an important determinant of myocardial viability and procedural planning. However, the interplay between anatomical lesion complexity and the development of collateral vessels remains incompletely elucidated.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between J-CTO scores and Rentrop collateral grades in patients with CTO undergoing coronary angiography for chronic coronary syndrome (CCS).MethodsIn this single-center, retrospective study, a total of 243 patients with chronic coronary syndrome and angiographically confirmed CTO lesions, who underwent evaluation between October 2022 and October 2024, were included. Patients were categorized according to their J-CTO scores into two groups: a low J-CTO group (score 0–1) and a high J-CTO group (score ≥ 2). Collateral circulation was assessed using the Rentrop grading system (Grades 0–3). Clinical, laboratory, and angiographic parameters, as well as the Rentrop classification, were compared between the groups.ResultsPatients in the high J-CTO group (score ≥ 2; n = 159) exhibited a significantly greater prevalence of advanced collateral circulation compared with the low J-CTO group (score 0–1; n = 84; p < 0.001). Partial or complete collateral filling (Rentrop Grades 2–3) was observed in 79.2% of patients in the high J-CTO group versus 13.1% in the low J-CTO group. Despite a metabolically less favorable profile, left ventricular ejection fraction was significantly higher in the high J-CTO group (52.5% vs. 48.7%, p = 0.003). J-CTO score demonstrated a strong monotonic association with Rentrop grade (Spearman r = 0.671, p < 0.001). ROC analysis revealed that J-CTO score predicted advanced collateral development (Rentrop ≥ 2) with an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.800–0.902; sensitivity 92.0%, specificity 68.9% at cut-off ≥ 2). On multivariate logistic regression, higher J-CTO score (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.20–2.32, p = 0.002) and longer estimated ischemic duration (OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.06–1.22, p < 0.001) independently predicted Rentrop Grades 2–3.ConclusionsElevated J-CTO scores are significantly associated with more advanced coronary collateral development as assessed by the Rentrop classification, and with relatively higher left ventricular ejection fraction. These associations are consistent with adaptive vascular responses to prolonged ischemia; however, given the cross-sectional retrospective design, the exclusion of statin users, and the modest sample size, causal inferences cannot be drawn and generalizability is limited. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require prospective multicenter validation.

Introduction

Chronic coronary syndromes (CCS) represent a prevalent and clinically significant subset of coronary artery disease (CAD), characterized by stable manifestations associated with underlying myocardial ischemia [1, 2]. Within this spectrum, chronic total occlusions (CTOs)—angiographically defined as coronary artery occlusions with TIMI 0 flow persisting for at least three months—are encountered in approximately 15–30% of patients undergoing diagnostic coronary angiography [3, 4].

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of CTO lesions remains technically challenging, with lower success rates compared to non-occlusive lesions, owing to factors such as long lesion length, calcification, vessel tortuosity, and ambiguous proximal caps [5, 6]. Nevertheless, successful CTO revascularization is associated with meaningful clinical benefits, including angina relief, improvement in left ventricular systolic function, enhanced quality of life, and potentially reduced long-term cardiovascular events [7]. Therefore, reliable pre-procedural assessment of lesion complexity is critical for patient selection and optimization of procedural outcomes.

The J-CTO score is a validated and widely employed angiographic scoring system developed to predict the technical difficulty of CTO PCI. It incorporates five parameters—blunt proximal cap, lesion length > 20 mm, calcification, tortuosity, and prior failed attempt—each assigned one point. Higher scores are associated with lower procedural success rates and prolonged procedural time [1, 8].

Equally important in procedural planning is the extent of coronary collateral circulation, which maintains myocardial perfusion distal to the occlusion. Coronary collaterals function as natural bypasses, preserving myocardial viability and correlating with favorable clinical outcomes in CTO patients [9, 10]. The Rentrop classification, ranging from Grade 0 (no collateral filling) to Grade 3 (complete distal epicardial filling), remains a widely used angiographic tool for evaluating collateral development [2, 11].

Coronary collateral circulation develops as an adaptive response to chronic myocardial ischemia, predominantly through arteriogenesis driven by sustained pressure gradients and increased shear stress [9, 10]. This process results in functionally significant vessels that help maintain distal myocardial perfusion. The Rentrop classification provides a semi-quantitative angiographic measure of collateral development based on the degree of distal vessel filling [11–14]. It is therefore hypothesized that more complex and longstanding CTO lesions, reflected by higher J-CTO scores, are associated with higher Rentrop grades due to prolonged ischemic stimulus and vascular remodeling.

While the general relationship between CTO chronicity and collateral development is biologically established, systematic angiographic characterization of this relationship using standardized scoring tools — specifically the J-CTO score alongside the Rentrop classification — within a well-defined CCS population remains limited. The J-CTO score was developed primarily as a procedural difficulty tool; its utility as a correlate of collateral adequacy in a consecutive, systematically assessed cohort has not been formally quantified. This analysis aims to contribute that characterization, with the understanding that the findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically conclusive.

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between J-CTO scores and Rentrop collateral grades in patients with CCS and angiographically confirmed CTO. The objective was to characterize the association between lesion complexity and collateral development using standardized angiographic scoring systems within a contemporary CCS cohort.

Materials and methods

Study population and eligibility

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted following approval from the local ethics committee and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study population consisted of patients who underwent coronary angiography for stable coronary artery disease (CAD) between October 1, 2022, and October 1, 2024.

Patients diagnosed with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS), presenting with asymptomatic ischemia or stable angina pectoris, and undergoing coronary angiography were considered for inclusion. Chronic total occlusion (CTO) was defined as angiographic evidence of TIMI 0 flow in at least one coronary artery with an estimated duration of more than three months. The duration of CTO was estimated based on the time since first documented ischemic symptoms, prior myocardial infarction in the target vessel territory, or previous angiographic evidence, when available. Indications for coronary angiography were based on positive non-invasive ischemia testing, including exercise stress testing, stress echocardiography, or myocardial perfusion scintigraphy.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had anemia, renal impairment or diseases affecting renal clearance, prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), malignancy, collagen vascular disease, acute cerebrovascular events, recent acute coronary syndrome, fever, active infection, severe valvular heart disease, or congestive heart failure (CHF). CHF patients were excluded to minimize the confounding effects of advanced ventricular dysfunction on collateral development and Rentrop grading. Additionally, patients receiving statins or other lipid-lowering therapies were excluded, as these agents may influence endothelial function, angiogenesis, and arteriogenesis, potentially affecting collateral formation independently of lesion complexity. After applying these criteria, a total of 243 patients were enrolled.

Clinical and laboratory assessment

Demographic, clinical, and angiographic data were recorded for all participants. Detailed medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), hyperlipidemia, smoking status, prior myocardial infarction, family history of CAD, and medication use. Hypertension was defined as the use of antihypertensive medication or ≥ 3 measurements of systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg. DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose > 126 mg/dL on at least two occasions or current antidiabetic therapy. Hyperlipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL, or a documented diagnosis of dyslipidemia.

Echocardiographic assessment

Echocardiographic assessment was performed using the Epiq 7 cardiac ultrasound system (Philips Healthcare) with a 2.5–5 MHz probe. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured using the modified biplane Simpson’s method.

Angiographic assessment

Coronary angiography was performed via femoral artery access using 6 F Judkins catheters and iodixanol as the contrast agent on the Allura Xper FD 10 system (Philips Healthcare). A minimum of four projections of the left coronary system and two projections of the right coronary system were acquired in both digital and analog formats. Angiograms were independently reviewed by two cardiologists blinded to patient characteristics. The culprit vessel (defined as stenosis ≥ 100%) was categorized as right coronary artery, left coronary artery, or circumflex artery.

Revascularization difficulty was assessed using the J-CTO score, which incorporates five variables: blunt proximal cap, lesion length > 20 mm, calcification, tortuosity (curvature angle < 45°), and prior failed attempt, with one point assigned for each criterion [6]. Based on total scores, patients were categorized into a low J-CTO group (score 0–1) and a high J-CTO group (score ≥ 2).

Collateral circulation was evaluated using the Rentrop classification: Grade 0, no collateral filling; Grade 1, filling of side branches via collaterals without opacification of the main epicardial artery; Grade 2, partial collateral filling of the distal segment of the occluded vessel; and Grade 3, complete collateral filling of the distal segment. The relationship between lesion complexity and collateral development was assessed by comparing Rentrop grades between the low and high J-CTO groups.

The primary aim of this study was to retrospectively investigate the relationship between lesion complexity, as determined by the J-CTO score, and the extent of coronary collateral circulation, as assessed by the Rentrop classification, in patients with chronic total coronary occlusion.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Comparisons between patients with low and high J-CTO score were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed variables or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, Spearman rank correlation was calculated to assess the monotonic association between J-CTO score (continuous) and Rentrop grade (ordinal). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory capacity of J-CTO score for predicting advanced collateral development (Rentrop Grades 2–3); the optimal cut-off was determined using the Youden index. Area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) are reported.

Results

A total of 243 patients with chronic total occlusion (CTO) were included in the study. The mean age of the study population was 63.1 ± 9.5 years (Table 1), and 25.1% of the patients were female (Table 2). Hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity (78.2%), followed by hyperlipidemia (51.4%), diabetes mellitus (37.9%), and a history of coronary artery disease (33.7%). Chronic kidney disease was present in 15.6% of patients, and 63.0% were active smokers (Table 2).

Patients were stratified according to J-CTO score into a high J-CTO group (score ≥ 2; n = 159, 65.4%) and a low J-CTO group (score < 2; n = 84, 34.6%). The estimated duration of ischemic heart disease, based on clinical history and prior documentation, was longer in patients with high J-CTO scores, indicating a more chronic disease course in this group (Table 1).

Clinical and laboratory characteristics according to J-CTO score

Compared with the low J-CTO group, patients in the high J-CTO group exhibited significantly higher left ventricular ejection fraction (52.5 ± 8.8% vs. 48.7 ± 10.9%, p = 0.003), uric acid levels (6.7 ± 1.7 vs. 6.2 ± 1.6 mg/dL, p = 0.040), fasting glucose [135 (109–189) vs. 103 (93–120) mg/dL, p < 0.001], triglycerides [213 (154–270) vs. 105 (84–143) mg/dL, p < 0.001], and total cholesterol (199.8 ± 46.8 vs. 178.9 ± 49.0 mg/dL, p = 0.002). High-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were significantly lower in the high J-CTO group (37.1 ± 9.1 vs. 42.0 ± 11.2 mg/dL, p < 0.001). Serum albumin levels were modestly higher in the high J-CTO group (4.0 ± 0.3 vs. 3.9 ± 0.3 g/dL, p = 0.026). No significant differences were observed with respect to creatinine, hemoglobin, leukocyte count, or platelet count (Table 1).

Angiographic findings

Right coronary artery CTOs were significantly more frequent in the high J-CTO group compared with the low J-CTO group (61.0% vs. 45.2%, p = 0.019), whereas no significant differences were observed regarding left anterior descending or circumflex artery involvement (Table 2).

Collateral circulation and rentrop classification

Collateral circulation differed significantly between the low and high J-CTO groups according to the Rentrop classification (p < 0.001, Table 3). Patients in the low J-CTO group predominantly exhibited limited collateralization, with Rentrop Grade 1 observed in 77.4% of cases. In contrast, patients in the high J-CTO group demonstrated more advanced collateral development, with partial collateral filling (Rentrop Grade 2) present in 69.2% and complete collateral filling (Rentrop Grade 3) in 10.1% of patients (Table 3).

When patients were analyzed according to Rentrop grade, those with higher Rentrop grades (Grades 2–3) were more likely to have higher J-CTO scores and a longer estimated duration of ischemic symptoms compared with patients with lower Rentrop grades (Grades 0–1). Patients with higher Rentrop grades also demonstrated higher left ventricular ejection fraction; however, given the observational design of the study, this association should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal protective effect of collateral circulation.

Spearman rank correlation demonstrated a strong positive monotonic association between J-CTO score and Rentrop grade across the full cohort (r = 0.671, p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis indicated that J-CTO score was a strong predictor of advanced collateral development (Rentrop Grades 2–3), with an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.800–0.902). At the Youden-optimal cut-off of ≥ 2, the J-CTO score achieved a sensitivity of 92.0%, specificity of 68.9%, positive predictive value of 79.2%, and negative predictive value of 86.9% for identifying patients with Rentrop Grades 2–3. These findings indicate that a J-CTO score ≥ 2 identifies patients with advanced collateral development with high sensitivity in this statin-naive cohort.

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of advanced collateral development (Rentrop Grades 2–3). Variables included age, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, estimated duration of ischemic heart disease, target vessel location, left ventricular ejection fraction, and J-CTO score. In the adjusted model, higher J-CTO score and longer ischemic duration remained independently associated with higher Rentrop grades, whereas traditional metabolic risk factors did not retain independent significance. (Table 4) Although the J-CTO score remained independently associated with higher Rentrop grades, this relationship should be interpreted with caution, as lesion chronicity—reflected by ischemic duration—likely plays a central mediating role.

These findings further support the hypothesis that lesion complexity and chronicity are closely interrelated processes influencing collateral development.

Discussion

This study aimed to characterize the angiographic, clinical, and metabolic profile of patients with chronic total occlusion (CTO), stratified according to J-CTO score. The findings indicate that patients with higher J-CTO scores tend to exhibit a metabolically less favorable profile, more advanced collateral vessel development as graded by the Rentrop classification, and relatively preserved left ventricular systolic function. While these associations are consistent with the known biology of progressive coronary occlusion and arteriogenesis, the cross-sectional retrospective design of this study precludes causal interpretation, and the findings should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Patients classified within the high J-CTO group demonstrated significantly higher fasting glucose, triglyceride, total cholesterol, and serum uric acid levels, accompanied by lower concentrations of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). This laboratory pattern reflects a metabolically unfavorable milieu consistent with advanced systemic atherosclerosis [15]. Notably, elevated serum uric acid may further indicate increased oxidative stress and impaired endothelial function in this cohort [16].

Interestingly, despite their adverse metabolic profile, patients with higher J-CTO scores exhibited modestly higher LVEF values. One possible explanation is the presence of more advanced collateral circulation in this group. However, given the retrospective design and the relatively small absolute difference in LVEF, this observation should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal protective effect of collateral circulation and may reflect residual confounding or selection bias. Collateral circulation differed significantly between J-CTO categories, with Rentrop Grades 2–3 being substantially more frequent among patients with higher J-CTO scores, supporting a close association between lesion complexity and collateral development. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating an association between collateral maturation and long-standing CTO lesions [12, 13, 17].

Angiographically, right coronary artery (RCA) involvement was more prevalent in the high J-CTO group. This pattern may relate to anatomic predispositions that promote both occlusion formation and enhanced visualization of collateral channels within the RCA territory [15, 18]. Although a statistically significant difference in albumin levels was observed, this finding is unlikely to be clinically meaningful and should be interpreted cautiously [19].

Although widely used, the Rentrop classification is limited by its semi-quantitative nature and dependence on visual interpretation, which may not accurately represent the functional capacity of collateral circulation. Future studies employing quantitative techniques—such as the collateral flow index (CFI) or pressure-based hemodynamic assessments—could allow for a more precise evaluation of collateral function in CTO patients [16, 17, 20].

The J-CTO score, originally developed to predict procedural difficulty during CTO-PCI, demonstrated a strong discriminatory capacity for collateral adequacy in the present cohort, as confirmed by the ROC analysis (AUC 0.851; sensitivity 92.0%; specificity 68.9% at cut-off ≥ 2) and the strong Spearman correlation with Rentrop grade (r = 0.671, p < 0.001). These quantitative metrics provide a more granular characterization of the J-CTO–Rentrop relationship than previously reported in the literature. Higher J-CTO scores, typically reflecting more chronic and structurally complex lesions, are associated with longer ischemic duration and greater arteriogenic stimulus, consistent with the observed association with higher Rentrop grades. We acknowledge, however, that this association is partly driven by the shared confounder of occlusion chronicity, which limits its incremental mechanistic value.

Importantly, the presence of well-developed collateral circulation should not be interpreted as an indicator of reduced clinical relevance or absence of ischemia. In some patients, preserved left ventricular function may mask ongoing ischemic burden. Therefore, the combined evaluation of anatomical lesion complexity and collateral status may contribute to a more individualized approach to decision-making in CTO patients, particularly when determining the need for revascularization.

Clinical implications

These observational findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of integrated anatomical and collateral assessment in CTO patients. Higher J-CTO scores were associated with more advanced collateral circulation, which in turn may reflect greater myocardial adaptation to chronic ischemia. An integrated approach considering J-CTO score, Rentrop grade, and metabolic profile — while acknowledging the limitations of this retrospective analysis — may support more individualized treatment decisions. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should be confirmed in prospective studies with functional endpoints.

An important methodological consideration is the exclusion of statin-treated patients from this study. While this limits generalizability to real-world CTO populations — the majority of whom receive statin therapy — the statin-naive design provides a unique opportunity to observe the natural history of collateral development in the absence of pharmacological modulation of arteriogenesis and endothelial function. Statins are known to influence collateral formation through pro-angiogenic, anti-inflammatory, and vasoprotective mechanisms; their inclusion would have introduced pharmacological confounding that is difficult to adjust for analytically. The present cohort therefore represents a biologically controlled observation of the anatomical determinants of collateral adequacy, trading generalizability for internal biological clarity. We fully acknowledge this limitation and recommend that future studies include statin use as a covariate rather than an exclusion criterion. The modest sample size (n = 243) and single-center design further constrain external validity, and the findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating pending multicenter prospective validation.

Limitations

Several important limitations of this study must be explicitly acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center, cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits generalizability. The observed associations between J-CTO score, Rentrop grade, and LVEF should be interpreted as descriptive only. Second, the modest sample size (n = 243) from a single tertiary center restricts statistical power for multivariate subgroup analyses and limits the ability to detect smaller effect sizes. A larger, multicenter cohort would be necessary to validate these findings and establish their external validity. Third, collateral circulation was assessed using only the semi-quantitative Rentrop classification without functional hemodynamic validation (e.g., collateral flow index); Rentrop grade may not accurately reflect true collateral function or microvascular reserve.

Fourth — and critically — patients receiving statin or lipid-lowering therapy were excluded to minimize pharmacological confounding on arteriogenesis and collateral development. While this exclusion was methodologically motivated, it represents a fundamental limitation of generalizability: the majority of real-world CTO patients receive statin therapy, and the exclusion of this group creates a highly selected, non-representative cohort. We acknowledge that statin use would ideally be retained as a covariate in the analysis rather than used as an exclusion criterion, and that future studies should address this important gap. The metabolic differences observed between J-CTO groups (lipid profile, glucose) should therefore be interpreted in the context of this statin-naive population and cannot be generalized to statin-treated patients.

Fifth, the estimated duration of ischemic heart disease was based on clinical history and prior documentation rather than objective imaging, introducing significant subjective bias in this important determinant of collateral maturation. Sixth, the J-CTO score incorporates lesion parameters (length, calcification, tortuosity) that partly overlap with the determinants of collateral maturation, introducing conceptual circularity into the J-CTO–Rentrop association. The central confounder of occlusion chronicity likely contributes to both higher J-CTO scores and more advanced collateral development. Consequently, the observed association should be interpreted cautiously, as part of the relationship may reflect overlapping biological processes rather than entirely independent phenomena. Seventh, interobserver variability in both Rentrop grading and J-CTO scoring was not formally assessed. Eighth, the difference in LVEF between groups, while statistically significant (52.5% vs. 48.7%), is modest in absolute terms and both values remain within the preserved-to-mildly-reduced range; the LVEF difference may reflect selection bias rather than a true collateral-mediated protective effect and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality.

Finally, the absence of procedural outcome data, long-term follow-up, and multivessel CTO burden data preclude any conclusions about the impact of these findings on revascularization strategy or clinical outcomes. The study was not designed or powered for outcome analysis, and all conclusions regarding clinical implications should be interpreted accordingly.

Conclusion

In this single-center, retrospective study of 243 patients with chronic coronary syndrome and angiographically confirmed chronic total occlusion, higher J-CTO scores were independently associated with more advanced collateral development as graded by the Rentrop classification, and with relatively preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. The central association between J-CTO score and Rentrop grade is driven in part by the shared underlying factor of lesion chronicity, which limits the incremental novelty of this finding. The exclusion of statin users and the single-center design with modest sample size further restrict generalizability. These findings should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. The combined use of J-CTO score and Rentrop classification may provide complementary anatomical and functional information in CTO assessment; however, prospective, multicenter studies with functional collateral endpoints and inclusive of statin-treated populations are required to establish clinical utility and validate these observations.
