Correlation of base deficit and age shock index with in-hospital mortality in geriatric major trauma patients: a 1-year retrospective study at a single level I trauma center in Indonesia
Authors: Miftahul Khairat Musmar Elbama, M Iqbal Rivai, Irwan, Avit Suchitra, Aulia Rahman, Rose Dinda Martini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBase deficit (BD) and age shock index have been utilized as an indicator of resuscitation adequacy and a predictor of poor outcomes in trauma cases, respectively. However, evidence regarding their correlation with in-hospital mortality among geriatric major trauma patients remains scarce in the literature.MethodsThis analytical observational study employed a retrospective cohort design involving 82 geriatric major trauma patients treated at our institution between November 2023 and November 2024. Data were collected from patients’ medical records at admission (age, trauma mechanism, vital signs, Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS], Injury Severity Score, hemoglobin, BD, and comorbidities) and at discharge (survival or death).ResultsThe geriatric major trauma patients who experienced in-hospital mortality were predominantly male, with an average age of 69.6 years. Traffic accidents constituted the most common trauma mechanism. Most patients presented with a GCS score between 13 and 15, and hypertension was the most frequently recorded comorbidity. BD demonstrated a significant correlation with in-hospital mortality (P<0.05). Severe BD was associated with the highest odds of in-hospital mortality (adjusted odds ratio, 40.72; 95% confidence interval, 2.90–560.86). Although age shock index did not directly correlate with mortality, it played a confounding role. Additionally, a GCS score of <9 was significantly correlated with in-hospital mortality (P<0.05).ConclusionsThe findings of this study can inform initial clinical management strategies for geriatric major trauma patients at trauma centers. Prompt resuscitation and treatment should be prioritized for patients presenting with moderate or severe BD to reduce preventable mortality in this population.Keywords: Acid-base imbalance, Shock, Geriatrics, Hospital mortality, Acute care surgery

INTRODUCTION

Background

The increasing elderly population is a global phenomenon requiring appropriate attention from the trauma community. It is estimated that by 2050, elderly individuals will constitute more than 21% of the world’s population [1]. At our institution, geriatric trauma patients represent 19.2% of the total trauma cases annually across all age groups. Trauma management in geriatric patients necessitates particular attention due to high rates of morbidity and mortality [2]. Geriatric trauma patients often present to the emergency department (ED) with discrepancies in clinical manifestations, such as severe anatomical injuries accompanied by normal vital signs or vice versa. Additionally, medical emergencies like myocardial infarction or stroke that precede trauma events can complicate triage assessments in this population [3]. These factors may contribute to under-triage during the initial evaluation of geriatric trauma patients.

Major trauma can result in hemodynamic instability and mortality secondary to uncontrolled bleeding. Assessing hemodynamic status in geriatric trauma patients is challenging due to physiological aging processes and preexisting comorbidities [4]. Conventional vital signs and routine blood tests have limited reliability in detecting hemodynamic changes in this group. Base deficit (BD)—defined as the amount of base (in millimole) required to titrate 1 L of arterial blood to a pH of 7.40 at 37 °C with a PaCO2 of 40 mmHg—reflects the buffering effects of hemoglobin and CO2 in the blood. BD has been employed as a biochemical marker of shock, injury severity, and mortality [5–7]. Meanwhile, age shock index (ASI) is an indicator used to assess hemodynamic status, predict the need for significant blood transfusion, and estimate 48-hour mortality risk in geriatric trauma patients [8,9]. Despite BD and ASI being utilized as a parameter of resuscitation and a predictor of poor trauma outcomes, respectively, studies evaluating their correlation with in-hospital mortality in geriatric major trauma patients remain scarce. Moreover, most existing studies were conducted more than a decade ago.

Objectives

This study aims to identify the characteristics of geriatric major trauma patients, evaluate the correlations of BD and ASI with in-hospital mortality in this population, and explore potential confounding factors influencing these relationships.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of Dr. M. Djamil Padang General Hospital (No. DP.04.03/D.XVI.XI/491/2024). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and setting

This analytical observational study employed a retrospective cohort design and was conducted at Dr. M. Djamil Padang General Hospital (Padang, Indonesia).

Participants

The participants in this study were geriatric trauma patients who fulfilled the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All trauma patients aged ≥60 years with an Injury Severity Score of ≥16 who were treated at Dr. M. Djamil Padang General Hospital between November 2023 and November 2024 were included. Geriatric trauma patients with any of the following were excluded: (1) history of return of spontaneous circulation after cardiac arrest in prehospital settings or in the ED; (2) drug or alcohol intoxication; and (3) discharge against medical advice. Patient follow-up was conducted by retrieving data recorded upon admission and discharge from medical records.

Variables

The primary variables analyzed in this study were BD, ASI, and in-hospital mortality. Potential confounding variables identified included age, trauma mechanism, comorbidities, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, and hemoglobin level.

Data sources and measurement

All data were retrospectively acquired from patient medical records (secondary data). BD data were collected from blood gas analyses performed within 2 hours of admission to the ED, with follow-up measurements taken at intervals of 6–8 hours within the first 24 hours, as clinically indicated. ASI was calculated using the initial vital signs documented at admission, by multiplying patient age by the heart rate to systolic blood pressure ratio. In-hospital mortality was determined from discharge summaries, indicating the timing of death during hospitalization.

Bias

To minimize potential bias, we adhered to our institution’s research protocol and developed a detailed checklist for data collection.

Study size

A total of 82 patients met the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Quantitative variables

Quantitative variables were initially used in descriptive analyses and subsequently transformed into categorical data for proportion analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS ver. 30.0 (IBM Corp). Numerical data were presented to describe the characteristics of the patients. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test. The nonparametric Mann-Whitney U-test was applied for ordinal data that was not normally distributed. Multiple logistic regression analysis was used to control for potential confounding variables, and Spearman rank correlation was employed to evaluate correlations between confounding variables. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Participants

Between November 2023 and November 2024, a total of 82 geriatric major trauma patients met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in this study, comprising 35 patients who survived and 47 patients who died during hospitalization (Fig. 1).

Flowchart illustrating the selection of geriatric major trauma patients eligible for this study. ROSC, return of spontaneous circulation.

Clinical characteristics of geriatric major trauma patients

Among geriatric major trauma patients who died during hospitalization, the average age was 69.6 years, with the largest proportion (38 of 47 patients, 80.9%) falling within the 60- to 74-year age group. No patients aged >90 years were included in the study. Male patients who died represented a larger proportion of the overall study sample (34 of 82 patients, 41.5%) than female patients who died (13 of 82 patients, 15.9%). Among mortality cases, the most common trauma mechanism was traffic accidents, accounting for 29 cases (61.7%), and hypertension was the most frequently reported comorbidity, identified in 12 cases (25.5%). The most frequent GCS score range upon ED admission among the patients who died was 13 to 15 (28 patients, 59.6%), with an average GCS score of 12.2. Regarding ASI upon ED admission, out of 47 patients who died in-hospital, 18 (38.3%) exhibited stable hemodynamics (ASI <50), whereas 29 (61.7%) were hemodynamically unstable (ASI ≥50), with an average ASI of 50.4. Also among mortality cases, the mean hemoglobin level was 10.7 g/dL, with normal hemoglobin levels reported most frequently (31 patients, 66.0%). The most common severity category for BD in patients who died was mild, ranging from >−6 to −2 mmol/L, with 19 cases (40.4%); the average BD value at ED admission was −7.1 mmol/L (Table 1).

Correlations of BD and ASI with in-hospital mortality

Statistical analysis using the chi-square test revealed significant correlations between BD and in-hospital mortality (P=0.026) and between ASI and in-hospital mortality (P=0.028). In subgroup analysis, patients with normal BD levels predominantly survived (10 patients vs. 4 patients), while those with severe BD were most likely to die during hospitalization (2 patients vs. 11 patients). The patients who died in the hospital were primarily those with mild and moderate BD levels, with an average BD of −7.11 among mortality cases. Similarly, patients with stable hemodynamics (ASI <50) mainly survived (22 patients vs. 18 patients), whereas those with unstable hemodynamics (ASI ≥50) more frequently died (13 patients vs. 29 patients), with an average ASI of 50.4 among patients who died in-hospital (Table 1). When categorizing in-hospital mortality based on the timing of death (≤48 hours vs. >48 hours), no significant differences were observed in BD severity or ASI (both P>0.05) (Table 2).

Confounding factors influencing the correlations of BD and ASI with in-hospital mortality

Multiple logistic regression analysis evaluating the correlations between each primary variable, potential confounding factors, and in-hospital mortality indicated significant associations between BD and in-hospital mortality (P=0.046) and between GCS score and in-hospital mortality (P=0.026). Severe BD demonstrated the highest odds ratio (OR) for in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR, 40.72; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.90–560.86). This analysis was adjusted for age, trauma mechanism, comorbidities, and hemoglobin level (Table 3). ASI acted as a confounder in the correlation between BD severity and in-hospital mortality, as well as in that between GCS score and in-hospital mortality (Table 4). BD and ASI demonstrated a weak positive significant correlation, while GCS score and ASI demonstrated a very weak negative significant correlation.

DISCUSSION

The characteristics of geriatric major trauma patients in this study reflect the demographics of Indonesia’s elderly population. The average patient age of approximately 69 years, with no patients aged ≥90 years, aligns with the bell-shaped distribution of the Indonesian population by age group reported in 2020 by the United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific [10]. The sex composition in this study reflects the predominant trauma mechanism observed: motor vehicle collisions, particularly involving motorcycle riders. These individuals are primarily male, as reported by Savitri and Indawati [11]. Hypertension was the most frequently identified comorbidity in this study, consistent with the findings outlined in the 2023 World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) guidelines for managing trauma in elderly and frail patients [2]. Additionally, the average hemoglobin level observed was within the normal range, consistent with a study by Raisinghani et al. [12], which indicated that increasing age in geriatric patients is not significantly associated with anemia.

This study demonstrated a significant correlation between BD and in-hospital mortality among geriatric major trauma patients. Patients who experienced in-hospital mortality exhibited an average BD of −7.11, predominantly categorized as mild BD (adjusted OR, 9.14; 95% CI, 1.0–82.4). No significant differences were observed in BD severity levels when comparing early (≤48 hours) and late (>48 hours) mortality. These findings align with the study by Callaway et al. [13], cited in the 2023 WSES guidelines [2], indicating that geriatric trauma patients with a BD of −4 or less experience a 5.2-fold increase in mortality risk. Consequently, rapid correction of BD and restoration of physiological function should be prioritized in geriatric major trauma patients in the ED to reduce the risk of both early and late mortality, as similarly emphasized by van Wessem et al. [14].

Even though ASI demonstrated a significant correlation with in-hospital mortality in the bivariate analysis, multivariate analysis employing a multiple logistic regression model showed no direct association. This finding differs from a prior study by Kim et al. [8], which validated ASI as a predictor of mortality and reported a mortality rate of 69.4% among geriatric trauma patients with an ASI of 50 or greater. It also contrasts with the findings of Zarzaur et al. [9], who identified ASI as a superior predictor of 48-hour mortality and recommended its use for early identification of geriatric trauma patients at high risk of early mortality. Nonetheless, in this study, ASI acted as a confounder affecting the significance of the correlation between BD and in-hospital mortality. This result suggests that geriatric major trauma patients with an ASI ≥50 should undergo prompt resuscitation to prevent deterioration of BD, which directly influences in-hospital mortality.

The aging process places geriatric trauma patients at increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage following trauma. However, due to cerebral atrophy, intracranial pathology may initially present with normal or even high GCS scores upon neurological examination [4]. In this study, most geriatric trauma patients presented with GCS scores of 13 to 15 in the ED, consistent with findings from Kehoe et al. [15], who stated that geriatric trauma patients with traumatic brain injury often present with higher GCS scores than younger patients with similar injury severity. Conversely, geriatric trauma patients with GCS scores below 9 in this study predominantly died during hospitalization (adjusted OR, 32.96; 95% CI, 2.47–438.75; P=0.008). This result corroborates findings from Lalwani et al. [16], who reported that low GCS scores strongly predict mortality in geriatric trauma patients and indicate both intracranial trauma pathology and compromised cerebral perfusion secondary to hemorrhagic shock. However, the findings of Ghneim et al. [17] should also be considered, indicating that survival from moderate/severe traumatic brain injury in geriatric patients is influenced more by injury severity than by age itself.

Limitations

This study has several potential limitations. First, its retrospective design and the absence of a consistent trauma-specific registry at our institution may have introduced biases in data collection. Second, the relatively small number of participants, limited study duration (1 year), and single-center design may not fully reflect broader trends among geriatric trauma patients.

Conclusions

BD significantly correlates with in-hospital mortality among geriatric major trauma patients. ASI does not directly correlate with in-hospital mortality in this patient group; however, it acts as a confounder influencing the strength of the association between BD and in-hospital mortality. Aggressive resuscitation efforts targeting the control of ASI and the correction of BD are essential for reducing preventable mortality among geriatric major trauma patients. Future multicenter studies involving larger cohorts and extended observation periods are warranted to better understand trends and outcomes in these patients.
