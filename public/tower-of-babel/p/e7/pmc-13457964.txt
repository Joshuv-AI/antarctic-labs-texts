Dual-tracer [18F]-AV133 and [18F]-FDG PET/MR multiparametric characterization of Parkinson’s disease motor subtypes
Authors: Ying Zhou, Wei-Zhao Lu, Xin-Ru Cheng, Chang Yang, Bixiao Cui, Jie Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTwo major motor subtypes are widely recognized in Parkinson’s disease (PD): tremor-dominant (TD) subtype and the postural instability-gait difficulty (PIGD) subtype. These distinct clinical phenotypes imply divergent underlying neural mechanisms. This study aimed to investigate the neural correlates of TD and PIGD subtypes using multiparametric analysis with hybrid positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance (PET/MR) and two tracers: [18F]-fluoropropyl-(+)-dihydrotetrabenazine ([18F]-AV133) and [18F]-fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]-FDG).MethodsA total of 42 patients with PD (22 TD, 20 PIGD) and 27 healthy controls (HCs) were enrolled. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc tests were performed to compare presynaptic dopaminergic function, cerebral glucose metabolism, and gray matter volume (GMV) across groups. Correlation analyses further explored relationships between imaging metrics and Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS-UPDRS-III) scores. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted.ResultsRelative to TD-PD patients, PIGD-PD patients showed significantly lower [18F]-AV133 binding in the bilateral putamen (contralateral anterior putamen: 1.72±0.33 vs. 2.05±0.56, P=0.033; ipsilateral anterior putamen: 1.57±0.38 vs. 1.82±0.40, P=0.044; contralateral posterior putamen: 1.69±0.40 vs. 1.43±0.17, P=0.011; ipsilateral posterior putamen: 1.37±0.14 vs. 1.55±0.33, P=0.030), with more symmetric dopaminergic impairment in the posterior putamen (0.09±0.11 vs. 0.19±0.17, P=0.025). PIGD-PD also exhibited reduced [18F]-FDG uptake in the lateral/medial prefrontal cortices (P=0.008 and 0.019), putamen (P<0.001), thalamus (P<0.001), pons (P<0.001), and cerebellum (P<0.001). Reduced GMV was noted in the bilateral cerebellum [(lobule IX, lobule IV/V) right lobule IX: P=0.035, left lobule IX: P=0.044, right lobule IV/V: P<0.001, left lobule IV/V: P<0.001], orbital superior frontal gyrus (right: P=0.007, left: P=0.003), postcentral gyrus (right: P=0.025, left: P<0.001), right cerebellar lobule VIII (P=0.048), left cerebellar lobule VIIb (P<0.001), left inferior temporal gyrus (P<0.001), and right thalamus (P=0.005). For PIGD-PD patients, correlation analyses revealed that bilateral putaminal [18F]-AV133 binding, prefrontal/putaminal [18F]-FDG uptake, and GMV in the orbital superior frontal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, and cerebellum were significantly negatively correlated with UPDRS-III scores (r ranging from −0.833 to 0.607, all P<0.05). Multimodal integration achieved excellent discrimination [area under the curve (AUC) =0.923].ConclusionsTD-PD and PIGD-PD exhibit distinct patterns of presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction, cerebral hypometabolism, and gray matter atrophy. These neuroimaging differences may partially account for the divergent clinical manifestations between the two motor subtypes, Multimodal integration achieved excellent discrimination.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common chronic neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, yet its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood (1). Two core histopathological hallmarks are well established: extensive loss and degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, and the presence of Lewy bodies in the cytoplasm of surviving neurons. Clinically, PD manifests with both motor and non-motor symptoms; among motor features, resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability/gait disturbance are most characteristic. Based on predominant motor phenotypes, PD patients are classified into two major subtypes: tremor-dominant (TD) and postural instability-gait difficulty (PIGD) (2). These subtypes differ significantly in motor profiled and cognitive trajectories. Specifically, the PIGD subtype is associated not only with more severe gait and postural instability but also with a high risk of cognitive decline, incident dementia, and faster disease progression compared with the TD subtype (3). These distinct clinical profiles suggest divergent neural mechanisms underpinning the two motor subtypes. Elucidating the differences between PIGD-PD and TD-PD may help identify subtype-specific neural pathways and inform targeted therapeutic strategies.

As an in vivo molecular imaging modality, positron emission tomography (PET) enables the noninvasive characterization of brain pathophysiology, facilitating mechanistic investigations in PD (4). Over recent decades, presynaptic dopaminergic imaging has been used to assess nigral dopaminergic dysfunction. Prior studies have shown that PIGD-PD patients exhibit more severe dopaminergic denervation in the bilateral putamen than TD-PD patients. However, limited specificity restricts the utility of dopaminergic imaging for distinguishing these motor subtypes (5-9).

Dihydrotetrabenazine (DTBZ) binds to vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), a reliable biomarker of monoaminergic neuronal integrity. Compared with traditional tracers, VMAT2 imaging shows reduced susceptibility to pharmacological interference from dopamine-modulating agents such as levodopa. Furthermore, [18F]-labeled radiotracers have a longer half-life than earlier 11C-labeled agents, enhancing their potential for routine clinical translation (10).

Beyond nigrostriatal dopaminergic impairment, PD is increasingly recognized to involve extensive dysfunction in extranigral brain regions. Accordingly, [18F]-fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]-FDG) PET has been widely employed to characterize and quantify global cerebral metabolic abnormalities in PD patients. To investigate the interplay among cerebral metabolism, dopaminergic dysfunction, and clinical phenotypes, dual-tracer studies combining [18F]-fluoropropyl-(+)-dihydrotetrabenazine ([18F]-AV133), and [18F]-FDG PET have been performed (11-14). Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that PD motor subtypes exhibit distinct patterns of cerebral microstructural injury, structural connectome alterations, and clinical progression rates (15-17). However, few studies have systematically examined the multivariate relationships among dopaminergic function, cerebral metabolism, gray matter integrity, and clinical phenotypes across these subtypes.

In this study, we employed a dual-tracer [18F]-AV133 and [18F]-FDG PET approach using hybrid PET/magnetic resonance (MR) to evaluate the correlations and differences in dopaminergic function, cerebral glucose metabolism, gray matter volume (GMV), and clinical manifestations. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0629/rc).

Methods

Participants and consent

This study included 42 patients with PD and 27 healthy controls (HCs) who underwent [18F]-AV133 and [18F]-FDG PET imaging at the Department of Radiology and Nuclear Medicine of Xuanwu Hospital from January 2022 to December 2023, for whom a diagnosis was established according to the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for PD (18). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. [2023]044). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Strict exclusion criteria were applied to ensure cohort homogeneity. Patients were excluded if they had tremor, marked cognitive impairment [defined as a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score below 24], or severe concurrent conditions that could affect gait assessment, including acute systemic diseases, visual impairment, orthopedic or musculoskeletal disorders, and prior stroke. Additional exclusion criteria comprised inadequate response to dopaminergic therapy, current use of psychotropic medications, and contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning, including claustrophobia or metallic implants. Furthermore, all enrolled candidates underwent comprehensive screening for atypical parkinsonism features, including early-onset severe autonomic dysfunction, severe dementia with impairments in memory, language, and praxis, supranuclear gaze palsy, and refractory postural instability. Those with clinical signs suggestive of multiple system atrophy, corticobasal degeneration, progressive supranuclear palsy, or other atypical parkinsonian syndromes were excluded from the final analysis.

Clinical evaluation

Patients were classified into TD-PD and PIGD-PD subgroups based on the MDS-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) (2). Motor subdomain scores were calculated as follows. The mean TD score was calculated by summing the scores of MDS-UPDRS items 2.10 (tremor), 3.15 (postural tremor), 3.16 (kinetic tremor), 3.17 (rest tremor), and 3.18 (rest constancy), and dividing the total by 11, which corresponded to the total number of body-region subitems. The mean PIGD score was calculated by summing the scores of items 2.12 (walking and balance), 2.13 (freezing), 3.10 (gait), 3.11 (freezing of gait), and 3.12 (postural stability) and dividing by 5. Patients with a TD/PIGD ratio ≥1.15 were assigned to the TD-PD group (n=22), and those with a ratio ≤0.90 were assigned to the PIGD-PD group (n=20). All the participants underwent complete Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS-III) motor scores, Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) stage, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and [18F]-AV133 and [18F]-FDG PET/MR scanning between January 2022 to December 2023 at Xuanwu Hospital.

Image acquisition and processing

Prior to imaging, all participants rested quietly in a dimly lit, quiet room to ensure full relaxation.

Integrated PET/MR examinations were performed using a hybrid 3.0-T PET/MR scanner (uPMR790, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). The acquisition time for both [18F]-AV133 and [18F]-FDG scans was 15 minutes. Two radiotracers were administered according to standardized protocols, each with a fixed post-injection scanning window: for [18F]-AV133, 222 MBq was injected intravenously and PET acquisition began 90 minutes post-injection. The biodistribution characteristics and scanning time window adopted in this study were selected according to well-established [18F]-AV133 PET protocols for evaluating VMAT2 activity. [18F]-AV133 exhibits stable striatal uptake and minimal nonspecific brain clearance at the applied acquisition time point, which ensures optimal signal-to-background contrast and reliable quantification of nigrostriatal VMAT2 availability. For [18F]-FDG, 150–200 MBq was injected intravenously and PET acquisition was performed 45–55 minutes post-injection. The MRI protocol included a three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) 7.9 ms, echo time (TE) 2.8 ms, matrix size 256×256, and isotropic voxel size of 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm. PET images were reconstructed using time-of-flight and point spread functions with a matrix size of 256×256, voxel size of 1.5625×1.5625×2.0000 mm3, with 4 iterations and 20 subsets, Gaussian filter [full width at half maximum (FWHM) =3 mm], attenuation and scatter corrections were applied. In addition, high resolution 3D T1WI images were acquired with the following parameters: TR =7.9 ms, TE =3.8 ms, 176 sagittal slices, field of view (FOV) =256×256 mm2, and voxel size of 1 mm3.

[18F]-AV133 data were processed using MATLAB 2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and the Spatial Normalization of Brain PET Images (SNBPI) toolbox. Images were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard brain via the DARTEL algorithm, resampled to 2-mm isotropic voxels, and smoothed with an 8-mm FWHM 3D Gaussian kernel in SPM12. Region of interest (ROI) masks for the bilateral caudate nucleus, anterior putamen, posterior putamen, and occipital lobe were generated with SNBPI using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) template. The putamen was automatically divided into two equal anterior and posterior segments. The occipital cortex, which lacks VMAT2 expression, was selected as the reference region. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was calculated as striatal uptake divided by occipital uptake; mean SUVR was derived for the bilateral caudate nucleus, anterior putamen, and posterior putamen. The asymmetry index was defined as [(higher SUVR − lower SUVR)/higher SUVR] * 100% to assess striatal uptake asymmetry.

[18F]-FDG PET images were converted from Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) to analysis format using Dicom2niiX (Center for Brain imaging, University of South Carolina, Colombia, SC, USA). In MATLAB 2024b, images were normalized to the MNI template via SPM12 and smoothed with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel for subsequent analysis. Peak cortical activity values were extracted and compared with an age-matched normal database. Z-score subtraction was performed to generate hypometabolic maps in eight standard directions. A total of 26 ROIs were automatically defined, including the bilateral medial and lateral prefrontal cortices, temporal lobes, sensorimotor areas, precuneus, superior and inferior parietal lobes, anterior and posterior cingulate cortices, occipital lobes, primary visual cortices, whole cerebellum, and pons. SUVR was calculated relative to the whole brain mean. Z-scores were computed as (patient SUVR-normal mean)/normal standard deviation (SD) to quantify deviations from normative data, and bilateral ROI Z-scores were averaged.

Statistics analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the software SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Demographic and imaging data were presented as mean ± SD. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of data distribution. For quantitative data among the three groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the least significant difference (LSD) t-test was applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons. The Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni correction was used for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons between two groups were conducted via the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine linear relationships between imaging indicators and UPDRS scores. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, with the area under the curve (AUC) in assessing the diagnostic performance for PIGD-PD and TD-PD. A two tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Presynaptic dopaminergic function in the TD-PD, PIGD-PD, and HCs

Striatum, [18F]-AV133 binding (SUVR) differed significantly among the three groups. Both PD subgroups showed reduced SUVR compared with HCs. The SUVR was significantly lower in the bilateral putamen of PIGD-PD patients than in TD-PD patients (contralateral anterior putamen: 1.72±0.33 vs. 2.05±0.56, P=0.033; ipsilateral anterior putamen: 1.57±0.38 vs. 1.82±0.40, P=0.044; contralateral posterior putamen: 1.69±0.40 vs. 1.43±0.17, P=0.011; ipsilateral posterior putamen: 1.37±0.14 vs. 1.55±0.33, P=0.030) (Table 2, Figure 1A). No significant differences in SUVR were found in bilateral caudate. Furthermore, dopaminergic loss in the posterior putamen was more symmetrical in the PIGD-PD than in the TD-PD patients (0.09±0.11 vs. 0.19±0.17, P=0.025), whereas no significant differences were observed in the asymmetry index of caudate or anterior putamen (Table 2, Figure 1B).

Gray matter abnormalities in the TD-PD, PIGD-PD, and HCs

Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) was applied to compare GMV differences among the TD-PD, PIGD-PD, and HCs groups. Age, sex, and total intracranial volume (TIV) were included as covariates. ANOVA with family-wise error (FWE) correction was performed at a voxel-level threshold of P<0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of P<0.05. In total, 15 significant clusters were identified (Figure 3A), with peak regions located in the right cerebellum lobule VIII, left cerebellum lobule VIIb, bilateral cerebellum lobule IX, left inferior temporal gyrus, left gyrus rectus, bilateral cerebellum lobule IV/V, left middle temporal, bilateral superior frontal gyrus (orbital part), right thalamus, left insula, and bilateral postcentral (Table 4). The left inferior temporal gyrus contained the highest voxel proportion. PIGD-PD patients showed significantly lower GMV than TD-PD patients in the right cerebellum_lobule VIII, left cerebellum lobule VIIb, bilateral cerebellum lobule IX, left inferior temporal gyrus, bilateral cerebellum lobule IV/V, bilateral superior frontal gyrus (orbital part), and bilateral postcentral gyrus. Notably, the right thalamic GMV was significantly higher in PIGD-PD patients than in TD-PD patients (Figure 3B, Table 5).

Discussion

This is the first study to explore the utility of dual-tracer [18F]-AV133and [18F]-FDG PET/MR for distinguishing PD motor subtypes. PIGD-PD patients were characterized by more symmetric and severe bilateral putaminal dopaminergic degeneration. PIGD-PD patients also exhibited reduced prefrontal, caudate, putaminal, thalamic, pontine, and cerebellar metabolism relative to TD-PD patients. Significant GMV reductions were observed in the cerebellum, left inferior temporal gyrus, orbital superior frontal gyrus, right thalamus, and postcentral gyrus in PIGD-PD patients. These findings advance understanding of the interplay among dopaminergic function, brain metabolism, gray matter structure, and clinical phenotypes across PD motor subtypes.

PIGD-PD patients had lower MoCA scores than TD-PD, consistent with prior reports (19). Poorer cognition in PIGD-PD patients may relate to greater hippocampal subfield involvement. PIGD-PD patients also had worse UPDRS-III scores. TD-PD patients are more strongly associated with the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit (20), which may underpin its motor phenotype; however, PD pathogenesis is complex and requires further validation. No inter-subtype differences were observed in sex, age, disease duration, H&Y stage, or UPDRS-III improvement rate.

Characteristics of presynaptic dopaminergic function in TD-PD and PIGD-PD

Previous dopamine neuroimaging studies of PD motor subtypes using [18F]-AV133 or [18F]-FP-CIT have widely reported that non-TD-PD patients experience more severe dopaminergic loss in the putamen than TD-PD patients (6,7,21). In this study, dopaminergic imaging results revealed that PIGD-PD patients showed more pronounced nigrostriatal dysfunction in the anterior and posterior putamen compared with TD-PD patients, which is consistent with prior studies using presynaptic dopaminergic tracers. Moreover, we further investigated dopaminergic deficiency on the ipsilateral and contralateral sides relative to the clinically more affected limb and found that, regardless of laterality, dopaminergic loss in the putamen was more severe in PIGD-PD patients, particularly on the contralateral side. This finding suggests that a more extensive loss of dopaminergic neuronal activity in the putamen and highlights a posterior-to-anterior gradient of degeneration. One previous study reported that the difference in VMAT 2 binding between the PIGD-PD and TD-PD group was more significant on the ipsilateral side (21); in contrast, we found the between-group difference to be more significant on the contralateral side. This discrepancy may be primarily attributable to differences in disease duration and H&Y stage across study cohorts, resulting in varying degrees of dopaminergic deficit, although the deficits remain largely concentrated in the putamen. No significant differences in dopaminergic loss were observed in the ipsilateral or contralateral caudate between the two groups. Although the SUVR value in PIGD-PD patients was substantially lower than that in TD-PD patients, analysis of the asymmetry index revealed that dopaminergic loss was more symmetrical in the PIGD-PD patients. This may be related to the more rapid disease progression in PIGD-PD patients (22) which is more likely to involve both hemispheres simultaneously. To better understand the impact of regional differences in VMAT2 binding on motor symptoms, we analyzed the correlations between [18F]-AV133 uptake in striatal subregions and the UPDRS-III score. In PIGD-PD patients, [18F]-AV133 binding in the bilateral caudate nucleus and putamen was significantly negatively correlated with the UPDRS-III score, whereas in TD-PD patients, significant negative correlations were observed only in the contralateral anterior and posterior putamen. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating greater dopaminergic neuron loss in non-TD-PD patients (23), and further suggest that dopaminergic deficiency is more severe and widespread in PIGD-PD than in TD-PD.

Characteristics of cerebral metabolism in TD-PD and PIGD-PD

[18F]-FDG PET has long been applied in PD research and plays a key role in establishing the Parkinson’s disease-related pattern (PDRP), which reflects the distribution of glucose metabolism across various brain regions (24). The PDRP is grounded in basal ganglia neural circuits and fiber network connections and is characterized by decreased metabolism in the premotor area and posterior parietal lobe, with concomitant increased metabolism in the putamen, globus pallidus, thalamus, pons, and cerebellum (25). Earlier studies have demonstrated that PDRP expression is not correlated with tremor symptoms but is associated with the severity of rigidity and postural gait disturbance (26).

Compared with patients with TD-PD, those with PIGD-PD exhibited decreased glucose metabolism in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, caudate nucleus, putamen, thalamus, pons, and cerebellum. Striatal hypometabolism is likely associated with impairment of core motor circuits, whereas hypometabolism in the prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortices indicates cognitive dysfunction in PIGD-PD patients. These findings suggest that PIGD-PD may arise from functional deficits in the basal ganglia and cerebral cortex, which is consistent with results from previous studies (27).

Reduced metabolism in the thalamus and brainstem may impair the transmission of ascending motor signals. Concurrent metabolic abnormalities within motor and cognitive networks serve as one of the contributors to aggravated gait disturbances and recurrent falls in PIGD-PD patients. We also found that metabolic levels in the frontal lobe and putamen were correlated with UPDRS-III scores in PIGD-PD patients. This further demonstrates that the distinct neural mechanisms underlying PIGD-PD predominantly involve the prefrontal cortex and striatum.

Characteristics of gray matter in TD-PD and PIGD-PD

With respect to brain structure, VBM analysis in this study revealed reduced GMV in regions involved in motor, cognitive, and communicative functions in the PIGD-PD patients, which is consistent with previous findings (28). The predominant motor symptoms of the PIGD-PD subtype are gait disturbance and postural instability. Normal gait and postural control are not entirely automatic processes, they also require the integration of attention, executive function, visuomotor processing, and interregional information transfer within the brain (29). Other studies have proposed that gait disturbance in PD may primarily involve perceptual-cognitive system impairment mediated by the frontal cortex (30). Patients without gait disturbance can compensate for motor deficits by allocating greater attentional and cognitive resources to maintain normal gait (31). In the correlation analysis of this study, the UPDRS-III score in PIGD-PD patients was significantly negatively correlated with GMV in the superior frontal gyrus and postcentral gyrus, regions involved in higher-order cognitive functions such as attention and decision-making. This suggests that the development of PIGD-PD may be related to the impairment of advanced cognitive functions mediated by frontal cortical networks. Although decreased GMV in cerebellar lobule VIII has been reported in TD-PD and associated with tremor symptoms (32), our results showed that GMV in cerebellar lobule VIII was reduced in the PIGD-PD patients compared with the TD-PD patients, and was negatively correlated with the motor score in PIGD-PD patients, indicating widespread cerebellar damage. We speculate that the cerebellum is primarily involved in fine motor regulation, postural balance, and motor skill learning; enhanced local cerebellar activity may serve a compensatory role in the development of tremor symptoms in PD, but in PIGD-PD patients, this compensation fails through the joint actions of cerebello-cortical circuits, ultimately resulting in pathological damage. Our results also showed that thalamic GMV was reduced in the TD-PD patients and negatively correlated with the UPDRS-III score, indicating that the development of TD-PD may involve limbic system damage. Taken together, the VBM-based findings suggest that structural changes and differences in gray matter across PD motor subtypes may involve the limbic system and cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuits, providing valuable insight into their distinct pathophysiological bases.

Evaluation and comparison of diagnostic performance

ROC analysis demonstrated that [18F]-AV133, [18F]-FDG, and GMV exhibited moderate-to-good discriminatory capacity in distinguishing PIGD-PD from TD-PD, with AUC values ranging from 0.599 to 0.713. Although single-modality measures—reflecting striatal dopaminergic function, regional glucose metabolism, and focal gray matter alterations—showed partial efficacy in subtype differentiation, they were individually limited in diagnostic accuracy. In contrast, multimodal integration of [18F]-AV133, [18F]-FDG, and GMV using logistic regression substantially enhanced predictive performance, yielding an excellent AUC of 0.923. This notable improvement highlights that combining dopaminergic integrity, metabolic network patterns, and structural brain changes comprehensively captures the unique pathophysiological signatures underlying PIGD-PD and TD-PD.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, the sample size is relatively modest, therefore, the current findings should be regarded as preliminary. Further validation and replication in larger, multi-center cohorts are strongly warranted. Second, this investigation employed a cross-sectional design rather than a prospective longitudinal design, precluding definitive establishment of causal relationships between the observed imaging alterations and the clinical manifestations of PD. Third, although all patients underwent imaging assessments in a practically defined “off” state, potential residual effects of long-term dopaminergic therapy on the imaging parameters cannot be entirely excluded. Future prospective studies with expanded sample sizes and stricter medication control protocols are expected to address these limitations and provide more robust evidence on this topic.

Conclusions

This study identified multimodal imaging features distinguishing PIGD-PD from TD-PD using [18F]-AV133, [18F]-FDG PET, and VBM. PIGD-PD exhibited bilateral striatal dopaminergic deficiency and prefrontal-striatal hypometabolism, whereas TD-PD showed asymmetric contralateral putaminal dopaminergic loss, elevated thalamic metabolism, and enhanced cerebellar activity, linked to cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuits. Subtype-specific gray matter changes involved limbic and cerebello-thalamo-cortical regions. Multimodal integration achieved excellent discrimination (AUC =0.923).
