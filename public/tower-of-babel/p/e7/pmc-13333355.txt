Impact of living environmental quality on sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults: evidence from cross-sectional and cohort analyses
Authors: Lei Huang, Yu Huang, YuHao Chen, YuNa Huang, Yong Sun, Changlin Zhou, Bin Qian, Xianxu Zhang, Shicheng Li, YuHui Wang, Zhiqiang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractA composite living environmental quality score was constructed using outdoor PM2.5 exposure, household fuels, drinking water source, building type, and indoor temperature. Cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses from the CHARLS cohort demonstrated a dose–response association between poorer living environmental quality and an increased risk of sarcopenia among middle-aged and older adults.Keywords: Sarcopenia, CHARLS, Living environmental quality, Cohort

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related, progressive, systemic syndrome characterized primarily by loss of skeletal muscle mass, decline in muscle strength, and/or impaired physical function [1]. According to the 2019 consensus of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), its diagnosis focuses on three core domains: muscle strength, muscle mass, and physical performance [2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis including 70,202 older adults reported a global prevalence of sarcopenia in community-dwelling older populations of 18.8% [3], with approximately 15% among Chinese adults aged 60 years and older [4]. Sarcopenia imposes a substantial multidimensional burden on middle-aged and older adults: the risk of falls is 2–3 times higher than that of healthy peers, significantly increasing hospitalization rates and healthcare costs [5]. Furthermore, multiple studies have indicated that sarcopenia can exacerbate the progression of chronic diseases, including diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease, thereby amplifying both familial and societal caregiving and economic pressures [[6], [7], [8]].

The etiology of sarcopenia is multifactorial. Aging-related suppression of the insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1)/Akt/mTORC1 anabolic pathway, coupled with overactivation of the FoxO-mediated ubiquitin–proteasome system, leads to protein metabolic imbalance [9,10]. Mitochondrial dysfunction–induced oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation, mediated through pathways such as NF-κB, further accelerate muscle fiber degradation [11]. Additional intrinsic contributors include motor unit loss, selective atrophy of type II muscle fibers, and declines in anabolic hormones such as growth hormone and androgens [12]. On the social and lifestyle level, inadequate intake of protein and vitamin D, sedentary behaviors, social isolation, low socioeconomic and educational status, and adverse habits such as smoking and alcohol consumption can exacerbate sarcopenia risk through the aforementioned biological pathways [[13], [14], [15]].

Among these factors, however, the influence of living environmental quality—including outdoor PM2.5 exposure, household cooking and heating fuel types, drinking water sources, building type, and indoor temperature—has received limited systematic attention. Unlike individual behaviors such as diet and exercise, environmental exposures are typically “passive” and “persistent,” potentially affecting muscle health through multiple mechanisms: pollutants from solid fuel combustion may trigger systemic inflammation and accelerate muscle protein breakdown [16]; non-clean water sources may disrupt muscle metabolism via heavy metal accumulation [17]; suboptimal indoor temperatures can increase energy expenditure and metabolic stress, accelerating muscle loss [18]; and building type may indirectly influence muscle function by shaping daily physical activity patterns [19]. Although prior studies have explored associations between single environmental exposures, such as air pollution or solid fuel use, and sarcopenia, few have incorporated these multidimensional environmental factors into a unified analytical framework, and systematic evaluations based on large-scale prospective data remain lacking.

Against this research backdrop, the present study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the impact of living environmental quality on sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults using both cross-sectional and cohort analyses. Distinct from previous research, this study, firstly, incorporates multiple environmental dimensions—including building type, household temperature, water source, energy use, and outdoor PM2.5 exposure—into a unified analytical framework; secondly, employs both cross-sectional and longitudinal data, enabling assessment of associations with prevalent sarcopenia and prediction of incident sarcopenia over time; and finally, leverages nationally representative CHARLS data with large sample size and high-quality follow-up, enhancing the generalizability of findings.

Methods

Study population

This study was based on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey designed to collect multidimensional information on socioeconomic status, health, and healthcare among Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. CHARLS employs a multistage probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method, covering 150 counties and districts across 28 provinces in China. The baseline survey was officially launched in 2011, with data collected via face-to-face structured questionnaires, and follow-up assessments conducted biennially. Follow-up data from 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 have been publicly released. All study procedures were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (Approval No.: IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The initial study population comprised 17,708 participants from the 2011 CHARLS baseline survey. For the cross-sectional analysis, participants with missing data on living environmental factors (n = 314), age (n = 635), height or weight (missing or implausible, n = 3,784), or baseline sarcopenia diagnosis (n = 92) were sequentially excluded, resulting in a final sample of 12,883 participants. For the cohort analysis, participants with possible sarcopenia or sarcopenia at baseline (n = 4,056) and those missing 2-year follow-up data (n = 2,579) were further excluded, leaving 6,248 participants. Assessment of living environmental quality

A composite living environmental quality score was developed to evaluate individuals’ environmental exposures. The score was based on five core environmental factors: outdoor PM2.5 exposure, household cooking and heating fuel types, drinking water source, building type, and indoor temperature.

Outdoor PM2.5 exposure was assessed using the annual mean concentration in participants’ cities, derived from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). This dataset integrates multiple sources, including ground monitoring networks, satellite remote sensing, atmospheric reanalysis, and numerical models, providing long-term, high spatial resolution (1 km grid) air quality data across China. According to China’s national ambient air quality standards, annual mean PM2.5 ≥ 35 μg/m3 was classified as high exposure and assigned a score of 1, whereas <35 μg/m3 was scored 0. Household energy use was evaluated based on primary fuels used for cooking and heating. Clean fuels for cooking included natural gas, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), electricity, and biogas, while clean heating fuels included natural gas, LPG, electricity, solar energy, and district heating. If both cooking and heating used clean fuels, a score of 0 was assigned. Use of solid fuels (coal, crop residues, firewood, or charcoal) for either cooking or heating was scored 1 for each domain. Drinking water source was assessed by asking participants whether they had access to tap water. Availability of tap water was scored 0; otherwise, 1. Building type was classified by residence: multi-storey buildings were scored 0, while single-storey or bungalow-type dwellings were scored 1. Indoor temperature was objectively assessed by interviewers during the home visit. Homes rated as “comfortable” were scored 0, while those rated as “very hot,” “hot,” “cold,” or “very cold” were scored 1.

Scores for all five factors were summed to generate a total score, ranging from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating poorer living environmental quality. Based on existing epidemiological frameworks, the total score was further categorized into three risk levels: low (0–1), medium (2–3), and high (4–6).

Assessment of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia status was evaluated according to the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria, which encompass three core domains: muscle strength, skeletal muscle mass, and physical function.

Muscle strength was assessed by handgrip strength using a Yuejian TM WL-1000 digital dynamometer. Measurements were taken for both the dominant and non-dominant hands, at least twice per hand, and the maximum value across all attempts was used as the final grip strength. If one hand could not be measured due to injury or other reasons, the maximum value of the opposite hand was used. Low muscle strength was defined according to AWGS 2019 cut-offs: <28 kg for men and <18 kg for women. Skeletal muscle mass was estimated using appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM). Since CHARLS does not directly measure muscle mass, a validated anthropometric equation previously applied in Chinese populations was used, showing good agreement with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) measurements:

ASM = 0.193 × weight (kg) + 0.107 × height (cm) − 4.157 × sex−0.037 × age (years) − 2.631

Height-adjusted muscle mass index (ASM/height2) was calculated to account for body size. Low muscle mass was defined using the lowest 20th percentile of sex-specific ASM/height2 in the study population, corresponding to <6.79 kg/m2 for men and <4.89 kg/m2 for women. Physical function was assessed using the five-time chair stand test and gait speed. Low physical function was defined as ≥12 seconds for the chair stand test or gait speed <1.0 m/s, according to AWGS 2019 criteria. Participants unable to complete the tests were classified as having low physical function.

Based on these three domains, sarcopenia status was classified as follows: participants with normal muscle strength and physical function were defined as non-sarcopenic; those with low muscle strength or low physical function but not low muscle mass were defined as possible sarcopenia; and those with both low muscle mass and either low muscle strength or low physical function were defined as sarcopenia.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous studies and available information in the CHARLS database. Sociodemographic variables included age (continuous), sex (male/female), marital status (married vs. other, including never married, divorced, widowed), residence (urban/rural), and education level (categorized as incomplete primary, private/home/primary, middle school, and high school or above).

Anthropometric and physiological measurements included body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and pulse. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Blood pressure and pulse were measured on the left arm three times using an automatic electronic sphygmomanometer (OMRON Model HEM-7112), and the average was used for analysis. Chronic disease history was self-reported based on physician diagnoses and included hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, and stroke. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥90 mmHg, or self-reported physician diagnosis. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL, postprandial glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or self-reported diagnosis. Other chronic diseases were assessed based on participants’ responses. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), with scores ranging from 0 to 30. Consistent with prior studies, CESD-10 ≥10 was defined as presence of depressive symptoms. Lifestyle factors included smoking (never, former, current) and alcohol consumption (never, former, current).

Statistical analysis

This study employed a combined cross-sectional and cohort design. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1), and two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). In the cross-sectional analysis, participants’ baseline characteristics were compared across groups of living environmental quality (low, medium, high risk). Differences among groups were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables, depending on the data distribution, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Additionally, to describe the distribution of individual environmental factors, the five core factors (outdoor PM2.5 exposure, household energy use, drinking water source, building type, and indoor temperature) were stratified by sarcopenia status. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between living environmental quality and prevalent sarcopenia. Living environmental quality scores were first entered into the models as a continuous variable and subsequently categorized into three groups (low, medium, high risk) with the low-risk group as the reference. Four sequentially adjusted models were constructed: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for demographic variables (age, sex, marital status, residence, education); Model 3, additionally adjusted for lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption); and Model 4, further adjusted for clinical factors (BMI,hypertension, diabetes, depressive symptoms). Results from logistic regression are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

For the cohort analysis, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine associations between baseline living environmental quality and incident sarcopenia, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residual tests and further evaluated graphically by plotting log survival curves for the three environmental risk groups (Supplementary Figure S1). Although the Schoenfeld test has limited power with only two event times, the log‑log curves were approximately parallel, supporting the proportionality assumption for the main exposure. Sensitivity analyses, including a stratified Cox model (by age and BMI groups) and a time‑varying coefficient model (allowing age and BMI to interact with log time), were also performed; the results remained consistent (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). To explore potential dose–response relationships between living environmental quality scores and sarcopenia prevalence and incidence, trend analyses were conducted. Living environmental quality scores (0–6) were treated as ordinal variables in both logistic and Cox regression models to assess the linear trend in sarcopenia risk with increasing environmental risk levels. To evaluate the relative contribution of the five environmental factors (outdoor PM2.5, household energy use, drinking water source, building type, indoor temperature) to sarcopenia risk, all factors were simultaneously included in the fully adjusted logistic regression model (Model 4). Standardized regression coefficients were compared to quantify the relative importance of each factor.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of associations across different population strata, including age (<60, ≥60 years), sex (female, male), BMI (<28, ≥28 kg/m2), residence (urban, rural), education level (incomplete primary, private/home/primary, middle school, high school or above), marital status (married, other), smoking status (never, former, current), and alcohol consumption (never, former, current). Fully adjusted models (Model 4) were applied within each subgroup, and potential interactions between living environmental quality and subgroup variables were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests by comparing models with and without the interaction terms.

To assess robustness to the indirect muscle mass assessment, we repeated the cross‑sectional analysis and cohort analysis after excluding confirmed sarcopenia cases and using only handgrip strength to define possible sarcopenia. The environmental quality score was used as the exposure variable.

In terms of health conditions, participants in the high-risk group exhibited different distributions across several disease indicators: the prevalence of depressive symptoms, lung disease, and overall chronic conditions was higher than in the low-risk group (all P < 0.05), whereas the prevalence of hypertension, cancer, and heart disease was slightly lower. Regarding lifestyle factors, the proportion of current alcohol consumption was higher in the high-risk group compared with the low-risk group (P < 0.01), and the proportion of current smokers was also higher, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05).

Notably, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 29% in the high-risk group, higher than 24% in the low-risk group (P < 0.01). To further clarify the contributions of individual environmental factors, Table S4 shows the distribution of building type, heating fuel, drinking water source, cooking fuel, indoor temperature, and outdoor PM2.5 across different risk groups. Overall, the high-risk group had a significantly higher proportion of solid fuel use, non-tap water consumption, and high-level PM2.5 exposure (all P < 0.001).

As shown in Table 2, all five environmental factors differed significantly between participants with sarcopenia and those without (all P < 0.05). Regarding the composite living environmental quality score, a higher proportion of participants with sarcopenia were classified in the high-risk category (40%) compared with those without sarcopenia (35%), whereas the proportion in the low-risk category was lower. With respect to specific residential conditions, participants with sarcopenia were more likely to live in multi-storey buildings, use solid fuels for heating, consume non-tap water, and use solid fuels for cooking, compared with non-sarcopenic participants (all P < 0.001).

Characteristics of the cross-sectional study population

As shown in Table S6, the prevalence of sarcopenia in the cross-sectional population increased with higher living environmental quality burden, with rates of 22%, 29%, and 37% in the low-, medium-, and high-risk groups, respectively (P < 0.001). Table S7 shows that among participants with sarcopenia, a higher proportion were exposed to high-risk environmental conditions (45%) compared with those without sarcopenia (35%), and the prevalence of adverse environmental factors such as solid fuel use and non-tap water consumption was also higher (all P < 0.001).

Further stratification of sarcopenia status in Table S8 indicated a dose–response relationship: the proportion of participants with high-risk environmental burden increased progressively from non-sarcopenia (35%) to possible sarcopenia (44%) and to sarcopenia (58%) (P < 0.001), suggesting that greater living environmental quality burden is associated with increased severity of sarcopenia.

Cross-sectional associations between living environmental quality and sarcopenia

Table 3 presents the results of cross-sectional analyses examining the association between living environmental quality scores and sarcopenia. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), treated as a continuous variable, each one-unit increase in the living environmental quality score was associated with a significantly higher odds of sarcopenia (OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.12–1.21). In categorical analyses, using the low-risk group as the reference, participants in the medium-risk group had a significantly higher odds of sarcopenia (OR = 1.31, 95%CI: 1.08–1.59), and those in the high-risk group had an even greater odds (OR = 1.65, 95%CI: 1.36–2.01) in Model 4. A significant dose–response relationship was observed (P for trend <0.001), indicating that the likelihood of sarcopenia increased progressively with higher living environmental quality burden.

In cohort analyses, building floor type emerged as the most influential factor (62.0%), followed by cooking fuel (26.5%) and outdoor PM2.5 exposure (9.1%), whereas the contribution of tap water declined substantially to 1.5%.

Subgroup analyses in cross-sectional and cohort studies

Table S10 presents the results of subgroup analyses. In cross-sectional analyses, a significant interaction was observed only for building type (P for interaction = 0.002), with residents of multi-storey buildings exhibiting a stronger association between high living environmental quality burden and sarcopenia (OR = 2.25).

In cohort analyses, significant interactions were detected for age, heating fuel, and drinking water source (P = 0.037, <0.001, and 0.027, respectively), indicating that the impact of environmental burden on incident sarcopenia risk was more pronounced in these subgroups. No significant interactions were observed for sex, residence, BMI, marital status, smoking status, or alcohol consumption (all P for interaction >0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses using grip‑defined possible sarcopenia, the environmental quality score was significantly associated with prevalent (OR = 1.12, 95%CI: 1.06–1.18) and incident (HR = 1.10, 95%CI:1.04–1.17) outcomes (both P < 0.001), confirming the robustness of our main findings. Details are provided in Supplementary Table S11 and Figure S3.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates a significant association between living environmental quality and the risk of sarcopenia among middle-aged and older adults. In cross-sectional analyses, participants in the high-risk living environmental quality group had higher odds of sarcopenia compared with the low-risk group (OR = 1.65, 95%CI: 1.36–2.01); treating living environmental quality as a continuous variable, each one-point increase in the score was associated with a 16% higher odds of sarcopenia (OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.12–1.21). Longitudinal analyses similarly showed that participants in the high-risk group had an increased risk of incident sarcopenia (HR = 1.29, 95%CI: 1.04–1.61), and each one-point increase in living environmental quality score was associated with a 2% higher risk of new-onset sarcopenia (HR = 1.02, 95%CI: 1.00–1.09). Trend analyses indicated a significant linear increase in both sarcopenia prevalence and incidence with rising living environmental quality risk (P for trend <0.001). Relative contribution analyses identified drinking water source, cooking fuel type, and building type as the primary environmental determinants of sarcopenia. Subgroup analyses suggested that age significantly modified these associations, with stronger effects observed among individuals younger than 60 years. Collectively, these findings indicate that poor living environmental quality may be an important risk factor for sarcopenia in middle-aged and older populations.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies examining individual environmental exposures and muscle health. A recent study of older adults in China and India reported that both moderate and poor housing quality were significantly associated with higher sarcopenia prevalence, with a clear linear trend [20]. A study in Sweden also found that long-term exposure to air pollutants (NOx, PM2.5, PM10) was significantly associated with increased risk of possible sarcopenia among older adults, with NOx and PM2.5 exposure increasing risk by approximately 25% and PM10 by 33% [21]; higher pollutant exposure also accelerated declines in lower-limb strength and gait speed. In addition, household use of solid fuels for cooking and heating was associated with significantly increased sarcopenia risk in longitudinal analyses (HR = 1.56 and 1.26, respectively) [22]. Other studies have shown that high-risk living environmental factors can exacerbate cardiovascular disease risk (HR = 1.541) and, over a nine-year follow-up, significantly increase the risk of pain in multiple body regions, including upper and lower limbs, trunk, and head/neck, with consistent dose–response relationships (HR = 1.18–1.39, P for trend <0.001) [23,24]. Our study is the first to confirm, within a comprehensive assessment framework, the positive association between poor living environmental quality and sarcopenia risk.

Mechanistically, PM2.5 exposure may induce skeletal muscle oxidative stress and upregulate myostatin expression, promoting age-related muscle atrophy [25]. PM2.5 can penetrate the respiratory barrier into the circulation, triggering excessive mitochondrial reactive oxygen species in skeletal muscle, impairing oxidative phosphorylation efficiency, and disrupting mitochondrial dynamics [26], as evidenced by upregulation of fission protein Fis-1, downregulation of fusion proteins Mfn-1/2, overactivation of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, and reduced expression of biogenesis regulators such as PGC-1α, Tfam, and COX-1 [27]. PM2.5-induced oxidative stress also activates NF-κB, upregulates myostatin, inhibits satellite cell differentiation, and exacerbates muscle atrophy [28]. Pollutants generated from solid fuel combustion, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide, can induce systemic low-grade inflammation and accelerate muscle protein degradation via activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway [29]. Wang et al. further reported that depressive symptoms partially mediated the association between solid fuel use and sarcopenia (mediation effect ∼16.27%), suggesting that psychosocial pathways may represent an important mechanism linking environmental exposures to muscle health [30]. Regarding building type, a study in South Korea found that older adults residing in multi-storey apartments were more likely to engage in moderate-to-high intensity leisure-time physical activity compared with those living in single-storey houses (OR = 1.24–1.78), suggesting that building type may indirectly influence muscle function via daily activity patterns, such as stair climbing [31]. Our study also found that uncomfortable indoor temperature was associated with increased sarcopenia risk. From a metabolic perspective, skeletal muscle is the largest thermogenic organ; sustained exposure to suboptimal indoor temperatures may impose chronic energy stress, accelerating muscle catabolism over time [32].

Notably, the relative contributions of environmental factors differed between cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, reflecting potential differences in temporal mechanisms. In cross-sectional analyses, drinking water (36.1%), cooking fuel (25.4%), and building type (24.5%) were the most influential factors, highlighting their key roles in current sarcopenia prevalence. The high contribution of drinking water suggests that water safety may be an important determinant of prevalent sarcopenia, as water constitutes approximately 76% of muscle mass and intracellular water content is critical for maintaining protein synthesis and degradation balance. Non-tap water may contain heavy metals or pathogens, inducing chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation that impair muscle function [33]. In longitudinal analyses, the contribution of drinking water declined markedly to 1.5%, indicating that the detrimental effects of water contamination may accumulate over longer exposure periods. In contrast, the contribution of building type increased to 62.0%, emerging as a major predictor of incident sarcopenia, possibly due to its sustained influence on daily physical activity patterns [34]. Multi-storey buildings often require frequent stair climbing, which supports lower-limb muscle maintenance [35]; such effects can manifest within a relatively short follow-up period (4 years). The contribution of cooking fuel remained stable across both analyses (25.4% cross-sectionally, 26.5% longitudinally), suggesting that indoor air pollution is a persistent and long-term risk factor, with solid fuel combustion accelerating muscle protein degradation through systemic low-grade inflammation [36]. Moreover, this age‑by‑environment interaction was significant only in the cohort analysis (P = 0.037) but not in the cross‑sectional analysis, where the interaction was observed only for building type. This discrepancy may be partly explained by selective attrition and survival bias in the older subgroup during follow‑up, as well as by the application of uniform AWGS 2019 diagnostic thresholds, which likely underestimated baseline sarcopenia in middle‑aged adults and thus inflated their observed incidence, whereas prevalence estimates, reflecting long‑standing cases, were less affected.

This study has several strengths. First, it is based on a nationally representative CHARLS cohort with a large sample size and a 4-year follow-up, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Second, the combined cross-sectional and longitudinal design allows assessment of both prevalent associations and temporal relationships, strengthening causal inference. Third, we developed a comprehensive living environmental quality score encompassing multiple indoor and outdoor factors, capturing the complexity of coexisting environmental exposures. Fourth, we evaluated the relative contribution of individual environmental factors and compared differences between cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, providing evidence to inform targeted intervention strategies.

This study also has limitations. First, sarcopenia was assessed using an anthropometric equation to estimate muscle mass; although validated in Chinese populations and highly consistent with DXA measurements, it is not a gold standard. Second, living environmental quality was measured only at baseline and may not reflect dynamic exposure, potentially underestimating cumulative effects. Third, PM2.5 exposure was assessed at the city level, introducing potential measurement error, and indoor temperature relied on interviewer judgment without objective continuous measurements. Finally, certain variables known to be associated with sarcopenia, including physical activity, dietary intake, and household-level socioeconomic indicators, were not included in the main models. We prioritized a parsimonious model to avoid overadjustment and to preserve the interpretability of the environmental quality score, as some of these factors may lie on the causal pathway or share variance with environmental exposures. However, we acknowledge that their absence may introduce residual confounding, and future studies incorporating these variables are needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

Poor living environmental quality is independently associated with both prevalent and incident sarcopenia among middle-aged and older adults, exhibiting a clear dose–response relationship. These findings suggest that improving living environmental conditions—such as promoting clean household fuels, enhancing tap water infrastructure, and optimizing housing design—may represent effective public health strategies for sarcopenia prevention.
