Cost-effectiveness of a smartphone-based personalized lifestyle intervention for symptoms of depression in primary care: protocol for a cluster-randomized controlled trial (LIDIAS)
Authors: E M Jagtenberg, S A Ligthart, O G Muilwijk, R P Akkermans, M Spoelder, H Burger, O R Maarsingh, W J J Assendelft, T C olde Hartman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is common and mostly managed in primary care. In the Netherlands, general practitioners (GPs) and mental health nurses (GP-MHNs) generally start with education and lifestyle advice, yet the latter remains underused despite evidence of its effectiveness. Smartphone-based eHealth tools may help integrate lifestyle interventions into routine care by offering personalized support and monitoring. This study evaluates the (cost-)effectiveness of a Personalized eHealth Lifestyle Intervention (PLI) delivered via a smartphone app for patients with symptoms of depression in primary care.MethodsThis multicenter, cluster-randomized controlled trial will be conducted in Dutch general practices. Practices will be randomized (1:1), stratified by region and area-level socioeconomic status, into two groups: (1) intervention group including care as usual plus the PLI, or (2) control group including care as usual plus a monitoring app. Within the PLI, patients will use an app for 6 months to set lifestyle goals, monitor progress in lifestyle behavior, and track symptoms of depression and anxiety. GPs and GP-MHNs will have access to patient progress through a web-based version. Control group patients will use a similar app for symptom tracking only. The primary outcome is the change in severity of symptoms of depression assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Secondary outcomes include lifestyle behaviors, satisfaction with lifestyle, anxiety symptoms, patient satisfaction with treatment by GP and GP-MHNs, mental health-related quality of life, social support, use of antidepressants, use of anxiolytics, and utilization of medical and mental health services. Cost-effectiveness will also be evaluated. A total of 105 patients per arm (PHQ-9 ≥ 5) will be recruited.DiscussionThe results of this study will provide valuable insights into the clinical and economic impact of a digital lifestyle support tool. This trial aims to contribute to more accessible, personalized, sustainable, and cost-effective care for people with symptoms of depression in primary care.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07022184. Registered on June 22, 2025.Keywords: Lifestyle, eHealth, Depression, Randomized controlled trial, Primary care, Measurement-based care

Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

An estimated 280 million people worldwide suffer from depression, impacting their daily lives, surroundings, and broader society [1–3]. In the Netherlands, most patients with symptoms of depression are treated in primary care [4]. Typically, general practitioners (GPs) start their treatment with education and lifestyle advice, followed by psychological treatment and/or medication when necessary [5]. However, in practice, lifestyle advice remains underutilized in clinical practice [6].

Previous results report that lifestyle interventions can be effective in treating depression, sometimes yielding results comparable to medication or cognitive behavioral therapy [7–13]. Lifestyle interventions may focus on diet, physical activity, sleep quality, social engagement, stress management, and reducing harmful substance use. These behaviors play a crucial role in depression management, as unhealthy patterns may exacerbate symptoms and vice versa, creating a vicious cycle [14, 15].

To translate knowledge gained from lifestyle interventions into daily practice, practical tools for implementing lifestyle interventions in primary care are needed [16]. Digital technologies may help by offering personalized eHealth lifestyle interventions to reduce symptoms of depression [17]. Previous published reviews highlight the potential of eHealth lifestyle interventions as valuable adjuncts to traditional depression treatment [18–20]. Moreover, systematic monitoring of behaviors and symptoms, with feedback according to the measurement-based care (MBC) principle [21], is easily enabled by using eHealth tools. Previous studies in primary care settings show that monitoring daily behaviors can effectively promote lifestyle changes in people with depression [22, 23].

To date, a structured, evaluated, and effective digital framework for assessing lifestyle interventions targeting symptoms of depression in primary care is lacking. Here we present the protocol of a cluster-randomized controlled trial to evaluate the (cost-)effectiveness of a Personalized eHealth Lifestyle Intervention (PLI) to reduce symptoms of depression in patients treated in primary care.

Explanation for the choice of comparator {9b}

In this pragmatic trial, we compare the intervention group receiving care as usual (CAU) plus the PLI, with the control group receiving CAU plus symptom monitoring only. CAU is in accordance with Dutch GP guidelines [5]. In addition to CAU, both study groups use the Patient Journey App (PJA). In the intervention group, the PLI includes setting lifestyle goals, tracking progress, monitoring symptoms of depression and anxiety, and receiving tailored education and lifestyle-related interventions. GPs and GP-MHNs can access a web-based version of the app to monitor symptom and lifestyle goal progression, supporting follow-up consultations. Patients in the control group use the app only to track their symptoms of depression, and the GP and GP-MHN do not have web-based access.

Objectives {10}

Our primary objective is to evaluate whether a Personalized eHealth Lifestyle Intervention (PLI), delivered via a smartphone app, can (cost-)effectively reduce symptoms of depression among patients in general practice. Our secondary objectives are to assess the potential effects of the intervention on patient satisfaction with their lifestyle, lifestyle behaviors, symptoms of anxiety, patient satisfaction with treatment by GP and GP-MHN, mental health-related quality of life, social support, use of antidepressants and anxiolytics, and the utilization of medical and mental health services.

Methods: patient and public involvement, and trial design

Patient and public involvement {11}

(Former) patients with symptoms of depression, GPs, and GP-MHNs were involved in the development of the PLI. Additionally, an expert by experience contributed to the study design.

Trial design {12}

The LIDIAS study is a 6-month follow-up, cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT) in patients with symptoms of depression in general practice. This study is designed as a superiority trial, aiming to demonstrate that the intervention is more effective than the control condition. Patients will be recruited through their GP or GP-MHN in Dutch general practices. The general practices will be cluster-randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (CAU + PLI) or the control group (CAU + monitoring) prior to patient inclusion. The inclusion period covers 12 months, resulting in a total study duration of 18 months.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Trial setting {13}

The study is coordinated by three Dutch university medical centers: Radboud University Medical Center (Radboudumc, Nijmegen), Amsterdam University Medical Center (Amsterdam UMC), and University Medical Center Groningen (UMC Groningen). Radboudumc serves as the sponsor and lead center. Each site recruits general practices in its own region, mainly through established Dutch Academic General Practitioner Networks: (1) AHN, Dutch: Academisch Huisartsen Netwerk (Radboudumc), (2) ANHA, Dutch: Academisch Netwerk Huisartsgeneeskunde Amsterdam (Amsterdam UMC), and (3) AHON, Dutch: Academisch Huisartsen Ontwikkel Netwerk (UMC Groningen), collectively covering over 200 practices. In addition, general practices outside these networks and regions may also be invited to participate in the trial, depending on inclusion rates and the need to accelerate recruitment. This will be evaluated and implemented during the course of the study. Interested practices receive study information and training. GPs and GP-MHNs will screen and enroll patients.

Eligibility criteria for participants {14a}

Inclusion criteria for patients include experiencing a current episode of symptoms of depression or depression, as defined by the Dutch GP guidelines [5] and being 18 years or older. Patients are eligible if they have been receiving treatment for this current episode from their GP or GP-MHN for less than 6 months at the time of screening for inclusion. Previous episodes of depression do not exclude participation. Furthermore, patients are required to have a smartphone and be proficient in Dutch. Exclusion criteria include severe psychiatric comorbidity (such as psychosis or bipolar disorder), severe alcohol or drug addiction requiring specialized secondary care, high suicide risk, and moderate to severe cognitive impairment.

In this study, all patients presenting with symptoms of depression or a depression diagnosis, as clinically assessed by their GP or GP-MHN, are eligible to participate, regardless of symptom severity. This approach is based on two considerations. First, the clinician’s judgment is leading. When the clinician identifies symptoms of depression, this is regarded as a clinical diagnosis and serves as a key criterion for the potential effectiveness of the intervention. Second, administering a standardized severity questionnaire prior to enrollment was not feasible due to time constraints and the practical demands of routine consultations. For the primary analysis and sample size calculation, we will focus on patients with a baseline Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score ≥ 5, which indicates at least mild depression. This subgroup is expected to benefit most from the intervention, as they have greater potential for measurable improvement. The PHQ-9 is a validated, nine-item self-report questionnaire that assesses the severity of symptoms of depression [27].

Eligibility criteria for sites and those delivering interventions {14b}

All general practices are eligible to participate, regardless of size, patient volume, or socioeconomic status of their patients. GPs and GP-MHNs in the general practices will receive instructions and training on patient screening and inclusion.

Who will take informed consent? {32a}

If the GP or GP-MHN identifies an eligible patient according to the eligibility criteria, (s)he fills out the patient contact details via the Radboudumc study webform. These contact details include the patient’s name, email address, date of birth, and telephone number. The researcher will then provide the patient with the study information letter by email. Patients can reply on the email and inform the research team whether they have questions and/or want to receive the app activation link. If they do not reply to the email within 1 week, the research team will call the patient by phone in the subsequent week to answer questions and ask if they would like to receive the app activation link. After activating the app via the link in the email, patients follow several steps. First, in addition to the study information, the same information is presented again through short videos in the app. Second, patients provide digital informed consent (IC) via the app. This includes entering their name, date of birth, place of residence, and the current date.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {32b

Patients will be asked for optional consent to allow their study data to be made available for future research. This reuse will only occur after careful review by Radboudumc and provided that the data remains within the European Union. No biological specimens are collected or used in ancillary studies as part of this protocol.

Intervention and comparator

Intervention and comparator description {15a}

Shared features of the intervention and control group

Care as usual

Both groups receive CAU in line with Dutch GP guidelines, typically including (psycho-)education and, when appropriate, pharmacological treatment with antidepressants [5]. In the Netherlands, GPs act as gatekeepers for mental healthcare. They may refer patients to GP-MHNs for short-term support and treatment [28]. If further diagnostics or specialized care is needed, patients can be referred to mental health professionals such as psychologists or psychiatrists.

The Patient Journey App

In addition to CAU, both study groups will receive the PJA, which includes questionnaires related to the study outcomes and enables patients to monitor their symptoms of depression every 6 weeks using the PHQ-9. The course of the PHQ-9 scores is visualized in a graph, with a visual indication of the severity categorization of the score. Importantly, the app includes a disclaimer indicating that responses to the questionnaire are not actively monitored by the GP, the GP-MHN, or the research team. The app provides emergency contact details for situations that may require immediate assistance, such as suicidal thoughts. Additionally, the app features an email function allowing patients to contact the research team for (non-medical) support. The app operates through a timeline interface, which patients access directly upon login. Relevant components are pre-arranged on this timeline based on the individual patient’s needs. For example, once a questionnaire is completed, it disappears from the timeline. See Fig. 1 for an overview of the study group features.

Overview of the differences between the study groups

Additional features in the intervention group

In addition to CAU, the intervention group will receive PLI, i.e., a blended-care intervention that combines the use of the app by the patient with the involvement of the GP and GP-MHN. It encourages patients with symptoms of depression to improve their lifestyle and to track their symptoms.

The app was developed in collaboration with GPs, GP-MHNs, patients, experts by experience, and the software company Interactive Studios. Interactive Studios is a company which is specialized in developing digital healthcare monitoring applications and complies with the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR). The development process followed a participatory action research approach, involving interviews and focus groups with stakeholders to gather feedback and optimize the app. The Plan-Do-Check-Act principle was applied throughout, with the app being continuously developed, presented, and refined based on insights from subsequent interviews and focus groups [19]. To test the app’s feasibility, a pilot study was conducted in the region of Nijmegen, in which a total of 15 patients and experts by experience used the app for a period of 1–3 months. Feedback obtained during the pilot phase informed several additional revisions to the app, after which the final version was completed for use in the RCT.

The app integrates multiple components based on lifestyle interventions and the principle of measurement-based care [7–13, 21]. It addresses six key lifestyle domains: mental wellbeing, minimizing harmful substances, social relationships, nutrition, sleep, and physical activity. The GP or GP-MHN has access to a web-based version of the app, which provides insight into the patient’s lifestyle goals, progress, and symptom development. Both patients and their GP or GP-MHN can view the results of the PHQ-9, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) [29], and the Recovering Quality of Life questionnaire (ReQoL), which assesses the quality of life for people with different mental health conditions [30]. GPs and GP-MHNs receive email notifications when a patient completes a new questionnaire, inviting them to review the results via the web-based version of the app. This supports shared decision-making during follow-up consultations, including the formulation of lifestyle goals. Data from all other questionnaires are collected solely for research purposes and are not accessible to patients, GPs, or GP-MHNs. While patients, GPs, and GP-MHNs are advised to schedule at least one consultation after inclusion to define lifestyle goals, further use of the (web-based version of the) app and consultations is guided by individual preferences and usual care.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated intervention/comparator {15b}

The study will be discontinued for a patient if the patient requests this, or if the GP, GP-MHN, or researcher decides this is best for the patient.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention/comparator {15c}

To support adherence in both study arms, the PJA provides longitudinal feedback on symptoms of depression, visualized through graphs. This feedback may motivate patients to engage with the app and complete questionnaires. Additionally, GPs and GP-MHNs have access to the web-based version of the app and receive email notifications when patients complete questionnaires, allowing them to reinforce its use during follow-up consultations. In the intervention group, adherence is further supported through a personalized app interface and by engaging app content, including short videos and practical tips designed to boost motivation. Patients only see content and lifestyle tips tailored to their selected goals, increasing relevance and engagement. The app offers a broad range of lifestyle advice, aiming to make it applicable to most users. Additionally, GPs and GP-MHNs have access to the web-based version of the app and receive email notifications when patients complete questionnaires, allowing them to reinforce its use during follow-up consultations.

Concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {15d}

There are no restrictions in the intervention and control group regarding concomitant care, in line with the pragmatic nature of the study.

Ancillary and post-trial care {34}

In this study, CAU in general practice will be maintained in both intervention and control group. Therefore, post-trial care can be obtained or prolonged via the GP or GP-MHN of the patient. Given the 18-month study duration, patients who enroll early in the inclusion period may continue using the app independently beyond the initial 6-month follow-up. They can use the app for up to 12 months, with an optional extra assessment at that time, provided it occurs before the end of the study.

Socio-demographic factors

Age, gender, marital status, education level, daily occupation, country of birth, and nationality.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study is the severity of symptoms of depression, measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [27]. The PHQ-9 is a validated, 9-item self-reported questionnaire (score range 0–27) assessing the severity of depression symptomatology. The PHQ-9 is brief, easy to complete, and takes a few minutes to fill out. Patients will complete the PHQ-9 every 6 weeks via the PJA.

Secondary outcomes

Lifestyle behaviors

A set of self-constructed lifestyle questions is included, covering key lifestyle behaviors such as smoking, alcohol use, drug use, sleep, physical activity, and nutrition. Patients are asked whether they smoke (yes/no), and if so, how much; whether they consume alcohol (yes/no), and if so, the quantity; whether they use drugs (yes/no), and if so, how much; how many hours of sleep they typically get per night; whether they meet the Dutch Physical Activity Guidelines as defined by the Dutch national institute of Public Health and the Environment (in Dutch: RIVM) [32]; and whether they eat a varied and sufficient diet in accordance with the Netherlands Nutrition Centre (“the wheel of five”) [33].

Patient satisfaction with their lifestyle

In addition, we collect patients’ satisfaction with lifestyle domains: mental wellbeing, minimizing harmful substances, social relationships, nutrition, sleep, and physical activity, on a scale from 0 to 10. A score of 10 indicates that the patient is very satisfied with the lifestyle domain, while a score of 0 indicates very low satisfaction. The intervention is not designed to force behavioral change on pre-determined lifestyle factors, but rather to focus on the domains with which individuals are personally dissatisfied and motivated to improve. This approach enables, for instance, when a patient smokes, and he/she is currently content with smoking as part of their lifestyle, the patient can score his satisfaction with this lifestyle as high, e.g., 8 (even though smoking is not a healthy lifestyle).

Anxiety symptoms

To assess the severity of symptoms of anxiety, patients will complete the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a brief self-report questionnaire that captures how frequently they have experienced anxiety symptoms over the past 2 weeks [29].

Patient satisfaction with treatment by GP(-MHN)

The Consumer Quality Index (CQi), a 16-item questionnaire, will be used to evaluate various dimensions of satisfaction with the treatment received [34].

Quality of life

To evaluate mental health-related quality of life, the Recovering Quality of Life (ReQoL, 20-item version) questionnaire will be administered [35]. The ReQoL is a validated instrument designed specifically to assess mental health-related quality of life for people with different mental health conditions. This questionnaire contains 20 mental health items and one physical health question.

Social support

Perceived social support will be measured using the Social Support List-12 (SSL12), offering a view into the patients’ social environment and its potential impact on recovery [36].

Use of antidepressant and anxiolytics

Medication use regarding antidepressants and anxiolytics will be assessed at baseline and during follow-up using self-constructed questions.

Utilization of medical and mental health services

Visits to a psychologist, psychotherapist, psychiatrist, self-help group, GP or GP-MHN, mental health services, and stays in psychiatric institutions will be evaluated using the Treatment Inventory of Costs in Patients with psychiatric disorders (TIC-P) [37].

Covariates

Depression history

History of depression will be assessed using self-constructed questions.

Life events

Life events will be assessed using the 13-item List of Threatening Experiences (LTE) [38].

Psychiatric comorbidities

Psychiatric comorbidities will be explored using items from the Life Dissatisfaction and Adjustment Scale (LIDAS), which helps identify broader psychological challenges that may accompany depression [39].

Relationship with GP(-MHN)

The quality of the therapeutic relationship between the patient and their GP(-MHN) will be assessed using the Patient-Doctor Relationship Questionnaire-9 (PDRQ-9), which provides insight into the perceived support and connection within the treatment context [40]. In this study, the PDRQ-9 has been adapted to refer specifically to the GP or GP-MHN, rather than to “doctor” alone.

App usability

Satisfaction with the app will be measured using the mHealth App Usability Questionnaire (MAUQ), a validated tool designed to assess both usability and user experience in mobile health applications [41]. The domains of ease of use, satisfaction, and usefulness were specifically selected in the questionnaires used in this study.

App analytics

App metadata will be tracked and analyzed. The analytics include application usage data such as the frequency of app openings, the number of times patients access information, and which features or sections are most frequently used or viewed. We aim to analyze whether the app metadata correlates with the effectiveness of the PLI to reduce depression symptomatology.

Cost-effectiveness

The TIC-P will be used to assess medical care utilization and costs in order to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the PLI from a societal perspective [37]. The main outcome in the cost-effectiveness analysis is quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) based on the EuroQol-5D-5L (EQ-5D-5L). The EQ-5D-5L measures general quality of life [30].

Harms {17}

This study involves no invasive procedures and does not impose any treatment. Instead, only a supportive tool is provided alongside usual care of the GP or GP-MHN. The patient and GP or GP-MHN jointly manage the treatment plan. Therefore, we do not expect any adverse events or harms to occur.

Sample size {19}

For the calculation of the sample size, we aimed to detect a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) on PHQ-9 scores. The threshold for what constitutes a clinically meaningful effect size remains uncertain in the literature. Bauer-Staeb et al. reported a clinically significant difference with an effect size of 0.6, corresponding to a minimum clinically important difference of 3.7 points on the PHQ-9 scale (out of 27 points) [42]. However, we anticipate that our study population will present with less severe symptoms of depression compared to the population studied by Bauer-Staeb et al., as our sample consists of patients with symptoms of depression in primary care. Therefore, we expect a potentially smaller effect and adopted a more conservative approach, selecting an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.5. Incorporating these parameters into power analyses with α = 0.05 (two-sided) and a desired power of 0.80 (1 − β), we calculated a required minimum sample size of 64 patients per group (N = 128 total). However, we adjusted for several factors that may influence the required sample size: (1) repeated measurements of the primary outcome, (2) the cluster-randomized design, (3) anticipated participant dropout, and (4) variation in cluster size.

First, applying an adjustment for repeated measures with an assumed correlation between two measurements of 0.5 (using the formula (1 − r2) = (1 − 0.25) = 0.75) reduces the required sample size to 96 patients (rounded) for the two treatment arms [43]. To account for the cluster-randomized design, we used data from the Family Medicine Network (FaMe-Net), a Practice Based Research Network in the Netherlands (https://www.famenet.nl). FaMe-Net includes 6 GP practices, 30 GPs, and over 40,000 enlisted patients in 2022. The incidence of depression (ICPC code P76) in this data was 6.2 per 1000 patient-years [44]. Based on data from approximately 100 GP practices in the Radboudumc Family Medicine Network, each practice serves an average of about 4620 patients, resulting in roughly 27 new depression diagnoses per year. This approach is conservative because patients with symptoms of depression (ICPC code P03) are also eligible to participate in this study. Assuming one-third of these patients consent to participate, we expect to recruit about 9 patients per practice (range 5–15). Using an ICC of 0.06 [45], the design effect is 1.48, increasing the required sample size from 96 to 142 patients [46]. To further account for potential participant dropout (25%) [47], and variations in cluster size (with a 10% increase in sample size) [48], we determined that a final total sample size of 210 patients (105 per treatment arm) is required. This will involve the inclusion of at least 23 general practices, with an average of 9 patients recruited per practice.

Recruitment {20}

General practices will be recruited via mail, telephone, and in-person visits by the researcher to explain the study and invite to participate. To achieve the required sample size, in total, at least 30 general practices will be included. Patients with symptoms of depression will be recruited by their GP or GP-MHN. Inclusion can occur in two ways: (1) during a patient visit in which the GP or GP-MHN may identify symptoms of depression and ask the patient whether they are interested in participating in the study; (2) via a search in the electronic health record (e.g., using International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC) codes P03 or P76). Patients with relevant symptoms can be identified and invited to participate via telephone or letter. General practices will receive €25 compensation for each patient they include in the study. Additionally, recruitment is supported through printed and digital materials in waiting rooms.

Assignment of interventions: randomization

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}

In this RCT, randomization is conducted at the cluster level (i.e., general practices), rather than at the individual patient level. Cluster randomization is chosen to prevent contamination, as it would be challenging for GPs or GP-MHNs to consistently differentiate their treatment approach between patients assigned to the intervention or control group. Each participating general practice will be randomized within its respective region. The random allocation sequence will be generated centrally by an independent statistician not involved in recruitment or intervention delivery.

Sequence generation: type of randomization {21b}

The randomization schedule uses restricted randomization through random permuted blocks (block size of 4), stratified by the socioeconomic status (SES) of the practice’s location (high vs. low SES based on zip code) and the region of the practice.

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}

To ensure allocation concealment, the randomization sequence is generated and held by an independent statistician using a computer-generated sequence. The sequence is concealed from the research team and participating general practices prior to assignment. This process prevents foreknowledge of upcoming allocations, thereby minimizing selection bias.

Implementation {23}

After obtaining participatory consent from the general practice, the research team will submit the name of the practice and the average socioeconomic status of its patient population to an independent statistician. The statistician will conduct the randomization using a pre-specified stratified block randomization procedure. The research team and enrolling personnel will have no access to the random allocation sequence and only receive the allocation outcome after randomization.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}

After randomization, due to the nature of the intervention, GPs, GP-MHNs, patients, and researchers involved in recruitment and implementation cannot be blinded to treatment allocation, as it is evident which general practice, and therefore which patients, are assigned to the intervention or control group. Both groups receive their version of the app.

How will be blinding be achieved {24b}

Not applicable. This study will not involve any blinding.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

Not applicable. This study will not involve any blinding.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {25a}

All questionnaires related to primary and secondary outcomes are digitally integrated into the PJA, allowing patients to complete them at their own pace and from home. The only exception is the TIC-P, which will be administered by phone by a researcher at baseline and at 6 months. If a patient requires assistance in completing the questionnaires, a researcher will be available during working hours to provide support.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {25b}

To promote retention and ensure complete follow-up, several strategies will be applied. All patients receive reminders via the app to complete scheduled questionnaires, which are divided into five manageable blocks of 5 to 15 min each that can be filled out over several days. Push notifications are sent to encourage timely completion, and researchers monitor progress, sending reminders via email or phone when needed. At baseline and 6 months, a researcher will also call each participant to administer one questionnaire (the TIC-P) by phone. If the patient declines to use the app, we will request consent to contact them by phone or email at month 6 to administer only the PHQ-9, as this is our primary outcome measure. Since the general practices are part of regional research networks, communication resources such as newsletters and meetings are leveraged to support retention. Finally, patients in both groups receive a one-time financial compensation of €40 upon completing all questionnaires at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months.

Data management {26}

In this study, data is collected via the PJA platform, which is used by patients, GPs (and GP-MHNs), as well as the research team. Researchers use the platform to activate app accounts and grant the treating GP or GP-MHN access to their respective patients. The PJA platform collects personal data, app usage metadata and logs, outputs from symptom and goal tracking, and questionnaire-based outcome data. Access to the platform is strictly controlled: each research team at the participating academic centers, as well as each GP or GP-MHN, can only view data from their own patients via a personal login secured with two-factor authentication. Access to questionnaire-based outcome data is further restricted to the research coordination team at Radboudumc. This team can request raw data exports (e.g., Microsoft Excel files) or extract data directly at any time. All data is securely protected and inaccessible to external parties. Upon completion of the study (after 18 months), all data will be exported to secure Radboudumc servers. Subsequently, all data stored on the PJA platform will be permanently deleted.

Confidentiality {33}

Patients are assigned a unique identification code prior to data collection on the PJA platform. After exporting the data, these codes are recorded in a separate identification log, which is stored independently from the research data. Access to this log is strictly limited to the research team.

All research data are securely stored on Radboudumc servers and will ultimately be archived in the Radboudumc Data Repository (RDR). A comprehensive data management plan is included in the study protocol. In accordance with institutional guidelines, the data collected in this study will be archived for a period of 10 years following study completion.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize baseline characteristics including means, standard deviations, medians, ranges, counts, and percentages, as appropriate based on the distribution and scale of each variable. Stratification factors, such as socioeconomic status and region of general practices, will be included in all analyses.

All continuous variables and residuals will be visually inspected (Q-Q plot, histogram) and tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Logarithmic transformations will be applied when data is not normally distributed and subsequently the normality will be re-tested.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome is the severity of symptoms of depression (PHQ-9). Between-group differences (PLI + CAU vs. CAU) in mean PHQ-9 scores from baseline to 6-month follow-up will be assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). In this model, the PHQ-9 score at 6 months will serve as the outcome variable, with the baseline PHQ-9 score and potential confounders included as covariates. Statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05 (two-sided).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will be analyzed in the same manner as the primary outcome, using ANCOVA. These analyses will assess between-group differences in secondary outcomes (Table 2) over the 6-month period. Between-group differences in patient satisfaction with treatment by GP(-MHN) and lifestyle satisfaction at 6 months will be assessed using chi-square. Other variables will be described using descriptive statistics and, where relevant or feasible, included as covariates.

To assess changes in PHQ-9 scores over time using repeated measurements at each assessment time point, we will use a linear mixed-effects model that accounts for repeated measures and handles missing data under the missing-at-random or missing-completely-at-random assumptions. Fixed effects will include time (T0, T1, T2, optionally T3), study group, and their interaction (time × study group), with the interaction term representing the measure of effectiveness.

Cost-effectiveness

Cost data will be collected from a societal perspective using the TIC-P questionnaire. Cost categories include (1) healthcare utilization (primary, secondary, complementary, and home care), (2) productivity losses (absenteeism, presenteeism, unpaid work), and (3) patient costs (e.g., informal care). Valuation will follow Dutch costing guidelines, using standard costs and medication prices from the Royal Dutch Society for Pharmacy [49]. Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) will be calculated based on responses to the EQ-5D-5L. The friction cost approach will be applied to value absenteeism from paid work. Mean differences in costs, functioning, and QALYs between groups will be calculated over a 6-month time horizon, according to the intention-to-treat principle. Where applicable, incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) will be calculated by dividing incremental costs by incremental effects. Uncertainty will be addressed using non-parametric bootstrapping (1000 replications), with 95% confidence intervals derived via the percentile method. Results will be visualized using cost-effectiveness (CE) planes and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs), showing the probability of cost-effectiveness at various thresholds.

A budget impact analysis will be performed from a healthcare decision-maker perspective, following Dutch guidelines [50]. The cost-effectiveness results of the study will be projected over a 5-year period by using national data on the prevalence and incidence of the condition. The associated costs for healthcare services and treatments will be calculated based on the standardized tariffs set by the Dutch Healthcare Authority (in Dutch: Nederlandse Zorgautoriteit, NZa) [51]. The analysis will distinguish between incidental and structural costs (or savings) and estimate their budgetary impact.

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

All patients with a baseline PHQ-9 score ≥ 5 will be included in their respective group (intervention or control) in the primary and secondary analyses. Patients with a baseline PHQ-9 score ≤ 4 will also be included in the additional analysis.

How missing data will be handled in the analysis {27c}

All primary and secondary outcome analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Missing values will be imputed under the assumption that data are missing at random (MAR), with the missingness mechanism being assessed to confirm this assumption. Multiple imputation will be applied to handle missing data, using appropriate models to generate plausible values for missing observations based on the observed data. If treatment conditions differ in their distributions of potential confounders at baseline, despite randomization, these variables will be included as covariates in the analyses.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {27d}

Patients will be categorized into subgroups based on their baseline PHQ-9 scores. This analysis will explore whether the intervention effect differs across the severity groups. Furthermore, an exploratory analysis will be conducted to examine whether the intensity of app usage influences the effect of the intervention.

Not applicable as interim analyses are not planned for this study.

Discussion

This study aims to evaluate whether a Personalized eHealth Lifestyle Intervention (PLI), delivered via a smartphone app, can (cost-)effectively reduce symptoms of depression among patients in general practice. Both clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness will be evaluated by examining changes in, respectively, symptom severity and healthcare utilization, after 6 months of treatment.

To date, there is a clear gap in primary care regarding guidance for lifestyle interventions and the monitoring of symptoms of depression. Practical tools to support patients in managing lifestyle factors day-to-day alongside their mental health are lacking. The PLI addresses this need by offering a user-friendly, easily implementable solution that extends the role of the GP and GP-MHN. The PLI is grounded in the MBC principle, which emphasizes systematic monitoring of patient symptoms and progress to tailor treatment [21]. This approach supports healthcare providers by providing real-time insights into patients’ lifestyle adjustments and symptoms of depression, allowing for timely adjustments in care without increasing workload. By integrating patient-reported outcomes into routine care, the PLI helps GPs and GP-MHNs make informed decisions, improving treatment personalization.

Developed in close collaboration with patients, experts by experience, GPs, and GP-MHNs, the PLI is tailored to meet the needs and preferences of its users. It personalizes care by encouraging patients to reflect on their own lifestyle satisfaction and set meaningful, self-formulated goals together with their healthcare provider. This collaborative goal-setting fosters intrinsic motivation, making lifestyle changes more relevant and sustainable.

The blended-care format allows patients to engage with the intervention at their own pace and in their own environment. This is especially important in primary care, where many patients first present with symptoms of depression. The PLI can serve as a valuable fledged treatment step, before or next to medication and/or psychological treatment. In those needing specialized psychological care, the PLI serves as a supportive bridge during waiting periods. Additionally, the remote format may reduce healthcare costs and ease pressure on the system by promoting self-management outside traditional settings [20].

Ensuring accessibility is also a key priority. The app meets B1 language standards and is designed to be user-friendly, ensuring accessibility for patients from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, including those with limited literacy skills. To support this, study information is also presented through short videos, such as an introduction to the study and instructions on how to use the app. Practices participating in the study were selected from varied neighborhoods to enhance representativeness. Prior research shows digital interventions can effectively reach large populations and produce meaningful outcomes [20, 52, 53].

Nevertheless, this study presents several challenges. First, recruiting 210 patients with symptoms of depression in primary care may be difficult [54, 55]. To address this, we will collaborate with at least 35 general practices across the Netherlands and use both prevalent (via electronic health record searches) and incident (new consultations) sampling. Recruitment is supported through targeted outreach, printed and digital materials in waiting rooms, and direct invitations by GPs and GP-MHNs.

Second, because randomization occurs at the cluster level and the study is not blinded, there is a risk of selection bias: patients in the intervention group may be more open or receptive to lifestyle interventions.

Third, a key challenge concerns participant adherence and drop-outs [56]. Literature shows that dropout rates from studies of apps for depression can be nearly 50% when accounting for bias [57]. Patients may stop engaging or delete the app during the study period, leading to missing data. To minimize attrition, telephone consultations will be conducted by a research assistant at baseline and 6-month follow-up. Moreover, due to the blended-care aspect of this study, GPs and GP-MHNs may encourage ongoing participation. Studies found that digital interventions with therapeutic support are more effective than unguided versions [19, 57]. Furthermore, as an incentive, the control group will receive the lifestyle advice and tips included in the intervention app after the study is completed. Additionally, both groups will receive a small financial compensation for the time invested in completing the questionnaires [56]. Given the pragmatic nature of the RCT and the intention-to-treat analysis, the results will resemble effectiveness as in practice.

Fourth, for patients with symptoms of depression, engaging in any form of activity can be extremely difficult due to the effects of depression, such as low energy and lack of motivation. These barriers make it hard to initiate or sustain even small tasks, which can reinforce a cycle of inactivity that deepens symptoms of depression [15]. To address these challenges, our study focuses on setting small, achievable goals in collaboration with the GP(-MHN). Together they can break down tasks into manageable steps, making them feel more attainable and fostering a sense of accomplishment. In addition to breaking tasks down, personalization of the goals ensures that they resonate with the patient and are feasible within their daily routine [58]. However, despite these efforts, engaging patients on a personal level remains a challenge, as depression manifests uniquely in each individual.

In conclusion, the LIDIAS study will evaluate whether a smartphone-based personalized lifestyle intervention can (cost-)effectively reduce symptoms of depression in patients in primary care. Findings from this cluster-randomized trial will provide valuable evidence to guide the development, implementation, and optimization of digital tools for depression treatment.

Should the results prove positive, the next step will involve engaging with Dutch health insurers to explore financing options for broader implementation. Moreover, collaboration with other relevant (digital) platforms and stakeholders is sought to maximize reach and impact. In addition, versions in other languages, adapted to other healthcare systems, can be considered.
