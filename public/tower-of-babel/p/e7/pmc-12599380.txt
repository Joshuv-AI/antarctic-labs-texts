Hypercalcemia associated with relapsed medulloblastoma due to bone metastasis: illustrative case
Authors: Rikuto Ito, Masayuki Kanamori, Yoshiteru Shimoda, Masahiro Irie, Hidetaka Niizuma, Hirofumi Watanabe, Mika Watanabe, Yonehiro Kanemura, Atsuo Kikuchi, Hidenori Endo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMedulloblastoma can cause extraneural metastasis, particularly to the bones. However, only a few reports have shown that hypercalcemia occurs with medulloblastoma, and there are no reports on hypercalcemia causing consciousness disturbance.OBSERVATIONSA 17-year-old male patient was treated for medulloblastoma with spinal and intracranial metastasis. He underwent subtotal resection, followed by radiotherapy to the craniospinal axis, posterior fossa, and primary site, as well as high-dose chemotherapy with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. He achieved complete remission after the initial treatment. However, 30 months after tumor resection, he developed headache, nausea, fatigue, and pain in the limbs and back, followed by consciousness disturbance. He had severe hypercalcemia (17.7 mg/dL) and multiple bone metastases. His symptoms improved with hypercalcemia treatment, and salvage chemotherapy resulted in complete remission of the recurrent disease.LESSONSPrevious studies have not reported consciousness disturbance caused by hypercalcemia due to medulloblastoma bone metastasis. Hypercalcemia causes various symptoms, affecting the neurological, cardiac, and gastrointestinal systems. Some of its symptoms, such as fatigue, nausea, vomiting, and bradycardia, are similar to those of increased intracranial pressure. Therefore, hypercalcemia attributed to bone metastases should be considered in the differential diagnosis of consciousness disturbance during the course of medulloblastoma. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25318 Keywords: medulloblastoma, hypercalcemia, bone metastasis, consciousness disturbanceABBREVIATIONS: FDG = 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose; PET = positron emission tomography; PTH = parathyroid hormone; PTHrP = PTH-related peptide; T1WI = T1-weighted imaging; 1,25(OH)2D = 1,25-(OH)2 vitamin D

Medulloblastoma is one of the most common malignant brain tumors in children. It originates from the superior medullary velum and causes various symptoms such as cerebellar ataxia and cranial nerve palsy, which are caused by compression of the cerebellum and brainstem, as well as headache, nausea, and disturbance in consciousness, which are attributed to obstructive hydrocephalus. Recent advancements have led to significant improvements in imaging, surgical techniques, and postoperative chemotherapy and radiation therapy for managing patients with medulloblastoma.1 The 5-year event-free survival rate of patients with medulloblastoma in the average-risk group has been reported to be 83%.2 However, the prognosis of patients with recurrence remains poor.3 The 5-year event-free survival rate of patients with medulloblastoma and metastatic disease before treatment is 30%–35%.4,5 Based on clinical characteristics, molecular profiles, and type of treatment,3,6 recurrence can develop at the primary site, at an intraneural or extraneural distant site, or at both.3 Of these failure patterns, extraneural metastasis develops more frequently in medulloblastoma than in other primary CNS tumors.7,8 Bone metastasis is the most common type of extraneural metastasis in patients with medulloblastoma. It causes various symptoms, including pain, pathological fracture, neurological manifestations in the spinal cord and nerves, and hypercalcemia. Hypercalcemia is considered an oncological emergency because it can lead to lethal arrhythmia, pancreatitis, acute renal failure, and disturbance in consciousness.9 Therefore, this condition should be diagnosed sooner, and appropriate treatment must be immediately provided. However, only a few reports have described hypercalcemia in bone metastases from brain tumors.10–19

Herein, we present a rare case of medulloblastoma with bone metastases, in which hypercalcemia caused disturbance in consciousness.

Illustrative Case

A 17-year-old male patient with no significant medical, developmental, or family history presented to our institution with headache and nausea. He did not present with neurological deficits on admission. MRI revealed that the mass lesion was located in the fourth ventricle. The tumor appeared hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging and hypointense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI). Furthermore, it was partially enhanced on T1WI after gadolinium administration (Fig. 1A). The tumor had a low apparent diffusion coefficient (0.565 × 10−3 mm2/sec). Metastases were also present in the cerebellar hemisphere, cerebellar surface, left hippocampus, and subarachnoid space around the spinal cord and cerebellum on brain and spinal gadolinium-enhanced T1WI and diffusion-weighted imaging (Fig. 1B and C).

The patient underwent tumor resection via the cerebellomedullary approach. Postoperative MRI revealed a small residual lesion on the cranial side. Pathological examination with hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed that the tumor consisted of undifferentiated small round cells with a medullary structure. Brisk mitosis and Homer Wright rosettes were also observed (Fig. 1D). Immunohistochemical examination revealed that the tumor cells were positive for synaptophysin and integrase interactor 1. The Ki-67 labeling index was 62%. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with classic medulloblastoma.20 According to the molecular subgrouping of medulloblastoma using NanoString, the tumor was classified into group 4, WHO grade 4.20,21 MYCN amplification was not detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization. The patient underwent postoperative therapy according to the protocol for high-risk groups. He received craniospinal radiotherapy at a dose of 36 Gy and focal radiation therapy at a dose of 54 Gy. Hydrocephalus gradually progressed during radiation therapy, and the patient underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement 2 months after tumor resection. He received four courses of high-dose chemotherapy with autologous peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (SJMB96; day 1, 75 mg/m2 cisplatin and 1.5 mg/m2 vincristine; days 2 and 3, 2 g/m2 cyclophosphamide; day 5, peripheral blood stem cell with a dose of 2 × 106 CD34 cells/kg; and day 11, 1.5 mg/m2 vincristine).2 These initial treatments resulted in the complete remission of both the residual lesion on the primary site and the distant lesion. Thereafter, the patient was followed up regularly every 2 months. He did not exhibit any symptoms or abnormalities on brain and spinal MRI until 28 months after resection.

The patient presented with headache, nausea, general fatigue, and pain in the limbs and back 30 months after tumor resection at 20 years of age. He visited the emergency department of another hospital 2 days after the onset of these symptoms. He had disturbance in consciousness and complained of severe pain in his limbs and back. Brain CT imaging showed no evidence of recurrence. Based on the laboratory examination, the serum calcium and albumin levels were 17.7 mg/dL and 4.9 g/dL, respectively (Table 1). Systemic CT imaging revealed osteosclerotic and osteolytic changes in the vertebral bodies, pelvis, and ribs (Fig. 2A). The patient was diagnosed with hypercalcemia due to bone metastasis and treated with saline hydration, furosemide, elcatonin, and zoledronic acid. He was referred to our hospital the next day. On admission, he was drowsy and complained of severe nausea and systemic pain. Laboratory examination showed that the serum calcium and albumin values were 15.7 mg/dL and 3.8 g/dL, respectively (Table 1). The inorganic phosphorus value was elevated. The 1,25-(OH)2 vitamin D [1,25(OH)2D], calcitonin, and whole parathyroid hormone (PTH) values were 10, 4.91, and 5.8 pg/mL, respectively. The intact PTH-related peptide (PTHrP) values were normal (Table 1). The treatment for hypercalcemia was continued, and disturbance in consciousness and hypercalcemia improved 4 days after diagnosis. MRI showed no evidence of brain and spinal recurrence. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) revealed a significant uptake of FDG in the vertebral bodies, pelvis, ribs, femur, and humerus (Fig. 2B). Histological examination of the bone marrow obtained via iliac crest puncture revealed the presence of small round cells, resembling the primary lesion (Fig. 2C). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with bone and bone marrow metastasis from medulloblastoma. He received six cycles of combination chemotherapy consisting of intravenous temozolomide 100 mg/m2/day (days 1–5) and irinotecan hydrochloride hydrate 20 mg/m2/day (days 1–5 and 8–12) every 3–4 weeks.22 Salvage therapy was effective in relieving pain and led to complete remission of multiple bone metastases on FDG-PET.

Discussion

Observations

Herein, we report a rare case of medulloblastoma with hypercalcemia secondary to bone and bone marrow metastasis. The patient presented with headache, nausea, vomiting, appetite loss, and pain in the limbs and back, followed by disturbance in consciousness attributed to hypercalcemia. The patient’s symptoms significantly improved with the early diagnosis of hypercalcemia and rapid treatment. In this study, the clinical features of extraneural recurrence of medulloblastoma and the basic mechanism and clinical features of hypercalcemia for managing medulloblastoma were discussed.

At recurrence, the patient had extraneural metastases without local recurrence at the primary site or intraneural distant metastases. The proportion of patients with intraneural distant lesions increases at the time of recurrence. The rates of intraneuronal distant recurrence in patients with average-risk medulloblastoma in the HIT-SIOP-PNET4 study and all patients with medulloblastoma in the literature review were 82% and 91%, respectively.23,24 In addition to intraneural distant recurrence, extraneural distant recurrence was observed in 7.1% of patients with recurrence.25 It occurred more frequently than in patients with germ cell tumors, glioblastoma, ependymoma, and atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumors.7,8 In 2010, Mazloom et al. reported a literature review of 119 cases with extraneural metastasis from medulloblastoma between 1961 and 2007.26 A total of 36 additional cases were reported after their review, and 1 case from 1993 was also identified.8,16,27–37 Adolescents and young adults are at high risk of developing extraneural recurrence. The median age at initial treatment was 14.0 years,26 which is older than the typical median onset age of 9 years for medulloblastoma.20 The incidence of extraneural recurrence was as high as 36%–48% in a study on adult medulloblastoma.36,38 Extraneural metastases commonly developed during recurrence, and only 5 of 145 patients (3.4%) had extraneural metastasis at the initial presentation.16,26–42 Concomitant intraneural recurrence has been reported in 36%–49% of the recurrent cases,26,39 and these patients had a poorer prognosis than those without intraneural recurrence.26 Factors such as the use of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt8,43 and not undergoing chemotherapy during the initial treatment6 are associated with an increased risk of extraneural medulloblastoma. The combination of chemotherapy and radiation therapy has been reported to reduce extraneural metastases.6 Furthermore, early chemotherapy before radiation therapy may prevent recurrence outside the CNS. In the POG 9031 trial conducted in North America, the standard treatment, that is, chemotherapy with cisplatin and etoposide after radiation therapy, was compared with the experimental treatment, that is, chemotherapy with cisplatin and etoposide before and after radiation therapy, for patients with high-risk medulloblastoma, and no difference in event-free survival rate were observed.44 However, bone metastasis occurred in 3 of 112 patients (2.7%) in the standard treatment group and in none of the patients in the experimental treatment group.44 Recent reports have revealed the histological and molecular profiles of medulloblastoma with extraneural metastasis.30,32,35,39,41,45 The predominant histological diagnosis was desmoplastic or large cell/anaplastic medulloblastoma.35,39 The molecular subgroup was the sonic hedgehog group in 6 reports30,35,41 and group 3 in 3 reports.35,45 Two cases presented with MYCN amplification.32,45 Considering these previous findings, our case presented with risk factors including the age of onset and use of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt for extraneural metastasis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a patient with group 4 medulloblastoma who developed extraneural metastasis. These findings indicate that the risk of extraneural recurrence should be examined based on clinical findings and integrated diagnosis. Moreover, regular examinations with FDG-PET must be performed in patients at high risk for extraneural recurrence.

The patient presented with acute symptoms attributed to hypercalcemia. Hypercalcemia is defined as an increase in the serum calcium level above the upper limit of the reference range. It can cause various mild to severe symptoms in different organ systems. Patients with mild to moderate hypercalcemia present with digestive system disorders (such as anorexia, constipation, nausea, and vomiting), urinary system disorders (such as polyuria and polydipsia), neuronal system disorders (such as muscle weakness, fatigue, and hyporeflexia), and cardiovascular system disorders (including electrocardiographic changes such as shortened ST segments and QT intervals, widened T waves, bundle branch block, and depressed ST segments).9 Severe hypercalcemia (> 14 mg/dL) can lead to lethal acute pancreatitis, acute renal failure, disturbance in consciousness, cardiac arrest, arrhythmia, and ventricular tachycardia.9 Physiologically, serum calcium levels are regulated by PTH, 1,25(OH)2D, and calcitonin via their action on the bone, kidney, and intestine.9 In patients with cancer, hypercalcemia is caused by increased bone resorption and insufficient renal clearance of calcium via one of the following four mechanisms: PTHrP, bone metastasis, excessive production and activation of extrarenal 1,25(OH)2D, and ectopic PTH.9 Hypercalcemia occurs in 10%–30% of patients with cancer, particularly in multiple myeloma, breast cancer, and renal cancer.9 PTHrP and bone metastasis account for 80% and 20% of all hypercalcemia cases in patients with cancer, respectively. Meanwhile, the frequency of excessive production and activation of extrarenal 1,25(OH)2D as well as ectopic PTH is low. In contrast to studies on patients with cancer, only a few reports have described the occurrence of hypercalcemia during treatment for primary brain tumors. Thus far, hypercalcemia has been reported in 10 patients (Table 2). The age at hypercalcemia onset ranged from neonate to 45 years. The histological diagnoses were medulloblastoma in 4 patients,11–13,16 malignant glioma in 4,10,14,15,19 germinoma in 1,18 and congenital craniopharyngioma in 1 patient.17 Hypercalcemia was reported to develop before treatment in 3 patients, after resection in 2, and at recurrence 12 months to 8 years after resection in 5. The symptoms at the time of hypercalcemia diagnosis were general fatigue, nausea, or anorexia in 4 patients;12,13,15,18 polyuria in 2;12,13 and constipation in 1 patient.16 In addition, 6 patients presented with pain due to bone metastasis. Seven patients presented with bone and bone marrow metastasis,11–16,19 whereas 3 did not.10,17,18 Two of 4 patients exhibited elevated PTHrP levels.13,17 The combination of hypocortisolism, central diabetes insipidus, hyperthyroidism,18 and elevated 1,25(OH)2D10 levels has been reported to cause hypercalcemia in patients without bone and bone marrow metastasis.

In this case, hypercalcemia was primarily attributed to bone and bone marrow metastasis, as evidenced by extensive bone metastasis and high calcium and inorganic phosphorus levels, without elevated 1,25(OH)2D, calcitonin, whole PTH, or PTHrP levels. Headache, nausea, and disturbance in consciousness were caused by hypercalcemia, and pain in the limbs and back by bone metastasis. These symptoms are similar to those of elevated intracranial pressure, diffuse dissemination, or spinal dissemination. There was only 1 case report showing disturbance in consciousness secondary to hypercalcemia due to a metastatic bone lesion from anaplastic oligodendroglioma.15 However, screening for hypercalcemia should be considered in patients with these acute symptoms.

Lessons

The patient developed nonspecific symptoms such as headache, nausea, and vomiting, followed by acute disturbance in consciousness and severe pain in the limbs and back. This clinical course was comparable to that of recurrent disease with increased intracranial pressure and spinal metastasis. The similarity can lead to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment of hypercalcemia. For the early detection of hypercalcemia, regular monitoring and timely measurement of serum calcium levels should be included in the follow-up examination.
