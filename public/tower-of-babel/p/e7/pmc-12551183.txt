Two unusual cases of tongue schwannoma with self-injurious factors: case reports and systemic literature review
Authors: Gefei Du, Jiali Zhang, Xinming Chen, Rui Lu, Jing Zhang, Enno Schmidt, Gang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present two cases of tongue schwannoma in two young males. The unusual, exogenetic clinical manifestation might be a big challenge for most dentists in making a correct diagnosis. The two patients had no special genetic or environmental background. Both patients denied cigarette smoking or alcohol abuse. Physical examination of the cervical lymph nodes yielded negative results. Their astonishing medical histories revealed that both had self-injurious practices using sharp instruments. The diagnosis of tongue schwannoma was confirmed by histopathology, revealing typical Antoni type A and B areas, and reactivity with S-100 by immunohistochemistry. The lesions were excised transorally under local anesthesia with no signs of recurrence for more than two years.

Introduction

Schwannoma, also called as neurilemmoma, is a rare benign nerve sheath neoplasm originating from Schwann cells that comprise myelin sheaths surrounding peripheral nerves [1–4]. The tumor normally consists of solitary, slow-growing, epineurium-encapsulated soft tissue, with a predilection for the head and neck region. These tumors can arise from the cranial nerves (except for the optic and olfactory nerves), spinal nerves, and autonomic nervous system. It has been reported that approximately 25–45% of all schwannomas are located extra-cranially in the head and neck region, but only 1% of those occur in the oral cavity [5, 6]. The tongue is the most common location in the oral cavity, although it can occur anywhere in the intraoral sites, including the palate, buccal mucosa, lip, and gingivae [7, 8]. After complete resection, schwannomas seldom recur and undergo malignant transformations. It is widely accepted that schwannomas occur equally in both sexes between the second and fifth decades of life [7, 8]. Herein, we present schwannomas in two young males with extremely unexpected clinical manifestations and self-injurious practices.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 29-year-old male was referred to the Department of Oral Medicine, School and Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University, with the chief complaint of a slowly enlarging mass in the left lateral part of the tongue. Clinical examination revealed an unusual, exophytic lesion on the left lateral-ventral surface of the tongue. The mass was palpated as a moderately firm mass with a total size of 4.0 cm×3.0 cm×2.0 cm, including an exogenous part (1.5 cm×1.5 cm×1.0 cm). The whitish, cauliflower-like safarisurface was surrounded by an erythematous area (Fig. 1A). The patient’s medical history was unremarkable, and no family history was found. The patient later reported that the mass had been asymptomatic, for approximately two years. He had recently tried to snip the mass, with a pair of scissors by himself, but it grew even larger with mild pain. The patient had no specific genetic or environmental background. Cigarette smoking and alcohol abuse were denied. Physical examination of the cervical lymph nodes yielded negative results.

According to its astonishing appearance, the primary diagnosis could be provisionally established as a neoplasm of the tongue, and suspicion of verrucous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) or oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMD) could not be ruled out. An incisional biopsy was performed under local anesthesia, followed by histopathological and immunohistochemical examinations.

Microscopic findings showed both Antoni type A and B lesions. In the Antoni type A areas, dense elongated spindle cells with wavy nuclei were arranged as palisades, named as Verocay bodies (Fig. 1C). They were circumscribed by a thin, well-defined fibrous connective tissue capsule. In the Antoni type B areas, less cellular and more randomly arranged spindle cells were found in a loose myxoid stroma (Fig. 1E). Intense positivity against S-100 protein (ZM-0224, Zhongshan Goldenbridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) was detected by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1G, I).

Case 2

A 24-year old male was reported to suffer by a `painless blister on the tongue for more than one year before referred to the Department of Oral Medicine, School & Hospital of Stomatology of Wuhan University. Clinical examination revealed that the lesion was not a blister but an exophytic mass near the right lateral part of the tongue (Fig. 1B). The total size of the small, soft lesion was about 0.5 cm*0.5 cm*0.5 cm, covered by yellowish pseudomembrane with erythematous area around. Despite the appearance of the exogenetic mass was unusual, physical examination of the cervical lymph nodes was unremarkable. The patient had no special genetic or environmental background, and no history of cigarette smoking or alcohol abuse. The patient later disclosed that he had pricked the lesion with a needle one week before, and then it became smaller with a small amount of fluid. Subsequently, it gradually grew to the same size as before again.

The erythematous area and exophytic appearance suggested a primary diagnosis of verrucous or a warty neoplasm of the tongue. Excisional biopsy of the mass was performed under local anesthesia.

Histological examination revealed hypercellular Antoni type A areas with palisading nuclei and Verocay bodies as well as myxoid hypocellular Antoni type B areas. Immunohistochemical staining for the S-100 protein (ZM-0224, Zhongshan Goldenbridge Biotechnology Beijing, China) was positive (Fig. 1D, F, H, J).

Based on the medical history, clinical examination, histological and immunohistochemical observation, the definite diagnosis was tongue schwannoma for these two unusual cases. Both patients were treated by transoral resection under local anesthesia and followed up at three months interval. The patients were recurrence-free for more than two years.

We performed a literature search on PubMed from January 1st 2000, to October 31st 2023, using the following search terms: schwannoma, neurilemmoma, tongue and lingual. Only English and Chinese literatures was included. A total of 144 case reports were found, with 177 potentially relevant cases (Fig. 2). Thirty-nine schwannomas with other parts, but not the tongue, in the oral cavity, 19 hypoglossal schwannomas, 11 schwannomas in the craniocerebral areas, 7 schwannomas in the laryngeal or neck areas, and 7 other tumors resembling schwannoma were later excluded after reviewing all 144 case reports (Fig. 2). Finally, 94 cases of tongue schwannomas (including the oral tongue and base tongue) were confirmed in the present study. Interestingly, case reports of tongue schwannoma were most frequent in PubMed between 2005 and 2014. However, there were no significant differences (P > 0.05) in the reported number of tongue schwannomas on PubMed every five years, from 2000 to 2023 (Table 1). Schwannomas occurred significantly more often in the oral tongue (P < 0.05) than in the base tongue (Table 1). More than half of the schwannomas (19 of 32) were localized at the base of the tongue and were invisible in the oral cavity (Table 1). These concealed schwannomas were detected using computerized tomography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Tongue schwannomas are often diagnosed in relatively younger populations (Fig. 3), with a mean age of 27.7 ( range, 7–77 years ). Moreover, no significant differences (P > 0.05) were found between the mean age of oral tongue and base tongue schwannoma cases in PubMed (Table 1). Overall, the sex predilection for tongue schwannomas was not clear (Table 1). No significant differences (P > 0.05) were found in the number of tongue schwannomas (oral tongue and base tongue) between the sexes (Table 1).

Discussion

Schwannomas are rare benign tumors of the oral cavity [9]. Usually, they are not in the mind of a dentist for differential diagnosis. Although some of these tumors may cause dysphagia, dysarthria, and obstructive sleep apnea when they extend to a significant size, schwannomas are usually asymptomatic [9–11]. The identification and treatment of schwannomas may be delayed, because they are often neglected. Here, we aimed to provide some useful epidemiological information to dentists by reviewing literatures in PubMed focusing upon tongue schwannomas.

Tongue schwannoma cases in PubMed are particularly limited. Our present study also supports the view of other researchers that tongue schwannomas affect both sexes equally. Among 94 tongue schwannomas, the number of male cases (49 cases, 53.3%) was not significantly (P > 0.05) higher than that of female cases (43 cases, 46.7%), and gender information was not available in 2 cases (Table 1). To date, there is no consensus on the predilection age of tongue schwannomas in the general population [12–16]. According to the published data on PubMed from 2000 to 2023, tongue schwannoma tends to occur in younger adults in the second decade of life (Fig. 3), most often at the mean age of approximately 30 (27.7 ± 15.4, Table 1), which was confirmed in our reported cases, 29 and 24 years old, respectively.

Differential diagnosis of a mass in the tongue is complex. Tongue schwannomas often develop beneath intact mucosa and occur as solid masses with an oval shape and regular margins [17, 18], without ulceration or exogenous presentations. However, the clinical manifestations in these two cases were different from those of common schwannomas. Both lesions were traumatized and the exogenous mass grew outside the surface of the tongue. The whitish appearance was suggestive of oral white lesions (e.g. oral leukoplakia). It resembles oral malignant lesions located in a high-risk areas. This unusual manifestation may lead dentists to diagnose it as oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) or OPMD, which often presents as ulceration with raised exophytic or fissuring margins [19]. Head and neck cancer (about half of which were OSCC) ranked the 7th most common cancer worldwide, affected around 890,000 new cases annually, and caused 450,000 deaths (450,000 / 890,000 = 50.6%) in 2018 [20]. It is such a heavy health burden among the general population, especially in developing countries. Even with advances in diagnosis and treatment in recent years, the annual motality of cancer in lip and oral cavity was still high (177,757 / 377,713 = 47.1%) in 2020 [21], and the recurrent or/and metastatic rate was more than 65%(20). Early detection of OSCC need thorough history collection and physical examination, and long-term follow-ups of OPMDs, which are any oral mucosal abnormalities associated with statistically increased risk of developing oral cancer [22]. OPMD is a group of mucosal disorders that may precede the diagnosis of OSCC, including leukoplakia, proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL), erythoplakia, oral submucous fibrosis (OSF), oral lichen planus (OLP), actinic keratosis (actinic cheileitis), palatal lesions in reverse smokers, oral lupus erythematosus (OLE), dyskeratosis congenita, oral lichenoid lesions (OLLs) and oral graft versus host disease (OGVHD) [22]. OPMDs may manifest a wide range of clinical features, such as color changes (white, red or both), topographic variarations (plaque-like, smooth, corrugated, verrucous or granular), and unusual sizes. Some clinical characters of the present two cases were similar to OSCCs or OPMDs, while the age of two cases were not consistent with the majority (often middle-aged or elderly) of OPMDs.

The following three aspects helped us make a differential diagnosis. First, the masses had enlarged slowly for approximately one or two years. In contrast, OSCC and OPMD develop in the tongue and usually progress to involve the surrounding tissues, such as the ventral part of the tongue, floor of mouth or adjacent gingival tissues. Second, the two patients complained of no limitation of tongue movement, while the lesions continued to extend. Third, no enlargement of the cervical lymph nodes was detected during the entire progress. Other benign tumors may not be excluded, such as neurofibromas, myofibromas, and leiomyomas, etc. The differential diagnoses for these diseases are shown in Table 2. These two cases were not confirmed as neoplasms of the nervous tissues until histological examination was performed [17, 23, 24].

Histologically, most schwannomas are unilocular masses surrounded by a capsule of epineurium and residual nerve fibers, often with the peripheral nerve attached to the edge of the neoplasm [23–25]. Two cellular patterns beneath the capsule were named Antoni type A and B [25, 26]. They are seen as intermingled but sharply defined. In the Antoni type A pattern, compact spindle cells with twisted nuclei are arranged in bundles of fascicles. In highly differentiated areas there may be nuclear palisading and the formation of Verocay bodies, which are formed by cytoplasmic fibrils between two rows of nuclei and cell processes, assuming oval-shaped acellular, eosinophilic masses. Antoni type B areas are meshworks with far less cellular areas, representing degenerated Antoni type A areas. They are composed of less orderly arranged spindle or oval cells in a loose myxoid stroma, punctuated by microcysts, inflammatory cells, and delicate collagen fibers [25, 26]. In our two cases, histological examination revealed typical Antoni type A and B areas but failed to indicate a complete capsule of the epineurium. This may be due to the traumatic factors in the lesion. Moreover, both of the two cases showed no signs of epithelial dysplasia, which are regarded to indicate an increased risk of malignant transformation. Epithelial dysplasia means abnormal growth, describing histological changes without clinical morphololgical equivalents [27]. However, there might be a gradual accumulation in mutations and chromosomal derangements in dysplastic lesions, including cytological atypia and architectural changes, until finally, these dysplastic lesions transform into malignancies. Thus suspicion of OPMD or OSCC in our two cases were definitely excluded through histological examination.

The Antoni type A and B areas are used strictly for pathological determination of the tumor and have no clinical significance. Immunohistochemical expression of S-100 is used for the identification of the Schwann cells because it is often found in both the central and peripheral nervous systems [25, 26, 28, 29]. Our staining also revealed diverse positive S-100 staining, which indicated that the lesion originated from nervous tissues.

Although the clinical diagnosis of schwannoma is complicated, recent studies have suggested that MRI increases the possibility of a correct diagnosis. Schwannomas in the tongue are isointense to muscle on T1-weighted images and homogeneously hyperintense on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted or T2-weighted images [30]. In the present report, MRI was not performed because the lesion was superficial, and it was convenient to perform an incisional biopsy for histopathological diagnosis.

Schwannomas show no recurrence if completely excised [31]. Transoral resection remains the standard approach for treating these tumors [12, 13]. Transoral carbon dioxide laser extirpation has also been introduced. All these methods can provide complete tumor removal. Reported malignant transformations are rare. In young patients as the present two male cases, sufficient care is required for preservation of normal speech, during the procedure of complete excision of tumor. It is still controversial whether surgical excision of the involved nerve segment or conservative management should be the choice of treatment when schwannoma appears near an important nerve such as the facial nerve. Graft replacement of the affected nerve has also been introduced to reconstruct nerve function and improve palsy.

Conclusions

The unique traumatic history of these two cases resulted in complicated clinical manifestations, which hindered a definite diagnosis. Both histopathological examamination and immunohistochemical analysis are essential for the diagnosis of schwannoma. Slow growing, painful or painless lumps in the oral cavity are frequently found in dental practice, and the differential diagnosis should include seldom occurring schwannomas.
