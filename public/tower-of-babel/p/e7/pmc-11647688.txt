Metabolic pitfall of pseudoprogression after epcoritamab treatment
Authors: Osamu Imataki, Shumpei Uchida, Makiko Uemura
Published: 2024

“Pseudoprogression” is a characteristic phenomenon observed during immunotherapy, including immunomodulatory drugs, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR‐T) therapy, in which, initially, tumor lesions may appear to worsen, but they subsequently respond favorably. To differentiate between pseudoprogression and true progression, it is essential to assess the pathologic status. However, a biopsy to confirm a pathological diagnosis is not always performed to evaluate the viability of residual lesions because of its invasive procedure. In the recent topic of bispecific T cell engager therapy, epcoritamab, we feel difficulty in estimating treatment efficacy by 18‐fluoro‐FDG‐positron emission tomography (18F‐FDG‐PET) especially in a case of pseudoprogression.

A 62‐year‐old man with diffuse large B cell lymphoma primarily occurring in bilateral adrenal glands, accompanied by multiple skin lesions, received epcoritamab as forth‐line treatment in a recurrence condition after CAR‐T therapy. One month after the initial epcoritamab administration, multiple skin lesions regrew. We confirmed an optimal immune response in one of the skin lesions by skin biopsy (Figure 1A), which implied pseudoprogression pathologically. Even after four cycles of epcoritamab therapy, 18F‐FDG‐PET showed multiple hot spots (maximum standardized uptake values [SUVmax] was 6.03) (Figure 1B) consistent with initial skin lesions. All the skin lesions had been previously documented before epcoritamab therapy. We performed a second skin biopsy, and the pathological complete response was confirmed again (Figure 1C). This suggested that pseudoprogression may manifest as stable metabolic FDG‐avid mass even after achieving pathological remission. The patient underwent allogeneic stem cell transplantation while in complete remission following four cycles of epcoritamab therapy. The patient has been still free from therapy for six months post‐transplantation.

The patient's skin lesion detected pathologically and metabolically. (A) The first skin biopsy after the first course of epcoritamab therapy (on day 30). The pathological finding showed lymphocyte infiltration in subcutaneous tissue. CD20+ lymphoma cells were surrounded by CD3+ lymphocytes. (B) On day 26 after the third course of epcoritamab therapy, 18‐fluoro‐FDG‐positron emission tomography (18F‐FDG‐PET) revealed an FDG‐avid spot in all the preexisting lesions in the patient. Remarks: *biopsy site (C) The second skin biopsy after the four courses of epcoritamab therapy (on day 7). CD20+ lymphoma cells were eliminated completely, and CD3+ cells still remained.

It's important to recognize that 18F‐FDG‐PET imaging alone is not sufficient for making crucial clinical decisions during immunotherapy. Other factors and assessments should be considered to ensure accurate interpretation and informed decision‐making. Nevertheless, the clinical implications of this case suggest that a clinically and metabolically stable mass for months may possibly indicate pseudoprogression rather than true progression.
