Spatial single cell transcriptomic analysis informs tumor developmental hierarchy of DICER1 syndrome related sarcoma
Authors: Felix K F Kommoss, Joyce Yu Han Zhang, Branden J Lynch, Shary Yuting Chen, Janine Senz, Yana Moscovitz, Lesley A Hill, Ding Ma, R Wilder Scott, Jonathan Bush, Kenneth S Chen, Andrew Roth, Andreas von Deimling, William D Foulkes, Gregg B Morin, T Michael Underhill, Yemin Wang, David G Huntsman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDICER1-related tumor predisposition (DRTP), also known as DICER1 syndrome, encompasses a spectrum of malignancies mainly in children and young adults. Most are sarcomas, exhibiting histological and molecular similarities regardless of their anatomical origins, and only express the RNase IIIb domain-defective DICER1. To uncover their cellular origin and developmental hierarchy, we establish a lineage-traceable genetically engineered mouse model with controlled activation of hemizygous Dicer1 RNase IIIb mutation in Hic1+ mesenchymal stromal cells. This causes renal tumors closely mirroring the developmental continuum of human DRTP sarcoma histologically and molecularly. Spatial single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals a Hic1+Pdgfra+ Dpt+Pi16+ fibroblastic progenitor population, corresponding to universal fibroblasts subjacent to transitional epithelium of renal collecting ducts, that can undergo rhabdomyoblastic differentiation or become proliferative sarcomatous cells. Investigation of patient samples identifies analogous cell states and developmental trajectories. This study uncovers a fibroblastic origin for DRTP sarcoma and provides a faithful mouse model for future mechanistic and translational investigation.Subject terms: Cancer models, Cancer genetics

Introduction

DICER1-related tumor predisposition (DRTP) (MONDO: 0100216), also known as DICER1 syndrome, is caused by germline pathogenic variants (PVs) in DICER1 that predisposes children and young adults to cancer1,2. This includes several tumor types which arise at distinct anatomical locations, including lung, kidney, gynecologic tract, and central nervous system, among other rarer sites1–3. Germline DICER1 PVs typically inactivate the affected allele4. In DRTP, tumor development is usually associated with the occurrence of a hotspot missense mutation on the second DICER1 allele, all of which result in the production of RNase IIIb catalytic domain-dead DICER1 protein5. This compound heterozygous state of DICER1 leads to impaired processing of pre-miRNAs and subsequent loss of mature miRNAs derived from their 5p-strands (5p-miRNA)5–7.

Although arising in different anatomical sites, it has long been noted that many DRTP cancers share key histomorphological features8. This includes subepithelial condensation of malignant mesenchyme (cambium layer), as well as frequent rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, resembling rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS). This overlap has raised important questions about whether these morphologically similar tumors arising at different sites constitute a common tumor entity. Recently, we addressed this hypothesis by studying a large series of DICER1 syndrome-associated cancers9. Our study identified shared clinicopathological, genetic, and epigenetic (DNA methylation) features in a distinct group of DICER1 syndrome-associated mesenchymal neoplasms. This group encompasses well established tumor entities and comprises three clinically meaningful classes termed low-grade mesenchymal tumor with DICER1 alteration (LGMT DICER1), sarcoma with DICER1 alteration (SARC DICER1), and primary intracranial sarcoma with DICER1 alteration (PIS DICER1). While our findings suggest a common cellular origin for DRTP sarcoma, the underlying developmental biology and processes involved in DICER1-mediated tumor cell transformation remains largely unknown.

Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMM) are powerful tools for studying tumor development in a cell type specific manner. We have recently developed a conditional Dicer1 RNase IIIb mutant allele (Dicer1+/f-D1693N)10, which, upon Cre-mediated recombination, expresses the mouse p.D1693N mutant Dicer1 (Dicer1D1693N) that is equivalent to human p.D1709N mutant DICER1, one of the most common RNase IIIb mutations in human patients1. With this tool strain and a Dicer1fl/fl strain, we also developed a GEMM of DRTP that recapitulates the compound heterozygous Dicer1 mutations seen in most DRTP-associated cancers10. Through an anti-Mullerian hormone receptor type 2 (Amhr2)-Cre driver, these Dicer1 mutations were activated in stromal cells throughout the Mullerian tract, leading to the development of adenosarcomas, a cancer type well described in DRTP11. Subsequently, Oikawa et al. developed a conditional human DICER1 RNase IIIb hotspot mutation (p.G1809R), whose activation through an Albumin-driven Cre enabled the development of epithelial lesions in the liver12. However, these and other models do not allow for controlling mutation activation and lineage-tracing, limiting their application to study tumor development continumm13,14.

In this work, we cross our Dicer1 tool strains with our recently generated CreERT2 mouse line-Hic1CreERT2, which expresses Cre in mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) of most adult tissues upon tamoxifen treatment15. This inducible DRTP GEMM develops renal tumors that closely recapitulate the continuum of human DRTP sarcoma development. We leverage single-cell whole transcriptomics (scRNA-seq), a targeted spatial transcriptomics platform, and primary cell cultures to uncover the putative cellular origin and developmental trajectory of sarcomagenesis in this GEMM of DRTP sarcoma, which is found conserved in human disease.

Results

Development of a lineage-traceable and genetically true mouse model of DRTP sarcoma

We utilized a tamoxifen-inducible Hic1CreERT2 driver, which marks quiescent mesenchymal progenitors in most vascularized tissues15, and the Rosa26LSL-tdtomato strain, permanently labeling cells with tdTomato fluorescence upon Cre induction16, to develop a conditional and lineage-traceable Dicer1 GEMM, Hic1CreERT2/CreERT2;Dicer1fl/fl-D1693N;Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice (referred to as HDT hereafter, Fig. 1a) that express hemizygous Dicer1D1693N upon tamoxifen treatment in Hic1+ cells, recapitulating the compound heterozygous state of DICER1 mutations in human tumors. Postnatal activation of Dicer1 mutations in HDT mice, regardless of sex (Supplementary Fig. 1a), resulted in the development of unilateral or bilateral renal lesions (Supplementary Data 1). Macroscopic analysis revealed a tumor continuum that ranged from small tumors which maintain the integrity of the kidney structure to large masses that had completely overgrown the kidney (Fig. 1b), with 80% (52/65) penetrance and a median latency of 378 days (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 1). Different control genotypes did not lead to development of renal tumors and did not differ in overall survival (Supplementary Fig. 1b–f). While induction timing of Dicer1 mutations did not correlate with HDT tumor penetrance or laterality (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h), late Dicer1 mutation activation correlated with a prolonged tumor latency (Fig. 1d), suggesting that the number of cells amenable to mutant DICER1-driven oncogenesis decreases along kidney development, consistent with the predominant childhood onset of these cancers in human patients.

Microscopically, renal HDT tumors closely mirrored human DRTP-associated mesenchymal neoplasms with tumor exhibiting a continuum from LGMT-like features to frank sarcomatous tumors showing features akin to SARC DICER1/PIS DICER1 (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 2). No correlation was observed between tumor histology and Dicer1 mutation activation timing (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j). Notably, this continuum included histological features typically associated with cystic nephroma, which is classified under the LGMT DICER1 group, as well as anaplastic sarcoma of the kidney, categorized within the SARC DICER1 class9. In detail, this included small tumors, typically limited to the kidney’s parenchyma (Fig. 1f, g), exhibiting hypocellular and primitive mesenchyme (Fig. 1h) with focal condensation adjacent to cystically dilated renal ducts (Fig. 1i, j). Conversely, large tumors that typically showed sarcomatous overgrowth of neighboring tissue consisted of sarcomatous mesenchyme with a pattern-less or fascicular growth (Fig. 1k, l). In these tumors, subepithelial aggregation of tumor cells underneath transitional cell epithelium (bona fide cambium layer) (Fig. 1m), prominent rhabdomyoblastic differentiation (Fig. 1n), and prominent anaplasia (Fig. 1o), often accompanied with necrosis, was noted. In line with human disease2,17, tumors stained for PDGFRA and DESMIN, with PDGFRA marking areas of primitive tumor cells and DESMIN highlighting regions of rhabdomyoblastic differentiation (Fig. 1p and Supplementary Data 2). While the mesenchyme of all tumors was diffusely positive for lineage-tracing marker tdTomato, associated lining epithelium was negative for tdTomato throughout (Fig. 1q), indicating that it was not developed from the neoplastic lineage. Targeted sequencing of HDT tumors confirmed the expected Dicer1 RNase IIIb (D1693N) mutation in all sequenced samples and revealed recurrent hotspot mutations in Kras and Trp53, which are commonly seen in human DRTP sarcoma9,18–21, in 3/35 and 3/35 tumors, respectively (Fig. 1r and Supplementary Data 3). Furthermore, qPCR for Kras identified an amplification in one tumor with a concurrent Kras missense mutation, which was confirmed by fluorescence in-situ hybridization (FISH) (Fig. 1s and Supplementary Data 3). Notably, these mutations were only identified in neoplasms with SARC-like differentiation. Shallow whole-genome sequencing (sWGS) revealed gain of chromosome 1 in a significantly greater proportion (Fig. 1r and Supplementary Fig. 1k, l, Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.03051, odds ratio = 8.41408, 95% Confidence Intervals = 1.2288, 78.5872) of SARC-like lesions (23/25) than LGMT-like lesions (5/9) (Supplementary Data 3), suggesting that aneuploidy may represent a secondary event associated with tumor progression. Overall, these results demonstrate that renal neoplasms from our HDT GEMM fully recapitulate the tumor development continuum of human DICER1 syndrome-associated mesenchymal neoplasms.

Organization of the Hic1+ mesenchymal stromal cell lineage in mouse steady state kidney

As pediatric tumors often mirror the normal development of affected organs, understanding the spatial composition of Hic1+ MSC lineage in normal mouse kidney is critical for establishing the tumor development trajectory of our renal HDT tumors. Notably, despite that Hic1 effectively identifies various MSC populations, including fibroblasts and pericytes, alongside rare perineural cells in mouse skeletal muscle, heart, and skin15,22,23, the characteristics of the Hic1+ MSC lineage in kidney (Supplementary Fig. 2) remains elusive. To address this, we analyzed renal tissues from control mice using scRNA-seq and targeted spatial transcriptomics (10× Genomics, Xenium, 379 genes; Supplementary Data 4 and Fig. 2a). ScRNA-seq analysis of normal mouse kidneys from 7 mice (post-natal week (PNW) 4–36, Supplementary Table 1) identified multiple clusters correlating to physiological kidney cell composition (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d)24–26. Focused analysis of tdTomato+ MSCs identified 6 clusters that were grouped into 5 cell types based on marker expression (Fig. 2b–d and Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). This included: (1) a cluster of mural cells (Mural, Myh11+, Acta2+, Tagln+), (2) two clusters of Pdgfra+ and Dpt+ fibroblasts, one characterized by Pi16high and Ly6ahigh (Uni Fibro Pi16high) and the other by Col15a1high and Mfap4high (Uni Fibro Col15a1high), and (3) two clusters of Pdgfra+ cells that were largely negative for Dpt, Pi16 and Col15a1, one (Fibro) showing a signature resembling kidney interstitial fibroblasts and mesangial cells (Itga8+, Robo2+, Ptn+) and the other (Cycle) enriched for cycling markers (Cenpf+, Top2a+, Mki67+).

Spatial transcriptomic analysis of 22 cored kidney regions of 10 kidneys and 2 whole kidney tissues from 9 normal control mice (Supplementary Data 5) corroborated the scRNA-seq data, revealing the cellular composition of normal kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 4a–e). Uni Fibro population was lower in aged (PNW: 81) vs young (PNW: 10) adult kidney of normal control mice (1.8% vs 2.3% of all cells identified, Supplementary Fig. 4f). Targeted analysis of kidney MSCs (Fig. 2e) identified mural cells (Mural) mapping to pericytes and vascular smooth-muscle cells (Rgs5+, Myh11+, Tagln+), fibroblasts/mesangial cells (Fibro: Bgn+, Cfh+, Itga8+), and universal fibroblasts (Uni Fibro: Bgn+, Lum+, Mfap4+) that appeared to overlap with the Uni Fibro Pi16high and Uni Fibro Col15a1high populations by scRNA-seq analysis. In line with their expression profile, Fibro mapped to interstitial fibroblasts of cortex and medulla, as well as to rare mesangial cells, while Uni Fibro was predominantly found in the perivascular niche in the hilar region, the basal membrane niche subjacent to transitional cell epithelium, and the renal capsular surface (Fig. 2f–h). Although the resolution of the targeted gene panel did not allow for subclassification of Uni Fibro based on UMAP, Pi16 expression was notably enriched in Uni Fibro within the perivascular niche, while Mfap4 expression was high in Uni Fibro subjacent to transitional cell epithelium (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, a recent study of murine fibroblasts also identified two groups of Dpt+ fibroblasts that are present universally across various tissues (in this paper kidney was not studied)27. The first group included Dpt+Pi16+ Uni Fibro that shared features with adventitial stromal cells28, which are found in perivascular niches29,30. The second group was composed of Dpt+ Col15a1+ specialized fibroblasts and showed an association with the basement membrane. Therefore, our study identifies two types of fibroblastic progenitor populations originated from Hic1+ MSCs in the steady-state mouse kidney with Pi16high Uni Fibro enriched within the perivascular niche and Col15a1high Uni Fibro more associated with the subepithelial basement membrane of lining transitional epithelium.

Spatial cell composition of Dicer1 renal sarcoma from HDT mice

Next, we characterized the transcriptomic landscape of 5 primary HDT renal tumors by scRNA-seq (Fig. 3a), one of which harbored a Trp53 mutation in their corresponding formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue (Fig. 1r), revealing the presence of multiple cell lineages, such as transitional epithelial, mesenchymal, immune and endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). Unsupervised clustering of tdTomato+ MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c) revealed 10 distinct cell populations, all of which expressed some specific markers of steady kidney MSC lineages (Supplementary Fig. 7b). To further compare their developmental relatedness, we co-clustered scRNA-seq data of both HDT tumor and steady-state kidney samples (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). This led to the identification of two clusters in HDT tumors corresponding to steady-state kidney mural cells, pericytes (Peri; Rgs5+) and vascular smooth muscle cells (SM Vasc; Acta2+), a cluster overlapping with steady-state kidney Uni Fibro that we termed Progenitor (Progen), and a ground population (Ground) closely resembling Fibro of steady-state kidney (Supplementary Fig. 7f and Fig. 3b–d). A Proliferative cluster (Prolif) enriched in genes related to cell proliferation, similar to cycling cells of steady-state kidney, was also identified, some of which also expressed signature genes of Progen or Ground clusters, suggestive of their heterogenous nature. Moreover, HDT tumors also contained three populations not identified in steady-state kidney, including a cluster of perturbed-state fibroblasts (Fibro Perturb) and two myogenic populations. The Fibro Perturb cluster expressed a high level of Pi16 with low Dpt and also expressed signature genes of perturbed-state fibroblasts (Comp+, Cpxm2+, and Itgbl1+) that were reported by Buechler et al.27. The two additional clusters expressed genes linked to skeletal muscle development (e.g., Pax7) and committed muscle cells (e.g., MyoG), termed transiting-differentiated myogenic cells (TR Diff Myo) and differentiated myogenic cells (Diff Myo)31.

To spatially visualize these clusters, we performed targeted spatial transcriptomics on renal tumors (n = 24) and non-neoplastic kidneys (n = 15) harvested from HDT mice (Supplementary Data 5 and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). This revealed clusters corresponding to cell states in both steady-state kidney and tumors characterized by our scRNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g). Notably, the Progen cluster represented the dominant MSC population in LGMT-like tumors (Supplementary Fig. 8c and e), ubiquitously expressing Mfap4 with a portion expressing Dpt and Pi16 (Fig. 3e), indicating that this subepithelial cell cluster was composed of rare tumor initiating progenitors and abundant progenitor-like tumor cells. In SARC-like tumors, Progen cells were enriched in the subepithelial condensed cambium-like layer (Fig. 3f), where abundant Diff Myo and TR Diff Myo cells were often detected, suggestive of their lineage relatedness. Cells assigned to Ground were usually scattered within other cell states, e.g., Progen and Prolif clusters (Fig. 3g). The Ground and especially Prolif cluster cells showed frank sarcomatous differentiation (Fig. 3h).

Taken together, our scRNA-Seq and targeted spatial transcriptomic analysis not only revealed the spatial complexity of HDT tumors, but suggest that HDT tumors arise from perturbed differentiation of renal Hic1+ MSC progenitors subjacent to transitional epithelial cells of renal collecting ducts.

A Dpt+Pi16+ universal fibroblast lineage as the cell of origin for mouse Dicer1 sarcoma

To identify the tumor development trajectory, we cultured tdTomato+ cells isolated from kidneys of control and HDT mice 4 weeks post tamoxifen treatment at 3-week-old. Bulk RNA-Seq analysis demonstrated that activation of Dicer1 mutations led to increased expression of multiple Uni Fibro markers (Dpt, Dcn, Pi16, Ly6a, Ly6c1, Col15a1) and reduced expression of lineage markers for differentiated fibroblasts (Itga8) or pericytes (Myh11, Acta2) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 9a), which were validated by real-time qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 9b). This finding suggests that Dicer1 mutation reprograms the multipotent Hic1+ MSC, impairing the differentiation ability of their derivative primitive Uni Fibro, which primes them for subsequent malignant transformation towards a variety of cell states.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis from the Dpt+Pi16+ Uni Fibro-like population revealed 6 trajectories (Fig. 4b, c), all passing through the Progen population that was accompanied by a loss of Uni Fibro-specific markers (e.g., Dpt, Pi16, Mfap4) (Fig. 4d). Trajectories 2 and 3 led into Diff Myo (Myog+), passing through the “intermediate state” TR Diff Myo cells (Pax7+), including a highly proliferative state of TR Diff Myo. Trajectories 1, 4, and 5 transitioned into the Ground state (Itga8+). Trajectory 6 transitioned into the Pericyte state (Pdgfrb+Rgs5+). To validate the lineage relationship, we conducted copy number (CN) analysis using scRNA-Seq datasets from HDT renal tumors, which revealed high CN changes in tdTomato+ mesenchymal cells, but not other cell types (Supplementary Fig. 9c), consistently with our finding using the sWGS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1k, l). Analysis of normal control kidneys revealed minimal CN changes (Supplementary Fig. 9d), further confirming CN changes in HDT mesenchymal cells were tumor-specific. Focused analysis of tdTomato+ mesenchymal cells of HDT tumors identified CN changes in all Hic1+ clusters except Fibro Perturb and SM Vasc populations. In contrast, a sizable subset of Progen cells accumulated gains in chromosomes 1 and 6 (Fig. 4e). Notably, the Ground and Prolif populations exhibited near-universal gains in these same chromosomes. Additionally, only a minority of the TR Diff Myo and Diff Myo populations demonstrated gains in chromosomes 1 and 15, although these CN changes were more pronounced than those observed in Progen cells, suggesting that chromosome gains can occur early before their committed differentiation but are not essential for rhabdomyogenic differentiation. These findings support a model of complex tumor progression, in which Dicer1 mutation perturbs the differentiation potential of Hic1+ MSC, priming Dicer1-mutant Dpt+Pi16+ Uni Fibro-like cells for oncogenic transformation via distinct mechanisms: a portion of these progenitors undergoes aberrant myogenic differentiation, whereas accumulation of CN alterations promotes tumorigenesis towards the Ground state, from which a subset progresses to proliferative sarcoma upon acquiring specific genomic changes (Fig. 4f). Consistent with this model and the recurrent occurrence of p53 mutations in both human and mouse tumors, deletion of p53 in Dicer1-mutant primary cells significantly accelerated cell growth (Fig. 4g). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes among HDT tumor cell states revealed that the Prolif population was characterized by downregulation of p53-associated pathways and concomitant upregulation of MAPK signaling (Fig. 4h). Therefore, the identification of HDT tumor development trajectory may inform future therapeutic development.

Cell states of mouse Dicer1 sarcoma are largely recapitulated in human DRTP sarcoma

To determine whether cell states informed from the mouse Dicer1 sarcoma are present in human DRTP sarcomas, we characterized the transcriptional landscape across 16 human DRTP mesenchymal tumors (4 LGMT DICER1, 11 SARC DICER1, and 1 PIS DICER1), including Cystic nephroma, pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB) I/II/III, nasal chondroid mesenchymal hamartoma, DICER1-mutated embryonal RMS, and primary intracranial sarcoma (PIS DICER1), using a targeted spatial transcriptomics platform (Fig. 5a). Unsupervised clustering identified 13 Louvain clusters (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). These clusters correspond to distinct cell populations, including non-neoplastic cell types (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). Guided by lineage markers (e.g., PDGFRA) and spatial morpho-molecular correlation, we identified fibroblast lineage clusters (Supplementary Fig. 10e)32, which were grouped into cell states that largely recapitulated those identified in our mouse HDT tumors (Fig. 5b–d). These included a neoplastic progenitor cluster (Progen) expressing PDGFRA and universal fibroblastic marker DPT, a proliferative cluster (Prolif) dominated by proliferation and cell cycle genes, two myogenic subpopulations expressing skeletal muscle-related genes (e.g., DES, PROX1) as well as committed muscle cell markers (e.g., MYBPC1), termed transiting-differentiated myogenic cells (TR Diff Myo) and differentiated myogenic cells (Diff Myo). Additionally, a PDGFRA+ fibroblastic population lacking other signatures (Ground), and a distinct fibroblast cluster (Fibro) mapping to non-neoplastic tissue and marked by PDGFRA+ and C7+ were identified. Spatial analysis revealed Progen cells in all tumors at varying frequencies (Fig. 5e), often mapping to the cambium layer of LGMT and SARC DICER1 (Fig. 5f) as seen in our HDT renal tumors (Fig. 3f). Like HDT tumors, TR Diff Myo states were associated with increased proliferation (Fig. 5g). Bona fide rhabdomyoblastic differentiation (Diff Myo) co-localized with TR Diff Myo and the committed muscle marker MYBPC1 (Fig. 5h). Ground and Prolif cells were abundant in SARC/PIS DICER1 and exhibited sarcomatous growth (Fig. 5i). In agreement with the elevated MAPK pathway in the highly proliferative state cells of HDT tumors (Fig. 4h), immunohistochemistry staining of human tumors revealed elevated p-ERK levels in SARC DICER1 compared to LGMT DICER1 (Supplementary Fig. 10f, g). These results alongside trajectory analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10h), closely mirrored spatial relationships observed in murine HDT tumors.

Human DRTP mesenchymal neoplasms closely mimic RMS clinically and morphologically. A recent scRNA-based meta-analysis identified a robust tripartite cell state landscape across various human RMS tumors, including Progenitor, Proliferative, and Differentiated metaprograms33. This paper also inspired some of our cluster names. Our transcriptomics data showed that these RMS metaprograms are enriched in signatures present in DICER1 mesenchymal neoplasms (Supplementary Fig. 10i), highlighting their transcriptional relatedness.

Discussion

Patients with DRTP are predisposed to a variety of cancers, many of which are aggressive sarcomas, arising in diverse anatomical locations. Recent multi-omics studies have significantly advanced our understanding of the genetic underpinnings of these cancers and uncovered the similarities of DRTP sarcomas regardless of their anatomic sites of onset. However, despite the well-recognized mesenchymal nature of DRTP sarcomas, their cellular origin and developmental trajectory remain unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that expression of patient tumor-relevant hemizygous Dicer1D1693N in Hic1+ MSC drove the development of renal sarcomas that closely resemble the human disease, both histomorphologically and genomically, which is consistent with the observations in tumors from our Amhr2+/cre-driven Dicer1 GEMM10. Importantly, with this lineage-traceable GEMM, we identified the spatial composition of Hic1+ MSCs in steady state normal mouse kidneys and discovered that upon activation of Dicer1 mutations, Hic1+ MSC-derived universal fibroblast, marked by Pdgfra, Dpt, and Pi16, gained the potential to differentiate into a myogenic lineage and/or became proliferative sarcomatous cells. We further identified these cell states and their developmental hierarchy in human DRTP sarcomas. Thus, we uncovered universal fibroblasts as the putative cellular origin of mouse Dicer1 sarcoma and deconvoluted their developmental trajectory, which appears conserved in human patients.

In steady-state murine kidneys, we show that Dpt+ universal fibroblasts are localized to the perivascular, capsular, and subepithelial basal membrane niches. This is particularly intriguing given that human DRTP sarcomas frequently arise in organs with epithelial lining, such as the lungs, kidneys, and uterus, and form a cambium layer (sarcoma botryoides)2, yet they can also develop in non-epithelial tissues like the brain20. Our data suggest that the anatomical predilection for DRTP sarcomas may be linked to the proposed cellular origin within the universal fibroblastic niche, which spans basal membrane–associated, capsular, and perivascular compartments, with the perivascular localization potentially accounting for tumor development in non-epithelial tissues such as the brain. Specifically, the enrichment of transitional epithelial cells in our renal tumors, revealed by both scRNA-Seq and targeted spatial transcriptomic analysis (Supplementary Figs. 6d and 8c, d), strongly supports that these renal tumors arose from Hic1+ MSC progenitors subjacent to transitional epithelial cells of renal collecting ducts. Future global spatial transcriptomics will help identifying the specific features of universal fibroblasts in the subepithelial basal membrane niche that distinguish them from those in other niches to enable tumorigenesis.

DRTP predominantly affects children and young adults, suggesting that the tumor susceptibility in DRTP may be influenced by the developmental stage. Mirroring this, we observed prolonged tumor latency in HDT mice with a late activation of Dicer1D1693N mutation (Fig. 1d). Thus, earlier developmental windows may lead to the retention of certain universal fibroblasts prone to malignant transformation in the kidney. Moreover, despite that Hic1 marks MSC populations across various tissue types, tumor development was restricted to kidneys in our HDT model. We hypothesize that while universal fibroblasts exist across a variety of tissues in mice27, their specific developmental state in the kidney may influence the susceptibility to Dicer1-associated transformation. Future analysis is needed to address whether these mutant Dicer1 transformable universal fibroblast populations are (1) declining along mouse kidney development and (2) uniquely present or more abundant in postnatal juvenile mouse kidneys than other organs due to delayed disappearance after embryogenesis. Given the potential sampling bias during kidney dissociation for scRNA-Seq, global spatial transcriptomic analysis is desired to profile mouse kidneys and other organs during their development continuum to identify such unique and rare cell population.

Notably, the Fibro Perturb and SM Vasc clusters appeared to be the non-active components of HDT renal tumors. The absence of lineage relationship between these clusters and the putative tumor cellular origin (Dpt+Pi16+ cells) (Fig. 4b) and absence of copy number alterations in these clusters (Fig. 4e) suggest that they are likely untransformed tdTomato+ cells. Notably, Hic1+ MSC cells are a mixture of cells at various states including primitive universal fibroblasts and also differentiated cells, e.g., fibroblasts and pericytes, which are abundant in the kidney at all stages but appear not transformable by Dicer1D1693N mutation as evidenced by the prolonged tumor latency when Dicer1D1693N mutations were activated late (Fig. 1d). Thus, it is possible that Fibro Perturb cells in HDT tumors may arise from activating Dicer1D1693N in Hic1+ fibroblasts, whereas the SM Vasc may derive from activating Dicer1D1693N in Hic1+ mural cells. However, future studies are needed to address whether these rare subsets, particularly the Fibro Perturb cluster, can contribute to sarcomagenesis upon accumulating additional genomic mutations.

We further demonstrate that the epithelial lining in our kidney tumors was negative for tdTomato, indicating that it did not belong to the neoplastic lineage. Nevertheless, these epithelial areas frequently displayed a botryoid or even cystic appearance. We propose that such cystic changes, commonly observed in tumors from the LGMT DICER1—such as PPB I and CN—are not a result of DICER1 disruption in epithelial cells, as previously suggested4. Instead, we hypothesize that these cystic transformations are likely a reactive phenomenon, driven by neoplastic mesenchymal proliferation, which may exert pressure on adjacent structures, such as kidney tubules or alveolar spaces, leading to their compression and subsequent cystic dilation due to fluid or air entrapment. Notably, while previous studies suggested that cystic changes could reflect a non-autonomous mechanism where epithelial loss of Dicer1 promoted the transformation of the underlying mesenchymal cells13,34, their contribution to the development of LGMT DICER1, which is driven by RNase IIIb-mutant DICER1 rather than loss of DICER1, remains to be addressed. It is possible that DICER1 may be epigenetically silenced in the epithelium of a subset of these tumors, potentially influencing the growth of the subepithelial mesenchymal cells that are already transformed by RNase IIIb-mutant DICER1 through this non-autonomous mechanism.

Notably, while human DRTP-associated mesenchymal neoplasms closely mimic RMS clinically and morphologically, our previous molecular analysis distinguished DRTP sarcomas from bona-fide RMS subtypes (fusion-negative, fusion-positive, and MYOD1-mutated RMS) based on DNA methylation profiles, suggesting distinct cellular origins for these tumors9. Conventional RMS subtypes are typically thought to originate from satellite-cell derived post-natal muscle and results from aberrant skeletal muscle differentiation35,36. However, recent studies have challenged this notion, suggesting that RMS may originate from endothelial progenitors37,38 or mesenchymal stem cells33,39. In our Dicer1 GEMM, although our HDT tumors closely mimic conventional types of RMS morphologically and transcriptomically, we observed the expansion of the fibroblastic progenitor population that gave rise to divergent trajectories, including a step-wise continuum of rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, including Pax7+ and Myf5+ populations, supporting a fibroblastic origin of RMS in DRTP sarcomas. Accordingly, a recent study demonstrated that fibroblasts derived from the lateral plate mesoderm can trans-differentiate by activating myogenic programs during physiological development40. Yaseen et al. showed that these fibroblasts not only acquire myogenic features and fuse with existing myofibers but also retain partial expression of their original fibroblastic transcriptional program. Hic1+ fibroblasts typically do not express Pax7, and do not contribute to the myogenic lineage in the developing limb41. However, these cells have a somitic origin41,42. Notably, in our Hic1+ GEMM of synovial sarcoma, re-expression of Pax7 has been linked to unmasking of their embryonic epigenome, suggesting a potential for lineage plasticity43. In contrast, there was no expression of terminal myogenic transcriptions factors, such as Myf5 or MyoD1, and no evidence of rhabdomyoblastic differentiation in the latter model. This suggests that our observations are specifically linked to the disruption of Dicer1, rather than being a broader feature of the Hic1+ lineage. In line with this, our findings provide clear evidence that these fibroblasts have the potential to undergo rhabdomyoblastic transformation upon Dicer1 disruption, which may be linked to re-expression of embryonic programs and challenges the long-standing dogma that all RMS develop from a predetermined myogenic lineage.

Finally, although our spatial transcriptomic analysis is limited by the targeted panel, our data reveals a striking concordance between the cellular origins of murine and human DRTP sarcomas. We confirm that in the human disease, cancer cell states share transcriptomic similarities with those observed in our murine model, including the presence of a progenitor population of universal fibroblastic lineage (PDGFRA+, DPT+). This striking similarity in cell states alongside the common frequent activation of the MAPK pathway identified in murine and human DRTP tumors further substantiates the relevance of our GEMM model for DRTP sarcoma and underscores its potential as a tool for advancing mechanistic understanding and developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Model development and cohort assembly

All animal experiments were conducted following ethical approval by the University of British Columbia animal care committee (A21-0023 and A21-0047). The conditional knockin mice expressing the floxed Dicer1 p.D1693N (Dicer1+/fl-D1693N) was previously developed by our group10. The Dicer1fl/fl mice44 [RRID:MMRRC_015388-UNC] were obtained from the Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centres (MMRRC) depository. The Rosa26LSL-tdtomato strain16 was obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (stock #: 007909). Hic1CreERT2 mice were previously generated as described elsewhere15. Mouse lines were maintained on a pure C57Bl/6J genetic background. We outcrossed these 4 strains together to develop the Hic1CreERT2/CreERT2; Dicer1fl/fl-D1693N; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato and corresponding control strains. Genotyping was performed using primers in Supplementary Table 2 using ear punches obtained at 3 weeks of age. To induce CreERT2 nuclear translocation, mice were injected at postnatal 10 (P10) days to 7 weeks old intraperitoneally with tamoxifen (dose: 2 mg every 24 h for 5 consecutive days for mice at 3–7 weeks old; 1 mg every 24 h for 3 consecutive days for mice at 2 weeks old; 1 mg for mice at P10).

For tumor development studies, we used 65 HDT mice (33 male, 32 female) and 55 control mice (26 male, 29 female). Mice were either found dead or sacrificed because they were morbid or reached the experimental end point (1.5 years). At the experimental endpoint, the tumor burden in each mouse did not reach the approved humane endpoint limit (10% of body weight). For penetrance analysis, the presence of macroscopic or microscopic tumors at death was used as the primary endpoint. For overall survival (OS) analysis, events were defined as either the death of the mice or their euthanasia due to morbid conditions. Five tumors from 5 HDT mice (4 males and 1 female) with age ranging PNW 36 to PNW 66 were used for scRNA-Seq analysis. Twenty-four tumors from 8 HDT males and 10 HDT females, age ranging from PNW 36 to PNW 81, and 15 non-neoplastic kidneys from 13 HDT mice (9 males, 4 females), age ranging from PNW 10 to PNW 61, were used for spatial transcriptomic analysis.

For scRNA-Seq analysis of normal mouse kidneys, 7 control mice (3 males, 4 females) were sacrificed at the age of PNW 4–36. For spatial transcriptomic analysis of normal mouse kidneys, 9 control mice (5 males and 4 females) were sacrificed at the age of PNW 10 to PNW 81.

Patient samples

A cohort of 16 patient samples of DICER1 mesenchymal neoplasms from 10 males and 6 females, with age ranging from 1 month to 69 years, was included in this study. They had all undergone histopathological evaluation by specialized pathologists, as well as molecular testing whenever applicable. Samples were collected at McGill University-affiliated hospitals in Montreal, Quebec, Canada, and the Institute of Pathology, University Hospital Heidelberg, mainly from collaborating institutions in accordance with ethics review board regulations. All eligible participants, regardless of sex, were included with signing an informed consent form. Clinical data, histological features, and/or sequencing results of tumors have previously been reported elsewhere9. Study of patient samples was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Heidelberg University, S660/2020) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the rareness of the disease, we do not have sufficient power to perform sex-specific analysis.

Tissue microarray construction

For murine tissue microarray (TMA, n = 2) construction, 1.0 mm cores of FFPE tissue from control and HDT mice were used, as described previously45. For human samples, a previously constructed TMA was used, that included duplicates of 0.6 mm cores of FFPE tissues, including the 16 samples of DICER1 mesenchymal neoplasms used in this study.

Primary cell culture

The whole kidneys from Hic1CreERT2/CreERT2:Rosa26LSL-tdTomato and Hic1CreERT2/CreERT2:Dicerfl/fl-D1693N;Rosa26LSL-tdTomato GEMM mice 4 weeks post tamoxifen treatment, which occurred when mice were 3-week-old, were minced with forceps to small chunks pipette-able by a 5 mL serological pipette. Tumour chunks were then incubated in Gibco HBSS (no calcium, magnesium, phenol red, Catalog #14175103) and Stemcell 10× Collagenase/hyaluronidase in DMEM (Catalog # 07912), in a glass flask at 37 °C on a hot plate, until everything was dissolved, usually takes between 30 and 45 min. Enzymatic digestion was stopped with Advanced DMEM/F-12 (Catalog #12634010). Cells and tumour chunks were spun at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Pellets were resuspended in Gibco 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Catalog #25200072) for 5 min at 37 °C, followed by filtering through a Falcon 70 μm cell strainer (Catalog #08-771-2). Cells were pelleted and resuspended in Stemcell Mouse MesenCult™ Expansion media (Catalog #05513). tdTomato positive cells were enriched by FACS and subsequently cultured in Mesencult media or DMEM/F12 media supplemented with 10%FBS.

Histo- and Immunohistochemistry

Four micrometers-sections were cut and mounted on Permaflex Plus Microscopic Slides (Leica Biosystems). Sections were stained using standard protocols for Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). For immunohistochemistry of murine samples slides were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in a high-pH buffer. Staining was performed using the Ventana Benchmark Ultra automated immunostainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA). Immunohistochemical staining of murine samples was performed with antibodies to tdTomato (Rockland, anti-RFP, 600-401-379, Dilution 1:400), PDGFRA (Cell Signaling, D1E1E, Dilution 1:100), p-ERK (Cell Signaling, 4376S, clone 20G11), and DESMIN (Abcam, Y66, ready-to-use).

Targeted panel sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted with QIAamp DNA FFPE Advanced UNG Kit (Cat. # 56704). Mutations in Kras (exons 2 and 3), Nras (exons 2 and 3), Hras (exons 2 and 3), Trp53 (exons 2-11), and Dicer1 (exon 24) were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 machine using in house designed primers, and libraries were prepped with in house. Targeted panel data was processed using a modified version of the Nextflow46 and Sarek47 pipeline, version 3.4.2. Raw reads were trimmed with FastP and aligned with bwa-mem2. Overlapping primer intervals were merged with bedtools merge, version 2.30.0, and 100 bp of padding was added to either side of each interval. No duplicate marking was carried out and the “wes” parameter was set to true. Somatic variants were called on the primer intervals with GATK Mutect2 with “ignore_soft_clipped_bases” set to true, and annotated with SnpEff48 and Ensembl VEP49. For the variant calling step, the pipeline was modified to disable downsampling of the reads. Variants of interest were selected by filtering for those with variant allele fraction > 0.1, and excluding those annotated as “synonymous_variant” or “intron_variant” by SnpEff.

Kras qPCR and FISH

To evaluate Kras copy number status, we used a qPCR assay with SYBR Green detection and a custom primer for mouse Kras (Chr.6:145220129) and Tfrc (Chr.16:32626732). Samples were normalized to Tfrc and evaluated for a relative gain in Kras. HDT361 showed a high count for Kras upon qPCR, and gene amplification was identified using a fluorescence in-situ hybridization (FISH) assay. For this assay, we designed custom probes for Kras on 6qA1 (RP23-300B22, green; Empire genomics) and 6qG3 (RP23-118J6, red; Empire genomics).

sWGS and data analysis

DNA was extracted from cored FFPE HDT tumor or non-neoplastic kidney samples and sheared for library preparation and sequencing as we described previously50. Single-end reads were processed with a previously validated Nextflow pipeline. Read quality was assessed via FastQC 0.11.9 and reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic 0.39.29. Ilumina adapters and leading/trailing bases with quality scores below 3 were removed, as were 4 base sections with average quality scores below 15. Reads shorter than 40 base pairs were dropped. Reads were aligned to mm10 with bwa-mem2 2.1.30. Following alignment, reads were processed with QDNAseq 1.34.32 to determine segmented relative copy-number and call gains and losses using various bin sizes: 30, 50, 100 kbp. The 50 kbp bin size was selected as the best balance of resolution and variance. One sample was excluded due to a highly variable and fragmented copy-number profile, consistent with poor sample quality. For statistical comparisons of copy number alterations, a two-sided Fisher’s exact test was used as implemented in the exact2Tomato positive cells were enric2 R package, which implements improved selection of the matching confidence interval for such two-sided tests.

Bulk RNA-seq

RNA from frozen cell pellets was extracted using the Zymo Quick-RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo cat# R1054). Total RNA integrity is assessed with the TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies; RNA Assay Cat No.5067-5576) and quantified with the Qubit RNA HS assay (ThermoFisher; Cat No. Q32852). Total RNA is used as input to enrich for mRNA with the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB; Cat No. E7490). Strand-specific libraries are prepared using the DNBSEQ Fast RNA Library Prep kit (Complete Genomics; Cat No. 940-001522-00) and sequenced at Vancouver Prostate Centre. Library quality and size are checked with the TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies; D1000 Assay Cat No. 5067-5582) and then quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (ThermoFisher; Cat No. Q32854). The libraries are sequenced using a Complete Genomics DNBSEQ-G400RS High-throughput Sequencer to generate 30 million 100-bp paired-end reads per library.

Bulk RNA-Seq data analysis

Paired-end reads were processed with the nf-core rnaseq pipeline, version 3.14.0 and aligned to mm10. The trimmer was parameter was set to “trimgalore” and the aligner to “star_salmon”, with extra salmon quant arguments supplied as “–seqBias –gcBias”. Following alignment and quantification, the counts were imported with tximport version 1.26.1 and differential expression between the Dicer1 mutant and control samples was assessed within each media type using DESeq2 version 1.38.3. Genes with fewer than 10 counts in at least 3 samples were excluded from analysis.

Cloning, lentivirus packaging, and cell proliferation

The gRNA sequences for knocking out Trp53 in murine cells was aggagctcctgacactcgga51 and gRNA2 sequence was accctgtcaccgagacccc52. They were cloned into the lentiCRISPRv2GFP (Plasmid #82416) vector following the Zhang lab protocol53. For viral packaging, early passage HEK293T17 cells, cultured in DMEM + 10% FBS, authenticated by short tandem repeat analysis and negative for mycoplasma contamination, were seeded in 10 cm dishes and then transfected with 5 µg of lentiCRISPRv2GFP-p53gRNA plasmids, 4 µg of 2 and 1 µg of VSV.G plasmids using 20 µL of Mirus Bio™ TransIT™-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Catalog #MIR2300). Virus was harvested in DMEM + 10% FBS + 1.1 g/100 ml BSA (Catalog # A2153-10G) + 0.01 M HEPES (Catalog #15630080). All the collected media was filtered with a 0.45 μm filter (Catalog #09-761-35). Beckman Optima XE Ultracentrifuge was prechilled to 4 °C. 36–40 mL of filtered harvest media was aliquoted into a 50 ml tube (#NC9530407) and spun down at 25,000 rpm at 4 °C for 90 min. Reconstituted viral pellet was stored at −80 °C. Fifty microliters of concentrated virus was used per 50,000 cells. Plates were spun at 2000 rpm at 37 °C for 2 h. Selection with eGFP sorting was carried out one week after transduction.

Growth curve generation

Primary cells were seeded in 96-well plates in triplicates (500 cells/well) and were allowed to attach for 24 h to achieve an initial confluency of 10%. Live-image for 7 days was performed with CELLCYTE X™ from Cytena. The mean confluency of cells was plot over time for illustrating their growth behavior.

scRNA-seq

Sample preparation

Tumour and normal kidney tissues were minced to 1 mm3 with forceps and incubated at 37 °C with Stemcell 10× Collagenase/hyaluronidase in DMEM (10× Genomics, C#07912) in HBSS for 30 min. The minced tissues were subsequently incubated in Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) for 5 min at 37 °C. Cells were then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Live cells were enriched with Miltenyi Biotec Dead Cell Removal Kit. Mesenchymal cell populations were then isolated by FACS sorting with tdTomato positive enrichment and antibody depletion of Epcam, CD45, CD31, CD11b, and F4/80. Cells were then prepared according to the 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell Universal 3′ Gene Expression kit protocol.

Alignment and quality control

Raw reads were aligned with Cell Ranger count 7.1.0 to a combined reference genome, composed of the mm10 reference with the addition of the tdTomato reporter sequence15. The combined reference genome was generated with Cell Ranger mkref 7.1.0. Following alignment, per-sample quality control was conducted. Low quality cells with > 25% of reads originating from mitochondrial genes, <1000 UMIs detected, or <500 genes detected were discarded.

Integration, clustering, cell-type annotation, and pathway activity

Samples originating from HDT and/or control mice were integrated using Seurat 4.3.0.154. Counts were normalized per cell by library size followed by log1p transformation. The top 2000 highly variable genes per sample were selected using the VST method and the top 2000 genes across samples were used for integration via reciprocal principal components analysis (PCA). After integration and dimensionality reduction via PCA, shared nearest neighbors were identified in PCA space and clustered with the Louvain algorithm, using the first 30 components. The resulting clusters were manually annotated via the examination of differentially expressed upregulated genes, in combination with markers of kidney and Hic1+ MSC types obtained from the literature. To achieve a pure Hic1+ MSC lineage following examination of all cell types, the data was subset to the relevant clusters in the control (Uni Fibro, Fibro, Mural, Cycle) or HDT (Fibro Perturb, Fibro, Mural, Cycle, TR Diff Myo, Diff Myo) samples. If necessary, residual tdTomato- cells, or cells expressing epithelial (Epcam+, n = 102), immune (Ptprc+, n = 113), or transitional epithelial (Krt19+, n = 376) markers were excluded from the subset data. The resulting data, filtered for the Hic1+ MSC lineage, was reprocessed as described above for the unfiltered data; a reduced number of principal components were used for neighbor finding and clustering after selection via an elbow plot.

Trajectory inference

The Hic1+ MSC lineage obtained from the HDT samples was used as input for trajectory inference with Monocle 3, version 1.3.455. Based on the UMAP obtained during dimensionality reduction with Seurat, trajectory was inferred, and the cells were placed in a pseudotime ordering, setting the node in the graph surrounded by the greatest number of Dpt+ cells as the earliest point. Trajectories to Ground, Cycle, and Diff Myo were examined by selecting the relevant branches, holding the start point constant, and varying the end point to the most distant node in the ending cluster of interest. Selected genes of interest were visualized in pseudotime over the respective trajectories.

Copy number analysis

To infer copy-number from the scRNA-seq data, inferCNV 3.2.0 was used. The raw counts for all cells present in the control samples and the Hic1+ MSC lineage from the HDT samples were combined and used as input to inferCNV. All parameters were set to their default setting, except for “cutoff”, which was set to 0.1 as prescribed for 10× data in the inferCNV documentation.

Xenium spatial transcriptomics

Slide preparation and imaging

For spatial transcriptomic analyses (10× Genomics Xenium), four slides were prepared. Two slides each contained a section from one of the two murine TMAs. A third slide included full kidney sections from three control mice and one HDT mouse harboring a LGMT-like lesion. The fourth slide featured a section of the human TMA. Tissue sections were prepared as per the Xenium In Situ for FFPE—Tissue Preparation Guide (10× Genomics, CG000578 version Rev C) protocol. Five micrometer sections were cut and mounted onto the Sample Area of a Xenium slide. The slides were air dried, followed by a 3 h incubation at 42 °C. Subsequently, the slides were processed, over a two-day span, as per the Xenium In Situ for FFPE—Deparaffinization and Decrosslinking (10× Genomics, CG000580 version Rev C) and Xenium In Situ Gene Expression (10× Genomics, CG000582 version Rev C) protocols. On day one, slides were incubated at 60 °C for 2 h on a Xenium thermocycler adaptor plate (BioRad, C1000 Touch Thermocycler), followed by deparaffinized through a series of immersions in xylene, 100%, 96%, and 70% ethanol, and rehydration with nuclease-free water. Slides were assembled into Xenium cassettes for all downstream steps, starting with decrosslinking and permeabilization. Gene panel probes were hybridized overnight (minimum 18 h) at 50 °C. The pre-designed dataset for the murine slide included 379 genes (10× Genomics, Mouse Tissue Atlassing Panel, PN1000627). The pre-designed dataset for the human slide included 379 genes (10× Genomics, Human Multi-Tissue and Cancer Panel, PN1000626). On day two, slides were subjected to a post-hybridization wash (37 °C for 2 h), ligation (37 °C for 2 h), amplification (30 °C for 2 h), autofluorescence quenching and nuclei staining steps. Slides were then stored in PBS-T, overnight, at 4 °C, in the dark. The following day, slides and consumables were loaded onto the Xenium Analyzer instrument according to the Xenium Analyzer (10× Genomics, CG000584, version Rev B) protocol. The whole slide area was selected for probe decoding and image acquisition, and the run was initiated. After run completion, slides were H&E stained as per the “Xenium in Situ Gene Expression—Post Xenium Analyzer H&E Staining” (10× Genomics, CG000613, version Rev A) protocol, and scanned using a slide scanner (AperioAT2, Leica).

Segmentation and Xenium data analysis

The Xenium bundles were segmented with Xenium Ranger 1.7.1.1, with the expansion distance around nuclei set to 5 µm. The bundles were then re-segmented with Proseg 1.1.4 using the “xenium” preset, converted to the Baysor format with the “proseg-to-baysor“ utility bundled with Proseg, and finally re-imported with Xenium Ranger 1.7.1.156. Cell visualization and morpho-molecular correlation was performed in the 10× Xenium Explorer (Version 3.0.1). Resulting Xenium objects were analyzed using Seurat 4.3.0.154. Quality control was conducted and low-quality cells with less than 3 counts and less than 3 features, were discarded. After normalization, integration, and dimensionality reduction via PCA, shared nearest neighbors were identified in PCA space and clustered with the Louvain algorithm. The resulting clusters were manually annotated via the examination of differentially expressed upregulated genes, in combination with markers of kidney and Hic1+ MSC types obtained from the literature, as well as morpho-molecular correlation. To achieve a pure tumor cell-associated lineage following examination of all cell types, the data was subset to the relevant clusters in the patient samples (Fibro, Progen, Ground, TR Diff Myo, Diff Myo, and Prolif). The resulting data was reprocessed as described above for the unfiltered data; a reduced number of principal components were used for neighbor finding and clustering after selection via an elbow plot. Trajectory inference of patient samples was performed with Monocle 3, version 1.3.455. On the basis of the UMAP obtained during dimensionality reduction with Seurat, trajectory was inferred, and the cells were placed in a pseudotime ordering, setting the node in the graph surrounded by the greatest number of DPT+ cells as the earliest point.

For comparison of human cell states to metaprograms of RMS tumors33, RMS gene signatures were intersected with genes present on the targeted panel. Based on a limited representation of the RMS signatures, the AddModuleScore function of Seurat was used to infer metaprogram scores onto human cell states.

Statistics and reproducibility

Sample size determination was based on anticipated variability and the effect size that was observed in the investigator’s lab for similar experiments. No data were excluded from the analyses. Kaplan–Meier plots were generated using Prism 8. For pairwise comparisons, unpaired two-tailed t tests were used to calculate P-values. Both statistical methods were carried out using Prism (GraphPad Software v. 9). p-values < 0.05 were considered significant.
