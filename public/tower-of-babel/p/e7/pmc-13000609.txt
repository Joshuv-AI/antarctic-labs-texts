Copper Oxide-Based Schottky Diode Using Poly(3-hexylthiophene) as an Interlayer
Authors: Vishwas D. Patel, Ashutosh Mohanty, Amit Roy, Dhritiman Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report the electrical properties and charge transport behavior of an electrodeposited, vacuum-annealed (100 °C) pure Cu2O and air-annealed (at 300 °C) Cu–O (mixed phase of Cu2O and CuO) films in the device geometry of ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/Al. An intermediate poly­(3-hexylthiophene) (P3HT) layer was deposited by spin coating to fabricate devices with the structures ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al and ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al. The incorporation of P3HT significantly alters the device characteristics from ohmic to Schottky-type behavior. The device ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al shows better I–V characteristics compared to ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al in terms of its lower leakage or reverse saturation current (of the order ∼10–7 mA). Furthermore, the Schottky barrier height (φB) at P3HT/Al for both devices is of the same order ∼0.9 eV. Impedance Spectroscopy (IS) measurements were performed on all the devices in the frequency range of 40 Hz–100 MHz to understand the device characteristics and AC charge transport phenomenon. An equivalent circuit model with two RQ parallel (Q: constant phase element) components in series best fits the IS data, highlighting the separate roles of bulk and interfacial effects. Mott–Schottky analysis confirms p-type behavior for Cu2O films, whereas Cu–O exhibits n-type characteristics arising due to oxygen vacancies. This work demonstrates the potential of Cu2O/P3HT interface engineering for optoelectronic device applications.

Introduction

The metal oxide-based charge-selective layer in organic and perovskite solar cells is an important area of research due to their cost-effectiveness, abundance, nontoxicity, favorable energy level alignment for hole transport, and high stability. Binary metal oxides naturally exhibit n-type character because their Fermi energy lies close to the conduction band minimum. The choice of p-type metal oxides that can function as hole transport and electron-blocking layers is limited primarily to copper and nickel oxides. The fundamental requirement for better charge carrier selectivity is that a hole transport layer (HTL) should possess higher hole mobility and lower mobility for electrons near the hole contact. Two stable phases of binary oxides of copper, namely, cuprous oxide (Cu2O) and cupric oxide (CuO), are among the very few p-type oxides. They provide better charge selectivity by allowing holes to transport while blocking electrons, thereby decreasing charge carrier recombination at the electrodes. These oxides also benefit from the low formation energy of copper vacancies, which gives rise to appreciable hole concentration and high hole mobility originating from the coupling of copper 3d and oxygen 2p states in the valence band. While both Cu2O and CuO are direct-band gap materials, the former has a slightly higher band gap (E g ≈ 2.0–2.5 eV) and hole mobility (μp ≈ 256 cm2V–1s–1) compared to CuO (E g ≈1.3–1.7 eV; μp ≈ 48.44 cm2V–1s–1).

Moreover, to facilitate the efficient transfer of holes at the interface between the HTL and the photoactive layer (PAL), the valence band maximum (VBM) of the HTL should align with the HOMO of the donor polymer in organic solar cells (OSCs) and with the VBM of the perovskite layer in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Literature studies have explored the integration of copper oxides into photovoltaic devices to evaluate the viability of Cu2O and CuO as HTLs. For example, Chatterjee and Pal fabricated an ITO/Cu2O/CH3NH3PbI3–x Cl x /PCBM/Al device, where Cu2O was prepared via the successive ionic layer adsorption and reaction (SILAR) method, achieving a device efficiency of 8.30%. Zuo and Ding fabricated p–i–n PSCs using Cu2O and CuO HTLs prepared by a low-temperature in situ conversion technique, achieving efficiencies of 13.35% (Cu2O) and 12.16% (CuO). Similarly, Guo et al. employed RF magnetron sputtering to deposit Cu2O and CuO as HTLs in OSCs (ITO/HTL/PTB7:PC71BM/Ca/Al), resulting in efficiencies of 8.51% and 6.38%, respectively.

Cu2O and CuO films have been deposited by various techniques such as electrochemical deposition, physical vapor deposition, e-beam evaporation, pulsed laser deposition (PLD), sputtering, and the SILAR technique. − Electrodeposition (ED) is a bottom-up technique that facilitates the deposition of target films onto a conductive substrate (ITO or FTO) by an electrochemical reaction. This technique has advantages over other solution-processed, chemical, and physical thin-film deposition techniques, which require high-temperature treatment for phase formation in a high vacuum environment, whereas ED requires only the desired concentration of the precursor solutions for film deposition. Thus, ED is a versatile, simple, and cost-effective technique for preparing uniform thin films and for producing PSCs and OSCs on a larger scale in the future. Moreover, by manipulating parameters such as the pH and deposition time of the precursor solution, the crystallinity, thickness, and morphology of the film can be controlled.

The p-type behavior of Cu2O thin films refers to Cu vacancies and excess oxygen. It is significant to note that the semiconducting material’s behavior (p- and n-type) can be altered by varying its atomic vacancies, depending on the experimental techniques and conditions. Reported literature also shows the formation of n-type Cu2O films grown on a conducting substrate via an electrodeposition technique. − The p-type or n-type conductivity of Cu2O depends on the pH of the electrolytic (plating) solution. The electroplating solution with a pH range of 4.5–5.5 is used for growing n-type Cu2O thin films, whereas p-type conductivity of Cu2O films can be achieved by using a basic electrolyte solution (pH > 7). Hence, it is significant to have control over the pH of the precursor solution for the deposition of n-Cu2O and p-Cu2O films while fabricating Cu2O as an HTL for OSCs or PSCs using the ED technique.

In this work, we have successfully grown a pure-phase Cu2O and a mixed-phase Cu–O (Cu2O + CuO) film on an ITO substrate. The layers were coated for 5 min, with transparency depending on the deposition time. The current–voltage (I–V) analysis of ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/Al devices reveals ohmic behavior, which was further modified by placing an intermediate layer of P3HT. P3HT was introduced atop the copper oxide layer to mimic a commonly used solar cell device structure. P3HT was selected as an interlayer due to its p-type semiconducting nature, with a suitable HOMO level for hole extraction to the bottom electrode (ITO/FTO) via Cu oxide films. In the literature, CuO (or Cu2O)/P3HT hybrid HTLs have been shown to outperform pristine copper oxide layers, primarily because of the improved energy-level alignment at the organic–inorganic interface that enhances exciton dissociation and promotes more efficient charge separation. , In most of the literature, the charge transport dynamics at the oxide and polymer interface are largely ignored. Also, the shift from ohmic to Schottky-type behavior is not addressed in the literature, although it is commonly observed.

In this paper, we studied the interface and the bulk properties of the p-type oxide and hybrid p-type oxide/ polymer (P3HT) based devices via I–V measurements and impedance spectroscopy (IS), analyzing the results using the Nyquist plot and Bode plot. All the devices were fitted with two RQ parallel (Q = constant phase element) components in series, suggesting two different relaxation processes, i.e., bulk and interface phenomena. The bulk and interface play distinct roles in terms of their frequency response. Relaxation time (τ) of charge carriers in the bulk is on the order of τ ∼ ns, whereas at the interface, it is in the range of (τ ∼ ms − μs). This significant difference shows that interfacial phenomena play a crucial role in charge transport. Thus, there is a necessity to optimize the interfacial properties to achieve better device performance.

Experimental Details

Materials

All chemicals and materials used in the experiment are of a high standard of purity. Indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates with a thickness of ∼100 nm were commercially obtained from Ossila. Copper (II) sulfate (CuSO4) anhydrous, Lactic acid (C3H6O3), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets, and P3HT (Poly­(3-hexylthiophene)) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Aluminum (Al, 99.999% purity) for thermal evaporation was purchased from Kurt J. Lesker and deposited to a thickness of ∼100 nm.

Fabrication of Copper Oxide-Based Devices

Copper oxide films were electrodeposited on conducting ITO substrates by using a conventional three-electrode cell system (CHI-601C). The electrolyte bath solution was prepared using 0.4 M CuSO4, 3 M lactic acid, and 5 M NaOH to maintain the solution at pH 11. Copper oxide films were deposited on a precleaned ITO substrate, working electrode, using a constant applied voltage of −0.3 V vs Ag/AgCl (reference electrode) at room temperature, with a platinum (Pt) coil serving as the counter electrode. Using the electrodeposition technique, we prepared pure-phase Cu2O films. To remove volatile surface contaminants, the prepared films were annealed at 100 °C in a vacuum. Furthermore, annealing the Cu2O films at 300 °C on a hot plate for 30 min under ambient conditions yielded a mixed Cu–O phase (containing both Cu2O and CuO), as shown in Figure S1a. The detailed procedure of electrodeposition, optical analysis of the prepared films (UV absorption, band gap analysis, and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) analysis), and the phase purity of Cu2O and Cu–O were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) in our previous work.

To understand the charge transport mechanism in copper oxide films, we fabricated two sets of devices. The first set consisted of ITO/Cu2O or Cu–O/Al structures. The second set of devices (ITO/Cu2O or Cu–O/P3HT/Al) was prepared by spin-coating a layer of P3HT (20 mg mL–1 in dichlorobenzene) onto the Cu2O or Cu–O layer at 2000 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, an Al top electrode (∼100 nm) was deposited by thermal evaporation using a metal shadow mask (OSSILA) under a high vacuum (∼10–6 mbar) conditions. Cross-sectional FESEM analysis was carried out for the ITO/Cu–O/P3HT device structure (Figure S1b). The Cu–O layer exhibits a uniform thickness of ∼150 nm, consistent with the values reported in our previous study. Additionally, FESEM surface morphology analysis of both Cu2O and Cu–O films revealed uniform and compact surfaces. The P3HT layer deposited on top of the copper oxide film showed a thickness of ∼200 nm, forming a well-defined bilayer structure (Figure S1b). These measurements confirm the reproducibility and uniformity of the copper oxide films within the device architecture.

The current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of all the fabricated devices were analyzed using a source-measuring unit (OSSILA) under ambient conditions. The I–V measurements were typically swept in the range of −5.0 V to +5.0 V at a step rate of 20 mV/s to understand the diode characteristics. Moreover, impedance spectroscopy (IS) analysis was subsequently carried out using a Keysight E4900A and Agilent 4294A impedance analyzer to gain a deeper understanding of charge transport in both the bulk and interfacial regions of the device. All the electrical measurements were carried out on devices with an active area of 4 mm2 (0.04 cm2).

Results and Discussion

Electrical Measurements and Junction Properties

The electrical characterization were carried out on the fabricated ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/Al devices under dark conditions. The energy diagrams for ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/Al devices are schematically shown in Figure S2. The dissimilar work functions of 4.7 eV and 4.2 eV for ITO and Al, respectively, suggest that the device should display a Schottky diode behavior. However, the current–voltage (I–V) measurements for ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/Al show ohmic behavior, as shown in Figure a. The inset of Figure a shows the plot in a semilogarithmic scale, where the Cu–O-based device shows a higher current magnitude compared to the pure Cu2O-based device, suggesting better utility of Cu–O devices. The defect states (Figure b) at the interface can significantly alter the electronic properties by modifying the barrier height and Fermi level. Such modification reduces the dominance of the barrier and can give rise to ohmic behavior instead of Schottky. The Schottky barrier plays a crucial role in solar cell devices for better charge separation and for optimizing the cell to achieve higher open-circuit voltage (V oc) and efficiency. Thus, to passivate these defect states, we introduced a homogeneous layer of P3HT that was spin-coated over Cu2O and Cu–O films, and the I–V characteristics of the devices show a rectifying Schottky-type behavior (Figure c). The device ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al shows better I–V characteristics compared to ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al in terms of its leakage or reverse saturation current (of the order ∼10–7 mA), as seen in Figure d. The energy band alignment demonstrates the charge collection in the devices toward their respective electrodes: holes via the valence band of Cu–O or Cu2O to ITO, and P3HT acting as a barrier and an interlayer between the copper oxide layer and the top electrode, Al.

It is important to note that for the ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al device, the series resistance is higher by ∼250 kΩ compared to ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al, which indicates Cu2O films are more resistive than Cu–O films. Charge carriers can be trapped at the metal/P3HT and P3HT/semiconductor interfaces, introducing additional recombination paths inside the depletion/space-charge regions. Furthermore, the existence of interfacial inhomogeneity at the interface leads to a modification of the barrier height, arising from defects, surface roughness, and possibly due to nonuniform chemical composition at the interface, which leads to deviations from ideal behavior. ,

Nyquist Plot

The impedance (frequency (f)-dependent) data of the sample can be expressed as a complex, Z(f) = Z′ (f) − iZ″(f), where Z′ is the real and Z″ is the imaginary part of the impedance (Z=Z′2+Z″2) . The impedance data can be analyzed through a Nyquist plot, i.e., the real part (Z′) versus imaginary (Z″) for all the devices, as shown in Figure a–d, respectively. The data for all of the devices were fitted with equivalent circuit models, as shown in the inset of Figure . The circuit of ITO/Cu–O/Al consists of a resistance (R) in series with two parallel RQ (Rresistance and Q constant phase element (CPE)) components, whereas the other set of devices (ITO/Cu2O/Al, ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al and ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al) were fitted with an additional component inductor (L) at high frequency. The inductance (L) in the circuit near the origin at high frequency could be due to the contact pads (anode and cathode) of the device, which are in close proximity to one another. This sometimes occurs in devices such as organic solar cells, memristive devices, and capacitor devices, where the electrodes are separated by a small distance. The Nyquist plots do not display ideal semicircular behavior, indicating a deviation from a pure capacitive response. To account for this nonideal impedance behavior and obtain an accurate fit, the ideal capacitor is therefore replaced by a constant phase element, Q. The quality and the roughness of the film can be correlated with the component Q of the circuit. These inhomogeneities lead to depressed or asymmetric semicircles in the Nyquist plot, indicating a wide distribution of relaxation times in the devices. The impedance of a CPE is defined as ZCPE(Q)=1(jω)nP , where ω is the angular frequency (ω = 2πf) and P is a constant parameter, and the exponent “n” takes values between 0 and 1, describing the deviation from ideal capacitive behavior. The exponent n provides a physical interpretation of CPE (Q). If the value of n corresponds to 1, then “Q” behaves as an ideal capacitor (C). Furthermore, if the value of n is zero (0), “Q” will act as a resistor (R), and if it is 0.5, then it is a Warburg element and Q will be equal to 1/R. The effective capacitance (C eff) for an RQ circuit is calculated using the expression: Ceff=R(1n−1)P(1n) for the devices.

Relaxation Time

The relaxation time (τ) of the charge carrier in the device is calculated using the Bode plot phase angle (φ) versus frequency (Hz), as shown in Figure a–d, for all the devices. The phase angle is related to Z′ and Z″ expressed as φ=tan−1(Z″Z′) , and relaxation time can be calculated as τ=12πfm , where f m is the peak frequency. Distinct peaks are observed in all the sets of devices for all bias voltages (0.0–2.0 V) and are tabulated in Table S2. The peaks at high frequency (∼100 kHz–1 MHz) and low frequency (∼100 Hz–10 kHz) correspond to the relaxation process in the bulk and at the interface of the device, respectively. For the devices ITO/Cu2O or Cu–O/Al and ITO/Cu2O or Cu–O/P3HT/Al, the bulk relaxation time arises from dipolar and space-charge polarization in the copper oxide layer, , where the incorporation of P3HT introduces additional energetic disorder and localized hopping pathways that slightly modify the bulk response. The interfacial relaxation time can be associated with charge trapping and detrapping processes at the interfaces of ITO/Cu2O or Cu–O, Cu2O or Cu–O/Al, or Cu2O or Cu–O/P3HT, and P3HT/Al interfaces. The applied bias potential (0.0 to 2.0 V) strongly influences the bulk and the interface of the devices, and the f m shifts toward a high-frequency range. The observed shift in relaxation frequencies under applied bias can be attributed to the electric-field-induced modification of charge carrier dynamics. When the potential is applied, the electric field alters the occupancy (trapping/detrapping processes) of trap states in the bulk and at the interface of the device. This effect is particularly pronounced in the P3HT-containing structures, where the polymer introduces additional shallow trap states and energetic disorder. Another relaxation process is observed in ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al devices (Figure c and d), at the midfrequency (∼100 kHz) range, which may be due to the multiple grain boundaries in the system. In all these devices, the charge carriers take a longer time (∼1 ms) to redistribute at the interface, whereas in bulk, they redistribute quickly (∼1 ns) with applied frequency. The role of bias furthermore suggests that the relaxation process can respond differently at the interface and in the bulk. The larger variations of peak frequency at the interface due to bias indicate that the interface plays a significant role in relaxation processes and charge transport.

AC Conductivity

The AC conductivity σ­(ω) of all the devices was analyzed from the plots of ln­(σ) versus ln­(f) (Figure ). The behavior of the frequency-dependent conductivity follows Jonscher’s double power law, expressed as: σT(ω)=σDC+A1ωS1+A1ωS2 . The symbol σDC denotes the DC conductivity, which is independent of frequency, and, ω, defined as 2πf, corresponds to the angular frequency. The constant parameters, A 1 and A 2 depend on both temperature and material properties, while S 1 and S 2 (0 < S < 1) are the frequency exponents that describe the frequency dependence of the conductivity and provide insight into the charge transport mechanism. In Figure , AC conductivity for all the devices is analyzed using Jonscher’s double power law, with exponent S 1 at lower frequency and S 2 at higher frequency. The values of the exponents lie between 0 and 1 and are tabulated in the inset of Figure . The values of S 1 and S 2 for ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al are 1.0 and 0.30, respectively, suggesting that in the lower frequency regime, AC conductivity has a stronger frequency dependence. Whereas for the devices ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al and ITO/Cu2O/Al, both exponents S 1 (0.45 and 0.44) and S 2 (0.37 and 0.30) show similar sensitivity toward the high- and low-frequency regions. However, the ITO/Cu–O/Al device (S 1 = 0.21; S 2 = 1.0) shows stronger frequency dependence of AC conductivity at higher frequencies. The variation in the sensitivity of AC conductivity with frequency for different devices may be due to the relative disorder in the films.

Capacitance versus Frequency (C–F)

The capacitance–frequency (C–F) characteristics for Cu2O and Cu–O-based devices are shown in Figure a–d. The devices ITO/Cu2O/Al, ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al, and ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al exhibit a gradual increase in capacitance with increasing bias voltage (0.0 V–2.0 V) in the low-frequency range (100 Hz to 10 kHz). At higher frequencies (100 kHz to 1 MHz), the capacitance for all applied voltages saturates to a lower, nearly constant value. The increase in capacitance at low frequencies can be attributed to several factors: (i) energy band mismatch at the Cu2O/P3HT interface, which can create interface states; (ii) the presence of surface defect states in both Cu2O and P3HT layers that trap charge carriers. This leads to the accumulation of charges at the interface and within the bulk, resulting in bias-dependent capacitance at low frequencies. As the frequency increases, the device’s capacitance often becomes independent of the applied bias voltage. At higher frequencies, the capacitance is independent of the applied bias voltage, suggesting that charge storage mechanisms like traps and interface states are no longer able to fully respond to the high-frequency AC signal. However, the device ITO/Cu–O/Al (Figure c) exhibits an opposite trend at low frequencies, where the capacitance decreases with increasing bias voltage. The capacitance of the device decreases from approximately 9 to 3 nF as the bias increases from 0.0 to 2.0 V in the low-frequency range (100 Hz to 10 kHz), while the high-frequency (100 kHz to 1 MHz) capacitance remains nearly constant, indicating minimal bias dependence in the bulk response. This behavior suggests that trap states enable passivation of charge carriers rather than accumulation, leading to a reduction in the interfacial capacitance with applied voltage.

Mott–Schottky Plot Analysis

The Mott–Schottky plot (Figure ) is a measure of the capacitance (C) of the space-charge layer as a function of the applied potential (V), which is used to investigate the charge carrier density (N) of the films. The plot is expressed as: 1C2=2eεrε0NA2(V−Vfb−kBTe) , where e is the electron charge, k B is Boltzmann’s constant, T is the absolute temperature, A is the surface area of the device, V fb is flat band potential, εo is the permittivity of the vacuum, εr is the relative permittivity. For Cu2O and Cu–O, we adopted the values of 7.60 and 10.26, respectively. The flat band potential (V fb) is calculated from the intercept on the voltage axis (x-axis).

Mott–Schottky plots calculated from the capacitance data in the high-frequency range for Cu2O and Cu–O films (R 2 for Cu2O and Cu–O is ∼0.99).

The Mott–Schottky plot calculation was done at 10 kHz frequency. The flat band potential (V fb) is the potential required to bring back the band bending of the semiconducting material to its flat band position, which was 0.66 V and −0.06 V for Cu2O and Cu–O, respectively. P-type behavior for Cu2O and n-type behavior for Cu–O were deduced by analyzing the slope observed in Figure . The n-type nature of the Cu–O film is attributed to oxygen vacancies, which occur when oxygen atoms leave the crystal lattice. The Cu2O film, when annealed at 300 °C, releases some of the oxygen atoms, O0↔12O2(g)+V0+2+2e− from its surface, as shown in Figure . As each oxygen atom leaves the lattice, it forms an oxygen vacancy (VO+2) , and leaving two extra electrons at the vacant site, , thereby making the film oxygen deficient and Cu rich. Furthermore, the acceptor density of the p-type Cu2O semiconductor (N A = 7.84 × 1017 cm–3) and the donor density of the n-type Cu–O semiconductor (N D = 1.10 × 1016 cm–3) were calculated from the slope of 1/C2 vs V­(voltage).

Conclusion

In this study, we have explored the charge transport behavior of the device structures ITO/Cu2O/Al and ITO/Cu–O/Al. Furthermore, the role of P3HT as an intermediate layer was also investigated in the device geometries of ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al and ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al. The electrical characterization of devices ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al and ITO/Cu–O/P3HT/Al shows rectifying Schottky behavior with a distinct rectification ratio of 103. ITO/Cu2O/P3HT/Al device exhibits superior performance with reduced leakage current (∼10–7 mA) compared to its Cu–O counterpart. However, the high ideality factor (η ≈ 8–10), large series resistance, and barrier inhomogeneity suggest nonideal film morphology in these devices due to the presence of trap states and possible grain boundaries. Nevertheless, the present results establish a foundation for further development of oxide/organic rectifying Schottky devices. Impedance spectroscopy measurements in the range of ∼40 Hz to 100 MHz and equivalent circuit modeling with two RQ parallel components in series indicate the presence of two relaxation processes in all devices, characterized by bulk relaxation (τ ∼ 10–9 s) at high frequency and interfacial relaxation (τ ∼ 10–3–10–6 s) at low frequency. AC conductivities were analyzed through the double Jonscher’s power law, and the results from this analysis show different sensitivities of AC conductivity in the higher (∼MHz) and lower frequency regions (kHz). Capacitance–voltage (C–V) measurements confirm p-type conductivity for all devices, except for the ITO/Cu–O/Al structure, which demonstrates n-type behavior. This n-type nature is potentially due to oxygen vacancies at the surface. The research findings suggest that incorporating P3HT as an intermediate layer with appropriate interfacial engineering significantly affects the charge transport and can improve overall device performance. Furthermore, polymer–oxide heterostructures can be an efficient alternative to silicon-based devices for various applications in next-generation solar cells and optoelectronic devices demanding mechanical flexibility, low-temperature processing, and low-cost device fabrication, enabling the development of emerging portable, large-area electronic technologies, as well as smart window applications.
