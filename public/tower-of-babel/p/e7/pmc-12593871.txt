Beyond traditional models: a content analysis exploring the applicability of harm reduction in eating disorder care
Authors: Tanya Gilmartin, Emma Spiel, Jasmin Watson, Michelle Roberton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorders can place an individual at significant risk of physical harm. Despite this, there is minimal guidance on how to empower individuals to recognise and minimise potential harms to their physical safety. This paper explores the recommendation to incorporate harm reduction strategies within eating disorders care by an eating disorder consultation service to clinicians working with individuals who have eating disorders. The aim of this paper was to examine how often, and in what contexts, HR approaches were deemed applicable during eating disorder specific public mental health case consultations.MethodsAdult case consultation records from an eating disorder consultation service in Australia were retrospectively analysed to examine the frequency at which harm reduction approaches were considered applicable to the consultation question. Content analysis was performed to examine the context in which the potential utility of harm reduction was discussed.ResultsOut of 45 adult secondary case consultation records accessed, almost three-quarters reported that a harm reduction framework was discussed as part of the secondary consultation. Content analyses revealed that 33.33% of consultations recommended harm reduction in the context of treatment engagement, 21.21% in the context of treatment ambivalence, and 45.45% in the context of concerns for physical safety whilst an individual was engaged in eating disorders care.ConclusionsOur results give preliminary indication that harm reduction approaches may have utility in supporting individuals with eating disorders to reduce their risk of physical harm across the spectrum of motivation and treatment engagement. Given the high incidence of physical harms associated with eating disorders, further studies examining the application and effectiveness of harm reduction approaches for eating disorders would likely be of benefit to the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-025-01426-4.Keywords: Eating disorder, Harm reduction, Minimisation, Medical, Physical, Risk, Adjunct approach

Introduction

Eating disorders have the highest risk of mortality of all mental health diagnoses due to the physical health complications [25] and elevated risk of death by suicide [29, 34]. For the purposes of this paper, harm is defined as problematic physical consequence to an individual’s body as a direct result of disordered eating behaviour. Eating disorders are associated with significant risk of physical harm to almost every system in the human body [23, 24]. For example, restriction of food or fluid intake has been associated with bradycardia, alopecia and infertility [23], and purging behaviours (i.e., vomiting, use of laxatives) are associated with dehydration, dental concerns and hypotension [24].

Individuals experiencing eating disorders may continue engaging in disordered behaviours due to perceived benefits such as emotional coping (i.e., binge eating to numb emotional experience) or predictability/routine [3, 13, 16, 26]. These are often referred to as ego syntonic aspects of the experience [11]. Eating disorder behaviours also often persist during early treatment stages [7, 22], and may persist beyond this due to high treatment drop-out rates [19, 35]. In addition, lapses and relapses to risky disordered eating behaviours are common following treatment (between 10 and 25% of individuals; [6, 25]), and up to one third of individuals who complete treatment experience a longstanding (“severe and enduring”) eating disorder (symptoms which significantly impact upon functioning, despite substantial previous active treatment; [6, 25, 30]. People experiencing eating disorders therefore may be at risk of physical harm across a spectrum of motivation and engagement in care.

Harm reduction (HR) approaches include practices that aim to reduce the adverse health, social and economic consequences of risky behaviour without directly targeting cessation of risky behaviour [5, 12, 31]. HR approaches empower individuals to understand the risk associated with their behaviour and incrementally work towards an individual taking responsibility for their own safety with the support of their treatment team and community [1, 12, 39]. Principles of HR can be applied across medical, psychiatric, and occupational health and safety domains and examples can be found across any domain where harm is possible, from wearing a helmet while riding a bicycle to using a clean razor when engaging in non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI; [5, 21]). HR approaches are implemented across the spectrum of care with individuals who use alcohol or other drugs, or engage in NSSI, with evidence indicating reduced harm and increased engagement in treatment [5, 12, 15, 20, 31]. It is important to differentiate HR from risk management, which refers to the treatment team’s role in reducing harm. Risk management and HR can usefully coincide within care.

HR approaches have been applied to improve safety and enhance quality of life for people experiencing longstanding eating disorders who were not engaged in recovery-oriented approaches [2, 36, 38], and in the context of palliative care, where there is a focus on easing suffering through comfort care [36, 40]. People with a longstanding eating disorders have been found to experience improved quality of life, reduced eating disorder symptoms, increased value on relationships, and decreased hopelessness and distress [37], in addition to experiencing the treatment as less threatening where a HR approach has been applied [10]. Whilst some suggest that HR-focused approaches might be an appropriate alternative to recovery-oriented approaches [39], it is also possible that HR approaches could usefully support individuals experiencing eating disorders to take responsibility for their safety, regardless of motivation to change or commitment to a treatment program [12]. It is striking that the observed outcomes of implementing a HR framework with individuals experiencing a longstanding eating disorder are consistent with findings from the implementation of HR frameworks within other areas of mental health, such as alcohol and other drug (AOD) use and with individuals who engage in NSSI [15, 20, 37]. We therefore posit that HR may also be applicable to reducing physical harms associated with eating disorders where risky behaviour is yet to cease, re-emerges or persists. However, at the time of writing, there is no current discussion in the literature about incorporating HR frameworks more broadly in eating disorders care.

Study aims

Whilst preliminary work has commenced to support the translation of HR into eating disorder care [Link to CEED HR Resources: https://ceed.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Harm-Reduction-Booklet-2024_Final.pdf], it appears that no studies have examined the use and applicability of HR approaches earlier in an individual’s experience of an eating disorder, and across the trajectory of eating disorder treatment and care. Therefore, the aim of this paper was to examine how often, and for what reasons, HR approaches were recommended during eating disorder specific mental health case consultations. Specifically, our research has two primary objectives:

To identify whether HR resources were recommended within secondary case consultation reports provided to mental health clinicians regarding individuals with eating disorders by a Victorian eating disorder consultation service, and the frequency of recommendation.

To identify whether HR resources were recommended within secondary case consultation reports provided to mental health clinicians regarding individuals with eating disorders by a Victorian eating disorder consultation service, and the frequency of recommendation.

To systematically explore the written content within secondary case consultation records in order to identify patterns within the records as to the context in which clinicians were seeking consultation and the recommendation to consider HR.

Method

Setting

The Victorian Centre of Excellence of Eating Disorders (CEED) is a state-wide program of the Victorian public mental health system whose team of senior clinicians provide secondary consultation to health and mental health clinicians regarding individuals with eating disorders. CEED provides secondary consultations to both public and private mental health clinicians, delivering upwards of 100 consultations annually. In the majority of cases, the consultation consists of one meeting, with a focus on enhancing care, improving service connections, and identifying referral pathways. Although the reasons for seeking consultation are varied, consultations are typically sought in complex cases involving co-occurring issues, elevated risk, or severe eating disorder presentations, aiming to empower clinician decision making and strengthen support for individuals, their families, and supports. Initially, a health or mental health clinician completes an online consultation request form outlining background information about the individual with an eating disorder and the reason/goal for request for secondary consultation. Primary consultations (i.e. direct consultations to individuals with eating disorders or their support network) are not provided by CEED.

For the majority of cases, only one consultation meeting is provided. A brief consultation report outlining the recommendations provided within the consultation is completed following the secondary consultation. The consultation summary report is then provided to the consultee, in addition to being filed electronically within the same folder as the information received within the request for secondary consultation. For the current paper, only the consultation report for the initial consultation was included, as follow-up consultations were infrequent, and tend to follow a different format.

For this study, anonymised records of secondary consultations conducted by CEED regarding individuals presenting with eating disorders over a 12-month period between August 2021 and August 2022 were retrospectively accessed to examine the frequency and reasons for recommendation of HR approaches in eating disorders care. The methodology described within this manuscript (detailed below) refers to both the data included within the request for secondary consultation form and the consultation summary report.

Ethics information

Upon advice from Melbourne Health institutional ethics committee, the methodology of the study was considered to be consistent with the definition of a Quality Assurance project as (a) The outcomes of the study were expected to inform ongoing development of HR resources for eating disorders (improved health care); (b) The project sought to identify perceived clinician concerns about delivering care for individuals with eating disorders (problems within healthcare) and identify HR as a potential solution for perceived concerns; and, (c) The project sought to evaluate the introduction of new HR resources, by understanding the context in which they were recommended. The methodology was considered to be consistent with principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and National Health and Medical Research Council National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2023 [4, 18, 28].

Data extracted

Data extracted from the consultation summaries included age of the individual presenting with an eating disorder and the reasons for consultation request, whether HR resources were discussed with the consultee and the reasons for suggesting a HR approach. No data was gathered about if a person had a longstanding ED, so we were unable to consider this population separately.

Data analysis

A content analysis was conducted in order to identify the patterns that emerged within the case consultation requests and summary reports regarding the contexts in which HR was discussed [8]. A content analysis was selected as the goal of the current research was to understand patterns and trends that emerged within text, in the form of secondary case consultation records.

Data preparation and units of analysis

The written consultation request in addition to the consultation summary report provided to consultees by CEED for each case were included within the content analysis. The content analysis performed on the open text in the consultation requests and reports summarised the concerns raised by consultees that resulted in recommendations of HR resources. The context in which HR was discussed within the key consultation questions, discussion points, and recommended resources were the units of analysis throughout the content analysis process, in line with recommended processes [14].

Coding scheme and coding consistency

Following the initial phase of becoming familiar with the data, codes were generated that reflected the latent concepts that were evident in the reasons for consultation request and consultation recommendations. The coding scheme was developed inductively from the data using a constant comparative method in which each newly identified item that was coded within a category was compared with each already assigned within that category. The categories were clearly outlined (described within results) by the primary coder (TG), who coded each of the consultation requests and summaries. A second researcher (MR) reviewed the code definitions and a subsection of the consultation requests and consultation summaries. The researchers agreed on the code definitions in all cases as the most appropriate description of the data.

Following the identification of latent concepts, we identified the frequency and percentage of occasions that each of the contexts emerged within the case consultation reports.

Reflectivity

This research was conducted by CEED clinicians about their own work, with some authors involved in developing CEED resources and one participating in both data coding and the consultations studied. It must be noted that at the time the consultations were conducted, the CEED team had not considered a retrospective analysis, so the consultations were not influenced by plans for retrospective analysis. While efforts were made to maintain objectivity grounded in clinicians sound knowledge of evidence-based treatments and the system of care for eating disorders, the team’s involvement in the consultation process may have unintentionally influenced their perspectives and interpretations.

Results

A total of n = 33 (73.3%) of all complete consultation requests for service users over 16 who were not engaged in FBT involved some form of discussion regarding the applicability of HR and were included in this analysis. Content analysis performed on case consultation reports revealed three main contexts in which HR was recommended to clinicians, First, HR approaches were seen as applicable when the consultation was focused on identifying referral pathways. Second, HR approaches were seen as applicable when the consultation specified service user treatment ambivalence/low motivation in the context of disordered eating behaviours that can elevate physical risk. Lastly, HR approaches were seen as applicable when the service user was continuing to engage in disordered eating behaviour that can elevate physical risk in the context of treatment engagement. The three contexts are discussed separately below.

Context one: potentially harmful behaviours in the context of initial response/treatment delay

In 11 cases (33.33%), HR approaches were discussed as a potentially useful approach while the individual with an eating disorder was yet to engage with treatment services. Consultation records showed that clinicians requesting consultation expressed concerns about the physical safety of new service users and existing service users who had recently disclosed potentially risky eating behaviour. At times, this was in the context of the eating disorder being the primary presentation, and at times, the emergence of eating concerns in the context of an existing mental health concern. Clinicians were often expressing both a need to support the person to access appropriate treatment supports and/or pathways and to address concerns around physical safety whilst they were assisting people into appropriate treatment/care.

Context two: potentially harmful behaviours yet to cease due to ambivalence within treatment

Concerns regarding an individual with an eating disorder who demonstrated ambivalence for treatment in addition to engaging in disordered eating behaviour that could potentially place their medical stability at risk was a core concern in seven (21.21%) of the case consultation records. Case consultation records showed that clinicians held concerns about risk of harm for service users where the individual was not yet ready to address this, or the service user was not accepting a referral to a service that was able to offer evidence-based eating disorder treatment. This presented where the client may have been otherwise engaged in treatment for another presenting concern, or where there was motivation to address some, but not all eating disorder behaviours.

Context three: potentially harmful behaviours yet to cease with treatment

An individual with an eating disorder who was engaged in treatment with a mental health clinician, however, that individual was continuing to engage in disordered eating behaviour that could potentially put them at risk of medical instability was the focus on consultation in 15 (45.45%) of the completed case consultations. Consultation records revealed clinician concerns about physical deterioration despite active treatment participation, particularly in cases involving long-term eating disorders, slow but steady behaviour change, or ongoing but not yet ceased disordered behaviours. This also included ways to support people to navigate longer term physical risks associated with previous behaviours.

Discussion

The aim of this paper was to examine how often, and for what reasons, HR approaches were recommended during eating disorder specific mental health case consultations. To achieve this, we explored the frequency with which HR approaches were considered applicable within case consultations for adults experiencing eating disorders within Victoria’s public mental health system. We then explored the key contexts in which HR approaches were recommended. Our study showed that HR approaches were frequently discussed, with three-quarters of all adult case consultation records referring to the potential utility of this in supporting the service user and the requesting clinician to address risk of potential physical harms. Content analysis showed three major contexts regarding the potential application of HR approaches; First, in the context of initial response/treatment delay; second in the context of ambivalence within treatment, and lastly where potentially harmful behaviours are yet to cease with treatment.

Harm reduction in the context of initial response/treatment delay

In approximately one-third of case consultation records, clinicians expressed concern regarding eating disorder-related physical harm in the context of early intervention and prior to the engagement in eating disorder specific treatment. Clinicians reported concerns about being able to navigate risk of physical harm where the person was experiencing delays in accessing a comprehensive treatment approach. In the broader mental health literature, such as when addressing AOD and NSSI, HR approaches have been shown to empower individuals to take responsibility for their health and wellbeing, whilst they attempt to address their use with outcomes being reduced harm, as well as increased treatment seeking behaviour and access to healthcare [12, 20]. Embedding HR approaches within interventions which have been shown to be effective in supporting individuals experiencing eating disorders to reduce ED behaviours whilst awaiting more intensive treatment, such as Single Session Intervention [9, 32, 33], could have clinical utility in help to address the need for addressing potential harms before the person is able to access a more comprehensive treatment approach.

Harm reduction in the context of ambivalence within treatment

Nearly one fifth of consultation summaries discussed the usefulness of HR in the context of low/fluctuating motivation to engage in ED behaviour change whilst the person was engaged with mental health services, but not eating disorder specific interventions. This suggests that clinicians are also grappling with the challenge of supporting people to reduce their risk of physical harms where there is engagement with care, but fluctuating motivation. Further research is warranted to examine whether this is the case for a broader number of clinicians. HR approaches have been related to increased motivation to change in both the AOD and longstanding eating disorders literature [15, 21, 37], by providing people experiencing ambivalence with the opportunity to build rapport and develop steps towards change in a non-judgemental environment [21]. Given that ambivalence within eating disorder treatment is common [3, 13], HR approaches may function to meet individuals at their current stage of motivation to change and uphold autonomy in decision-making [12, 39]. There is no current evidence that implementing HR in care for individuals with eating disorders will improve motivation to change, however, observations from the broader mental health literature, noted above, indicate that the notion warrants further investigation.

Harm reduction in the context of harmful behaviours yet to cease with treatment

Nearly one-half of the consultation summaries examined in our study indicated clinician concern regarding the risk on an individual due to eating disorder behaviour whilst the individual was actively engaged in behaviour change attempts. Given that evidence-based treatment programs such as Enhanced Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (CBT-E; [7]) and Specialist Supportive Clinical Management (SSCM; [22]) are designed to span several months to completion, there will consequently be a period of time where people continue to engage in potentially harmful behaviours, whilst they develop new skills and gradually let go of risky behaviours. Our study suggests that clinicians are seeking support around how to navigate risky eating and exercise behaviours within the context of evidence-based treatments. Where HR has been implemented within other areas of mental health suggest that HR approaches are effective at mitigating risks whilst the person works actively to reduce behaviours in treatment [12], and can be used as a foundational framework for the entire length of the treatment relationship [21]. As a consequence of incorporating HR approaches in other fields, there is an effect of reduced session time spent focusing on risk, reduced potential for pauses in treatment due to medical instability, and reduced anxiety of the care team, family and friends [12], all of which could be transferrable benefits within the context of eating disorders.

Limitations

Our study highlights clinician concerns for the safety of individuals across the spectrum of eating disorder care, and proposes that HR approaches may offer a potential solution to these concerns. However, outcomes should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Firstly, our study examines decisions made by CEED clinicians and their consultees about using HR in cases involving physical risk. While evidence-based recommendations were prioritised, some clinicians were involved in developing the HR resources, which may have influenced their alignment with the framework. Although findings align with HR use in other mental health contexts, potential conflicts of interest should be acknowledged. The data of this study may only be interpreted as preliminary and requiring further testing. Second, clinicians typically to seek case consultation in the context of working with individuals experiencing co-occurring mental health and psychosocial challenges, higher physical and psychiatric risk and more severe ED presentations. As such, the frequency with which HR approaches may be useful in cohorts where presentations are less severe and/or complex and in other contexts requires further examination. Third, whilst a number of studies have examined approaches to eating disorders care with a HR component for individuals with longstanding eating disorders [10, 37], to our knowledge, the effectiveness of HR approaches among individuals with eating disorders is yet to be empirically tested within the contexts described. Fourth, although individuals with lived experience of an ED and their supports were consulted in the development of the HR resources for eating disorders (see Supplemental material) we did not consult individuals with a lived or living experience of an eating disorder in the design of our analyses. The methodology of this study also did not allow for the examination of the acceptability and usefulness of HR approaches for service users, or to service user examine outcomes. Inclusion of lived experience advocates and perspectives is shown to be crucial for enhancing service provision, particularly in the mental health sector [17]. Finally, the data available did not provide a manner of assessing whether in individual was presenting with a longstanding eating disorder, so we were unable to consider this population separately.

Implications

The results of this preliminary examination into the frequency and contexts in which HR may be useful, indicate potential merit for further research examining the implementation of HR approaches across the spectrum of care for eating disorders, from initial response through to treatment and post-treatment support. More specifically, ongoing exploration of the implementation of HR approaches throughout the stepped system of care for eating disorders [27] from prevention through early intervention to treatment and ongoing psychosocial support will provide further insight into the applicability of HR in eating disorders, and potential benefit of broader implementation.

Conclusions

Previous research has indicated that the implementation of HR frameworks among individuals with longstanding eating disorders can contribute to reduced harm and increased engagement with treatment services [10, 15, 37]. This finding indicates substantial parallels with findings regarding HR approaches in other areas of mental health, such as AOD use, and NSSI [12, 15, 20]. However, no previous research has considered a broader application of HR principles in eating disorders care. Therefore, the aim of this paper was examine how often, and for what reasons HR approaches were deemed applicable during eating disorder specific public mental health case consultations. Our results indicated that a HR approach was frequently recommended for adults presenting with an eating disorder, and three core contexts for the recommendation of HR were identified using content analysis. Further studies examining the application and effectiveness of HR approaches for eating disorders would likely be of benefit to the field.
