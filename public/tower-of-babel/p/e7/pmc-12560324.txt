Assessment and management of suicidality in a mental health survey among poverty affected adolescents in Nepal
Authors: Rakesh Singh, Karuna Chhetri, Parinati Khanal, Sabina Maharjan, Emily Garman, Mark J D Jordans, Crick Lund, Nagendra Luitel, Kamal Gautam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent suicidality currently ranks as the third leading cause of death among adolescents, affecting global adolescent population, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Nepal. This study explores the assessment of suicidality as an adverse event in adolescent mental health research in Nepal and outlines practical strategies for integrating suicide management protocols into mental health surveys in LMICs. It offers guidance on suicide risk assessment and referral pathways for vulnerable adolescents and contribute to suicide prevention efforts.MethodsThis study, a part of a cross-sectional survey with 490 adolescents aged 13–15 in public secondary schools in Kathmandu valley, used a three step approach of the Suicide Management Protocol (SMP) to identify and manage participants at risk of suicidality. Using the specific item under the measurement of mental health among adolescents and young people at the population level (MMAPP) tool followed by administering the suicide screening tool, those at risk of suicidality were identified by the researchers. Clinical assessments were then conducted by the psychosocial counsellor to categorize risk levels using Beck Suicide Intent Scale (BSIS) and provided appropriate management according to the severity.ResultsOf total 490 participants, while the single item under the MMAPP assessment identified 33 (6.7%) participants at risk, further screening using the suicide screening tool confirmed 25(5.1%) as positives, by the researchers. The psychosocial counsellor clinically assessed the 25 participants screened as suicide risk positive and found that 10 (40%) as high risk, 8 (32%) as moderate, and 7 (28%) as low risk. The clinical management of the cases at a differing level of risk was then managed as per the designated clinical pathways by developing protective behavior and reducing suicide related risk factors. All the participants were found to be safe and functioning normally through the course of clinical management, ranging from 2 to 6 sessions.ConclusionsOverall, integrating suicidality measures in mental health surveys was found to be effective suicide assessment and preventive strategy for adolescents in Nepal. Developing suicide management protocols within mental health surveys is essential and feasible for real-time monitoring and response to suicidal behavior among at-risk populations in LMICs, including Nepal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24943-y.Keywords: Adolescent suicidality, Suicidality assessment, Suicidality management, Suicide management protocol, Mental health survey

Introduction

Suicidality is a major global concern, with over 720,000 deaths by suicide annually, equating to one person every 40 s [1]. A meta-analysis of epidemiological and school-based studies reported that the international prevalence of suicide ideation and attempts among youth ranges from 14 to 23% and 5–16%, respectively [2]. It is the third leading cause of death among adolescents, with 73% of global suicides occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Notably, adolescents aged 10–19 make up 25% of Nepal’s population and may represent a significant share of country’s suicide risk [3], underscoring the need for adolescent suicide prevention to be a top priority. A study conducted among Nepalese adolescents aged 13–17 showed suicidal attempt rate to be 12.42% [4]. A recent mental health survey with representative sample of adolescent population found the prevalence of suicidality to be 4.1%, with 3.9% current suicidal thoughts and 0.7% lifetime suicide attempt in Nepal [5]. Findings from a global school based survey reported that the prevalence of suicidal ideation in Nepal was 13.59% [6]. The suicide rate in Nepal is 9.8 per 100,000 people, with a significant proportion affecting those aged 15 to 29 [7].

Suicide is strongly associated with depression and anxiety among adolescents and youth [8–10]. In South Asia, the prevalence of depression among those who die by suicide ranges from 8% to 79% [11]. Despite its serious impact, suicidality is often overlooked in mental health research due to challenges in assessment and limited clinical resources, especially in LMICs. The lack of mental healthcare infrastructure, socioeconomic disparities, and cultural stigma further complicate accurate measurement and intervention. Identifying high-risk populations through comprehensive evaluation is crucial for targeted prevention and resource allocation [12].

Mental health surveys can offer a cost-effective method for identifying individuals at risk of suicidality, enabling better allocation of resources and timely psychosocial support [13]. Indeed, incorporating suicide risk assessment into mental health surveys can raise awareness, reduce stigma, promote early intervention, and ultimately improve mental health outcomes by reducing the number of suicides [14]. This ultimately can lead to influence public health strategies, improve mental health services and reduce suicide rates. However, integrating suicidality assessment in LMICs remains challenging due to limited training and resources.

To ensure participant safety in mental health research, suicidality should be systematically managed as an adverse event. Suicide Management Protocols (SMPs) provide structured risk assessment, continuous monitoring, and crisis intervention strategies [15]. For instance, pairing brief suicide assessments with immediate telephone counselling and follow-up care has been shown to significantly reduce suicidal thoughts, attempts, and deaths [16]. Similarly, brief suicide prevention interventions in primary healthcare settings have been proven effective in lowering future suicidality risk [17–19]. However, while high-income countries (HICs) have established SMPs, researchers in LMICs have a very low opportunity for training in suicide management, resulting in inconsistent protocol implementation [20]. A scoping review conducted to identify core components and strategies under suicide and risk management protocol revealed that out of 22 peer-reviewed papers included, none were from LMICs [15]. This indicates that LMICs face significant challenges in developing and implementing SMPs in mental health research.

Nepal faces significant challenges in suicide surveillance due to the absence of a centralized database. Currently, police records and media reports serve as primary data sources, often underestimating the true burden of suicidality [21, 22]. To address this gap, this study, which is a part of a larger research – improving adolescent mental health by reducing the impact of poverty (ALIVE) survey [23], examines the methodology and implications of assessing suicidality as an adverse event in mental health research among Nepalese adolescents. Unlike previous studies that focus solely on prevalence and associated risk factors, this research provides concrete guidance on suicide risk assessment and management pathways (see Supplementary File 1 for flowcharts related to assessment and management). By integrating an SMP into mental health surveys, we aim to improve suicide prevention strategies, ensuring better support for at-risk adolescents in LMICs.

Methods

Sample

The population in the current study was Nepalese students from public secondary schools in Budhanilkantha municipality and Kathmandu metropolitan city of Kathmandu Valley, who were enrolled in grades 6, 7, or 8 in the study year 2023/2024. To be included in the study, participants had to be aged 13–15 years old, be fluent in Nepali, and living in adverse condition of poverty as defined by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) [24] This was assessed by screening potential participants using an adapted poverty screening tool consisting of eight items adapted from multidimensional poverty index to identify adolescents living in poor households. The survey was completed by 635 adolescents from eight public secondary schools. Of these, 491 were identified as living in poverty and subsequently completed the Measurement of Mental Health Among Adolescents and Young People at the Population Level (MMAPP) [25] tool. One adolescent later withdrew from the study, resulting in a final analytical sample of 490 participants.

Procedure

Data collection and probable case identification and categorization

As all adolescent participants were minors, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or guardians, and written informed assent was obtained from the adolescents before collecting data from them in the study. This study is a part of the larger study (cross-sectional survey of ALIVE project), which was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from an ethical review board of the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) (ref. no. 896) and the ethics committee of King’s College London (ref. no. HR/DP-23/24–38680). The survey was conducted by conducting a face-to-face interview with the participants using a structured questionnaire with an open data kit (ODK) on tablets. To measure the adolescent mental health conditions including depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and sleep quality, the MMAPP tool [25] was used in the ALIVE survey [23]. For contextual relevance, the 25-item MMAPP tool was expanded to 28 items by breaking down double/triple-barreled questions, particularly those related to sleep and appetite, into separate and clearer items.

The MMAPP tool includes one item that assesses if a participant has a risk of suicidality (particularly suicidal ideation) – “During the past two weeks, how often have you had thoughts that you would rather be dead or thoughts of hurting yourself?” The participants who responded ‘sometimes’, ‘often’, or ‘always’ were then directed to follow the ALIVE Suicide Management Protocol (SMP). The details regarding the SMP are presented in supplementary file 2. According to the protocol, the researchers used a short screening tool to identify the probable cases of participants with risk of suicidality as a part of an initial screening process. The suicide screening tool consists of four items related to suicidal risk behavior which were adapted from suicide screening questions from Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ) kit [26], Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) [27], and Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) [28]. The four questions included: 1) Have you felt that life is not worth living? 2) Have you thought about harming yourself (e.g., cutting, taking poison, jumping, banging your head against walls)? 3) Have you ever hurt/harmed yourself (in any of the ways mentioned in question #2) before? 4) Do you feel that you want to harm yourself right now? Participants who reported current suicidal thoughts or a recent suicide attempt (within the past 30 days) in the suicide screening tool were classified as high-risk cases by the researchers. All other participants, based on their self-report responses in the suicide screening questions, were categorized as low-risk by the researchers. For participants with a probable high risk of suicidality as identified by the researchers, immediate and active referral to the ALIVE psychosocial counsellor for further clinical assessment and appropriate management was done. Subsequently, identification of all such high-risk cases was recorded using a standard template (Adverse Event Reporting and Management Form, see Annex 1 of Supplementary File 2) by the researchers and reported to the study investigator and counsellor within 24 hours of the case identification. For the probable low-risk suicidality identified through the initial screening, the researcher explained to the participant that they seem to be facing difficulties and offered a passive referral to a psychosocial counsellor, and provided them with information related to the national suicide prevention helpline (1166) and Transcultural Psychosocial Organization Nepal’s (TPO Nepal’s) helpline (1660-010-2005), and other relevant service providers (see Annex 2 of Supplementary File 2). TPO Nepal is one of the mental health organization dedicated towards enhancing the mental wellbeing of the population through various clinical and counseling services, research, interventions or trainings and advocacy for mental health.

Clinical assessment

Adolescents identified as probable cases of high risk of suicidality by the researchers were actively referred to the psychosocial counsellor for further clinical assessment. The psychosocial counsellor deploying this role had a Bachelor’s degree in Arts with a psychology and sociology major, along with a six-month certified psychosocial counseling training accredited by the Council for Technical Education and Vocational Training (CTEVT). CTEVT is a national institution responsible for developing skilled professionals in various technical and vocational fields, including psychosocial counseling in Nepal. The six-month training encompasses developing essential skills in communication, intervention techniques, and ethics, assessing and managing suicidality, including suicide risk evaluation, crisis intervention, and targeted counseling approaches. A dedicated module on suicide intervention within the 6-month training offered specialized training in risk assessment, legal aspects, and counseling strategies, supported by supervised practice and role-playing. This type of training equips psychosocial counsellors to effectively handle suicidal cases among adolescents, making them valuable mental health professionals for suicide prevention in low-resource settings.

After getting in contact with the actively referred participants, a written consent was obtained by the counsellor using the consent form (see Supplementary File 3 for the format). Subsequently, the clinical assessment followed a 5 step process. First, an intake interview gathered comprehensive background information, including developmental, medical, and psychosocial history, while building a strong therapeutic alliance. Second, a clinical interview was performed to assess the current suicidal ideation, plans, past attempts, and lethality using open- and closed-ended questions. Third, an evaluation of risk and protective factors was done, focusing on depression, anxiety, and substance use and also identifying strengths like family support and positive coping mechanisms [29–31]. Fourth, suicidal intent was further assessed using Beck’s Suicide Intent Scale (BSIS), a tool widely used in Nepal, to evaluate the severity of suicidal risk [32]– [33]. Based on these clinical assessment process, the psychosocial counsellor categorized the participants into low, moderate, and high risks of suicidality. Low-risk cases showed minimal suicidal intent and strong protective factors, while moderate-risk cases involved suicidal thoughts and mild self-harm without immediate plans. High-risk cases exhibited severe symptoms, recent attempts, and poor psychosocial functioning, requiring immediate intervention. Lastly, clinical case management strategies were tailored to the risk level, ensuring appropriate support and care and these were also recorded using the Adverse Event Reporting and Management Form template and reported to the study investigator. The details of this stepwise process are provided in Supplementary File 4.

Data analysis

In addition to demographic information, score of the suicidality related item under MMAPP tool and suicidality categorizations (based on clinical assessment process), qualitative information was compiled from clinical assessments including interviews and risk evaluations. The qualitative information obtained during the various counseling sessions was documented in written format using two forms; participant intake form and clinical session form. These forms were used to document the session specifics, interventions, observations, impressions, plans, and progress of participants across multiple counseling sessions. The templates for these recording forms are provided in Supplementary File 5. These narratives were crucial in monitoring the level of risk and determining appropriate intervention strategies during unfolding case management.

Both qualitative and quantitative approaches were used to describe the data. The risk of suicidality across age and gender was presented using frequency and percentage distributions. The qualitative data were analyzed using narrative synthesis.

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of 490 participants included in the study, the mean age of the participants was 13.85 years (SD = 0.73), with 52.5% being girls, 22.2% were from grade 6, 42.8% from grade 7, and 34.8% from grade 8. With regards to ethnicity, most of the participants were Janajati (49.1%), followed by Brahmin/Chhetri (28.3%), Dalit (10.4%), Madhesi (6.5%), Muslim (4.1%), and others (1.2%).

Probable cases at risk of suicidality

Of the total participants, 33 individuals (6.7%) gave a positive response to the suicide-related item in the MMAPP assessment. Among them, 25 participants (75.8%) were classified as probable high-risk cases and were actively referred for clinical assessment and management. The remaining 8 participants (24.2%) were considered low-risk cases and were passively referred by being provided with suicide-related helpline numbers.

Clinical case management

Clinical case management was done based on the three categories of clinical risk of suicidality, i.e., low, moderate, and high risk.

Clinical management of cases with low risk

In managing low-risk cases of suicidality, the counsellor focused on providing emotional support, building a strong relationship with the adolescent, and teaching coping strategies. The first session involved a thorough assessment of the adolescent’s mental, physical, and socioeconomic status, assessing risk and protective factors, and providing psychoeducation. Informed consent was obtained to involve parents, and while some adolescents were initially hesitant about breaching confidentiality, they agreed after understanding it was for their safety. In the second session, the counsellor introduced anger management, relaxation techniques, and self-care practices as needed to help the adolescents manage their emotions. Follow-up sessions showed that most of these adolescents had adjusted well and were no longer at risk. Once they were stable and functioning normally without any suicidal thoughts, these cases were closed. The detail of the session is included in the Supplementary File 6.

Clinical management of cases with moderate risk

Participants with moderate risk were characterized with elevated levels of anger and hostility, self-harm attempts within the past month, strained interpersonal relationships with peers and family members leading to a lack of support, feelings of loneliness and hopelessness, and persistent suicidal ideation.

In response, the counsellor implemented a structured intervention plan, closely monitoring the adolescents’ symptoms and distress levels weekly to detect any escalation in suicidal thoughts or self-harming behaviors. The intervention included strategies similar to those used in low-risk cases, with additional tailored approaches. These included frequent monitoring, advanced coping strategies such as progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) and grounding exercises —techniques that help adolescents stay present by reconnecting their bodies to the environment (e.g., identifying five things they see, four things they feel, three things they hear, two things they smell, one thing they taste) — as well as strengthening the adolescent’s support network through role-plays and communication skill-building exercises. A comprehensive safety plan was developed in collaboration with the adolescent, caregivers, and school teachers focusing on identifying warning signs and fostering a supportive environment both at home and at school. For a session-wise detailed plan, refer to the Supplementary File 6. Case study 1 below represents the typical feature of the clinical case management of a participant with moderate risk for suicidality.

Case 1: moderate risk for suicidality

A 14-year-old female, studying in grade 8, living with her parents in Kathmandu, was referred for counseling due to a high risk of suicidality identified during data collection. She reported feelings of hopelessness, fear, guilt, low self-esteem, suicidal thoughts, and a history of self-harm (cutting her hand two months prior). She disclosed past physical abuse by a close relative (no longer present in her life), which had caused significant psychological distress, poor relationships at home and school, and also the adolescent lacks emotional support due to her father’s absence. Based on the clinical assessment, the participant was identified as a moderate risk of suicidality. Before the counseling process, her parents were also informed about the biopsychosocial challenges of adolescent as well as about the safety measures at home. The counsellor provided emotional support and validation of her feelings, provided psychoeducation, allowed the adolescent to practice positive social skills, and also guided her through a safe place exercise (a guided visualization/imagery to create a mental image of a calming and secure environment) throughout different sessions and helpline numbers were shared. The counsellor offered continuous emotional support, focusing on the safety measures and supporting wellbeing of adolescent. Assertiveness and fear management skills were taught using role-playing exercises. Over the time as the session progressed, the participant improved her patterns of daily functioning, strengthened interpersonal relationships, effectively applied the skills learned during sessions, and received consistent validation and support from her caregiver, along with helpline access for ongoing assistance. In this way, the session was considered closed.

Clinical management of cases with high-risk

For the clinical case management of high-risk participants, approximately 4–6 sessions were planned, tailored to the needs of each participant. The adolescents in this category exhibited impulsive behavior, had easy access to lethal means of suicide, had experienced major losses such as death or separation of parents, faced exam failures, struggled with poor emotional regulation, and had attempted self-harm within the past month. Additionally, they often lacked supportive relationships, with some caregivers refusing to help, and had pervasive feelings of hopelessness, despair, and persistent worthlessness, alongside disturbed sleep patterns. These factors significantly contributed to their current suicidal ideation and suicide-related behaviors.

Similar to moderate-risk case management, high-risk participants involved comprehensive risk assessment, psychoeducation, and development of positive coping strategies. However, additional measures were implemented to address the elevated risk, including an immediate safety plan, 24-hour follow-up sessions for safety reassessment, and, when necessary, early psychiatric consultation for medical stabilization. Therapeutic techniques, such as disputing negative thought patterns, activity monitoring, and identifying social resources, were introduced to address suicidal ideation more intensively. Caregivers were actively engaged through both in-person and telephonic counseling to ensure collaborative support and address emergent issues. Resilience-building strategies, such as mindfulness, gratitude practices, and positive self-affirmations, were emphasized throughout the sessions for long-term recovery. Frequent monitoring and reassessments were conducted to ensure sustained safety and progress before case closure. Two typical case studies related to high-risk participants managed either through counseling sessions (see Case 2) or requiring psychiatric consultation and medication (see Case 3) are detailed in the boxes below.

Case 2: high risk suicidality managed through psychosocial counseling

A 15-year-old female from a low socioeconomic background was referred for psychosocial counseling due to high suicidality risk, confirmed through clinical assessment. The participant faced significant distress due to harsh parental discipline, emotional neglect, interpersonal conflicts, and maladaptive coping mechanisms, including self-harm.

Counseling sessions focused on providing emotional validation, fostering a therapeutic alliance, and developing a comprehensive safety plan involving caregivers. Additional measures included addressing immediate safety concerns, psychoeducation on adolescent challenges, and empowering the participant with adaptive coping strategies such as emotion regulation, anger management, and relaxation techniques like PMR. To enhance her support system, the "Me Mapping" exercise (involving enabling participant to self-identify own emotions, beliefs, values, problem, strength, and resources as well as develop goals and strategies for change) was employed, and communication skill-building activities were integrated. Collaborative sessions with her mother aimed to improve family dynamics and foster a more supportive environment. Regular suicide risk monitoring was conducted throughout therapy, using open-ended and structured tools to track shifts in ideation, intent, and protective factors, ensuring the client’s safety at every stage. A crisis intervention plan was developed and communicated to caregivers, providing clear steps to manage emergencies effectively. Collaboration with school teachers was emphasized to provide additional monitoring and academic support. Positive reinforcement techniques were incorporated, encouraging the participant’s consistent use of coping strategies, with caregivers actively involved in reinforcing progress at home. Trauma-informed techniques were introduced to help the participant process past experiences of harsh discipline, minimizing long-term psychological effects. Additionally, a long-term follow-up plan was established, scheduling check-ins over month to ensure sustained progress and well-being.

These integrated measures resulted in significant improvement in the participant’s emotional regulation, coping abilities, and interpersonal relationships. The comprehensive approach ensured her safety, enhanced her support system, and facilitated a holistic recovery, highlighting the effectiveness of psychosocial counseling in managing high-risk suicidality.

Case 3: high risk suicidality managed with psychiatric consultation alongside counseling

A 14-year-old female, studying in grade six, was referred for counseling due to high suicidality risk. Clinical assessment revealed a history of sexual abuse, poor emotional regulation, lack of social support, and multiple self-harm attempts using sharp objects. During initial counseling, emotional support and validation were provided, along with grounding exercises to manage overwhelming emotions. Psychoeducation about adolescence as a vulnerable period and healthy coping strategies was introduced. The participant’s mother was informed and educated on suicide prevention and emotional support strategies for safety measures. Despite this early intervention, the adolescent attempted to jump off a cliff following a dispute at school, emphasizing the severity of her condition immediate psychiatric consultation was arranged, leading to a diagnosis of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and anxiety disorder. Medication (Tab. Sertraline 25mg) was prescribed, and a follow-up was planned. The adolescent continued counseling alongside psychiatric care, focusing on emotional regulation, anger management, and trauma-informed support. Close monitoring revealed significant improvement in her emotional state, coping skills, and daily functioning. Self-harming behaviors ceased, and suicidal ideation resolved. The integration of psychiatric treatment with psychosocial counseling was critical in addressing the adolescent’s complex needs, leading to recovery.

The importance of seeking professional help was reemphasized, and helpline numbers were provided. With sustained progress and stability, the case was terminated.

Discussion

Out of the 490 adolescents included in the study, 25 (5.1%) were initially identified by the researchers as exhibiting signs of high suicidality risk. However, clinical assessments confirmed that only 10 of these participants were truly at high risk, representing 2.04% of the total study population. This prevalence rate aligns with a previous study conducted by NHRC, which reported 4.1% risk of suicidality among adolescents [5]. Based on the findings of the global school-based survey, relatively higher prevalence of suicidality (13.59%) among school-going adolescents [6] was found, possibly influenced by factors such as a wider age range (13–17 years) and larger study population, and the use of a single item to identify suicidal ideation tool. Our study also revealed that female adolescents are at a higher risk of suicidality compared to males. This aligns with the findings from previous studies in Nepal [4–6] as well as studies conducted in other countries [34–37]. Our study also found that adolescents of younger ages (13 years) had the lowest prevalence of suicidality based on clinical assessment, consistent with previous research showing that older adolescents are at higher risk [38].

In our study, two-fifth of the participants who were clinically assessed were classified as having a high risk of suicidality. During the clinical assessment process, we followed a detailed approach to assess the risk of suicidal behavior, starting with gathering background information, conducting clinical interviews, evaluating risk and protective factors, using screening tools, and finally categorizing the risk based on these steps. This method is consistent with the clinical guidelines proposed by Sarkhel et al. [39] and Shah et al. [40] for the assessment and management of psychiatric emergencies among children and adolescents in India, which recommend the risk assessment with an initial screening using suicide screening questions, followed by identifying risk and protective factors [40]. Other experts with research, clinical expertise, and experience in suicide screening have also recommended a similar strategy for screening and assessing suicidality risk using a three-tiered approach [41, 42]. They recommend that the first step involve using suicide screening tools to identify those at positive risks of suicide, followed by a clinical assessment using a structured questionnaire to categorize the participants who screened positive as per the severity. This should also include open-ended conversations with the participants, their caregivers and teachers to gain detailed insights into the participant’s thoughts and behaviors, risk factors (e.g., access to lethal means or history of suicidal attempt) and protective factors (e.g., family support). The final step is to plan for the interventions and follow-ups based on the results of clinical assessment, categorizing participants into low, moderate, and high risk.

In general, low risk cases showed fewer or no recent self-harm attempts, and possibly better emotional regulation skills or milder distress which allowed quicker adjustment with minimal intervention. They also showed more openness to parental involvement after reassurance. The moderate risk cases often showed strained interpersonal relationships such as conflict with family or friends which limited their emotional support. They also had recent engagement in self-harm attempts within the past month. They also reported feelings of loneliness, hopelessness, and persistent suicidal ideation. The high risk cases showed multiple compounding vulnerabilities such as major life losses, academic stressors, lack of supportive caregivers. They had frequent persistent hopelessness, worthlessness, and disturbed sleep. Across all levels of suicidality risks, clinical case management followed a relationship-centered and holistic approach that combined individualized assessment, resilience building interventions, and collaborative safety planning by engaging family, school, and sometimes medical professionals to ensure long term recovery and well-being among adolescents.

Furthermore, our SMP illustrates the need for safety planning and ensuring no means of contact with self-harm in the first stage of suicide management for participants with high risk of suicidality. This is also in line with the guidelines for adolescent depression in primary care (GLAD-PC) toolkit, which strongly suggests safety planning by restricting access to lethal means and supervision by family members [43]. Similarly, our SMP also aligns with the Suicide Prevention Resource Center (SPRC) toolkit in terms of safety planning, referral, treatment, and follow-up [44]. Similar to the suicide management pathway adopted in our study, other guidelines have also recommended categorizing levels of suicide risk and tailoring management accordingly. For example, they advise immediate assessment by psychosocial service providers for individuals at moderate to high risk, while recommending referral or follow-up for those identified as low-risk cases [45].

One of the key findings from our study showed that accessibility to mental health support is essential for the identification and management of participants at risk of suicidality. As illustrated in this study through SMP, for a clear path of managing suicidality – (1) a mechanism of case identification followed by categorization of the severity of the risk, (2) a primary point of contact to handle high risk cases, (3) a referral mechanism in cases of severe life-threatening conditions, is required within researches to make mental health support accessible. A few hours of training built within data collection training to research assistants seemed to be effective in identifying the participants at high risk of suicidality requiring immediate care by the psychosocial counsellor. The psychosocial counsellor, the primary point of contact, an undergraduate with six months of psychosocial counseling training, also proved to be effective in clinically assessing and managing most of the participants at risk of suicidality. Establishing such a non-specialist focal contact is a feasible approach in countries with limited access to specialized mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) providers. Having a consistent point of contact throughout the study also supported participant engagement, especially for those in need of ongoing services. For severe cases requiring specialized care, which are often very minimal, a built-in referral mechanism enabled the primary point of contact to connect participants with psychiatrists or clinical psychologists. This approach aligns with the principle of task-sharing and is well-suited for resource-limited settings with a shortage of mental health professionals. In addition to this, providing support through the study in local travel expense to those participants who cannot afford to reach the referral site makes it accessible to the participants to receive the specialized care that they require. The suicide management mechanism followed in this study was found to be an effective strategy for suicide prevention among the study participants. Embedding SMPs within mental health research—and equipping primary healthcare workers with training in suicide risk assessment and clinical management, supported by ongoing supervision—can significantly enhance suicide prevention efforts, even in low-resource settings. According to the principals of the schools from the study site (personal communication, March 31, 2025) during the stakeholders meeting related to the ALIVE project, the adolescents have been doing really well (both from a psychosocial wellbeing and academic perspective) after receiving the MHPSS services through this survey who were initially identified as having at high risk of suicidality. This approach recognizes the need to adapt strategies to the local context and utilize existing healthcare personnel to manage adverse events like suicidality [46]. The analysis of suicides reported as adverse events in the Northern Denmark Region indicated that the majority of the suicidal cases did not seek support from mental health facilities and thereby recommended areas for improvement including training, documentation, communication, and data sharing [47]. This further illustrates the importance of SMPs and even more so in LMICs.

In this study, we found that parental engagement plays a crucial role in managing suicidality among minor populations. To ensure their involvement, informed consent should be obtained from parents and adolescents, outlining confidentiality limits for their safety and well-being. The study also highlighted the complexities of breaching confidentiality in cases of suicidal or self-harming behaviors. On the positive side, active engagement of parents/caregivers often provided adolescents with emotional support and ensured continuation of counseling sessions, reducing the risk of adverse outcomes. This aligns with the study done in Kathmandu valley highlighting suicidality risk and how it is associated with family support among adolescents [48]. However, in the present study, disclosing the risk of suicidality in adolescents to their parents sometimes resulted in resistance to seeking counseling services or refusal to allow their child to continue participating in the study. These findings underscore the counsellor’s critical role in breaching confidentiality sensibly to prioritize the adolescent’s safety and facilitate proper case management. This finding is similar to a study done in New Zealand among 9–13-year-olds, where emotional support from caregivers was a major protective factor [49]. In the present study, helpline numbers were provided as a passive referral services to adolescents identified with low-risk by the researchers as a means to seek psychosocial support anytime in the future if they require it. Such crisis lines often help to reduce present distress or suicidal ideation and can provide referral to mental health care [50], an important strategy for suicide prevention [51].

Overall, embedding SMP in mental health surveys revealed that it was an important public health strategy for the identification of adolescents at risk of suicidality and suicide prevention among them. Some HICs have also identified rigorous research as an important contribution towards suicide prevention at the national level [52]. The National Mental Health Strategy and Action Plan (2020–2025) of Nepal prioritizes protecting and promoting the mental health of people with lived experience of mental health conditions and advancing inclusive, evidence-based service delivery nationwide [53]. However, Nepal has yet to develop a national suicide prevention strategy and action plan. The National Adolescent Health & Development Strategy (NAHDS, 2018) of the Ministry of Health and Population seeks to promote adolescent mental health and provide psychosocial counselling services to help adolescents cope with stress, regulate emotions, and solve problems [54]. The SMP developed and implemented in this study aligns with both National Mental Health Strategy and Action Plan and NAHDS’s overarching goals, yet such protocols are not consistently applied across mental health surveys and research. We recommend that health policies, acts, and regulations formally prioritize SMP as a national suicide prevention strategy.

As there is a lack of population-based surveys and suicide databases from the perspective of suicide surveillance in LMICs such as Nepal, developing and implementing SMPs within mental health research irrespective of study designs could be an essential method of identification of at-risk populations and suicide prevention. Addressing adverse events like suicidality promptly improves data quality and maximizes the use of existing resources particularly in settings with limited mental health professionals. This not only advances scientific knowledge but also strengthens the local healthcare infrastructures building the capacity to manage suicide risk as an adverse event. Therefore, we recommend NHRC, institutional review boards and ethics committees to include a provision in the research proposals to assess and manage the risk of suicidality in mental health research by embedding SMP within all health-related research as part of researchers’ ethical and social responsibility, creating opportunities for its integration into ongoing and future studies.This will help ensure the safety and well-being of participants in mental health research. Similarly, the screening tools and SMPscould be utilized in various sectors, be that research, program, or clinical service delivery, which could help to ensure the safety of both service providers/researchers and clients/research participants.

A key limitation of this study is that it was embedded within the larger ALIVE project, which recruited participants solely from the Kathmandu Valley and from poverty-affected settings. This recruitment frame reflects the ALIVE project’s focus on adolescents experiencing urban poverty—a major social determinant of mental health [23]. While this focus was appropriate for the ALIVE project’s aim of developing and pilot-testing an intervention to strengthen self-regulation and mitigate the mental health impacts of poverty, it provides valuable insights for assessing and managing suicidality into a particularly vulnerable adolescent population within research process. Additionally, the lessons learned from this study provides an important foundation for refining and expanding the use of SMP across more diverse populations and research contexts in Nepal and similar LMICs.

Conclusion

Assessing suicidality as an adverse event in mental health surveys is a complex but important action, especially in LMICs like Nepal. Our study revealed, like other contexts, Nepalese adolescents are at risk of suicidal behavior, and this is more so for girls. We also found that leveraging the integration of measuring suicidality in mental health surveys can overcome some of the structural challenges in Nepal in terms of suicide prevention. Developing a suicide management protocol in mental health surveys is vital for real-time monitoring and response to suicidal behavior among at-risk populations in LMICs such as Nepal. Non-governmental organizations and research-based organizations should invest in developing organizational protocols for suicide reporting and management and integrate them into all programs and research irrespective of study designs to put into action by necessary infrastructure development. Such ethical practices within research could improve measurement methodologies in mental health research and overcome barriers to improve mental health outcomes and suicide prevention among adolescents in LMICs.
