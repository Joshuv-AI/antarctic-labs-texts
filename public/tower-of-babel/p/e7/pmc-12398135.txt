Development of the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) optimal short scale for Chinese children and adolescents: based on FasterRisk machine learning modeling
Authors: Jina Li, Tianyu Liang, Yi Hou, Gengchao Wang, Zhiyong Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) is a widely used tool for assessing anxiety, its 41-item format makes it a time-intensive method for identifying children and adolescents at high risk of anxiety. This study aims to develop an optimized version of the SCARED for Chinese children and adolescents using a novel machine learning approach, Fast and Accurate Interpretable Risk Scores (FasterRisk), to improve the efficiency of prediction and intervention.MethodThe full version of the SCARED scale and sociodemographic information were given to 8,315 children and adolescents aged 4–9 years in Henan Province, China. The FasterRisk model was utilized to select the optimal items for constructing the Chinese version of SCARED, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to determine the optimal cutoff scores.ResultsThe results showed that a 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED accurately reproduced full SCARED scores. By evaluating the performance of risk scoring models containing 1 to 8 items, the 5-item model showed the best performance in AUC (0.96), and other performance indicators, with high prediction accuracy (R²= 0.82). Under the condition of an equal number of items, the AUC value of the newly developed 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED (0.96) surpassed that of the existing SCARED-5 (0.92), with the optimal cutoff score determined to be 14.ConclusionThe 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED is a reliable self-report tool that aids users with limited time and resources in assessing anxiety among children and adolescents in China.Trial registrationEthical approval in this study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Social Development and Public Policy at Beijing Normal University (SSDPP-HSC20230014).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24187-w.Keywords: SCARED, Anxiety, Chinese children and adolescents, FasterRisk

Introduction

Anxiety is a distressing emotional state characterized by excessive and uncontrollable worry about events, activities, and potential negative outcomes [1]. Among emotional disorders, anxiety is the most prevalent condition affecting children and adolescents. Globally, approximately one in every seven children and adolescents aged 10–19 experiences symptoms of anxiety. In China, the prevalence of anxiety among children and adolescents has been reported to range from 11.3% to 18.8% [2]. Clinical interviews with 17,524 Chinese children and adolescents by Li, Cui [3] found 4.7% had anxiety disorders, with higher rates in more developed regions. If left unaddressed, anxiety can lead to more severe mental health disorders and behavioral challenges, including poor academic performance [4], decreased sleep quality [5, 6], and increased behavioral problems [7]. Therefore, accurate assessment of anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents is beneficial for timely intervention and treatment, which can support their overall physical and mental development. This underscores the critical need for developing an efficient and reliable screening tool tailored for children and adolescents to facilitate early detection and intervention.

Currently, several tools are used to assess anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents, such as the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), the Spence Children’s Anxiety Scale (SCAS), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC), and the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) [8–12]. Among these, the SCARED scale has significant advantages in assessing anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents. First, the SCARED was specifically developed to assess childhood and adolescent anxiety rather than being a modified version of a tool originally developed to assess adult anxiety [13]. Second, the scale encompasses five dimensions and evaluates symptoms based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) diagnostic criteria for specific anxiety disorders, including social phobia, generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), separation anxiety disorder, and panic disorder [14]. Additionally, while school anxiety is not explicitly classified as an anxiety disorder in DSM, the SCARED includes measures for its symptoms, reflecting the scale’s comprehensive applicability in identifying anxiety-related issues. Finally, the scale is internationally recognized as an effective and reliable screening tool for assessing anxiety in children and adolescents. It has demonstrated strong psychometric properties and has been validated across child and adolescent populations in over 10 countries, including Finland, Germany, Spain, and Brazil [15–24]. Moreover, multiple studies have provided robust evidence for the scale’s strong psychometric properties, including internal consistency, test-retest reliability [15, 20, 23], parent-child correlations [23, 25], convergent validity [26, 27], and discriminant validity [26, 27].

Although the SCARED scale has many advantages for screening anxiety, its full version requires an average completion time of 10 min, depending on the comprehension ability of the child or adolescent [28]. This limitation reduces its practicality in everyday contexts. To address this issue, a 5-item version of the SCARED scale was developed and validated by Birmaher, Brent [9] through discriminant function analysis using clinical samples of children and adolescents in Europe and North America. Cross-cultural validation is essential for the large-scale implementation of a screening tool [29]. However, Birmaher’s SCARED-5 has not yet undergone validation in diverse cultural contexts, nor has it been tested on samples of children and adolescents in China. This highlights the urgent need to develop a short-form SCARED scale that is efficient, accurate, and culturally tailored for large-scale screenings in the Chinese population. The new short version should include fewer items to significantly reduce the completion time, thereby improving its practicality for identifying anxiety symptoms. Furthermore, it should be designed with a simplified scoring system to facilitate manual calculation of total scores. Finally, to ensure cultural appropriateness, the development sample should consist of the Chinese adolescent population, aligning the tool with the specific needs and contextual factors of this group.

To meet new requirements for concise scales, we are adopting the FasterRisk algorithm to develop a short form of SCARED. FasterRisk is a machine learning algorithm designed to construct sparse and interpretable risk scores by selecting a minimal subset of predictive items and assigning each an integer weight optimized for outcome prediction [30]. It uses a fast beam search to explore candidate models and applies a sequential rounding technique to convert real-valued coefficients into small integers, enabling transparent and practical scoring. The resulting risk score is easy to calculate manually and maintains strong predictive performance while remaining simple enough for real-world applications. In contrast, traditional factor analysis methods, such as confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), model the latent structure of psychological constructs by analyzing correlations among items. CFA retains items based on their loadings onto predefined factors and assumes that observed variables reflect underlying unobserved dimensions [31]. While CFA emphasizes internal structure and construct validity, FasterRisk prioritizes predictive accuracy. It selects items based on their contribution to outcome prediction rather than their fit within a latent structure. This distinction is particularly important for brief screening tools, where predictive performance and simplicity are prioritized. Given that the factor structure of the SCARED scale varies across cultural contexts [32–34], FasterRisk provides a flexible, data-driven alternative for generating a shortened scale optimized for practical screening. Due to these advantages, FasterRisk is increasingly being adopted by researchers to develop new short-form scales.

The present study aims to develop a short form of the SCARED for Chinese children and adolescents using the FasterRisk algorithm to improve efficiency and applicability. We will develop a risk score model using a training dataset (N = 5,544) to refine the SCARED by reducing its items. The accuracy of the resulting SCARED short form will then be evaluated using a test dataset (N = 2,771) through ROC analysis. Next, we will evaluate the screening accuracy of the newly developed short scale compared to the full SCARED in screening anxiety symptoms. Finally, the screening accuracy of the newly developed short scale will be compared to Birmaher’s SCARED-5.

Method

Participants

Participants included 8540 Chinese students in grades 4–9 from six schools in Pingdingshan, Henan province. Of the initial 8540 students, 225 (2.63%) were excluded because of incomplete data. Participants with more than 10% of missing items were classified as incomplete and excluded from the analysis. The final sample consisted of 8,315 students with a mean age of 12.35 years (SD = 1.42). Among them, 48.02% were girls and 51.98% were boys. Additionally, 46.36% reported good health, 6.55% were from single-child families, and 89.69% lived with both biological parents. Measures

The Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED)

The Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) developed by Birmaher, Brent [9], is a self-report questionnaire designed to assess anxiety disorders in children and adolescents aged 8 to 18 years. It consists of 41 items scored on a 3-point scale (0 = No such problem; 1 = Sometimes; 2 = Often). The scale evaluates anxiety across five dimensions: somatic/panic (13 items), generalized anxiety (9 items), separation anxiety (8 items), social phobia (7 items), and school phobia (4 items) [9]. The total score is calculated by summing all item scores, with higher scores indicating greater severity of anxiety symptoms. A total score above 25 is considered the cut-off for significant anxiety [9].

The SCARED scale has demonstrated strong applicability among children and adolescents in China [35]. In the present study, the internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s α) of the scale was 0.95.

SCARED-5

The 5-item SCARED was used as a self-report screening instrument for anxiety in children and adolescents [9]. The items include “I get really frightened for no reason at all”, “I am afraid to be alone in the house”, “People tell me that I worry too much”, “I am scared to go to school”, “I am shy”. Items scored on a scale from 0 to 2 (0 = No such problem; 1 = Sometimes; 2 = Often). A cutoff of 3 can be used for discriminating anxiety from non-anxiety [9]. In the present study, the SCARED-5 was used as a comparison measure for evaluating the newly developed brief scale.

Procedure

The recruitment and consent procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee for Social Development and Public Policy at Beijing Normal University. Informed consent forms were provided to all participating schools, students, and their parents. Parents who did give permission for their child to participate were asked to return a signed informed consent form. To ensure consistent adherence to the study protocol, research assistants guided each child and adolescent through the survey using a standardized research manual. The survey included demographic information, the SCARED scale, and other related scales, with an approximate completion time of 30 min. In the survey instructions, students were assured that the study would not affect their academic performance and that all information and data would be treated with strict confidentiality. Participants were encouraged to respond truthfully and independently. After the survey was completed, feedback was provided to the participating schools.

Statistical analyses

Before proceeding with the formal data analysis, the missForest method was used to handle missing data. Descriptive statistics were then conducted to examine the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants. All analyses were performed with R version 4.1.1.

The FasterRisk algorithm

To develop the optimal short form of the SCARED scale, the FasterRisk algorithm was conducted using R version 4.4.1. This algorithm generates a set of high-quality risk assessments in minutes, compared to traditional development methods [30]. To evaluate diagnostic accuracy, participants were initially divided into anxiety and non-anxiety groups based on a cutoff score of 25 on the SCARED scale. The simplified SCARED model, developed using the FasterRisk algorithm, was then employed to estimate each participant’s predicted probability of anxiety. The model’s diagnostic performance was assessed by calculating the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, which quantifies diagnostic accuracy. An AUC between 0.70 and 0.80 is considered acceptable, between 0.80 and 0.90 is excellent, and an AUC of 0.90 or greater is outstanding [36]. Furthermore, we examined the correlation between the predicted scores from the new short version of the SCARED and the original SCARED total score. We also computed the R² value for the new short version, which represents the proportion of variance explained by the model, calculated as the ratio of the regression sum of squares to the total sum of squares. Higher R² values reflect better model fit, greater precision, and more meaningful regression results [37].

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis

To determine the optimal cut-off value for the best SCARED short form, we selected the point on the ROC curve closest to the top-left corner, which balances sensitivity and specificity [36]. In this study, we calculated several metrics to evaluate diagnostic accuracy: sensitivity (the probability of screening positive when a true diagnosis is present), specificity (the probability of screening negative when no diagnosis is present), positive predictive value (PPV: the probability of having a positive diagnosis after screening positive), negative predictive value (NPV: the probability of having a negative diagnosis after screening negative), overall efficiency (chance of being classified appropriately) [38]. Additionally, we compared the diagnostic accuracy of the 5-item Chinese version of SCARED with that of existing SCARED short forms to evaluate its relative performance.

Comparison of FasterRisk model with SCARED-5

Previous studies have used confirmatory factor analysis to develop a shortened version of the SCARED scale: a 5-item version (SCARED-5). In the present study, we compared the specific items of our recommended 5-item short SCARED with those of the existing SCARED-5 (Table 4). All items on the two brief scales were different, with the five items on the SCARED-5 scale representing the five-factor structure of the original version, while our recommended 5-item brief SCARED covered four of these factors (panic/somatization, generalized anxiety, school anxiety, and social anxiety). We further compared the AUC values for diagnosing anxiety between our recommended 5-item Chinese version of SCARED, and the existing SCARED-5 (Fig. 4). The 5-item short version of SCARED achieved an AUC of 0.96, while the SCARED-5 scale exhibited a significantly lower AUC of 0.92 (P < 2.2e-16). The 5-item short version of SCARED consistently outperformed the SCARED-5 scale across varying thresholds of specificity and sensitivity. These results suggest that while both tools are highly effective, the Risk Score Model offers superior accuracy.

Discussions

The high prevalence of anxiety among children and adolescents has led to calls for screening entire school populations or specific year groups for anxiety symptoms. Although the SCARED scale provides stronger evidence of convergent validity, its length limits its practicality for widespread use. This research aimed to develop a shorter version of the SCARED scale for rapid screening of anxiety in children and adolescents, with a sample from China used to assess its applicability. In this study, we developed a new 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED scale using the FasterRisk algorithm and evaluated its diagnostic accuracy by measuring R² and AUC curves. The newly developed 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED scale presents notable advantages. For example, it is brief, easy to administer, has good diagnostic accuracy, and reduces respondent burden. These features suggest that the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED may be a better screening measure for anxiety.

The results of this study showed that the 5-item model performs well in assessing anxiety symptoms. Specifically, when the number of items was increased to 5, all indicators reached an optimal level. Beyond 5 items, the curve tended to stabilize with minimal fluctuations. Based on these findings, we established the short form as a 5-item model and proceeded to the next stage of validation. In general, an AUC of 0.50 suggests discrimination (i.e., ability to diagnose patients with and without the disease or condition based on the test), 0.70 to 0.80 is considered acceptable,0.80 to 0.90 is considered excellent, and more than 0.90 is considered outstanding [36]. In the present study, the AUC value of the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED was 0.957, indicating a very high diagnostic value in the Chinese children and adolescent population. This finding suggests that the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED offers a simplified yet efficient approach for anxiety screening. Previous studies also suggest that an R² value exceeding 0.80 suggests high model performance in explaining the relationship between predictive and response variables [37]. The R² value of the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED is approximately 0.82, indicating its good ability to explain the relationship between the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED and the full SCARED scale. According to ROC analysis, a sensitivity and specificity of 0.80 or above are generally considered to have efficient identification capabilities [39]. The ROC analysis of this scale shows that its sensitivity is 0.89 and specificity is 0.88, indicating that the model has an excellent performance in identifying children and adolescents at risk of anxiety from healthy individuals, achieving high screening accuracy. This means that the model can identify individuals who truly have anxiety symptoms to the greatest extent (high sensitivity) while reducing the possibility of incorrect judgments (high specificity). Considering the performance of 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED in the sample in terms of various indicators and R² value, and the fact that the number of items has been reduced by 89% compared to the original scale, we believe that 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED can be promoted as the best short form for the convenience of researchers and clinicians to use.

A previous study used confirmatory factor analysis to simplify the SCARED scale into a 5-item version, with each item corresponding to one of the five original subscales: Panic/Somatic Symptoms, Separation Anxiety, Generalized Anxiety, School Anxiety, and Social Anxiety [9]. The specific items include: “I get really frightened for no reason at all.” “I am afraid to be alone in the house.” “People tell me that I worry too much.” “I am scared to go to school.” “I am shy.” In contrast, the five items selected in our study—although not all aligned with the five original subscales—captured four of them: Generalized Anxiety, Panic/Somatic Symptoms, School Anxiety, and Social Anxiety. Notably, the inclusion of the School Anxiety domain contrasts with findings from previous studies conducted in other countries, where this factor was often not retained [32–34]. This difference may reflect a distinct cultural feature rooted in China’s unique educational environment. Chinese adolescents experience intense academic pressure, and both schools and families often use academic performance as the primary indicator of student success. Prior research has shown that test anxiety is strongly associated with general anxiety symptoms among Chinese students, with 72.43% of surveyed youth reporting anxiety related to exams [40]. Thus, academic stress represents a prominent and culturally specific source of psychological distress in the daily lives of Chinese adolescents.

On the other hand, the absence of the Separation Anxiety dimension in our short form may also reflect cultural differences. According to attachment theory, separation anxiety in adolescence typically manifests as distress over separation from parents. However, in the context of Chinese filial piety culture, co-sleeping with parents or expressing concern for parental well-being is socially accepted and even encouraged [41]. As a result, items such as “I don’t like to be away from my family” may lack sensitivity in the Chinese context. Behaviors considered indicative of separation anxiety in Western cultures may be viewed as normative in China. Meanwhile, the 32.03% retention rate and the 9.66% boarding rate in this study may partially explain the weakening of the separation anxiety dimension. Prolonged parent-child separation may lead to adolescents’ desensitization to separation emotions, leading to diminished self-reported responses [42]. Thus, this dimension had relatively weak predictive power in the localized screening.

It is also worth noting that the most frequently endorsed somatic symptom items in the original scale, such as “When I get frightened, my heart beats fast,” were not included in our short form. Prior research has reported significant cross-ethnic variation in the factor structure of panic/somatic symptoms [25, 34]. Among Chinese adolescents, distress may be more likely to be internalized or expressed through generalized anxiety, panic-like symptoms, or social fears, rather than being captured directly by specific somatic symptom items.

Additionally, when comparing the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED developed in this study with the most widely used Birmaher’s SCARED-5, we notice that with the same number of items, the sensitivity of the 5-item SCARED is only 74%, and the specificity is 73%, while the diagnostic accuracy of the short version developed in this study is significantly higher than that of Birmaher’s SCARED-5. This may be explained by two reasons. First, this study uses an integer-weighted scoring method to assign different integer weights to each item. The varying weight ratios among the five items primarily reflect the relative importance or influence of each item within the overall anxiety scoring framework [30]. In the present study, the differences in weights are small, making the newly developed 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED easy to score while maintaining its diagnostic accuracy. Second, Birmaher, Brent [9]developed SCARED-5 using confirmatory factor analysis, where items are selected based on factor loading values. However, these factor loadings can vary significantly across different cultures, potentially limiting the cultural adaptability of this short version in China. In summary, the 5-item Chinese version of the SCARED developed in this study can effectively distinguish between anxious and non-anxious children and adolescents in China, significantly enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of screening.

This study has the following advantages. First, since the full version of the SCARED scale takes approximately 10 min to complete [28], this would place greater pressure on children and adolescents to complete the scale, thus limiting its use in mass screening efforts. In the present study, the number of items was shortened from 41 to 5 items with good predictability and stability using the FasterRisk model, and we estimate that the short version saves 8 to 9 min of completion time, which is significant for the original scale that takes approximately 10 min to complete, and the reduction in items greatly improves screening efficiency, reducing screening costs and helping children and enabling timely professional attention and intervention. Second, integer weighting plays a critical role in enhancing the usability of a scoring tool in applied settings such as schools, clinics, or community screenings. Compared to continuous weights, small integer weights allow for simple additive scoring that can be computed manually without software or electronic devices. This is especially valuable in school settings, where screening may be conducted by non-specialist staff (e.g., teachers or counselors) and digital infrastructure may be limited. The transparency of integer scores also facilitates understanding, communication with caregivers, and ease of integration into workflows. This approach follows prior work on interpretable risk scores [30, 43], which emphasized the importance of integer coefficients for deployment in high-stakes, resource-constrained environments. Third, our 5-item scale is the first SCARED short form revised using a large Chinese sample. It better suits Chinese children and adolescents and offers a reference for localizing and simplifying SCARED.

The present study had several limitations. First, while the full SCARED is a widely validated and commonly used instrument, it remains a self-report screening tool rather than a gold-standard diagnostic measure. As such, the criterion validity established here applies to the full SCARED, rather than formal clinical diagnoses. This limits the ability to make claims about the diagnostic utility of the short form in clinical settings. Future research should include structured diagnostic interviews (e.g., the Kiddie-SADS or MINI-KID) to validate the short form against clinician-rated anxiety disorders and more robustly establish its diagnostic accuracy. Second, the large sample size provides sufficient statistical power, it may not be representative of the broader population, especially adolescents from different regions or socio-economic backgrounds. Additionally, because the sample was non-clinical, the applicability of the short form in clinical populations still needs to be further validated, especially in contexts where symptom severity and co-morbidity patterns may differ. Future studies should aim to replicate these findings in diverse geographic regions, socioeconomic groups, and clinical settings. Third, although the five items retained in the current study demonstrated strong statistical contribution and cultural relevance in a Chinese context, they represent four of the five original SCARED domains. The domain of Separation Anxiety was not represented. This omission highlights an inherent trade-off in the process of scale reduction: prioritizing brevity and feasibility inevitably means sacrificing some dimensional breadth. Future research may explore whether alternative combinations or modular approaches could preserve greater domain coverage without compromising simplicity.

Conclusion

In summary, the FasterRisk algorithm was used to develop the 5-item SCARED scale, which showed a good fit in a sample of Chinese children and adolescents. The SCARED-5 combines fewer items with high accuracy, making it a recommended tool for effective anxiety screening.
