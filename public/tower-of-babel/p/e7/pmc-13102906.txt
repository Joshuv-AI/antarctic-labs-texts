Preferences of Women for Maternal Health Services in Sidama Region, Ethiopia: Discrete Choice Experiment
Authors: Daniel Gashaneh Belay, Gizachew A. Tessema, Jennifer Dunne, Melaku Birhanu Alemu, Richard Norman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkilled maternal health services, including antenatal care (ANC) services, childbirth services and postnatal care (PNC) services, can save the lives of women and newborns. However, women in resource-limited countries, such as Ethiopia, tend to have a relatively lower uptake, partly because current services may not align with their preferences. This study assessed the stated preferences ofwomen for maternal health services in the Sidama region, Ethiopia.MethodsA multi-stage sampling technique was used to select reproductive-aged women (15–49 years) from the Central Sidama Zone, Sidama region, Ethiopia. A D-efficient discrete choice experiment (DCE) with two blocks of 24 choice tasks was designed. Six attributes, including healthcare provider attitude, availability of medication and supplies, distance to the health facility, privacy, waiting times, and service cost, were identified through literature reviews, focus group discussions and in-depth interviews with women and other stakeholders. Respondents were randomly assigned to one of the three interrelated but distinct maternal health services (ANC, childbirth and PNC). A latent class analysis (LCA) model was used to estimate preferences and estimate the relative importance of attributes, uptake probability, and willingness-to-pay (WTP). The association between individual sociodemographic characteristics and latent classes was examined using a class membership model.ResultA total of 1558 women, including 526 for ANC services, 537 for childbirth services and 495 for PNC services, were included in the analysis. For each service, three classes were identified, and notable differences in preferences were observed across the classes within each maternal health service. For ANC services, Class 1 was a medication-sensitive group (34.3%) that prioritised the availability of essential medications. In contrast, Class 2 was a price-sensitive group (21.9%), exhibiting high disutility for costly services (7500 ETB ≈ US$137). For childbirth services, Class 1 was a time-sensitive group (21.1%) exhibiting strong aversion to long waiting times (24 hours), while Class 2 was a price-sensitive group (19.3%). For PNC services, Class 1 was a medication-sensitive group (48%), whereas Class 3 was a price-sensitive group (25%). The predicted uptake probability in ideal conditions was above 96% across all maternal health services; however, it ranged from 60% for class 2 PNC services to 99% for class 2 childbirth services in average conditions. Overall, women showed a higher willingness to pay (WTP) approximately 8604 ETB (US$159) and 5118 ETB (US$95 ), to avoid childbirth services that lacked medications and supplies or had long waiting times, respectively. Sociodemographic factors, including age, residence, and education level, significantly shaped women's preferences for maternal health services.ConclusionThe cost of service, availability of medication and supplies, and waiting time were the most important attributes influencing maternal health service preferences for most of the classes of all services, while privacy and distance to the health facility were frequently the least important. Maternal health services should address the specific needs of women, such as subsidising costs, ensuring sustainable medication supply, and optimising service efficiency to enhance maternal healthcare uptake and outcomes in resource-limited settings, including Ethiopia and similar contexts. The findings will provide policymakers with valuable insights and inform future research on maternal health services in other contexts, including the Afar and Somali regions, where nomadic lifestyles are common, and data are sparse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01027-5.

Introduction

Maternal health services encompass healthcare services provided to support the health and well-being of women during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period, as well as to promote foetal and newborn health [1]. The services include antenatal care (ANC), health facility delivery and postnatal care (PNC), which are primarily used to save the lives of women and newborns [2, 3].

It has been established that maternal health services uptake is crucial for reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [4]. Most maternal deaths (90%) are preventable if managed in a timely manner by a skilled health professional in a supportive environment [1, 5]. Studies have shown that ANC alone, when initiated early and attended regularly [6], can reduce maternal mortality by 20%. Moreover, 66% of maternal deaths and 43% of neonatal deaths can be prevented in births that occur with skilled healthcare provider assistance [6]. For example, a study in 88 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) showed that universal coverage of midwife-delivered interventions could avert 67% of maternal deaths and 64% of neonatal deaths [7]. Moreover, findings showed that if 90% of babies and mothers received routine postnatal care (PNC), 10–27% of newborn deaths could be averted [8, 9]. Ensuring universal health coverage for maternal health care and strengthening the health systems to respond to the needs and priorities of women are strategic objectives of the World Health Organization (WHO) for ending preventable maternal mortality (EPMM) [10].

Despite the importance of maternal health services uptake in reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, there is still low uptake of maternal health services in LMICs [4]. Evidence showed that, in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), only 24% of pregnant women attended at least four ANC visits, which is much lower than the ANC coverage in, for example, Latin America (90%) in 2021 [11]. Similarly, only 68% of all births in low-income countries and 78% in lower-middle-income countries are assisted by skilled healthcare personnel, compared to 99% in high-income and upper-middle-income countries in 2020 [3]. As a low-income country, Ethiopia has also observed a low rate of uptake of maternal health services. For instance, in 2019, fewer than half of pregnant women completed at least four ANC visits (43%), had a health facility childbirth (48%), and about one-third (35%) received PNC services [12].

Numerous factors hinder women from receiving or seeking care during pregnancy and childbirth. These factors can be related to the health system (supply), to consumers (demand), or to other external factors [3, 13]. Supply-side barriers, such as availability, accessibility, and quality of maternal health services [14], a shortage of health professionals working in maternity care [15, 16], and inappropriate treatment such as verbal and physical abuse [17–19] are factors that often result in low uptake and attrition from service utilisation.

Similarly, social determinants of healthcare, such as income, access to education, religion, gender norms, race, ethnicity, and cultural preferences, act as a demand-side barrier, preventing women from seeking care [3, 13, 20, 21]. Measuring women’s preferences for maternal health services is one of the key consumer-related factors. It is crucial for understanding demand-side dynamics, including why women may have lower uptake or are more likely to be lost to follow-up [13]. Additionally, it can predict women’s future engagement and continued use of these services. In low-income countries, where the uptake of maternal healthcare services is low, it is crucial to identify the preferred attributes of healthcare services for women [22]. In Ethiopia, there is limited evidence evaluating women’s preference for facility delivery [23, 24], and women's preferences for ANC and PNC services have not yet been studied. Despite that, preferences have been shaped over time by advances in technology [25], the available evidence in investigating the preference for facility delivery was last conducted in 2015 [23].

Therefore, this study aimed to identify the preferences of women of childbearing age for using maternal healthcare services in Ethiopia, using a Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE). This method is a commonly used analytical technique that elicits stated preferences for health goods and services by presenting them with a series of hypothetical choices and estimating the contribution of different attributes to those preferences [26–28]. Thus, this ultimately contributes to the design of more responsive health systems within the setting. The findings will enable policymakers to design maternal health services that align with women’s preferences and inform revisions to health policy strategies.

Methods and Materials

Study Setting and Population

This research was conducted among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in the Central Sidama Zone, Sidama Regional State, Ethiopia, from 15th of August to 15th of December 2024. Ethiopia is the second most populous country in Africa, after Nigeria [29]. The Sidama region is one of Ethiopia's 13 administrative regions, with Hawassa town as its administrative centre. As of 2020, the region's population was estimated to be around 4 million. In this region, there were 13 public hospitals, 138 health centres, and 540 health posts that provided maternal, newborn, and child health services to the communities [30]. In the same year, approximately 132,031 pregnant women attended ≥4 ANC visits and 127,585 births were assisted by skilled birth attendants [30]. In a study in 2020, 24.8% of pregnant women in the Sidama region had no antenatal care visits [31].

Ethiopia’s health services are decentralised into a three-tier system, and most maternal health-related services are provided in the Primary Health Care Unit (PHCU), or the first tier of the health system [32]. The first tier (PHCU) comprises a primary hospital, four health centres, and five health posts within each health centre. Each health post is responsible for 3000–5000 people and provides family health services, including maternal and child health, family planning, and immunisation. A health centre provides preventive and curative services and is responsible for 15,000–20,000 people. It provides maternal health services such as ANC visits, vaginal assisted delivery, and PNC visits. The primary hospital provides curative and preventive services to 60,000–100,000 people, as well as emergency surgical services, including caesarean sections and blood transfusion services, and other maternal health services.

The secondary level of care consists of general hospitals and a primary hospital referral centre, and it is responsible for providing curative and preventive healthcare services for 1–1.5 million people. The tertiary level of care comprises specialised and university hospitals, which serve as referral centres for general hospitals, covering an area with a population of around 5 million people [32].

Attributes and Levels Development

For attribute development and level selection, a three-step approach was followed [33]. These include attribute identification (through systematic review and qualitative study), attribute development (via ranking), and attribute refinement (via expert discussion and pilot testing) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Steps in the development of attributes and levels

Steps in the development of attributes and levels

A systematic literature review was initially conducted to identify potential attributes and attribute levels related to women's preference for maternal health services in the attribute identification step. This review has been published elsewhere [34]. Further workshops were conducted with six experts in maternal health services, including obstetricians and gynaecologists, midwives, district maternal health programme managers, and maternal health researchers, to contextualise the attributes identified in the literature review for the local setting and to identify additional attributes and labels. A semi-structured interview guide guided the expert group discussion. Moreover, in-depth interviews were conducted with health extension workers (HEWs) and traditional birth attendants (TBAs), who are in close contact with women, providing maternal care through primary health services and traditional practices, respectively. Seven in-depth interviews were conducted using open-ended guides, involving three HEWs and four TBAs, who were proportionally selected based on their place of residence.

Furthermore, eight focus group discussions (FGDs) among women with six members in each different group, such as maternal health services uptake experience (have experience/do not have experience), educational levels (less than secondary/secondary or above) and residence (urban/rural) [35] were conducted to capture attributes that matter to utilising maternal health services. Attributes identified through the literature review, interviews, and expert discussion were also explored through FGDs.

We followed the reflexive thematic analysis steps [36] to analyse the data and prepare a separate qualitative study with in-depth exploration. For this study, to address and narrow the evidence gap and generate actionable insights, each participant was asked to list and prioritise the most important attributes influencing women’s preferences for maternal health services at the end of each discussion or interview. Based on this, each participant listed four to seven relevant attributes and ranked them according to their perceived importance. In total, more than 15 unique attributes were identified across all participants. Based on the evidence source, across FGDs with women and a round table discussion with other stakeholders, some attributes, such as providers’ attitudes and the availability of drugs and supplies, were commonly identified as shaping women’s preferences for maternal health services. Insights from HEWs and TBAs further highlighted issues such as access to ambulances, the availability of a support person, and cultural factors, including birth ceremonies (Supplementary Table 1). In the attribute development stage the final ranking of all identified attributes was calculated using the mean score values based on the attributes identified and ranked by each study participant. Based on this, attributes such as provider attitude, availability of drugs and supplies, distance to health facilities, privacy, service cost, and waiting time were identified as the top six attributes (Supplementary Table 2).

Finally, in the attribute refinement steps, a virtual discussion with a DCE expert was conducted on the 21st of October 2024, to determine the DCE design, the number of attributes, and the values of levels in each attribute. Lastly, a pilot study was conducted with 121 women to determine the prior knowledge, assess comprehension, and refine the wording of attributes and their levels. The attribute and level description screen was added after the pilot study. Finally, the attributes and levels used in the final survey were determined through expert discussion (Supplementary Table 3).

Variables of the Study

In the DCE methodology, the characteristics of the health facility are referred to as attributes, while the specific values or categories of these attributes are referred to as attribute levels. The DCE variables are attributes of maternal health services, including the availability of medication and supplies, treatment costs, health provider attitude, service waiting times, distance from the health facility, and privacy (Supplementary Table 3). The non-DCE variables include sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, education status, occupation, marital status, average monthly income, residence, and religion, as well as maternal health-related variables, including health insurance, distance to health facilities, and pregnancy experience. These questionnaires were adopted from the demographic and health survey (DHS) data collection instruments [37] (Supplementary Table 4). Participants were first asked about their sociodemographic and maternal health-related characteristics before proceeding to the choice tasks. Then, on the first page of the choice tasks, an introductory screen presented the list of attributes and their levels. The choice tasks began only after we ensured that participants fully understood the attributes and their corresponding levels.

Experimental Design

The Bayesian D-efficient design algorithm with an informative prior was used, utilising the Ngene software [38]. The main effects were identified in the design. We used priors from the pilot study, with a mean D-error of 0.768 (Supplementary Table 5). The 24 choice sets were randomly divided into two blocks of 12 sets each. Each participant was assigned to complete only one block (12 choice sets). This approach reduces cognitive burden and respondent fatigue while ensuring that all choice sets are evaluated across the study sample. Each choice task presents two unlabelled possible alternatives with an unforced choice [42]. All six attributes vary across alternatives; any set has no holdout attributes. The opt-out alternative was added if the women had chosen neither of the two options to maximise realism (Supplementary Table 6). A dominant choice task was administered to each participant to assess the validity and rationality of their choices. The dominant choice task is a choice scenario where one option is better than the other across all attributes [43].

The study employed a full-profile design that incorporated all relevant attributes [39]. The DCE questions had three conditions or cases that were prepared based on the three maternal health services. For comparability across the three maternal health services, we used the same attributes and levels for each service. Respondents were randomly assigned to one of the three conditions or maternal health services within each block. For instance, for ANC visits, the following conditions were presented in each choice task: “Assume you are pregnant and planning an ANC visit. Which of the following maternal health services options would you prefer for your ANC visit? If you don’t prefer either option, select opt-out.” These are the cases in which the women put their preference on maternal health services [41] (Supplementary Table 7).

Sample Size Determination

After determining the number of choice tasks, the number of attributes, and their levels based on the pilot results, the sample size was calculated. There are various methods for calculating sample sizes in discrete choice experiments [44–48]. Different factors driving sample size include design (orthogonal or efficient), number of tasks, number of attributes and levels, type of analysis (main effect or interaction), and the need for subgroup analysis to determine the minimum sample required [49, 50].

The minimum sample size was calculated as used for similar studies [51, based on the rule of thumb formula as proposed by Johnson and Orme [52, 53].

N = 500c/(t × α); where it depends on the number of choice tasks (t), the number of alternatives (a), and the number of analysis cells (c) [53]. Based on the pilot results, we identified six attributes and aimed to create 12 choice tasks with two alternatives, with a maximum of four levels per attribute.

Therefore,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = \, 500 \times 4/ \, \left( {12 \times 2} \right); \, N \, = \, 83.33$$\end{document}N=500×4/12×2;N=83.33

For interaction effect analysis between attributes and sociodemographic variables, we multiplied the result by the maximum number of levels per attribute, which is four, resulting in a necessary sample size of 333 (83.33 × 4 = 333.32). Moreover, by considering the three maternal health service outcome variables, a sample size of 1000 (333.32 × 3 = 999.96) was planned. Additionally, since we used a cluster sampling technique to select reproductive-aged women in the setting, and no previous information in the setting about the study design, a 1.5 cluster design effect was applied [54] (999.9×1.5 = 1500). Finally, after accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the total was planned to be 1650. However, data collection was possible from 1628 samples.

Recruitment and Sampling

To capture the diverse needs of participants, we collected data from women in the community and health facilities. For our community survey, two districts (Dale District and the Yirgalem City Administration) were first selected using simple random sampling from the seven districts in the Central Sidama Zone. Subsequently, proportionally three ‘kebeles’ (suburbs) from the total of 17 ‘kebeles’ of Yirgalem City Administration and four ‘kebeles’ from the total of 31 ‘kebeles’ of Dale District were selected using simple random sampling. ‘Kebele’ was selected as the sampling cluster, as it is the smallest administrative level in Ethiopia. A cluster sampling technique was employed to recruit reproductive-age women in the selected ‘kebeles. Subsequently, reproductive-age women residing in the selected kebeles during the survey home-to-home visit were included in the study.

Purposive sampling of health facilities from urban and rural areas of both districts was conducted for the health facility-based survey. Hence, we included reproductive-age women from four sites: a general hospital, an urban health centre, a rural health centre, and a private clinic (Fig. 2). Reproductive-aged women who visited the selected health facilities during the survey period were included in the study. Women who were seriously ill and unable to comprehend the questionnaires were excluded from the interview (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic presentation of the sample selection (Bold colours were selected districts, N population; n sample)

Data Collection Instruments and Procedures

The survey was administered using REDCap data collection software. A total of 10 female data collectors participated in the data collection. They had bachelor’s degrees in health sciences and could fluently speak Amharic and Affan Sidamo. We used a face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaire data collection method through tablets. A 1-day training course was conducted for the data collectors on the study's objectives, respondent rights, confidentiality of information, and techniques for conducting the DCE interviews. The training was also repeated after considering the feedback from pilot results. The survey was translated into Amharic and Afan Sidamo languages to better understand the chosen tasks and all other questions. A house-to-house survey was conducted for the community survey. Before starting the questionnaire, informed consent was obtained, and the questions became available only after the respondent provided their informed consent to participate in the study. Otherwise, the system ended the survey. Respondents were aware that they could withdraw their consent at any time. The questionnaire began with sociodemographic and maternal health characteristics and concluded with the choice tasks. The choice tasks began with an introduction to the attributes and levels, ensuring respondents understood the choices they would be making. The collected data were transferred to the REDCap server daily and cleared from the data collection tablet. The data quality was ensured through rigorous attribute identification methods, comprehensive data collector training, pilot testing, dominant tasks, and daily data transfer with routine analysis and feedback.

Data Management and Analysis

Once the participant interview was completed, the data were transferred and stored on the University’s REDCap server for analysis. The data were analysed using STATA 17. The descriptive findings are presented using tables, figures, and charts for the background characteristics. A chi-square test was used to assess differences in baseline sociodemographic characteristics across maternal healthcare services. We used the record number to cluster the panel data. A separate analysis assessed the difference in preferences of three maternal health services (ANC service, health facility childbirth, and PNC service). A latent class model (LCM) was used to estimate respondents' preferences and reveal unobserved preferences using the lclogitml2 STATA package [55]. The optimal class model was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). For each service, three classes were identified.

The association between individual sociodemographic characteristics and latent classes was examined simultaneously using a class membership model within the LCA. Each sociodemographic variable was included as a binary-coded variable. The estimated coefficients represent the log-odds of belonging to a given class relative to the reference class. The sociodemographic variables considered included age group, education level, place of residence, setting (community/health facilities), pregnancy status, and health insurance coverage.

To compare the relative importance of each attribute in the model, we used the absolute value of the estimated incremental effect of each attribute. This approach allowed for the direct comparison of their contributions to each maternal health service [47]. First, the individual attribute incremental effect was calculated as the difference between the most and least important levels of each attribute. Based on evidence from the literature, the predicted uptake probability for median or real-world scenarios was calculated using the average levels of the attributes observed in the setting.

After running the LCA model with the cost variable treated as linear, the willingness to pay (WTP) for each attribute level was calculated by dividing the coefficient of each attribute by the cost coefficient [39, 57]. It shows, in monetary terms, how much individuals value a particular attribute of a service. We treated the cost attribute as linear to estimate the disutility of spending one Ethiopian Birr.

Data quality was assessed in the analysis to ensure both external and internal validity. External validity was examined by assessing differential incompleteness, with no evidence of systematic differences between participants who completed the survey and those who did not. However, to maintain data quality, we excluded participants who did not complete all assigned choice tasks (n = 30) from the final analysis. Internal validity was evaluated by checking for potential sources of bias, including interviewer effects, language effects, block assignment, and dominant task responses; no significant differences or systematic patterns were identified. A sensitivity analysis excluding participants who failed the dominant task (n = 40) showed no substantial changes in the results; however, these participants were removed from the final analysis to maintain data quality.

Results

Socio-demographic and maternal health characteristics of participants

Among the 1628 women who participated, 1598 completed all choice tasks. Of these, 40 women failed the dominant choice tasks and were excluded, which resulted in a total of 1558 women finally. Almost an equal number of women were included in each service. More than two-thirds (67.5%) of the participants were from the community rather than health facilities. Two-thirds (65.2%) of women completed at least secondary education. The majority (69.9%) of women had been pregnant at least once in their lifetime. Two-thirds (66.2%) of the women did not have health insurance (Table 1). Predicted Uptake Probability and Marginal Change in the Uptake of ANC Services

The predicted uptake probabilities of ANC services varied across the three latent classes under different choice task scenarios. In the base case, uptake was nearly universal, with rates of 99.7%, 96.7%, and 99.6% for Classes 1, 2, and 3, respectively. However, under the median or real-world scenario, uptake probability decreased most notably for Class 1 to 70.4%, while remaining high for Class 2 (94.5%) and Class 3 (97.9%). In addition to the median scenario, offering free service or ensuring immediate service or medication availability improved uptake in Class 1 to 74.9%, 85.4%, and 98.3%, respectively, whereas Classes 2 and 3 showed smaller increases. In the least preferred scenario, uptake dropped sharply for Classes 1 (3.3%) and 2 (2.1%) but remained relatively high for Class 3 (84.5%) (Table 3). Discussion

This study examines the preferences of women for maternal health services in the Sidama region of Ethiopia. The results demonstrate that the cost of service, availability of medication and supplies, waiting time, health provider attitude, distance to the health facilities, and privacy affect women’s choice to use maternal health services. Moreover, the predicted uptake of maternal health services reached its highest levels when all attributes were set to the most favourable conditions in the base-case scenario.

Our study found that women had a low preference for maternal health services when service costs were high. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Tanzania and Kenya, where increased treatment costs led to a decrease in women’s preferences for maternal healthcare services [35, 58, 59]. Financial barriers have been widely recognised as a key determinant of maternal healthcare preference, particularly in low-resource settings where out-of-pocket expenses significantly affect healthcare-seeking behaviour [60]. The evidence overwhelmingly supports the notion that providing free maternal healthcare services increases women’s preferences for this care and ultimately leads to higher utilisation. Several studies have demonstrated that removing financial barriers leads to higher rates of antenatal care attendance, skilled birth attendance, and postnatal care visits [61, 62]. Countries that have implemented free maternal health policies, such as Kenya’s Free Maternity Services (FMS) program, have reported significant improvements in maternal healthcare utilisation and outcomes [61, 63]. India’s Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY), a conditional cash transfer programme, has also significantly increased ANC and in-facility births [62]. Although free maternal health services have been implemented in public health facilities in most LMICs, studies show that there is still a high out-of-pocket payment due to the unavailability of drugs, sonography, and laboratory services [64]. For instance, the absence of reliable pharmaceutical distributors in a study in Ghana led to several intermediary groups being involved in the distribution of medications, resulting in prices increasing by 5–200% in pharmacies [65]. Similarly, a study in Ethiopia showed that nearly three-fifths of the households paid out-of-pocket expenses that exceeded 20% of their monthly household income for maternal healthcare [66]. Moreover, in the private sector, unnecessary caesarean sections increased delivery-related health costs by at least 10-fold [67]. To improve maternal health service utilisation, governments should ensure the full implementation of free care policies, including the availability of essential supplies. Sustained investment and monitoring are crucial to eliminating hidden costs and enhancing women’s access to quality maternal healthcare.

Our study also found that the availability of medications and supplies is a key factor influencing women’s preferences for maternal health services. This finding aligns with studies conducted in various settings, which have identified the availability of equipment and drugs as key attributes of maternal health service preference [23, 24, 26, 58, 59, 68, 69]. For instance, a study in Tanzania, providing a reliable supply of drugs and medical equipment at government dispensaries, dramatically increased the overall preference for childbirth at these facilities from 2.9 to 26.9% of women [26]. This is also supported by another study in Tanzania, which reported that households were three times more likely to utilise healthcare services when health facilities had a continuous supply of essential medicines [70]. This underscores the direct correlation between the availability of medications and healthcare preferences, which eventually lead to utilisation. Moreover, the WHO emphasises that access to treatment is heavily dependent on the availability of affordable medicines [71]. This highlights the fundamental role of medication availability in shaping women's healthcare preferences.

In this study, the least preferred maternal health service was long waiting times, especially for health facility childbirth care. This aligns with a study in India, which found that expectant mothers did not prefer health facility childbirth services as waiting times increased [72]. For instance, a comprehensive review examining the effects of waiting room ambience on stress and anxiety found that factors such as overcrowding, noise, and lack of privacy can exacerbate anxiety in patients [73]. Long waiting periods, especially in maternal waiting areas, may sometimes cause both physical and psychological distress, particularly during late pregnancy when women may experience heightened pain, anxiety, or fear of complications. A study assessing antenatal clinic waiting times and patient satisfaction also found that extended waits were a major disincentive for pregnant women, constituting a barrier to the utilisation of ANC [74]. This emphasises that enhancing the responsiveness of maternal health services, especially by reducing waiting times, is crucial for promoting facility-based childbirth and boosting the utilisation of maternal healthcare services.

Conversely, in this study, maintaining privacy and distance from health facilities are persistently among the least important attributes influencing women’s choice of maternal health services in most analyses. While privacy is often considered an essential component of quality maternal healthcare, studies have shown that in many low-resource settings, women may prioritise accessibility and affordability over privacy concerns. A study conducted in rural Tanzania found that while some women valued privacy during childbirth, their primary concern was the availability of skilled birth attendants and medical supplies [35].These findings suggest that in resource-limited settings, women may be willing to forgo certain amenities, such as private maternity wards, in favour of facilities that offer free or affordable services.

The lower value assigned to distance in our study aligns with other preference studies in Ethiopia [23, 75], where distance to healthcare facilities is considered less important compared to the availability of medications and supplies. This can be explained by the fact that the Sidama region is a densely populated area with a relatively small landmass; hence, there are fewer geographical barriers to accessing health services in comparison to other regions, such as the Afar Region [23]. Therefore, women could have geographical access to multiple healthcare facilities either by walking or using public ambulance services [59]. Moreover, the use of alternative transport systems, such as motorcycles, is common in the study areas where roads are inconvenient and other means of transport are unavailable. Women may also be willing to travel longer distances to access affordable services, such as public health facilities, rather than nearby lower-level private facilities that are available in almost every area. Overall, these factors could lead to an underestimation of the disutility of distance in the current model.

Women exhibited distinct preferences for maternal health services, with significant differences shaped by sociodemographic and contextual factors, including age, residence, and education level. For instance, women from rural areas were more likely to belong to latent classes characterised by a stronger negative preference for health facility childbirth with long waiting times. This may reflect the structural barriers rural women face, including fewer health facilities, longer travel distances, and limited opportunities for timely care. Consequently, reducing waiting times in facilities could be particularly impactful for these groups, improving both perceived service quality and actual service utilisation.

Women with secondary education or above were more likely to belong to latent classes characterised by lower preferences for services that lacked medication availability. This is supported by other similar studies, which showed that women of high educational status preferred maternity care that has technical [58, 59], and non-technical qualities [76]. Educated women are more likely to have access to quality health services and greater exposure to the media, which in turn raises their expectations [35, 77]. Hence, preference for modern medical equipment and drugs increased with greater media exposure [35]. Therefore, to improve quality maternal health care utilisation, there is a need to focus on women's education beyond the primary level [78].

The high predicted uptake probabilities of maternal health services in the base-case scenario of the DCE, which assumes ideal conditions (a kind and friendly attitude, consistent availability of medication and supplies, proximity within a 30-minute walking distance, provider-maintained privacy, one-stop service, and free service), indeed contrast with the low uptake observed in actual practice. This discrepancy suggests that the real-world services provided may be considerably different from the ideal conditions presented in the DCE, where the base case reflects optimal service delivery. Factors such as inadequate infrastructure, stock-outs of medications, long waiting times, and financial constraints likely contribute to the gap between preferences expressed in the DCE and actual service utilisation in Ethiopia [79].

Conversely, despite unfavourable conditions, a considerable proportion of women still express a predicted uptake probability towards health facility childbirth services, followed by ANC services and PNC services. This suggests that while women prefer high-quality services, they may still utilise maternal healthcare even in less-than-ideal circumstances, possibly due to a lack of alternatives or a strong perceived need for institutional care. Previous studies have similarly highlighted that in resource-limited settings, women often prioritise accessibility and availability of services, particularly for life-saving interventions such as childbirth [80]. The moderate predicted uptake probabilities of facility-based childbirth services under less favourable conditions may reflect the increasing awareness of the risks associated with home births and the benefits of skilled birth attendants.

In our study, the WTP was generally similar across the three services (ANC, health facility childbirth, and PNC). Women demonstrated a higher WTP to avoid maternal health services that lacked medications and supplies, as well as those associated with long waiting times. Women were willing to pay approximately 5118 ETB (approximately US$95) for a one-stop service at a health facility for childbirth, compared to a 24-hour waiting time. For ANC services, they were also willing to pay approximately 8604 ETB (approximately US$159) for facilities that consistently had medications and supplies available, compared to those where such items were not often available. This is in line with a study in Ethiopia, which showed that women were more willing to pay for childbirth services at facilities with available drugs and medical equipment [24]. These findings highlight that long waiting times and inconsistent availability of medications significantly shape women’s preferences and health-seeking behaviour. Enhancing the timeliness and reliability of maternal health services may be more effective than cost reduction alone in increasing women's preference for utilising these services.

Strengths and Limitations

This study assesses women’s preferences for ANC, health facility childbirth, and PNC services. It is essential to identify the characteristics of hypothetical health facilities that women in Ethiopia value highly and to compare the differences in the relative importance of attributes among the three maternal health services. Moreover, matching the sex of interviewers and respondents was conducted to reduce socially desirable answers and response bias [23].

However, this study has some inherent limitations. Although each woman was randomly assigned to complete a choice task with one of the three maternal health services, some demographic variables differed significantly among the groups. Moreover, for comparison among the three maternal health services, the same attributes and levels were used across all three maternal health services in this study. However, practically, the attribute value levels will not be similar across all maternal health services. For example, the service cost for childbirth at health facilities differs from that of ANC or PNC services, and similarly, the waiting time varies in actual scenarios. This results in the relative importance of attributes highly depending on the level at which the attributes are used. As a result, the interpretation of the relative importance of attributes and their heterogeneity in relation to sociodemographic variables should consider the value level.

Moreover, even though we used a small number of attributes and levels, considering the low literacy level of the study population, a comprehensive understanding of all attributes of the maternal health service and making comparisons was not easy for some women. Due to the constraints of RedCap software, attribute order, task sequence, and alternative positions were not randomised. This may have introduced order or position effects, potentially biasing responses.

Additionally, although we tried to include representative samples of the target population using cluster sampling, the demographic characteristics of the respondents, such as education status, geographic residence and household monthly income, may not accurately represent the target population. For example, although the Sidama region is densely populated with a relatively small land area compared to other regions [23], according to the World Bank (2023), 77% of Ethiopia’s population resides in rural areas, compared with only 44% of respondents in our study [81]. The monthly household income level may also have been influenced by the season of data collection, particularly during the coffee harvest period, when households typically generate income from the sale of coffee, their primary source of livelihood. This might influence the estimation of WTP.

Lastly, we acknowledge that including attitudinal or psychosocial measures, such as the Sense of Coherence (SOC) scale [82], which captures coping capacity and perceptions of comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness, could have offered additional insight into women’s preferences. However, due to the length of the questionnaire and our focus on behavioural and service-related attributes, these measures were not included. Incorporating such tools would be valuable for future research to better understand psychological factors influencing maternal health service preferences.

Public Health Implications

Our findings have important public health implications for improving the utilisation and quality of maternal health services in Ethiopia and similar low-resource settings. The study highlights that financial barriers, particularly high service costs and hidden out-of-pocket expenses, significantly reduce women's preference for maternal healthcare. This underscores the urgent need for governments and health systems to ensure the full implementation of free maternal health service policies. Such implementation should not only eliminate official service fees but also guarantee the consistent availability of essential medications, diagnostic services, and equipment. Public health programmes must prioritise resource allocation and supply chain strengthening to prevent stock-outs and reduce the financial burden on households, which will ultimately enhance service utilisation and reduce maternal and neonatal mortality.

Additionally, non-financial factors such as waiting time and provider attitude significantly influence women’s choices. Health system responsiveness, including efforts to reduce waiting times and ensure respectful, friendly care, should be central to maternal health interventions. Tailored approaches that consider the sociodemographic diversity of women are also essential. These insights support the development of women-centred care models and community-level strategies that reflect local preferences, ultimately contributing to more equitable and efficient maternal health systems.

Future Research Directions

Future research should address several gaps identified in this study. First, using attributes and levels that reflect service-specific realities could enhance the external validity of the DCE [83]. Second, incorporating visual tools may enhance comprehension among low-literacy populations, enabling all participants to engage meaningfully in the choice tasks. Finally, including a more representative sample from various regions of the country would help ensure that the elicited preferences capture the diverse experiences and needs across Ethiopia’s geographic and sociodemographic contexts.

Conclusions

The cost of service and the availability of medication and supplies were the most important attributes influencing preferences for ANC and PNC services, while waiting time and the cost of services were the key considerations for facility-based childbirth. Sociodemographic factors, such as age, residence, education, and setting, interact with preferences for attributes. The high acceptability of maternal health services under optimal conditions highlights the importance of enhancing service quality, including ensuring affordability, increasing the availability of medications and supplies, and providing one-stop services. Maternal health services should address specific needs of the women, such as subsidising costs, ensuring sustainable medication supply, and providing one-stop services to enhance service uptake in the Sidama region. The findings will provide policymakers with valuable input and inform future research on maternal health services.
