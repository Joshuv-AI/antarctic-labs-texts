Thiourea-derived g-C3N4 with time-tuned properties for efficient dye degradation and in vitro cytotoxicity evaluation
Authors: Helly Parmar, Krupa Majethia, Jinal Patel, Devanshi Soni, Jyoti V Thaikoottathil, Neha Taneja, Arunima Nayak, Syed Shahabuddin, Mohammed E Ali Mohsin, Suleiman Mousa, Rama Gaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe escalating issues of water pollution, driven by rapid industrialization, urbanization, and population growth, pose a significant threat to both environmental and public health. In this study, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) via thermal polymerization at varying reaction times, and its photocatalytic degradation and biocompatibility properties were systematically evaluated. Comprehensive structural, morphological, functional, and optical analyses were performed on the synthesized materials. The crystallite size and band gap of the photocatalyst ranged from 3.31 nm to 4.3 nm and 2.78 to 2.85 eV, respectively. The findings indicated a reduction in both dislocation density and micro-strain with increasing reaction time. These parameters significantly influenced the photocatalytic performance. The electrochemical parameters supported sample prepared at 4 h (TU4) with a negative conduction band potential and low charge transfer resistance. Photocatalytic studies for the degradation methylene blue dye were carried out in the sunlight for 1 h. Among the samples of g-C3N4 prepared at 4 h, the highest photocatalytic efficiency was achieved, with 99% degradation of methylene blue (MB) under optimized conditions. Total organic carbon (TOC) analysis confirmed 96.54% mineralization, highlighting the material’s potential for effective wastewater treatment. The results emphasize a strong correlation between annealing duration and photocatalytic activity, offering key insights for the rational design and optimization of g-C3N4-based photocatalyst to ensure their environmental safety. The biological impact of synthesized g- C3N4 nanoparticles was also assessed. Introduction

Due to rising levels of industrial activity, development and increase in population, water contamination has become a global problem1. Among all the hazardous pollutants, organic dyes are more toxic and harmful due to their widespread usage. One of the most studied dyes in wastewater treatment in methylene blue (MB), a cationic dye has a number of application and widely been used in dying, paper and fabric industries. MB is highly soluble in water and toxic. Long exposure of MB contaminated water leads to the cyanosis, vomiting, and increasing heart rate in humans. Its presence in aquatic systems affects the marine living organism by altering oxygen balance and disrupting metabolic function. Therefore, the removal of MB from wastewater has become a major focus in environmental research2–5. Effective removal of dyes is a tough challenge. Photocatalysis, offering a sustainable and effective approach to wastewater treatment in which a semiconductor material is used to accelerate the chemical reaction rate under light irradiation through generating highly reactive species which are capable of breaking down the complex structure into harmless by-products6,7. There are many semiconductor photocatalyst such as metal oxide, metal sulphides, carbon nitrides, etc6,8–10.

Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has become a fascinating candidate among the numerous semiconductors photocatalyst because of its unique properties such as metal free composition, moderate band gap (~ 2.7 eV), ease of synthesis, thermal and chemical stability, sp2 hybridization, surface area and optical properties11–16. Its wide range of applications extend over various domains such as hydrogen generation through water splitting, waste water treatment, pollutant removal, air purification, carbon dioxide reduction and solar fuel cells, contributing to advancement of sustainability17–22.

However, limited surface area and rapid recombination of electron-hole pairs limits the efficiency of g-C3N411,23. To overcome the limitations various approaches have been employed including optimization of reaction condition (precursors, reaction time, reaction temperature and heating rate), by doping and forming heterojunctions. Properties of g-C3N4 is greatly influenced by its synthesis conditions, particularly the choice of precursor, reaction temperature and heating time. The type of precursor used plays a crucial role in determining the final morphology, crystallinity, surface area and optical properties of g-C3N4. Nguyen et al.24, compared thiourea and urea as precursors for g-C3N4 and found that thiourea led to more porous structures with better dye degradation performance due to enhanced polymerization aided by sulphur vacancies. Liu et al.25, focused on three different precursor cyanamide, dicyandiamide and urea and found that cyanamide resulted in enhance crystalline g-C3N4 with superior photocatalytic performance because of enhanced surface area and well-ordered morphology. Similarly, reaction temperature is a key factor in controlling polymerization and structural quantity. Papailias et al.26, , Yang et al.27, and Caux et al.28, in their studies shows that g-C3N4 prepared below the 550 °C temperature showed incomplete polymerization, low surface area and less crystallinity. While g-C3N4 prepared at around 550 °C showed better crystallinity, layered structure and porous structure indicates that optimum temperature for synthesis of g-C3N4 is 550 °C, below this temperature, complete polymerization does not take place and above this temperature, there is disruption of the structure of g-C3N4.

Optimizing the reaction time can enhance the crystallinity, surface area and morphology, which directly affect the material’s efficiency for photocatalytic activity. However not much reports discuss the optimization of reaction time, only three papers are available. Yang et al.27, synthesized g-C3N4 at 1/2/3 h of reaction time in which the g-C3N4 is not completely polymerized at 1 h of reaction time while that prepared at 3 h shows high crystallinity and porous structure which shows 85% degradation of MB dye under visible light in 180 min. Hung et al.29, synthesized g-C3N4 at 0.5 to 2.5 h of reaction time in which the g-C3N4 prepared at 2.5 h shows that it has large surface area and better crystallinity and shows 99% removal of RhB dye under visible light in 120 min. These papers indicate that increasing the reaction time can influence the structural property of g-C3N4. The g-C3N4 prepared at high reaction time indicates better crystallinity, high surface area, porous structure and high photocatalytic activity. Yuan et al.30, in their study showed that extending the reaction time to 8 h significantly enhanced the crystalline quality, surface area and photocatalytic hydrogen production rate. These findings collectively indicate that longer reaction times promote better crystallinity, higher surface area and improve photocatalytic efficiency of g-C3N4.

The present work aims to investigate the effect of thermal annealing time on the synthesis of g-C3N4 from thiourea31–34 and its morphological, structural photocatalytic, and cytotoxic properties. The increasing use of g-C3N4 in environmental and photocatalytic applications raises concerns regarding their safety for environment, particularly in contexts where human exposure could occur. Due to their lightweight, nanoscale nature, such materials have potential to become airborne and enter the human body through inhalation, posing possible risks to respiratory tissues. This makes it essential to evaluate their cytotoxicity, especially toward lung epithelial cells, which represent the first line of contact in the respiratory system. In the present study, thiourea derived g-C3N4 nanoparticles were assessed for their potential cytotoxicity against the human lung epithelial cell line NCl-H23, a well-characterized model for lung epithelial tissue. While g-C3N4 has been extensively evaluated for environmental remediation and photocatalytic applications, studies examining its interaction with human epithelial cells remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, a systematic evaluation of cytotoxic and morphological effects of thiourea derived g-C3N4 nanoparticles on NCI-H23 cells has not been previously reported. The study focused on examining the impact of these nanomaterials on cell viability and cell morphology after exposure, providing insight into their biocompatibility and potential safety profile for environmental or biomedical use.

Experimental methods

Material & methods

Thiourea (SRL, CH4N2S, 99% pure, 76.12 g/mol), methanol (FINAR, CH3OH, analytical grade, 32.04 g/mol), methylene blue (Merck life science private limited, C16H18CIN3S, 319.85 g/mol), silver nitrate(LR (ACS), AgNO3, ACS grade), isopropyl alcohol (FINAR, C3H7OH, 99% pure, 60.10 g/mol), p-benzoquinone (Sigma-Aldrich, C6H4O2, 98% pure, 108.09 g/mol) and ammonium oxalate monohydrate (FINAR, (NH4)2C2O4.H2O, 142.11 g/mol). MTT Hi-AR (3-(4, 5-Dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide, Hi-AR) (Himedia), DMSO (Sigma Aldrich, C2H6OS, 78.13 g/mol), potassium ferrocyanide (K4[Fe(CN)6], 422.39 g/mol), potassium chloride (KCl, 74.55 g/mol), sodium sulphate (Na2SO4, 142.04 g/mol), ethanol (C2H5OH, 46.07 g/mol), and Nafion (C7HF13O5S)n. Chemicals were employed directly as obtained from suppliers, without any further treatment. In photocatalytic reaction, dye solution was prepared using distilled water.

Synthesis of g-C3N4

Graphitic carbon nitride was prepared via thermal polymerization approach using thiourea as the precursor. A weighed amount of thiourea was finely ground and placed in a covered alumina crucible, then subjected to thermal polymerization in a muffle furnace at 550 °C with heating rate of 5 °C/min for various time durations of 1, 4, 6 and 8 h. The samples named as TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8 respectively, where TU stands for thiourea and 1, 4, 6, 8 stands for the reaction time27,29,30.

Characterization

The synthesized samples were characterized to determine their crystalline phase, functionalities, morphology and optical properties. Phase analysis was done using a Rigaku Miniflex600-C X-ay diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.53 Å), operating at a scanning rate of 5°/min over the 2θ range of 5° to 80°. Functional group analysis was done through the Perkin Elmer FT-IR spectrometer in ATR mode, within the spectral range of 4000 –500 cm− 1. The DRS spectra was carried out using Perkin Elmer Lambda 365 + UV-Vis spectrophotometer in the range of 200 to 800 nm. Photoluminescence spectra were recorded using a Perkin Elmer fluorescence LS 55 spectrometer at room temperature and it was recorded in the range 360 to 710 nm at the excitation wavelength of 350 nm. Surface morphology of the samples was evaluated using ZEISS Ultra 55 field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM). LABINDIA’s UV-Vis spectrometer was used to record UV-Vis spectra during photocatalytic study in the range of 200 to 800 nm. XPS was performed on a Thermo Fisher NEXSA (Al Kα, 1486.6 eV) at 12 kV, 6.5 mA, pass energy 50 eV, under 9 × 10−⁸ mbar. Binding energies were referenced to C 1s (284.5 eV); peaks deconvoluted using CASA with Shirley background. Total organic carbon was analysed using TOC-L analyser (Shimadzu). Electrochemical characterization was conducted out using potentiostat (Metrohm, Nova 2.1.5.). The electrochemical impedance (EIS) was analysed using three-electrode set-up, using glassy carbon electrode (3 mm diameter), Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) and Pt wire electrode as working, reference and counter electrode, respectively. The EIS and Mott Schottky analysis 5 mM K4[Fe(CN)6] in 0.1 M KCl and 0.1 M Na2SO4 was used respectively. The catalyst ink was prepared by the addition of 1 mg sample in 1 mL ethanol and water (1:1) and 20 µL nafion, and the solution was sonicated for 30 min.

Photocatalytic activity

For the degradation 10 ppm aqueous solution of MB was prepared and 1 mg/mL catalyst was added to 10 ml of MB solution and sonicated for the uniform dispersion. After the sonication dispersion were left in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium before light irradiation. After the attainment of adsorption equilibrium 30 µL of H2O2 was added to dispersion as the initiator and the dispersed solutions were kept in natural sunlight for 1 h. Then the dispersions were centrifuged to settle down particles of photocatalyst and remaining dye solution in the supernatant was monitored using a UV-Vis spectrometer with the help of quartz cuvette. The photocatalytic studies were carried out in the presence of natural sunlight at Pandit Deendayal Energy University Gandhinagar, Gujarat, India. The experiments were conducted in the month of March and April 2025. The precise geographic coordinates of the experimental sites are 23.2156° N latitude and 72.6369° E longitude. The average Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI) obtained from NASA POWER was 270.9 W/m² in March 2025 and 299.2 W/m² in April 2025.

For the kinetics study 3 ml of solution was taken out at regular time interval, centrifuged and analysed by UV-VIS spectrometer. The remaining concentration of MB in solution was determined by measuring the intensity of the characteristic absorption peak at 663 nm. The dosage study was conducted to found optimum dosage for maximum removal of MB by g-C3N4. The studies conducted with the dosage amount of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1 and 2 mg/ml. To elucidate the main reactive species participating in the degradation of MB, scavenger study was carried out. Silver nitrate (AgNO3), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), benzoquinone (BQ) and ammonium oxalate (AO) were used as scavengers for electrons, hydroxyl radicals, superoxide radicals and holes, respectively. The recyclability of the photocatalyst was done for the MB dye for up to 10 cycles. The remaining residue was then dissolved in acetonitrile and analyzed using a GC–MS instrument (Agilent).

Toxicity analysis

Cell culture

The human non-small cell lung cancer cell line (NCI-H23) originally established by American type culture collection (ATCC –CRL 5800), were obtained from NCCS, Pune. The NCI-H23 cell line is a widely studied cell line derived from human lung epithelial tissue that exhibit an epithelial morphology and have been extensively characterized for their growth characteristics, genetic profile, and response to different treatments. These cells were cultured in RPMI − 1640 medium (Biogenuix, cat#MSC0486L-500) containing 10% Fetal Bovine serum (FBS – Gibco cat#10270106), Sodium pyruvate, sodium bicarbonate, high glucose (4.5 g) and antibiotics (Penicillin and Streptomycin). These cells were maintained in the cell culture incubators at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 85% humidity.

Assessment of cellular toxicity of TU nanoparticle

Cytotoxic effects of different TU nanoparticles was assessed using Human lung epithelial cell lines NCI H-23 cell. These cells were seeded in a 24 well plate (1 × 105 cells/ well) and allowed to grow for 4 days. On the day 5, cells were 100% confluent and they were treated with various concentrations of TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8 nanoparticles for 24 h. After 24 h treatment cells were observed under the microscope to study any morphological changes in the cell phenotype. Later the effect of TU nanoparticles on cell viability was assessed using MTT assay.

Briefly, after the 24 h incubation with either media alone or with different concentrations of TU nanoparticles, 50 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added in the each well and cells were incubated in the cell culture incubator for 3 h. The most viable cells due to their mitochondrial enzyme activity converts yellow colour tetrazolium salt in to purple colour formazan crystals which accumulates inside the cells. After 3 h incubation with MTT reagent, cell culture media was carefully removed and 150 µL DMSO was added in the well to dissolved purple colour formazan crystals in the cells. Plates were gently shaken for 5–10 min and 100-µL solution was transferred from each well into a 96 well plate well. Absorbance of purple formazan was recorded at 570 nm in a 96 well plate reader spectrophotometer. Cell viability was calculated as the percent of control cell (no treatment) absorbance and data is presented as Mean ± SEM in the graph.

Characterization of g-C3N4

Characterization of prepared samples has been done through various characterization techniques, which has been discussed in details as below.

The comparison among TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8 highlights the influence of structural characteristics on photocatalytic activity. TU1 have smaller crystallite size, showed the highest dislocation density and lattice strain, suggesting the presence of more lattice defects, which may be, serve as charge carrier traps, which can reduce the charge recombination and enhance the photocatalytic activity. TU6 showed large crystalline size and improved crystallinity while TU4 and TU8 had moderate values of micro-strain and dislocation density, reflecting a balanced defect level that may support effective electron-hole separation. The trend of increasing crystalline size with longer thermal treatment was accompanied by gradual band gap widening from 2.78 eV to 2.85 eV from TU1 to TU6 and then 2.84 eV for TU8 and a slight red shift in PL emission aligns with enhanced crystallinity and modified electronic transitions. This trend suggest that extended thermal treatment plays a key role in improving the structural integrity of g-C3N4 by reducing internal parameters.

Functional group analysis

To identify the functional group of prepared samples FT-IR spectroscopy was used. As shown in Fig. 3 FT-IR spectra of prepared samples reveals several characteristic absorption bands, which confirms the successful synthesis of g-C3N4. The successful formation of g-C3N4 is confirmed by the absorption peaks at 888 cm− 1 and 811 cm− 1, which are due to the bending vibrations of tri-s-triazine and s-triazine ring units, respectively. The absorption band observed between 1238 cm− 1 and 1461 cm− 1 corresponds to the typical vibrational modes of bridging units of C-N(-C)-C and C-NH-C. Bands located at 1635 cm− 1 and 1548 cm− 1 are attributed to C = N stretching vibration within the aromatic heterocyclic structure. The comparatively weaker intensities of C-N and C = N stretching modes in TU1 suggest incomplete formation of the g-C3N4 framework. The broad absorption band observed between 3350 and 3000 cm− 1 shows increased intensity with longer reaction times, suggesting a decrease in -OH stretching vibrations and a corresponding rise in amino functionalities formed during extended thermal treatment. Further, it observed that bands of typical C-NH-C in TU8 is less intense than that in TU6, which indicates that increasing the reaction time from 6 to 8 h, g-C3N4 nanosheets can reduced in size.

Morphological analysis

FE-SEM analysis was conducted to investigate the morphological features of g-C3N4 prepared at different reaction duration, as illustrated in Fig. 4. TU1 shows compact and undeveloped structures, indicating limited thermal condensation. TU4 shows partially exfoliated layers with increased porosity emerge, suggesting improved polymerization. TU6 shows the layered structures are broken and large nanosheets are formed. However, extended thermal heating in TU8 leads to agglomeration and loss of structure and nanosheets have fragmented into smaller flakes. With longer heating time, thermal exfoliation occurs and stacked layered are converted into porous and ultrathin morphology.

PL spectroscopy

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the charge carrier separation and electron-hole recombination behaviour of prepared samples under an excitation wavelength of 350 nm, as shown in Fig. 5 (b). All samples displayed a broad emission band spanning from 400 to 600 nm. The PL intensity of peak around 430 nm initially decreases from TU1 to TU4, followed by an increase for TU6 and a further rise for TU8. Since PL emission intensity is directly related to the recombination rate of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, a lower PL intensity suggests suppressed recombination and improved charge separation efficiency. The reduced emission observed for TU4 indicates enhanced separation of photogenerated carriers, which is beneficial for photocatalytic performance. The variation in PL intensity with annealing time can be correlated with structural modifications revealed by XRD analysis. Controlled annealing may reduce lattice strain and optimize defect distribution, thereby facilitating charge carrier migration and suppressing radiative recombination. However, prolonged annealing (TU6 and TU8) may introduce structural distortions or defect states that act as recombination centres, leading to increased PL intensity.

It is important to note that PL spectroscopy primarily reflects recombination dynamics rather than directly quantifying defect types or concentrations. Therefore, the interpretation of defect-related behaviour is supported by complementary crystallographic parameters such as micro-strain and dislocation density. The combined structural and optical analysis suggests that annealing time plays a critical role in balancing defect density and crystallinity, which ultimately governs charge carrier dynamics and photocatalytic efficiency.

Photocatalytic studies

Adsorption, degradation and % removal study

The photocatalytic activity of synthesized samples (TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8) were investigated by assessing the ability to degrade methylene blue (MB) dye under natural sunlight. The removal activity of the photocatalyst was determined by the decrease in the characteristic peak intensity of MB at 663 nm in dark and after irradiation with sunlight as shown in Fig. 8(a-b). The MB peak not only decreases but also shifts towards the lower wavelength under natural sunlight, while the position of this peak is unchanged when the solution was kept in dark, indicating MB molecules was degraded through the formation of intermediates. All the sample were found to be effective for the removal of MB dye from aqueous solutions with approximately 97.75% removal. A deeper investigation pointed towards differential adsorption and catalytic performance of the prepared photocatalyst. Figure 8(c) shows the % adsorption 6.17%, 6.74%, 29.21% and 25.84% by TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8, respectively and % degradation 58.98%, 91.01%, 68.53% and 71.34% by TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8, respectively. Higher adsorption by TU6 and TU8 sample can be attributed to better exfoliation due to longer reaction times, hence offering higher surface sites for enhanced adsorption. While TU4 exhibited highest photocatalytic removal of MB in presence of natural sunlight. The higher photocatalytic response can be correlated to the low recombination of charge carriers, as indicated in the PL analysis.

Performance assessment of the photocatalyst

The conclusion of above study indicates that the TU4 is the best photocatalyst of all. It has suitable band gap, nanosheets like structure, less recombination rate for charge carriers and shows better photo degradation capacity. Thus, the parameter studies have been done for TU4 such as dosage, pH, and scavenger for MB dye degradation.

Dosage study

To determine the optimal dosage of TU4 for maximum removal of MB various dosage of TU4 such as 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1 and 2 mg/mL were systematically tested in the series of experiment. Figure 11(a) shows the bar graph of % removal MB using TU4 at different dosages. It can be observed that as the TU4 dosage increased from 0.1 to 1 mg/mL the removal efficiency improved significantly from 75.31% to 98.87% due to increased number of active sites available for the photocatalytic degradation at higher dosage. Beyond the dosage of 1 mg/mL, no notable improvement in MB removal efficiency was observed, indicating that the active sites on the photocatalyst surface had reached saturation. Hence, 1 mg/mL was selected as the optimal dosage.

(a) Bar graph of % removal of MB at different dosages of TU4 (b) bar graph of % removal of MB at different pH (Dye concentration: 10 ppm, Catalyst dosage: 1 mg/mL, Irradiation time: 60 min).

pH study

The influence of pH on the degradation efficiency of MB dye was investigated to understand its effect on the photocatalytic process. The experiment was done at pH 2, 4, 6.8, 9 and 11. Figure 11(b) presents the percentage removal of MB at various pH levels. The removal efficiency of MB was found to be 69.14%, 97.38%, 99.41%, 99.42% and 99.31% at pH levels of 2, 4, 6.8, 9 and 11, respectively. At lower pH 2 the % removal of MB was less compared to other pH conditions because of the electrostatic repulsion of cationic MB dye and H+ ion in the dye. Not much variation in degradation % was observed at basic pH. The study suggest that the basic pH is more favourable for the maximum % removal of MB dye.

Scavenger study

To determine the key reactive species involved in the photocatalytic degradation process, a scavenger study was conducted, as illustrated in Fig. 12(a) IPA, AO, BQ and AgNO3 were employed as scavengers for hydroxyl radicals, holes, superoxide radicals and electrons. A notable decline in degradation efficiency was observed in the presence of AgNO3 and AO, with reductions of 57.83% and 85.94%, respectively. In contrast, IPA and BQ had slight change, with degradation efficiencies 92.43% and 97.29%, respectively. These results suggest that electron and holes are the primary reactive species in the degradation process, while hydroxyl and superoxide radicals play a less significant role.

Recycle, regeneration and stability study

Reusability and regeneration of catalyst are important factor in photocatalytic degradation studies. After each cycle, the catalyst was collected, thoroughly washed multiple times with water and acetone, centrifuged and dried overnight at room temperature. The recycle study has been done for the degradation of MB dye. Photocatalyst exhibited strong reusability across 10 consecutive cycles, with degradation efficiency slightly decreasing from 99.47% to 90.26% (shown in Fig. 12(b)). Degradation pathway and Mechanism of Degradation

To identify the intermediate products and potential degradation pathways of MB, GC–MS analysis was performed at different adsorption and degradation times. The corresponding mass spectra obtained for blank MB, after adsorption and photodegradation under visible light, are shown in Fig. 14.

Mass spectra of (a) blank MB, (b) 30 min dark, (c) 30 min light, and (d) 60 min light (degraded products) and (e) Proposed degradation pathway based on the identified transformation products of MB using GC-MS studies.

In the spectrum, a prominent peak was observed at m/z 284, which corresponds to the molecular ion of MB (Fig. 14a). In the dark condition (30 min) the metabolites of MB are generated at m/z 221 and m/z 269,corresponding to 2,5-diaminobenzenesulfonic acid-methanol (1/1) and 3-(dimethylamino)-7-(methylamino)phenothiazine-5-ium, respectively are shown in Fig. 14b. These indicate that minimal degradation happened in the absence of light. Also, a peak at m/z 136 are formed during the adsorption which is corresponding to N¹, N¹-dimethylbenzene-1,4-diamine, which suggests demethylation and ring-opening reactions. Upon 30 min of visible light exposure (Fig. 14c), the spectra show a significant change. New intermediates appeared at m/z 158 and 167, which were assigned to benzenesulfonic acid and 4-((hydroxy-λ²-methyl)(methyl)amino)benzene-1, 2-diol, respectively. This suggests that sulfonic and amino groups in MB were oxidized and broken down under light irradiation. Finally, after continued irradiation at 60 min (Fig. 14d), a major product at m/z 101, were formed identified as cyclohexanol. This suggests that complete ring cleavage occurred, leading to the formation of simpler end products.

Based on the identified GC-MS spectra of different intermediates a possible degradation pathway of MB is proposed as shown in Fig. 14(e). The photocatalytic degradation pathway of MB confirms the stepwise breakdwon involving demethylation, ring-opening, desulfonation, and eventual mineralization.

Initially, MB undergoes demethylation, resulting in the formation of 3-(dimethylamino)-7-(methylamino)phenothiazin-5-ium. This intermediate then undergoes ring-opening reactions, producing 3 different aromatic amine derivatives. Subsequently, desulfonation of sulfonated intermediates occurs, leading to the formation of benzenesulfonic acid. Further mineralization into cyclohexanol suggesting significant breakdown of the aromatic structure into simpler, less toxic fragments. These results confirm that the photocatalytic process not only decolorizes MB but also leads to the transformation into smaller products.

Photocatalytic degradation mechanism of MB dye removal

Photocatalysis is a light driven chemical reaction that occurs more rapidly in the presence of a photocatalyst, which facilitates the transformation without being consumed in the process as shown in Fig. 15. Upon absorbing light energy equal to or greater than its band gap, the photocatalyst becomes activated as electrons are excited from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving behind positive holes in the valence band. This results in the formation of electron-hole pairs, which serves as active sites for redox reactions. The electrons in the CB can reduce electron acceptors such as dissolved oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) like superoxide radicals. At the same time, the holes in the VB can oxidize donor species like water or organic pollutants, producing hydroxyl radicals or directly oxidizing the contaminants. These reactive intermediates take part in breaking down complex molecules into non-toxic substances such as carbon dioxide and water making photocatalysis a green and effective technique for dye degradation35–38.

Conclusion

This study provides an in-depth investigation into how reaction time influences the synthesis and photocatalytic performance of g-C3N4. Synthesis of g-C3N4 has been done via thermal polymerization of thiourea at different reaction time. The synthesized samples (TU1, TU4, TU6 and TU8) were analysed using UV-DRS, FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM and PL spectroscopy. The results indicated that reaction time influenced the structural parameters crystallinity, interlayer spacing, morphology and electronic property of prepared g-C3N4 samples. Photocatalyst prepared at 4 h (TU4), demonstrated the most favourable characteristics, including optimal crystallinity, reduced electron-hole recombination, suitable band gap and well developed nanosheets like morphology. Degradation of methylene blue was done under natural sunlight to evaluate the photocatalytic activity in which TU4 exhibited the highest photocatalytic performance, achieving 91.01% degradation and 97.75% overall removal. Kinetic study revealed first order behaviour of adsorption and second order kinetics for photo degradation. Further parameter studies such as dosage, pH, and scavenger studies confirmed TU4’s efficiency. Recyclability study confirmed that TU4 retained over 90% degradation efficiency over up to 10 cycles, with minimal structural deterioration, highlighting its stability and reusability. These findings offer valuable insights for design of efficient, metal-free photocatalysts for sustainable wastewater treatment. Assessment of environmental safety of TU nanoparticles using human lung epithelial cells and bacterial cells showed all TU nanoparticles showed no significant acute cytotoxicity under the tested conditions .
