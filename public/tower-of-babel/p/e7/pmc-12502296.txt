Identification of C4BPA as a genetically informed drug target in NSCLC: an integrative single-cell and multi-omics study based on the druggable genes
Authors: Zhihan Xiao, Xinji Liu, Wei Tang, Yan Lv, Tongyu Zhang, Xu Zhan, Qihang Sun, Willis Wasonga Omindo, Qi Wang, Ruijie Zhang, Wei Ping, Ni Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite advancements in treatment, drug resistance and limited therapeutic efficacy persist, underscoring the urgent need for novel and mechanistically informed therapeutic strategies. Identifying genetically supported drug targets may accelerate the development of precision therapies in NSCLC.MethodsWe implemented an integrative multi-omics framework combining single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), genome-wide association studies (GWAS), and molecular quantitative trait locus (QTL) datasets including expression (eQTL), protein (pQTL), and DNA methylation (mQTL) QTLs. Druggable candidates were systematically evaluated using a suite of Mendelian randomization (MR) approaches—including summary data-based MR (SMR), generalized SMR (GSMR), and genetic risk score (GRS) analysis. Epigenetic regulation and downstream signaling were further explored through mediation MR analysis.ResultsC4BPA, a complement-regulatory macromolecule, emerged as a risk factor for NSCLC across multiple MR models, with consistent findings validated at both transcriptomic and proteomic levels. Epigenetic activation of C4BPA via DNA methylation was observed, and C4BPA expression was shown to promote NSCLC progression through the inflammatory chemokine CCL8 signaling axis. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of association inference.ConclusionsOur findings identify C4BPA as a genetically validated and biologically plausible therapeutic target for NSCLC. This study demonstrates the power of integrating single-cell transcriptomics with population-scale omics and association inference to uncover actionable targets, offering a scalable framework for advancing precision oncology in lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00829-3.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is among the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases. Its incidence and mortality remain persistently high across the globe [1, 2]. According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN report by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were an estimated 2.49 million new lung cancer cases globally, representing 12.4% of all cancer diagnoses, and 1.8 million deaths, accounting for 18.7% of all cancer-related mortality—the highest among all cancer types [1]. NSCLC encompasses two major histological subtypes: adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma [2]. Marked geographical heterogeneity exists in its incidence; for example, age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR) are 68.9 per 100,000 in North America and 64.8 per 100,000 in Europe, while in China, the ASIR is 62.5 per 100,000 [1]. Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death among men in China. The IARC further projects that over the next two decades, the global incidence and mortality of lung cancer will increase by 58.8% and 64% [1], respectively, underscoring the escalating public health burden of NSCLC amid global aging and worsening environmental pollution.

Therapeutic strategies for NSCLC have evolved from surgical resection for early-stage disease to include molecularly targeted therapy and immunotherapy in advanced stages, surpassing the traditional reliance on cytotoxic chemotherapy [3, 4]. Targeted agents directed against actionable driver mutations—such as EGFR, ALK, ROS1, and BRAF (e.g., osimertinib, alectinib)—have significantly improved outcomes for select patients [5]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., pembrolizumab, nivolumab) have shown promising efficacy in individuals with high PD-L1 expression or in those lacking targetable mutations [6, 7]. Despite these advances, several challenges persist [8–10]: (1) 30–40% of patients harbor no known actionable genomic alterations; (2) although targeted therapies initially yield robust responses, most patients develop acquired resistance within 12–18 months via mechanisms such as on-target mutations, bypass signaling activation, or histological transformation; and (3) immunotherapy shows relatively low response rates and carries the risk of immune-related adverse events. Thus, there remains a substantial unmet clinical need in terms of treatment coverage, durability of response, and toxicity management.

In recent years, the concept of “druggable genes” has gained traction as a promising avenue for identifying novel therapeutic targets. Druggable genes are defined as those encoding proteins that can be modulated by drug-like small molecules, typically based on sequence or structural homology to established drug targets [11]. These genes often participate in key signaling pathways or disease-related biological processes, and are associated with a higher likelihood of successful drug development [12]. Finan et al.. conducted a systematic integration of pharmacological databases and structural prediction algorithms, identifying 4,479 druggable genes [11]. Among these, 1,427 have been clinically validated or are currently targeted by approved or investigational drugs, while nearly 3,000 share structural features with known drug targets or belong to druggable protein families—thereby constituting a rich resource for target discovery [11].

Nonetheless, a major challenge in NSCLC research lies in effectively prioritizing disease-relevant and therapeutically actionable genes from among thousands of putative druggable candidates. To address this, our study employed an integrative framework combining single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and multi-omics data—including expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), protein QTL (pQTL), and methylation QTL (mQTL)—to identify target genes with strong associations to NSCLC [13–15]. We utilized Mendelian Randomization (MR), a robust genetic epidemiological approach, to infer the effects of gene expression on NSCLC risk using germline genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs). MR offers advantages in mitigating confounding bias and reverse causation, and has been widely adopted in target validation and drug repurposing studies [16].

Through multi-layered association inference and cross-omics validation, we identified C4BPA as a novel, druggable target gene characterized by tissue-specific expression, upregulated protein abundance, regulated DNA methylation, and strong association with NSCLC pathogenesis. This finding not only expands the therapeutic target landscape for NSCLC but also validates the practical utility of an integrated “single-cell multi-omics–MR” strategy for target identification in complex diseases. Our work provides a theoretical foundation and translational direction for precision medicine in NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Study design

The overarching framework of the study is visually summarized in Fig. 1. Briefly, our investigation began with the analysis of scRNA-seq data derived from NSCLC to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with the disease. From these DEGs, we prioritized druggable genes for subsequent examination. A two-sample MR analysis was then performed, leveraging cis-eQTLs of the selected druggable genes in blood as exposures and GWAS data for NSCLC as outcomes, to elucidate the relationship between gene expression and disease. Stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria were employed to select appropriate single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs. To ensure robustness, a comprehensive suite of sensitivity analyses was conducted to validate the quality of the MR findings. Subsequently, we performed cis-pQTL analysis to provide an additional layer of internal consistency checks by examining the influence of protein expression levels on NSCLC outcomes. Moreover, we employed SMR, GSMR and GRS methodologies to further interrogate the associations between the identified druggable genes and NSCLC. Finally, we delved into the upstream and downstream regulatory mechanisms governing these genes, offering deeper insights into their biological roles within the context of NSCLC.

Data sources

The 10x scRNA-seq dataset was obtained from GSE200972 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [17]. This dataset comprises 8 samples of NSCLC tissue and 6 samples of adjacent non-cancerous tissue. The selection of these specimens was strategically designed to illuminate the cellular heterogeneity inherent in NSCLC and its neighboring microenvironment, thereby enabling a comprehensive exploration of gene expression profiles at single-cell resolution.

Finan et al.. identified a comprehensive set of 4,479 druggable genes, encompassing 1,427 genes encoding targets of approved or clinical-phase therapeutics, 682 genes encoding proteins that interact with known drug molecules or share similarity with approved drug targets, and 2,370 genes belonging to pivotal druggable gene families or encoding proteins exhibiting distant homology to validated drug targets [18]. This diverse repertoire of druggable genes presents a broad array of potential avenues for therapeutic exploration. Of these, cis-eQTL data in blood were available for 2,525 genes, as determined through the eQTLGen Consortium [19]. This consortium integrates 37 datasets encompassing a combined cohort of 31,684 individuals, providing a robust resource for gene-expression analysis.

The pQTL dataset, encompassing 4,907 plasma proteins measured in 35,559 participants, was derived from the Iceland cohort [20]. The mQTL data were obtained from the GoDMC database (http://mqtldb.godmc.org.uk/downloads) [21]. This comprehensive dataset encompasses information derived from 27,750 samples analyzed using the Illumina 450 K platform. Further details regarding these datasets are available in the corresponding original publications.

Circulating inflammatory proteins were predominantly derived from a GWAS encompassing 14,824 healthy individuals. This analysis identified numerous common genetic variants influencing circulating cytokine levels, shedding light on the genetic underpinnings of systemic inflammation [22]. The corresponding data, including GWAS summary statistics for 91 distinct circulating inflammatory proteins, are accessible through the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/summary-statistics) under accession numbers GCST90274758 to GCST90274848.

The GWAS data for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) were obtained from the Finngen database (https://www.finngen.fi/en), comprising 6,446 cases and 378,749 controls. For comprehensive details regarding sample collection, analytical methodologies, and key findings, readers are encouraged to consult the original publication.

scRNA-Seq data processing

The quality control, analysis, and interrogation of the scRNA-seq data were conducted using the R package “Seurat” (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat) [23]. The analytical workflow unfolded as follows: (1) Cells failing to meet predefined quality thresholds were excluded based on specific cellular characteristics, including nFeature_RNA > 200, 200 < nCount_RNA < 30,000, percent.mt < 20, and percent.rb < 20. (2) Data normalization was performed using the “LogNormalize” method, which facilitated batch correction and data adjustment. (3) Cellular clustering was achieved through a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization-based algorithm implemented via the “FindClusters” function, employing a resolution parameter set at 0.6. Finally, the identification of DEGs was carried out using the “FindAllMarkers” function, with a log fold-change (logFC) threshold of 1 and a min.pct of 0.25.

Cell annotation and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on cell types

Cell annotation was primarily informed by established literature and the CellMarker database (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/). Following cell annotation, differential gene expression analysis was performed for each cell type utilizing the “FindAllMarkers” function. Genes meeting the criteria of an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an average log2FC > 1 were deemed significant DEGs and advanced to subsequent stages of the analysis.

Instrumental variables selection

To ensure the robustness and validity of our findings, it is imperative that three fundamental assumptions of MR analysis are satisfied [24–26]: (1) the IVs must exhibit a strong association with the exposure; (2) the IVs must be independent of any confounding variables; and (3) the IVs must influence the outcome solely through the exposure, without any alternative pathways, thereby avoiding horizontal pleiotropy. In adherence to these assumptions, a meticulous selection protocol was employed for each druggable gene investigated in our study. Initially, we applied a stringent threshold, selecting SNPs from eQTL, pQTL, and mQTL datasets, restricting consideration to those with P-values surpassing the genome-wide significance threshold of 5.0 × 10⁻⁸. Subsequently, to ensure the independence of the selected SNPs, we implemented clumping based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) metrics derived from the 1,000 Genomes Project population reference panel. This procedure employed an LD threshold of r² < 0.01 with a clumping window of 10,000 kb, yielding a set of mutually independent SNPs for each druggable gene. The F-statistic served as a critical measure in the MR analysis, quantifying the strength of the association between the IVs and the exposure variable. This statistic also facilitated the identification and exclusion of weak instruments, which could otherwise introduce bias. Accordingly, SNPs with F-statistics below 10 were excluded from further analysis to mitigate the risk of weak instrument bias [27].

Mendelian randomization analysis of eQTLs, pQTLs and mQTLs

MR analysis was conducted utilizing the R package “TwoSampleMR” [28]. For instances where only a single SNP served as the IV, the Wald ratio method was employed. In cases where the IV comprised two or more SNPs, five statistical approaches were applied: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, Weighted median, Simple mode, and Weighted mode [29]. Prior studies have demonstrated that while the IVW method is relatively conservative, it offers greater robustness compared to the alternative techniques [30]. Consequently, the primary results were derived from the IVW method, regardless of the presence of heterogeneity, with findings from the supplementary methods serving as secondary support. To ensure the reliability of the analysis, a series of sensitivity tests was performed. The potential heterogeneity among IVs was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, with a P-value below 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity [31]. Additionally, MR-Egger regression was employed to detect potential pleiotropy in the relationship between exposures and outcomes [31]. A P-value below 0.05 for the MR-Egger regression intercept was indicative of pleiotropy, undermining the validity of the MR analysis results.

Summary-data-based MR (SMR) analysis

To evaluate the association between druggable genes and the risk of NSCLC, we employed SMR. This approach, leveraging the most significantly associated cis-eQTLs, achieves substantially greater statistical power compared to traditional MR analyses, particularly when the exposure and outcome datasets are derived from two independent cohorts with large sample sizes [32]. The selection of top cis-eQTLs was based on a genomic window spanning ± 1000 kb around the target gene, with a stringent significance threshold of P < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸. To differentiate true relationships from spurious associations due to linkage, the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test was employed. Variants with P-HEIDI values < 0.05, indicative of likely linkage, were excluded from subsequent analyses. Both SMR and HEIDI tests were conducted using the SMR software package (v1.3.1), ensuring methodological rigor and reproducibility.

Generalized summary-data-based MR (GSMR) analysis

In addition to using the SMR method, generalized summary-data-based MR (GSMR) was used as a complementary method to assess the robustness of the association under an alternative MR framework. GSMR, an extension of SMR that uses weights from variance-covariance structures constructed from correlations between IVs, A generalized linear model was used to calculate the total effect size. GSMR can more accurately assess the influence of genetic factors on complex traits [33, 34]. GSMR analyses were performed with “gsmr2” package.

Genetic risk scores (GRSs)

To further assess internal consistency, we performed a supplementary sensitivity analysis using the GRS methodology. This analysis was implemented within the R programming environment, utilizing the “gtx” package (version 0.0.8 for Windows), which includes the grs.summary module and its integrated GRS function. The grs.summary module operates on SNP association summary statistics derived from GWAS, employing an approach akin to regressing an outcome on an additive GRS [35, 36]. Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on paraffin-embedded NSCLC tumor and adjacent normal lung tissues. Sections were deparaffinized using xylene substitute, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and washed with Tris-buffered saline (TBS). Antigen retrieval was carried out in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) using a pressure cooker (high-temperature, high-pressure) for 1 min and 30 s. After natural cooling to room temperature, sections were washed with TBS and incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Following treatment with a hydrophobic barrier, sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit monoclonal anti-C4BPA antibody (Abcam; 1:2000 dilution). After equilibration to room temperature and TBST washing, slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam; 1:2000) for 45 min at 37 °C. Signal development was achieved using a DAB detection kit (Maixin, DAB-4033; 1:20 dilution), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Sections were then differentiated, blued, dehydrated in graded ethanol, air-dried, and mounted using an eco-friendly mounting medium. Staining results were observed using a bright-field microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and relevant R packages as indicated in the corresponding methodological subsections. For MR analyses, statistical significance was primarily determined using the IVW method, while supporting methods including MR-Egger regression, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode were used for sensitivity analysis. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Correction for multiple testing was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method where applicable, and adjusted P-values (False Discovery Rate, FDR) < 0.05 were considered significant in multi-gene comparisons.

Cochran’s Q test was applied to assess heterogeneity among instrumental variables, and the MR-Egger intercept was used to test for horizontal pleiotropy. For SMR, P-HEIDI < 0.05 was considered indicative of heterogeneity due to linkage. For gene expression and protein level comparisons between NSCLC and normal tissues, two-tailed wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used. Immunohistochemistry results were evaluated qualitatively and semi-quantitatively under blinded conditions.

Results

We undertook a comprehensive analysis of the scRNA-seq dataset, meticulously filtered according to the criteria outlined in the preceding methods. This endeavor culminated in the identification of 36 discrete cell clusters through t-SNE clustering analysis (Fig. 2A-B). Across all samples, five principal cell types were annotated using the SingleR algorithm: Dendritic cells (DCs), Eosinophils, Macrophages, Monocytes, and Stromal cells (Fig. 2C-D). Furthermore, we examined differential gene expression at the single-cell level between NSCLC and control groups. The DEGs derived from each individual cell type were subsequently incorporated into next analyses (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Tables 1–5).

eQTL analysis for druggable genes

From the DEGs identified through scRNA-seq analysis, we prioritized druggable genes for further investigation. Subsequently, we performed cis-eQTL analysis to assess the relationship between the expression of these druggable genes and NSCLC. In accordance with stringent criteria for IV selection, a total of 2,399 cis-eQTLs associated with druggable genes were identified as IVs. The F-statistics for all IVs exceeded the threshold of 10, indicating a robust instrument set devoid of weak instrument bias. Using IVW and the Wald ratio method, we identified significant associations between the expression of druggable genes and NSCLC across various cell types. After correction for FDR, the expression of 200 druggable genes was significantly correlated with NSCLC, of which 107 were identified as risk factors and 93 as protective factors in DC. The expression of 15 druggable genes showed significant correlations, with 10 serving as risk factors and 5 as protective factors in Eosinophils. Within Macrophages, 8 druggable genes exhibited significant associations, with 5 acting as risk factors and 3 as protective factors. In Monocytes, the expression of 91 druggable genes was significantly correlated with NSCLC, with 49 identified as risk factors and 42 as protective factors. Finally, in Stromal cells, 44 druggable genes were significantly associated with NSCLC, including 25 risk factors and 19 protective factors (Supplementary Table 6).

pQTL analysis for druggable genes

To further substantiate the effect of druggable gene expression on NSCLC, we examined plasma protein levels using pQTL data. Before correction for FDR, the pQTL analysis revealed that elevated plasma levels of five druggable genes — C4BPA, CD33, CLEC11A, CBR1, and SORD — were associated with an increased risk of NSCLC. Conversely, higher plasma levels of two druggable genes—SECTM1, and B4GALT1 — were linked to a reduced risk of NSCLC, findings that align with the results of the eQTL analysis (Table 1). After correction for FDR, the elevated level of C4BPA was still associated with an increased risk of NSCLC, which was consistent with the results of the eQTL analysis (Table 1). Moreover, the absence of heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy in IVs for these significant druggable genes, as confirmed by Cochran’s Q test and MR-Egger regression, further bolsters the robustness of these observations (Table 1). In addition to C4BPA, CD55 also emerged as a candidate gene of interest. CD55 was identified as a significant DEG in dendritic cells and monocytes, and showed nominal associations in eQTL-based MR (OR = 1.0405, P = 0.0235; Supplementary Table 6). However, when assessed in pQTL-based MR, the effect direction was inconsistent (OR = 0.9387, P = 0.0399; Supplementary Table 7), and the association did not remain significant after FDR correction (FDR = 0.319). Further analyses using SMR (P = 0.2141) and GSMR (P = 0.1052) also failed to support a causal role for CD55 (Supplementary Table 8). Taken together, while CD55 has been previously implicated in NSCLC and showed preliminary signals in transcript-level analyses, the lack of consistency across proteomic and multi-model MR frameworks suggests weaker support relative to C4BPA.

Validation using lung-specific eQTL data

To validate whether the genetically regulated expression of C4BPA is relevant in the lung tissue microenvironment, we further conducted MR analyses using lung-specific cis-eQTL data from the GTEx database. The results consistently demonstrated a positive association between predicted C4BPA expression in lung tissue and NSCLC risk: IVW: OR = 1.09 (95% CI:1.04–1.15), P = 0.0010; Weighted median: OR = 1.12 (95% CI: 1.06–1.19), P = 1.76 × 10⁻⁴; Weighted mode: OR = 1.13 (95% CI: 1.06–1.20), P = 0.015 (Supplementary Table 9). These findings corroborate the associations observed using blood-derived eQTL data and further emphasize the tissue-specific relevance of C4BPA in the pathogenesis of NSCLC.

Tissue-specific expression profile of C4BPA and neighboring genes

To assess the tissue specificity of C4BPA, we analyzed both transcriptomic and proteomic expression profiles of C4BPA and its neighboring genes (within ± 500 kb) across multiple normal human tissues. Transcriptomic data were retrieved from the GTEx database, and normalized expression values [log₂ (nTPM + 0.01)] were used for comparison. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, C4BPA is moderately expressed in lung tissue, while its expression is low or undetectable in most other tissues. In contrast, neighboring genes such as CD55 and MAPKAPK2 displayed either broad and high expression across many tissues or minimal expression in lung tissue, suggesting lower tissue specificity. To further validate these findings at the protein level, we queried the HPA database. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, C4BPA exhibits high protein expression in lung tissue but is undetectable in other normal tissues, whereas several neighboring genes demonstrated broad, low, or no expression in the lung. These findings collectively indicate that C4BPA shows a relatively lung-specific expression pattern at both mRNA and protein levels, reinforcing its potential biological relevance in the context of NSCLC.

External validation of C4BPA expression

To strengthen the reliability of C4BPA as a potential therapeutic target, we performed a multi-level validation across independent datasets. First, public transcriptomic data from three GEO cohorts (GSE1037, GSE6044, and GSE42830) consistently showed significantly higher expression of C4BPA in NSCLC tissues compared to adjacent or normal lung tissues (Fig. 3C–E). Second, proteomic data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) demonstrated strong immunohistochemical positivity for C4BPA in lung cancer tissues, whereas normal lung tissues exhibited no detectable expression (Fig. 3F). Third, we conducted immunohistochemical staining on paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues from our clinical cohort, which revealed markedly elevated C4BPA protein levels in NSCLC samples (Fig. 3F). These findings confirm the overexpression of C4BPA in NSCLC at both the mRNA and protein levels, further supporting its potential as a clinically relevant drug target.

DNA methylation epigenetically activated C4BPA

To elucidate the mechanisms through which C4BPA facilitates the initiation and progression of NSCLC, we employed a mediated MR framework to investigate upstream regulatory factors influencing C4BPA expression. From an initial pool of ten C4BPA DNA methylation sites, five loci (cg14856606, cg17803430, cg22491058, cg26430305, cg26514552) were retained following rigorous IV screening based on stringent inclusion criteria. In the first stage, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis to examine the relationship between DNA methylation sites and NSCLC. The findings revealed significant associations for three methylation sites (cg14856606: OR = 0.869, P = 0.004; cg17803430: OR = 0.481, P = 0.029; cg22491058: OR = 1.586, P = 0.011; Table 3). In the second stage, these three NSCLC-associated methylation sites were analyzed as exposures in an MR framework, with cis-eQTLs of C4BPA serving as outcomes. This analysis identified a single methylation site significantly associated with C4BPA expression (cg14856606: OR = 0.123, P = 2.802 × 10⁻⁵; Table 3). In the final stage, we integrated the results from the MR analyses of C4BPA DNA methylation sites and NSCLC, the MR associations between methylation sites and C4BPA, and the prior MR findings linking C4BPA expression to NSCLC. Through mediated MR analysis, we demonstrated that DNA methylation (cg14856606) influences NSCLC development via its regulatory effect on C4BPA expression. Notably, the mediation proportion attributed to C4BPA was approximately 50% (Fig. 4A).

C4BPA promotes the development of NSCLC through the CCL8 signaling pathway

To further elucidate the downstream mechanisms by which C4BPA influences the onset and progression of NSCLC, a multi-step analytical approach was undertaken. In the initial phase, we employed a two-sample MR analysis, using 91 circulating inflammatory proteins as exposures and NSCLC as the outcome. This analysis identified five circulating inflammatory proteins significantly associated with NSCLC (Table 4). In the subsequent phase, C4BPA cis-eQTLs were utilized as exposures, and the five inflammatory proteins identified in the first step were examined as outcomes in a second two-sample MR analysis. Among these, only monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 levels (CCL8) demonstrated a significant association with C4BPA. In the final step, we conducted a mediation MR analysis, with C4BPA cis-eQTLs as exposures, CCL8 as the mediating factor, and NSCLC as the outcome. The results suggested that C4BPA may contribute to NSCLC pathogenesis through its regulation of CCL8, with CCL8 mediating approximately 5% of the effect (Fig. 4B). We further performed transcriptomic analyses using the TCGA-LUAD and TCGA-LUSC cohorts to examine the expression pattern of CCL8. The results showed that CCL8 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in NSCLC tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 3G), providing transcript-level evidence supporting its involvement as a downstream effector in the C4BPA-mediated inflammatory axis. Consistently, in the earlier stage of our study, single-cell transcriptomic analysis also revealed that CCL8 was highly expressed in tumor cells but exhibited low expression in adjacent non-malignant tissues, further reinforcing its tumor-associated expression profile (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Through integrative analysis of scRNA-seq data and multi-omics genetic information, combined with multi-model MR frameworks, this study identifies C4BPA as a potential druggable target in NSCLC. C4BPA was found to be significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues and exhibited consistent associations with disease risk across transcriptomic, proteomic, and DNA methylation layers, highlighting its potential as a key player in tumorigenesis. The rigorous inference approach and comprehensive cross-analysis pipeline presented herein propose a novel paradigm for therapeutic target discovery and provide mechanistic insights into the epigenetic and immuno-inflammatory regulation of NSCLC.

We first performed DEG analysis on five major cell types — DC, eosinophils, macrophages, monocytes, and stromal cells — identified within NSCLC tissues using scRNA-seq. This analysis yielded a panel of candidate genes with putative therapeutic relevance. To assess their roles, we constructed a multi-omics MR platform leveraging publicly available datasets including eQTLGen, DeCODE, and GoDMC. The platform integrates eQTL, pQTL, and mQTL datasets to systematically evaluate these “druggable genes” in NSCLC pathogenesis. Notably, C4BPA emerged repeatedly as significantly associated with NSCLC risk. Its elevated expression correlated robustly with increased disease susceptibility, supported by consistent results in both blood- and lung-derived eQTL datasets. pQTL analysis further confirmed a positive association between C4BPA protein levels and NSCLC risk.

We further validated C4BPA overexpression in NSCLC tissues using external transcriptomic (GEO) and proteomic (HPA) datasets, as well as independent immunohistochemistry evidence. These findings collectively strengthened its candidacy as a therapeutic target. Importantly, C4BPA displayed strong tissue specificity at both mRNA and protein levels in lung tissues, suggesting its potential involvement in lung cancer–specific biological processes.

To elucidate upstream regulatory mechanisms, we conducted mediation MR analyses based on mQTL data. We identified cg14856606 as a DNA methylation site inversely associated with both C4BPA expression and NSCLC risk, suggesting that hypomethylation at this locus may activate C4BPA transcription. Mediation MR revealed that approximately 50% of cg14856606’s effect on NSCLC risk could be attributed to its influence on C4BPA expression. These results imply that C4BPA may act not merely as a passive responder but as an epigenetically driven oncogenic factor in NSCLC.

To further investigate the functional consequences of C4BPA upregulation, we assessed its impact on circulating inflammatory factors via MR analysis of 91 cytokines. CCL8 emerged as a significant mediator, with C4BPA expression positively associated with elevated CCL8 levels, which in turn were linked to increased NSCLC risk. Mediation analysis estimated that CCL8 accounted for ~ 5% of the C4BPA-to-NSCLC effect, indicating it may act as a downstream effector in C4BPA-driven inflammatory signaling. Although the direct molecular mechanism linking C4BPA to CCL8 remains to be fully elucidated, prior studies suggest a plausible pathway. For instance, C4BPA has been reported to activate the NF-κB pathway, thereby modulating inflammation and apoptosis in cancer cells [39]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma models, C4BPA upregulation was shown to induce expression of chemokines such as CCL2 and IL-8, reinforcing its potential role in tumor-associated inflammation [40]. These findings support the hypothesis that C4BPA may broadly regulate inflammatory chemokines, warranting further validation in NSCLC models.

It is also important to acknowledge the potential role of CD55 in NSCLC. Previous genetic and mechanistic studies have demonstrated that CD55 contributes to lung cancer susceptibility and immune evasion. For example, a case–control study in a Chinese population reported that CD55 polymorphism (rs2564978) was significantly associated with NSCLC risk, with the risk allele reducing promoter activity in functional assays [41]. More recently, Shao et al. showed that EGFR/Wnt signaling upregulates CD55 expression via the LINC00973–miR-216b axis, thereby inhibiting complement activation, suppressing CD8⁺ T cell function, and contributing to immune checkpoint blockade resistance [42]. These studies underscore CD55 as a biologically plausible and clinically relevant candidate gene. In our analyses, CD55 was also identified as a significant DEG in dendritic cells and monocytes, and showed nominally significant associations in eQTL-based MR. However, these associations were not consistently replicated: pQTL-based MR yielded effect estimates in the opposite direction and failed to retain significance after FDR correction, and both SMR and GSMR analyses were non-significant. Taken together, while CD55 remains an important candidate supported by previous studies and merits future investigation, the convergent and reproducible evidence across transcriptomic, proteomic, and epigenetic levels observed for C4BPA renders it the more robust candidate in the present study. This comparative analysis further illustrates the importance of applying a systematic, multi-layered framework to prioritize targets with the most consistent cross-omics evidence.

This study is characterized by a systematic and multi-layered analytical framework. We employed SMR, GSMR, and GRS methods to enhance MR-based association inference and performed multiple robustness checks across models. This integrative strategy provides a reproducible blueprint for therapeutic target identification in complex diseases.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the scRNA-seq dataset was limited in sample size, potentially constraining the breadth of differentially expressed gene identification and downstream target screening sensitivity. Second, the majority of QTL data were derived from peripheral blood, which—despite lung tissue supplementation—may limit generalizability due to tissue-specific differences. Third, our study is the lack of publicly available proteomic data on CCL8. Although we attempted to address this by conducting transcriptomic validation using TCGA, which revealed significant upregulation of CCL8 mRNA in NSCLC tumor tissues, the absence of direct protein-level evidence remains a gap. This limitation may partially hinder the strength of inference regarding the C4BPA–CCL8 axis. Lastly, all mechanistic and pathway insights were inferred from statistical models and thus require validation through in vitro and in vivo functional assays.

To address these limitations and build upon our findings, future studies should: (1) Expand scRNA-seq analyses across larger and more diverse NSCLC cohorts, incorporating various molecular subtypes and immune landscapes; (2) Conduct functional assays in cellular and animal models to delineate C4BPA’s role in cell proliferation, migration, and immune modulation, particularly its regulation of the CCL8 axis; (3) Explore development of C4BPA-targeting agents such as inhibitors or neutralizing antibodies and evaluate their efficacy in combination with immunotherapy or targeted therapy; (4) Investigate whether C4BPA influences other inflammatory mediators or immune cell pathways, establishing its potential as a broader immunomodulatory target.

Conclusion

This study systematically identifies and validates C4BPA as a potential therapeutic target in NSCLC through integrative scRNA-seq, multi-omics QTL data, and advanced MR analyses. Our findings reveal that C4BPA may be epigenetically regulated via DNA demethylation and contributes to NSCLC pathogenesis partly through modulation of inflammatory cytokines such as CCL8. These insights expand the molecular landscape of NSCLC targets and offer a conceptual foundation for future translational strategies in tumor immunology and precision oncology.
