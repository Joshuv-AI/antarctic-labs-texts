A Cross-Sectional Study to Evaluate the Alteration of Cytokine Expression and Activation of Inflammatory Pathway in Response to NOD1 and NOD2 Signal in Leprosy
Authors: Abu Hena Hasanoor Reja, Abhishek De, Disha Chakraborty, Sk. S. Ahmed, Aarti Sarda
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Leprae bacilli are identified as foreign by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) present in the microbes but absent in the host. The Nucleotide oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor (NLR) family comprises the nucleotide-binding oligomerisation domain (NOD1 and NOD2) proteins, which are two well-known PRRs. The objectives of this study were to study the expression of cytoplasmic NOD1 and NOD2 in the pathogenesis of leprosy and the serum level of expressed cytokines and to measure the messenger Ribonucleic Acid (mRNA) expression.Methods:Clinically suspected Hansen’s patients were analysed for 4 years. Newly diagnosed leprosy patients were considered leprosy disease control (LDC). The cases with active or new lesions and an increase in Bacteriological index (BI) by at least 2 + after 12 months of completion of Multidrug therapy (MDT) were considered leprosy disease relapse (LDR) cases. Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals served as our control group (healthy control (HC)). enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed to measure the concentration of five human cytokines in serum, including three pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, Interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and IL-6), one anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) and one chemokine (IL-8). Quantitative expression of receptor genes (NOD1 and NOD2) and cytokine genes (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-8) was evaluated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (qRT-PCR). We studied NOD1 and NOD2 expression in the tissues through fluorescence immunohistochemistry. Differential NLR intracellular expression on peripheral blood monocytes (PBMs) and their response to stimulation with specific ligands (lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and muramyl dipeptide (MDP)) were studied.Results:A significant difference in the expression of the NOD1 gene was observed in unstimulated monocytes of the LDC and LDR cases when compared to HC. The NOD2 transcript level was significantly higher in stimulated monocytes from LDC and LDR patients than in similarly stimulated cells from HC. The LDC patients had a significantly higher level of pro-inflammatory cytokines as compared to the HC.Conclusion:In conclusion, this study has demonstrated the expression of both cytokines and chemokines in response to NLR activation in the skin of leprosy patients.

Introduction

The innate immune system of our body is responsible for the initial defence against all kinds of microbes. There is a constant balance between stimulation and suppression of the host immune response mediated by innate immunity genes. Any disturbance in these homeostatic mechanisms can lead to chronic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases. The first step in microbial defence is identifying them as foreign. This is mediated by conserved molecular motifs that are present in microbes but absent in the host. As microbial sensors, ‘pattern recognition’ molecules identify them. The NOD-like receptor (NLR) family comprises the nucleotide-binding oligomerisation domain (NOD1 and NOD2) proteins, which are two well-known pattern recognition receptors (PRRs).[1]

While other PRRs, such as Toll-like receptors, target cell surface microbial ligands or those within endosomes, NOD receptors prevent pathogen invasion by sensing the bacterial products in the host cytoplasm. The N-terminal of NLR is their effector domain, which controls downstream signalling, while the C-terminal has leucine-rich regions (LRRs) responsible for ligand recognition. NLRs are inactive until their C-terminal recognises a ligand, which induces conformational changes in the N-terminal, resulting in the activation of downstream signalling pathways. The activation regulates intracellular signalling pathways (such as nuclear factor κβ (NF-κβ), caspase-1 inflammasome and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs)), which leads to altered gene expression of proteins involved in inflammatory responses.[23]

Due to uncertainty regarding the association of cytokines with leprosy disease manifestation and severity, this study aimed to evaluate the role of the cytoplasmic receptors such as NOD1 and NOD2 in mediating host immunity against pathogens through subsequent activation of cytokines.[45]

The objectives of this study were to study the expression of cytoplasmic NOD1 and NOD2 in the pathogenesis of leprosy by fluorescent immunohistochemistry, to study the serum level of expressed cytokines by ELISA and to measure the mRNA expression by real-time PCR using SYBR Green.

Materials and Methods

Clinically suspected Hansen’s patients reporting to the different leprosy clinics in Kolkata, West Bengal, were selected following World health organization (WHO) guidelines and classified according to the Ridley–Joplin classification for 4 years. The clinical diagnosis was performed by two independent, dermatology consultants.

Age, sex and ethnicity-matched healthy adults without any history suggestive of leprosy, attending the outpatient department of the same institute for the treatment of skin diseases other than leprosy, constituted our control group. Newly diagnosed leprosy patients were selected following WHO guidelines, classified according to the Ridley–Joplin classification and considered as leprosy disease control (LDC). The cases with active lesions and an increase in BI by at least 2 + after completion of MDT were considered leprosy disease relapse (LDR) cases. Also, we did rpoB and folP mutation analysis and none of the cases were positive.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Collection of serum samples

A 2 mL venous blood sample was collected in an Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated tube by venipuncture from the subjects and stored at -20º C until used.

Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and separation and purification of monocytes

PBMCs were isolated by density gradient separation and washed twice with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium. Monocytes were isolated from the PBMCs by incubation with magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) beads coated with a monoclonal antibody against human cluster of differentiation (CD)14.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for IL-6, IL-10, IFN-g, TNF-a and IL-8 from clinical isolates.

ELISA was performed to measure the concentration of five human cytokines in serum, including three pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-6), one anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) and one chemokine (IL-8).

Isolation of RNA from CD14 + monocyte cells and complementary Deoxyribonucleic Acid (cDNA) preparation.

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 1 X 106 monocyte cultured cells using TRIzol reagent, which was reverse transcribed using Moloney murine leukaemia virus (M-MuLV) reverse transcriptase and oligo (dT) primer to create complementary DNA (cDNA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Quantitative expression of receptor genes (NOD1 and NOD2) and cytokine genes (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-8) was evaluated by qRT-PCR. Fresh media supplemented with 10% FBS were added to the overnight cultured cells and stimulated with 10 μl/ml of muramyl dipeptide (MDP) and 10 μl/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS) overnight.

Harvesting of activated cells

The overnight activated cells were harvested using a sterile cell scraper and fractionated for nuclear extract according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The remaining parts of the harvested cells were then subjected to RNA isolation.

Purification of cellular nuclear extract from harvested cells

Cellular nuclear extract was purified from the first part of the harvested cells using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), phosphatase inhibitor, hypotonic buffer and 1X extraction buffer.

Statistical analysis

Age, sex and nutritional status were compared among the study groups by Student’s two-tailed t-test. The significance of the difference between corresponding groups of observations was evaluated by the Mann–Whitney U–test, and all values were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE) and range. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient on the logged data was used to determine the relation in serum levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-8.

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad software (Prism 5 2010, File version 5.0.3.0). Acceptable significance was recorded when P values were < 0.05. Continuous data are given as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis for genotype and sex distributions was evaluated by the Chi-square test.

Results

We screened 457 clinically suspected cases, of which there were 246 LDC cases and 136 LDR cases. Patients with negative slit skin smears or suspected pure neuritic cases were excluded. We had 112 healthy controls. Differential NLR intracellular expression on peripheral blood monocytes (PBMs) in healthy control (HC), LDC patients and LDR cases and their response to specific ligands were studied by stimulating the PBMCs with LPS and MDP.

mRNA expression of NOD1

A significant difference in the expression of the NOD1 gene was observed in the unstimulated monocytes of LDC patients and LDR cases when compared to HC (P < 0.0001). The effect of stimulation with LPS and MDP on NOD1 gene expression had a potent effect in HC, whereas in LDC patients, we detected increased mRNA expression in comparison with unstimulated cells (P < 0.0001). However, we did not detect a significant difference in LDR cases when each stimulation was compared, for both the representative ligands (P < 0.0001 for LPS and P < 0.0001 for MDP) [Figure 3a].

mRNA expression of NOD2

NOD2 transcript level was significantly higher in stimulated monocytes from LDC patients than in similarly stimulated cells from HC (P < 0.0001). The effect of stimulation with LPS and MDP on NOD2 gene expression of LDC patients had a higher effect when stimulated with MDP (P < 0.0001) compared with stimulation with LPS (P < 0.005). A similar, significant effect in LDR cases with similar stimulations was observed. The transcript levels of the representative subjects could not reach any significant differences with stimulation of the LPS (P = 0.14) in LDR cases as compared to LPS (P < 0.0001) stimulation in LDC patients [Figure 3b].

An abundance of pro-inflammatory cytokines in HC, LDC patients and LDR cases was observed.

Among the pro-inflammatory cytokines, the LDC patients had a significantly higher level of serum TNF-α (P < 0.0001), IL-6 (P < 0.0001) and chemokine IL-8 (P < 0.0014), as compared to the HC. Also, these pro-inflammatory cytokines were assessed for LDR cases, which had a significantly higher level of serum TNF-α (P < 0.0057), IL-6 (P < 0.0001) and chemokine IL-8 (P < 0.0001), as compared to the LDC [Figure 3c-e], respectively. The serum IFN-γ did not show any significant rise in LDC patients (P = 0.2548), whereas it showed a minor significance in LDR cases (P = 0.0299), as shown in Figure 3f.

Expression of TNF-α

The impaired TNF-α expression in monocytes of LDC patients and LDR cases was confirmed at the transcript level. The mRNA expression of TNF-α isolated from monocytes of LDC was highly increased following stimulation with LPS and MDP than that observed for unstimulated cells.

Stimulation with MDP showed a higher increase in TNF-α (P < 0.0001 for all cases) than that of the stimulation with LPS in LDC patients (P < 0.005). Similarly, significantly increased expression of the cytokine was also seen following stimulation with LPS (P < 0.0001) and MDP (P < 0.0001) for all the cases from monocytes of LDR cases [Figure 3g].

Expression of IL–6

Stimulation with LPS showed a higher increase in IL-6 (P < 0.001 for all the cases) and MDP in LDC patients (P < 0.001 for all the cases). Similarly, significantly increased expression of the cytokine mRNA was also seen following stimulation with LPS (P < 0.0001 for all the cases), but a lesser expression was seen when stimulated with MDP (P < 0.005 for all the cases) from monocytes of LDR cases [Figure 3h].

Expression of IFN-γ

The mRNA expression of IFN-γ showed an insignificant increase in the monocyte of LDC and LDR cases when stimulated with LPS and MDP (P = 0.451 for all the cases). The efficient expression of the cytokine mRNA was also observed in the case of HC after stimulations with LPS (P < 0.001) and MDP (P < 0.001). Similarly, reduced expression of cytokine mRNA was observed in LDR cases when stimulated with LPS (P = 0.235) and MDP (P = 0.112) when compared to baseline [Figure 3i].

Expression of IL-8

The impaired IL-8 expression in monocytes of LDC patients and LDR cases was confirmed at the transcript level. The mRNA expression significantly increased in LDC patients when stimulated with MDP (P < 0001 for all cases) followed by when stimulated with LPS (P < 0.005 for all cases).

For LDR cases, an increase in expression mRNA was observed when stimulated with MDP (P < 0.0001 for all cases). Stimulation of LPS did not show any significant rise in mRNA expression (P = 0.124) of IL-8, as shown in Figure 3j.

Alteration of anti-inflammatory cytokines

Of the anti-inflammatory cytokines, IL-10 in the serum of LDC patients revealed a somewhat similar slightly significant pattern in LDC (P = 0.0209) and in LDR cases (P = 0.0309) compared with HC and LDC, respectively, as shown in Figure 3k.

Expression of IL-10

mRNA expression of IL-10 was reduced in the monocyte of HC, LDC patients and LDR cases when stimulated with LPS and MDP (P = 0.342) for all the cases. We observed efficient expression of the cytokine mRNA in cases of HC after stimulations with LPS (P < 0.001) and MDP (P < 0.001), but there was reduced expression of cytokine mRNA, which was observed in LDR cases when stimulated with LPS (P = 0.354 for all cases) and MDP (P = 0.1113 for all cases) when compared to baseline, as shown in Figure 3l.

Discussion

Our immune system starts developing upon exposure to microbial stimuli, and the innate immune system plays a vital role in activating adaptive immunity. According to epidemiological data, atopic diseases are largely due to microbial exposure through the induction and expression of specific genes in early childhood. High endotoxin exposure causes reduced sensitisation rates.[6] Peptidoglycan is a common component of the cell wall of both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, while endotoxin is specific for gram-negative bacteria. NOD1 and NOD2 respond to different peptidoglycan motifs with NOD1 being specific for gram-negative peptidoglycan.[7] The muramic acid component of peptidoglycan is known to activate the innate immune system via Toll like receptor (TLR)-2, which stimulates TNF-a, IL-1 and IL-6 in human monocytes and IL-6, IL-8 and β-defensin-2 in human respiratory epithelial cells.[89]

The impact of leprosy is profound as it is a leading cause of deformity in patients all over the Indian subcontinent. In light of the evidence linking inflammation and leprosy, it is of utmost importance to determine whether cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in vivo production of this protein culminate in the pathogenesis of this disease.

It is speculated that NOD1 and NOD2 functions contribute to the balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic signalling pathways. This is mediated by cytokines through Th1 and Th2 immune pathways. They cause nuclear factor-kB activation and caspase-9-induced apoptosis. They also enhance the function of caspase-1, which is an important regulator of inflammatory reactions by proteolytic processing of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and pro-IL-8.[10]

As bacterial entry into cells was important in cytokine production by macrophages, we were interested in the role of the cytosolic receptor, NODs, in recognising the molecular pattern from pathogen phagocytosed into a cell. This was examined by measuring the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. We monitored the level of NOD-dependent expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and TNF-α. We observed that the expression of NOD1 and NOD2 is increased in the cells transfected with MDP and LPS. These are potent ligands for NOD expression in the macrophages derived from monocytes isolated from the infected patients. This suggests that M. leprae infection plays an active role in the activation of NLR pathways.

We also observed that transfected cells induced IL-6 and TNF-α expression to M. leprae through specific mRNA. As previously reported, the host response to M. tuberculosis was NOD2-dependent. We further assessed whether the immune response to M. leprae was dependent on NOD1 or NOD2. There was no difference between NOD1 and NOD2, in terms of the response of cells to M. leprae infection. However, there was a minimal response to LPS for NOD1 in transfected cells. A significantly higher response was observed for MDP for NOD2 in transfected cells. This shows that NOD2 possessed a significantly higher potential to identify and activate the inflammatory cascade for M. leprae infection in host cells in comparison with NOD1.

Elevated serum levels of TNF-α in leprosy patients suggest its involvement in the disease pathogenesis. In addition to acting as an immune regulatory agent, it also damages myelin, and oligodendrocytes stimulate bone resorption and inhibit collagen synthesis. The higher levels of TNF-α mRNA in transfected cells with LPS and MDP justify its role in clinical symptoms such as nerve impairment, disability and deformity that are often observed in leprosy patients.[111213]

IL-6 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in acute-phase reactions. It is produced by cells of both innate and adaptive immune responses and contributes to B-cell growth and differentiation. The higher expression of the cytokines in response to MDP and LPS suggests that IL-6 might play an active role in controlling the severity and thereby the long-term prognosis of the disease deformities.[14]

IL-8 is another pro-inflammatory cytokine that predominantly recruits neutrophils, monocytes and T lymphocytes, which express its receptors C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR)1 and CXCR2. Thus, the presence of IL-8 in leprosy lesions strongly suggests the role of this chemokine as a monocyte and lymphocyte recruiter.[15]

To determine whether NOD1 and NOD2 can promote the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines or chemokines in M. leprae-infected monocytes, we examined IL-8 production in response to LPS and MDP. Both LPS and MDP induced IL-8 production in the cultured monocytes. This result is based on previous studies showing that MDP alone did not induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines or chemokines in most of the cells except those of intestinal origin.[16]

Of the anti-inflammatory cytokine, serum levels of IL-10 and IFN-γ were abundant in the leprosy patients. This is expected because the pro-inflammatory response produced by M. leprae infection is expected to be countered by anti-inflammatory cytokines. Interestingly, the levels of IL-10 and IFN-γ increased following MDT therapy.[17]

In leprosy patients, serum levels of several inflammatory cytokines correlate, reflecting that the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α by the infected host immune cells is counterbalanced by anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and IFN-γ. Previous studies have shown that NOD1 and NOD2 have synergistic or additive effects with TLRs concerning the production of cytokines and chemokines in immune cells and mesothelial cells. NOD1 and NOD2 agonists in combination with TLR agonists synergistically enhanced β-defensin 2 secretions in many cell types. Likewise, in this study, NOD1 and NOD2 agonists (LPS and MDP) synergised with TLR agonists (LPS or Pam3CysSerLys4-It is a synthetic triacylated lipopeptide (LP) and a TLR2/TLR1 ligand (Pam3CSK4)) to induce the production of IL-8 and IL-6 in monocyte cells.[181920]

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has demonstrated the expression of both cytokines and chemokines in response to NLR activation in the skin of leprosy patients. Our study provides some evidence that overexpression of these receptors and subsequent activation of cytokines and chemokines may drive the disease towards a delayed-type hypersensitivity response to M. leprae antigen. Whether the expression of different cytokines and chemokines and their corresponding receptors may influence clinical manifestations of leprosy other than leucocyte recruitment to inflammatory sites requires further investigation.
