Association of systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) with clinical outcomes in hemodialysis patients: a single-center observational study
Authors: Xiaoyan Su, Jinze Rong, Jianhua Chen, Haowen Zhong, Linqing Chen, Aiqin Cao, Yanshan Liang, Yin Chen, Lianghong Yin, Shaohong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) carry a substantial burden of cardiovascular disease and mortality. Accessible prognostic biomarkers are needed to improve risk stratification. We examined the association between the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) and clinical outcomes, and compared SIRI with established inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), as well as conventional clinical parameters such as C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count, and serum albumin.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted in a cohort of 221 prevalent hemodialysis patients enrolled at Dongguan Tung Wah Hospital between January and July 2019. Patients were categorized into tertiles based on SIRI for baseline comparison. Associations between SIRI (modeled as a continuous variable) and the risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and restricted cubic spline analyses.ResultsDuring a median follow-up period of 28 months, a total of 30 MHD patients (13.6%) died from any cause, and 47 patients (21.3%) experienced cardiovascular events. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that higher SIRI levels were significantly associated with increased cumulative incidence of cardiovascular events and elevated all-cause mortality (both P < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment, higher SIRI values were independently associated with greater risks of cardiovascular events (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] per unit increase, 1.121; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.041–1.206; P = 0.003) and all-cause mortality (adjusted HR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.11–1.29; P < 0.001). In receiver operating characteristic analyses, SIRI exhibited the numerically highest AUC among the evaluated biomarkers for predicting cardiovascular events (AUC, 0.677) and all-cause mortality (AUC, 0.794). DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant differences between the AUC of SIRI and those of NLR, MLR, CRP, WBC, or albumin for cardiovascular events (all P > 0.05). For all-cause mortality, SIRI showed no statistically significant differences compared with NLR (P = 0.467), MLR (P = 0.941), and CRP (P = 0.573), but was significantly superior to WBC count (P < 0.001) and albumin (P = 0.012).ConclusionElevated SIRI is independently associated with adverse outcomes in maintenance hemodialysis patients. In direct comparison with NLR, MLR, CRP, WBC count, and albumin, SIRI demonstrated comparable predictive performance for both cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05190-8.Keywords: Hemodialysis, Inflammation, Cardiovascular diseases, Mortality, Systemic inflammatory response index

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasingly prevalent worldwide and represents a substantial and expanding burden on healthcare systems [1, 2]. Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), the most prevalent form of kidney replacement therapy (KRT) globally, continues to be a critical treatment option. Despite ongoing advancements in MHD technology and practices, the mortality rate among MHD patients remains alarmingly high [3]. Moreover, studies reveal that cardiovascular disease (CVD) accounts for the majority of deaths in MHD patients, representing the leading cause of mortality in 77% of 159 countries worldwide [4]. Therefore, the early identification and targeted management of high-risk patients within MHD populations could play a pivotal role in reducing mortality rates.

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a central feature of CKD pathophysiology and is independently associated with cardiovascular disease and increased mortality [5]. Early intervention targeting the micro-inflammatory state in CKD patients can effectively enhance their quality of life and delay renal function deterioration [6]. Therefore, identifying inflammation markers specifically related to CKD is crucial for enhancing its diagnosis and treatment [7, 8]. Current evidence suggests that elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels are independently associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in MHD patients [9]. However, its routine use is limited in resource-limited settings, highlighting the need for more accessible markers derived from complete blood counts.

Among these, the SIRI has attracted significant attention due to its reliance on routine blood tests and high clinical applicability [10–12]. Recent studies have demonstrated that SIRI is independently associated with all-cause mortality in large maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) cohorts and is associated with mortality in patients with diabetes [13, 14]. Building on this, Shen et al. reported that SIRI was independently associated with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, and suggested that its discriminative performance might exceed that of CRP [15]. Furthermore, inflammatory indicators derived from routine blood tests, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), demonstrate reliable prognostic value for MHD patients [16, 17]. Accordingly, this study was specifically designed to benchmark the predictive performance of SIRI against both emerging inflammatory indices (NLR, MLR) and conventional clinical markers (CRP, WBC count, and albumin) for cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in MHD patients. While the independent prognostic value of SIRI in this population has been reported by recent independent cohorts, a systematic head-to-head comparison across such a broad panel of readily available biomarkers remains limited.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study enrollment period was from January 1 to July 1, 2019, with follow-up conducted through December 31, 2021. A total of 251 individuals with end-stage renal disease received maintenance hemodialysis at Dongguan Tung Wah Hospital over the study interval. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients younger than 18 years; (2) patients who had undergone maintenance hemodialysis for fewer than 3 months; (3) patients with a history of peritoneal dialysis or kidney transplantation; (4) patients with malignant tumors, autoimmune diseases, severe active infections, or hematological disorders; (5) patients who had received immunosuppressants, massive blood transfusions, or blood products within the preceding 6 months. Following eligibility screening, 221 participants remained for analysis. The study enrollment process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Patient selection and follow-up. All 221 patients were included in the analysis. During follow-up, 46 patients were censored (transfer to another center [n = 36], kidney transplantation [n = 9], or switch to peritoneal dialysis [n = 1]). A total of 175 patients reached a primary endpoint (death or cardiovascular event) or the study termination

Data collection

This study reviewed the electronic medical records of patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) between January 1 and July 1, 2019, and collected their baseline demographic and biochemical data. All baseline measurements were obtained pre-dialysis, encompassing demographic characteristics, comorbidities, medication use, dialysis parameters, and laboratory values.

Demographic data included each patient’s age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, and dialysis vintage. We assessed the presence of key comorbidities, specifically cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Details of the dialysis procedure were also captured, including the spKt/V (single-pool Kt/V), the volume of ultrafiltration, pre-dialysis systolic blood pressure, the type of vascular access employed, and the dialysis membrane type (polyethersulfone or cellulose acetate). A comprehensive laboratory evaluation was performed for all participants. This involved a complete blood count to quantify white blood cells, including neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and platelets, as well as hemoglobin and the red blood cell distribution width. Furthermore, we analyzed serum to determine the concentrations of blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, uric acid, albumin, phosphorus, calcium, parathyroid hormone, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), iron, ferritin, and C-reactive protein (CRP).

The systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) was defined as the product of the neutrophil and monocyte counts divided by the lymphocyte count. In parallel, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) were calculated directly from the corresponding absolute leukocyte counts. Corrected serum calcium (mmol/L) was computed to account for albumin concentration according to the following equation: measured serum calcium (mmol/L) + [40 − serum albumin (g/L)] × 0.02.

Diabetes mellitus was identified in accordance with the 1999 diagnostic criteria established by the World Health Organization. Hypertension was considered present when blood pressure reached or exceeded 140/90 mmHg on at least three separate, nonconsecutive occasions, or when antihypertensive therapy was being administered. CVD was treated as a composite outcome encompassing acute myocardial infarction, acute decompensated heart failure, unstable angina, ischemic cardiomyopathy, Adams–Stokes syndrome, clinically significant arrhythmias, intracerebral hemorrhage, and cerebral infarction.

Outcomes

All-cause mortality was designated as the primary outcome, while cardiovascular events served as the secondary outcome. Participants were followed until the occurrence of an endpoint or were censored at the time of transfer to another medical facility, conversion to peritoneal dialysis (PD), receipt of kidney transplantation, or at the predefined study end date (December 31, 2021).

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into three categories according to SIRI tertiles: T1 (low SIRI, < 1.12), T2 (intermediate SIRI, 1.12–2.01), and T3 (high SIRI, > 2.01). As no clinically validated thresholds for SIRI in hemodialysis populations have been established, tertiles were used as a data-driven exploratory approach for baseline comparison and visualization; the primary prognostic analyses relied on SIRI as a continuous variable. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables or as median with interquartile range for skewed distributions. Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages. Comparisons across the three groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for variables with normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test for variables with non-normal distribution, and the chi-square test for categorical data.

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations between SIRI and relevant clinical variables. To explore potential nonlinear associations between SIRI and the risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events in MHD patients, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were constructed. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were used to estimate the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events across the SIRI groups, with differences evaluated using the log-rank test. The median duration of follow-up and corresponding interquartile range (IQR) were determined using the reverse Kaplan–Meier approach.

SIRI was analyzed as a continuous variable. Its association with the outcomes was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests. No significant violations were observed for the final multivariable models (all global P > 0.05). To address potential confounding, sensitivity analyses were conducted. In line with the EPV ≥ 10 criterion to prevent overfitting, we limited the number of covariates in the models. Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF). Variables with VIF values exceeding 10 were considered to exhibit significant collinearity and were therefore removed from subsequent analyses. Covariate selection was ultimately based on clinical relevance.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted among the 175 patients who remained under follow-up at our center to assess the prognostic performance of SIRI, CRP, WBC, albumin, MLR, and NLR for predicting cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. The remaining 46 patients were censored due to transfer to other centers (n = 36), kidney transplantation (n = 9), or conversion to peritoneal dialysis (n = 1) and were excluded from the ROC analysis because their outcomes after censoring could not be reliably ascertained from our institutional electronic medical records. To ensure consistent directionality with risk-related biomarkers and allow valid comparison of AUC values, albumin levels were multiplied by − 1 before analysis. To formally evaluate differences in discriminative performance among the biomarkers, pairwise comparisons of the corresponding AUCs were conducted using DeLong’s test. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) and R (version 4.4.1). Two-tailed tests were applied throughout, and P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline clinical characteristics among the low, intermediate, and high SIRI groups

A total of 221 MHD patients were included in this study. During the follow-up period, 46 patients were censored due to kidney transplantation (n = 9), transition to peritoneal dialysis (n = 1), or transfer to other medical centers (n = 36). The remaining 175 patients were followed up until the occurrence of a primary endpoint event—defined as death or a cardiovascular event—or until the predetermined study termination date of December 31, 2021, whichever occurred first (Fig. 1). The mean age of the overall cohort was 56.3 ± 14.8 years, and 141 patients (63.8%) were male. The median follow-up duration was 28 months, during which 30 all-cause deaths (13.6%) and 47 major cardiovascular events (21.3%) were recorded. Patients were stratified into three groups according to tertiles of the SIRI: low (T1, < 1.12), intermediate (T2, 1.12–2.01), and high (T3, > 2.01), as detailed in Table 1. The proportion of smokers increased progressively from the low to high SIRI group (P < 0.001). Inflammatory indicators—including neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts; the NLR; the MLR; and C-reactive protein levels—demonstrated a progressive and statistically significant increase across higher SIRI tertiles (all P < 0.05). In contrast, lymphocyte counts showed a significant decline (P = 0.020). Elevated SIRI levels were also associated with lower serum iron concentrations (P < 0.001) and higher total cholesterol levels (P = 0.035). Dialysis vintage differed significantly among the three groups (P = 0.011), with patients in the intermediate SIRI tertile exhibiting the shortest treatment duration (Table 1).

Correlation analysis between SIRI and clinical indicators

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed that SIRI was positively correlated with MLR (ρ = 0.829, P < 0.001), NLR (ρ = 0.701, P < 0.001), monocyte count (ρ = 0.691, P < 0.001), neutrophil count (ρ = 0.660, P < 0.001), white blood cell count (ρ = 0.602, P < 0.001), CRP (ρ = 0.264, P < 0.001), platelet count (ρ = 0.318, P < 0.001), RDW (ρ = 0.172, P = 0.010), and LDL cholesterol (ρ = 0.155, P = 0.021). Conversely, SIRI showed significant inverse correlations with lymphocyte count (ρ = −0.217, P = 0.001), serum iron (ρ = −0.343, P < 0.001), and albumin (ρ = −0.156, P = 0.020) (Table 2).

Association between SIRI and cardiovascular events

During the follow-up period, cardiovascular events occurred in 47 patients (21.3%), including 7 cases (14.9%) of acute myocardial infarction, 1 case (2.1%) of unstable angina, 16 cases (34.0%) of acute heart failure, 2 cases (4.3%) of ischemic cardiomyopathy, 13 cases (27.7%) of acute cerebral hemorrhage, and 8 cases (17.0%) of cerebral infarction. Over 439.0 person-years of follow-up, the overall incidence of cardiovascular events was 10.7 per 100 person-years. Using the reverse Kaplan–Meier approach, the median follow-up time was calculated to be 36.0 months (IQR: 18.0–36.0 months). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significantly higher cumulative incidence of cardiovascular events in the T3 group compared to the lower tertiles (log-rank test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Kaplan-Meier curves were used to analyze the study endpoints in patients with different systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) levels: (a) cardiovascular events and (b) all-cause mortality (n = 221)

In the crude Cox proportional hazards analysis, each one-unit increment in SIRI corresponded to a 12.9% increase in the risk of cardiovascular events (HR: 1.129; 95% CI: 1.054–1.209; P < 0.001). This relationship persisted after adjusting for age and sex (Model 1: HR: 1.117; 95% CI: 1.041–1.198; P = 0.002) and remained statistically significant following additional adjustment for diabetes status and serum albumin (Model 2: HR: 1.121; 95% CI: 1.041–1.206; P = 0.003) (Table 3). Notably, the magnitude of association was consistent across adjustment levels, suggesting robustness of the SIRI-cardiovascular risk relationship. To assess potential nonlinearities, we performed restricted cubic spline analysis adjusted for Model 2 covariates. This revealed a significant nonlinear relationship (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinearity = 0.003; Fig. 2A), with risk rising sharply at lower SIRI values before plateauing. Notably, the confidence intervals widened progressively at higher SIRI values, reflecting reduced precision in this range due to the smaller number of observations at extreme SIRI levels.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the association between SIRI and cardiovascular events across various multivariable-adjusted models. After sequential adjustment for clinically relevant variables—including dialysis vintage and vascular access, traditional cardiovascular risk factors (BMI, smoking, pre-dialysis SBP), CVD history, laboratory parameters (hemoglobin, Ca×P product), and concomitant medication use (statins, RAASi)—the hazard ratios for SIRI remained statistically significant, ranging from 1.108 to 1.128 (all P < 0.05; Table S1).

Association between SIRI and all-cause mortality

During the follow-up period, 30 of the 221 patients died (13.6%). The causes of death were categorized as follows: 11 deaths (36.67%) attributed to cardiovascular events, 8 deaths (26.67%) caused by sepsis, 4 deaths (13.33%) resulting from multiple organ failure, and 7 deaths (23.33%) attributed to unknown factors. During a total of 439.0 person-years of follow-up, the overall mortality incidence was 6.8 per 100 person-years. The median follow-up time, calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier approach, was 36.0 months (IQR: 14.0–36.0 months). Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated significantly reduced cumulative survival in the T3 group compared with the lower tertiles (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3).

In the unadjusted Cox proportional hazards model, each one-unit increment in SIRI was associated with an 18.1% increase in the risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.181; 95% CI: 1.107–1.261; P < 0.001). Given the limited number of death cases (n = 30), we constructed a simple Cox model (Model 3), which adjusted for the three pre-selected key clinical confounding factors: age, serum albumin, and CVD history. In the parsimonious model, the relationship between SIRI and all-cause mortality persisted and remained statistically significant (HR: 1.177; 95% CI: 1.096–1.265; P < 0.001; Table 3). Notably, the significance of this association was maintained across all sensitivity analyses (all P < 0.001; Table S2). We further evaluated this relationship using a restricted cubic spline model adjusted for the covariates included in Model 3. The findings indicated a statistically significant nonlinear association (P for overall effect < 0.001; P for nonlinearity < 0.001; Fig. 2B). Specifically, the risk of mortality rose steeply at lower SIRI levels before reaching a plateau. The confidence intervals widened markedly at higher SIRI values, indicating reduced estimation precision in the upper range.

The predictive performance of SIRI

ROC curve analysis was conducted to compare the predictive performance of different biomarkers by examining their respective AUCs (Fig. 4; Table 4). SIRI exhibited the numerically highest AUC among the evaluated biomarkers for both cardiovascular events (AUC, 0.677) and all-cause mortality (AUC, 0.794). For cardiovascular events, DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant differences between the AUC of SIRI and those of NLR (P = 0.087), MLR (P = 0.096), CRP (P = 0.186), WBC (P = 0.577), or albumin (P = 0.205). For all-cause mortality, SIRI showed no statistically significant differences compared with NLR (P = 0.467), MLR (P = 0.941), or CRP (P = 0.573), but was significantly superior to WBC count (P < 0.001) and albumin (P = 0.012).

Discussion

Among individuals with CKD, inflammatory processes (increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and CRP) are pivotal in determining mortality outcomes, being closely associated with the occurrence of cardiovascular events and contributing to increased mortality rates among patients undergoing MHD [18–20]. As an accessible and cost-effective composite inflammatory marker, the SIRI has garnered increasing attention, particularly in clinical settings with limited resources or where routine measurement of CRP is not feasible [21, 22]. The prognostic value of SIRI in MHD populations has been previously established by several independent groups, including Zhu et al. in a large hemodialysis cohort [13], Yan and Li in diabetic MHD patients [14], and Shen et al. in patients evaluated for both mortality and cardiovascular events [15]. Our findings are broadly consistent with this emerging evidence. Rather than re-establishing the SIRI–outcome association per se, the present study adds value by offering a direct, within-cohort comparison of the discriminative performance of SIRI against NLR, MLR, CRP, WBC count, and albumin using receiver operating characteristic analyses with formal pairwise comparisons (DeLong’s test). While prior reports established the independent prognostic utility of SIRI, these comparative findings may inform the selection of the most accessible inflammatory marker for risk stratification in routine clinical practice.

Analysis of SIRI levels across the low, intermediate, and high groups revealed that patients with a history of smoking tended to exhibit higher SIRI values. This observation may be attributed to the effects of smoking on immune effector cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, and T lymphocytes, which can induce immune dysregulation and subsequently lead to elevated SIRI levels [23]. We note that smoking was adjusted for in sensitivity analyses, and the SIRI–cardiovascular event association remained significant. While smoking may contribute to the inflammatory gradient, it does not fully account for the independent prognostic association of SIRI. Residual confounding from smoking intensity or duration—data not captured—cannot be entirely excluded. Notably, patients with higher SIRI exhibited significantly lower serum iron levels. Previous studies have consistently documented marked decreases in serum ferritin among MHD patients, with hypoferremia strongly associated with systemic inflammation and serving as an independent prognostic indicator for adverse outcomes in this population [24–26], findings that are consistent with our study data. Platelets, as ubiquitous cellular elements of blood, function as robust inflammatory biomarkers due to their involvement in cellular immune responses. Elevated platelet counts have been established as predictive of increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality risk in MHD patients [27, 28], results that align with our observed findings. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between SIRI levels and RDW. Numerous studies have demonstrated that RDW, as a biomarker associated with elevated systemic inflammation, is correlated with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events [29, 30]. Furthermore, this study observed a progressive increase in TC and LDL-C levels with higher SIRI values. Notably, in dialysis patients, higher TC levels are frequently associated with reduced mortality [31]. This paradoxical observation may be attributed to confounding factors such as systemic inflammation or malnutrition. Collectively, these associations suggest that SIRI, as an integrated index, may provide a more comprehensive assessment of elevated cardiovascular and mortality risk in patients.

A large-scale investigation involving heterogeneous demographic populations in the United States reported that elevated SIRI independently predicts both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality among patients with CKD [32]. Moreover, its prognostic utility has been documented across a wide range of clinical contexts, including oncology and latent tuberculosis infection [33–35]. Mechanistically, the SIRI formula (neutrophils × monocytes / lymphocytes) encapsulates the balance between pro-inflammatory drivers (neutrophils and monocytes) and immunoregulatory components (lymphocytes) [36–40]. A high SIRI thus signifies a state of heightened inflammatory burden coupled with relative immune suppression or dysregulation, which represents a profile strongly linked to atherosclerosis, oxidative stress, and poor clinical outcomes. This biological plausibility strengthens the case for SIRI as a clinically informative biomarker.

Notably, although SIRI exhibited the highest AUC among the evaluated biomarkers, the absence of statistically significant differences in DeLong testing indicates that its discriminative performance should be regarded as comparable to, rather than superior to, that of NLR, MLR, CRP, and the other biomarkers evaluated. The AUC for cardiovascular events was 0.677, indicating, at best, modest discriminatory ability, whereas the AUC for all-cause mortality was 0.794, approaching an acceptable level of discrimination. Collectively, these values suggest that SIRI alone is insufficient for individual risk prediction and should not be used as a standalone decision-making tool. In routine clinical practice, SIRI should be considered as one component of a broader, multimodal risk assessment strategy that integrates established clinical factors—such as age, comorbidity burden, dialysis vintage, and albumin levels—rather than as an independent prognostic instrument. Given that SIRI is derived from routine complete blood counts and incurs no additional cost, its role is not to replace clinical judgment, but rather to serve as an easily accessible screening marker that complements existing risk assessments. For example, an elevated SIRI value might prompt closer monitoring or more aggressive management of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, whereas a low SIRI value should not be interpreted as indicating low risk in isolation. Ultimately, clinical decisions should continue to be guided by comprehensive evaluation rather than by any single biomarker.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the single-center design may restrict external validity. Dialysis practice patterns, vascular access distribution, baseline inflammation burden, and comorbidity profiles in our Chinese cohort may differ substantially from international populations, potentially affecting both SIRI distributions and the prognostic performance of SIRI. Consequently, the SIRI tertile cutoffs used in this study (T1, < 1.12; T2, 1.12–2.01; T3, > 2.01), which were derived in a data-driven manner from this specific cohort, should not be regarded as clinically validated thresholds and are unlikely to be directly applicable outside this Chinese population. Future multicenter studies across diverse ethnic and geographic settings are needed to establish externally validated, clinically meaningful SIRI thresholds for risk stratification. Validation in multicenter, ethnically diverse cohorts with varying dialysis practices is warranted. Second, the limited number of all-cause death events (n = 30) warrants careful consideration regarding the stability of hazard ratio estimates. Although we applied parsimonious Cox models and adhered to the EPV ≥ 10 criterion to minimize overfitting, the modest event count may still affect the precision of point estimates, as reflected in the relatively wide confidence intervals. While the SIRI–mortality association remained statistically significant and robust across all sensitivity analyses, these HR estimates should be interpreted as indicative of the direction and approximate magnitude of association rather than as precisely quantified effect sizes. Prospective validation in larger cohorts with a greater number of events is warranted to confirm the stability and generalizability of these estimates. Third, the restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated progressively widening confidence intervals at higher SIRI values, particularly for all-cause mortality. This pattern indicates reduced estimation precision in the upper range of SIRI, likely attributable to the smaller sample size at extreme values. Consequently, while the overall nonlinear trend is supported, the specific hazard ratio estimates at high SIRI levels should be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, the ROC curve analysis was confined to 175 patients with complete follow-up data, excluding 46 censored patients (kidney transplantation, n = 9; transfer to another center, n = 36; conversion to peritoneal dialysis, n = 1). Transfers to other centers in maintenance hemodialysis populations typically reflect changes in residential location or other non-clinical factors rather than clinical deterioration; thus, the censoring mechanism was likely administrative in nature. The primary prognostic analyses—Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier survival curves—were performed in the full cohort using survival analysis methods that appropriately handle censored data, and the principal conclusion regarding the prognostic value of SIRI is unlikely to be substantially influenced by this analytical choice. Fifth, SIRI was assessed only at a single time point (baseline). As a measurement derived from routine clinical care, this baseline value may be influenced by short-term fluctuations in a patient’s inflammatory state. Inflammatory biomarkers in maintenance hemodialysis patients exhibit substantial temporal variability due to intercurrent infections, dialysis-related stimuli, and other clinical events. A single baseline measurement may therefore fail to capture an individual’s true inflammatory burden and could introduce regression dilution bias, potentially underestimating the true strength of association between SIRI and adverse outcomes. Sixth, our assessment of dialysis adequacy relied solely on spKt/V, which does not reflect the clearance of protein-bound compounds and middle molecules. Seventh, the composite cardiovascular endpoint included events with heterogeneous mechanisms. Owing to the limited number of events within each subtype, we could not perform component-specific analyses to assess potential heterogeneity in the predictive performance of SIRI. Collectively, these considerations underscore the need for future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating serial SIRI measurements to independently validate and extend the current findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, elevated SIRI is independently associated with adverse outcomes in maintenance hemodialysis patients. In direct comparison with NLR, MLR, CRP, WBC count, and albumin, SIRI demonstrated comparable predictive performance for both cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. These findings suggest that SIRI may serve as an accessible alternative for risk stratification in routine clinical practice.
