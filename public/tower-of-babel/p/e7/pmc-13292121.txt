Comparing and optimizing protein extraction methods with different lysis buffers for the analyses of human fecal microbiome via metaproteomics approach
Authors: Shuaixin Gao, Li Chen, Huan Zhang, Amrik Singh Khalsa, Michael T Bailey, Brett R Loman, Colleen K Spees, Jiangjiang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetaproteomic analysis offers critical insights into gut microbiome function; however, efficient microbial protein extraction from fecal samples remains challenging due to the complexity of different types of bacterial cell walls in the microbiome. In this study, we systematically compared three representative detergent-based lysis buffers (sodium dodecyl sulfate_urea, dodecyl β-D-maltoside_urea, sodium dodecyl sulfate_ dodecyl β-D-maltoside_urea) for metaproteomics sample preparation. After multiple levels of analyses, we identified SDS_DDM_urea as the most efficient option for extracting diverse microbial proteins, peptides, and identifying microbial species. Applying this optimized method to samples from a community based dietary intervention study (Summer Harvest Adventure), we found minimal group-level microbial diversity shifts during this type of intervention, but substantial individual-specific variations reflected by metaproteomics results. Functional analyses also revealed microbial protein changes, especially proteins related to metabolic adaptations, including enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, vitamin transport, and increased expression of membrane-associated proteins. Our results highlighted the personalized microbiome response to dietary interventions and underscored the importance of selecting appropriate protein extraction methods to accurately capture microbiome functional dynamics in microbiome analyses via metaproteomics.Keywords: Metaproteomics, Gut microbiota, Protein extraction, Dietary intervention, Microbial diversity, Lysis buffers, Microbial functional analysis

Introduction

The close relationship between the human gut microbiota and human health and diseases has been increasingly garnering widespread attention in clinical research. A growing body of evidence suggests that the gut microbiota plays a significant role in maintaining human health and contributes to the development of various diseases, including diabetes(Iatcu et al., 2021), gastrointestinal disorders(Round et al., 2009) and Alzheimer’s disease(Jiang et al., 2017). Metagenomics(Thomas et al., 2019), metatranscriptomics(Jiang et al., 2016) and metabolomics (Visconti et al., 2019) technologies have been widely applied in the study of the gut microbiota, enabling scientists to delve into the structure and functionality of the microbial community in the intestines. Recently, metaproteomics technology has also been gradually introduced into microbiome research, which can assist the analyses of the composition and function of proteins within the microbial community. Through this technique, researchers can unveil the specific roles of microorganisms in metabolism, immunity, and other physiological processes. This provides a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying diseases and interventions, and offers a new perspective on our exploration of the gut microbiota(Zhang et al., 2017). However, due to the intricate sample preparation and challenging data analysis, metaproteomics faces difficulties in achieving large-scale clinical applications, unlike some other omics approaches.

The extraction of proteins from bacteria presents unique challenges due to the structure of bacterial cell walls, which are typically more complex and rigid compared to eukaryotic cells(Zhang et al., 2018). This structural complexity significantly complicates the process of disrupting bacterial cells to efficiently release intracellular proteins, particularly when dealing with the diverse microbial community in the human gut. The difficulty of bacterial lysis and subsequent protein extraction has been a central challenge in gut microbiota studies, where understanding protein composition and function is crucial.

In proteomic research, surfactants are widely used to enhance the efficiency of protein extraction and digestion. Among them, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) has been frequently tested in various combinations to improve protein extraction from bacterial cells(Zhang et al., 2018). SDS is effective for cell lysis due to its strong denaturing properties. Researchers have also explored combinations involving other surfactants such as urea and different mechanical lysis techniques to enhance efficiency. For example, SDS combined with bead grinding has been reported to improve the yield of bacterial proteins from gut samples, emphasizing the effectiveness of surfactant combinations in overcoming the challenges of bacterial cell wall lysis(Baniasad et al., 2022). In addition to SDS, dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM) has attracted attention as a mild nonionic detergent that effectively solubilizes membrane proteins while maintaining enzymatic activity and compatibility with LC–MS/MS analysis. Liu demonstrated that a lysis buffer containing DDM and urea achieved protein extraction efficiency comparable to strong detergents such as SDS, but without the need for detergent removal steps, thereby minimizing protein loss and improving sensitivity in glycoproteomic analysis(Liu et al., 2015). Dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM) has been widely used in conventional proteomics due to its ability to effectively solubilize membrane proteins. Numerous studies have demonstrated the significant role of DDM in improving protein extraction efficiency(Zhang, 2015) and maintaining protein integrity during sample preparation(Nie et al., 2022), making it a valuable reagent in proteomic research. Despite its success in conventional proteomics, its potential for extracting proteins from the gut microbiota has not yet been explored. Despite its success in conventional proteomics, its potential for extracting proteins from the gut microbiota has not yet been systematically evaluated, providing the rationale for its investigation in this study.

Given the challenges of extracting proteins from the complex microbial community in the gut, it is worthwhile to investigate whether DDM could address these specific challenges. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of DDM in combination with other commonly used detergents for extracting proteins from the gut microbiota. We compared three lysis buffers, each containing different combinations of detergents and denaturants—SDS_urea, DDM_urea, and SDS_DDM_urea—to determine their efficiency in extracting proteins from fecal microbiota. The S-Trap method was used for sample cleanup, and subsequent protein identification was conducted using mass spectrometry. Each extraction protocol was tested for reproducibility by applying it to replicate fecal samples and analyzing the protein yield and composition. Our study then applied the optimized extraction method to investigate the impact of dietary interventions on the gut microbiota. We used samples from the Summer Harvest Adventure (SHA) study, a community-based cardiovascular disease prevention program that provided a 10-week randomized, controlled, and multifaceted lifestyle intervention to underserved participants(Hill et al., 2022). The intervention included nutrition and physical activity education, produce harvesting, cooking demonstrations, nutrition counseling, and exercise sessions. Fecal samples were collected before and after the intervention, and microbial extractions were performed to assess the intervention’s impact on gut microbiome composition and associated functions. This enabled us to assess how dietary changes influenced the composition of the gut microbiota at the protein level, providing insights into the physiological impact of diet on the gut microbiome and chronic disease outcomes.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents and supplies

The anionic surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), urea, and n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP), trypsin, and iodoacetamide (IAA) were obtained from Promega Corporation (Madison, WI, USA). Acetonitrile (ACN) was acquired from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, MA, USA), while acetic acid for pH adjustment was supplied by Fisher Scientific (Hampton, NH, USA). The same vendor provided essential laboratory equipment for bacterial protein extraction workflows, including benchtop centrifugation systems and corresponding conical tubes designed for high-speed pelleting applications.

Sample preparation

In this study, we analyzed thirteen samples collected from seven volunteers. The participants had a mean age of 25 years (±10), with a male-to-female distribution of 4:3, and an average BMI of 30 (±6). The optimized method was then applied to investigate the impact of dietary quality improvements on the composition of the gut microbiota, using human fecal samples obtained from the Summer Harvest Adventure (SHA) study involved a community garden-based intervention targeting children and their parents in low-resource neighborhoods(Hill et al., 2022; Khalsa et al., 2022). These samples were crucial for analyzing the impact of diet on the gut microbiome. The original study protocol facilitated effective collection and processing of these samples at a remote location, providing valuable insights into diet-related physiological changes and chronic disease outcomes. The study protocol was approved by The Ohio State University Institutional Review Board, adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised 1983), and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT05367674). Participants for this study were recruited from low-access, low-resource communities within Columbus, Ohio. These individuals were enrolled in a cardiovascular disease prevention initiative lasting ten weeks, during which stool samples were collected at two points: at baseline and immediately after the intervention. The detailed study description can be found in our prior publications(Hill et al., 2022).

To optimize the method, 2 g of fresh feces were weighed and placed into a sterile 15 mL centrifuge tube. Then, 10 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to the tube, and the fecal sample was thoroughly resuspended. After mixing, the samples underwent centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was carefully removed and saved. The remaining pellet underwent two additional rounds of resuspension with cold PBS, each followed by centrifugation under identical conditions. All collected supernatants from these steps, totaling about 30 mL, were combined and centrifuged three additional times at low speed (300 g, 5 min, 4 °C) to eliminate any remaining large debris or particles. Subsequently, the cleaned supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and centrifuged at high speed (14,000 g, 20 min, 4 °C) to pellet the microbial cells, discarding the supernatant afterward. This high-speed washing step using cold PBS was repeated twice to further purify microbial cells(Cheng et al., 2023)(). Finally, the resulting microbial pellet was divided evenly into nine aliquots, each containing about 30 mg wet weight, to perform protein extraction. The protein extraction experiments were independently conducted three times to confirm the reproducibility of different lysis buffer treatments detailed subsequently.

Protein extraction

Three different lysis buffers were prepared to optimize protein extraction, each with a unique composition as follows: (1). SDS_urea Buffer: Contained 4 % SDS (w/v), 50 mM Tris-HCl, and 2 M urea, adjusted to pH 8.0; (2). DDM_urea Buffer: Consisted of 0.2 % DDM (w/v), 50 mM Tris-HCl, and 2 M urea, set to pH 8.0; (3). Mix (SDS_DDM_urea) Buffer: Combined 4 % SDS (w/v), 0.2 % DDM (w/v), 50 mM Tris-HCl, and 2 M urea, with a pH of 8.0.

Each buffer was prepared fresh and used on the same day to ensure optimal activity and stability of the components. Protease inhibitors were freshly added to each of the three lysis buffers immediately before use. Subsequently, the lysates were transferred into 2 mL bead-beating tubes containing approximately 10 zirconium beads (0.15 mm diameter, BioSpec Products Inc.). Cell disruption was carried out using a FastPrep-36 homogenizer (MP Biomedicals Inc., USA) at 6 m/s for a total duration of 200 s, with brief pauses of 1 min on ice after every 45 s to prevent overheating. After mechanical lysis, the samples were centrifuged at high speed (16,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C) to eliminate beads and cellular debris)(Baniasad et al., 2022). Finally, the clear supernatants obtained were carefully transferred into new tubes for subsequent protein precipitation and quantification via the BCA assay.

Trypsin digestion

Protein digestion was performed using the S-Trap™ micro spin column (Protifi, Huntington, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 100 μg protein samples were reduced by incubation with 20 mM TCEP at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by alkylation using 40 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min protected from light. Subsequently, the alkylated samples were acidified with phosphoric acid to reach a final concentration of 1.2 %, after which six volumes of binding buffer (90 % methanol and 100 mM TEAB at pH 7.1) were added. Following gentle mixing, the protein solutions were loaded onto filters and centrifuged briefly at 4000 ×g for 30 s. The retained proteins were then washed two to three times with a solution comprising 90 % methanol with 10 % 50 mM TEAB, then digested with trypsin at 47 °C for 1 h, maintaining a protein and enzyme ratio of 10:1 (w/w).

After digestion, peptides were sequentially eluted twice using three distinct buffers (200 μL per elution), elution 1: 50 mM TEAB in water, elution 2: 0.2 % formic acid in water, and elution 3: 50 % acetonitrile containing 0.2 % formic acid. All eluted peptide fractions were pooled together, purified using a C18 desalting column, dried using a speed vacuum concentrator, and finally reconstituted in 50 μL of 0.1 % formic acid aqueous solution prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

All method optimization samples were analyzed using an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer from Thermo Fisher (San Jose, CA, USA). Peptides were initially separated via a Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 RSLC nano-liquid chromatography (nanoLC) system operating in trapelute mode, before being introduced into the Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer. Mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.1 % formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1 % formic acid in 80 % acetonitrile). Peptides were loaded onto a trapping column (Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 C18, dimensions: 75 μm × 2 cm, particle size: 3 μm, pore size: 100 Å; Thermo Scientific) for 3 min at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. Subsequently, the peptides were eluted onto an analytical column (Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 C18, dimensions: 75 μm × 25 cm, particle size: 2 μm, pore size: 100 Å; Thermo Scientific) at a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min. The gradient for elution started with solvent B increasing from 3 % to 30 % over 104 min, followed by a steep increase to 90 % solvent B within the next 10 min. A wash step of 6 min at 90 % solvent B and a re-equilibration period of 10 min completed the chromatographic procedure. Mass spectra were acquired in positive ion mode, covering an m/z range from 350 to 2000, with a resolution setting of 60,000 (measured at m/z 200). The automatic gain control (AGC) was targeted at 4e5, and maximum injection time was set at 50 ms. Data-dependent MS/MS (dd-MS2) acquisition was configured using a top-speed 3 s cycle, and previously analyzed ions were excluded dynamically for 30 s.

After extraction method optimization, to further increase the detection sensitivity, all SHA samples were analyzed utilizing a Bruker timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA), coupled with a nanoElute liquid chromatography system from the same manufacturer. Approximately 200 ng of peptide digest from each sample was separated on a C18 column (IonOpticks, Australia; dimensions: 25 cm × 75 μm, particle size: 1.6 μm) over a 120-min gradient at a consistent flow rate of 300 nL/min. Mobile phases consisted of solvent A (water containing 0.1 % formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile containing 0.1 % formic acid). The gradient was programmed as follows: solvent B was gradually increased from 2 % to 24 % over 104 min, then e increased to 72 % in the subsequent 10 min, and further raised to 81 % in the next 1 min, holding at this level for the final 5 min. Mass spectrometry was performed using the timsTOF Pro equipped with a CaptiveSpray nano-electrospray ionization source, operating in positive ion and data-dependent acquisition modes. Ion accumulation and ion mobility ramping times were each set at 100 ms. Mass spectral data were acquired across an m/z range of 100–1700, with ion mobility scans conducted from 0.6 to 1.6 Vs/cm2. The instrument operated with acquisition cycles of approximately 1.16 s, each composed of one full TIMS-MS scan followed by 10 parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF) MS/MS scans.

Metaproteomics data analysis

The obtained LC-MS/MS datasets were processed using the MetaLab-MAG software (desktop version 1.0.2)(Cheng et al., 2023; Duong et al., 2024). During data processing, cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, while protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were selected as variable modifications, allowing up to two missed cleavage sites. Protein and peptide identifications were performed with a precursor mass tolerance of ±20 ppm and a fragment mass tolerance of 0.5 Da. Both protein- and peptide-spectrum match (PSM)-level false discovery rates (FDRs) were controlled at 1 % (FDR = 0.01). For protein-level reporting, identifications were grouped into protein groups based on shared peptide evidence. Quantification was performed using the default methods implemented in the software, without additional manual adjustment. Database searches were conducted against version 2.0.1 of the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) database(Almeida et al., 2021). Taxonomic assignments of peptides were determined using the lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm in the Unipept Desktop software version 2.0.0 (Van Den Bossche et al., 2024; Verschaffelt et al., 2021), and only unique peptides with unambiguous taxonomic information were considered. The relative abundance of each taxon was calculated by summing the intensity values of assigned peptides and normalizing to the total peptide intensity per sample. Functional annotation of the identified proteins was performed using UniProtKB, KEGG Orthology (KO), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), and Gene Ontology (GO). Functional abundances were obtained by summing peptide/protein intensities mapped to each functional category and normalizing against the total peptide intensity, and the resulting taxonomic and functional profiles were compared across extraction methods and dietary intervention groups.

For subsequent statistical analyses, MetaX software (desktop version 1.109.5) (Wu et al., 2024) was employed. Prior to statistical testing, peptide and protein intensities were normalized to the total intensity per sample to correct for differences in overall signal. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test to evaluate normality. Differentially expressed proteins or microbial species were identified using a two-sided t-test. Proteins or species exhibiting a fold-change greater than two-fold (upregulated) or less than 0.5-fold (downregulated), with a statistical significance threshold of p-value <0.05, were considered significant. Multiple testing corrections were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to calculate adjusted p-values.

Results and discussion

Evaluating protein extraction methods using different lysis buffers

First, we systematically compared the protein extraction efficiency of three lysis buffers—SDS_urea, DDM_urea, and SDS_DDM_urea—using human fecal microbiota samples. Notably, SDS_DDM_urea yielded the highest protein recovery efficiency (1.1–1.3 %), whereas SDS_urea showed intermediate performance (1.1 %), and DDM_urea displayed the lowest efficiency (~0.9 %)(Table S1). Proteins and peptides identified via mass spectrometry among the three methods are shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, the total number of proteins identified using SDS_DDM_urea was highest (21,842 proteins), followed closely by SDS_urea (21,452 proteins), and DDM_urea (20,449 proteins) (Fig. 1A). In all samples, a total of 316 human proteins were identified, which are negligible compared with the microbial proteins and did not significantly affect the overall conclusions(Table S2). At the peptide level, SDS_DDM_urea identified the largest number of peptides (55,507), SDS_urea identified slightly fewer peptides (51,938), while DDM_urea had the lowest peptide identification (49,669) (Fig. 1B). As a comparison, Long et al. identified 9078 protein groups and 21,510 peptides from the healthy volunteers group, and 7761 protein groups and 18,368 peptides per sample from a colorectal cancer patient group(Long et al., 2020). Zhang et al. identified 19,011 protein groups in the human metaproteome(Zhang et al., 2016). Although DIA generally identifies a larger number of proteins compared to other acquisition methods, Long et al.—who used the same instrument and DIA workflow—reported fewer identifications than obtained in our study. This suggests that our sample preparation strategy was more efficient and contributed to deeper proteome coverage. Similarly, Zhang et al. employed a Q Exactive mass spectrometer, which is essentially the same platform as the Fusion used in our study, yet our results still surpassed theirs, further supporting the superiority of our methodological approach. The corresponding Venn diagrams reveal substantial overlaps and method-specific identifications among the three lysis buffers. At the protein level, a core of 17,523 proteins were commonly identified by all three methods. Additionally, SDS_DDM_urea uniquely identified 882 proteins, which was substantially higher than the numbers identified uniquely by SDS_urea (176 proteins) and DDM_urea (327 proteins) (Fig. 1C). At the peptide level, the overlap among methods was again significant, with a common core of 28,109 peptides identified by all three buffers. Nevertheless, SDS_DDM_urea exhibited a notable advantage by uniquely identifying 6811 peptides, far exceeding those uniquely identified by SDS_urea (1221 peptides) and DDM_urea (5292 peptides) (Fig. 1D).

Statistical significance is indicated as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****). (C-D) Venn diagrams depicting the overlap and unique proteins (C) and peptides (D) identified among the three methods, highlighting the extensive coverage provided by the SDS_DDM_urea method.

These results clearly indicate that SDS_DDM_urea provides superior extraction efficiency compared to the other tested methods, both at protein and peptide identification levels. The advantage of this combined detergent approach is evidenced by its ability to extract and identify a greater diversity of microbial proteins and peptides, including many unique components not detectable by either SDS_urea or DDM_urea alone. The higher number of uniquely identified proteins and peptides with SDS_DDM_urea highlights the importance of combining the strong denaturing properties of SDS with the membrane-solubilizing capabilities of DDM. This combination likely facilitates more effective lysis and solubilization of proteins from diverse bacterial populations, including those embedded in complex cell membranes or otherwise resistant to extraction by a single detergent. In comparison, SDS_urea alone showed a slightly lower overall extraction efficiency but still maintained considerable effectiveness, indicating its utility for general microbial protein extraction. The DDM_urea method, although capable of extracting a substantial number of peptides and proteins, had the lowest extraction efficiency overall, likely due to limited effectiveness in lysing certain types of bacterial cells or insufficient solubilization of specific intracellular proteins. These findings highlight the practical benefits of employing mixed-detergent strategies, such as SDS_DDM_urea, for comprehensive and efficient protein extraction in gut microbiome metaproteomic analyses.

Comparison of microbial species detected

We further investigated the taxonomic profiles of microbial communities identified by the three different lysis buffers (SDS_urea, DDM_urea, and SDS_DDM_urea). The number of microbial taxa detected at both the phylum and species levels was assessed, and the results are summarized in Fig. 2. At the phylum level, the SDS_DDM_urea buffer identified the greatest number of microbial phyla (16 phyla), slightly exceeding those identified by the SDS_urea (15 phyla) and DDM_urea (14 phyla) methods. At the species level, however, a clearer distinction emerged: SDS_DDM_urea buffer detected 312 microbial species, while the SDS_urea and DDM_urea buffers identified fewer species—283 and 263 species, respectively (Fig. 2A). In comparison, Carmen et al. in a recent study identified 11 phyla and 106 species in healthy individuals (García-Durán et al., 2021). Venn diagram analysis at the species level revealed that 242 microbial species were common to all three extraction methods. Additionally, each method uniquely identified certain microbial species. Specifically, the SDS_DDM_urea method uniquely detected 29 species, while the SDS_urea and DDM_urea methods uniquely identified 5 and 27 species, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Taxonomic identification results of microbial communities using different lysis methods. (A) Bar graph illustrating the number of identified microbial taxa at phylum and species levels by SDS_urea, DDM_urea, and SDS_DDM_urea. (B) Venn diagram illustrating shared and unique microbial species identified by the three methods.

These results demonstrate that the SDS_DDM_urea buffer offers superior efficiency in revealing microbial diversity within human gut samples, particularly at the species level. Although all three buffers identified comparable numbers of microbial phyla, significant differences were evident when comparing species-level identifications, highlighting the importance of extraction method optimization to fully capture microbiome complexity. The substantial number of species uniquely identified by the SDS_DDM_urea buffer suggests that this method effectively captures microbial populations resistant to simpler detergent extractions. The enhanced capability of the SDS_DDM_urea buffer to solubilize membrane-bound and structurally complex microbial cells likely contributes to this broader microbial coverage, which is critical for comprehensive microbiome characterization. On the other hand, the DDM_urea method, while identifying fewer total species, showed a relatively high number of unique species, indicating it might preferentially target microbial taxa with specific cell wall structures or membrane compositions. Conversely, SDS_urea demonstrated moderate effectiveness but identified fewer unique species, suggesting its limited selectivity or inability to extract proteins from some microbial populations effectively. Collectively, these findings reinforce the significance of combining detergents with complementary extraction properties. The SDS_DDM_urea method emerges as an optimal approach to accurately reflect microbial diversity and capture a wider range of taxa, thereby ensuring more robust and insightful metaproteomic analyses. Hence, this method is used for our application study detailed below.

The implication of method optimization to functional readouts

Next, biological processes, cellular component, and enzymes analyses were conducted using Unipept, a bioinformatics platform designed to analyze metaproteomic data by assigning peptides to taxonomic and functional categories. Unipept enables detailed insights into the microbial functionality within complex communities by linking peptides to specific biological functions and cellular components. Analysis of the top 15 molecular functions of identified peptides indicated overall similarities among the three extraction methods; however, a noticeable variation was observed in the last functional category (Fig. 3). Specifically, the DDM_urea method uniquely captured a higher number of peptides associated with extracellular regions, indicating its effectiveness in solubilizing and extracting proteins localized to extracellular spaces or cell surfaces.

The top 15 molecular functions identified by the three lysis methods. Grouped bar chart showing peptide numbers across molecular functional categories. Error bars represent mean ± SEM. Statistical significance is indicated as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****).

Additional functional analysis revealed significant differences in the biological processes identified by the three extraction methods (Fig. S1). While the SDS_DDM_urea method again displayed the highest peptide numbers across most biological processes, significant divergence was observed between DDM_urea and SDS-based methods. Specifically, DDM_urea preferentially extracted proteins involved in distinct metabolic processes and response mechanisms, suggesting a unique selectivity attributed to the detergent DDM. In contrast, SDS_urea and SDS_DDM_urea methods exhibited similarity, emphasizing common biological processes related to cellular metabolism, stress response, and energy production. These findings highlight the detergent-specific biases in protein extraction methods and underscore the importance of carefully selecting extraction methods to accurately represent the microbial community’s functional landscape.

Further functional analysis also highlighted differences in extraction effectiveness among methods at the cellular component level (Fig. S3). The top five cellular components identified showed all three methods targeted similar primary cellular structures, but SDS_DDM_urea consistently identified a higher number of peptides for each component compared to SDS_urea and DDM_urea. Specifically, SDS_DDM_urea consistently extracted a higher quantity of peptides associated with essential cellular components such as ATP binding and ribosomal structural constituents, suggesting greater efficiency and a broader extraction spectrum. These findings clearly demonstrate the superior capacity of SDS_DDM_urea to comprehensively extract proteins related to fundamental cellular processes, reinforcing its importance for detailed metaproteomic analyses.

Finally, analysis of the top 15 enzymes identified (Fig. 4) revealed that SDS_DDM_urea consistently covered enzymes found by the other two methods, highlighting its enhanced efficiency in enzyme protein extraction. Interestingly, DDM_urea, while generally extracting fewer peptides compared to SDS_urea, uniquely identified specific enzymes associated with energy metabolism. This finding indicates a selective extraction capability of DDM_urea, likely due to its unique interaction with membrane proteins and metabolic enzymes, thus providing complementary insights into microbial metabolism. These results underscore the superior efficiency of SDS_DDM_urea in extracting proteins representative of major cellular components and enzymes, reinforcing its utility for comprehensive metaproteomic analyses.

Application of metaproteomics methods to assess the impact of community garden-based intervention on gut microbiota

Applying the method of our choice (SDS_DDM_urea) to our archived samples from a community garden-based intervention study, we evaluated changes in gut microbiota composition in six participants by comparing their microbiota before and after the intervention. Fecal samples collected before and after the intervention were analyzed using detailed metaproteomic analysis, with a focus on evaluating the impact of dietary interventions on microbial diversity, specific bacterial abundance, and individual-specific variations.

Changes in microbial composition between pre- and post-intervention samples are visualized in Fig. 5. The Sankey diagrams illustrate shifts in microbial community structure at the phylum level, with dominant phyla such as Bacteroidota and Bacillota exhibiting slight variations in their relative abundances post-intervention. The bar charts highlight a slight fluctuation in microbial diversity following the intervention, demonstrated by slightly fewer phyla and species detected in the post-intervention samples compared to pre-intervention (Fig. 5A). Specifically, the Venn diagram shows 88 species consistently identified both before and after intervention, while 31 species were exclusively identified pre-intervention and 15 species uniquely detected post-intervention (Fig. 5B).

Microbial composition analysis before and after dietary interventions. (A) Bar chart comparing the number of identified microbial phyla and species before and after dietary interventions, highlighting reduced microbial diversity following dietary changes. (B) Venn diagram displaying overlap and unique microbial species identified before and after dietary interventions, demonstrating core stability and intervention-specific microbiome adaptations.

Detailed analysis of uniquely identified species (Table S4) shows distinct microbial taxa specific to pre- or post-intervention periods. Species detected exclusively before intervention, such as Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, Blautia glucerasea, and Clostridium neonatale, may reflect microbial populations sensitive to dietary alterations. In contrast, species uniquely found post-intervention, including Eisenbergiella massiliensis, Parabacteroides johnsonii, and Roseburia porci, likely indicate taxa that benefit from the SHA intervention.

Detailed enzyme profiles before and after dietary intervention (Fig. 6) revealed distinct shifts in microbial metabolic functions. Core metabolic enzymes like beta-galactosidase, phosphopyruvate hydratase, beta-glucosidase, NADH peroxidase, and pyruvate kinase remained abundant across both periods, signifying stable core metabolic activities. Post-intervention, we observed increased abundance of glucose-6-phosphate isomerase, glucuronate isomerase, and glutamate dehydrogenase, potentially reflecting enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, detoxification, and amino acid biosynthesis in response to dietary changes. Unique enzymes detected after intervention, such as amino-peptidases and L-fucose isomerase, suggest activation or enrichment of microbial metabolic pathways linked to newly available dietary substrates.

Functional analysis at the biological process level (Fig. S3) further supported these observations. Processes such as proteolysis, carbohydrate metabolism, and translation remained highly represented in both pre- and post-intervention periods. However, the increased identification of peptides related to proteolysis and peptide transport post-intervention indicates enhanced microbial utilization of dietary proteins. Notably, cobalamin (vitamin B12) transport was exclusively identified post-intervention, suggesting an adaptation to the availability of specific nutrients. Meanwhile, the analysis of cellular components (Fig. S4) revealed consistent abundance of primary cellular structures such as cell outer membrane, cytosol, and cytoplasm pre- and post-intervention. However, peptides related to the plasma membrane, outer membrane-bounded periplasmic space, and extracellular region exhibited noticeable differences after dietary intervention. Moreover, increased identification of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter-associated peptides post-intervention likely indicates increased microbial transport activities and nutrient absorption capacity in response to dietary substrates.

Conclusions

This study provides critical insights into the methodological considerations for metaproteomic analyses of gut microbiota, and underscores how optimized extraction strategies can influence downstream taxonomic and functional interpretations. By demonstrating the superior performance of the SDS_DDM_urea buffer in extracting a broad range of microbial proteins, our findings establish this method as a robust and comprehensive approach for metaproteomic studies. These methodological advances will be valuable for future applications, providing a reliable foundation for studying complex microbial communities under different experimental conditions. Additionally, the application of these optimized extraction techniques to dietary studies offers a promising avenue for exploring the role of nutrition in modulating gut health and disease. Overall, our work highlights the importance of rigorous methodological optimization as a prerequisite for reliable biological insights, paving the way for future research into the dynamic interactions between diet, gut microbiota, and host health, with potential applications in personalized nutrition and therapeutic strategies.
