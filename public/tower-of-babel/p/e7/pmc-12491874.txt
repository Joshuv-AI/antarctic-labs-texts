Rare presentation of Behçet's disease in a 22-year-old male with combined retinal artery and vein occlusions
Authors: Brennan Isaac, Ahmad Rehmani, Lee Angioletti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo demonstrate how the systemic work-up of a combined retinal and vein occlusion (CRAVO) led to a diagnosis of Behcet's disease.ObservationsA 22-year-old male presented with sudden, profound vision loss in the left eye upon waking up. Presenting visual acuity was no light perception (NLP) in the left eye and fundus examination revealed findings consistent with both central retinal artery and vein occlusion. Laboratory workup for common infectious etiologies was negative; however, inflammatory markers were elevated. Further testing revealed human leukocyte antigen (HLA)∗B51 positivity as well as a patent foramen ovale (PFO) that was found on echocardiogram. The patient was initiated on high dose steroids and immunosuppressants for Behçet's Disease and underwent surgical closure of PFO.Conclusions and importanceThis study highlights the value of a proper diagnostic approach in patients presenting with CRAVO without suspected risk factors. Investigation of autoimmune, genetic, cardiologic, and inflammatory diseases led to the diagnosis of Behçet's Disease, an uncommon condition where timely recognition and early treatment can prevent further vision loss and reduce the risk of future vascular events.Keywords: Behçet disease, CRAVO, Combined retinal artery and vein occlusion, Vasculitis, PFO, Patent foramen ovale

Introduction

Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) and central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) are distinct vascular ocular events, each typically resulting in sudden, painless vision loss. Risk factors often are linked to systemic issues, the most common of which are hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, hypercoagulability, and age. On examination, CRAO typically presents with an afferent pupillary defect, retinal edema, cherry red spot, and arteriolar attenuation. In contrast, CRVO consists of multiple retinal hemorrhages which may be accompanied by cotton wool spots, disc edema, and dilated and tortuous vessels. In a retrospective study conducted Raval et al., the prevalence and characteristics of combined retinal vascular occlusion were examined. The study reviewed 5151 patients with retinal vascular occlusions evaluated during the study period; of these, 17 patients were identified with combined retinal artery and vein occlusion (CRAVO), yielding a prevalence of approximately 0.3 %.1 In total, only 41 similar cases of CRAVO were noted from 2009 to 2018, highlighting the rarity of this condition. Such cases often indicated systemic issues including hypertension, diabetes, coagulopathy, and inflammatory disease. In this study we present a previously healthy, 22-year-old male with sudden, painless visual loss in the left eye caused by CRAVO. His underlying condition of Behçet's Disease was identified as the key contributing factor.

Case presentation

A 22-year-old male presented to the emergency department with sudden, painless, and profound vision loss in his left eye. He denied any history of cardiovascular, hypercoagulable or autoimmune disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, trauma, or recent medication or drug use. The patient reported an 18-h flight from South Africa six weeks prior, and had reported a non-recurrent, non-granulomatous iritis six years earlier that responded to short term use of topical steroid therapy. Relevant review of systems revealed recurrent oral ulcers occurring approximately every month (none were present at the time of examination), knee arthritis, and transient skin rashes on his arms and legs (Fig. 1), all of which were not worked up in his prior medical encounters.

Dermatologic examination approximately 4 weeks after presentation reveal papulo-vesiculo-pustular eruptions.

On ocular examination, the patient's visual acuity in the right eye (OD) was 20/20 and the left eye (OS) had no light perception (NLP). The left pupil was more dilated compared to the right, 4.5mm and 4mm respectively with a Grade II APD present OS. The remaining anterior segment examination was within normal limits in both eyes. Funduscopic examination of the right eye was within normal limits. The left eye revealed optic disc edema, venous engorgement, and diffuse retinal hemorrhages as well as macular pallor with a cherry red spot accompanied by retinal whitening consistent with CRVO and CRAO respectively (Fig. 2). No emboli were noted in the arterial circulation. These findings were consistent with a final diagnosis of CRAVO. Other differentials included ophthalmic artery occlusion (OAO) and anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (AION). OAO was ruled out as it generally causes more diffuse ischemia with absence of a cherry-red spot due to involvement of choroidal vasculature. AION, while a common cause of acute vision loss, typically involves optic disc edema and field loss rather than widespread retinal ischemia and hemorrhage. The next day, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) and fluorescein angiography of the left eye confirmed the diagnosis, revealing significant cystic fluid as well as intraretinal hyperreflectivity and thickening (Fig. 3) and late leakage, hyperfluorescence of optic nerve and macula, scattered peripheral hypofluorescence consistent with capillary nonperfusion, and dilated and tortuous vessels, respectively. After out-patient imaging, the patient was referred back to the hospital to undergo an extensive systemic evaluation including a full metabolic, inflammatory, cardiac, coagulopathic, and genetic workup. Infectious and inflammatory workup was negative for common etiologies such as toxoplasmosis, syphilis, HIV, HSV, and Lyme. CMV IgM (30.4 Au/ml) and IgG (7.5 u/ml) were abnormal. Prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR) and partial thromboplastin time (PTT) were normal with values of 14.1, 1.07, 32.2 respectively. Factor V Leiden, Protein C, and Protein S, deficiencies were also negative. Inflammatory markers were elevated with erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) at 37 mm/h and C-Reactive Protein (CRP) at 0.5 mg/dl. Common autoimmune antibodies such as ANA, Anti-dsDNA, Anti-Smooth Muscle, Anti-Scl-70, Anti-Centromere, Anti-SSa/SSb, Anti-B2-glycoprotein, Anti-Cardiolipin, Anti-SLP, Anti-Jo, and Anti-U1-RNP were within normal limits. HLA∗B51 was found to be positive. Imaging including CT without contrast, CTA, and MRI of the brain and orbit with and without contrast were normal. Venous Doppler was negative for DVT bilaterally. Cardiac workup revealed presence of a patent foramen ovale (PFO) on transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE).

Management and outcome

The patient hospitalized and received methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3 days) after syphilis, tuberculosis, and Lyme disease were ruled out. IV acyclovir was initially prescribed empirically but was discontinued after PCR testing of aqueous and vitreous humor determined no evidence of HSV-1, HSV-2, and HZV. CMV and toxoplasmosis were also not found. Ganciclovir was not prescribed as the clinical findings were not consistent with CMV retinitis despite positive CMV titers. Diagnosis of Behçet's disease was later established based on the International Study Group (ISG) criteria.2,3 Required criteria consists of recurrent oral ulcers and minor criteria consists of genital ulcers, ocular lesions, skin lesions, and positive pathergy. As our patient fit the criteria, demonstrating recurrent oral ulcers, ocular lesions, and skin lesions, rheumatology established the diagnosis and initiated azathioprine without the need for standing glucocorticoid therapy. Also, our patient was followed by cardiology who started the patient on Apixaban for the possibility of an underlying thromboembolic source. Secondarily, 8 months after presentation, the patient underwent surgical closure of the PFO as recommended by the cardiologist to prevent any unwanted embolic events. Despite appropriate interventions, the patient's left eye remained NLP with the current focus being on preventing complications in the fellow eye.

Discussion

Our patient presented with the classical features of Behçet's disease such as oral ulcers, ocular and skin manifestations, and vascular disease. Blood work also revealed HLA∗B51 positivity, a marker which is present in up to 60 % of people.4 However, it is important to establish that the HLA marker is not required for diagnosis of Behçet's as it is not included in the ISG guidelines. Behçet's is a systemic vasculitis affecting vessels of all sizes, commonly occurring in males between the ages of 20 and 404,5. Presenting symptoms include oral and genital ulcers, vascular disease, and skin lesions such as pseudofolliculitis, papulopustular lesions, erythema nodosum, and acneiform nodules. Also present can be a positive pathergy test and CNS involvement such as intra-axial cranial neuropathy, ophthalmoparesis, pyramidal disease, focal and multifocal cerebral lesions, and various brainstem syndromes. Ocular manifestations typically include uveitis (anterior, intermediate, posterior, and panuveitis), retinal vasculitis, scleritis, episcleritis, retinal artery/vein occlusion, and optic neuritis.

Typical retinal findings in Behçet's disease include an occlusive retinal vasculitis—most often periphlebitis but also periarteritis—with associated vascular sheathing, intraretinal hemorrhages, venous dilation, macular edema, and optic atrophy.5 Secondary vascular occlusions may arise from the heightened inflammatory state associated with systemic vasculitis. One explanation for the obstruction of both vascular systems in our patient is thrombotic occlusion secondary to vasculitis, as inflammation-induced endothelial dysfunction plays a key role in thrombosis affecting both veins and arteries.6,7 Another proposed mechanism is secondary arterial compromise due to venous outflow obstruction. As venous pressure rises, arterial inflow may slow, briefly reverse, and even halt. This, together with optic disc edema related to CRVO, can compress the central retinal artery, producing a functional or secondary arterial occlusion.8

In a study by Taylor et al. involving 107 patients with ocular Behçet's disease, the authors aimed to identify predictive factors for visual loss. Analysis revealed that the most frequent cause of irreversible vision loss was ischemic maculopathy.9 Other variables that were statistically significant risk factors included male sex, unilateral disease, and left eye involvement. It is important to note that this study revealed that biologic agents were less likely to have severe visual loss and led to better outcomes.

Treatment for Behçet's disease is guided by European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations which are dependent on posterior segment ocular involvement. If the criteria are met, systemic corticosteroid therapy along with biologics such as azathioprine is recommended. In severe manifestations, treatment should include infliximab or cyclosporin alongside azathioprine and corticosteroids.10 Our patient was started on azathioprine and given his NLP status could have benefited from additional therapy with infliximab/cyclosporin.

Review of literature reveals there have been 2 reported cases of CRAVO due to Behçet's. Both cases, similar to this one, had patients that had a high degree of inflammation demonstrated by elevated inflammatory markers, however there was no evidence of a hypercoagulable state. Peter et al. mentions a 51-year-old male who was started on infliximab therapy and Loukil et al. mentions a 30-year-old male who was started solely on corticosteroid therapy.11,12

Unlike the reported cases, our patient presented with an additional possible cause of thrombus formation with the presence of a PFO. PFOs are estimated to be in up to 25 % of the general population, rarely presenting unless found incidentally or during symptomatic cardiac work-up such as in a stroke. Our patient had returned from an approximate 18-h flight, allowing a possible window for a deep venous thrombosis (DVT) to form due to the sedentary nature of traveling. Although rare, there have been CRAOs due to paradoxical emboli from PFOs.13 Since the flight occurred 6 weeks prior and lower extremity dopplers were negative, it is unlikely that a paradoxical embolus was the cause of his presentation and the PFO should be considered as an incidental finding. Closure of the PFO may not have been indicated being that the CRAVO was likely due to vasculitis in origin. The RESPECT trial evaluated PFO closure accompanied by medical therapy versus medical therapy alone. It demonstrated that closure is primarily indicated for patients with cryptogenic stroke, recurrent strokes, suspected paradoxical embolism, or high-risk anatomy; all of which were absent in our patient.14 Nonetheless, following the cardiologist's recommendation and through shared decision-making with the patient, surgical closure was performed.

Our patient also presented with positive IgG and IgM for CMV. Although CMV can be an underlying cause for vascular occlusions, its presentation is markedly different from that of our patient. CMV is an opportunistic infection that presents in immunocompromised individuals with findings of retinal hemorrhages and necrosis, periphlebitis, exudation, and in some cases retinal detachments.15 Our patient had no evidence of HIV/AIDS or other immunodeficiencies despite antibody positivity making this etiology less likely. Additionally, CMV positivity presents in up to 80 % of people in their lifetime, with approximately 50 % of adults having been infected by age 40.16 Also, false positives are possible with CMV antibody testing, with various studies showing cross-reactivity with Epstein-Barr Nuclear Antigen (EBNA).17 It is thus extremely unlikely that the CRAVO was due to CMV in our patient.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of a comprehensive work-up in a young, otherwise healthy patient presenting with combined retinal artery and vein occlusion (CRAVO). Although several potential contributors—such as CMV positivity, patent foramen ovale (PFO), and elevated inflammatory markers—were identified, a detailed review of systems and proper diagnostic evaluation ultimately revealed findings consistent with Behçet's disease. Proper diagnosis, timely treatment, and multidisciplinary care were important to prevent further systemic and fellow-eye manifestations of Behçet's disease. However, the patient's poor visual outcome underscores the critical need for early recognition and management to decrease morbidity.

Claims of priority

As of 2025, after conducting a literature review utilizing Pubmed, EUJR Med, AAO Journal, American Journal of Ophthalmology, Indian Journal of Ophthalmology, La presse Medical, and ACTA Ophthalmologica, I have not found a combined CRAVO in a patient with Behcet's disease with an underlying Patent Foramen ovale in an otherwise healthy young patient with no risk factors or family history.
