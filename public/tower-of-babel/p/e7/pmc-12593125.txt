Design and Evaluation of Dual-Targeted Radionuclide Therapeutics for NTS1 with Improved Tumor Retention through Endolysosomal Trapping
Authors: Audifás-Salvador Matus-Meza, Sadie Klein, Jered C. Garrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the development of neurotensin receptor subtype 1-(NTS1-) targeted constructs incorporated with an irreversible cysteine protease inhibitor (i.e., cysteine protease trapping agent (CPTA)) to improve tumor retention through adduct formation. Specifically, in this CPTA-incorporated NTS1-targeted agent (CPTA-NTS1TA) paradigm, we investigated the structure–activity impact of a series of alkyl, alkylamine, and ether linkers. The CPTA-NTS1TAs, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e, exhibited nanomolar binding affinities (9–38 nM) to NTS1 and were capable of rapid and irreversible inhibition (18,900 ± 1800–54,000 ± 7000 M–1 s–1) of a cysteine protease (i.e., cathepsin B). Linker modifications that included a positive charge ([ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c) or were less sterically restrained ([ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a) exhibited improved NTS1-targeting capabilities in an HT-29 colon cancer mouse model. Although, the level of tumor uptake (4.6 ± 0.7–16.5 ± 1.2%ID/g) of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e was often well below the benchmark control [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (20.8 ± 1.1%ID/g), a NTS1-targeted agent that has reached clinical trials. However, all experimental constructs containing the irreversible inhibitor had higher tumor retention (17.8 ± 1.5–28 ± 7%) as a percentage of initial uptake compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (13 ± 2%). In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the structure–activity relationships of these dual-targeted constructs for future development to improve tumor uptake and reduce the renal retention of NTS1-targeted radiotherapeutics.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common cancers worldwide. , While localized CRC exhibits five-year survival rates exceeding 90%, patients with metastatic CRC face a significantly worse clinical survival rate of approximately 16%. The lethality of CRC is multifaceted, largely driven by the high prevalence of relapse, frequent occurrence of late-stage diagnoses, and a dearth of effective, systemic treatment options. These clinical challenges have driven the impetus to develop more effective and specific approaches to combat CRC, particularly for patients suffering from metastatic disease.

Neurotensin receptor 1 (NTS1) is a Class A G protein-coupled receptor that belongs to the neurotensin receptor family (NTS1, NTS2, and NTS3/Sortilin) with native expression primarily in the gastrointestinal and nervous systems. , However, the high NTS1 expression in CRC and many other malignancies has prompted the exploration of NTS1 as a promising therapeutic target for systemic cancer therapy. , For radiopharmaceutical-based NTS1-targeted agents (NTS1TAs), early work focused on synthetic peptide derivatives of neurotensin, the endogenous ligand of NTS1. A relatively recent pathway under exploration in NTS1 radiopharmaceutical development is using small molecule-based pharmacophores, such as SR142948A, a known antagonist developed at the turn of the century. This approach resulted in the development of a novel theranostic NTS1TA ([ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227), which demonstrated excellent in vivo tumor targeting in preclinical and clinical (NCT03525392) studies. While [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 demonstrated substantial NTS1-mediated tumor uptake, the construct’s rapid washout, akin to many low-molecular-weight constructs, diminished the deliverable radiation dose and clinical potential.

Our laboratory has been interested in approaches that extend the tumor residence time of low-molecular-weight radiopharmaceuticals to maximize their radiation dose delivery and overall effectiveness. − Recently, we have begun exploring the use of irreversible cysteine protease trapping agents (CPTAs) based on epoxysuccinyl peptides to improve the tumor residence times of NTS1TAs. ,, Mechanistically, we have demonstrated that these inhibitor-incorporated constructs form intracellular adducts upon NTS1-mediated uptake. Presumably, these adducts are formed with cysteine cathepsins, a well-known and highly expressed cysteine protease family located in the endolysosomal compartments. Herein, we continue this avenue of research by examining an epoxysuccinyl peptide in conjunction with the small molecule NTS1-targeting vector SR142948A. Specifically, we sought to investigate the structure–activity relationships of linker modifications on the dual-targeting (i.e., cysteine proteases and NTS1) efficacy of the constructs, see Figure . The biological performance of these CPTA-NTS1TAs was examined alongside [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (clinical control) in in vitro and in vivo models of colon cancer.

Results

Synthesis of CPTA-NTS1TAs

The synthesis CPTA-incorporated NTS1TAs (CPTA-NTS1TAs) involved a series of multistep chemical reactions utilizing standard amide coupling agents (Scheme ). While the synthesis of 1a–e was successfully achieved using EDCI-NHS, in the reaction with the linker c, two major byproducts with m/z 580 were observed, which outcompeted the formation of the desired product, negatively impacting the yield and complicating purification. This byproduct formation maybe associated with the stabilization of the O-acyl-urea intermediate during the coupling process. Unlike other linkers, linker c contains multiples N-methylated groups and a flexible propyl chain, which could transiently stabilize the O-acyl-urea intermediate, potentially delaying its conversion into the NHS-activated ester. This extended intermediate lifetime might allow for alternative reaction pathways, leading to the formation of two byproducts before the NHS can proceed efficiently (see Scheme S1 and Figure S12, Supporting Information). Alternatively, the observed byproducts could result from an unexpected rearrangement pathway facilitated by electronic and steric effects specific to linker c. In contrast, other linkers, such as linker d and e, which incorporate ether functionalities, may influence the electronic environment in a way that favors the expected amide formation rather than rearrangement. However, further experimental validation is required to confirm these mechanistic proposals, both of which are outside our proposed goals. To mitigate this issue, 1c was successfully coupled using HBTU/HOBt, which improved yield and purity.

Chemical Synthesis of the CPTA-NTS1TAs - 8a–e

Overall, the synthesis of the other intermediates (2, 3a–e, 3a–e, 4a–e, 5a–e, 6, and 7a–e) proceeded without difficulties with moderate to good yields (40–90%). Standard purification techniques for the intermediates involved simple product extractions from the reaction mixture using ethyl acetate, allowing product isolation without undergoing exhaustive purification. Reaction progression was monitored using liquid chromatography/electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (LC/ESI-MS). After t-Bu and Boc deprotection of 7a–e using TFA, (±)-trans-oxirane-2,3-dicarboxylic acid was conjugated to the primary amine to yield 8a–e.

The CPTA-NTS1TAs were purified through semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) at UV214 and UV280, achieving a purity of ≥95%.

Labeling of CPTA-NTS1TAs

Radiolabeling yield for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e ranged in efficiencies between 60–80%. Both nonradioactive and radioactive Lu-labeled CPTA-NTS1TAs (Lu-8a-e and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e, respectively) were synthesized by reacting the appropriate precursor (8a–e) with LuCl3 and 177LuCl3 (As ≥ 740 GBq/mg) respectively, in 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH = 5.5) at 85 °C (Scheme ). To improve chromatographic resolution and facilitate purification, CuSO4 was added after the labeling reaction to chelate any remaining unreacted precursor. This strategy improved separation between the nonradioactive and 177Lu-labeled species during HPLC purification by eliminating or reducing peak overlap and improving compound isolation. All unlabeled and Lu-labeled CPTA-NTS1TAs were unambiguously identified and characterized using high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), see Figures S22–S31 (Supporting Information).

In Vitro NTS1 Receptor Competitive Cell-Binding Studies

The NTS1-positive human colon cancer HT-29 cell line was utilized to evaluate NTS1 binding affinity studies of 8a–e, the corresponding Lu complexes (Lu-8a-e) and 3BP-227 (positive control). All CPTA-NTS1TAs exhibited low nanomolar binding affinities (9–37 nM), see Figures and S1. The binding affinities of the constructs were highly similar to 3BP-227 (9.6 ± 0.4 nM), with only 8e reaching a significant difference (p < 0.001). No significant (p > 0.99) differences were observed between the CPTA-NTS1TAs (8a–e) and their corresponding nonradioactive Lu complex (Lu-8a-e), suggesting that metalation did not substantially impact NTS1 affinity. The NTS1-targeted constructs with the alkyl and alkylamine linkers (8a–c, Lu-8a-c) generally had better affinities than the ether linkages (8d–e, Lu-8d-e). Regarding the Lu-labeled constructs, the construct with the shortest linker (Lu-8a) exhibited the lowest IC50 values (9.3 ± 1.4 nM, respectively), followed by Lu-8c (14 ± 2 nM), although no significant difference was observed between them. Lu-8a did demonstrated significantly (p < 0.038) better NTS1-affinity compared to Lu-8b, Lu-8d, and Lu-8e, while Lu-8c only achieved statistical significance (p < 0.01) against Lu-8e. The constructs Lu-8b, Lu-8d, and Lu-8e showed similar and statistically identical NTS1 affinities in the 25–35 nM range. Speculatively, the higher NTS1 affinity of Lu-8a compared to Lu-8b suggests that the methylation of the amide linkers in Lu-8b inhibits NTS1 affinity due to increased steric hindrance and decreased linker flexibility. However, overall modification of the length and nature (i.e., ether vs alkyl vs alkylamine) did not substantially influence NTS1 affinity.

Cathepsin B Inhibition

Inhibition kinetic assays for 8a–e and Lu-8a-e were performed against human cysteine cathepsin B (CatB). The second-order rate constant (k inact/K I) values, depicted in Figure , were determined from linear regression of the plots of observed inhibition rates versus concentrations of the conjugate (inhibitor) shown in Figures S2–S11 (Supporting Information). The shortest linker (8a) yielded the lowest rate constant (18,000 ± 3000 M–1 s–1), while the addition of methylene to the linker (8b) gave the highest rate constant (54,000 ± 17,000 M–1 s–1). With the exception of 8e/Lu-8e, the labeling of the constructs resulted in lower (1.9–4.7-fold) inhibition kinetics relative to their unlabeled analogs, with statistical significance (p < 0.001) achieved for 8b-d/Lu-8b-d. Given these results, we speculate that the contrasting charge of the unlabeled vs labeled chelator may influence CatB binding and inhibitor potency. For the labeled constructs, Lu-8e exhibited the fastest kinetics (32,000 ± 7000 M–1 s–1) that was significantly (p ≤ 0.0003) faster than any other Lu-labeled construct, except Lu-8b. The inhibition kinetics data suggest that the linker structure modestly impacts inhibitor performance. However, a general trend was observed regarding the negative effect of Lu-labeling relative to unlabeled constructs on inhibition kinetics.

Cell Internalization Studies

Cellular uptake of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 was evaluated over 4 h using the HT-29 cell line with the results of the internalized and surface-bound fractions reported in Figure (see Table S1 for additional data). [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a,d,e demonstrated an approximately 1:1 distribution of internalized: surface-bound, while [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b,c favored a larger internalized fraction. At the end of the 4 h experiment, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (7.15 ± 0.3%) exhibited the highest amount internalized, which was slightly better than [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b (5.03 ± 0.19%), followed by [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c (4.9 ± 0.9%), [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d (3.7 ± 0.7%), [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a (1.87 ± 0.19%), and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8e (1.08 ± 0.05%). Interestingly, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a, which had the best NTS1-affinity, was found to have one of the lowest internalization profiles. In contrast, despite the lower NTS1 affinity of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b showed the highest cellular uptake. This suggests that NTS1-binding affinity does not necessarily predict receptor-mediated endocytic uptake, indicating that additional factors beyond receptor affinity influence internalization dynamics.

In Vitro Adduct Studies

Autoradiographic SDS-PAGE studies, depicted in Figure A,B, were conducted to assess the ability of the CPTA-NTS1TAs to form adducts with human CatB and in vitro using HT-29 colon cancer cells. For all studies, the loaded samples were normalized to radioactivity content. [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e was incubated with CatB (human liver) for 1 h before analysis, Figure A. Two bands were observed, one with a molecular weight corresponding to CatB adducts (∼32 kDa) and the other band (<10 kDa) corresponding to the low molecular weight fraction (presumed to be predominantly nonadducted [177Lu]­Lu-CPTA-NTS1TAs). The CatB adduct band accounted for 25.8–27.4% of the total signal for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e. The autoradiographic SDS-PAGE analysis of HT-29 cell lysates after a 4 h incubation with [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e is shown in Figure B. Multiple bands were observed in the region of ∼25–35 kDa, consistent with the molecular weight region of various members of the cathepsin family. Similar to the enzyme SDS-PAGE studies, a low molecular weight fraction (<10 kDa) was also observed. Adduct bands corresponded to approximately half of the signal, with radioactive adduct values of 53.8, 50.3, 52.7, 49.8, and 51.7% for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e, respectively. While these adducts confirm the ability of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e to form adducts, these studies also demonstrate that the in vitro adduct formation efficacy (i.e., % of radioconjugate adducted) was not substantially impacted by the structural variations investigated. As previously reported, no adduct formation, as expected, was observed for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 when incubated with CatB or HT-29 cells.

Biodistribution Studies

Biodistribution studies of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 were investigated in an HT-29 tumor xenograft mouse model at 4, 24, 72, and 168 h postinjection time points, see (Figure A and Tables S2–S5). [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e exhibited better on average blood clearance (1.04 ± 0.14–3.3 ± 0.6%ID/g) by 4 h compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (14 ± 4%ID/g). The LogD7.4 values [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e were lower (−1.03 to −2.14) compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (−2.46), see Table S10. This suggests hydrophilicity was not the main driving force behind the more rapid blood clearance of the experimental radioconstructs. Other factors, such as differences in plasma protein binding, renal filtration rate and differential tissue uptake, may contribute to the faster blood clearance profiles of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e compared to the control. With the exception of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b, no significant differences in blood retention were detected between [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a,c–e, or [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 at later time points. By 168 h postinjection, [177Lu]­Lu-8b exhibited exceptionally high blood clearance and significantly lower retention in blood compared to [177Lu]­Lu-8c and [177Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (p < 0.0018), as shown in Table S5. Nontarget organ uptake in the heart, lung, liver, pancreas, stomach, spleen, small intestine, large intestine, muscle, bone, and brain exhibited lower uptake (<2.7%ID/g) at 4 h compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227. [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e exhibited good clearance at the remaining time points, resulting in minimum background signal and a good tumor-to-nontarget organ ratio, see Tables S6–S9.

At 4 h, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c (16.5 ± 1.2%ID/g) demonstrated the highest tumor uptake (p < 0.0001) of the experimental constructs investigated, followed by [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a (11.6 ± 1.1%ID/g). The tumor uptake of these linker constructs was significantly (p < 0.0001) higher than [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b (5.2 ± 0.4%ID/g) and the ether linkers, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d (6.5 ± 1.0%ID/g) and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8e (4.6 ± 0.7%ID/g). In vivo tumor uptake correlated well with receptor affinities determined from the in vitro competitive binding assays. In comparison to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (20.8 ± 1.1%ID/g), the tumor uptake of the radioconstructs under investigation was significantly (p ≤ 0.0004) lower. Interestingly, from 4 to 24 h, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 exhibited less clearance from HT-29 tumors compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e, see Figure B. This may be explained at least in part by the continued tumor uptake of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 due to its substantially higher blood retention offsetting clearance during that time. From 24 to 72 h, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e exhibited a much greater retention of radioactivity compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227. Tumor retention of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 by 168 h, as a percentage of initial 4 h uptake, was 13 ± 2%. All of the constructs containing the cysteine protease inhibitors demonstrated higher on average tumor retention (17.8 ± 1.5 – 28 ± 7%) relative to the [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227, with [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c (23.8 ± 1.5%) and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d (28 ± 7%) reaching statistical significance (p ≤ 0.0199). Similar to the in vitro adduct formation studies, in vivo tumor retention did not correlate with results from the in vitro inhibition studies. This suggests that CatB kinetics assays alone are not a good predictor of in vivo tumor retention efficacy.

The kidneys are widely recognized as a dose-limiting organ for small-molecule-based targeted radionuclide therapeutics due to their high perfusion, active reabsorption mechanisms, and susceptibility to radiation-induced nephrotoxicity, particularly from radiometals that undergo intracellular residualization in proximal tubular cells. The incorporation of the cysteine protease trapping agent led to a substantial (p ≤ 0.0007) increase in renal uptake (8.6 ± 0.5 – 21 ± 3%ID/g) relative to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (3.8 ± 0.4) at 4 h. Interestingly, the utilization of the ether linker in [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d led to a significant (p ≤ 0.0040) increase in initial renal uptake relative to all alkyl and alkylamine linker constructs, but not quite meeting statistical thresholds for the fellow ether linker group analog [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8e (p = 0.0619). From 4 to 24 h, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d exhibited the largest percent clearance from the kidneys, see Figure B, of any construct, excluding [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227. This resulted in statistically indistinguishable levels of (5.9 ± 0.8 – 8.7 ± 1.7%ID/g) of renal retention for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e by 24 h. After 168 h, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e yielded renal retentions of 0.92 ± 0.06 – 3.1 ± 0.3%ID/g, which were significantly (p ≤ 0.0001) higher than [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 (0.20 ± 0.04%ID/g). Discussion

Therapeutic radiopharmaceutical development aims to deliver effective radiation doses to tumors while minimizing radiotoxicity. Despite its superior NTS1-targeting capabilities compared to agonist-based constructs, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 shares the common limitation of limited tumor retention with many low molecular weight radiopharmaceuticals, which diminishes the delivered tumor radiation dose. To address this, our laboratory has explored the integration of irreversible cysteine protease inhibitors into low molecular weight NTS1-targeted constructs. , This approach, termed endolysosomal trapping, aims to prolong tumor retention by facilitating irreversible adduct formation with endogenous cysteine proteases. This study incorporated varying linker lengths and chemistries to connect the pharmacophoric moiety with the chelating agent and the irreversible inhibitor (Figure ). We systematically evaluated the impact of these linker designs on in vitro (e.g., NTS1 affinity and inhibition kinetics) and in vivo (renal uptake, tumor targeting, and retention) performance to optimize the dual-targeted (NTS1/cysteine protease) construct.

The in vitro competitive NTS1 binding affinity study for the constructs revealed that chelation with Lu did not significantly alter binding affinity compared to the unchelated CPTA-NTS1TAs (p > 0.96). Among the tested CPTA-NTS1TAs, 8a and [ 177 Lu]Lu −8a exhibited the lowest IC50 values (9.7 ± 1.1 and 9.3 ± 1.4 nM, respectively), followed by 8c and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c (15 ± 2 and 15 ± 2 nM, correspondingly). The IC50 values of the remaining CPTA-NTS1TAs and their Lu complexes were slightly higher but below 40 nM, indicating optimal NTS1 affinity. These values are comparable to unlabeled 3BP-227 (8.3 ± 0.6 nM). While the linker choice appears not to have imposed significant steric hindrance (i.e., short vs long linkers), the subtle structural modulation clearly impacts the interactions between the CPTA-NTS1TAs and the NTS1. This suggests that the flexibility of the linker and electronic properties of the heteroatoms influence CPTA-NTS1TAs conformation and receptor affinity.

Kinetic assays with human CatB revealed significant differences in inhibitory activity between unchelated and Lu-labeled complexes for three out of five CPTA-NTS1TAs (i.e., 8b–d vs [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b-d). Unlabeled constructs generally had more rapid adduct formation kinetics. This suggests that lutetium complexation with DOTA can negatively influence the CatB inhibitory activity by altering the conformation of the CPTA-NTS1TAs or modifying affinity by neutralizing the charges of the chelator. Further computational investigation would be needed to clarify the contributing factors. Despite these differences, the enzymatic inhibitory rates remained high, with the lowest rate observed of 10,000 ± 3000 M–1 s–1 for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a. In vitro adduct formation experiments with human CatB and HT-29 cells demonstrated comparable amounts of adduct formation for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e. These findings imply that second-order rate constants of >10,000 M–1 s–1 do not yield substantial or practical increases in adduct formation and that the inhibition rate of the trapping agent is not the rate-limiting step for adduct formation of these dual-targeted constructs. However, some caution should be taken when applying these conclusions to cellular adduct formation. The CatB-specific inhibition rates of the CPTA-NTS1TAs may not correlate with their actual adduct formation rates with the numerous cellular cysteine proteases encountered by the constructs.

In vitro SDS-PAGE autoradiographic studies in HT-29 cells demonstrated that [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e are capable of efficiently forming adducts with intracellular proteins, presumably cysteine proteases such as CatB, given the known selectivity of highly analogous epoxysuccinyl peptide inhibitors. Previously, internalization was found to be a prerequisite of adduct formation for similar dual-targeted agents. Based on the SDS-PAGE image, approximately half of the cumulative activity for each cellular lysate was adduct-associated at 4 h, indicating efficient adduct formation once internalized. The similarity of the intracellular adduct bands between ∼25–35 kDa for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e suggests that modification of the linker did not influence the protease selectivity of the constructs.

Biodistribution studies of all radioconstructs were carried out in an HT-29 tumor xenograft mouse model. At 4 h postinjection, all CPTA-NTS1TAs exhibited lower blood retention than [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227. While cysteine proteases are ubiquitously expressed in tissues, including serum, the premise that the incorporated irreversible inhibitors are serum stable and do not nonspecifically bind to serum proteins is supported by our results. In vivo, the relatively low blood retention of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a–e at 4 h and similar or lower retention values compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 at later time points suggest adducts are not abundantly formed with serum proteins. The in vitro SDS-PAGE analysis showed cellular adduct bands that indicate a highly selective protein association and not a promiscuous adduct formation profile. Additionally, in vivo studies with similar epoxysuccinyl inhibitors in other targeted constructs support the serum stability of the incorporated inhibitors. ,

The inclusion of a tertiary amino group in the linker (i.e., [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227) led to a clear increase in HT-29 tumor uptake compared to the other constructs. The tertiary amine imparts a positive charge under physiological conditions, while the other linkers do not. The charged linker of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c exhibited one of the highest NTS1 affinities and internalization efficiencies, making it the most well-rounded construct investigated, likely contributing significantly to its in vivo targeting capabilities. This finding suggests that a positively charged linker helps facilitate in vivo NTS1-mediated targeting. Interestingly, the construct with the shortest linker, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a demonstrated significant differences in NTS1 affinity, as shown in Figure . [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a exhibited the highest in vitro NTS1 affinity and second highest in vivo HT-29 tumor uptake of any of the experimental constructs but also demonstrated one of the poorest internalization rates. The linker of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a, an ethyl linkage connecting amides, outperformed [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8b, which has a slightly longer propyl linkage connecting N-methylamides. This data suggests that amides with their reduced sterics and increased flexibility compared to N-methylamides contribute positively to optimizing NTS1 affinity. As a percentage of initial uptake, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e demonstrated significantly higher tumor retention over 168 h than [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227, attributable to the expected adduct formation within the tumor. However, the substantially reduced HT-29 tumor uptake of most of the constructs compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 resulted in lower absolute tumor retention values (i.e.,%ID/g). Further structure–activity refinement to improve in vivo NTS1-mediated uptake is needed (e.g., further adjustment of the linker or inclusion of an albumin binder) to optimize the endolysosomal trapping approach and demonstrate possible clinical utility. In addition, although blocking studies have been previously conducted using analogous NTS1-targeted constructs employing a CPTA, receptor blocking studies were not performed with the constructs evaluated in this study. This omission may limit the ability to definitively attribute observed effects to NTS1-specific binding. Future studies will be undertaken to confirm NTS1-specificity, either by in vivo blocking studies or through comparison with an isogenic NTS1 knockout cell line control.

All constructs, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e, demonstrated significantly higher renal retention at all time points compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227, which can be largely ascribed to the incorporation of the inhibitor and the resultant adduct formation. The inclusion of a single ether linkage ([ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d) led to a substantial increase in initial renal uptake in comparison to other constructs, including the diether linkage ([ 177 Lu]­Lu-8e). Interestingly, the increased uptake of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d was offset by rapid renal clearance of the construct from 4 to 24 h, which resulted in all experimental constructs showing similar %ID/g values by 24 h. Regarding retention as a percentage of initial uptake, the charged tertiary amine linker of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c exhibited increased renal retention relative to all other constructs over the time points investigated. This may imply that the adduct formation of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c is more efficient in renal tissues than other constructs, but further investigation is needed to substantiate this hypothesis. While [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c demonstrated higher tumor uptake, the more rapid renal clearance of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a resulted in better tumor-to-kidney ratios by 168 h, 2.72 ± 0.29 vs 1.25 ± 0.13. By comparison, the tumor-to-kidney ratio of [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 is considerably better at 13.68 ± 3.45. Moving forward, a primary aim for optimization will focus on structural modifications of the constructs and exploration of renal blocking agents to decrease kidney uptake.

Conclusions

This study investigated a series of novel NTS1-targeted radiopharmaceuticals incorporating irreversible cysteine protease inhibitors (i.e., CPTAs) with the intent of enhancing tumor retention. Specifically, we explored the impact of a series of alkyl, alkylamine, and ether linkers on the biological performance of this construct. All of the CPTA-NTS1TAs, [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e, demonstrated nanomolar binding affinities to NTS1 and good inhibition (adduct formation) kinetics. Linker modifications had modest impacts on inhibition kinetics and cell internalization profiles. In vivo biodistribution studies revealed that [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e exhibited faster blood clearance than the clinical control [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227. While the tumor uptake was lower for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227, the constructs demonstrated better tumor retention at later time points, with [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8c and [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8d showing statistically significant improvements. However, incorporating CPTAs led to increased renal uptake for [ 177 Lu]­Lu-8a-e compared to [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227. These results provide valuable insights into the structure–activity relationships of CPTA-NTS1-targeted constructs, suggesting the incorporation of irreversible cysteine protease inhibitors may be a promising strategy for enhancing the efficacy of NTS1-targeted radiopharmaceuticals. Future studies will focus on optimizing these novel constructs to mitigate renal retention while improving tumor uptake and retention properties.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of the Compounds (1a–b and 1d–e)

To a 100 mL round-bottom flask, compound 4-(5-(2,6-dimethoxyphenyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-3-isopropylbenzoic acid (1.41 mmol) was mixed with N-hydroxysuccinimide (1.27 equiv) and EDCI (2.54 equiv) in 12.75 mL DMF. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h. Then, this mixture was added to a solution of the linker (a-b or d-e, 5.10 equiv) in DMF (38.50 mL), followed by the addition of DIEA (2.54 equiv). The resulting mixture was stirred overnight and after completion of the reaction, the mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 60 mL). The combined organic phase was washed with brine (3 × 50 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated by rotary evaporation. The residue obtained was dried by high vacuum to provide a light yellow colored sparkling solid (yield 70–95%). The product was used for the next step without further purification.

Synthesis of the 5-(2,6-Dimethoxyphenyl)-1-(2-isopropyl-4-(methyl­(3-(methyl­(3-(methylamino)­propyl)­amino)­propyl)­carbamoyl)­phenyl)-1H-pyrazole-3-carboxylate (1c)

A mixture of 4-(5-(2,6-dimethoxyphenyl)-3-(methoxycarbonyl)-1H-pyrazol-1-yl)-3-isopropylbenzoic acid (1.18 mmol), HBTU (1.25 equiv), and HOBt (2.80 equiv) was dissolved in DMF (15.50 mL) with DIEA (3.30 equiv), and stirred at room temperature for 15 min. The combined organic phase was washed with brine (3 × 40 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated by rotary evaporation to afford the crude product (amber oil) which was used for the next step without further purification.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of N2-(((9H-Fluoren-9-yl)­methoxy)­carbonyl)-N6-((tert-butoxycarbonyl)-l-leucyl)­lysine (2)

Boc-Leu-OH·H2O (100 mg, 0.401 mmol) was mixed with EDCI (1.00 equiv) and NHS (1.10 equiv) in 2 mL of DMF and allowed to stir at room temperature overnight. Next, Fmoc-Lys-OH·HCl (1.05 equiv), dissolved in 2 mL of DMF was added to this solution and DIEA (5.00 equiv) was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 3 h at room temperature and then extracted with ethyl acetate (4 × 30 mL). The combined organic phase was washed with 1 M HCl (2 × 30 mL) and brine (3 × 30 mL), before dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated by rotary evaporation. The residue obtained was dried under a high vacuum to afford a white solid (yield: 80%). The product was used for the next step without further purification.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of the Compounds (3a–e)

A mixture of 2 (0.172 mmol), HBTU (1.25 equiv), and HOBt (2.80 equiv) in DMF (4.00 mL) with DIEA (3.30 equiv) was stirred at room temperature for 15 min. Then, the appropriate compound (1a–e, 1.15 equiv) in DMF (2.00 mL) was added. The mixture was stirred for 2.5 h before extraction with ethyl acetate (3 × 30 mL), and the combined organic phase was washed with brine (3 × 30 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated by rotary evaporation. The residue obtained was dried under a high vacuum to afford a yellow-colored sparkling solid (yield 40–80%).

General Procedure for the Synthesis of the Compounds (4a–e)

General Procedure for the Synthesis of the Compounds (5a–e)

Determination of the Inhibition Rate Constant (k inact/K I)

The fluorogenic inhibition assays were conducted in 96-well plates for all constructs. The assay buffer (pH = 6.0) consisted of KH2PO4 (350 mM), Na2HPO4 (50 mM), EDTA (6 mM), DTT (2.7 mM), and Brij 35 (0.25 mM). The CPTA-NTS1TAs, the fluorogenic substrate (Z-Arg-Arg-AMC, MW: 621.7 g/mol), and CatB were each prepared in the assay buffer solution. To each well, 30 μL of each of the three components was added. The final (running) well concentrations of the CatB was 2 nM and the Z-Arg-Arg-AMC was 800 μM. The final well concentrations of the CPTA-NTS1TAs were 1666.7, 833.3, 416.7, 208.3, 104.2, 52.1, 26.0, 13.0, and 6.5 nM. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C, and the fluorescence from each well was measured using an excitation of 340 nm and emission measurement at 440 nm. Measurements were taken every 1 min for 10 min, and then every 5 min until completing 1 h. The signal Y (fluorescent intensity) was plotted against time (t) using nonlinear regression (Y = Y max × (1 – exp­(−k obs × t))) to determine the pseudo first-order rate constant (k obs). The second-order rate constant (k inact/K I) were determined through linear regression of k obs vs inhibitor (conjugate) concentrations using GraphPad Prism 10.4.2.

In Vitro NTS1 Receptor Competitive Cell-Binding Studies

The half-maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the unlabeled CPTA-NTS1TAs and nonradioactive Lu-complexed CPTA-NTS1TAs was determined by competitive displacement cell-binding assays using HT-29 cells. [ 177 Lu]­Lu-3BP-227 served as the competitive radioligand for comparing the relative binding affinities of the CPTA-NTS1TAs. 100 μL (500,000 cells) of the cell suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 40 min in the presence of 100 μL of the radioligand (100,000 CPM) in medium and 100 μL of the conjugate (concentrations range from 10,000–0.001 nM) in medium. At the end of the incubation time, the cells were centrifuged and the supernatant was aspirated, and washed with fresh medium a total of three times (3 × 1000 μL). The remaining cell-associated activity was measured using a γ counter. IC50 values were calculated by nonlinear regression, using the One Site - Fitlog IC50 method of GraphPad Prism 10.4.2.

Cell Internalization Studies

HT-29 cells were cultured and adhered in six-well plates overnight at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. The cell medium was discarded and replaced with 1 mL of fresh medium containing the radioconjugate (100,000 CPM) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. At time points of 30, 60, 120, and 240 min, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with fresh medium (3 × 1 mL) to remove unbound radioconjugate. Surface-bound radioactivity was obtained by twice washing cells with an ice-cold acidic buffer (1 mL, 50 mM glycine–HCl, 0.1 M NaCl, pH 2.8). An SDS solution (5% in DI H2O, 800 μL) was utilized to lyse cells and isolate the internalized fraction. The radioactivity within each fraction was measured by a γ counter. The cellular uptake of the 177Lu-complexed CPTA-NTS1TAs was plotted as internalized and surface-bound radioactivity as a percentage of total added radioactivity (Y) and time point (X).

In Vitro Adducts Formation Studies (SDS-PAGE)

For the Samples without Cells

The CatB (6 nM, 30 μL) in storage buffer (50 mM sodium acetate and 1 mM EDTA, pH 5.0) was incubated with the 177Lu-labeled radioconjugate (30,000 CPM) in 50 μL of binding assay buffer (5 mM Tris, 5 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM DTT, pH = 5.5) in the absence and presence of the cysteine protease inhibitor E-64 (10 μM, 10 μL) for 1 h.

For the Samples with Cells

HT-29 cells were cultured in six-well plates overnight at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. The cells were exposed to 100 μL of each 177Lu-complexed conjugate (5000 cpm/ μL). The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h followed by washing with PBS (3 × 1 mL) and trypsinized with TriPLE Express (700 μL). At this point, three wells were combined (∼3 × 106 cells) and centrifuged for 4 min at 1000 G. Then, 120 μL of the lysate cocktail (RIPA buffer, 118 μL; Halt protease inhibitor, 1.2 μL; and EDTA, 1.2 μL) was added to the cell pellet and vigorously vortexed for one min. The suspension was incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged to remove insoluble debris. Nineteen μL of the supernatant was mixed with NuPAGE LDS Sample buffer (4×) (19.5 μL) and NuPAGE Sample Reducing Agent (10×) (3 μL) and incubated for a further 10 min at 70 °C. The cooled-down solution was loaded onto a NuPAGE 4 to 12%, Bis-Tris, 1.0–1.5 mm, Mini Protein gel and run at 90 V for 15 min, and then at 120 V for 75 min. After shaking in shrinking buffer (50 mL, 65% methanol, and 0.5% glycerol in DI water) at 4 °C overnight, the gel was dried for 6 h at room temperature and the ladders were painted with a small amount of radioactivity. The gel was then exposed to a phosphor screen for 72 h, which was subsequently scanned by Typhoon FLA 9500 imaging system at a 25 μm resolution to achieve an autoradiograph image.

Biodistribution Studies

Female five-week-old athymic mice were subcutaneously injected with 100 μL of HT-29 cells (1 × 106 cells/flank) suspended in Matrigel. Upon reaching a tumor volume of 50–200 mm3, the mice were assigned to the four time point groups to achieve similar tumor size distributions. The mice received intravenous injections of 10 μCi (0.37 MBq) of the purified radioconjugate via the tail vein. The mice were euthanized, and their tissues were excised at the desired time points postinjection (i.e., 4, 24, 72, and 168 h). Alternatively, mice were sacrificed and excluded from the study when other end point criteria were met, including signs of infection, deterioration of health (i.e., rapid weight loss, dehydration), or a tumor volume exceeding 2000 mm3. At each study time point, the organs of interest (e.g., blood, tumor, liver, etc.) were excised, weighed, and the radioactivity in each sample was quantified using a γ counter. The percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g) and the tumor to nontargeted tissue ratios were calculated.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA to evaluate differences between radio CPTA-NTS1TAs and time points, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to determine statistical significance. Data were log-transformed or baseline-corrected where necessary to ensure normality and homogeneity of variances. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant, although in some cases more stringent thresholds were applied, with p ≤ 0.01, p ≤ 0.001, and p ≤ 0.0001 reported when appropriate to highlight significant findings.
