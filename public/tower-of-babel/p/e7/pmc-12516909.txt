Xiaoxianxiong Decoction Attenuates LPS‐Induced Pneumonia in Mice by Inhibiting Inflammatory Response Through Activation of SIRT1
Authors: Linfeng Shi, Jiayan Zhang, Yuntao Cai, Junyun Bao, Huichao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTXiaoxianxiong decoction (XXXD) is a widely used clinical prescription effective in the treatment of pneumonia. The study investigates the efficacy and mechanism of XXXD in pneumonia. In the study, an in vivo pneumonia model was induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in mice, and an in vitro pneumonia model was induced by LPS in A549 cells. The study assessed the potential therapeutic effect of XXXD by detecting inflammatory cells in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, lung wet/dry weight ratio, and histology. ELISA kits were used to detect the release of inflammatory factors. Apoptosis was identified by TUNEL labeling and flow cytometry, and c‐caspase3/caspase3 expression was analyzed by Western Blot. SIRT1 expression was detected by immunofluorescence labeling, while Western blotting measured the expression levels of SIRT1 and its downstream proteins. Finally, the SIRT1 inhibitor EX527 was used in LPS‐induced mice, and a rescue assay was performed in LPS‐induced A549 cells by interfering with SIRT1 to investigate the regulatory mechanism of XXXD. XXXD greatly reduced the effect of LPS on apoptosis, significantly decreased the expression of p66shc and p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB downstream of SIRT1, and also significantly increased the expression of SIRT1. Interestingly, the anti‐inflammatory effect of XXXD on the pneumonia model disappeared after interfering with SIRT1. XXXD protects against LPS‐induced pneumonia by inhibiting the inflammatory response through activating SIRT1, offering a fresh approach to the creation of focused pneumonia treatment plans.

Introduction

Pneumonia is a prevalent respiratory illness that causes acute lung inflammation due to pathogenic bacteria, immunological damage, and environmental and chemical stimuli, resulting in roughly 120 million new cases and 1.3 million fatalities per year [1, 2]. It is characterized by severe pulmonary inflammation, and the clinical manifestations include fever, cough, dyspnea, somnolence, and chest discomfort [3]. Antibacterial drugs are still the mainstay of treatment for pneumonia nowadays, but drug resistance incidence has skyrocketed as a result of the overuse of antibiotics for prevention, multiple antibiotic usage, and increased use of high‐dose antibiotics [4]. Furthermore, long‐term usage of antibiotics may induce negative effects in patients [5]. Thus, pneumonia is a serious clinical problem, and greater knowledge of the pathological process of pneumonia is urgently required for the development of appropriate therapy methods [6]. Pneumonia is accompanied by the influx of inflammatory cells, and the inflammatory mediators such as proteases produced during the inflammatory response increase lung permeability [7]. As a result, treating pneumonia requires blocking the release of inflammatory mediators and lowering inflammatory responses.

Silent information regulator sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) is a niacinamide adenine dinucleotide‐dependent class III histone deacetylase and is well known for its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties [8, 9]. Consequently, the therapeutic uses of SIRT1‐targeted anti‐inflammatory treatments in the treatment of inflammatory illnesses are garnering more and more interest [10]. For instance, SIRT1 has been shown to promote the anti‐inflammatory phenotype of macrophages [11]. It also has been shown that knockdown of SIRT1 causes greater release of inflammatory cytokines, whereas activation of SIRT1 reduces the production of these cytokines [12]. In addition, studies have shown that by inhibiting SIRT1, miR‐199a exacerbates the inflammatory response triggered by lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [13]. More critically, SIRT1 has been discovered to affect the immunological response to pneumonia, and boosting SIRT1 may improve the clinical outcome of pneumonia [14]. Thus, the discovery of medicines that activate SIRT1 could be useful in the treatment of pneumonia. At present, there are many drugs that exert therapeutic effects by activating SIRT1, including some traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) [15, 16]. However, up to now, there has been no study on the treatment of pneumonia by activating SIRT1 with TCM. Therefore, it is of great significance to explore whether SIRT1 is the potential mechanism by which certain TCM exert their therapeutic effects on pneumonia.

TCM has unique advantages in treating pneumonia, including multi‐targeting, improving prognosis, and reducing the inflammatory index of pneumonia [17, 18]. For instance, Tian et al. demonstrated that the Qingfei Yin alleviated Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumonia by promoting complete autophagy to suppress necroptosis [19]. Another study found that Fuzheng Huayu Tablets can alleviate pulmonary fibrosis after COVID‐19 [20]. It is worth noting that Xiaoxianxiong Decoction (XXXD) has been used in ancient China to treat patients with severe pneumonia exhibiting symptoms such as abnormal breathing, depression, fever, and cough [21]. XXXD in febrile illness is a common prescription in clinical practice, with the effect of clearing heat and resolving phlegm [22]. Studies have shown that XXXD plays a role in the treatment of the critical phase of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) [23]. Other studies have shown that XXXD‐assisted bronchoalveolar lavage in the treatment of severe pneumonia patients has a certain anti‐inflammatory effect [24]. Furthermore, some of the active components in XXXD have been proven to be able to activate SIRT1. As Zhang et al. have demonstrated, berberine inhibits LPS‐induced inflammation in RAW264.7 cells by regulating the SIRT1/NF‐κB signaling pathway [25]. However, the underlying mechanism of XXXD in the treatment of pneumonia is unknown, and no studies have confirmed whether XXXD acts through SIRT1 in pneumonia.

Based on the above, considering the effect of XXXD in pneumonia and the important significance of SIRT1 in anti‐inflammation, we propose the hypothesis that XXXD inhibits LPS‐induced inflammatory response by upregulating SIRT1, thereby improving pneumonia. In the present study, we revealed through a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments whether XXXD inhibits the inflammatory response by activating SIRT1 and thereby attenuates LPS‐induced pneumonia. The research conducted in this study on the above hypothesis will enable a better understanding of the etiology of pneumonia and the discovery of better prospective treatment methods for pneumonia.

Preparation and Ingredient Analysis of XXXD

XXXD consists of the following three components: 6 g Huang Lian (Coptis chinensis Franch.), 12 g Ban Xia ( Pinellia ternata Ten. ex Breitenb.), and 20 g Gua Lou (Trichosanthes kirilowii Maxim.). The sources of all the herbs were the affiliated hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, Jiangsu Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine. All of these herbs underwent a 1‐h soak in ten times cold distilled water, followed by a heating and boiling process, a 40‐min decoction, filtering, and another boiling in eight times distilled water. Following the combination of the two filtrates, the crude drug was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at a concentration of 1 g of draw materials per milliliter (w/v). The filtrate was then filtered through 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm filters, respectively, and kept at 4°C.

The primary chemical components of XXXD were first determined using the High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) technique. After filtration with a 0.22 μm filter, the XXXD solution (10 μL) was injected into HPLC and separated at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (solvent A) and water containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–30 min: 5% A–30% A, 30–70 min: 30% A–45% A, 70–90 min: 45% A–90% A. The column temperature was set at 30°C, and UV detection was performed at 260 nm. Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co. Ltd. provided the reference standards for adenosine, palmatine, berberine, and cucurbitacin B (purity > 98%, Shanghai, China). The concentration of XXXD used for HPLC analysis was 1 g/mL (1 mL solution contains 1 g of the original herbs).

Subsequently, we further identified the components of XXXD through mass spectrometry analysis. A Q Exactive Orbitrap MS (Thermo Fisher, USA) was connected to the UHPLC system using a heated electrospray ionization source. The whole MS scan range was 85–1250 m/z, and the mass spectrometer was concurrently run in positive‐ and negative‐ion detection modes. The optimized MS parameters were as follows: spray voltage: 3.5 kV (positive) and 3.5 kV (negative); capillary temperature: 350°C; sheath gas: 35 arbs; auxiliary gas: 10 arbs; sweep gas: 0 arbs; S‐lens RF level: 50 V; auxiliary gas heater temperature: 400°C; scan mode: Full MS/dd‐MS2 mode, and the collision energy gradient was 10, 30, 45 eV.

LPS‐Induced Pneumonia in Mice

The male C57BL/6 mice, aged 12–14 weeks, free of specific pathogens, and weighing between 22 and 28 g, were supplied by SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co. LTD. Every animal had unrestricted access to food and water in a standard environment, with an ambient temperature of 22°C ± 3°C and 40%–70% relative humidity. The Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine approved all animal testing conducted for this study (202306A115).

Using a previously described procedure [26], 10 mg/kg of LPS was used to create an acute pneumonia model. To put it simply, after 3 days of adaptive feeding of mice, the pneumonia model was established. Starting from day 4, 5 μL of LPS (10 mg/kg) was instilled into the nose of the mice for three consecutive days, and then the mice were upright and rotated vertically so that the fluid was evenly distributed in the lungs to establish a lung infection model. All mice were continuously observed and weighed during this period. The sample size in this study was determined through a power analysis based on the ANOVA analysis results observed in the pre‐experiment compared to untreated animals (p = 0.047 for treatment vs. control), with a desired power of 90% and a significance level of 0.05. The mice were divided into the sham group (receiving 0.9% sodium chloride, NaCl), LPS model group, LPS + L‐XXXD (1.35 g/kg) group, LPS + M‐XXXD (2.7 g/kg) group, LPS + H‐XXXD (5.4 g/kg) group, and the LPS + DEX (0.018 g/kg) group. The dosage of XXXD in this study for mice was 9.1 times the clinical dosage used in humans. According to the formula, it was assumed that the average human weight was 65 kg and the drug in the prescription was 38 g. The dose for mice was calculated to be 5.32 g/kg. Therefore, ultimately, 5.4 g/kg was chosen as the highest dose in this study. After 3 days of modeling, the LPS + L‐XXXD (1.35 g/kg) group, LPS + M‐XXXD (2.7 g/kg) group, and LPS + H‐XXXD (5.4 g/kg) group were given gavage XXXD twice a day for 5 days. Similarly, after 3 days of modeling, the LPS + DEX (0.018 g/kg) group was given gavage of DEX twice a day for 5 days to establish a positive control [27]. In the experiment of administering the SIRT1 inhibitor EX527, mice were randomly divided into the sham group, model group, LPS + H‐XXXD (5.4 g/kg) group, LPS + EX527 (10 mg/kg) group, and the LPS + EX527 (10 mg/kg) + H‐XXXD (5.4 g/kg) group. The modeling process of LPS and the administration process of XXXD were the same as above. Among them, EX527 was intraperitoneally injected at the same time as the administration time of XXXD.

Preparation of the Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) for Cell Count

After drug treatment, all the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (i.p., 50 mg/kg). After collecting and centrifuging BALF samples, the amount of total leukocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages was determined using the supernatants. Once the trypan blue‐stained dead cells were eliminated, we could count the total number of inflammatory cells by squaring the cells in at least five squares of a hemocytometer. 100 μL of each solution was spun onto a slide after the cell pellets had been suspended in 0.5 mL PBS. Following drying, the cells were ready for staining by using Diff‐Quik Stain reagents (Polysciences Asia Pacific Inc.) and following the manufacturer's instructions.

Lung Wet/Dry (W/D) Weight Measurement

The mice were killed following the collection of BALF. Initially, the mice's body weights in each of the six groups were measured. The fresh lung tissues were then taken out, and the moist weight was calculated. Dry weight was calculated after the tissues were dried for 72 h at 60°C. In the end, the mouse lungs' W/D ratio was determined.

Measurements of Cytokines in BALF, Serum and Cell Supernatant, and MPO Activity in Lung Tissues

Using the appropriate ELISA kit and the manufacturer's instructions, the levels of TNF‐α, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐10, and IL‐1β in BALF, serum, and cell media were determined. The specific methods to achieve the following: After balancing the kit at room temperature for 30 min, take out the required strips from the aluminum foil bag, and seal the remaining strips in a self‐sealing bag and return them to 4°C. Set up the standard wells and sample wells, and add 50 μL of standard substances of different concentrations to each standard well. Add 50 μL of the sample to be tested to the sample wells, but not to the blank wells. Except for the blank wells, add 100 μL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐labeled detection antibody to each well of the standard and sample wells. Discard the liquid, pat dry on absorbent paper, add 350 μL of washing solution to each well, let stand for 1 min, discard the washing solution, pat dry on absorbent paper, and repeat this washing process 5 times. Add 50 μL of substrate A and 50 μL of substrate B to each well and incubate at 37°C in the dark for 15 min. Finally, 50 μL of stop solution was added to each well, and the OD450 value of each well was determined within 15 min. A myeloperoxidase assay kit was used to identify MPO activity in lung tissues. Weighed and homogenized with appropriate extraction buffers were the mouse lung tissues. The manufacturer's instructions were followed to take precise measurements. Finally, OD460 was used to quantify the MPO levels in lung tissues.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

To analyze histopathological alterations, lungs were preserved in 4% formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. A rotary microtome was used to slice the paraffin block containing the brain tissue into 4 μm pieces. Sections were successively cleared with xylene, dewaxed with xylene, dehydrated with varying concentrations of ethanol, stained with hematoxylin for the nucleus, differentiated with varying concentrations of differentiation medium, stained with eosin for the cytoplasm, dehydrated with varying ethanol concentrations, and mounted with neutral resin. Finally, a microscope (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, Germany) was used to look at the tissue sections.

XXXD‐Containing Serum Preparation

Randomly selected forty Sprague–Dawley rats were split into two groups: the XXXD group (n = 15) and the control group (n = 25). The rats had a standard diet, unrestricted access to tap water, and a room with air conditioning that was maintained at 22°C ± 2°C with 12 h light and dark cycles. XXXD (5.40 g/kg) was administered twice a day to the rats in the XXXD group for five days straight. Normal saline was given twice daily to rats in the control group for 5 days. Between the two groups, the amount of gavage remained constant. Within an hour after the final dosage, pentobarbital was injected intraperitoneally into the animals to make them unconscious. Blood samples from the abdominal aorta were extracted, and they were centrifuged for 15 min at 4000 rpm and 4°C. After gathering the serum, it was filtered using a 0.22 μm filter, thermally inactivated for 30 min at 56°C, and then kept at −80°C until needed.

Cell Culture and Treatment

In Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, A549 cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2. Following 80%–90% culture time, the cells were digested using pancreatic enzymes and subsequently seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL onto 6‐well plates. After 24 h of culture, the cells were treated with 10 μg/mL LPS for 24 h, and the LPS‐injured cell model was established. The cells of the control group were not modeled, and the XXXD groups were treated with different concentrations of XXXD‐containing serum after modeling, and samples were collected 24 h later for detection.

After being resuspended to reach a cell density of 2 × 105 cells/mL, A549 cells in the logarithmic development phase were sown in 4 mL/well within 60 mm Petri dishes and cultured overnight in a 37°C environment with 5% CO2. Using siRNA targeting SIRT1 or the appropriate control vector, A549 cells were transfected on the second day. The cells were grown in the new culture medium for a duration of 48 h after the original medium was changed after 24 h. Western blot analysis was used to confirm the effectiveness of transfection. General Biology (Anhui, China) Co. LTD was the source of both the siRNAs and the corresponding control vectors.

Cell Viability Assay

After plating A549 cells in 96‐well plates, we assessed the vitality of the cells using the MTT assay. After treatment, each well received MTT (0.5 mg/mL), and the wells were incubated for 3 h at 37°C. After adding 150 μL DMSO, the mixture was incubated for 15 min. Finally, OD values were determined by using an enzymic labeled meter (M5, MD, USA) at a 570 nm wavelength.

Immunofluorescence

After first treating the samples of fixed cells or tissues with 4% paraformaldehyde, they underwent steps including drying, paraffin embedding, slicing, deparaffinization, hydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, further to a primary antibody incubation at 4°C for the whole night. Sections were exposed to three PBS washes and then 1 h at 37°C to FITC‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Then, sections were seen under a fluorescence microscope (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, Germany) following an additional washing and mounting process using a DAPI‐containing fade‐resistant solution.

Western Blot Analysis

To extract all of the protein from cells or lung tissues, PMSF, protease inhibitors, and phosphatase inhibitors were mixed with the RIPA cell lysis solution. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA protein assay kit, and equal amounts of protein were put onto SDS‐PAGE gels, separated, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated for the primary antibody incubation for an overnight period at 4°C after being blocked with 5% BSA. After three TBST washes, goat anti‐mouse or goat anti‐rabbit antibodies labeled with HRP were applied to the membranes, and they were left to incubate for an hour at room temperature. Following three TBST washes, peroxidase‐labeled protein bands were found using an ECL kit and captured by the ChemiDoc XRS system (Chemi Doc XRS+, Bio‐Rad, USA). The protein's intensity was measured using Image J software.

TUNEL Staining

To find evidence of cell apoptosis in lung tissue, TUNEL labeling was employed. Using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, Germany), we studied fluorescence staining after applying DAPI staining to nuclei. Lastly, the total number of cells and TUNEL‐positive cells was counted using Image‐J.

Flow Cytometry for Apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was identified using the Annexin V apoptosis assay, and the product's operating instructions were adhered to. To put it briefly, 5 × 105 cells/mL were centrifuged, re‐suspended in PI solution, and then exposed to Annexin V‐FITC solution for 15 min in the dark. Following the addition of Binding Buffer to the resuspension, the multicolor analytical flow cytometer (Gallios, Beckman, USA) was used to detect it. Within an hour, the level of cell apoptosis was identified.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical studies were conducted with the aid of GraphPad Prism 8.0. Error bars were presented as Mean ± SD. After doing a one‐way ANOVA, Tukey's post hoc test was run to determine the statistical significance across multiple groups. p < 0.05 was used to indicate significance, and p < 0.01 was regarded as extremely significant.

Results

Ingredients Analysis of XXXD

Utilizing diode array detection coupled with high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC‐DAD), the primary component profile of XXXD was investigated. The mobile phase systems were acetonitrile and 0.025% formic acid in water, and the HPLC chromatogram was produced using a gradient elution technique. By comparing the UV spectra and retention periods of five components of XXXD to genuine standards, the constituents were determined. As indicated in Figure 1, the four components of XXXD were Adenosine, Palmatine, Berberine, and Cucurbitacin B. Then, the chemical profile of XXXD was analyzed by UPLC‐Q Exactive Orbitrap MS with the full MS/dd‐MS2 scan mode. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) in positive and negative ion modes of XXXD is displayed in Figure 2. Using the data processing program TraceFinder and pertinent literature, retention time, precise and high‐resolution quality, and MS/MS fragments were compared in order to screen unknown compounds. XXXD Protects Lung Tissues Against LPS‐Induced Pneumonia

The effects of XXXD on pulmonary histology, lung W/D ratio, and MPO activity in LPS‐induced pneumonia mice were investigated initially. As shown in Figure 3C,D, in comparison to the sham group, LPS treatment severely damaged the lung tissues with the phenomena of interstitial edema, inflammatory cells entering the interstitium and alveolar gaps, as well as thickening of the alveolar wall. On the contrary, morphological changes were markedly inhibited after XXXD or positive drug DEX treatment. Based on the results of the lung W/D ratio, LPS treatment significantly elevated the lung wet/dry ratio. Interestingly, treatment with XXXD or DEX reversed this phenomenon, and the effect of XXXD was concentration‐dependent (Figure 3B). Generally, MPO was considered a specific biomarker of neutrophils, indirectly reflecting the increases in neutrophil accumulation in lung tissues [28]. XXXD also down‐regulated an LPS‐induced increase in MPO activity in lung tissues (Figure 3E). To further understand the role of XXXD in LPS‐induced acute pneumonia mice, we measured total leukocytes, macrophage, and neutrophils counts in BALF. In comparison with the sham, the total number of leukocytes, macrophage, and neutrophils significantly increased in the model group (Figure 3F–H). However, BALF cell count was markedly reduced after XXXD treatment compared with the model group (Figure 3F–H). The findings indicated that XXXD had a protective effect against LPS‐induced pneumonia mice.

XXXD Attenuates Inflammatory Responses in BALF and Serum of LPS‐Induced Pneumonia Mice

To investigate the effect of XXXD on inflammatory responses, we examined typical cytokines including IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐4, and IL‐10 in serum and BALF by ELISA. The findings demonstrated a considerable rise in the levels of pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory factors in the model group's serum or BALF as compared to the sham group (Figure 4). Then, after treatment with XXXD or the positive drug DEX, the elevation of pro‐inflammatory factors IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α in serum or BALF was significantly reduced, and the effect of XXXD was concentration‐dependent (Figure 4A–C,F–H). On the contrary, anti‐inflammatory factors IL‐4, IL‐10 in serum or BALF continued to rise after treatment with XXXD or the positive drug DEX compared to the model group, and the effects of XXXD remained concentration‐dependent (Figure 4D,E,I,J). Together, these results signified that XXXD could reduce the inflammatory response in mice with pneumonia.

XXXD Reduces the Level of Apoptosis in Lung Tissues of LPS‐Induced Pneumonia Mice

Inflammation is directly linked to apoptosis and can trigger cell apoptosis [29]. Thus, the levels of apoptosis in lung tissues of LPS‐induced pneumonia mice were detected by TUNEL staining and Western Blot. As shown in Figure 5A,B, LPS promoted apoptosis in lung tissue in the model group compared with the sham group, but this phenomenon was partially reversed after XXXD treatment in a dose‐dependent manner. To further identify apoptosis in lung tissues, the expression of caspase 3 and c‐caspase 3, two proteins linked to apoptosis, was then investigated using a Western Blot technique (Figure 5C). As represented in Figure 5D, the ratio of c‐caspase 3/caspase 3 was substantially higher in the model group. However, these effects were significantly reversed after XXXD treatment, and XXXD was dose‐dependent. Overall, these results suggested that XXXD inhibited apoptosis to relieve LPS‐induced pneumonia in mice.

XXXD Drove the Activation of SIRT1 in LPS‐Induced Pneumonia Mice

To further elucidate the underlying anti‐inflammatory mechanism of XXXD in mice with pneumonia, we detected the protein expression of the SIRT1 signaling pathway by Western Blot. According to the findings in Figure 6A–D, in contrast to the sham group, the model group's SIRT1 expression level was much lower, but it increased noticeably after receiving XXXD therapy. Furthermore, in comparison to the control group, the levels of p66shac and p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB were significantly higher in the model group. Still, however, XXXD therapy reversed this alteration, and its impact was dose‐dependent. After that, to ascertain the expression of SIRT1 in lung tissues, we employed immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Figure 6E,F, in comparison to the sham group, the model group's SIRT1 expression dramatically dropped, but XXXD could reverse this phenomenon in a dose‐dependent manner. According to the consistent results of immunofluorescence staining and Western Blot, XXXD may inhibit the inflammatory response by stimulating the SIRT1 pathway, which would help to lessen the severity of LPS‐induced pneumonia in mice.

XXXD Inhibits Inflammatory Response and Cell Apoptosis in LPS‐Induced A549 Cells

To better understand the protective mechanism of XXXD in pneumonia, we investigated its effects on the inflammatory response and apoptosis in LPS‐induced A549 cells in vitro. Initially, the MTT assay was employed to determine how various XXXD‐containing serum affects cell viability. The findings demonstrated that LPS treatment considerably reduced cell viability in the model group as compared to the control group, but different concentrations of XXXD‐containing serum significantly increased cell viability (Figure 7A). Then, we employed ELISA to identify pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory factors in each group's A549 cell supernatant. According to Figure 7B–D, the model group's cell supernatant exhibited considerably higher levels of pro‐inflammatory factors, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, as compared to the control group. However, after treatment with different concentrations of XXXD‐containing serum, these pro‐inflammatory factors were observably reduced in a concentration‐dependent manner. In addition, compared with the control group, the anti‐inflammatory factors IL‐10 and IL‐4 in the cell supernatant were also increased in the model group, but there was no statistical significance (Figure 7E,F). Nevertheless, compared to the model group, these anti‐inflammatory factors were significantly elevated in a concentration‐dependent manner after treatment with different concentrations of XXXD‐containing serum. These results further suggested that XXXD can inhibit the LPS‐induced inflammatory response of A549 cells and thus play an anti‐inflammatory role.

Subsequently, we utilized flow cytometry and Western Blot to investigate the impact of XXXD on the cell apoptosis of LPS‐induced A549 cells. The results of the flow cytometry showed that, in comparison to the control group, there was a considerable increase in cell apoptosis in the model group, but different concentrations of serum containing XXXD could partially reverse this phenomenon in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 8A,B). Afterward, a Western Blot approach was used to investigate the expression of the apoptosis‐related proteins caspase 3 and c‐caspase 3, with the goal of better identifying cell apoptosis. The model group had a significantly greater ratio of c‐caspase 3/caspase 3 in comparison to the control group, as illustrated in Figure 8C,D. However, the rise was partially reversed after treatment with different concentrations of serum containing XXXD and was dose‐dependent. These findings confirmed that XXXD prevented LPS‐induced A549 cells from going through apoptosis.

The Effect of XXXD on SIRT1 Signaling Pathway in LPS‐Induced A549 Cells

To better explore whether XXXD improves the mechanism of pneumonia by activating SIRT1, we further detect the expression of SIRT1 and its downstream signaling pathway in LPS‐induced A549 cells in vitro. Firstly, the protein expression levels of SIRT1 and its downstream signaling pathway were detected by Western Blot. According to the results in Figure 9A,B, compared with the control group, the expression of SIRT1 in the model group was markedly decreased, but this decline was reversed after treatment with different concentrations of serum containing XXXD in a concentration‐dependent manner. On the contrary, the downstream p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB and p66shc expression levels of SIRT1 were significantly increased in the model group but dropped following treatment with serum containing XXXD in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 9A,C,D). Then, immunofluorescence staining was used to detect the expression of SIRT1, and the results were consistent with Western Blot; that is, the model group's SIRT1 expression level dropped, but the decrease was reversed in a concentration‐dependent manner after treatment with serum containing XXXD (Figure 9E,F). These results corroborated those of the previously reported in vivo investigations and added evidence that XXXD could improve pneumonia by acting as an anti‐inflammatory by way of stimulating the SIRT1 pathway.

XXXD Improves LPS‐Induced Pneumonia by Activating SIRT1

To further investigate whether XXXD plays an anti‐inflammatory role by activating SIRT1, thereby improving pneumonia, we conducted the following in vivo studies using the SIRT1 inhibitor EX527. Initially, we used Western Blot to examine whether EX527 affected the effect of XXXD on SIRT1 signaling pathway proteins and apoptosis. As shown in Figure 10A–D, compared with the model group, XXXD markedly increased the expression of SIRT1 and decreased the expression of p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB and c‐caspase 3/caspase 3. However, after EX527 treatment, these effects of XXXD were almost eliminated. Next, we measured IL‐6 and IL‐1β levels in serum and BALF by ELISA. As demonstrated by the results, XXXD was able to considerably lower the levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines in the serum and BALF of LPS‐induced mice, but the effect disappeared after EX527 treatment (Figure 10E–H). These results suggested that XXXD acts on SIRT1 to produce anti‐inflammatory effects, which lessens the severity of LPS‐induced pneumonia in mice.

Discussion

Pneumonia is a widespread, complex, and dangerous inflammatory illness of the lungs caused by bacteria, fungi, and viruses that affects millions of people worldwide and is associated with quality‐of‐life issues and financial burdens [30, 31]. Therefore, the search for effective and safe treatment strategies is still an urgent problem to be solved, and thus, various treatment options are concerned by researchers, especially traditional Chinese medicine treatment of pneumonia [32]. At present, TCM has been widely used in the treatment of pneumonia, and there have been many studies to explore the mechanism of TCM in treating pneumonia. For instance, Tian et al. studied the immune and metabolic modulation of Qing‐Fei‐Pai‐Du decoction in mice with coronavirus‐induced pneumonia [33]. Compared with other TCM prescriptions for treating pneumonia, XXXD from the “Treatise on Febrile Disease” is a widely used clinical prescription that has been shown to be effective in the treatment of pneumonia [24]. The unique feature of XXXD in treating pneumonia lies in its ability to clear heat, eliminate phlegm, expand the chest, and disperse lumps. Furthermore, berberine, the main compound in XXXD, has been demonstrated in research to reduce pulmonary inflammation in mice with influenza virus pneumonia by preventing gasdermin D‐mediated pyroptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [34]. However, the exact mechanism by which XXXD treats pneumonia remains largely unknown. This study focused on the regulatory mechanism of XXXD on the progression of pneumonia, which confirmed that XXXD ameliorated the inflammatory response caused by pneumonia. This research may provide more experimental evidence for the clinical application of XXXD or its active ingredients.

LPS is an endotoxin produced by type G bacteria and is commonly used to induce pneumonia in experiments [35]. MPO is an enzyme mostly present in neutrophilic granulocytes that has considerable antibacterial activity, which is thought to be an essential indication of neutrophilic infiltration [36, 37]. In our study, we first explored the protective effect of XXXD against pneumonia by establishing a mouse model of LPS‐induced pneumonia. In animal experiments, the dosage of traditional Chinese medicine decoctions is generally calculated as 9.1 times the dosage used by humans in clinical practice. In this study, according to the formula, it was assumed that the average human weight was 65 kg and the drug in the prescription was 38 g. The dose for the animal experiment was calculated to be 5.32 g/kg. Therefore, ultimately, 5.4 g/kg was chosen as the highest dose for the research in this study. We discovered that, in a concentration‐dependent way, XXXD attenuated the effects of LPS on lung W/D ratio, histological alterations, MPO activity, and the total leukocyte, macrophage, and neutrophil counts in BALF. The above findings suggested that XXXD treatment can lessen inflammatory infiltration and protect mice from LPS‐induced pneumonia.

Nevertheless, the above findings alone are not sufficient to fully elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of XXXD in pneumonia. The exact mechanism of XXXD treatment of pneumonia still needs to be further studied. The pathogenesis of pneumonia is complex, often accompanied by inflammation, which seriously impairs the function of lung tissue [38]. There is mounting evidence to suggest that inflammatory responses play a key role in the pathogenesis of pneumonia. For instance, Qian et al. have shown that pinocembrin alleviates pneumonia generated by mycoplasma pneumoniae infection in mice by suppressing the inflammatory response [39]. In addition, UCF‐101, an inhibitor of High‐temperature requirement A2, has been shown to reduce the inflammatory response of LPS‐induced acute pneumonia in rats and may be a potential candidate for the treatment of pneumonia [40]. The results of the previous part of this study showed that XXXD can reduce the effect of LPS on inflammatory cells. Nonetheless, research on the possibility that XXXD treats pneumonia by controlling inflammation has not yet been conducted. Thus, we looked at the levels of pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory factors in the serum and BALF of mice given LPS, as well as in the supernatant of LPS‐induced A549 cells. We found that XXXD reduced the increase of LPS on pro‐inflammatory factors in a concentration‐dependent manner and promoted the release of anti‐inflammatory factors. This further suggested that XXXD may play a protective role against LPS‐induced pneumonia in mice by regulating the inflammatory response. Pneumonia can lead to pro‐inflammatory cytokine release, neutrophil infiltration, lung tissue destruction, and eventually apoptosis [41]. As a result, apoptosis was detected in lung tissue and A549 cells using TUNEL labeling and flow cytometry, respectively, and the apoptosis‐related proteins caspase and c‐caspase were measured for expression using Western Blot. The outcomes supported the hypothesis that XXXD might considerably lessen the impact of LPS on apoptosis.

The SIRT1 signaling pathway is critical to the inflammatory response [42]. Xu et al. demonstrated that HDAC4 deletion improves IL‐13‐induced inflammation by activating the SIRT1/NF‐κB signaling pathway [43]. Liu et al. found that hydrogen sulfide prevents particle‐induced inflammation in prosthesis loosening through the SIRT1 pathway [44]. Another study showed that Sphingosine kin‐1 (SPHK1) promotes inflammation in infantile pneumonia by regulating the expression of SIRT1 [45]. Additionally, research has demonstrated that the main component in XXXD, berberine, may work through SIRT1 to promote cell cleaning for lifespan and health [46]. Interestingly, our findings that XXXD significantly increased the expression of SIRT1 in LPS‐induced pneumonia mice in vivo and in LPS‐induced A549 cells in vitro and significantly decreased the expression of p66shc and p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB downstream of SIRT1 suggest that XXXD may play a protective role against pneumonia by promoting the expression of SIRT1. However, in this study, we did not investigate other downstream pathways of SIRT1 (such as Nrf2), and further studies could be conducted in the future.

To further confirm the hypothesis that XXXD ameliorates pneumonia by inhibiting the inflammatory response through up‐regulation of SIRT1, we used the SIRT1 inhibitor EX527 in an LPS‐induced mouse model of pneumonia and interfered with the SIRT1 gene in LPS‐induced A549 cells. The results showed that XXXD greatly reduced the inflammatory response without interfering with SIRT1, but once SIRT1 was interfered with, this effect largely disappeared. This situation further proves that XXXD may inhibit the inflammatory response mainly by up‐regulating the expression of SIRT1, which in turn alleviates pneumonia. Nevertheless, whether XXXD activating SIRT1 and inhibiting the inflammatory response will have a therapeutic effect on other lung diseases (such as ARDS, COVID) remains to be further studied in the future. Additionally, given the limitations of the LPS‐induced pneumonia model used in this study, which cannot fully simulate human pneumonia, as well as the complexity of the active ingredients in XXXD, in the future, it would be possible to analyze which components in XXXD are responsible for the effect and conduct tests in infection‐based models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we discovered that XXXD may improve pneumonia by suppressing the inflammatory response through the upregulation of SIRT1 expression. While further mechanistic investigations are necessary, this discovery opens up a potential therapy option for pneumonia. Considering how common pneumonia is, XXXD may have a lot of therapeutic potential. However, the limitation of this study is that it did not delve into the interaction between XXXD and SIRT1, the role of individual components in XXXD, which component of XXXD had the most significant effect on pneumonia, and the use of an infection‐based model. In future studies, we will further explore these issues.
