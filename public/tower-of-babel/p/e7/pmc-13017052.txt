Osimertinib induces reversible cardiac dysfunction through the GATA4-MYLK3-MYL2 axis
Authors: Kai Zhang, Alyssa Ayala, Ignacio Norambuena-Soto, Vertica Agnihotri, Tao Shu, Christian Nenninger, Yin Wang, Brittany Echevarria, Joel Aboagye, Katie Huang, Jenaro Espitia-Corredor, Xiwei Wu, Zhen B Chen, Yingfeng Deng, J Jefferson P Perry, Dan J Raz, Huaxiao Yang, Paul Cheng, Guosheng Fu, Zhao V Wang, June-Wha Rhee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsOsimertinib is a third-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting activating mutations of epidermal growth factor receptor with remarkable therapeutic efficacy against non-small cell lung carcinoma. However, its use has been limited by associated cardiotoxicity, primarily with heart failure. Herein, this study aims to better understand the mechanisms underlying osimertinib cardiotoxicity and explore cardioprotective strategies.MethodsThis study leverages an in vitro model of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) and a clinically relevant in vivo mouse model of osimertinib cardiotoxicity by co-employing transverse aortic constriction to mimic haemodynamic stress in cancer patients.ResultsOsimertinib treatment leads to significant contractile dysfunction in mice without cell death, inflammation, or fibrosis. By leveraging single-nucleus RNA sequencing of mouse heart tissues and in vitro assays of human iPSC-CMs, the study reveals significant downregulation of MYLK3 and a subsequent decrease in MYL2 phosphorylation with marked sarcomere disarray as the main mechanism of osimertinib cardiotoxicity. GATA4 is further identified as a putative target of osimertinib, connecting its decreased phosphorylation to repressed MYLK3 transcription. The reversibility of osimertinib-induced cardiac dysfunction upon discontinuation of osimertinib treatment supports the hypothesis that transient sarcomere disruption, rather than permanent cellular damage, serves as the key underlying mechanism. Finally, the myosin activator omecamtiv is shown to be effective in preventing osimertinib cardiotoxicity.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that osimertinib causes reversible cardiac dysfunction by disrupting MYL2 phosphorylation via GATA4 dephosphorylation-mediated suppression of MYLK3 and highlight the potential of myosin activation as a preventive or rescue strategy for osimertinib cardiotoxicity.

Osimertinib significantly increases the risk of systolic heart failure, compromising both oncologic and cardiovascular outcomes. By harnessing in vitro human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes and clinically relevant in vivo mouse models, the study first finds marked sarcomere disarray caused by MYLK3 downregulation as the main mechanism of osimertinib cardiotoxicity. The reversibility of osimertinib cardiotoxicity upon discontinuation of osimertinib further supports the notion of temporal sarcomere disruption as the main mechanism of osimertinib cardiotoxicity without permanent cellular injuries or pathologic remodelling. Finally, myosin activator omecamtiv may serve as a preventive or rescue therapy for osimertinib cardiotoxicity, highlighting its translational potential.

Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have revolutionized cancer treatment with improved survival across a wide spectrum of cancers.1,2 However, the use of TKIs has been hindered by significant and broad cardiotoxicity, ranging from asymptomatic reduction in left ventricular performance to highly symptomatic, advanced congestive heart failure.3–5 Tyrosine kinase inhibitor–induced cardiotoxicity can significantly limit its use in clinic and jeopardize cancer outcomes since discontinuation of TKI treatment, which is the only option due to cardiotoxicity, can lead to incomplete cancer treatment and potential worsening or recurrence of cancer.4,5

Osimertinib is a third-generation TKI targeting activating mutations of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). Osimertinib has been highly effective in treating metastatic non-small cell lung carcinoma, with improved progression-free survival.6–8 More recently, its use in the adjuvant setting has shown to markedly improve lung cancer survival following resection.9 Moreover, osimertinib has been explored to treat other cancer types that may harbour EGFR mutations, such as advanced colorectal cancer.10 However, growing clinical evidence suggests that osimertinib treatment significantly increases the risk of systolic heart failure, compromising both oncologic and cardiovascular outcomes.11–14 Patients with concurrent hypertension and osimertinib treatment would be at a particularly increased risk of cardiotoxicity.15 Cumulative epidemiological findings showed that osimertinib cardiotoxicity is largely reversible upon withdrawal of the therapy, without significant myocardial injury, fibrosis, and inflammation in humans.16,17 Despite the increasing clinical reports of osimertinib cardiotoxicity, our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanism is missing.

Herein, we delineate the mechanism of osimertinib cardiotoxicity by leveraging in vitro human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) and in vivo mouse models. We then identify clinically relevant therapeutics to ameliorate osimertinib toxicity in the heart.

Methods

Detailed experimental procedures are provided in Supplementary data online, Materials and Methods.

Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6N mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. All mouse procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Beckman Research Institute, City of Hope National Medical Center. All mice were maintained in a 12:12 h light: dark cycle in temperature-controlled rooms with free access to water and chow food.

Transverse aortic constriction

Transverse aortic constriction (TAC) was performed on 7–8-week-old (22–25 g) male C57BL/6N mice as previously described18 to induce pressure overload. Briefly, mice were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg BW) and xylazine (5 mg/kg BW). Instruments, supplies, and suture materials were sterilized before use. The left chest was opened to identify the aortic arch when the mouse had no toe-pinch reflex. A 26-gauge needle with 6-0 silk sutures was used to ligate the aorta between the innominate and left carotid artery. The needle was immediately removed after ligation, and the chest was closed. Animals were observed during recovery from anaesthesia and returned to the animal facility for housing. Sham operation was conducted in the same procedure without ligature constriction.

Differentiation of induced pluripotent stem cells into beating cardiomyocytes

Beating iPSC-CMs were differentiated using the chemically defined Wnt modulation protocol as previously described.19 Differentiated iPSC-CMs were maintained in culture until Day 30 before conducting functional and expression analyses.

Osimertinib and myosin modulator treatment

Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes were incubated with various concentrations of osimertinib (MedChemExpress, HY-15772, 0–10 µM). To examine cellular recovery following osimertinib treatment, osimertinib-treated iPSC-CMs were subjected to regular medium for the following 7 days. To evaluate the effects of myosin modulators in either rescuing or exacerbating contractile function, osimertinib-treated iPSC-CMs were co-incubated with or without myosin modulators for 7 days. Omecamtiv mecarbil (Selleck, CK-1827452, 0–1 μM) and mavacamten (Selleck, MYK-461, 0–1 μM) were used, respectively.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing and analysis

Eight mouse single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) libraries were prepared from four treatment groups (n = 2 per group)—sham, osimertinib (Osi), TAC, and TAC-Osi—using Next GEM 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at Translational Genomics Research Institute (TGen), an affiliate of the City of Hope. FASTQs were processed using Cell Ranger (v7.0.1) count pipeline vs mouse reference index refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A (downloaded from: https://cf.10xgenomics.com/supp/cell-exp/refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A.tar.gz) to generate raw count matrices, which were input to Seurat (v4.1.0) R package for subsequent analysis. Information regarding (i) quality control and clustering, (ii) reference- and marker-based cell type annotation, and (iii) differentially expressed gene analysis can be found under Supplementary data online, Materials and Methods.

Statistical analysis

If data were normally distributed, a Student’s t-test was used to compare the differences between two data sets. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, and post hoc test was used for all pairwise comparisons, depending on the properties of the data sets. A P < .05 was considered to be statistically significant. Unless otherwise stated, all data in bar graphs were presented as mean ± standard error.

Results

Osimertinib induces cardiac dysfunction in a mouse model in vivo

While osimertinib cardiotoxicity is a well-recognized clinical problem, severe cardiac dysfunction remains relatively rare, with an incidence reported ranging 3%–5%.12 Importantly, increased afterload and subsequent myocardial tension (e.g. hypertension) has been recognized as an essential risk factor for TKI-induced cardiac dysfunction.15,20 To model cardiotoxic effects of osimertinib, we therefore employed modest TAC21 to elicit haemodynamic stress at a degree that alone does not cause significant cardiac dysfunction18 (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1A–C). One week after either sham or TAC, we administrated osimertinib by daily oral gavage. Two different doses of osimertinib (25 and 50 mg/kg/day) were tested based on prior studies with comparable clinical dosing.22,23 We found that the lower dose, 25 mg/kg/day, did not significantly affect body weight gain (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1D), in contrast to the higher dose (50 mg/kg/day) which showed generalized toxicity.

Hence, we chose the lower dose of osimertinib (25 mg/kg/day) for subsequent experiments (Figure 1A). Echocardiographic analysis showed that osimertinib treatment, when combined with TAC, caused a significant decline in cardiac systolic performance, as revealed by decreases in both ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) (Figure 1B and C; Supplementary data online, Figure S2A). Although osimertinib alone, in the absence of TAC, also resulted in a statistically significant decline in systolic function compared with no treatment, the magnitude of this decline was modest and not expected to be of clinical significance. Importantly, heart rate was not affected (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2B) whereas left ventricular inner diameter at systole (LVIDs) was enlarged (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2C). Further, cardiac hypertrophic growth was not different between vehicle and osimertinib groups (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2D), indicating pathological cardiac remodelling was not exacerbated by osimertinib. Additionally, there was no apparent myocardial injury, inflammation, or fibrosis across all treatment groups (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2E). In accordance with the depression of heart performance, the ratio of lung weight/body weight (LW/BW), an indicator of cardiac function, was significantly elevated in the osimertinib treatment group under pressure overload (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2F). At the cellular level, histological analysis using wheat germ agglutinin staining revealed a modest but statistically significant increase in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area in the TAC groups, indicating activation of hypertrophic signalling in the setting of TAC (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3A). However, osimertinib treatment did not result in a significant difference in the cellular area. Otherwise, gene expression analyses of key hypertrophy and fibrosis markers revealed no significant alterations, supporting the absence of exacerbated pathological remodelling by osimertinib treatment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3B). Moreover, we did not observe significant fibrosis, cell death, or immune cell infiltration upon osimertinib treatment on histological assessment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3C). Taken together, these findings suggest that osimertinib leads to cardiomyopathy and heart failure in response to haemodynamic stress in vivo.

Osimertinib downregulates myosin light chain kinase 3

To investigate the molecular mechanism of osimertinib cardiotoxicity, we performed snRNA-seq analysis of mouse left ventricular tissues. The following groups were chosen, sham/vehicle (S-V), sham/osimertinib (S-O), TAC/vehicle (T-V), and TAC/osimertinib (T-O) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S4A). Data of all nuclei were pooled with an average of ∼10 000 nuclei per mouse sample. The nuclei capture efficiency and sequencing quality, including read quality, coverage and depth, and mapping rate, were comparable across all heart samples (see Supplementary data online, Figure S4B–D).

We then conducted unsupervised clustering (a total of 11 clusters) to annotate the respective cell populations. Using cell type-specific gene markers,24 we annotated major cell types of the ventricular myocardium, including ventricular cardiomyocyte, fibroblast, endothelial cell, smooth muscle cell, and immune cell clusters (Figure 2A; Supplementary data online, Figure S5A). We observed that cell type distribution was similar across all treatment groups (Figure 2B; Supplementary data online, Figure S5B). Importantly, the ventricular cardiomyocyte cluster had the most apparent gene expression changes following osimertinib treatment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S5C). Pathway analysis (Enrichr25–27) of the cardiomyocyte cluster comparing osimertinib vs vehicle treatments demonstrated that cardiac muscle development, contraction, and cardiac myofibril assembly were primarily dysregulated following osimertinib treatment (Figure 2C). We did not observe significant changes in genes and pathways related to cell death and inflammation (see Supplementary data online, Figure S6A and data not shown), suggesting that cardiomyocyte cell death is not significantly involved in osimertinib cardiotoxicity. Next, examining individual gene changes of osimertinib vs sham treatments, we found marked downregulation of myosin light chain kinase 3 (Mylk3), also known as cardiac myosin light chain kinase (cMlck),28,29 by osimertinib treatment, without significant changes in the expression of myosin light chain 2 (Myl2) (Figure 2D; Supplementary data online, Figure S7), a substrate of Mylk3. The trend of Mylk3 downregulation was further augmented when we compared gene expression among TAC mice treated with osimertinib vs vehicle (Figure 2E). Additionally, there was apparent upregulation of genes implicated in myocardial stress by osimertinib, such as Ankrd1, Nppa, and Nppb (see Supplementary data online, Figure S6B). Otherwise, no significant changes in metabolism-related genes were observed (see Supplementary data online, Figure S6C).

Osimertinib causes profound sarcomere disarray and contractile dysfunction

To further investigate the cardiotoxic effect of osimertinib, we used human iPSC lines derived from two healthy control subjects: SCVI 111 (data presented in main figures) and SCVI 274 (data presented in Supplementary Data). The iPSCs were differentiated into beating cardiomyocytes (CMs),19 and Day 30+ iPSC-CMs were subjected to treatment with increasing doses of osimertinib for up to 7 days. Importantly, viability remained >90% at a dose near a reported serum maximal concentration in humans (∼0.1–1 µM)30 (see Supplementary data online, Figure S8A and B). As the mouse snRNA-seq gene expression pathway analysis suggested dysregulated sarcomere and contractile dysfunction, we examined the degree of sarcomere disorganization in response to osimertinib using immunofluorescent staining for cardiac troponin (cTnT) and α-actinin. We observed marked sarcomere disarray with evidence of sarcomere fragmentation and aggregation following osimertinib treatment (Figure 3A; Supplementary data online, Figure S8C).

Next, we examined contractile function of iPSC-CMs in response to osimertinib treatment, using high frame rate video microscopy recordings of beating iPSC-CMs acquired and analysed with the IonOptix system.31 We performed daily assessments of cardiomyocyte contractility after osimertinib treatment (1 µM). Our data indicate that contractile dysfunction, measured as departure velocity, becomes evident between Days 3 and 4 following osimertinib treatment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S8D). In parallel, we evaluated relaxation kinetics, showing that diastolic abnormalities, measured as return velocity, emerged slightly later, around Days 5 to 6 (see Supplementary data online, Figure S8E). These findings suggest that systolic impairment precedes diastolic dysfunction, indicating that impaired contractility is likely the primary effect of osimertinib. We next evaluated the dose-dependent effect of osimertinib on systolic measures of the two tested iPSC-CM lines. Contractile dysfunction, as revealed by decreases in area under the curve (AUC), peak height, and departure velocity, was apparent in a dose-dependent manner, with 10 µM of osimertinib causing complete beating arrest (Figure 3B; Supplementary data online, Figure S8F). These IonOptix-based measurements were subsequently validated by analysing 2D iPSC-CMs with MUSCLEMOTION31 (see Supplementary data online, Figure S8G) and by quantifying physical distance of contraction of engineered heart tissues in 3D (see Supplementary data online, Figure S8H).

GATA4 is a putative target of osimertinib

We then asked whether MYLK3 protein level changes in osimertinib-treated iPSC-CMs. Consistent with the mouse snRNA-seq data, we observed a strong reduction in MYLK3 protein expression following osimertinib treatment (Figure 3C). MYLK3 is a primary kinase to phosphorylate MYL2 in cardiomyocytes, which causes conformational changes of myosin head, thereby facilitating myosin-actin binding to initiate contraction.32 Given the observed MYLK3 downregulation, we asked whether the degree of MYL2 phosphorylation may change following osimertinib treatment. We found markedly decreased levels of MYL2 protein expression as well as its phosphorylation following osimertinib treatment in both tested iPSC-CM lines (Figure 3C; Supplementary data online, Figure S9A).

Given the marked sarcomere disarray observed, we investigated whether osimertinib treatment would lead to total sarcomere loss, by performing a time-course analysis of two key sarcomere components, cTnT and α-actinin. We compared their expression patterns to those of MYLK3 and phosphorylated MYL2 (pMYL2) following osimertinib treatment in iPSC-CMs. Whereas osimertinib induced a progressive decline in MYLK3 expression and MYL2 phosphorylation over time, we did not observe a consistent reduction in cTnT and α-actinin levels (see Supplementary data online, Figure S9B). These molecular findings aligned with immunostaining results, which revealed progressive disruption of sarcomere architecture which occurred in parallel with the decline in MYLK3 and pMYL2 levels (see Supplementary data online, Figure S9C). Collectively, these results suggest that contractile dysfunction caused by osimertinib treatment is likely due to structural disorganization of sarcomere mediated by alterations in the MYLK3–pMYL2 axis, rather than total sarcomere loss.

We then evaluated whether MYLK3 downregulation plays a causative role in mediating osimertinib cardiotoxicity. We employed MYLK3 overexpression by lentivirus transduction of iPSC-CMs. We found that MYLK3 overexpression sufficiently prevented osimertinib-induced contractile dysfunction and partly restored MYL2 phosphorylation (Figure 3D and E). To further examine the causative role of MYLK3 suppression in mediating cardiotoxicity in vivo, we induced adeno-associated virus 9-mediated overexpression (OE) of human MYLK3 in mice prior to the treatment with 3 weeks of daily oral gavage of osimertinib (see Supplementary data online, Figure S10A and B). Consistent with the in vitro findings, we found that MYLK3 OE effectively prevented osimertinib-induced cardiac dysfunction (see Supplementary data online, Figure S10C–E).

To evaluate the transcriptional regulation of MYLK3 under osimertinib treatment, we then examined gene regulatory network based on TRRUST transcription factor.33 We found that GATA4 ranked as the top transcription factor gene candidate, showing the strongest significance in transcriptional changes associated with osimertinib cardiotoxicity (see Supplementary data online, Figure S11A). Further analysis leveraging publicly available data set containing GATA4 ChIP-seq data of human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes34 identified MYLK3 as 1 of the top 10 potential targets of GATA4 (see Supplementary data online, Figure S11B). Consistently, we found that osimertinib treatment in iPSC-CMs reduced GATA4 protein level, with a more pronounced decrease in phosphorated GATA4 (Figure 3F). To explore whether osimertinib directly binds with GATA4, we conducted a cellular thermal shift assay.35 We found that osimertinib treatment resulted in significantly higher relative protein intensity of GATA4, with no corresponding change observed for MYLK3, MYL2, or GAPDH (see Supplementary data online, Figure S12), indicating an interaction between GATA4 and osimertinib. We next employed GATA4 overexpression by adenovirus36 transduction of iPSC-CMs, which increased MYLK3 expression (Figure 3G; Supplementary data online, Figure S11C). Importantly, we found that GATA4 overexpression effectively prevented osimertinib-induced contractile dysfunction (Figure 3H).

The aggregate of our findings suggests that osimertinib induces sarcomere disruption and contractile dysfunction through disrupted phosphorylation and subsequent downregulation of GATA4, which leads to MYLK3 downregulation and defective contractility.

Osimertinib cardiotoxicity is reversible

One of the prominent features of osimertinib cardiotoxicity is that cardiac dysfunction is often reversible and improved upon the discontinuation of therapy.16 Therefore, we sought to recapitulate this reversibility and investigate the underlying molecular mechanism. We first treated iPSC-CMs with osimertinib at 1 µM, a dose comparable with the serum maximal concentration, for 7 days to induce cardiomyocyte contractile dysfunction. We then subjected the osimertinib-treated iPSC-CMs to regular medium without osimertinib for another 7 days to allow cells to recover. While there was contractile dysfunction following osimertinib treatment at Day 7, we observed significant improvement of contractile function comparable with the baseline upon 7 days of recovery (Figure 4A; Supplementary data online, Figure S13A). Additionally, there was complete restoration of sarcomere array upon recovery (Figure 4B; Supplementary data online, Figure S13B). Moreover, GATA4 phosphorylation, MYLK3 protein level, and MYL2 phosphorylation levels were also partially recovered following osimertinib washout (Figure 4C; Supplementary data online, Figure S13C).

To corroborate these findings in vivo, mice treated with 3 weeks of daily oral gavage of osimertinib were allowed to recover without osimertinib for the following 3 weeks (Figure 4D). Consistent with the in vitro findings, cardiac ventricular EF and FS of osimertinib-treated mice nearly recovered to the level comparable with no treatment upon 3 weeks of osimertinib withdrawal (Figure 4E and F; Supplementary data online, Figure S14A). No change in heart rate was observed (see Supplementary data online, Figure S14B) and left ventricular inner diameters at both diastole and systole returned to control levels (see Supplementary data online, Figure S14C). Furthermore, we did not find differences in pathological cardiac hypertrophy (see Supplementary data online, Figure S15A). Consistent with the recovery of cardiac function, the ratio of LW/BW was decreased to the control level after cessation of osimertinib treatment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S15B), and no histological differences were detected (see Supplementary data online, Figure S15C and D). Collectively, these findings suggest that osimertinib induces reversible cardiac dysfunction without permanent injuries or adverse cardiac remodelling, a phenomenon consistent with clinical findings.

Osimertinib cardiotoxicity is rescued by myosin activator

While our data suggest that withdrawal of osimertinib is sufficient to restore cardiac function, this may not be an ideal or realistic solution in clinic as cancer treatment cannot be interrupted.4,5 Therefore, we sought to identify preventive or rescue therapies for osimertinib cardiotoxicity. First, according to the 2022 ESC Cardio-Oncology guidelines (Recommendation Table 5),37 angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), beta-blockers, and statins are recommended as primary preventions for patients with high risks of anti-cancer therapies induced heart failure. We therefore first tested potential cardioprotective effects of beta-blockers (carvedilol), ARB and neprilysin inhibitor [sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto)], and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin) for osimertinib cardiotoxicity. Co-treatments of these medications with osimertinib, however, did not prevent osimertinib-induced cardiac dysfunction (see Supplementary data online, Figure S16A–C). Therefore, we turned our attention to our cumulative data which show that osimertinib-induced cardiomyopathy is caused by reduced MYL2 phosphorylation, leading to presumed weak actin-myosin binding and resultant sarcomere disarray and contractile dysfunction. We hypothesized that drugs primarily targeting sarcomere such as myosin activator,38 which promotes actin-myosin binding, may prevent or rescue osimertinib cardiotoxicity. On the other hand, myosin inhibitor,38,39 which disrupts actin-myosin binding, may exacerbate cardiotoxicity.

To test this hypothesis, iPSC-CMs were co-treated with osimertinib and either omecamtiv (myosin activator) or mavacamten (myosin inhibitor) for 7 days. Overall viability remained relatively similar across all treatment groups although there was a trend for slight improvement of cell viability following co-treatment with omecamtiv and modest worsening following co-treatment with mavacamten (see Supplementary data online, Figure S17A and B). Importantly, omecamtiv co-treatment significantly improved contractile function compared with osimertinib treatment only, whereas mavacamten co-treatment markedly worsened contractile function (Figure 5A; Supplementary data online, Figure S7H and C). In addition to the quantitative measures of contractile function, the shapes of the contractile wave forms were also normalized following omecamtiv treatment, while exacerbated after mavacamten treatment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S18). There was restoration of sarcomere array following omecamtiv co-treatment while we observed deterioration of sarcomere array following mavacamten co-treatment (Figure 5B; Supplementary data online, Figure S17D). Interestingly, we also observed partial restoration of MYLK3 protein level as well as MYL2 phosphorylation following co-treatments with myosin activator omecamtiv (Figure 5C). Importantly, these findings were confirmed in the other independent iPSC-CM line (see Supplementary data online, Figure S19).

To further evaluate the cardioprotective effect of omecamtiv, we subjected TAC mice to either co-treatment with osimertinib and omecamtiv or osimertinib-only treatment for 2 weeks (Figure 5D). We found that omecamtiv co-treatment effectively prevented left ventricular contractile dysfunction by osimertinib in the course of 2 weeks (Figure 5E). More importantly, when osimertinib-only mice with apparent cardiac dysfunction were introduced to omecamtiv treatment, there was restoration of left ventricular contractile function within a week (Figure 5E). Heart rate was maintained (see Supplementary data online, Figure S20A) and LVIDs was corrected by omecamtiv treatment (see Supplementary data online, Figure S20B), consistent with an improvement of cardiac function. To confirm that co-treatment with omecamtiv and osimertinib is well tolerated without compromising anti-cancer activity of osimertinib, we utilized a mouse model of human EGFR mutant lung cancer xenograft and treated the mice with or without osimertinib and omecamtiv. As expected, osimertinib treatment resulted in a significant and marked reduction in tumour volume compared with the group without osimertinib treatment. Importantly, co-treatment with omecamtiv did not impact the tolerability or the anti-cancer efficacy of osimertinib (see Supplementary data online, Figure S21). Taken together, these findings suggest that myosin activators such as omecamtiv may be a promising preventive or rescue therapy for osimertinib cardiotoxicity.

Discussion

In the present study, we established an in vivo model of osimertinib cardiotoxicity by employing mild pressure overload (TAC) to elicit cardiotoxic effects. Mice subjected to TAC exhibited significant left ventricular dysfunction following osimertinib treatment without histopathologic evidence of myocardial injury, fibrosis, or inflammation, consistent with clinical manifestation of osimertinib-induced cardiomyopathy. Leveraging snRNA-seq using mouse cardiac tissues, we then identified MYLK3 downregulation and a subsequent decrease in MYL2 phosphorylation and sarcomere disarray as the key mechanism underlying osimertinib-induced cardiac dysfunction. Further investigation revealed that decreased phosphorylation of transcription factor GATA4 by osimertinib leads to transcriptional repression of MYLK3 and subsequent cascade of molecular events leading to contractile dysfunction. Furthermore, we showed that osimertinib cardiotoxicity was reversible upon discontinuation of osimertinib, supporting the notion of temporal sarcomere disruption as the main mechanism of osimertinib cardiotoxicity without permanent cellular injuries or pathologic remodelling. Finally, we demonstrated that myosin activator prevented osimertinib cardiotoxicity during co-treatment and improved cardiac performance even after the manifestation of cardiac dysfunction due to osimertinib. Our findings suggest that myosin activation may serve as a preventive or rescue strategy for osimertinib cardiotoxicity, highlighting its translational potential.

Thus far, the mechanisms underlying cardiotoxicity of TKI remain poorly understood. Cardiotoxicity of anti-angiogenic TKIs, such as TKIs targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor used for renal cell carcinoma or thyroid cancer, has been attributed in part to the on-target effect of VEGF inhibition in myocardium and vasculature.40 Beyond this, mechanisms underlying cardiotoxicity of other TKIs, such as those targeting EGFR or BCR-ABL, remain elusive. Thus far, mitochondrial dysfunction,41 impaired autophagy,42 and inflammation43 have been suggested as possible mechanisms, although no study to date identified a direct molecular target of TKI that mediates cardiotoxicity. As TKIs often inhibit multiple tyrosine kinases, albeit with different specificity, it is postulated that TKIs may exert cardiotoxic effects through interaction with cardiac-specific off-target receptor tyrosine kinases.3 Herein, we find that osimertinib-induced cardiotoxicity is primarily mediated through effects on cardiomyocytes (data not shown) and identify the cardiac transcription factor GATA4 as a putative target of osimertinib leading to the observed cardiac dysfunction. GATA4 is a critical regulator of cardiac gene expression, playing an important role in embryonic heart development as well as cardiac stress response.44–46 Moreover, serine 105 phosphorylation of GATA4 has been found to be necessary to induce cardiac hypertrophy and stress compensation.47 We demonstrate that osimertinib treatment causes a reduction in GATA4 protein levels, with a more pronounced decrease in phosphorylated GATA4 at serine 105, which leads to decreased transcription of MYLK3 and subsequent contractile dysfunction. Overexpression of GATA4 was sufficient to restore MYLK3 levels as well as contractile phenotype. At the molecular level, osimertinib may directly bind to GATA4 and interfere with the latter’s stability and activity.

Mutations in MYLK3 have been recognized as a rare cause of familial dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in patients.48 Previous studies have demonstrated that pathogenic mutations in MYLK3, identified through familial segregation analysis, resulted in reduced phosphorylation of MYL2 in transfected HEK293T cells as well as in clinical samples, highlighting the important role of MYLK3 in MYL2 phosphorylation during normal contraction.49 This is corroborated by an in vivo study where heterozygous Mylk3 knockout mice recapitulated features of DCM.50 Moreover, genetic ablation of Mylk3 has been shown to cause maladaptive remodelling under pressure overload, as Mylk3-knockout mice exhibit impaired compensatory hypertrophy and accelerated acute heart failure following TAC.51 More recently, Hitsumoto et al. showed that cardiac dysfunction induced by Mylk3 frameshift mutations in mice significantly improved upon the restoration of MYLK3 activity, via adenovirus-mediated MYLK3 OE as well as a small molecule treatment that activates MYLK3.52 The observed cardiac dysfunction following MYLK3 downregulation by osimertinib and rescued cardiac function by MYLK3 OE in this study mimics clinical manifestation of pathologic mutations of MYLK3. However, it is important to note that, unlike transgenic models with targeted genetic alterations, TKIs such as osimertinib may affect a broader range of protein targets. Thus, additional or complementary mechanisms beyond the MYLK3–MYL2 axis may also contribute to the observed cardiotoxicity. In particular, osimertinib may blunt the necessary or compensatory hypertrophic response, as evidenced by the relatively lower Rcan1.4 expression in osimertinib–TAC mice compared with TAC mice, which could contribute to the observed cardiac dysfunction. Additionally, we used TAC to mimic the haemodynamic stress commonly experienced by cancer patients. However, TAC primarily induces pressure overload—a condition more characteristic of aortic stenosis than systemic hypertension. As such, caution is warranted when applying these findings to clinical settings.

Myosin light chain serves as the physical link between myosin thick filament and myosin head, and its phosphorylation leads to conformational changes to bring the myosin head in proximity to actin with ultimate formation of myosin-actin bridge to initiate contraction.32 Therefore, lack of MYL2 phosphorylation weakens the interaction between myosin and actin, leading to observed sarcomere disarray and contractile dysfunction. We sought to prevent or rescue osimertinib cardiotoxicity by augmenting myosin-actin interaction with myosin activator, omecamtiv. Treatment with omecamtiv effectively prevented contractile dysfunction both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting its translational potential. Importantly, we also observed complete restoration of sarcomere array and at least partial recovery of MYLK3 protein and MYL2 phosphorylation levels, suggesting that there might be close interplay and probably feedback mechanisms between myosin-actin interaction, sarcomere array, and MYLK3. Omecamtiv was previously explored as a new therapy in a randomized clinical trial for treating symptomatic chronic heart failure with reduced left ventricular EF of 35% or less.53,54 While there was a slight and statistically significant decrease in heart failure events among those treated with omecamtiv vs placebo, the degree of benefit was not deemed sufficient to warrant FDA approval. However, given the underlying mechanism targeting myosin-actin interaction, it is plausible that a subset of heart failure patients, for example, those with sarcomeric DCM, may derive greater benefit from myosin activator than other patients, such as those with ischaemic cardiomyopathy. As such, there is an ongoing clinical trial investigating the efficacy of danicamtiv, another myosin activator, among patients with primary DCM due to either MYH7 or TTN variants, which may provide insights into this hypothesis. Similarly, given sarcomere disruption being the primary mechanism, patients with osimertinib cardiotoxicity may benefit from therapies primarily targeting sarcomere such as myosin activator as seen in this study. Future clinical studies are warranted for its clinical translation.

Finally, we used osimertinib as a model TKI as we sought to understand the mechanism underlying cardiac dysfunction caused by non-anti-angiogenic TKIs. Our findings may have relevance beyond osimertinib and could potentially inform shared mechanisms of cardiotoxicity associated with other TKIs. MYL2 phosphorylation status in response to TKI treatment may serve as a surrogate for the mechanism of cardiac dysfunction as well as a predictor for potential response to myosin activator therapy. As TKI-induced cardiac dysfunction poses a major challenge when treating cancer, a targeted, cardiac-specific therapy such as myosin activator may provide an attractive option as a preventive or therapeutic combination treatment allowing patients to receive life-saving cancer therapies.

Conclusion

In summary, our study unveils a novel mechanism of TKI-induced cardiac dysfunction utilizing osimertinib, a third-generation TKI targeting EGFR mutations used for treating metastatic non-small cell lung carcinoma. We established mouse in vivo and human iPSC models of osimertinib cardiotoxicity and observed profound contractile dysfunction by osimertinib without cell death, inflammation, or fibrosis. Additional investigations leveraging snRNA-seq analysis revealed that osimertinib leads to dephosphorylation of GATA4, which results in significant downregulation of MYLK3 and decreased MYL2 phosphorylation leading to sarcomere disarray and subsequent contractile dysfunction. Consistent with clinical observation, osimertinib cardiotoxicity was reversible upon discontinuation of osimertinib treatment, further supporting the notion that temporal sarcomere disruption without permanent cellular damage or ventricular remodelling is a main driver of the observed contractile dysfunction. Finally, co-treatment with myosin activator, omecamtiv, effectively prevented and restored cardiac function after osimertinib treatment both in vitro and in vivo, highlighting its therapeutic potential for osimertinib cardiotoxicity.
