The ‘CIED Team’ Approach to Device-Related Complications
Authors: Ruggero Mazzotta, Leandro Cosco, Francesca Fedeli, Giuseppe Ricciardi, Gabriele Bambagioni, Marco Bernini, Roberto Scanferla, Paolo Pieragnoli, Laura Perrotta, Luca Checchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Complex cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED)–related complications often require expertise that extends beyond cardiac electrophysiology. We present 3 challenging cases of transvenous cardiac devices demanding multidisciplinary management, placing the electrophysiologist at the crossroads of diagnosis, risk assessment, and procedural strategy. These scenarios included: 1) a necrotic ulcer caused by a cold burn of a pacemaker pocket complicated by infectious endocarditis; 2) a dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans developing in proximity to a pacemaker pocket; and 3) an abandoned ventricular lead resulting in dehiscence and fistulization in the breast of a patient affected by a cardiac teratoma. Their management required the integrated expertise of plastic surgeons, infectious disease specialists, oncologists, orthopedic surgeons and cardiac surgeons—effectively constituting a “CIED team.” This case series highlights how modern cardiac electrophysiology increasingly intersects with complex clinical conditions, necessitating cross-specialty collaboration, and how recent technologies—such as leadless pacing systems and wearable defibrillators—can support safe and effective solutions.

The expanding use of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), together with increasing patient longevity and procedural complexity, has led to a growing burden of device-related obstacles. These clinical scenarios frequently extend beyond isolated electrophysiological issues, involving infectious, oncologic, and surgical challenges that cannot be adequately managed by the electrophysiologist alone. Consequently, contemporary CIED care increasingly requires a structured multidisciplinary approach integrating electrophysiology with surgical, infectious disease, oncologic, and reconstructive expertise to optimize patient outcomes. Patient 1: Cold Burn–Induced Pacemaker Pocket Necrosis With Lead-Related Endocarditis

An 84-year-old woman with hypertension, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and a dual-chamber pacemaker implanted 10 years earlier for advanced atrioventricular block was referred to our center because of extensive pacemaker pocket necrosis. The lesion developed after inappropriate prolonged application of ice packs to the surgical site following a recent generator replacement, resulting in cold burn, pocket erosion, and device exposure (Figure 1). On admission, she was afebrile, and hemodynamically stable, although laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers. Device interrogation showed pacemaker dependency. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed moderate tricuspid regurgitation and multiple mobile vegetations on both pacing leads, the largest measuring 16 × 7 mm, consistent with lead-related infective endocarditis (Figure 2). Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was started, and a complete transvenous system extraction (TLE) was required. Considering the wide extension of the necrotic tissue, a combined intervention involving both electrophysiologists and plastic surgeons was planned. After temporary femoral pacing was established, a complete TLE was performed using a locking stylet and rotational mechanical sheath. Plastic surgeons subsequently excised the necrotic tissue through a diamond-shaped approach and achieved primary wound closure (Figure 3A to C). Once blood cultures tested negative and pacemaker dependency was confirmed, the endocarditis team opted for a dual-chamber leadless pacing strategy in light of the high risk of infection recurrence. Right atrial (RA) and right ventricular (RV) leadless pacemakers were successfully implanted, preserving atrioventricular synchrony. The patient remained clinically stable and was discharged. At 1- and 6-month follow-up, the clinical course was uneventful, the pocket site completely healed, and echocardiographic findings and device performance were unchanged.

Patient 2: Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans Involving an ICD Pocket With Lead Entrapment

A 47-year-old woman with congenital long QT syndrome and a dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placed in 2012 for secondary prevention after a torsades de pointes, presented with a progressively enlarging mass over the left prepectoral ICD pocket. The system consisted of a passive-fixation RA lead and a DF-1 dual-coil RV lead. Her medical history was significant for anxiety disorder and insomnia, and current medications included beta-blockers and antidepressants. In 2020 she first noticed swelling over the ICD scar and a surgical revision was performed, revealing a dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) and allowing temporary symptom relief. However, from September 2025 the mass enlarged again with increasing discomfort but without any systemic symptoms.

Physical examination showed a firm, well-demarcated swelling over the ICD pocket, mildly erythematous but without signs of systemic infection (Figure 4); pain was elicited by mass palpation, but arm mobility was preserved. Blood laboratory tests were unremarkable. Ultrasonography revealed a 37 × 22 mm solid mass at the pocket site, and repeated core needle biopsy confirmed DFSP recurrence. Contrast computed tomography (CT) better defined a 53 × 52 × 45 mm encapsulated tumor in close contact to the ICD generator and leads but without pectoralis major invasion or metastases (Figures 5A and 5B). Given the malignant nature, size, and proximity to the ICD system, the case was evaluated by a multidisciplinary team including electrophysiologists, oncologists, and orthopedic surgeons. Consensus was to perform simultaneous radical oncologic excision with complete ICD system removal, followed by temporary protection through a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator. Under general anesthesia, in a hybrid surgical theater, surgeons performed a wide en bloc removal of the capsulated tumor as well as the surrounding subcutaneous tissues, and part of the pectoralis major (Figure 6). The ICD generator and both leads were partially entrapped within the specimen and were carefully isolated without damaging the tumor capsule. Complete TLE was then performed using locking stylets and powered mechanical sheaths, without any complication. Postoperatively, the patient remained stable and no pericardial effusion was observed, while histopathology confirmed DFSP without metastasis. Vacuum-assisted closure therapy promoted granulation and wound stabilization and allowed reconstruction through a latissimus dorsi flap performed by orthopedics and plastic surgeons. The patient was discharged with a wearable defibrillator as bridging therapy. Histopathological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed DFSP with free surgical margins and no evidence of metastatic disease. After multidisciplinary oncologic discussion, adjuvant medical therapy was not considered necessary. Four months after surgery and device extraction, total-body positron emission tomography showed no evidence of local disease recurrence or distant metastases.

Patient 3: Infected Abandoned ICD Lead Causing Breast Fistulization in a Patient With a Ventricular Teratoma

A 36-year-old woman with a left ventricular teratoma diagnosed in infancy presented with a cutaneous erosion and purulent drainage from a subareolar fistulous tract in the left breast. The teratoma, originally detected at 4 months of age, involved the inferior, posterior, and lateral ventricular walls, significantly reducing left ventricular cavity (Figure 7, Videos 1 and 2). Although this cardiac tumor is usually associated with low arrhythmic risk, the extent of the lesion prompted placement of a single-chamber ICD for primary prevention at the age of 15 years at another hospital.1 Over the subsequent years, the device never recorded arrhythmic events or delivered therapies, and the patient remained asymptomatic.Figure 7Transthoracic Echocardiography Showing the Ventricular Teratoma (6 × 4 cm), Significantly Reducing the Left Ventricular Cavity (Patient 3)

In early adulthood, RV lead failure occurred. Device interrogation showed no recorded arrhythmic events or appropriate delivered therapies over the years. Therefore, the electrophysiology team at the implanting center elected to remove the generator and abandon the malfunctioning defibrillation lead, as transvenous lead extraction was considered a high-risk procedure at that time.

At the current presentation, CT imaging (Figure 8) demonstrated that the abandoned ICD lead runs from the left subclavian vein through the pectoral muscle into the breast, terminating within a fistulous tract. No vegetations were visualized on transesophageal echocardiography, and blood cultures tested negative. To remove the infected abandoned lead, an intervention through a right minithoracotomy was performed by cardiothoracic surgeons. However, only a partial removal of the ICD lead was possible, with a small distal fragment left firmly adherent to the RV apex. The postoperative course was uneventful, blood and lead cultures tested negative, and empiric antibiotic therapy was continued for 4 weeks. The breast fistula healed completely, and the patient was discharged in good condition. Given the stability of the benign tumor and the low risk of arrhythmias, the multidisciplinary team decided not to proceed with reimplantation of a new ICD system. The 12-month follow-up visit was unremarkable.

Discussion

CIEDs are currently implanted in a growing number of patients with a high burden of comorbidities.2 In this setting, CIED complications occurs more frequently, markedly increasing diagnostic and therapeutic complexity and often requiring a structured multidisciplinary management. As depicted in our case series, CIED adverse events may be limited to the device pocket or may extend to surrounding tissues, with associated systemic involvement.2,3 Contributors include also older age, long dwelling time, multiple device replacement or pocket revisions, and retained or abandoned leads.2,3

International consensus documents state that early recognition and coordinated multidisciplinary care are central to effective treatment.2 Batchelor et al3 have highlighted the role of the heart team in cardiovascular medicine, emphasizing how structured collaboration across different specialties enhances decision-making and patient care in complex clinical scenarios. The present case series represents a practical application of this model, illustrating how, despite increasing superspecialization within cardiology, the management of complex CIED complications continues to require a broad multidisciplinary perspective to ensure a comprehensive and patient-centered approach.

In this context, the electrophysiologist assumes the role of clinical coordinator, integrating diagnostic findings and balancing infectious, surgical, and oncologic considerations to guide procedural strategy.2 CIED-related complications often require percutaneous extraction of the pacing system, a procedure that is inherently associated with a high risk of complications and a substantial rate of procedural failure.4 In patients requiring CIED system removal, transvenous lead extraction represents the preferred first-line strategy whenever technically feasible, in line with current European Heart Rhythm Association consensus recommendations favoring a percutaneous approach because of its lower procedural morbidity and mortality compared with upfront surgical extraction, even in the presence of lead vegetations.2 Surgical approaches should therefore generally be reserved for selected cases, including failed percutaneous extraction, large vegetations, concomitant surgical indications, or situations in which transvenous removal is deemed unsafe. In our series, this principle guided the initial management strategy in all 3 patients, while patient 3 ultimately required surgical escalation after unsuccessful percutaneous extraction because of severe chronic intraventricular fibrosis anchoring the lead tip.

Timing also represents a critical determinant of outcome, particularly in the setting of CIED-related infection. Current consensus recommendations emphasize that device extraction should not be unnecessarily delayed after diagnosis, as early removal has been associated with lower mortality and reduced infection-related complications.2 Accordingly, in our cases presenting with infective involvement, extraction procedures were performed promptly after multidisciplinary evaluation and procedural planning.

Emerging technologies further strengthen the management of these patients, expanding the available tools to treat complex device-related complications, with higher procedural success and lower risks for the patients. Leadless pacemakers markedly reduce the burden of intravascular and subcutaneous prosthetic material and are associated with a very low risk of reinfection. Therefore, they represent an attractive option following complete device system removal in patients with extensive pocket involvement or device-related infection5,6 Wearable cardioverter-defibrillators provide effective temporary protection from malignant arrhythmias when permanent reimplantation must be deferred, allowing definitive management of infection, malignancy, or wound healing without compromising patient safety.7

Across these 3 cases (see Table 1), the involvement of multiple specialists with complementary expertise was essential for patient care. Infectious disease specialists and oncologists were primarily involved in clinical and medical management, contributing to antimicrobial therapy, oncologic planning, and postprocedural patient management. Plastic and breast surgeons provided essential expertise in wound and tissue management, including reconstructive surgery after extensive debridement in patient 1 and treatment of the mammary fistula in patient 3. Orthopedic surgeons played a key role in patient 2 by enabling safe oncologic resection with free surgical margins, whereas cardiac surgeons became necessary in patient 3 after failure of percutaneous extraction, allowing escalation to surgical lead removal. Conclusions

This case series illustrates how contemporary CIED-related complications frequently extend beyond isolated electrophysiological issues and require structured multidisciplinary management to achieve optimal outcomes. The integration of electrophysiology with surgical, infectious, and oncologic expertise enables tailored decision-making, safer procedural strategies, and appropriate selection of definitive or bridging therapies. In this evolving landscape, emerging technologies—such as leadless pacing systems and wearable cardioverter-defibrillators—play a pivotal role in expanding therapeutic options and supporting complex clinical pathways. These findings support the concept of a dedicated multidisciplinary “CIED team” as a cornerstone of modern device care.
