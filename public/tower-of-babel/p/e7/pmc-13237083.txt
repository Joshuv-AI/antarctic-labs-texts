NLRP14 modulates the activity of E3 ubiquitin ligases during the oocyte-to-embryo transition
Authors: Sibei Liu, Qianqian Qi, Pengliang Chi, Haizhan Jiao, Li Yan, Yuechao Lu, Rongrong Zhang, Jinhong Li, Sicheng Ju, Zhuo Han, Zihan Zhang, Qingting Liu, Guojin Ou, Jialu Li, Jing Chen, Xiang Wang, Lei Li, Li Guo, Xue Jiao, Hongli Hu, Yongmei Jiang, Dong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNLRP14 is an essential maternal factor for mammalian embryonic development. Maternal ablation of NLRP14 in mice impairs DNA demethylation and calcium homeostasis in zygotes, causing early embryonic arrest. However, the underlying biochemical events remain largely unknown. Here, we identified two binding partners (KDM2A and UHRF1) of NLRP14 and further solved structures of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 and NLRP14-UHRF1. Structural analysis revealed that NLRP14 modulates the SKP1-CUL1-F-box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase and the RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase UHRF1 through two distinct mechanisms. Mechanistically, NLRP14 competitively inhibits KDM2A-mediated SCF assembly or allosterically inhibits the activity of UHRF1 by occupying the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (UBE2D) binding site of the ubiquitin-like (UBL) domain. Deletion of NLRP14 in mice increases ubiquitination levels in oocytes during maturation and after fertilization. Collectively, our findings identify NLRP14 as a dual regulator that restrains E3 ubiquitin ligase-driven ubiquitination by limiting SCF complex assembly and attenuating UHRF1 activity. This regulatory role is required to prevent excessive protein ubiquitination and maintain proteostasis during the oocyte-to-embryo transition, thereby supporting early embryonic development. Our study uncovers maternal regulation of proteostasis in oocytes and suggests that dysregulating proteostasis is an important factor in the pathogenesis of reproductive disorders.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Embryology, Post-translational modifications

Introduction

Before zygotic genome activation (ZGA), the oocyte-to-embryo transition predominantly relies on a series of maternal effectors1,2. Multiple members of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein (NLRP) family, including NLRP2, NLRP4, NLRP5, NLRP9, and NLRP14, have been identified as maternal effectors essential for early embryonic development in mouse3–6. A large number of NLRP gene variants are associated with female reproductive disorder 3,4,7. Most pathogenic variants of NLRP5 lead to embryo developmental arrest (EDA), resulting in severe female infertility8,9. Variants of NLRP2 and NLRP7 are associated with EDA, hydatidiform moles, miscarriages, and multilocus imprinting disorders (MLIDs)2,10–13. Two compound heterozygous variants in NLRP14 case EDA, resulting in infertility in women14.

Recent studies have shown that NLRP14 is vital for early embryonic development14–16. Similar to other reproduction-related NLRPs, NLRP14 is explicitly expressed in gonads and early embryos14. The Nlrp14-deficient female mice are infertile, and their embryos exhibit EDA16. Nlrp14 knockout mice display defects in oocyte maturation, disrupted calcium homeostasis, and aberrant zygotic DNA methylation14–16. Interestingly, NLRP14 deficiency leads to a sharp decrease in the protein level of ubiquitin-like-containing PHD and RING finger domain protein 1 (UHRF1) in oocytes and results in its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus in zygotes14,16. UHRF1, a nuclear protein in somatic cells, is an epigenetic factor that regulates DNA methylation and histone modifications17,18. In contrast to its nuclear distribution in somatic cells, UHRF1 is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of mouse oocytes and preimplantation embryos19,20. Notably, maternal UHRF1 regulates the proper architecture of the cytoplasmic lattice and is essential for the function of both oocytes and preimplantation embryos21–23. Maintaining cytoplasmic enrichment of UHRF1 is critical for normal early embryo development. However, the mechanisms by which NLRP14 regulates these processes remain unclear.

Here, through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo investigations, we demonstrate that NLRP14 prevents excessive ubiquitination and preserves proper histone modification states in oocytes and early embryos by inhibiting SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase assembly with KDM2A and by blocking E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme binding to UHRF1, thus coordinating the ubiquitination pathway and proteostasis of oocytes and early embryos.

Results

Identification of the binding partners of NLRP14

Proteomic data identified 265 NLRP14-interacting proteins in mouse oocytes16. To further identify binding partners of NLRP14, we employed AlphaFold 3 to predict the structures of NLRP14 in complex with each of these 265 candidates individually24 (Fig. 1a). The pTM and ipTM scores of the predicted complexes indicate the reliability of the predicted fold for individual subunit and the predicted relative positions of the subunits within the complex, respectively. Using a composite confidence score (0.8 × ipTM + 0.2 × pTM) with a threshold of 0.6, we identified three high-confidence potential NLRP14 binding partners: MTND (0.73), CUX1 (0.634), and KDM2A (0.626) (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Fig. 1a–f). MTND, an enzyme of the methionine salvage pathway (MTA), influences hepatoma growth25 and induces pathological cell migration26. CUX1 is a conserved transcription factor27, involved in cell proliferation and differentiation28. KDM2A is a histone demethylase that erases specific demethylated histone H3 at lysine 36 (H3K36)29. KDM2A knockout mice exhibited embryonic lethality at E10.5–12.5, accompanied by severe growth defects30. In the KDM2A conditional knockout (cKO) female mice, the patterns of H3K36me2/3 in germinal vesicle breakdown (GVBD)-stage oocytes are remarkably changed and oocyte development is blocked31. Interestingly, a well-known NLRP14-binding protein, a multi-domain containing E3 ubiquitin ligase UHRF1 participating in DNA methylation and histone modification in epigenetic regulation14–17, obtains a composite confidence score of only 0.506 with an ipTM score of 0.51and pTM score of 0.49 (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Fig. 1g, h).

To determine whether NLRP14 binds MTND, CUX1, KDM2A and UHRF1, we performed pull-down assays in vitro. As a result, KDM2A showed the strongest enrichment for NLRP14 among these candidates, whereas UHRF1 showed only weak binding to NLRP14 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1i). NLRP14, in turn, also pulled down KDM2A and UHRF1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1j, k). In contrast, MTND and CUX1 did not pull down NLRP14 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1i). KDM2B, similar to its paralog KDM2A (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), also interacted with murine NLRP14 (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Interestingly, although human NLRP14 shares 55.5% sequence identity with its mouse orthologue, human NLRP14 only retained the capacity to pull down the human KDM2B, indicating a species-specific difference in complex formation between humans and mice (Supplementary Fig. 2b, d). Consistently, the interaction between NLRP14 and UHRF1 is conserved in both mice and humans (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Of note, UHRF1 is a multidomain protein composed of five domains: a ubiquitin-like domain (UBL), a tandem tudor domain (TTD), a plant homology domain (PHD), a SET- and RING-associated domain (SRA), and a really interesting new gene (RING) domain (Supplementary Fig. 3c). To identify which domain of UHRF1 is necessary for mediating the interaction with NLRP14, we generated five truncated versions of UHRF1 from two species and performed pull-down assays. These pull-down results suggest that the UBL domain of UHRF1 is necessary for its interaction with NLRP14 in both mice and humans (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e).

Notably, deletion of NLRP14 in mice resulted in significant changes in KDM2A and UHRF1 (Fig. 1e, f). Firstly, in comparison to the wild-type (WT), the GV oocytes of Nlrp14null mice presented increased nuclear enrichment of KDM2A (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Interestingly, KDM2A demethylates H3K36me2 through its JmjC domain29. On account of the increased nuclear accumulation of KDM2A in GV oocytes caused by the deletion of NLRP14, we hypothesize that it will influence H3K36me2 methylation levels within the nucleus. We compared immunofluorescence (IF) staining for H3K36me2 in GV oocytes from wild-type and Nlrp14null mice. Notably, IF staining demonstrated that NLRP14 deficiency reduced H3K36me2 levels in GV oocytes (Supplementary Fig. 4b–e). Consequently, NLRP14 deficiency results in increased nuclear enrichment of KDM2A and decreased H3K36me2. On the other hand, UHRF1 is decreased in GV and Metaphase II (MⅡ) oocytes and enriched in the nuclei of zygotic and two-cell embryos upon NLRP14 deficiency (Fig. 1f), consistent with a previous report 14.

To further validate the interaction between NLRP14 and KDM2A/UHRF1 in vivo, we performed IF staining, coimmunoprecipitation (coIP), and proximity ligation assays (PLA) in oocytes, zygotes, and two-cell embryos. The IF staining revealed that KDM2A colocalized with NLRP14 in WT GV and MⅡ oocytes, whereas UHRF1 colocalized with NLRP14 in the cytoplasm during oocyte maturation and early embryonic development (Fig. 1g, h). Furthermore, the coIP assays confirmed the interactions between NLRP14 and KDM2A/UHRF1 in vivo (Fig. 1i,j). In addition, the PLA foci signals of NLRP14-KDM2A diffused in the cytoplasm of oocytes, while the PLA foci signals of NLRP14-UHRF1 appeared in the cytoplasm of oocytes and early embryos (Fig. 1k, l). Taken together, KDM2A and UHRF1 are binding partners of NLRP14. Therefore, we selected KDM2A and UHRF1 for further structural investigation.

Molecular insights into the mouse NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 complex

KDM2A, also known as FBXL11 or JHDM1A, consists of an N-terminal JmjC domain (including related JmjN and Hairpin), a CxxC domain, a PHD domain, an F-box domain, and a leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain29 (Fig. 2a). Notably, through its F-box domain, KDM2A interacts with SKP1 to participate in the formation of SCF (SKP1-CUL1-F-box protein) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex32. To determine whether NLRP14 forms a complex with KDM2A and SKP1, we performed a pull-down assay in HEK293F cells. Definitely, overexpressed NLRP14 bound to KDM2A along with SKP1 (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, the PLA assay suggested that the NLRP14-containing complex interacts with SKP1 in vivo (Fig. 2c).

To understand the molecular assembly of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1, we aim to determine the structure of this ternary protein complex. Based on the analysis of the interface in the predicted NLRP14-KDM2A structure (Supplementary Fig. 5a), we reconstituted the ternary complex by coexpressing the Flag-tagged full-length NLRP14 (1–993 aa), Strep-tagged KDM2A (815–1,161 aa), and Strep-tagged full-length SKP1 (1–163 aa) in HEK293F cells. Interestingly, the size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) profile of the purified NLRP14–KDM2A–SKP1 complex showed a main peak (Peak1) and a shoulder to its left (Peak2)(Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5b), indicating that NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 exists in different oligomerization states. We conducted structural determinations on the samples corresponding to the two peaks. Consequently, two structures of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 were solved at resolutions of 2.79 Å and 3.22 Å, respectively. (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 5–7a, and Supplementary Table 1). The density map of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 defines the C-terminal region of NLRP14 (234–993 aa), including part of the NACHT domain and the whole LRR domain, the F-box domain and the following LRR domain of KDM2A (889–1161 aa) and SKP1 (1–163 aa) (Fig. 2a, e, f).

The NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 from Peak1 forms a heterotrimeric complex with a 1:1:1 stoichiometry. We further analysed the interfaces among the three subunits in detail. The LRR domain of NLRP14 interacts with the LRR domain of KDM2A, while SKP1 interacts with the F-box domain of KDM2A (Fig. 2f). The Glu639, Asp641, Ser668, Leu696, Ser725 and Asp753 of NLRP14 form hydrogen bonds with Arg1060, Gln1079, Asn1082, Val1087, Arg1092, and Arg1116 of KDM2A (Fig. 2g). The NLRP14 variants (D641A and D753A) impaired the binding with KDM2A, while KDM2A variants (R1056A, Q1079A, N1082A, and R1116A) also weakened the interaction between NLRP14 and KDM2A (Fig. 2h). SKP1 inserts a C-terminal helix (147–163 aa) into the gap between F-box and LRR of KDM2A and direct contacts with the opposite helix of F-box (Fig. 2f), similar to the interaction between SKP1 and other F-box protein in reported structure of SCF33.

Additionally, the NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 from Peak2 assembles into a dimer of heterotrimers (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Two NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 protomers adopt almost identical conformation as the NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 heterotrimer from Peak1, exhibiting a root‑mean‑square deviation (RMSD) of 0.110 Å (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Therefore, we focused on the interface between two NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 heterotrimers in the high oligomer complex. Two NLRP14 molecules contact with each other and adopt an almost vertical position (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Two incomplete NACHT domains interact with each other, and the C-terminal LRR domain also interacts with the NACHT of another NLRP14 (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Interestingly, the complete NACHT domains in the predicted NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 complex clash with the opposite NLRP14 in the dimeric ternaries (Supplementary Fig. 7d). The proteins, interfaces, structures, and assemblies (PISA) program analysis revealed that the dimeric interface of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 contains four parts: interface I and interface I’ between two NLRP14s; interface II and interface II’ between NLRP14 and another KDM2A (Supplementary Fig. 7e). The NACHT domain of NLRP14 exhibits electrostatic interactions with the LRR of NLRP14’ and KDM2A’ respectively, and vice versa. Among the two NLRP14s, K959 and E462’ further form a salt bridge. The Flag pull-down shows a significantly diminished band for NLRP14-HA in the E462A lane and a slightly diminished band for the K959A variant (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Therefore, both electrostatic and van der Waals interactions seem to play a role in complex formation. Furthermore, when the heterotrimers fraction (elution volume 14.5 mL) was re-injected into the SEC column, the peak partially shifted to the position corresponding to the heterotrimers (16 mL), indicating a dynamic equilibration feature of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 in solution (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h).

NLRP14 competes with CUL1 to inhibit the formation of SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase complex

SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase composed of SKP1, CUL1, and an F-box protein34, mediates substrate-specific ubiquitination through variable F-box proteins (Fig. 2i). Typically, the F-box domain of KDM2A serves as a substrate adaptor for the SCFKDM2A E3 ubiquitin ligases32. This prompted us to investigate whether NLRP14 binds at the substrate-binding site of the KDM2A-SKP1 module, thereby preventing the recruitment of the scaffold subunit CUL1 and the assembly of the canonical SCF complex. Therefore, we performed a structural comparison between the NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 and CUL1-RBX1-SKP1-SKP2 (SCFSKP2) complexes35. Superposing the SKP1 in both structures shows an RMSD of 1.03 Å, suggesting an identical conformation of SKP1 (Fig. 2j). Meanwhile, two distinct F-box domains from KDM2A and SKP2 interact with SKP1 via the identical interface, adopting a similar position. However, the C-terminal LRR domain of KDM2A extends parallel to the CUL1 of SCFSKP2, resulting in NLRP14 steric clashes with CUL1 and suggesting NLRP14 competitively inhibits the SCF complex formation (Fig. 2k). To test this hypothesis, we employed KDM2A as bait protein and examined the impact of NLRP14 on SCFKDM2A complex formation. Our results demonstrated that the addition of NLRP14 significantly attenuates the binding between KDM2A and SCF components (CUL1, SKP1, and RBX1) (Fig. 2l). We further performed a PLA assay in GV oocytes to validate the competitive inhibitory capacity of NLRP14 in vivo. Compared to the PLA foci of SKP1-CUL1 in WT GV oocytes, Nlrp14null oocytes exhibited dramatically increasing PLA foci, suggesting an enhanced interaction between SKP1 and CUL1 (Fig. 2m, n). Therefore, NLRP14 competitively inhibits the formation of SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase complex in oocytes.

Structure of the human NLRP14-UHRF1 complex

To obtain the structural information of NLRP14-UHRF1, we attempted to in vitro reconstruct the mouse/human NLRP14-UHRF1 complex. Considering the disordered linker region connecting the five domains of UHRF1, we speculated that UHRF1 was highly dynamic and that obtaining the full-length UHRF1 structure would be difficult (Fig. 3a). Therefore, we coexpressed the UBL domain of UHRF1 with NLRP14 in Sf9 insect cells and obtained the human NLRP14-UHRF1UBL complex (Fig. 3b).

Furthermore, we evaluated the human NLRP14-UHRF1UBL interaction via cryo-EM (Supplementary Table 1). The cryo-EM structure of the NLRP14-UHRF1UBL complex was determined and refined at a resolution of 3.49 Å (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 8). The density map was well-defined for the UBL (1–75 aa) and NLRP14 (123–1,093 aa) (Supplementary Fig. 9). The pyrin domain (PYD) of NLRP14 was not visible, possibly due to its flexibility. The UBL adopts a classic ubiquitin fold, which is identical to the reported crystal structure of UBL (Supplementary Fig 10a). Comparing the structure of human and mouse NLRP14 revealed the absence of the nucleotide-binding domain (NBD) in the mouse ortholog, likely attributable to high flexibility in this region (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c).

Consistent with the domain-mapping results, UBL directly contacted the NBD of NLRP14 (Fig. 3d). The Arg6, Arg48, and Thr68 of the UBL formed hydrogen bonds with Glu282, Asn222, Asn265, and Glu226 in the NBD of NLRP14 (Fig. 3e). Additionally, the cation-π interaction between Phe287 of NLRP14 and Gln70 of the UBL is vital for specific recruitment (Fig. 3e). We generated four mutants of UBL (R6A, R48A, T68A, and Q70A) and three mutants of NLRP14 (E226A, F287A, and E226A/F287A) and performed pull-down assays to assess their interactions in HEK293F cells. These results revealed that four UBL variants and NLRP14 (F287A and E226A/F287A) disrupt the interaction between NLRP14 and UHRF1 (Fig. 3f).

NLRP14 inhibits the ubiquitination activity of UHRF1

The RING domain of UHRF1 possesses ubiquitin E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, enabling it to ubiquitinate itself (auto-ubiquitination) and its substrates, such as histone H336–38. Notably, the UBL domain of UHRF1 recruited UBE2 to facilitate the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of UHRF137,38. Comprehensive investigations revealed that Arg6 and Gln70 of human UHRF1 are located at the interface between the UBL and UBE2D38. In line with the previous biochemical study38, the complex structure of the UBL-UBE2D2 predicted by AlphaFold 3 also revealed the heterodimeric interface residues Arg6 and Gln70 on UBL24 (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 11). Structural observations revealed that human NLRP14 and UBE2D2 bound to the same site on UBL, suggesting a mutually exclusive interaction between NLRP14 and UBE2D2 for UBL binding. Consistent with the reported and predicted data37,38, the pull-down assay revealed that UBE2D2 binds to the UBL (Fig. 3h). Adding purified NLRP14 significantly decreased the interaction between UBL and UBE2D2 (Fig. 3h). Collectively, we proposed that the binding of NLRP14 to UHRF1 impairs the recruitment of UBE2D, thereby inhibiting the ubiquitination activity of UHRF1 (Fig. 3i).

We further established an in vitro system to assess UHRF1’s ubiquitination activity. First, we generated an N-terminal truncated UHRF1 lacking the UBL domain (residues 1–78 deletion, UHRF1ΔUBL) and performed a ubiquitination assay on H3 (Fig. 3j). Consistent with previous studies37,38, the human UHRF1ΔUBL showed lower activity, demonstrating the critical role of the UBL domain in UHRF1 (Fig. 3j). To further examine whether NLRP14 impacts the activity of UHRF1, we performed additional assays. Notably, adding NLRP14 reduced the ubiquitination level of H3, similar to the effect as removing the UBL domain (Fig. 3k). However, the addition of NLRP14E226A/F287A and NLRP2 (a homolog of NLRP14) had less inhibitory effect on UHRF1 auto-ubiquitination and H3 ubiquitination (Fig. 3k). Consistently, murine NLRP14 also negatively modulate the activity of murine UHRF1, suggesting the conserved mechanism of NLRP14 in mammals (Fig. 3l). Together, these findings suggest that the binding of NLRP14 leads to decreased ubiquitination activity of UHRF1 in vitro.

NLRP14 deficiency increases protein ubiquitination levels in vivo

NLRP14 competitively inhibits the assembly of SCF E3 ubiquitin ligases and also allosterically inhibits the activity of UHRF1 via occupancy of the UBE2D binding site of the UBL domain of UHRF1 (Figs. 2 and 3). Recent studies have suggested that NLRP14 binds to UHRF1 to maintain its abundance during the early embryos development14–16. Therefore, all these observations suggest that NLRP14 plays a regulatory role in protein ubiquitination and maintains protein homeostasis in oocytes.

To confirm the role of NLRP14 in ubiquitination regulation, we examine ubiquitination in oocytes and embryos using the reported strategy (an antibody against ubiquitinated proteins, clone FK2)39. We observed significantly increased aggregation signals of ubiquitinated proteins in Nlrp14null oocytes (Fig. 4a–d). The number and average size of aggregation signals in Nlrp14null oocytes are significantly greater than those in WT oocytes (Fig. 4b, c). Consistently, a similar increase in ubiquitination was observed in mature oocytes and fertilized eggs (Fig. 4b, c).

Recent studies have shown that immature mouse oocytes sequester ubiquitinated protein aggregates within specialized super-organelles, termed endolysosomal vesicular assemblies (ELVAs), and subsequently degrade these aggregates during oocyte maturation39. ELVAs contain proteasomes and lysosomes, which are involved in two typical pathways: the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) for the degradation of ubiquitinated proteins40,41. Thus, we used a core proteasome marker (20S proteasome) and the lysosomal marker lysosome-associated membrane glycoprotein 1 (LAMP1) to determine which ubiquitination degradation pathway was pathogenically activated by Nlrp14 knockout. Notably, the number and average size of the 20S proteasome puncta increased dramatically in Nlrp14null oocytes (Fig. 4e–h). Moreover, there was a significant elevation in the average size of LAMP1 puncta in Nlrp14null oocytes (Fig. 4i–k), suggesting that the autophagy-lysosome pathway was also involved. These results indicated that NLRP14 deficiency increased protein ubiquitination during mouse oocyte maturation and fertilization. Interestingly, aggregated proteins were sequestered in ELVAs in normal oocytes39. The IF staining results revealed that LAMP1 foci in Nlrp14null oocytes were increased to those in normal oocytes, suggesting that the aggregated proteins in ELVAs could be regulated by NLRP14.

Discussion

Using structure-based prediction and in vitro biochemical experiments, we evaluated the potential binding partners of NLRP14 and further characterized the interactions between NLRP14 and KDM2A/UHRF1 in vivo. The additional structural information on NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 and NLRP14-UHRF1 reveals that NLRP14 regulates the activity of E3 ubiquitin ligases in oocytes through two distinct mechanisms. Starting with the biochemical functions of proteins, it provides an approach for studying their biophysical functions in oocytes.

Proteostasis is critical to the developmental potential of mature oocytes and fertilized eggs, as it precisely regulates protein synthesis, folding, modification, degradation, and function42. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) in oocytes play central roles in protein quality control by eliminating damaged proteins and organelles, thereby preserving proteostasis and ensuring robust female reproductive function42. In this study, we demonstrate that NLRP14 regulates the activity of two E3 ubiquitin ligases, thereby bridging the biological pathways of protein degradation and storage. NLRP14 deficiency leads to abnormal activation of these E3 ubiquitin ligases, resulting in increased ubiquitination of their substrate proteins and further degradation (Fig. 5). In Nlrp14null mice, calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial function are disrupted, and the expression level of the key protein NCLX is sharply downregulated16. In addition, UHRF1 can regulate spindle stability by polyubiquitination of KIF11 in the cytoplasm43. Abnormal spindle formation occurs in Nlrp14null oocytes, also indicating that the ubiquitination activity is not effectively regulated14. Moreover, the observed downregulation of tubulins, including TBB1, TBB4B, TBB2B, TBA4A, and TBB5, in Nlrp14null oocytes may be related to the abnormal activation of E3 ubiquitin ligases16. Therefore, we identified NLRP14 as a modulator of protein ubiquitination during oocyte and embryo development. Interestingly, UHRF1, SKP1, NLRP14, and KDM2B were stored on cytoplasmic lattices (CPLs), which act as storage reservoirs for maternal proteins during oocyte maturation and embryogenesis44. The depletion of Nlrp14 in mice also impairs the integrity of CPLs14, pathogenically releasing stored proteins from CPLs and potentially inducing extensive abnormal activation of the ubiquitination machinery in oocytes.

NLRP14 modulates the ubiquitination activity of KDM2A and UHRF1 during the maternal-to-embryonic transition.

NLRP14 binds to both KDM2A and UHRF1. It competitively inhibits the assembly of the SCF complex mediated by KDM2A, and allosterically inhibits UHRF1 activity by occupying its UBE2D-binding site. In mice, deletion of NLRP14 elevates ubiquitination levels, dysregulating both the UPS and ALP, ultimately leading to embryonic arrest. Schematic illustration was created with BioRender. Deng, D. (2026) https://BioRender.com/1cn3dy8.

KDM2A mediates the H3K36me2 removal in the nucleus of growing oocytes to prevent the DNA hypermethylation45. Interestingly, KDM2A shows predominant cytoplasmic distribution from the GV stage to mature oocytes. Maternal UHRF1 is also located predominantly in the cytoplasm of oocytes and early embryos19,21,22. Our study demonstrates that NLRP14 deficiency causes nuclear accumulation of KDM2A in GV oocytes and leads to nuclear enrichment of UHRF1 in both zygotes and two-cell embryos. Histone modifications have an important role in regulating chromatin dynamics and function. KDM2A specifically mediates H3K36me2 demethylation. Nlrp14 knockout promotes KDM2A nuclear translocation, thereby decreasing H3K36me2 levels. In the nucleus, UHRF1 mediates the ubiquitination of H3 and further recruits DNMT146. As a result, maternal NLRP14 deficiency causes aberrant localization of UHRF1 in mouse zygotes, impairs DNA demethylation after fertilization, and impedes ZGA in embryos15. These findings suggest that NLRP14 indirectly regulates histone modifications by modulating the nuclear localization of both KDM2A and UHRF1, potentially playing a critical role in early embryonic development through modulating gene expression and ZGA.

We obtained two oligomeric states of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 in vitro. However, the biological relevance of the dimeric NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 complex in vivo remains elusive. In addition, the structures of NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 lack information on the N-terminal NBDs of NLRP14. Nevertheless, the interaction between NLRP14 and UHRF1 is primarily mediated by the NBD of NLRP14 and the UBL domain of UHRF1. Structural analysis reveals that UHRF1 in the NLRP14-UHRF1 complex sterically clashes with the dimeric NLRP14 in the NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 complex, suggesting a potential regulatory mechanism between these two complexes containing NLRP14 (Supplementary Fig 10c).

A previous study elucidated that NLRP14 interacts with TBK1 (TANK binding kinase 1), negatively regulating cytoplasmic nucleic acid-induced signaling47. In addition, NLRP14 recruits BAG2 to form the NLRP14-HSPA2-BAG2 complex, strongly inhibiting HSPA2 polyubiquitination and promoting the nuclear translocation of HSPA248. Notably, the NLRP14 interactome includes several ubiquitination-regulated proteins, such as TRIM33, UBE2N, and UBE2K16. Therefore, the relationships between NLRP14 and these interacting proteins warrant further investigation.

Methods

Ethical statement

All animal care and experimental manipulations were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 20220929004). Nlrp14-knockout mice on a C57BL/6 background were generated by Cyagen Biosciences using the CRISPR/Cas9 system as previously reported14. All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions on a 12 h light /12 h dark schedule at approximately 21–24 °C and 40–70% humidity. All mice were humanely killed via cervical dislocation. The knockout mice have been genotyped by PCR. Primers include:

KO-F1: 5’-CAGGAACACCAAAGACCATGAAG-3’

KO-R1: 5’-AAAGGACTCATACATCCAGTGGC-3’

WT-F2: 5’-GGAAACCGTGAGTGAGGACAAAG-3’

WT-R2: 5’-GTTTGTGGAGCCTCGGAAGTTTG-3’

Molecular cloning

For mammalian cell (HEK293F) expression, the human NLRP14, KDM2A (1–1162 aa, 815–1162 aa), KDM2B (942–1336 aa), UHRF1, NLRP2, and NLRP14, and murine Nlrp14, Kdm2a (815–1162 aa), Adi1, Cux1, Kdm2b (966–1312 aa), Skp1, CUL1, Rbx1 and Uhrf1 were synthesized, and the constructs were subcloned in PCAG vectors with denoted tags (Flag, Strep-tag II, HA and Flag-MBP). For insect cell (Sf9) expression, the human NLRP14 and murine Nlrp14 were cloned into a pFastBac-1 vector with Strep-tag, MBP tag, and a 3C cleavage site in tandem. The UBL domain (1–78 aa) of human and murine UHRF1 was cloned into a pFastBac-1 vector with an 8× His-tag, MBP tag, and a 3C cleavage site in tandem. Human UHRF1 and murine Uhrf1 were cloned into the pFastBac-1 vector with a C-terminal Strep-tag II. For E.coli expression, the human UBA1 and UBE2D2 were cloned into a pET15 vector with an N-terminal 6× His tag, following a drICE cutting site. The Ubiquitin was cloned into a pET15 vector with an N-terminal 6× His tag, a drICE cutting site, and an HA tag in tandem. The murine Hist1h3a gene (histone H3) was cloned into a pET21 vector, with a C-terminal 6×His tag.

Protein expression and purification

Standard instructions were followed to generate baculovirus for protein expression in Sf9 cells (Invitrogen). For the recombination complex of human NLRP14-UHRF1UBL, P2 viruses of Strep-MBP-hNLRP14 and His-MBP-UHRF1UBL were cotransfected into Sf9 cells. After 60 h of incubation, the cells were harvested by the lysis buffer (pH 8.0) containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and protease inhibitors, including 0.8 µM aprotinin (Sangon Biotech, A600153), 2 µM pepstatin A (Sangon Biotech, A610583), and 5 µg/mL leupeptin (Sangon Biotech, A600580). The cells were lysed by using a high-pressure cell disruptor at 4 °C. The lysate was centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then incubated with the Strep-Tactin resin (IBA Life Sciences, 2-4030-025). The resin was washed with lysis buffer and the tags were removed using 3 C protease. The eluted protein was further purified by gel filtration (size-exclusion chromatography, SEC, Superose 6 increase, 10/300GL, GE Healthcare) in buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT, and the peak fractions were concentrated to 1.7 mg/mL for further cryo-EM sample preparation.

For proteins used in biochemistry assays, P2 viruses encoding His-MBP tagged mNLRP14 and Strep-tagged hUHRF1/mUHRF1 were transfected into Sf9 cells. Strep-tagged hUHRF1UBL (79–793 aa) or hNLRP2 and hNLRP14, or hNLRP14E226A/F287A were transfected into HEK293F cells. His-tagged Human UBA1, UBE2D2, and Ubiquitin were expressed in E.coli. Cells were collected by lysis buffer comprising protease inhibitors and lysed at 4 °C using a high-pressure cell disruptor. The lysates were then centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C. The supernatant of His-tagged proteins flowed through the Ni-NTA column (Qiagen). The resin was rinsed with lysis buffer containing 30 mM imidazole (Sigma), and the protein was eluted with lysis buffer containing 300 mM imidazole. The supernatant containing strep-tagged UHRF1 was incubated with Strep-Tactin resin. The resin was rinsed with a high-salt buffer (pH 8.0) containing 25 mM Tris-HCl and 1 M NaCl, and the protein was eluted from the resin with 50 mM biotin in lysis buffer. The supernatant of Flag-tagged proteins (hNLRP3, hNLRP14, and hNLRP14E226A/F287A) was incubated with anti-Flag affinity gel for 1 h at 4 °C. The anti-Flag affinity gel was washed with lysis buffer. The proteins bound to the anti-Flag affinity gel were eluted by lysis buffer containing 0.2 mg/ml flag peptide. The eluted proteins were further purified by gel filtration in lysis buffer (Superose 6 Increase, 10/300GL, GE Healthcare). Histone H3 was expressed and purified following the described protocol49. The H3 was purified with Ni-NTA under denature buffer conditions (50 mM Tris 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 6 M urea). H3 was refolded by dialyzing against 1 L dialysis buffer I (50 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 2 M urea, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, pH 8.0) at 4 °C for 4 h. The protein was further dialyzed against 1 L dialysis buffer II (20 mM Na3PO4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 20% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, pH 7.0) overnight at 4 °C, and fresh cold dialysis buffer II was replaced to allow for another 4 h of dialyzation. The protein aggregate was removed by centrifugation. The purity and concentration of protein were confirmed by SDS-PAGE. All purified proteins were concentrated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Fresh human NLRP14-UHRF1UBL complexes were purified for electron microscopy (EM) sample preparation as described above. Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 grids (Au300, Quantifoil®) were glow-discharged at 15 mA for 60 seconds. The Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) was used for EM sample preparation, with conditions set at 4 °C and 100% humidity. Data collection was conducted using a 300 kV Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was equipped with a K2 Summit detector (Gatan) and operated with EPU 2.5 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All movie stacks, each containing 30 frames, were collected at a nominal magnification of 130,000 ×, with a pixel size of 1.1 Å and a defocus range of −1.1 μm to −1.8 μm. Each stack was recorded for 6.0 s, with an exposure time of 0.2 s per frame. Two data collection sessions were conducted with total doses of 55.13 e−/Å² and 53.45 e−/Å², respectively.

The purified mouse NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 monomer/dimer complex was deposited onto glow-discharged 300-mesh alloy grids (CryoMatrix Au300-R12/13) and subsequently vitrified using the Vitrobot Mark IV. Images were acquired in counted-Nanoprobe mode on a 300 kV Titan Krios G3i electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a Gatan K3 Summit detector and GIF Quantum energy filter. Using SerialEM software, movie stacks consisting of 50 frames each at a nominal magnification of 105,000× were collected with a pixel size of 0.85 Å and a defocus range of −1.2 μm to −1.8 μm. Each stack was recorded over 2.5 s, with a frame exposure of 0.05 s, resulting in a total electron dose of 53.63/55.22 e−/Å².

Single-particle data analysis

Data processing was performed using cryoSPARC version 4.1.250. A total of 4334 movie stacks were collected and motion-corrected with 2-fold binning using MotionCor251. The contrast transfer function (CTF) was then estimated using cryoSPARC50. Initially, 2181 micrographs were selected. Afterwards, 907,169 particles were automatically picked using a blob picker and extracted after binning (bin2) with a box size of 256 pixels. After 2D classification, the good particles were selected as templates for subsequent particle picking. A total of 1,687,452 particles were autopicked and extracted. Following 2D classification, 780,027 particles were used for ab-initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement. A low-resolution map was yielded by using non-uniform refinement with 371,938 particles. The good particles were selected as “seed” particles. To improve the resolution of the density map, 2143 additional movie stacks were collected. Following motion correction and CTF estimation, the particles were picked and extracted via a template picker and a blob picker. The particles were combined, and the duplicated particles were removed. Using a similar method, 894,535 particles were selected from the first dataset and combined with the particles from the second dataset. The particles were divided into six subgroups and further combined with six subgroups of the “seed” particles, respectively. A round of seed-facilitated 3D classification52 yield 715,938 high-quality particles. Several rounds of heterogeneous and homogeneous refinement were performed after the duplicated particles were removed, unbinned, and extracted, yielding a map at 3.73 Å resolution. To improve the density of the UBL domain, 3D classification was performed using a focus mask to enhance its density. After local refinement and deep EM enhancement53, we obtained a final map at 3.49 Å resolution.

For the mouse NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 monomer, a total of 3807 movies were collected and processed using RELION 5.0 and CryoSPARC. Beam-induced motion correction was performed with MotionCor2 before micrographs were imported into CryoSPARC for contrast transfer function parameter estimation using PatchCTF. 6,312,912 particles were picked using Template Picker and extracted at a pixel size of 1.70 Å. Following 2D Classification, Ab-initio Reconstruction and Heterogeneous Refinement, 1,319,851 particles were screened out. These selected particles were re-extracted in RELION at a pixel size of 0.85 Å and subjected to one round of 3D classification without alignment, utilizing a mask on the complex. Ultimately, 293,321 particles underwent Non-uniform Refinement and Local Refinement after CTF refinement and particle polishing, resulting in a density map with a resolution of 2.8 Å.

For the mouse NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 dimer, 2,718 movies were collected and processed using RELION 5.0 and CryoSPARC. Beam-induced motion was corrected with MotionCor2, and micrographs were imported into CryoSPARC for contrast transfer function estimation using PatchCTF. A total of 4,577,153 particles were selected with Template Picker and extracted at a pixel size of 1.70 Å. After 2D Classification, Ab-initio Reconstruction, and Heterogeneous Refinement, 232,686 particles were retained. These particles were re-extracted in RELION at a pixel size of 0.85 Å and subjected to one round of 3D classification without alignment, using a mask focused on the complex. Subsequently, 49,652 particles underwent Non-uniform Refinement and Local Refinement following CTF refinement and particle polishing. Local refinements, performed with a mask on each monomer, yielded electron density maps at 3.3 Å resolution for monomer A and 3.2 Å resolution for monomer B. A composite map was generated in Chimera using the vop maximum command, followed by map sharpening using Phenix. Autosharpen to enhance structural details.

Model building and refinement

The initial structures of NLRP14-UHRF1UBL and NLRP14-KDM2A-SKP1 were individually generated utilizing AlphaFold 2 and AlphaFold 324,54. Subsequently, Chimera X was used to preliminarily fit the structure into the density map55. COOT 0.9.8.1 was then used to manually adjust the model56. The model was further refined against the cryo-EM map; this process was followed by validation through the real-space refinement tool available in PHENIX 1.20.157.

In vitro ubiquitination activity assay

The ubiquitination assay followed the described protocol with minor modifications58. Briefly, 50 nM UBA1, 1 µM UBE2D2, 5 µM Ub, 5 µM histone3 H3, and 1 µM UHRF1 or UHRF1ΔUBL were incubated with or without 1 µM NLRP14, NLRP14E226A/F287A or 1 µM NLRP2 on ice in ubiquitination assay buffer (50 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, pH 7.5). The assay was started by adding 2 mM ATP and incubating at 30 °C. The ubiquitinated substrate was analysed by SDS-PAGE.

Pull-down assay, coimmunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting

For pull-down assay and coimmunoprecipitation in HEK293F cells, the constructs of NLRP14, KDM2A, KDM2B, SKP1, MTND, CUX1, CUL1, RBX1 and UHRF1 specified in the figures and figure legends were cotransfected into HEK293F cells with polyethyleneimine (25 kDa; Polysciences, 23966). The transfected cells were first cultured in a shaking incubator (37 °C, 120 rpm) for 60 h and then collected and suspended in lysis buffer. Cells were lysed by repeatedly freezing and thawing 5 times using liquid nitrogen, and the cell lysate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (input) was incubated with Strep-Tactin resin or anti-Flag affinity gel for 1 h at 4 °C. The Strep-Tactin resin or anti-Flag affinity gel was washed with lysis buffer containing 0.05% Triton X-100 (Sigma–Aldrich, 9036-19-5). The proteins bound to Strep-Tactin resin or anti-Flag affinity gel were eluted by lysis buffer containing 50 mM biotin or 0.4 mg/ml flag peptide. The input and eluate were analysed by immunoblotting with denoted antibodies.

For pull-down assay of UBL domain with UBE2D2 in the presence or absence of NLRP14, the purified Strep-GST-tagged UBL of hUHRF1 (30 µg) and His-tagged UBE2D2 (40 µg) were incubated with purified His-tagged hNLRP14 (100 µg) or an equal volume of buffer in a total volume of 400 µL system. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. GS4B resin was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Then, the resin was washed with lysis buffer containing 0.05% Triton X-100, and the resin was eluted with lysis buffer containing 20 mM reduced glutathione (GSH) (Sigma-Aldrich). The input and eluate were analysed by immunoblotting with denoted antibodies.

The antibodies used for immunoblotting were as follows: mouse anti-Flag tag (Proteintech, 66008-4-Ig), mouse anti-Strep tag (Huaxingbio, HX1988), mouse anti-His tag (Zen-bio, 230001), mouse anti-Actin conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Abclonal, AC028), mouse anti-UHRF1 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-373750), rabbit anti-NLRP14 polyclonal antibody (a gift from Professor Zhonghan Li at Sichuan University), goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with HRP (Zen-bio, 511203), and goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated with HRP (Zen-bio, 511103).

Collection of oocytes and embryos

For oocyte and embryo collection, 6- to 9-week-old female mice were injected with 10 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG; Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology, M2630). After 46–48 h, the ovaries were scraped to release GV oocytes from the follicles in the M2 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, M7167). Mature eggs were collected at 16–18 h after an additional treatment of 5 IU human chorionic gonadotrophin (HCG, Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology, M2530). Female mice were mated with males after hCG injection, and zygotes were collected 20 h post-mating. Two-cell embryos were obtained by culturing zygotes for 24 h in a KSOM medium (Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology, M1435).

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

The PLA was conducted with the Duolink® PLA Fluorescence kit (rabbit Plus probe DUO92002, mouse minus probe DUO92004, detection reagents DUO92014, washing buffer DUO82049) by following the manufacturer’s protocol (Sigma-Aldrich). Oocytes, zygotes, and two-cell embryos were collected and subsequently fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma, P6148) for 60 min at room temperature (RT). Following fixation, the samples were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 dissolved in PBS for 30 min at RT and blocked with Duolink® Blocking Solution in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 60 min. Primary antibodies were incubated with the samples in Duolink® Antibody Diluent at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the embryos were washed with PBSA (PBS containing 0.1% BSA). Subsequently, the samples were transferred to the probe mixture and incubated in a humidity chamber at 37 °C for 60 min. A washing step was then performed using 1 × Duolink® washing buffer A. Ligation of the PLA probes was carried out for 45 min at 37 °C, followed by amplification for 100 min at 37 °C in darkness. After a series of washes with 1 × Duolink® washing buffer B and 0.01 × Duolink® washing buffer B, the samples were counterstained with DAPI and visualized using an Olympus FLUOVIEW FV3000 microscope. The primary antibodies used for PLA were as follows: rabbit anti-UHRF1 polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-29884), mouse anti-NLRP14 monoclonal antibody (Wuhan Dia-An Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), rabbit anti-KDM2A (Proteintech, 24311-1-AP), mouse anti-SKP1 (Santa Cruz, sc-5281), rabbit anti-CUL1 (Invitrogen, Catalog # MA5-54225) and mouse IgG (Beyotime, A7028)

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy

Oocytes and embryos were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099) diluted by PBS) and 0.1% TritonX 100 at room temperature (RT) for 60 min, washed with wash buffer (Beyotime, P0106) three times, and permeabilized with Enhanced Immunostaining Permeabilization Buffer (Beyotime, P0097) for 60 minutes at RT. The samples were blocked with blocking solution (Beyotime, P0260) at RT for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The samples were washed in wash buffer three times and incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated fluorophore for 1 h at RT. The samples were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1006) or Hoechst 33258 (Beyotime, C1011) after washing with wash buffer three times. The confocal images were captured with an Olympus FLUOVIEW FV3000. The antibodies used for IF staining were as follows: mouse anti-NLRP14 monoclonal antibody (Wuhan Dia-An Biotechnology Co. LTD) and mouse anti-UHRF1 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-373750) conjugated to Alexa Fluor®647 and Alexa Fluor®555 respectively using the Apex™ Alexa Fluor™ antibody labeling kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, A20186, A20187), mouse anti-Ubiquitin monoclonal antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, ST1200), rabbit anti-LAMP1 monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 99437), rabbit anti-H3K36me2 (Cell Signaling Technology, C75H12) and mouse anti-20S proteasome monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-58410).

Coimmunoprecipitation of oocytes and ovaries

Approximately 800 GV oocytes were harvested from 3-week-old female mice after treatment with PMSG, and these oocytes were then lysed at 4 °C for 20 minutes using the cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, #P0013) containing proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Apexbio, #K1007). Then, the protein A/G magnetic beads (Bimake, #B23201) were washed three times with the lysis buffer. The prepared beads and NLRP14 antibodies were introduced to the samples and incubated overnight at 4 °C. A total of 10 ovaries from 8-week-old female mice were collected and cut into pieces. Then these ovaries were lysed at 4 °C for 1 h using the cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, #P0013) containing proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Apexbio, #K1007). The IgG antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #02-6102) and KDM2A antibodies (Proteintech, 24311-1-AP) were introduced to the samples and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The protein A/G magnetic beads (Bimake, #B23201) were washed three times with the lysis buffer. The prepared beads were introduced to the samples and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the proteins were precipitated and washed with IP lysis buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, #87787). The coprecipitated proteins were eluted in 1 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min and visualized by immunoblotting. The primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were as follows: mouse anti-NLRP14 monoclonal antibody (Wuhan Dia-An Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), mouse anti-UHRF1 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-373750), and rabbit anti-KDM2A monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, 24311-1-AP).

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

All experiments were performed at least three times independently with similar results, as described in the figure legends. When comparing oocytes of distinct genotypes, other experimental parameters, particularly the age of the mice, were rigorously controlled. To reduce individual differences, samples were collected from at least three mice in each experimental and control group, and at least 5 embryo/oocyte images were scanned by confocal microscopy. Fluorescence foci number and size were quantified in FIJI using the “Analyse Particle” function, selecting objects larger than 1 μm2 in the reference channel, as described by Jentoft, I.M.A. et al.44. Prism v10.6.0 was used to perform statistical analysis. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used to compare two different groups.
