A Spinning Nightmare of Twiddler's Syndrome?
Authors: Nathan Flores Miranda, Habib Rehman Khan, Ahmed T. Moustafa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwiddler's syndrome is a known complication of CIED implantation. Most published cases of Twiddler's syndrome are described as being secondary to patient manipulation of the pulse generator leading to horizontal rotation and “reeling” of the pacemaker leads around the generator. The authors of this paper present a case of an 81‐year‐old female who had her anchoring suture across the pulse generator break after implant. This led to rotation of the pulse generator in the vertical axis and subsequently led to RV lead rotation in the longitudinal axis. The patient developed rising thresholds and low impedance on the RV lead, with fluoroscopy confirming an insulation break and lead fracture. Due to the nature of the lead rotation, extraction was impossible, and the lead was capped and buried. The case report highlights a unique mechanism by which a lead can rotate along its longitudinal axis, and is a reminder to implanting physicians regarding the importance of proper anchoring techniques.

Introduction

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) such as implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs), permanent pacemakers (PPMs) and cardiac resynchronization therapy systems (CRTs) have increased in prevalence in the modern era due to expanding indications and an aging population [1, 2]. With the increasing prevalence of CIEDs, clinicians should be aware of the potential complications leading to device malfunction. One such complication, Twiddler's syndrome, is characterized by lead disruption secondary to intentional or unintentional manipulation of the CIED generator in the pocket [3]. Twiddler's syndrome was first described in 1968, and the modern‐day prevalence is estimated at 1.2%, with female sex, obesity, and antidepressant use as potential predictors [4]. The authors of this paper present a unique case with Twiddler's syndrome secondary to improper device implantation resulting in insulation breach and lead fracture.

Case Report

An 81‐year‐old female with a past medical history of permanent atrial fibrillation underwent implantation of a single‐chamber PPM with a Medtronic 5076 CapsureFix Novus MRI (Medtronic, Minneapolis) right ventricular (RV) lead prior to atrioventricular (AV) node ablation. Routine scheduled device interrogations revealed a gradual rising threshold on the RV lead from 0.750 V/0.4 ms at the time of device implant to 2 V/0.4 ms (Figure 1). The lead showed declining impedance in the unipolar configuration and also recorded > 60 short V‐V intervals. All of the above were concerning for a potential lead integrity issue. The patient mentioned the generator would sometimes stand on its edge (vertical orientation) within the pocket. A chest radiograph illustrated rotation of the pulse generator and significant torsion of the RV lead compared to radiographs post implant (Figures 2, 3).

The patient underwent a lead revision. During the case, it was noted that the anchoring suture for the PM generator was broken, and the generator was freely mobile and sliding in the suprapectoral plane. There was significant lead tortuosity where the lead connected to the header. With fluoroscopy, it was evident that the lead exhibited significant tortuosity along its longitudinal axis, wrapping around itself within the right atrium (Figure 4 and Video S1). Upon magnifying the RA portion of the lead, it was obvious the significant torsion completely avulsed the insulation. This explained the changes noted on lead testing. Several attempts were made to disengage the helix from the ventricular myocardium; however, applying counterclockwise rotation on the header end of the lead did not transmit to the helix, likely due to the significant tortuosity within the lead. Similarly, the torsion was not able to be undone, owing to adhesions within the RA. Gentle retraction was applied to the RV lead, and this also did not come apart from the myocardium. It was decided that further attempts to remove the lead would impart significant risk to the patient, and therefore the RV lead was capped and buried, and a new RV lead was implanted.

Discussion

Twiddler's Syndrome is a well‐established complication of CIED implantation; however, the typical case involves rotation or torsion of the lead within the pocket itself around the pulse generator, sometimes dislodging the lead. This is the first case in the literature demonstrating a mobile generator, which led to transmitted longitudinal torsion at the level of the deployed helix at the ventricular insertion point with significant lead torsion on the RA. This was a noteworthy complication, as it resulted in our inability to retract the helix and remove the lead. At the time of revision, we noted the anchoring suture was broken, allowing the device to be freely mobile inside the pocket. This could be secondary to improper knot‐tying technique, the use of an absorbable suture, or a small tissue bite on the anchoring stitch. The case is a reminder to implanting physicians of the importance of proper technique when anchoring the CIED within the pocket and the potential complications that can arise. The patient mentioned that she felt the mobile generator in the pocket and standing “vertical.” The case raises an important consideration for clinicians regarding the optimal timing for pocket exploration and re‐securing the generator as a means of preventing long‐term complications. The patient in the presented case report reported no further “spinning” of her generator in the pocket after the revision, and her lead parameters on the new RV lead have been stable.
