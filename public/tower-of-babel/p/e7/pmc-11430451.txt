Story of an infection: Viral dynamics and host responses in the Caenorhabditis elegans–Orsay virus pathosystem
Authors: Victoria G. Castiglioni, María J. Olmo-Uceda, Ana Villena-Giménez, Juan C. Muñoz-Sánchez, Esmeralda G. Legarda, Santiago F. Elena
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Orsay virus (OrV) is the only known natural virus affecting Caenorhabditis elegans, with minimal impact on the animal’s fitness due to its robust innate immune response. This study aimed to understand the interactions between C. elegans and OrV by tracking the infection’s progression during larval development. Four distinct stages of infection were identified on the basis of viral load, with a peak in capsid-encoding RNA2 coinciding with the first signs of viral egression. Transcriptomic analysis revealed temporal changes in gene expression and functions induced by the infection. A specific set of up-regulated genes remained active throughout the infection, and genes correlated and anticorrelated with virus accumulation were identified. Responses to OrV mirrored reactions to other biotic stressors, distinguishing between virus-specific responses and broader immune responses. Moreover, mutants of early response genes and defense-related processes showed altered viral load progression, uncovering additional players in the antiviral defense response.

INTRODUCTION

Viruses, the most abundant biological entities on Earth, infect all known living organisms (1, 2). From bacteria to eukaryotes, most organisms have immune systems to defend themselves against viruses. Viruses, in turn, adapt to their hosts by evolving strategies to evade or neutralize the host’s defenses. This ongoing arms race between viruses and their hosts has driven the development of complex interactions and defense mechanisms (3, 4).

The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans Maupas lacks adaptive immunity (5). Instead, it uses the innate immune system to defend itself against viruses and other microbes, such as its natural pathogen Orsay virus (OrV) (6). OrV has a positive-sense, bisegmented RNA genome of ~6.3 kb, which encodes four proteins: an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP), a viral capsid protein (CP), protein δ, and a protein fusion of CP-δ (6, 7). Free δ mediates nonlytic viral release (8), and the CP-δ fusion is incorporated into the virions as pentameric head fibers (9, 10).

OrV replicates in the intestinal epithelia of C. elegans (6, 11), which consists of 20 single-layer cells surrounding the central lumen. Infection of OrV is restricted to the anterior intestinal cells and, after cell egression, results in an orofecal route of transmission (6). The infection results in enlarged intestinal lumens and subcellular structural changes. However, the infection does not affect animal life span, suggesting that the innate immune system is able to effectively combat the virus (12).

Several conserved antiviral defense mechanisms that C. elegans uses against OrV infection have been described, including (i) the RNA interference pathway, which degrades viral RNA and triggers antiviral gene expression (12–14); (ii) viral RNA uridylation, which targets viral RNA to degradation (15); and (iii) expression of antiviral genes, which include protein ubiquitination for targeted degradation and the activation of the intracellular pathogen response (IPR), which is a common response to intracellular pathogens and stresses (13, 16–18). In addition, several studies have described pathways necessary for OrV replication, such as zinc and the lipid metabolism (19), and cytoskeleton rearrangements (20, 21). The latter have also been reported to have antiviral functions, as have chromatin remodelers (21).

Studies of OrV infection have allowed us to better understand virus-host interaction mechanisms. However, there is little known on the progression of the viral infection and in the dynamic interaction between C. elegans development and OrV during the course of an infection. To shed light in these matters, we have analyzed the progression of the infection and the host response in a time-resolved manner in the mildly resistant wild-type nematode strain N2. By examining the progression of the viral load, we observed that the infection could be divided into four stages, noted that the ratio of the two genomic RNAs varied during the different phases of the infection, and reported signs of viral egression after the first peak, suggesting that a viral generation would be complete. By looking at the changes in the transcriptome along the four infection stages, we pinpointed a set of genes up-regulated throughout the infection process, highlighted genes most correlated and anticorrelated with virus accumulation, and saw responses to OrV that are frequently linked to other biotic stressors and a unique response to OrV, which differed from the response to other biotic stresses. Last, we identified early responding genes and described proviral and antiviral genes.

RESULTS

Dynamics of OrV accumulation during larval development

Wild-type (N2) animals are mildly resistant to OrV infection (6). However, inoculations with high doses of OrV have been previously shown to produce high infection rates in N2 (22). Here, we used a highly concentrated stock and observed high infection rates in the N2-background strain ERT54, which contains a fluorescent reporter that gets activated upon OrV infection (fig. S1A) (16). The use of this highly concentrated stock enabled the study of a synchronous infection in wild-type populations during larval development.

We first followed the progression of the viral load in a synchronized population from hatching to the end of larval development (L4). For this purpose, we inoculated animals at the time of hatching and took samples at increasing hours post-inoculation (hpi) for quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and single-molecule inexpensive RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (smiFISH) analyses (Fig. 1A). Three biological replicates, with 700 animals for RT-qPCR and smiFISH or 1200 animals for RNA-seq, were done for each time point. Viral counts analyzed by RT-qPCRs targeted RNA2 were determined using the standard curve method and were used as a proxy for viral load. OrV reads counted by RNA-seq were relativized to the total reads in each sample and were also used as a proxy for viral load. Both analyses indicated that the progression of the viral load could be significantly divided into four different phases (Fig. 1B; best isotonic regression model R = 0.7562, F4,211 = 70.4516, P < 0.0001): (i) a prereplication phase in which the viral load is indistinguishable from background levels (0 to 6 hpi), (ii) an exponential viral replication leading to a viral load peak (8 to 12 hpi), (iii) a phase of moderate viral load levels (14 to 36 hpi), and (iv) a phase of persistent residual infection or resolution of the infection, in which the viral load came close to the initial background levels (38 to 44 hpi). RT-qPCR and RNA-seq quantifications of OrV showed a high correlation [rs = 0.9720, 10 degrees of freedom (df), P < 0.0001], confirming that both methods are reliable estimates of viral load.

Furthermore, we sought to characterize the percentage of infected animals throughout larval development. We inoculated animals at the time of hatching and took sequential samples for smiFISH analysis (23). RNA1 and RNA2 were labeled independently with 22 and 18 smiFISH probes, respectively. We observed that all the animals that expressed RNA2 also expressed RNA1, but the reverse was not necessarily true. RNA1 encodes the RdRP; therefore, it would be reasonable that its levels would rise faster than RNA2 during early infection. Accumulation of RdRP has been shown to precede the accumulation of the CPs (11). We observed that the incidence of infection in the animal population recapitulated the same four phases described above: (i) a prereplication phase in which the percentage of infected animals is close to zero and the smiFISH signal was hardly above background (4 hpi); (ii) an exponential increase in the percentage of infected animals in which the intensity of signal quickly increased, leading to a peak in infection (6 to 14 hpi); (iii) a phase of moderate stable signal (18 to 32 hpi); and (iv) a phase of persistent residual infection in which the percentage of infected animals was notably reduced and the intensity of signal was close to background levels but still detectable in puncta (36 to 44 hpi) (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the highest percentage of infected animals was observed earlier than the viral load peak, at 8 and 12 hpi, respectively, which may suggest that some animals are either able to resolve the infection quickly or not able to develop a productive infection.

We next asked whether the accumulation of RNA1 and RNA2 undergoes a shift in gene expression from early to late replication cycle stages. We hypothesized that accumulation of RNA1, which codes for the RdRP, would be higher at the beginning of the viral replication cycle when viral replication is higher, while levels of structural proteins from RNA2 would became more abundant toward the end of the viral replication cycle as virions are being assembled (6). For this purpose, we looked at both RNA1 and RNA2 labeling with smiFISH and at viral counts relativized to fragment size from RNA-seq. At the initial phases of the infection, a higher percentage of animals accumulate RNA1 in comparison with RNA2, indicating that higher RNA1 levels are needed before transcription of RNA2 (Fig. 1, C and D). Consistently, RNA-seq data indicated that the highest RNA1 to RNA2 ratio is at the beginning of the infection. However, RNA2 was always more abundant than RNA1, with an average 2.40 ± 1.62 RNA2 to RNA1 ratio throughout all time points and a ratio of 1.81 ± 0.43 RNA2 to RNA1 in our inoculum (Fig. 1E). The ratio shows a dynamic behavior, with higher values coinciding with the viral load peaks and the highest ratio coinciding with the second viral peak, indicating that RNA2 is more abundant before the end of the viral replication cycle and consistent with the need of RNA2-encoded products for encapsidation (Fig. 1E).

Together, our data show that the infection is highly dynamic, with the viral load, the percentage of infected worms, and the RNA2 to RNA1 ratios varying rapidly and going through four distinct infection phases from the beginning to the end of the larval development.

Spatial progression of infection

Next, we characterized the spatial distribution of viral RNAs in the animal. In the initial infection phases, viral particles were only observed inside intestinal cells (Figs. 1, C and D, and 2, A and B). However, at 14 hpi, viral RNA was first seen in the intestinal lumen, indicating a productive release of viral particles. By 24 hpi, most animals showed a strong signal in the lumen (Fig. 2, A and B). These findings suggest that RNA1 is prioritized for translation and transcription after viral entry. Around 6 hours after RdRP translation (11), RNA2 levels begin to rise, reaching their peak at 12 hpi. Following this peak, we notice a decline in RNA2 levels, along with a substantial relocation of OrV to the lumen, signifying egression. Overall, these results imply that a viral replication cycle might conclude in as little as 14 hpi.

OrV has been described to colonize the anterior intestinal cells (11), but whether a late infection can also colonize posterior intestinal cells or whether the number of infected cells increases over time is still unknown. To answer these questions, we quantified the relative infected area per animal at 10 and 24 hpi (Fig. 2C). We did not observe a significant difference in the relative infected area at 10 versus 24 hpi (Mann-Whitney test, P = 0.3713), suggesting that the infection does not spread within the animal from the first viral load peak to the second viral load peak. We also quantified the total number of infected cells per infected animal during time and did not observe a significant difference at any time point (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.3239; Fig. 2D), indicating that in most animals, throughout the viral replication cycle, OrV only colonizes, on median, one to two intestinal cells.

Together, these results show that posterior colonization of the intestine does not take place within the observed time span and that a viral replication cycle might conclude in as little as 14 hpi.

C. elegans dynamic transcriptional response to OrV infection

To better understand the complexity of the host’s responses to a viral infection, we next focused on the transcriptomic response of C. elegans to OrV through time. For this purpose, we compared the transcriptome of control and infected animals at 12 time points from 0 to 44 hpi (Fig. 1A and table S1) by performing a differential expression analysis (DEA) by time point, which revealed a complex landscape (Fig. 3). This is to date the highest resolution time-series dataset produced in this pathosystem. Our goal was to provide the most sensitive landscape of transcriptional responses, so we considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) all genes with a P < 0.05, without filtering by magnitude of change. We found a significant correlation (rs = 0.9000, 9 df, P = 0.0002) between the number of DEGs (Fig. 3A) and OrV accumulation at each time point (Fig. 1B) with a predominance of up-regulated genes in the first 18 hpi and a more symmetric number of up- and down-regulated responses from 22 hpi on, although the strongest changes still correspond to overexpressed genes (Fig. 3, A and C). Filters on the magnitude of change revealed similar results on the correlation test (DEGs with |log2FC| ≥ 1: rs = 0.8975, 9 df, P = 0.0002; |log2FC| ≥ 2: rs = 0.8909, 9 df, P = 0.0004). Moreover, we identified a group of 29 up-regulated DEGs at all time points with high viral loads (i.e., ≥0.5% of the total sample reads) (Fig. 3B and fig. S3). All these DEGs were up-regulated between 6 to at least 32 hpi; col-133 was up-regulated at every time point analyzed (Fig. 3C and fig. S3B). Twenty-one of these 29 genes—including eol-1, ddn-1, and the pals genes—were already characterized as part of the IPR (18). Among the rest, math-39 and Y105C5A.9 were identified as dependent of the Zinc-induced protein-1 (ZIP-1) transcription factor highly associated with IPR (24) and positive modulator of nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (25). col-133, math-15, trpl-5, Y43F8B.12, and Y6G8.5 had not been previously associated to the animal’s response to OrV. A smaller group—formed by col-133, ddn-1, eol-1, fbxa-182, math-15, pals-33, and pals-37—was differentially expressed from 6 hpi until the end of the time course (Fig. 3, B and C). Considering only genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1, the ubiquitous sets are reduced to 15 and 6, respectively (Fig. 3B, genes remaining highlighted in yellow), and the novel players math-15 and Y43F8B.12 remain in the selection.

We then performed a functional enrichment analysis (FEA). The FEA was carried out on the up- and down-regulated genes separately, and, contrary to what happened with DEGs, the number of significant categories in the down-regulated responses was bigger than in the up-regulated ones (Fig. 3D). Specifically, we found a first strong response between 12 and 18 hpi in the three Gene Ontologies (GOs) [biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC)] and in the metabolic data from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Wikipathways and a second massive response around 26 hpi in all the databases explored (Fig. 3D; complete results in table S2).

When focusing on the up-regulated responses in the early stages of infection, at 6 hpi, the following GO:BPs were enriched: nervous system development, axon extension involved in axon guidance, nuclear RNA surveillance, and regulation of transcription DNA templated (fig. S2A); and the GO:MFs related with 5′,3′-exonuclease activity and RNAII polymerase transcription factor activities (fig. S2B).

When focusing on the early down-regulated genes, we observed an earlier enrichment in significant terms. Within the first 2 hpi, there was a down-regulation of genes involved in extracellular matrix organization; within 6 hpi in genes involved in collagen and cuticle development (fig. S2A) and lipase activity (fig. S2B); and within 8 hpi in genes related to helicase activities and uridine diphosphate (UDP)–glycosyltransferase activities (fig. S2B). When analyzing the DEGs using KEGG pathways, we noticed an enrichment in other types of O-glycan biosynthesis at 8 hpi (fig. S2C), a down-regulation in mitochondrial translation and proteolysis at 12 hpi (fig. S2A), and significant effects upon peroxisome, lysosome, metabolism of sphingolipids, pentose phosphate pathway, and ABC transporters (fig. S2C).

During the moderate phase, we observed up-regulated genes related with the defense response to bacterium, the regulation of tube size and epithelial cell differentiation (fig. S2A), and polyadenylate [poly(A)]– and poly(U)-RNA binding and receptor tyrosine kinase binding (fig. S2B). In contrast, the down-regulated genes were associated with the control of proteolysis by protein deneddylation (removal of ubiquitin-like protein), regulation of transport and stress response to Cd++, as well as fatty acid elongation (fig. S2A). Towards the end of this phase, genes related to autophagy were also down-regulated.

During the persistent residual infection phase, there was an increase of significant terms among the overexpressed genes, including genes related with the regulation of mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, Rho protein signaling transduction, mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), and acetyl-CoA metabolic processes (fig. S2A). Genes related with the glutathione metabolism were enriched from 38 hpi (fig. S2C), and some categories previously identified as down-regulated were up-regulated in this phase, including fatty acid elongation and very long fatty acid metabolic process (fig. S2A), pentose phosphate pathway, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (fig. S2C).

DEGs that correlate or anticorrelate with the virus accumulation

Taking advantage of the time-course experiment design, we performed a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to identify the genes with an expression profile altered by the infection (interaction between condition and time). This contrast revealed a total of 2313 DEGs (adjusted P value ≤ 0.0499; Fig. 4A and table S3). For exploratory reasons, the genes were clustered by their profiles on 32 different groups (fig. S4). When focusing in the expression pattern of the 150 genes with the lowest adjusted P value (the most significant genes from hereon) (Fig. 4B), we noticed that they could be grouped into five different profiles (Fig. 4, C to G), distinguishing between early up-regulation (Fig. 4C), late up-regulation (Fig. 4, D and E), and late down-regulation (Fig. 4, F and G). Some of the genes highlighted above (ddn-1, eol-1, and ZC196.3), and multiple pals (pals-1, -3, -4, -5, -27, -30, -32, -37, -38, and -39) share the early up-regulation profile (Fig. 4C).

Once the genes with a differential expression pattern due to infection were identified, we searched among them for those that would present a differential expression pattern correlated with the dynamics of OrV accumulation. A total of 45 genes showed a highly positive correlation (r ≥ 0.8011, 9 df, P ≤ 0.0030) between their differential expression and OrV accumulation (Fig. 4H), while 13 presented an anticorrelated profile (r ≤ −0.8012, 9 df, P ≤ 0.0030) (Fig. 4I). Among the genes with a positive correlation, two had a r ≥ 0.9148 (9 df, P < 0.0001) bris-1, enriched in neurons and predicted to enable guanyl-nucleotide exchange factor activity and to be involved in regulation of ADP-ribosylation factor (ARF) protein signal transduction, and F46A8.13, a long noncoding RNA also enriched in neurons. In contrast, elo-9, a protein with fatty acid elongase activity, had an r = −0.9131 (9 df, P ≤ 0.0001). Some of the genes with a positive correlation were already associated with OrV response: Y39G8B.5 and ZC196.3 are cataloged as part of the IPR (18), and C17E4.6, pes-4, pxf-1, taf-6.2, T07A5.7, and Y39G8B.5 are ZIP-1 dependent (24). Moreover, clec-24, a C-type lectin predicted to be associated with the membrane and an antimicrobial effector, displays a differential expression pattern with a negative correlation with virus accumulation. Among the aforementioned, T07A5.7, Y39G8B.5, and ZC196.3 show the expression profile in Fig. 4C, while acs-5 is a characteristic representant of the expression profile shown in Fig. 4G.

Together, we found a group of 45 genes with a differential expression profile that highly correlates with OrV accumulation (r ≥ 0.8), with IPR genes such as ZC196.3, and 13 genes with a negative correlation (r ≤ −0.8), such as tpi-1.

Shared responses with other infections

After characterizing the animal’s responses to OrV infection on a temporal scale, we wanted to tease apart those specific to OrV infection from those common to other biotic stresses. For this purpose, we took advantage of the transcriptomic results available at WormExp 2.0 Microbes database (26) and the results of four more experiments performed with OrV, Nematocida parisii and Myzocytiopsis humicola (13, 27). A total of 56 experiments were included in this analysis (fig. S5 and table S4), comparing the results obtained in wild-type N2 animals upon infection with OrV (n = 12), Gram-negative (n = 21) and Gram-positive (n = 18) bacteria, and fungi (n = 5). With this analysis, we found 188 protein coding genes associated only to the OrV transcriptomic response (OrV-specific genes from now) and 208 genes affected by all pathogens categories (Fig. 5A). Differences in the developmental stage of the nematode at the time of inoculation, the sampling time since infection, the transcriptomic technique used, and the transcriptomic analysis itself likely contribute to the differences, but our goal here was to sketch a broad picture of comparisons that could open new avenues for further analysis.

We noticed a variety of gene expression profiles within the OrV-specific responding genes (Fig. 5B). A functional enrichment of the OrV-specific genes highlighted the cytoplasm and the mitochondria components and cellular metabolic processes. Amino acid biosynthesis and peptide-related categories were also significantly enriched, as well as the pentose phosphate pathway (fig. S6B and complete results in table S6). Some of the OrV-specific genes—akir-1, ash-2, pxf-1, smu-2, taf-6.2, unc-57, W04A8.1, Y65B4A.4, and ztf-6—are among those positively correlated to virus accumulation (Fig. 4H and fig. S6A, blue border), while F46B6.6 and tpi-1 are anticorrelated to OrV viral load (Fig. 4H and fig. S6A, burgundy border). The topology analysis also revealed tpi-1, a triose phosphate isomerase involved in several metabolic pathways, as the most connected gene in the StringDB network (fig. S6A).

Next, we asked whether these specific responses to viral infections were shared with other species of Caenorhabditis. To this end, we compared the DEGs upon infection of Caenorhabditis briggsae with Santeuil virus (13) with our results. We found that ~32% of the genes reported in (13) were also affected in this OrV–C. elegans experiment (table S4). From them, only C49C8.8 (WBGene000425 in C. briggsae) was differentially expressed upon infection with both viruses but not with the other biotic stresses (table S5). C49C8.8, which is overexpressed at 8 hpi and has an expression pattern resembling the damping waves profile (fig. S4, cluster 8), appears to be the most specific to both virus responses.

Within the set of genes affected by all pathogens (Fig. 5A), we found 23 of the previously identified as ubiquitous genes (B0507.6, C53A5.9, ddn-1, droe-8, F26F2.1, F26F2.3, F42C5.3, F49H6.5, fbxa-182, math-15, pals-4, pals-5, pals-14, pals-27, pals-30, pals-33, pals-37, pals-38, pals-39, T08E11.1, trpl-5, Y105C5A.9, and Y46G5A.20). In addition, a functional enrichment of this general responses highlighted the extracellular region, the innate immune responses, collagen- and cuticle-related genes, and metabolic pathways related with the biosynthesis of lipids (fig. S7 and table S7).

Next, we focused on three specific immune-related categories: IPR response, antimicrobial effectors, and lipid metabolism. We found 65 of the 80 IPR genes within our DEGs (adjusted P ≤ 0.0468), with activation responses earlier than previously documented (fig. S8A) (28), but more surprisingly, 60% of IPR genes were altered in all pathogen categories (fig. S7A, purple border). More specifically, some of the IPR genes were involved in the response to extracellular pathogens: 52.5% of the IPR genes were differentially expressed at 12 hpi with Bacillus thurigiensis and 48.8% at 24 hpi with Enterococcus faecalis (fig. S8B) (26), suggesting that genes involve in the IPR could play a role in the response to a wider range of pathogens.

We also noticed more antimicrobial effectors involved in antiviral responses than previously reported (29): Several C-type lectins were up- and down-regulated (fig. S9A) (30); the lysozyme lys-3 was up-regulated at 18 and 26 hpi; lys-2 at 32 hpi; and lys-4, lys-5, and lys-6 were down-regulated from 26 hpi. Moreover, the antimicrobial effectors clec-10, clec-45, clec-51, clec-53, clec-67, clec-70, clec-72, clec-80, clec-82, clec-166, clec-227, cnc-4, lys-2, lys-5, and lys-6 were described as differentially expressed upon OrV, Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, and fungi infections (fig. S9B).

The lipid metabolism plays key roles in a variety of diseases, whether pathogenic in origin or not (31). The feedback between the OrV replication and lipid metabolism has been already studied (19). Here, we found almost 35% of the genes associated with lipid metabolism (GO0006629) in our DEGs list (fig. S8A). Of these, hyl-1, pcyt-2.1, psd-1, scp-1, and T26C12.1 were among the OrV-specific genes, and hyl-1 and scp-1, which were significantly up-regulated at 18 and 22 hpi, respectively, are predicted to be regulatory genes. A general trend toward down-regulation was observed, as previously described (19). However, the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids was one of the most significant categories enriched in the common genes (fig. S7). Notably, the metabolism of lipids is also important in the responses to other biotic stresses (fig. S8B), and dld-1, fat-2, fat-5, fat-6, and sptl-2 are part of the common response to all pathogens tested so far.

Together, in these comparisons, we have identified a group of 208 genes altered in all pathogen categories where the extracellular compartment and the lipid metabolism are revealed as enriched, which includes 60% of the IPR genes. In contrast, a set of 188 genes were differentially expressed only in response to OrV, with a predominance of the cytoplasm and mitochondria components and amino acid– and peptide-related categories. The repertoire of antimicrobial effectors used by the animal against OrV infection has been also expanded.

Pro- and anti-viral functions of DEGs

We lastly sought to perform an initial characterization of the roles of some of the DEGs upon OrV infection. We selected the secondary argonautes ppw-1, sago-2, and vsra-1, which were differentially up-regulated early in infection (fig. S9A). We also selected lys-2, a lysozyme involved in the defense response to bacteria and fungi, and the antimicrobial peptide clec-60 due to their roles in the defense response against microorganisms (fig. S9A). In addition, we selected gst-35, a glutathione transferase that may have roles in detoxification and cell signaling; eol-1, an RNA pyrophosphohydrolase involved in the regulation of olfactory learning; and C53A5.9, a paralog of influenza virus NS1a binding protein homolog because they were significantly up-regulated during early infection (2 to 6 hpi) (fig. S9A). Last, we included drh-1 and pals-22 mutants as controls for sensitivity and resistance, respectively (12, 18).

To assess the role of these proteins upon OrV infection, we studied whether the absence of these proteins would affect viral accumulation and infectivity. We first examined the impact of these proteins on OrV infectivity by looking at three key factors: the percentage of infected animals (Fig. 6, A and B), the RNA2 signal-positive animals (Fig. 6C), and the number of infected cells at 12 hpi (Fig. 6D), which aligns with the initial peak of viral load in wild-type animals (Fig. 1B). For infectivity, the fit of the data to a logistic regression revealed significant differences among genotypes (χ2 = 394.9264, 10 df, P < 0.0001). Further Bonferroni sequential post hoc test identified five distinct groups (adjusted P ≤ 0.0395). Mutants clec-60, gst-35, lys-2, and sago-2 showed no significant difference from the wild type. By contrast, pals-22(jy3) and ppw-1(pk2505) formed a group with an ~80% reduction in infectivity, while eol-1(tm6609) and vsra-1(tm1637) had a significant but smaller reduction (33.92 and 50.47%, respectively). Conversely, drh-1(ok3495) was 17.96% more susceptible to infection.

(B) Percentage of infected animals at 12 hpi as determined by smiFISH. n = 49 ± 5 per point. Error bars represent ±1 SD. (C) Ratio of RNA2 to RNA1 positive animals determined by smiFISH in the indicated mutants. n = 27 ± 12 per point. Error bars represent ±1 SD. (D) Number of infected cells per infected animal over time as determined by smiFISH. Density plot represents the relative abundance of the number of infected cells within each mutant. (E) Viral load (±1 SEM; n = 5 replicates per time point) determined by RT-qPCR at 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 18, 24, and 44 hpi in clec-60, C53A5.9, eol-1, gst-35, lys-2, ppw-1, sago-2, and vsra-1 mutants (colored lines and dots) as well as wild type (black lines and dots). P values correspond to the result of the sequential Bonferroni post hoc pairwise test comparing the temporal dynamics of OrV accumulation on each mutant with the wild-type animals. See also fig. S9.

Regarding the RNA2-positive animals, significant differences were found among genotypes (logistic regression: χ2 = 176.7448, 10 df, P < 0.0001; Fig. 6C). Three significant groups emerged (post hoc–adjusted P ≤ 0.0473). pals-22(jy3) had an 88.36% lower accumulation of RNA2 compared to wild type relative to RNA1. ppw-1(pk2505) and vsra-1(tm1637) showed a moderate reduction (~36%). The rest of the mutants showed no significant difference from wild type.

We then analyzed the distribution of infected cells per animal (Fig. 6D). Because of the non-normal distribution and heteroscedasticity of the data, a Kruskal-Wallis test was used, showing significant differences among genotypes (χ2 = 184.4403, 10 df, P < 0.0001). Games-Howell post hoc analysis revealed three distinct groups (adjusted P ≤ 0.0473). At the one side, pals-22 (jy3) had only one infected cell per animal, significantly less than the wild type. At the other side, lys-2(tm2398) and drh-1(ok3495) had roughly 53% more infected cells than wild-type animals.

Together, these results suggest that LYS-2 and DRH-1 prevent multicellular OrV colonization and PALS-22, PPW-1, and VSRA-1, which had lower infectivity; a reduction in RNA2 expression relative to RNA1; and fewer infected cells per animal, hinder OrV amplification early in viral replication. The other mutants behaved similar to the wild type in these analyses.

To enhance our understanding of these proteins’ roles in OrV infection, we proceeded to examine the viral accumulation in the mutants. We monitored the progression of viral load in synchronized mutant populations from hatching to the end of larval development (L4 stage). OrV inoculation took place upon hatching, and samples were collected at various hpi for RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 6E). Five replicates with 700 animals each were used per condition and time point. The data from the accumulation curves were fitted to a generalized linear model with a gamma distribution and log-link function. Animal genotype and hpi were considered as model factors. We specifically looked for differences in the temporal dynamics of OrV accumulation between mutant and wild-type animals and conducted sequential Bonferroni post hoc pairwise tests. We observed significantly higher OrV accumulation in lys-2(tm2398) and sago-2(tm894) (P < 0.0001), with lys-2(tm2398) showing a fourfold increase in viral load at 10 hpi, and sago-2(tm894) exhibiting a 1.6-fold increase at 12 hpi. This suggests that these genes may play a direct role in the antiviral response. Conversely, we noted significantly lower OrV accumulation in the other secondary argonautes, ppw-1(pk2505) and vsra-1(tm1637) (P ≤ 0.0001), with viral load peaks at 18 hpi. This trend was also observed in the antimicrobial peptide clec-60(tm2319) (P < 0.0001), along with a disrupted OrV accumulation pattern in C53A5.9(tm10539) and eol-1(tm6609) (P ≤ 0.0009). Last, we did not detect differential OrV accumulation in gst-35(esv1) (P = 0.2109), suggesting that its altered expression upon infection may be an indirect effect unrelated to the defense response. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that redundancy within the glutathione transferase family might be masking a potential role in OrV infection.

From these experiments we conclude that C53A5.9, clec-60, eol-1, ppw-1, and vsra-1 had proviral functions, while lys-2 and sago-2 appear to have antiviral functions. Gst-35 appears to be either redundant or not involved in the defense response. However, more experiments would be necessary to clarify the role of any of these proteins in OrV infection.

DISCUSSION

OrV dynamics along larval development

In this study, we followed the progression of a viral infection within a population, which was synchronously infected. Regardless of the quantification method used, we observed that the infection was highly dynamic and followed four phases: (i) a prereplication phase in which the viral load and percentage of infected animals remained low and RNA1 was preferentially transcribed, which lasted ~6 hours; (ii) an exponential viral replication phase in which the viral load and the number of infected animals rapidly increased, spanning from 8 to 14 hpi; (iii) a phase of moderate replication in which the percentage of infected animals and the viral load remained fairly constant, from 16 to 36 hpi; and (iv) a phase of persistent residual infection from 38 to 44 hpi (Fig. 7). The ratio of RNA2 to RNA1 was dynamic throughout the infection and started with ~2 RNA2 molecules per RNA1 molecule, which opens the possibility that more RNA2 gets encapsulated in each virion. Moreover, RNA1 preferentially accumulated during the initial part of the exponential viral replication phase. During the second part of the exponential viral replication phase, RNA2 levels increased up to four times more than RNA1 levels, suggesting that the structural proteins encoded in RNA2 were needed to complete the viral replication cycle and egression. In agreement with these results, we started observing OrV smiFISH signal in the lumen at 14 hpi, which allowed us to determine the beginning of egression at around 14 hpi. Together, our data suggests that OrV accumulation during larval development is highly dynamic and that a viral replication cycle would be complete at 14 hpi, which would be consistent with the increase in RNA2 levels leading up to 12 hpi and the decrease in viral load after 12 hpi (Fig. 7).

Model representation of the OrV infection cycle.

Moreover, we observed that the number of infected cells per animal remained close to one throughout the rest of the infection, suggesting that the increase in viral load we observed by RT-qPCR and RNA-seq is due to intracellular viral replication, possibly in the same infected cells. The ability of neighboring cells toward off the virus raises the possibility that the infected cells share some diffusible signal with their neighbors to induce an antiviral state, as has been observed in mammalian cells (32). In lys-2 and drh-1 mutants, the number of infected cells was increased, as was the viral load, suggesting that the increase in viral load may be due to an increased number of cells and that LYS-2 and DRH-1 prevent multicellular colonization. Another member of the RNAi pathway, RDE-1, is also necessary to prevent multicellular colonization (33), hinting that DRH-1 may prevent multicellular colonization due to its role in the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway and not through activating the IPR response. Last, the viral load in lys-2(tm2398) was reduced to wild-type levels after the initial peak, suggesting that lys-2 is involved in preventing multicellular colonization but, unlike drh-1, is not involved in intracellular defense.

We also observed that the relative infected area per animal did not increase over time, which may be due to the differential localization of antiviral genes within the intestine. It has been recently reported that intestinal cross-linking collagens, which are restricted to the posterior intestinal cells, mediate antiviral immunity in the intestine (21), with intestinal RNAi of different collagens resulting in increased viral loads. The increase in viral load upon RNAi of these collagens may thus be due to an increased number of infected cells, as we observed in the lys-2 and drh-1 mutants.

By analyzing the transcriptomic response during the whole span of larval development, we have been able to characterize temporal responses to OrV infection. We identified a group of genes up-regulated through the whole infection and genes whose expression profiles were correlated or anticorrelated with viral accumulation. A comparative analysis of the responses elicited by other biotic pathogens in wild-type animals teased apart OrV-specific genes from defense genes responding in a non-pathogen–specific manner.

Last, the developmental stage of the host has an impact on the response to viral infections (34). Our experiments were performed in L1 larvae, unlike a previous study in which the transcriptomic response to OrV in wild-type animals was analyzed in L3 larvae (13). At 12 hpi, we see some key differences, such as the absence of molting-related genes in the L3-infected dataset. This is probably because, in their case, the L3 molt had already passed, and in our case, the 12 hpi was close to the L1 molt. Taking into consideration the developmental timing of the animals in future experiments will help us understand the effect of the viral infection in development and distinguish specific responses to OrV.

DEGs with pro- and antiviral roles

Our transcriptomic analysis provides, to our knowledge, the best transcriptomic temporal resolution of an OrV infection process. In an effort to characterize some of the genes that are differentially expressed in OrV infection, we followed the progression of the infection in eight mutants of differentially expressed genes upon OrV. One mutant per gene of interest was used in this analysis in an effort to try to perform an initial characterization of the function of each of these genes in OrV infection. A more in-depth analysis, with independently isolated mutants of these genes, would be necessary to improve our understanding of the function of these genes. Of the eight mutants analyzed, the following findings were of particular interest:

We observed that eol-1, a gene related with the nervous system, plays an important role from the first stages of the infection: The absence of EOL-1, which is related to olfactory learning, has an impact on the viral load profile, with a higher maximum viral load than the control at an earlier time point. Moreover, a subsequent decline in viral load suggests an earlier activation of the defense response, as the infection process continues similarly than that of wild-type animals. The involvement of eol-1 in the response to the virus is unclear, but our results suggest that it contributes to shape the process, possibly due to erratic behavior that fails to avoid the virus.

Our analysis of clec-60, which is involved in antimicrobial defense against Staphylococcus aureus and Microbacterium nematophilum (35, 36), indicates that clec-60 is also involved in viral infection in C. elegans and may have proviral roles, as clec-60(tm2319) had lower viral loads. Similarly to what was observed in other RNA viruses, C-type lectins could be involved in OrV entry into host cells (37, 38). However, further studies on the role of clec-60 and other C-type lectins in OrV infection would be necessary to clarify this.

Analysis of the lysozyme lys-2 indicated that this gene was up-regulated within the first 2 hpi. lys-2 was previously described to be regulated by all microbes; it was exposed to except by OrV and Yersinia pseudotuberculosis (29). Our results indicate that lys-2 is also regulated by OrV. We did not observe a higher percentage of infected animals in lys-2(tm2398) when compared to wild-type animals, possibly due to the highly concentrated viral stock used in these experiments. However, we observed a significant increase in lys-2(tm2398) viral load upon OrV infection—with a viral peak four times higher than in wild-type animals—suggesting that lys-2 has antiviral effects in C. elegans. Moreover, we observed a notable increase in the number of infected cells at 12 hpi, suggesting that lys-2 prevents multicellular colonization. The decrease in viral load in lys-2 mutants after the viral load peak suggests that the intracellular defense response, which is able to clear OrV after colonization, is not affected in lys-2 mutants, suggesting that the antiviral properties of lys-2 rely in the prevention of multicellular colonization and not in intracellular defense mechanisms.

Last, we studied the role of the Argonautes (AGOs), which were differentially expressed upon OrV infection (SAGO-2, PPW-1, and VSRA-1), all of which were overexpressed. Analysis of the AGO mutants upon OrV infection suggested that these AGOs have different functions during the infection. Sago-2(tm894) showed a higher viral load coinciding with the 12-hpi viral load peak, confirming that SAGO-2 is necessary in the defense response against OrV. We did not observe a higher percentage of infected animals in sago-2(tm894) when compared to wild-type animals, possibly due to the saturating viral stock used in these experiments. In contrast, ppw-1(pk2505) and vsra-1(tm1637) showed reduced viral loads, percentage of infected animals, and RNA2 to RNA1 ratios. These results suggest that PPW-1 and VSRA-1 are necessary for viral replication and that viral replication in these mutants is stalled after the beginning of RNA1 replication but before RNA2 replication, similarly to what was observed with ALG-1 (39), PALS-22 (18), and Nicotiana benthamiana AGO1 (40). However, it could also be that the loss of PPW-1 and VSRA-1 cause a misregulation of the immune system, which benefits the host and results in reduced OrV levels. PPW-1 regulates immune-responsive genes (41). Although the function of PPW-1 and SAGO-2 seems notably different in regard to the defense response to OrV, both proteins have 98.65% amino acid similarity.

Influence of OrV on lipid metabolism and the UPRmt

Our study unveiled a dynamic pattern of immune, stress, and metabolic responses throughout the OrV infection process. While extensive research on the impact of viral infections on metabolism has been conducted in mammals (42), studies in C. elegans have predominantly focused on bacterial infections (43).

Viral infections affect the lipid metabolism in several pathosystems: Some viruses trigger the accumulation of lipid droplets, while others reduce the lipid storage (44). OrV infection leads to a reduction in lipid accumulation, although OrV itself relies on lipids for effective infection (19). Our data revealed a general down-regulation of lipid metabolism genes from 22 hpi, with down-regulation of some responses as early as 12 hpi. Given that wild-type animals are capable of reducing the viral load, the reduction of the lipids could be modulated by the virus—as it is the case in other pathosystems (45–47)—or could be part of an active response of the animal. Certainly, the fatty acid oleate, generated by fat-6 and fat-7, plays a pivotal role in activating the innate system of C. elegans (48). This significance is underscored by the inclusion of fat-2, fat-6, or fat-7 as some of the DEGs shared by biotic stresses in all the categories tested (in addition to acox-1.5, dld-1, fat-5, gba-4, sptl-2, and Y49E10.18). This implies that C. elegans might have the capacity to adjust its lipid metabolism as a component of an engaged antiviral defense. Nevertheless, additional research is warranted to delve deeper into this phenomenon and gain a more comprehensive understanding.

Our study highlights the substantial impact of viral infection on mitochondria, a crucial cellular component for maintaining balance through pathways such as the unfolded protein response (UPRmt). This activation is vital for managing stress (49, 50). Under normal conditions, cytosolic activating transcription factor associated with stress-1 (ATFS-1) is transported into the mitochondria via the translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) complex for degradation. If this translocation fails, then ATFS-1 enters the nucleus, triggering the expression of genes associated with mitochondrial chaperones, proteolysis, glycolysis, and innate immunity, such as lys-2 (51), which we found to have antiviral effects. Although we observed an enrichment of UPRmt at 38 hpi, coinciding with the onset of the residual infection phase in which viral load decreased significantly, several indications point to an earlier pathway activation: (i) Genes linked to mitochondrial translation were down-regulated at 12 hpi; (ii) components of the TOM complex, tomm-22 and tomm-70, were also down-regulated at 12 and 26 hpi, respectively, hindering ATFS-1 translocation; and (iii) ZIP-3, known to inhibit UPRmt activation (49), displayed reduced expression (cluster 5 in fig. S4). In contrast, ZIP-1, identified as a key regulator in the IPR (24), showed an up-regulated expression profile (cluster 9 in fig. S4). The expression patterns of both bZIP transcription family members align with the activation of immune genes through both pathways.

Concluding remark

C. elegans stress responses often share common ground, and a single stressor can trigger various pathways. Consider, for instance, a proteotoxic stress that not only kickstarts the innate immunity but also sparks a heat shock response and activates the UPRmt. Moreover, the developmental stage of the host can influence the nature of these responses. While it may sometimes seem like untangling a tightly woven knot, our time-series work has shed a little light into the intricate sequences that dictate how the animal tackles an OrV infection at each stage of the process, making it the highest resolution transcriptome of the process to date. Our results also highlight the importance of studying early responses to infection, which are very different from those of advanced infection stages. This research offers a fresh perspective to appreciate the nuanced strategies these tiny creatures use in their battle against viral intrusion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

C. elegans strain maintenance

All C. elegans strains used in this study are derived from the N2 Bristol strain unless specified and are listed in table S8. All strains were maintained at 20°C on nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 bacteria under standard conditions (52, 53). Mutants and transgenic strains were backcrossed three times into the N2 strain used in the laboratory unless already backcrossed into N2.

clec-60(tm2319), ppw-1(pk2505), sago-2(tm894), and vsra-1(tm1637) were ordered from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center, while C53A5.9(tm10539), eol-1(tm6609), and lys-2(tm2398) were ordered from the National Bioresource Project and backcrossed three times to a wild-type N2 background. Drh-1(ok3495) was a gift from M.A. Félix, and pals-22(jy3) was from E.R. Troemel. A gst-35 mutant, gst-35(esv1), was generated using CRISPR-Cas9 and resulted in a 478–base pair (bp) deletion, which included part of the first exon and a splice junction, resulting in a truncated protein that only maintained homology to the first three amino acids (fig. S11, B to E).

To achieve synchronized animal populations, plates containing eggs were meticulously rinsed with M9 buffer to eliminate larvae and adult animals while retaining the eggs. After a 1-hour interval, plates were subjected to another M9 buffer wash to gather the larvae that had hatched during that period.

OrV stock preparation, quantification, and inoculation

JU2624 or SFE2 animals coming from one freshly starved plate were inoculated with OrV by soaking in viral stock kindly provided by M.A. Félix (6) for 1 hour. The volume was then divided among 30 9-cm NGM plates and allowed to grow for 5 days until starved. Animals were then resuspended in 15 ml of M9 (0.22 M KH2PO4, 0.42 M Na2HPO4, 0.85 M NaCl, and 0.001 M MgSO4), let stand for 15 min at room temperature, vortexed, and centrifuged for 2 min at 400g. The supernatant was centrifuged twice at 21,000g for 5 min and then passed through a 0.2-μm filter.

RNA of the resulting viral stock was extracted using the Viral RNA Isolation Kit (NYZtech). The concentration of viral RNA was then determined by RT-qPCR (details below) and normalized using a standard curve. Primers used can be found in table S9.

For the standard curve, cDNA of OrV was obtained using Accuscript High Fidelity Reverse Transcriptase (Agilent) and reverse primers at the 3′ end of the virus. Approximately 1000 bp of the 3′ end of RNA1 and RNA2 were amplified using forward primers containing 20 bp coding the T7 promoter and DreamTaq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The PCR products were gel purified using MSB Spin PCRapace (Invitek Molecular), and an in vitro transcription was performed using T7 Polymerase (Merck). The remaining DNA was then degraded using deoxyribonuclease I (Life Technologies). RNA concentration was determined by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the number of molecules per microliter was determined using the online tool EndMemo RNA Copy Number Calculator (www.endmemo.com/bio/dnacopynum.php). Primers used can be found in table S9.

Synchronized animal populations were inoculated with 2.8 × 109 copies of OrV by pipetting the viral stock on top of the bacterial lawn containing the animals. Seven hundred animals per plate were grown for RT-qPCRs and smiFISH and 1200 animals per plate for the transcriptomic analysis.

RNA extractions and RT-qPCRs

Inoculated and control animals were collected at the designated times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)–0.05% Tween. Samples were centrifuged for 2 min at 1350 rpm, and the supernatant was discarded. Another two wash steps were performed before freezing the samples in liquid nitrogen. A total of 500 μl of TRIzol (Invitrogen) was added to the animal pellet, and the pellet was disrupted by following five cycles of freeze thawing and five cycles of 30 s of vortex followed by 30 s of rest. A total of 100 μl of chloroform was then added, and the tubes were shaken for 15 s and let rest for 2 min. Samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 11,000g at 4°C, and the top layer containing the RNA was then mixed with the same volume of 100% ethanol. The sample was then loaded into RNA Clean & Concentrator columns (Zymo Research), and the rest of the protocol was followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RT-qPCRs were performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on an ABI StepOne Plus Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). A total of 10 ng of total RNA was loaded, and a standard curve was used for OrV quantifications. Primers used for RT-qPCRs can be found in table S9.

Total RNA extraction and preparation for RNA-seq

Library preparation and Illumina sequencing was done by Novogene Europe (www.novogene.com) using a NovaSeq 6000 platform and a Lnc-stranded mRNA-seq library method, ribosomal RNA depletion, directional library preparation, 150 paired end, and 6-Gb raw data per sample. Novogene checked the quality of the libraries using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), qPCR for quantification, and Bioanalyzer for size distribution detection.

RNA-seq data preprocessing

The quality of the Fastq files was assessed with FASTQC (54) and MultiQC (55) and preprocessed as paired reads with BBDuk (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/). Briefly, adapters were eliminated, the first 10 5′ nucleotides of each read were cut, and the 3′ end sequences with average quality below 10 were removed. Processed reads shorter than 60 nucleotides were discarded. (The parameter values were set to: ktrim = r, k = 31, mink = 11, qtrim = rl, trimq = 10, maq = 5, and forcetrimleft = 10, and minlength = 60).

The resultant processed files were use in the next steps. The N2 Bristol (Wbcel235, GCF_000002985.6 assembly) genome was used as reference to the alignment with STAR version 2.7.9a (56) using the run mode “alignReads” and the quantification mode “GeneCounts.” Quantification results were used for the transcriptome analysis.

Differential expression analysis and FEA

DESEq2 v1.38.3 (57) was used for the DEA using test = “LRT” (complete design = condition + time + condition:time, reduced design = condition + time). The DEGs by time point were extracted with test = “Wald” on each result (time). DEGs were considered significant with Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted P ≤ 0.05 values. In most heatmaps, asterisks indicate significance level by time point.

The FEAs were performed using the AnimalEnrichr site with the enrichr package v3.2 (58). Only terms with adjusted P ≤ 0.05 were considered.

Correlation with virus accumulation

A Pearson’s correlation between the differential expression of the DEGs (log2FC by time point) and the virus accumulation (percentage of viral reads in sample) was performed with cor.test function from stats package v.4.2.1. Only significant DEGs by LRT were evaluated. Genes with an r ≥ 0.8 were considered correlated, and those with an r ≤ −0.8 were considered to be anticorrelated.

Network functional enrichment and visualization

The network of the genes common to all biotic stresses and the specific genes in the OrV response was created with Cytoscape v3.10.0 (59) selecting the option “Full STRING network”. The enrichment and visualization of the categories were performed with the STRING plugin. Only the singletons with annotations were shown in the final networks.

smiFISH

The smiFISH protocol was adapted from previous studies (23, 60). Inoculated and control animals were washed with PBS–0.05% Tween and centrifuged for 2 min at 1350 rpm four times, discarding the supernatants. A total of 800 μl of Bouins fixation mix (400 μl of Bouins fix, 400 μl of methanol, 10 μl of β-mercaptoethanol) were added, and the sample was incubated at room temperature for 30 min in a rotatory shaker. Samples were then frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80°C overnight. The following day, samples were gently shaken at 4°C for 30 min and washed four times with borate Triton solution (20 mM H3BO3, 10 mM NaOH, and 0.5% Triton) and five times with borate Triton β-mercaptoethanol solution (20 mM H3BO3, 10 mM NaOH, 0.5% Triton, and 2% β-mercaptoethanol), leaving the borate Triton β-mercaptoethanol incubate for 1 hour between each wash in the last three washes. The sample was then incubated for 5 min with wash buffer A (1 ml of Stellaris RNA FISH wash buffer A, 1 ml of deionized formamide, and 3 ml of H2O).

Forty probes against OrV were designed using Oligostan (60), 22 against RNA1, and 18 against RNA2. The sequences of the probes can be found in table S9. The probes were annealed as follows: 2 μL of 0.83 μM probe set, 1 μl of 50 μM fluorescent labeled amplification product (FLAP) label, 1 μl of NEB3, and 6 μl of diethyl pyrocarbonate H2O were incubated at 85°C for 3 min, 65°C for 3 min, and 25°C for 5 min. The FLAP labels contained CAL Fluor 610 or Quasar 670 modifications at both the 5′ and 3′ ends of the following sequence: 5′-AATGCATGTCGACGAGGTCCGAGTGT (Biosearch Technologies).

One microliter of annealed probe solution targeting RNA1 and 1 μl of annealed probe solution targeting RNA2 were then mixed with 98 μl of hybridization buffer (100 μl of Stellaris smiFISH hybridization buffer and 25 μl of deionized formamide). A total of 100 μl of hybridization buffer containing the smiFISH probes were then added to the samples, and the samples were incubated overnight at 37°C in a rotary shaker. The following day, samples were washed with wash buffer A2 (1 ml of Stellaris RNA FISH wash buffer A and 4 ml of H2O), incubated for 30 min in wash buffer A2 at 37°C, and then incubated for 30 min in wash buffer A2 containing 25 ng of 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at 37°C. Samples were then incubated 5 min in Stellaris RNA FISH wash buffer B, centrifuged, and resuspended in a small volume of Stellaris RNA FISH wash buffer B. A total of 0.1 ng of DAPI was added to each sample, and samples were mounted using N-propyl gallate mounting medium. Samples were imaged using Leica Dmi8 microscope with Leica DFC9000 GTC scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera and objective HC PL APO 40×/0.95 CORR PH2. Images were analyzed and processed using ImageJ (Fiji) (61). smiFISH and DAPI signal were used to determine the number of infected cells in each animal, the percentage of animals with RNA2 versus RNA1, and the localization of the signal.

Percentage of infected animals

To determine the percentage of infected animals, the presence or absence of smiFISH, pals5p::GFP, or lys-3p::GFP signal was used to determine the infection status of each animal. From the total number of animals analyzed, the percentage of infected animals was calculated.

Clustering of gene expression profiles

All the significant genes (adjusted P ≤ 0.05) identified with the LRT were clustered using the hclust method from stats package v.4.2.1. A co-occurrence matrix was generated from the results through cutting the tree from 2 to 100 groups (cutree, k = 2:100). Only the genes that belonged to the same cluster in at least the 50% of the k were treated as connected for the adjacency matrix. To identify the modules, we used the method clusters from the igraph package v1.5.0 (62), keeping those formed by a minimum of five genes.

Comparison with other biotic stresses

The Microbes datasets from WormExp v2.0 (26) was used for the comparison with other biotic stresses with the restriction that only experiments performed on wild-type N2 strain were considered. Data from four additional experiments in which N2 strain was used and at least three biological replicates were done were also included: infection of L3 nematodes at 12 hpi with OrV (L3.O.12) and with Nycteris parisii (L3.Np.12) (13) and infection in L4 nematodes with M. humicola at 12 (L4.Mh.12) and 24 hpi (L4.Mh.24) (27). Our significant DEGs by time point (adjusted P ≤ 0.0499) were used in the comparison. A total of 56 experiments were included, and only protein coding genes were considered (table S4). All pathogens were classified into the following categories: virus, Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, and fungi. A gene was considered common when identified as DEGs in at least one experiment of each pathogen category and specific to OrV when it was described only in OrV infection experiments.

Lipids metabolism, antimicrobial effectors, IPR, and zip-1–dependent genes

C. elegans genes related to the lipid metabolism (GO0006629) where downloaded from Wormbase database (63). IPR genes were extracted from table S5 in (18) and ZIP-1–dependent genes from data S3 in (24).

gst-35 CRISPR knockout

The gst-35(esv1) deletion was generated by imprecise repair of a CRISPR-Cas9–induced DNA double-strand break and selected using the dpy-10 co-CRISPR approach (64). The fusion was generated using plasmid-based expression of Cas9 and single guide RNAs (sgRNAs). Two pairs of sgRNA plasmids were used to target the 5′ and 3′ ends of the gst-35 open-reading frame and generated by ligation of annealed oligo pairs into the pU6::sgRNA expression vector pJJR50 (Addgene) as previously described (65). The sgRNA targeting the dpy-10 locus (64) was cloned into the pU6::sgRNA expression vector pJJR50 as previously described (65). The injection mix was prepared in MilliQ H2O and contained Peft-3::Cas9 (60 ng/ml; Addgene) and each sgRNA plasmid (45 ng/ml). Young adult hermaphrodites were injected in the germline. For selection of edited genomes, injected animals were transferred to individual culture plates, incubated for 3 to 4 days at 20°C, and 96 nontransgenic F1 animals (wild type, Dpy, or Rol) from two plates containing high numbers of Dpy and Rol animals were selected and transferred to individual NGM plates. After laying eggs, F1 animals were lysed and genotyped by PCR using two primers flanking the gst-35 open-reading frame. Sanger sequencing was used to determine the precise molecular lesion (Eurofins Genomics). The sequences were targeted, and the primers used can be found in table S9.
