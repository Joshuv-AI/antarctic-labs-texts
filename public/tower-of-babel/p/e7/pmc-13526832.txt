Preclinical assessment of Preserflo MicroShunt implantation using an intraocular endoscope-holding robot
Authors: Keisuke Adachi, Kenta Ashikaga, Kosei Tomita, Toshinari Funaki, Shintaro Nakao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobotic assistance may overcome the limitations of intraocular visualization during glaucoma surgery, particularly in eyes with corneal opacity. This preclinical study investigated the feasibility and surgical utility of a robotic-assisted intraocular endoscope-holding system for real-time visualization during Preserflo MicroShunt implantation. An endoscope-holding robot (OQrimo) enabled stable, hands-free intraocular imaging throughout the procedure. Porcine eyes were used, and corneal opacity was simulated with a black-painted contact lens. Surgical feasibility, tube–endothelium (TE) distance, and intraoperative complications were evaluated. Continuous endoscopic visualization using the robotic system enabled successful MicroShunt implantation in all eyes without complications, including intraoperative mechanical trauma to the cornea, iris, or lens. The TE distance was significantly greater in the robotic group than in the conventional group under both normal visualization conditions (1.96 ± 0.27 mm vs. 1.13 ± 0.40 mm, p = 0.009) and simulated corneal opacity (1.85 ± 0.32 mm vs. 1.12 ± 0.28 mm, p = 0.015), indicating more favorable tube positioning. These results demonstrate that robotic-assisted intraocular endoscopic visualization provides stable and precise guidance for MicroShunt implantation independent of corneal clarity. This technology has the potential to enhance surgical accuracy and expand the feasibility of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery in eyes with compromised anterior segment visualization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56997-2.Keywords: Robotics, Robot surgery, Endoscope, MIGSSubject terms: Diseases, Engineering, Medical research

Introduction

The Preserflo MicroShunt is considered a minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) device, specifically a filtration-based MIGS, that lowers intraocular pressure by shunting aqueous humor from the anterior chamber to the subconjunctival space1. Preserflo MicroShunt is made of an inert polymer, poly(styrene-block-isobutylene-block-styrene, SIBS), which can minimize inflammation and fibrosis2. Its small size and ab externo implantation technique offers several advantages over traditional filtering surgeries3. Preserflo MicroShunt provides an outflow rate of approximately 2.5 µL/min when there is no resistance at the posterior end of the device, and it is theoretically designed to prevent excessive filtration. In addition, the surgical procedure for Preserflo MicroShunt implantation is considered relatively simple, with little variation among surgeons4,5. Batlle et al. reported sustained intraocular pressure reduction over 5 years in patients with primary open-angle glaucoma, without major adverse events6. However, when positioned too close to the corneal endothelium, the Preserflo MicroShunt may cause progressive endothelial cell loss, potentially leading to corneal decompensation7,8. Therefore, precise positioning within the anterior chamber is critical to maximize efficacy while minimizing complications.

We have developed a novel intraocular endoscope-holding robotic system, OQrimo (Riverfield Inc., Tokyo, Japan), originally intended for vitreoretinal surgery, which received medical device approval in Japan in 20239,10 (Fig. 1A, B, C). Although its approved indication does not include anterior chamber surgery, we hypothesized that this robotic platform could provide stable, high-resolution endoscopic visualization of the iridocorneal angle, enabling accurate assessment of MicroShunt positioning relative to the corneal endothelium.

In this preclinical study, we evaluated the feasibility and potential utility of using OQrimo to assist in the anterior chamber placement of the Preserflo MicroShunt by providing direct intraoperative visualization of the implantation site.

Material and methods

Surgical technique

A porcine eye was placed in the left orbital socket of a facial model, and surgical procedures were performed using the OPMI Lumera 700 microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Porcine eyes were obtained from a local abattoir as by-products of animals slaughtered for food consumption, and no animals were sacrificed specifically for research purposes. Therefore, ethical approval was not required according to the institutional guidelines of Juntendo University. The superior nasal quadrant conjunctiva (11 o’clock position) and Tenon’s capsule were dissected over two clock hours. A side-port incision was created in the inferior temporal quadrant, and ophthalmic viscoelastic device (Opegan Hi; Santen Inc., Osaka, Japan) was injected to fill the anterior chamber. A 25-gauge intraocular endoscope (OPH-25MFY-H; MACHIDA, Abiko, Japan) was held in place with OQrimo (Riverfield Inc., Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 1A, B, C). Then, the intraocular endoscope was inserted into the anterior chamber through the 4–5 o’clock side port and positioned to visualize the trabecular meshwork in the superior nasal quadrant, located diagonally opposite to the OQrimo (Fig. 1D). A mark was made with trypan blue using the tip of a caliper 3 mm posterior to the limbus. A scleral tunnel was then created parallel to the scleral surface using the Preserflo-specific insertion needle, entering the anterior chamber at the trabecular meshwork at the 12 o’clock position (Fig. 1D, E). The Preserflo MicroShunt (Santen Inc., Osaka, Japan) was advanced through the tunnel until it reached the anterior chamber. In the OQrimo group, the insertion of the Preserflo MicroShunt into the anterior chamber through the trabecular meshwork was confirmed using the OQrimo-held endoscope, and the position and angle of the MicroShunt within the anterior chamber were also verified. No sutures were placed on the conjunctiva. Furthermore, to simulate cases with corneal opacity, a black-painted contact lens was placed on the corneal surface of the porcine eye.

Evaluation of the anterior segment

An anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) system (CASIA2, TOMEI Corp., Japan) was used to evaluate the distance between the tube and the corneal endothelium. Measurements were taken from the distal superior end of the beveled tip of the tube, perpendicular to the internal surface of the cornea, to determine the tube–endothelium (TE) distance. TE distance was measured immediately after insertion of the Preserflo MicroShunt.

Results

Preserflo MicroShunt placement in the anterior chamber with OQrimo

The endoscope, held by the OQrimo system, was placed in the anterior chamber, allowing visualization of the angle in porcine eyes (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, the Preserflo MicroShunt could be positioned in the anterior chamber under endoscopic guidance (Fig. 2C, D, Supplemental Video). After insertion, adjustments were made to optimize the distance between the Preserflo MicroShunt and the corneal endothelium (Fig. 2E, F, Supplemental Video). Continuous endoscopic visualization using the robotic system enabled successful MicroShunt implantation in all eyes, without intraoperative complications such as mechanical trauma to the cornea, iris, or lens. In addition, we specifically assessed for potential misdirection of the scleral tunnel–creating knife, including inadvertent passage into the sub-iris space; the real-time endoscopic view allowed immediate recognition and correction of such deviations when they occurred, thereby minimizing tissue injury. In our porcine model, the total surgical time was significantly longer in the OQrimo-assisted group compared with the conventional group (651.8 ± 255.3 s vs 292.3 ± 75.2 s, p = 0.004).

Comparison of tube-endothelium (TE) distance with and without OQrimo

TE distance was evaluated in porcine eyes operated with OQrimo (OQrimo group) and in porcine eyes operated without OQrimo (non-OQrimo group) using AS-OCT (Fig. 3A, B). AS-OCT showed the mean TE distance was 1.96 ± 0.27 mm in the OQrimo group and 1.13 ± 0.40 mm in the non-OQrimo group (Fig. 3C). The TE distance was significantly longer in the OQrimo group than in the non-OQrimo group. (p = 0.009, Fig. 3C).

TE distance with and without OQrimo in eyes wearing black-painted contact lenses

Similarly, we evaluated eyes simulating corneal opacity by placing black-painted contact lenses (Fig. 4A). Under this condition, AS-OCT showed the mean TE distance was 1.85 ± 0.32 mm in the OQrimo group and 1.12 ± 0.28 mm in the non-OQrimo group (Fig. 4B, C, D). The TE distance was significantly greater in the OQrimo group compared with the non-OQrimo group (p = 0.015, Fig. 4D). These results suggest that, even in eyes with corneal opacity, Preserflo MicroShunt implantation under angle visualization using the OQrimo-held endoscope is useful for maintaining a safe distance from the corneal endothelium.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the implantation of the Preserflo MicroShunt using an intraocular endoscope held by the OQrimo robotic system. Our results demonstrated that the use of OQrimo allowed for precise anterior chamber visualization and significantly increased the tube-endothelium (TE) distance compared with procedures performed without the device. This effect was observed not only in normal eyes but also in eyes simulating corneal opacity with black-painted contact lenses, suggesting that the OQrimo-held endoscope facilitates safe MicroShunt placement even under challenging visualization conditions. These findings indicate that robotic endoscope assistance may enhance surgical precision and potentially reduce the risk of corneal endothelial damage during MicroShunt implantation.

Ibarz-Barber et al. evaluated corneal endothelial cell density (ECD) changes after anterior chamber implantation of the PRESERFLO™ MicroShunt in 46 eyes (40 patients) with 12 months of follow-up, and demonstrated that a shorter tube–endothelium (TE) distance was associated with greater endothelial cell loss8. They further concluded that maintaining a TE distance of at least 600 µm may effectively prevent such loss. In the present preclinical study, the use of the endoscope-holding robot OQrimo resulted in a significantly greater TE distance compared with the conventional technique. However, because the conventional group in our study also exceeded this proposed threshold, the additional clinical benefit of further increasing TE distance remains uncertain. Furthermore, direct comparison with prior human studies should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in species, ocular anatomy, and experimental conditions. Therefore, while these findings demonstrate that OQrimo can improve tube positioning accuracy, they do not necessarily support routine use in all cases. Rather, OQrimo-assisted implantation may be most valuable in selected complex situations with compromised corneal visualization, such as eyes with corneal opacity, advanced arcus senilis, corneal disease, or prior corneal transplantation, where enhanced intraoperative visualization and stable endoscopic positioning may provide particular advantages. Future human clinical studies are necessary to determine whether the anatomical benefits observed in this experimental setting translate into meaningful improvements in endothelial preservation, surgical safety, and cost-effectiveness. Although the current analysis was limited to anatomical positioning, these findings support the potential role of OQrimo as a useful adjunct in carefully selected clinical scenarios.

The IRIS registry reported that, among 79,887,324 patients, 6.5% had corneal opacity11. Furthermore, in cases of congenital glaucoma or similar congenital anterior segment anomalies, glaucoma is often accompanied by corneal opacity12. Even in patients without congenital glaucoma, corneal opacity may occur in association with glaucoma. Although there are no previous reports on the use of the Preserflo MicroShunt in eyes with corneal opacity, we conducted a preclinical experiment simulating this condition and confirmed that proper placement of the device was achievable. Even in cases without severe corneal opacity, repeated glaucoma surgeries can lead to corneal endothelial cell loss and reduced visibility, suggesting that this technique may have clinical utility13.

Endoscopic visualization has long been used in glaucoma surgery, particularly in endoscopic cyclophotocoagulation (ECP)14. Recent advances have produced high-resolution, miniaturized endoscopes, which offer advantages when combined with robotic assistance. Reports of intraoperative insertion of an endoscope into the anterior chamber remain limited. Takahashi et al. used an ophthalmic endoscope to observe the anterior chamber angle before and after intracanalicular injection of viscoelastic material during viscocanalostomy15. However, there have been no reports of glaucoma surgery performed while directly observing the angle. A major limitation is that holding the endoscope restricts the surgeon to one-handed manipulation. By using a robot to hold the endoscope, the surgeon can observe the surgical field via a foot switch while performing bimanual maneuvers9. In the present study, we utilized an endoscope-holding robot to insert the Preserflo MicroShunt while observing the anterior chamber angle. We could confirm no intraoperative complications during the procedure. Robotic assistance may facilitate various future procedures, such as trabeculotomy or other angle-based glaucoma surgeries, under direct endoscopic visualization. Furthermore, the introduction of artificial intelligence may enable more precise and quantitative surgery, including accurate assessment of the MicroShunt angle9.

The OQrimo used in this study was approved as a medical device in Japan in 2023; however, its current indication is limited to vitreoretinal surgery9,16. In the present study, a preclinical trial was conducted for glaucoma surgery, and its potential utility was suggested. In the present study, we examined the distance between the corneal endothelium and the Preserflo MicroShunt during implantation; however, the effect of other factors, such as the insertion angle of the tube, on actual intraocular pressure remains unknown. Future clinical trials are planned to evaluate its effectiveness and long-term safety, including corneal endothelial cell loss and intraocular pressure-lowering effects.

The OQrimo system appears to have a relatively shallow learning curve. Although surgeons are required to coordinate an additional foot pedal for robotic endoscope control alongside the conventional microscope foot switch, this adjustment was not found to be prohibitive with increasing experience. Familiarity with endoscopic visualization is advantageous for optimal use of the system. In addition, anterior chamber instability in the absence of a dedicated infusion port and potential spatial interference from the patient’s facial anatomy (e.g., nose and cheek) may initially pose ergonomic challenges. However, these issues can be progressively mitigated as the operator becomes accustomed to the system, suggesting that proficiency can be achieved within a limited number of cases.

This study has several limitations. In actual Preserflo MicroShunt implantation, the use of viscoelastic material in the anterior chamber is uncommon; however, it was used in the present study due to the use of porcine eyes, which may have influenced the procedure. The use of viscoelastic material may have artificially deepened the anterior chamber and altered tissue resistance, potentially contributing to greater TE distances in both groups than would be expected under routine clinical conditions. In addition, clinical application of this technique with routine viscoelastic use may increase surgical cost and operative time, introduce the risk of viscoelastic obstruction within the MicroShunt lumen, and potentially affect final tube positioning after viscoelastic removal. Therefore, this methodological difference may limit the direct translational applicability of the present findings. In addition, the robot was positioned at the 7 o’clock position to avoid interference with the nose, and the MicroShunt was inserted from the nasal-superior side. In clinical practice, however, insertion from other positions, such as the superior-temporal side, is also possible, and this should be investigated in future studies. Furthermore, this study was designed as a pilot preclinical investigation with a limited sample size, and no formal a priori power analysis was performed. Therefore, the possibility of Type I and Type II statistical errors cannot be excluded, and the findings should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, porcine eyes differ anatomically from human eyes in several respects, and the quantitative generalizability of the results will require future validation in human studies. The evaluator who measured TE distance on AS-OCT images was not blinded to surgical allocation, which may have introduced observer bias. Future studies incorporating masked image assessment are warranted to improve methodological rigor. Moreover, even when using the robot, no additional medical reimbursement is currently provided, and surgeons bear the economic burden of purchasing the robot. Future studies are needed to demonstrate the clinical utility of robotic-assisted MIGS.
