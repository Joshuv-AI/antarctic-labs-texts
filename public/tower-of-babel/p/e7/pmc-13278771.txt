Histologic signatures as predictors of intestinal barrier impairment in inflammatory bowel disease: integration with endoscopic and molecular markers
Authors: Antonio Lo Bello, Irene Zammarchi, Cecilia L Pugliano, Snehali Majumder, Robert Hughes, Rory Crotty, Brian Hayes, Louise Burke, Subrata Ghosh, Marietta Iacucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsIntestinal barrier impairment (BI) is a hallmark of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), driving immune activation, neutrophil-mediated injury, and chronic mucosal damage. While endoscopy and molecular profiling provide insight into epithelial and vascular assessment, the combined prognostic role of histology and BI remains insufficiently defined. This study aimed to determine the association between histology-derived signatures and BI, and identify outcome predictors by integrating histologic, advanced imaging, and molecular data.MethodsIBD patients underwent in vivo epithelial and vascular barrier assessment using probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy and endocytoscopy. Biopsies were collected for histologic and protein expression analysis. Epithelial (ZO-1, Claudin-2, E-cadherin) and vascular-barrier proteins (CD31, PV-1) were analyzed using confocal microscopy and automated quantification with QuPath. Correlation among histology, advanced imaging, and protein expression was assessed, together with the ability to predict outcome at 12 months of follow-up.ResultsEighty-eight IBD patients (52 Crohn’s disease [CD] and 36 ulcerative colitis [UC]) were included. BI was identified in 35/52 (67%) CD and 28/36 (78%) UC patients. Epithelial and lamina propria neutrophils correlated strongly with epithelial BI (r = 0.6). Chronic inflammatory infiltrate correlated significantly with vascular BI (r = 0.5) and PV-1 expression (r = 0.3) in CD. Finally, CD patients with both barrier alterations (advanced imaging + PV-1) and inflammatory infiltrate showed a higher rate of adverse outcomes than those with single alterations.ConclusionHistologic signatures correlate significantly with BI in IBD. Multimodal evaluation combining histology, advanced imaging, and molecular profiling improves outcome prediction beyond single-modality assessment and may identify patients with subclinical disease persistence, supporting accurate risk stratification and personalized management.

1. Introduction

Achieving deep remission has emerged as a key therapeutic goal in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).1 Increasing evidence indicates that deeper layers of disease control are more strongly associated with favorable long-term outcomes than clinical or endoscopic remission alone.2,3

Consequently, there is growing interest in biomarkers that more accurately reflect residual disease activity and are associated with future adverse outcomes. Histologic assessment is crucial for evaluating disease activity and guiding management decisions in IBD. International position statements define histologic remission as the absence of neutrophils in the epithelium and lamina propria. In contrast, the presence of chronic inflammatory infiltrate alone is not considered as disease activity.4 Importantly, recent evidence highlights the importance of neutrophil localization: epithelial neutrophils, compared to those confined to the lamina propria, are more strongly associated with active disease and major adverse outcomes (MAOs).5 Despite its clinical relevance, histology alone cannot fully capture the broader complexity of IBD pathogenesis or adequately stratify relapse risk, particularly in patients who otherwise meet conventional remission criteria.

The intestinal barrier, increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of disease course in IBD, is a multi-layered and dynamic system that includes a mucus layer with commensal microbiota; epithelial and vascular barrier proteins; and innate and adaptive immune cells.6 Disruption of this equilibrium at any level may result in barrier impairment, promoting immune cell infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, thereby further compromising barrier healing and establishing a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammation and tissue damage.7

Notably, the presence of barrier healing, defined as the absence of epithelial and vascular barrier abnormalities on advanced imaging, appears to be associated with lower MAOs than histologic remission alone,3 underscoring the need for accurate assessment of intestinal barrier status. Barrier healing can be evaluated in vivo using advanced endoscopic techniques such as probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy (pCLE) and endocytoscopy (ECS), which allow real-time visualization of epithelial and vascular integrity at near-histologic resolution. These modalities have been shown to correlate with validated histologic scoring systems.8,9

pCLE and ECS are based on different optical principles and provide complementary information on intestinal barrier features. pCLE enables real-time in vivo assessment of epithelial and vascular permeability, including crypts, fluorescein leakage, blood flow, and globet cell evaluation, whereas ECS provides ultra-high-magnification mucosal imaging of cellular and microvascular architecture.

In parallel, in vitro assessment of epithelial and vascular barrier-related protein expression provides molecular insight into structural and functional alterations.10 Although pCLE is widely acknowledged as a validated tool for barrier assessment, our group has recently demonstrated that both advanced endoscopic imaging (pCLE and ECS) combined with molecular assessment of barrier-related proteins enables a more accurate characterization of barrier status and predicts outcomes more effectively than either modality alone.11,12

However, the potential predictive role of a multimodal disease assessment integrating histopathology and barrier evaluation requires further investigation.

In this context, pCLE and ECS were incorporated into the multimodal study framework not as directly comparable techniques, but as complementary advanced imaging approaches integrated with histology and molecular profiling for intestinal barrier assessment.

This study aimed to: (1) explore the relationship between the presence of histology-derived feature signatures and impairment of intestinal barrier assessed by advanced endoscopy and multiplex immunofluorescence for barrier-related protein expression; and (2) assess whether a multimodal approach employing a three-domain model, integrating histologic activity, advanced imaging barrier impairment, and barrier protein expression, could stratify relapse risk within 12 months, thereby enhancing the prediction of MAOs.

2. Methods

We conducted a prospective, observational study at two tertiary referral IBD centers in Cork, Ireland: Cork University Hospital and Mercy University Hospital. The study received ethical approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Cork Teaching Hospitals (CREC), under references ECM 3(u) and ECM 3(1), dated December 5, 2023 (Study codes APC-159 and APC-180). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.1. Study population and design

The study prospectively and consecutively enrolled patients aged 18-75 years with an established diagnosis of CD or UC who underwent endoscopy for disease assessment or surveillance from May 2023 to April 2025. Exclusion criteria included being under 18 or over 75 years of age, inability to provide consent, treatment with fluorescent dye in the previous 4 months, and contraindications to endoscopy or biopsy collection. Patients with incomplete imaging evaluation were also excluded, including cases in which advanced imaging was initiated but not completed, or in which image quality was insufficient for reliable assessment of barrier features due to motion artifacts, poor bowel preparation, or suboptimal visualization of the mucosa.

2.2. Baseline data collection and follow-up

At baseline, demographic and clinical information was collected. The enrolled patients underwent endoscopic examination in accordance with standard-of-care procedures. Advanced endoscopy (pCLE and ECS) was performed to evaluate intestinal barrier status. Following endoscopy evaluation, between two and four targeted ileocolonic biopsies were obtained from the same anatomical area assessed by pCLE or ECS for research purposes (quantification of molecular protein expression). Biopsies were taken from inflamed and/or non-inflamed mucosal areas depending on endoscopic assessment, and paired sampling from both areas was performed when feasible. Corresponding targeted biopsies from the same anatomical areas were used for standard of care histologic evaluation.

The patients were then followed clinically for 12 months, with evaluation of the occurrence of MAOs (ie, disease flare-ups, IBD-related hospitalization, steroid initiation, treatment escalation, or modification due to persistent inflammation, surgery) during study-specific on-site or phone call visits.

2.3. Advanced endoscopic imaging for intestinal barrier assessment

Endoscopic disease activity was assessed using high-definition (HD) white-light endoscopy (WLE). Endoscopic remission was defined as Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES) = 013 and Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity (UCEIS) ≤ 114 for UC, and Simple Endoscopic Score for CD (SES-CD) < 3.15

High-resolution imaging was obtained using Olympus and Pentax (Pentax, Tokyo, Japan) endoscopes coupled with the Cellvizio system (Mauna Kea Technologies, Paris, France) for pCLE, while endocytoscopy was performed using a dedicated ultra-magnification Olympus endoscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan), depending on hospital availability. pCLE and ECS were not performed in the same patient.

No predefined criteria were applied for allocation to pCLE or ECS: the imaging modality was determined by center-specific platform availability and local expertise, as each center routinely performed one advanced imaging technique within clinical practice.

pCLE was performed using a dedicated mini-probe inserted through the endoscope channel; intravenous fluorescein (2.5-5 mL, 10%) was administered as a contrast agent, enabling assessment of crypt/villi alternation, fluorescein leakage, goblet cells, cell infiltration, vascular architecture, and blood flow. ECS was performed using methylene blue on the intestinal lumen surface, facilitating visualization of crypt and villous architecture, cell infiltration, goblet cells, and vascular pattern at magnifications up to 520×. Epithelial and vascular barrier impairment was graded using validated scoring systems harmonized to identify features suggestive of barrier impairment12 (Table S1-S4).

All procedures were performed and scored by experienced endoscopists (M.I., S.G.). Other IBD-experienced investigators additionally scored endoscopic findings (I.Z., C.L.P.) during the procedure.

2.4. Histologic assessment

Inflamed areas were defined as those with mucosal inflammation on WLE, according to the established endoscopic scoring systems. Non-inflamed areas were defined as segments meeting criteria for endoscopic remission. Targeted biopsies for histology were obtained as per standard of care from the same intestinal area, classified as inflamed or non-inflamed, corresponding to the region assessed by pCLE or ECS.

Samples were fixed in formalin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Histologic activity was assessed by two pathologists (R.C., B.H.) with experience in IBD. Histologic activity was graded through the Robarts Histopathology Index (RHI)16 and PICaSSO Histologic Remission Index (PHRI)17 (Tables S5 and S6). Histologic remission was defined as RHI ≤ 3, with no neutrophils in the epithelium and lamina propria, and PHRI = 0. The presence and distribution of neutrophils within the epithelium and lamina propria, as well as the presence of chronic inflammatory infiltrate, were also assessed.

2.5. Definitions of barrier- and histology-related terms

Epithelial and vascular barrier impairment were defined as the presence of modality-specific imaging abnormalities, according to validated pCLE and ECS scoring systems (Tables S1-S4). Accordingly, barrier healing was defined as the absence of epithelial and vascular barrier abnormalities on advanced imaging.

Inflammatory infiltrate was defined as the presence of inflammatory cell infiltrate on histology, including acute (neutrophils in epithelium and lamina propria) and chronic inflammatory components. Chronic inflammatory infiltrate was defined by the presence of a chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate, including lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages. Deep remission was defined as the simultaneous presence of endoscopic remission, histologic remission, and absence of barrier impairment on advanced imaging.

2.6. Epithelial and vascular protein expression analysis for intestinal barrier assessment

Expression of epithelial tight junction proteins (Claudin-2, ZO-1, E-cadherin) and vascular barrier proteins (CD31 and plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein-1 [PV-1]) was assessed in fresh ileocolonic biopsies using multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF). Tissues were embedded in OCT, cryosectioned, and stained using validated primary antibodies, with stringent negative, secondary, and single-fluorophore controls to ensure specificity and exclude autofluorescence. Confocal imaging (Olympus FV3000) enabled high-resolution visualization of protein localization across epithelial and lamina propria compartments. Automated quantitative protein expression was subsequently obtained using QuPath (v.0.5.1). The quantification process follows the procedures described in our recently published paper.12

2.7. Multimodal intestinal barrier assessment

Overall barrier impairment was evaluated using a predefined multimodal composite framework integrating advanced endoscopic imaging, histologic features, and molecular markers. In the absence of a universally accepted single gold standard for in vivo and/or ex vivo assessment of intestinal barrier impairment, complementary information across modalities was integrated to characterize epithelial and vascular barrier status.

Although advanced imaging scoring and mIF quantification were performed to confirm intestinal barrier impairment, they were not used to guide clinical management during follow-up.

2.8. Outcome definition and follow-up

The enrolled patients were clinically followed up for 12 months, and the occurrence of MAOs was assessed during study-specific scheduled on-site or phone call visits.

MAOs were defined as any of the following: (1) initiation of systemic corticosteroids for IBD flare (eg, locally acting corticosteroids for mild to moderate inflammation, prednisone-equivalent ≥20 mg/day or intravenous [IV] steroids for moderate–severe); (2) treatment escalation (addition or switch of biologic/small molecule, dose intensification); (3) IBD-related hospitalization; or (4) IBD-related surgery. Only the first event was used for time-to-event analyses.

Time-to-event analysis was performed to assess the predictive ability of histologic, imaging, and molecular biomarkers, as well as their combination. The MAOs were considered in time order, and, for survival analysis, the first MAO was used. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to evaluate the association between the multimodal barrier impairment profile and time to first MAO, adjusting for age, disease duration, and baseline endoscopic activity (MES and SES-CD).

2.9. Statistical analysis

2.9.1. Technical considerations

A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were retrieved on Microsoft Excel, and analyses were performed using R v.4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

2.9.2. Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or as means with standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

2.9.3. Correlation analysis

The strength of the correlation between histology and endoscopic findings or main barrier protein expression was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. It was interpreted using the thresholds proposed by Hinkle et al.,18 whereby absolute r values of (0.00-0.30) were considered weak, (0.30-0.50) low, (0.50-0.70) moderate, (0.70-0.90) high, and (0.90-1.00) very high.

2.9.4. Survival analysis

Time-to-event analyses were performed to evaluate the occurrence of MAOs. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed to compare the occurrence of MAOs across different predictive frameworks. First, survival curves were generated to evaluate the prognostic performance of histology alone and of the combined model integrating histology with advanced endoscopic imaging. Subsequently, a fully integrated model incorporating histology, advanced endoscopy, and protein expression was assessed. For this model, the cohort was stratified according to the number of altered domains detected (one, two, or all three), yielding three distinct Kaplan–Meier curves. Differences between survival distributions were tested using the log-rank method. A Firth-penalized ordinal logistic regression trend test was also performed as an exploratory subanalysis in the remission cohort.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

Eighty-eight patients were enrolled, 52 with CD and 36 with UC. The median age was 44 years, with 64% of participants being male. Disease duration differed between conditions, with a longer course in CD (12.5 years) than in UC (7.5 years). During the 12-month follow-up, 23/52 CD patients (44.2%) and 19/36 UC patients (52.8%) experienced a MAO. In CD, MAOs included treatment escalation in 15/52 patients (28.8%), systemic corticosteroid uses in 5/52 (9.6%), hospitalization in 2/52 (3.8%), and surgery in 1/52 (1.9%). In UC, MAOs included systemic corticosteroid use in 9/36 patients (25%), treatment escalation in 9/36 (25%), and hospitalization in 1/36 (2.8%), while no surgery occurred. 3.3. Correlation between histology and barrier impairment

Neutrophilic and chronic inflammatory infiltrates were significantly associated with intestinal barrier impairment, as assessed by advanced imaging modalities, in both CD and UC, with a notably higher correlation in CD (r = 0.6 vs r = 0.4; P < .05). In parallel, infiltration of cells between crypts detected by pCLE and ECS showed moderate correlations with histologic inflammatory burden (neutrophilic infiltrate) in both disease groups (r = 0.4 and r = 0.5; P < .05, respectively). Sub-analyses revealed that neutrophilic infiltrate within the epithelium and lamina propria correlated moderately with epithelial and vascular barrier impairment (Table 3).

3.4. Histology and molecular barrier signature

PV-1 expression was significantly associated with chronic inflammatory infiltrate in CD (Spearman’s ρ = 0.3; 95% CI 0.07-0.53; P = .009), whereas no association was observed in UC, underscoring a potentially greater contribution of endothelial barrier impairment than in UC (Table 3 and Table S8). No significant correlations were identified for CD31, CLD2, E-Cadherin, or ZO-1 in this exploratory cohort (Table S8).

3.5. Integrated multimodal prediction of MAOs

The integrated assessment of histologic inflammation, together with barrier impairment, encompassing the overall, epithelial, and vascular components, identified in both CD and UC patients a significantly higher risk of MAOs (Figures 1D and 2C) than either histology or advanced endoscopic evaluation alone (log-rank P < .05) (Figures 1A, B and 2A, B). In CD, the addition of PV-1 overexpression (according to our previously identified cut-off12) to advanced imaging findings and inflammatory infiltrates (neutrophilic and chronic) allowed patient stratification according to the cumulative number of altered domains (epithelial, vascular, and molecular) revealing a stepwise increase in MAO incidence from involvement of one, to two, and ultimately all three domains (log-rank P < .05) (Figure 1E, F).

In multivariate Cox regression analyses adjusting for age, disease duration, and baseline endoscopic activity, the multimodal barrier assessment combining inflammatory infiltrate and barrier impairment profile remained associated with time to first MAO in patients with UC (hazard ratio [HR] 8.14, 95% CI 1.06-62.76; P = .04) and CD (HR 3.15, 95% CI 1.05-9.40; P = .04) (Figure S2A, B).

3.6. Exploratory subanalysis in CD patients in endoscopic remission

Among CD patients in endoscopic remission (SES-CD ≤ 2; n = 28) and without neutrophils in the epithelium, residual histologic inflammation characterized by lamina propria neutrophils and/or chronic inflammatory infiltrates persisted in 14/28 (50%) patients. Despite endoscopic remission, histologic inflammation alone was associated with an increased risk of relapse. When applying the three-domain framework, including inflammatory infiltrate, barrier impairment detected by advanced imaging, and PV-1 overexpression, relapse risk increased progressively according to the cumulative number of impaired domains. Specifically, no relapses occurred among patients with barrier healing (0/3 domains altered; 0% at 12 months). Relapse was observed in 2/9 patients (22.2%) with a single impaired domain, 5/8 (62.5%) with two impaired domains, and 2/3 (66.7%) with all three domains impaired (Figure 3A). Therefore, a stepwise increase in the likelihood of relapse with each additional disrupted barrier component was observed (odds ratio [OR] 3.84 per domain; 95% CI 1.39-16.28; P = .0036). Kaplan–Meier relapse-free survival curves differed significantly according to the number of impaired domains (log-rank χ2 = 19.4, df = 3, P < .001) (Figure 3B).

4. Discussion

The therapeutic paradigm in IBD has shifted progressively toward achieving deep molecular remission, which is associated with better long-term outcomes than clinical or endoscopic remission alone.3,19 Within this evolving framework, both histologic remission and intestinal barrier healing have emerged as critical components of sustained disease control.

In the present study, we demonstrate that an integrated, multidimensional assessment combining histology, advanced imaging, and molecular characterization of epithelial and vascular barrier healing provides superior prognostic stratification compared with conventional approaches.

A major challenge in assessing intestinal barrier impairment is the absence of a universally accepted gold standard. Advanced imaging, histology, and molecular markers capture distinct and complementary aspects of epithelial and vascular status; however, no single modality alone can fully characterize barrier function. To address this limitation, we adopted this multimodal composite framework, aimed at integrating structural, functional, and molecular information and performing a more comprehensive evaluation of barrier impairment in IBD.

Our findings demonstrate that intestinal barrier impairment represents a cumulative, multimodal process that extends beyond mucosal appearance and routine histologic assessment, with direct implications for relapse risk and clinical outcomes.

Previous studies have independently explored the prognostic value of histologic remission and barrier healing, assessed using advanced imaging-based methods and barrier-related protein expression,3,11 as well as the quantification of epithelial (ZO-1, CLD2, ECAD) and vascular (CD31, PV-1) barrier proteins.12

Building on this evidence, we have further expanded our research by integrating, for the first time, histology with structural, functional, and molecular assessments of the intestinal barrier within a multilayer framework to evaluate the cumulative risk of adverse outcomes in CD and UC.

Advanced endoscopic imaging using pCLE and ECS demonstrated a strong correlation with histologic activity, including epithelial and lamina propria neutrophilic infiltrate and chronic inflammatory changes, quantified by validated histologic indices.9,20–22 Our results confirm these findings and further support the conclusion that advanced imaging accurately reflects mucosal inflammation at histologic resolution as a dynamic, real-time complement to static histopathological assessment. Importantly, we observed a strong correlation between cell infiltration between crypts detected by advanced endoscopy and histologic activity, including epithelial and lamina propria neutrophils, as well as chronic inflammatory infiltrate, as assessed by PHRI and RHI scores. These findings reinforce the ability of advanced imaging to evaluate mucosal inflammation at a cellular level and support its role as a complementary tool to histopathology.

Consistently, we observed a moderate-to-strong positive correlation between inflammatory infiltrates and epithelial and vascular barriers, as assessed by advanced imaging. This highlights the tight interplay between inflammatory activity and barrier impairment, supporting the concept that barrier impairment is not merely a downstream consequence of inflammation but an active component of disease persistence. Advanced endoscopy, therefore, offers a unique opportunity to integrate in vivo inflammatory and barrier-related information, enabling a more precise interpretation of mucosal health.

A significant finding of our study is that endothelial impairment emerged as a key element within this multidimensional framework. The presence of inflammatory infiltrate correlated with increased expression of PV-1, particularly in patients with CD. In this context, the association between PV-1 expression and inflammatory burden appears to capture vascular endothelial alterations more sensitively than epithelial tight junction markers. Conversely, the absence of significant associations for epithelial proteins in CD may indicate underlying biological differences between CD and UC. This observation is consistent with our previous findings linking PV-1 expression to vascular barrier impairment and increased disease activity in CD,12 and supports the hypothesis that endothelial barrier impairment is more pronounced in CD than in UC, aligning with the transmural behavior of CD. Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed in UC, potentially reflecting the limited statistical power of this dataset or a true absence of correlation due to disease-phenotype heterogeneity. Overall, our findings suggest that vascular-related markers, such as PV-1, capture key aspects of endothelial involvement associated with inflammatory burden, underscoring the vascular compartment as a relevant and often underappreciated contributor to barrier impairment and disease progression in IBD.

As a further step, we investigated our multimodal framework’s ability to stratify relapse risk.

By integrating histologic activity, advanced imaging-based structural and functional barrier impairment, and molecular endothelial dysregulation, measured by PV-1 expression in CD, we identified patients at progressively higher risk of MAOs. The observed stepwise increase in risk with the accumulation of impaired domains supports the concept that intestinal barrier impairment reflects a cumulative burden across epithelial, vascular, molecular, and immunological layers rather than a defect confined to a single compartment. Notably, these findings were particularly evident in CD patients in endoscopic remission without epithelial neutrophilic infiltration. In this subgroup, advanced imaging identified residual barrier impairment that was undetectable by standard endoscopy yet associated with an increased risk of clinical relapse.

These findings underscore a critical limitation of relying solely on endoscopic and histologic healing as markers of disease control and suggest that subtle inflammation and barrier abnormalities may persist despite apparent mucosal healing and continue to sustain disease activity.

From a pathophysiological perspective, these results align with the concept that the intestinal barrier is a complex, multilayered structure that interacts with the luminal environment.6,10 Disruption of the epithelial and vascular barrier facilitates bacterial translocation, triggering immune activation and neutrophil recruitment, which drive acute inflammation.23,24 Chronic inflammatory infiltrates, predominantly composed of lymphocytes and plasma cells, may persist beyond the acute phase, perpetuating a self-sustaining cycle of barrier impairment and inflammation. Alterations in any single component can therefore propagate across other domains, producing a multimodal signature of mucosal instability.25,26

Taken together, our findings strongly support the need for multimodal barrier assessment in IBD. A three-domain model integrating histology, advanced imaging, and molecular barrier markers provides robust risk stratification across a real-world IBD population and offers a pragmatic framework for identifying patients who remain at risk despite apparent clinical and endoscopic remission. This approach has direct clinical implications for treatment optimization, surveillance strategies, and development of barrier-targeted therapeutic interventions.

Our study also has some limitations. First, it was conducted at only two IBD centers, which may limit its generalizability. The sample size was relatively small, reducing statistical power for subgroup analyses. Second, patients underwent only pCLE or ECS, depending on center availability. Although both pCLE and ECS are ultra-high-magnification techniques that can assess most barrier elements, certain features suggestive of impairment may be more preferentially captured by a single modality (eg, fluorescein leakage). Therefore, larger studies will allow a comprehensive technique-specific comparison.

Although advanced imaging techniques provide highly accurate assessments, they are limited to specialized centers and may be influenced by operator expertise. To address these challenges, we have recently developed artificial intelligence-based tools for automated interpretation of pCLE and ECS features, with the aim of standardizing assessment and improving reproducibility.12

Third, biopsy co-localization with imaging may be imperfect, particularly in CD, due to patchy and segmental inflammation, potentially reducing the strength of some associations. Fourth, follow-up assessments were based on clinically driven evaluations and outcome events rather than on systematically repeated biomarkers; notably fecal calprotectin was not available longitudinally during follow-up. As a consequence, we were unable to evaluate the impact of dynamic changes in inflammatory burden over time. In addition, because the study was designed to evaluate time-to-first MAO and the number of multiple-event cases was limited, the statistical power for robust subgroup comparisons was insufficient.

Finally, given the study’s observational design, the identified association cannot be interpreted as a validated predictor. Further prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts are ongoing to validate these results.

Future research will require the integration of histological, advanced endoscopy, and multi-omics data to enhance risk stratification and move toward personalized and tailored therapy.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that intestinal barrier impairment in IBD is characterized by distinct multimodal signatures that extend beyond mucosal appearance and conventional histologic assessment. A comprehensive, integrated evaluation encompassing histologic, functional, and molecular components provides superior outcome prediction. It enables the identification of patients at risk of relapse despite apparent remission, highlighting a clinically relevant state of subclinical disease persistence. This multidimensional approach represents a critical step toward identifying therapeutic targets and optimizing disease management in IBD, with the aim of achieving durable remission.
