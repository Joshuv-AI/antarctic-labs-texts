Searching for an Animal Model with Clinical Features of Scleroderma
Authors: Harissios Vliagoftis, Satish K. Madala
Published: 2023

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a chronic autoimmune disease that affects connective tissue in various organs, leading to hardening and thickening of the skin, lungs, and other internal organs. Despite progress in understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms, the development of effective therapies for SSc remains challenging, in part because of the complexity of the disease and the lack of suitable animal models (1). Ideally, an animal model should recapitulate the key features of the human disease, including molecular, histological, immunological, and clinical aspects. Several animal models of SSc have been developed, including genetically modified mice, chemical-induced models, and spontaneous models (1–5). Genetically modified mouse models of SSc are the most widely used and well-characterized models, including the Fra2 (Fos-related antigen 2)–transgenic (tg) mouse (5, 6). The overexpression of Fra2 in these mice leads to the development of skin fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and vascular dysfunction that closely resemble the symptoms seen in humans with scleroderma. The use of Fra2-tg mice as an animal model for scleroderma research has several advantages. First, the development of skin fibrosis in these mice is highly reproducible and predictable, which allows researchers to study the progression of the disease over time. Second, the fibrosis seen in Fra2-tg mice is similar to the fibrosis seen in humans with scleroderma, making it a valuable tool for studying the underlying mechanisms of the disease. However, it is important to note that no animal model can perfectly mimic human disease; therefore, further characterization of molecular and pathological features of Fra2-overexpressing mice is needed to extrapolate findings from animal studies to human patients.

Th2 (T-helper cell type 2) cytokine-polarized T-cell responses have been shown to mediate tissue damage and fibrosis in SSc (7). Increased levels of IL-4 and IL-13 are found in human samples from patients with SSc (7, 8). IL-4 and IL-13 have been shown to contribute to inflammation and fibrosis in animal models of SSc, including the bleomycin model of SSc (9). IL-13 has been shown to activate fibroblasts, which participate in the production and deposition of excess collagen and fibronectin in the skin and lungs. Patients with SSc exhibit increases in total serum IL-4 and also T cells producing Th2 cytokines (7). Mice deficient for the Th1 transcription factor T-bet exhibit heightened sclerotic pathology mostly due to IL-4 and IL-13 (10). In this issue of the Journal, Mattoo and colleagues (pp. 404–421) characterize the progressive molecular changes in the pathogenesis of scleroderma and demonstrate the therapeutic benefits of neutralizing Th2 signaling in Fra2-tg mice (11). The investigative team identified pathways that might be responsible for lung pathology in patients with SSc in whom interstitial lung disease develops by analyzing existing gene-expression datasets from SSc/interstitial lung disease lungs (12, 13). Through Ingenuity Pathway Analysis, they identified type 2 cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and IL-33 as some of the main factors modulating gene expression in the lungs in patients with SSc.

The authors then studied Fra2-tg mice and performed a detailed temporal analysis of histological and inflammatory changes in the lungs of these mice. They show that Fra2-tg mice exhibit Fra2 overexpression on many cell types in the lung tissue and that Fra2 overexpression is associated with progressive lung pathology characterized by inflammatory infiltrate of the lungs starting at Week 7 of life, followed by progressive remodeling changes with increased collagen deposition and development of fibrosis, leading to the complete obliteration of pulmonary arteries. Pathology of the pulmonary arteries was also associated with perivascular inflammation, but bronchiolar architecture was preserved. Whole transcriptome analysis of the lungs validated these results by showing progressive increases in the expression of inflammatory mediators and changes in molecules associated with a T cell-driven response. The analysis also revealed an enrichment of Th2 and Th17 pathways, as well as chemokine signaling, dendritic cell maturation, leukocyte extravasation, and fibrosis pathways. The enrichment of the Th2 signature was especially strong, as shown by enhanced expression of genes associated with eosinophil adhesion and migration, IL-4–responsive genes, as well as IL-9 signaling and M2-macrophage pathways. They followed these results with single-cell RNA sequencing of CD45+ cells recovered from lungs of Fra2-tg mice at different time points, starting from early time points, before significant disease can be seen histologically, to late time points, when the lung is characterized by extensive inflammation and remodeling. They identified a cluster of CD3+CD4+ cells and a cluster of CD8+ cells found exclusively in Fra2-tg mice. In both cases, these appear to be activated lymphocytes that may allow us to identify immune pathways associated with fibrotic changes in the lungs.

Enrichment of the Th2 pathway was the most consistent finding across all the techniques used to characterize lung changes in Fra2-tg mice, which is similar to previous data showing activation of the Th2 pathway in these mice. To further test the significance of the activation of this pathway, the authors intervened in Fra2-tg mice through the administration of a bispecific anti–IL-4/IL-13 antibody between Weeks 10 and 14. The antibody improved many of the clinical and pathologic outcomes in the model and also attenuated or inhibited changes in transcriptomic analysis of Fra2-tg mice (Figure 1). However, this approach did not reverse all the changes seen in Fra2-tg mice. Even though the model used recapitulates findings that are also present in human disease, it may not answer the important question of what initiates lung disease in human SSc. Fra2 overexpression is observed on a number of cell types in this model, and it is very likely that changes in Fra2 expression are not the initiating factor of SSc-associated lung disease, but rather that another upstream trigger upregulates Fra2 and Fra2 target molecules in human disease. The Th2 pathway is one of multiple pathways affected by overexpression of Fra2 in this model of lung fibrosis. Whether this is the main pathway leading to mouse pathology and, even more importantly, whether the Th2 pathway is the main trigger for human lung disease in SSc is not easy to ascertain from these data. It is encouraging that blockade of the Th2 pathway improved many outcomes of lung disease in Fra2-tg mice, but the reversal was not complete. The authors analyzed only lung changes in the Fra2-tg mouse model, and further detailed studies will be needed to understand whether the mechanisms controlling fibrosis in other organs in these mice are similar to the ones for lung fibrosis. The study also offers a wealth of information that can be used to develop new hypotheses for lung fibrosis in SSc, and also for the association between lung inflammation and subsequent fibrosis and to test other therapeutic approaches. Testing of the efficacy of blocking other pathways in fibrosis prevention in Fra2-tg mice may also lead to the proposal of other therapeutic approaches that could be tested in humans.
