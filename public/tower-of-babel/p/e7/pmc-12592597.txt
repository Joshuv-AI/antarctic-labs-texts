B.uniformis IM01-derived IAA alleviates asthma via AhR/NLRP3 pathways in mice
Authors: Yujia He, Tao Yang, Jielan Mi, Siqin He, Zhihan Yang, Simin Lu, Kun Yue, Yuanming Huang, Liqiong Song, Yuchun Xiao, Zhihong Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Symbiotic flora exhibits a strong association with the pathogenesis of allergic disorders. Certain Bacteroides species have demonstrated potential in ameliorating allergic conditions. However, the specific role of Bacteroides uniformis in allergic asthma and its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study demonstrates that oral administration of B.uniformis IM01 significantly enhanced the production of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), suppressed airway inflammatory cell airway infiltration and aberrant T helper 2 (Th2) immune responses, and improved the epithelial barrier function in a murine model of asthma. Mechanistically, B.uniformis IM01 upregulated tryptophan metabolism, elevating IAA levels in both colon and serum, which activated the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and induced interleukin-22 (IL-22) production. Activated AhR may inhibit NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and facilitate the splenic differentiation of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), thus attenuating lung barrier dysfunction and improving allergic asthma symptoms. In summary, our results revealed that B.uniformis IM01 upregulated production of IAA to activate AhR leading to inhibited NF-κB/NLRP3-mediated immune responses, and ameliorated allergic asthma through the gut-lung axis.Graphical abstractB.uniformis IM01 upregulated production of IAA to active AhR leading to inhibited NF-κB/NLRP3-mediated immune responses, and ameliorated allergic asthma symptoms through the gut-lung axis. Introduction

Allergic asthma is a heterogeneous chronic respiratory disease characterized by airway inflammation, hyper responsiveness, and bronchial remodeling. It affects more than 300 million people worldwide [1], and its prevalence is expected to increase to 400 million by 2025 [2]. Allergic asthma can be categorized into two types based on different immune responses: type 2 or non-type 2 [3]. About 64%−73% of allergic asthma patients are suffering from type 2 asthma, which is characterized by the elevated level of type 2 inflammatory factors IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, as well as the secretion of serum IgE [4]. Currently, clinical therapy using inhaled glucocorticoids (ICS) and β2 receptor agonists (SABA) has been the established treatment for controlling type 2 asthma, but the efficacy of this treatment is limited [5]. Thus, it is urgently needed to develop more effective strategies for allergic asthma prevention and treatment.

Symbiotic microbiota can induce immune tolerance through interacting with the innate immune system or modulating the adaptive immune system, which is instrumental in protecting against allergic diseases [6]. A pivotal study by Bilal et al. demonstrated that Acinetobacter lwoffii alleviated allergic asthma in mice through IL-6-mediated epigenetic activation of IL-10 production, indicating the anti-asthmatic ability of the symbiotic microbiota [7]. In addition, Kathleen et al. found that lower abundance of Bacteroides at age 3–6 months was associated with transient and early asthma, suggesting that alterations in the Bacteroides could modify the risk of asthma [8]. Moreover, the difference between Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium species has been linked to the higher rate of asthma observed among children with food allergies, implying the therapeutic potential of Bacteroides in allergic diseases [9]. Although the effect of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron on alleviating allergic rhinitis has been revealed in prior study [10], the role of Bacteroides uniformis in allergic diseases is far less elucidated.

Tryptophan can be metabolized into a series of indole metabolites by the gut microbiome [11], such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), indole-3-carbaldehyde (I3C), and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA). They serve as ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [12]. Ligands binding to the AhR can regulate the transcriptions of target genes, such as AhR, NF-κB, and NLRP3 [13–16]. Elevated microbial-derived IAA levels mediated by hydrogen-rich water were shown to activate AhR to alleviate inflammation in asthma [17]. In line with this, administration of IAA by intraperitoneal injection improved the asthmatic symptoms of mice through restoring tryptophan metabolism and increasing the production of other tryptophan metabolites [18]. Moreover, gut microbiota-derived IAA has been shown to restore the integrity of the gut barrier, activate the AhR signal pathway, and inhibit the formation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby relieving neuroinflammation in mice [19]. However, the protective mechanisms of microbiota-derived IAA in allergic asthma are not yet fully understood.

In the current study, we observed that Bacteroides uniformis IM01 alleviateed OVA-induced allergic airway inflammation (AAI) by promoting the production of IAA, which activated the AhR signaling pathway and inhibited the formation of NLRP3 inflammasome and restored the lung barrier. These findings offer new insights into the therapeutic potential of commensal microbiota in the treatment of allergic asthma.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Bacteroides uniformis IM01

The strain of B.uniformis IM01 was isolated from healthy human feces and preserved in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) under the preservation number CGMCC No.30,981. The strain was anaerobically cultured for 24 h in sterilized De Man Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) medium at 37 ℃. Cultures were collected in the log phase and diluted to 109 colony-forming units (CFU) before gavage.

Animals

Female wild-type BALB/c mice aged 4–6 weeks obtained from Beijing Vital River (Certificate No. : SCXK (Beijing) 2021-0006, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Mice were maintained on a typical 12-hour light/dark cycle with free access to water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention Laboratory Animal Center (Approval number: 2024-032). Mice were euthanatized by CO2 inhalation, following cardiac puncture to ensure their euthanasia.

OVA-induced allergic asthma mouse model

Mice were randomly assigned to the PBS group, OVA group, and B.uniformis IM01 group (n = 9 per group) and received daily oral administration of either B.uniformis IM01 (1 × 109 CFU in 200 µL PBS) or PBS once a day for a total of 24 days in the experiments as shown in Fig. 1A. An OVA-induced allergic asthma mouse model was created as previously described [20]. Briefly, mice sensitized to OVA by intraperitoneal injection of 100 µg OVA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) with 4 mg alum diluted in 100 µL of PBS on days 7 and 14. An allergen challenge was performed on three consecutive days (21, 22, and 23) by daily administering an OVA aerosol (1% wt/vol OVA in PBS) to the airways for 20 min to induce allergic asthma. PBS was used as the control for OVA sensitization and challenge. All mice were euthanized 24 h after the final challenge.Fig. 1B.uniformis IM01 suppresses OVA-induced allergic asthma. For the treatment with NLRP3 inhibitor CY-09 (MedChemExpress, USA) and AhR antagonist CH223191 (MedChemExpress, USA), mice were treated with 10 mg/kg of CY-09 or CH223191 which were formulated in a vehicle containing 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% tween-80 and 45% saline by intraperitoneal injection 4 h before OVA challenge on days 21, 22 and 23, and the corresponding vehicle was used as a control.

For the IAA efficiency assay in AAI mice, IAA (Lablead, China) was dissolved in DMSO to make a stock solution and diluted in PBS immediately before use. Mice were treated with 50 mg/kg of IAA by daily gavage for 16 days. The vehicle group was given an equivalent volume of diluted DMSO by gavage.

Antibiotic treatment

For intestinal microbiota depletion, mice were treated with antibiotic cocktails (ampicilin, 100 mg/kg; metronidazole, 100 mg/kg; vancomycin, 50 mg/kg; neomycin, 100 mg/kg) by daily gavage for 7 days [21].

Histological analysis and goblet cell evaluation

Lung tissue was rolled, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, and embedded in paraffin wax. The sections of the embedded tissues were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) stain, Masson trichrome stain, and Periodic Acid Schiff (PAS) stain. The pathological analysis of stained sections was conducted blindly by three independent pathologists. All processes were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The slices were collected and evaluated in a blinded manner.

Flow cytometry

To make single-cell suspension, the collected lung and spleen were minced and then passed through a 70-µm filter for removing tissue debris, and the resulting single-cell suspensions were resuspended in FACS buffer for flow cytometric analyses. The following anti-mouse surface antigen antibodies were used for flow cytometry: anti-CD45-AF700, anti-Ly-6G-APC-Cy7, anti-CD49b-PE-Cy7, anti-CD170-BV421, anti-CD117-BV605, anti-FcεRI-PE, anti-Ly-6 C-PerCP/Cy5.5, anti-F4/80-FITC, anti-CD11b-APC, anti-CD4-FITC, and anti-CD25-PE (all from BioLegend, USA). For intracellular staining, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the True-Nuclear™ Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BioLegend, USA) and stained with anti-FOXP3-AF647. The percentage of each T cell type was measured using BD FACS Celesta and FlowJo analysis software (BD Biosciences, USA).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan) and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). RT-qPCR analysis was performed using ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) on LineGene 9600 Fluorescence Quantitative Detection System (Bioer Technology, China). The results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method and standardized with β-actin. All specific RT-qPCR primers are Listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

The lung tissues were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) containing a 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail to obtain protein samples. The total protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo scientific, USA). Proteins were separated with SDS-PAGE and transferred to the nitrocellulose filter membrane (Cytiva, Sweden). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk before being incubated with the primary antibodies overnight. The membranes were washed and then incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature, followed by signal detection with an ECL kit (Biosharp, China). Image J software was used to detect protein signal levels.

Elisa

Serum IgE levels and the concentration of cytokines IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 and IL-22 in the lung was detected using quantitative ELISA kits (Invitrogen, USA). All kits were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing

The genomic DNA was amplified using bacterial 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 region-specific primers: 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Amplicons were quantified, pooled, and sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform (Illumina, USA) to obtain paired-end reads. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were picked at 97% similarity cut-off. Each representative tag was assigned to a taxon using the RDP Classifier (v2.2). The quality criteria fulfilled sequences were examined and designated for bioinformatics assessment. QIIME 2 software was used for the 16 S rRNA sequencing analysis. The α-diversity was determined from the rarefied OTU table based on the Chao, Simpson, and Shannon indices. The β-diversity of gut microbiota was estimated from the Weighted UniFrac distances to reveal the differences between groups.

Untargeted metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomics was performed at BGI Genomics Co.,Ltd. on fecal samples using waters 2777c UPLC (waters, USA) in series with Q exactive HF high resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to separate and detect metabolites. In short, fecal samples (25 mg) were homogenized in 800 µL precooled extraction reagent (methanol: acetonitrile: water = 2:2:1) with 10 µL internal standard. The mixture was then centrifugated at 25,000×g under 4 °C for 15 min to obtain supernatant (10 µL) for LC-MS/MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining extracts from each sample, with an aqueous methanol solution used as a blank. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters, USA), and the column temperature was maintained at 45 ℃. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B) in the positive mode, and in the negative mode, the mobile phase consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient conditions were as follows: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–9 min, 2%−98% B; 9–12 min, 98% B; 12–12.1 min, 98% B to 2% B; and 12.1–15 min, 2% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min and the injection volume was 5 µL. Data collection was performed in positive and negative ion modes within a range of 70–1050 m/z. Compound discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) software in combination with bmdb (BGI metabolome database), mzcloud database and chemspider online database were used for analysis.

Targeted metabolomics of Tryptophan metabolites

Samples, including intestinal content, serum, and the strain supernatant, were mixed with extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1) and 10 µL internal standard solution (Trp-D5, 4000 ng/mL). The mixture was homogenized and centrifugated to obtain a supernatant for LC-MS/MS analysis. The LC-MS/MS analysis of the sample was carried out at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, the samples were separated by an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 maintained at 40 ℃. The total chromatographic separation lasted for 18 min. The mass spectrometric data was collected using a UHPLC coupled to a QTRAP® 6500 + mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive mode.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± SD. GraphPad Prism (v 6.0) was used for statistical analysis. An unpaired student’s t-test was used to test differences between the two groups, based on the assessment of data variance. Multiple data comparisons between more than two groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (for normally distributed data) or Kruskal-Wallis’s test (for non-normally distributed data). P < 0.05 was considered significantly different.

Results

B.uniformis IM01 administration relieves OVA-induced allergic airway inflammation (AAI) in mice

To validate the potential role of B.uniformis IM01 in respiratory allergies, wild type (WT) and B.uniformis IM01 pretreated-mice were exposed to allergen according to an asthma model as described in Fig. 1A. Analysis of cell populations in the lung of OVA-sensitized asthmatic mice showed a marked accumulation of inflammatory cells, including eosinophils, neutrophils and basophils, whereas treatment with B.uniformis IM01 reduced this infiltration (Fig. 1B and D). Serum IgE levels were lower in the B.uniformis IM01 group (Fig. 1E) compared with the OVA group. Consistent with these findings, B.uniformis IM01 also significantly reduced the expression of Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13) (Fig. 1F and H). Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining, periodic acid Schiff (PAS) staining, and peribronchial trichrome (Masson) staining were reduced in B.uniformis IM01 treated mice (Fig. 1I). To explore the universality at the Bacteroides species level, we accessed the capabilities to alleviate AAI of Bacteroides dorei and Bacteroides caccae. While supplementation with B.dorei or B.caccae resulted in a modest reduction of lung IL-13 levels (Figure S1B), neither strain significantly attenuated serum IgE concentrations nor lung IL-4 and IL-5 levels (Figure S1A and S1B). This comparative assessment underscores that the ability to ameliorate AAI is a strain-specific property of B.uniformis IM01, rather than a general characteristic of the Bacteroides genus. Together, our data support that B.uniformis IM01 administration protect mice from OVA-induced AAI.

B.uniformis IM01 attenuates lung mechanical barrier damage in AAI mice

To further determine the protective mechanism of B.uniformis IM01, we examined its effect on the lung mechanical barrier. Quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis revealed that the mRNA and expression of tight junction-related genes, including ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin5 was significantly more up-regulated in the lungs of the B.uniformis IM01 group compared to the OVA group (Fig. 2A and C). These findings were confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 2D). Additionally, the expression of adhesion junction β-catenin and E-cadherin were also higher in the lung tissues of B.uniformis IM01-treated mice compared to those of the OVA group (Fig. 2E and G). B.uniformis IM01 inhibits the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome by repressing NF-κB signaling in AAI mice

We next aimed to investigate how B.uniformis IM01 exerts its inhibitory effect on AAI. NF-κB/NLRP3 axis is well known to play a critical role in AAI development. Thus, we wondered whether B.uniformis IM01 inhibited the expression of the NF-κB/NLRP3 axis in lung tissues. The results showed that B.uniformis IM01 decreased the OVA-induced activation of NLRP3 inflammasome by inhibiting NF-κB signaling, as evidenced by reduction in IL-6/IL-1β cytokines secretion (Fig. 3A) and NLRP3, caspase-1 (p20), and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-P65) protein expression (Fig. 3B). To elucidate the role of NLRP3 inflammasome in B.uniformis IM01-induced attenuation of AAI, mice were treated with the NLRP3 inhibitor CY-09 (Fig. 3C). CY-09 treatment suppressed OVA-induced elevations in IgE and Th2-associated cytokine expression (Fig. 3D and E), and significantly decreased pulmonary IL-6 and IL-1β levels relative to the OVA group (Fig. 3F). Notably, the use of CY-09 in B.uniformis IM01 pretreated AAI mice failed to further reduce the OVA-induced immune response, suggesting that B.uniformis IM01 alleviates AAI by inhibiting NLRP3 activation (Fig. 3D and F). On the other hand, the significant difference in TJ proteins between the OVA and the B.uniformis IM01 groups were no longer present after the addition of CY-09 (Fig. 3G and H). In addition, no difference in the adhesion junction proteins between WT and B.uniformis IM01-pretreated mice were observed after CY-09 administration (Fig. 3I and J). B.uniformis IM01 alters the gut microbiota composition in AAI mice

Since alteration in the gut microbiome has been shown to be associated with AAI development, we further explored whether the protective effect of B.uniformis IM01 on AAI was mediated by modulation of the gut microbiome. Bacterial 16 S rRNA sequencing results of fecal samples indicated no significant difference in α-diversity between the OVA and B.uniformis IM01-treated mice (Fig. 4A); however, there were significant differences in β-diversity between the groups (Fig. 4B). PLSDA revealed the similarity and dissimilarity of gut microbial composition between the groups (Fig. 4C). B.uniformis IM01 supplementation resulted in a reversal of the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio in comparison to WT AAI mice (Fig. 4D). At the genus level, although no statistically significant difference was observed, B.uniformis IM01 treatment resulted in an increasing trend in the relative abundance of Bacteroides (Fig. 4E and Figure S2). Furthermore, compared to the control group, there was a decrease in the abundances of Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136, while an increase in Alistipes in the OVA group was observed (Fig. 4E). After B.uniformis IM01 treatment, the abundances of Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 increased, whereas the abundance of Alistipes decreased (Fig. 4E). B.uniformis IM01 increases IAA to alleviate OVA-induced AAI in mice

Given that administration of B. uniformis IM01 altered the gut microbiota composition in mice with AAI, we hypothesized that these changes might further influence intestinal metabolism. Earlier studies have found that Bacteroides uniformis contributed to the production of indole metabolites to alleviate colitis in mice [22]. To investigate the effect of oral administration of B.uniformis IM01 on tryptophan metabolism in asthmatic mice, we performed a targeted metabolome of tryptophan. Compared with the PBS control group, OVA challenge significantly reduced colonic IAA levels in mice, whereas B.uniformis IM01 treatment markedly elevated these concentrations (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, B.uniformis IM01 administration demonstrated an upward trend in serum IAA concentrations, though statistically non-significant (Fig. 5B), as confirmed by further ELISA (Fig. 5C). Additionally, we observed an increase of IAA in the supernatant of cultured B.uniformis IM01, which was higher with the addition of tryptophan (Fig. 5D), indicating that B.uniformis IM01 may have the potential to metabolize tryptophan into IAA. Subsequently, we profiled the tryptophan metabolic pathway activity of both B.dorei and B.caccae strains. Under basal culture conditions, both B.dorei and B.caccae demonstrated detectable production of IAA (Figure S3A and S3B). However, compared to the control basal medium, the increase in IAA production by B.caccae was less pronounced than that observed with B.uniformis IM01 (Figure S3B). IAA is an AhR ligand that crosstalks with NLRP3 pathway. Thus, we hypothesized that B.uniformis IM01 may alleviate AAI symptoms via impacting the AhR-mediated signaling pathway. As expected, the expression of AhR and CYP1A1 increased markedly in the lung of OVA-treated mice in response to B.uniformis IM01 (Fig. 5E). AhR is a transcriptional regulator involved in regulating IL-22 that plays a role in promoting epthelial barriers [23]. In line with AhR activation, we found B.uniformis IM01 increased the expression of IL-22 in serum and lung tissues (Fig. 5F), indicating that B.uniformis IM01 might alleviate allergic asthma by enhancing the lung barrier through the activation of AhR and IL-22 production.

To directly evaluate the protective effect of IAA on allergic asthma, 50 mg/kg IAA was administered to mice with or without OVA treatment (Fig. 5G). Similar to B.uniformis IM01, IAA significantly reversed OVA-induced Th2 immune responses, reduced lung pathology, decreased goblet cells and increased AhR and CYP1A1 expression in the lung, while decreasing the expression of caspase1 (Fig. 5H and N), suggesting that IAA is capable of alleviating AAI through AhR/NLRP3 pathway.

To elucidate the role of IAA produced by B.uniformis IM01 in mediating its protective effects against allergic asthma, we investigated whether B.uniformis IM01’s impact on host IAA metabolism and Th2-type immune responses is dependent on gut microbiota. Antibiotic-pretreated mice (to deplete native microbiota-derived IAA) were administered B.uniformis IM01 and subjected to the experimental protocol outlined in Fig. 5O. Administration of B.uniformis IM01 significantly attenuated the OVA-induced depletion of IAA in serum (Fig. 5P), mirroring the effects observed with direct oral IAA supplementation. Concomitantly, this intervention markedly suppressed the expression of serum IgE and Th2-associated cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) in the lung (Fig. 5Q and R). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that B.uniformis IM01-derived IAA plays a dominant and indispensable role in mediating host IAA upregulation and alleviating allergic immune responses. While this effect is evident in the absence of a native microbiota, its interaction with the intact gut ecosystem remains to be fully characterized.

AhR inhibitor treatment eliminates the anti-asthmatic effect of B.uniformis IM01

To examine whether pharmacologic inhibition of AhR abrogates B.uniformis IM01-mediated amelioration of AAI, mice were administrated AhR antagonist CH223191 (Fig. 6A). Our studies demonstrated that AAI was reduced in allergen-exposed B.uniformis IM01-treated mice, which was not observed under CH223191 treatment (Fig. 6B and C). Consistent with these results, B.uniformis IM01 treatment significantly increased the ratio of Foxp3+Tregs of spleen tissues; however, the proportion of Foxp3+Tregs between the OVA and B.uniformis IM01 group did not differ significantly after CH223191 administration (Fig. 6D and E). In addition, CH223191 treatment eliminated the inhibition of NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway by B.uniformis IM01 treatment (Fig. 6F). Compared with WT AAI mice, the presence of CH223191 significantly reversed the enhanced expression of tight junction proteins in B.uniformis IM01 treated mice (Fig. 6G and H), indicating that B.uniformis IM01 may inhibit the NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and attenuate the OVA-induced dysfunctional lung barrier through AhR activation. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effect of symbiotic microbiota B.uniformis IM01 on clinical symptoms of allergic asthma and further investigated the role of altered gut metabolites in immunoregulation through the gut-lung axis. Specifically, our findings demonstrate that B.uniformis IM01 induced-IAA enhancement could trigger AhR activation to inactive NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway, which increases the proportion of Foxp3+Tregs mediating immune tolerance. These effects alleviated impaired lung epithelial barrier and reduced OVA-induced allergic asthma.

Although an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that the administration of probiotics can significantly relieve allergic asthma via modulating gut microbiome composition or its metabolites [23], little is known about the benefit of using symbiotic microbiota in asthma. A study reported that oral application of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron could ameliorate airway inflammation through activating Foxp3+Tregs and inhibit Th2 response in allergic rhinitis and asthma [10]. However, the specific working mechanism for the effects of Bacteroides, for example, whether the alleviating effect of Bacteroides is associated with itself or its metabolites, remains poorly understood. Our results indicate that the protective effect of B.uniformis IM01 treatment is likely to be associated with the metabolites, specifically from converting tryptophan into IAA production.

Most microbial derived-indole metabolites are endogenous ligands of AhR, and AhR signaling-mediated immune response contributes to the protection against allergic asthma [17, 20]. However, AhR may lead to both beneficial and deleterious outcomes in asthma [24]. A previous study demonstrated that the expression of AhR and its downstream genes CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 increased in cockroach-induced asthmatic mice. Human cohort studies showed lower AhR gene expression in bronchial epithelial cells in the patients with severe asthma. Compared to wild-type mice, AhR−/− mice exhibited exacerbated asthma symptoms [25]. The adverse effects of AhR might be associated with different types of ligands and their affinity for AhR, the dose and the duration of AhR activation [26], the cell subsets targeted, and the accessibility of AhR transcriptional targets. We found that AhR inhibitor CH223191 treatment eliminated the alleviating effects of B.uniformis IM01, indicating that B.uniformis IM01-induced relief of airway inflammation may require AhR activation. Activated-AhR interacts with multiple transcription factors, such as NF-κB, NLRP3, and c-Maf, modulate their activity and, consequently, the expression of their target genes [27]. Inhibition of NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway is widely recognized to be a potential therapeutic target for attenuating AAI [28–30]. Consistent with previous findings [31], our data demonstrated that B.uniformis IM01 treatment suppressed the OVA-induced activation of NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway and attenuated lung barrier damage through AhR activation, an effect similar to the administration of NLRP3 antagonist CY-09. Foxp3+Tregs are key mediators in the regulation of immune tolerance in lung tissue, and they secrete various cytokines to suppress immnue responses and induce further Tregs expansion [32]. AhR plays pivotal roles in regulating Treg and Th17 cell differentiation through ligand-specific mechanisms [33]. As a prototypical AhR Ligand, 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) enhances AhR binding to conserved non-coding sequences (CNS), specifically the CABS and NCABS regions within the Foxp3 locus. This interaction consequently promotes Foxp3 transcription and drives Treg cell differentiation [34]. Furthermore, indirubin (IR) has been demonstrated to suppress LPS-induced dendritic cell maturation and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in an AhR-dependent manner, thereby expanding Foxp3+Treg subsets to ameliorate pulmonary inflammation [35]. Consistent with these established mechanisms, our findings confirm that B.uniformis IM01-derived IAA attenuates OVA-induced Th2 immune responses by AhR-mediated induction of Foxp3+Tregs, which was comprehensively abrogated upon co-treatment with the AhR antagonist CH223191.

Distant effects of gut microbiota have been confirmed in diseases such as atopic dermatitis, colitis, and cancer [36, 37]. Alterations of gut microbial composition are linked to immune responses in lung diseases, providing some insight into the development of allergic asthma. Oral administration of B.uniformis IM01 altered gut microbial β-diversity and modulated gut microbial composition. Notably, we observed an increase in Firmicutes and a decrease in Bacteroidetes in the OVA group, which is consistent with the trend of dysbiosis in the microbiota of lung tissue in severe asthmatic patients reported by Zhang et, al [38]. B.uniformis IM01 administration reversed the ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes and restored intestinal flora balance. Furthermore, B.uniformis IM01 reduced the proportions of Proteobacteria. Higher Proteobacteria were harbored in the sputum of active asthmatic adults and bronchial lavage of asthmatic children [39, 40]. Moreover, B.uniformis IM01 elevated the abundance of Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 and reduced that of Alistipes. However, we did not find evidence linking these specific bacterial changes to IAA metabolism. Furthermore, the protective effect of B.uniformis IM01 against asthma, mediated through IAA, remained evident even after antibiotic-induced depletion of the gut microbiota. Although there is possibility that alterations of gut microbiota caused by B.uniformis IM01 may affect the Th2 inflammatory responses, the enhancement of IAA induced by B.uniformis IM01 itself seems to be the primary mechanism underlying the suppression of allergic airway inflammation.

Our study has several limitations that need to be addressed in future investigations. Firstly, a single allergen-induced asthmatic model may not be adequate to account for all characteristics of allergic asthma. In future studies, multiple allergic asthma models could be utilized to evaluate the overall efficacy of B.uniformis IM01 in the treatment of allergic asthma. Additionally, by the current study, we could not rule out how B.uniformis IM01 regulates the production of IAA. In the future research, a B.uniformis IM01 strains with IAA synthesis defect could be used to define how B.uniformis IM01 regulate tryptophan metabolism to contribute to the observed effects. Lastly, while we demonstrated the effectiveness of B.uniformis IM01 in alleviating asthma symptoms in a mouse model of allergic asthma, its efficacy in human allergic asthma needs to be tested in future clinical trials.

In this study we demonstrated that B.uniformis IM01 alleviated allergic asthma by producing the AhR agonist IAA, which in turn inhibited the NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway. This study offers new insights into the beneficial effect of commensal microbiota on allergic asthma symptoms, which will help in the identification and evaluation of novel microbiota with allergic asthma-relieving properties.
