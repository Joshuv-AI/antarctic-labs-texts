Oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among high school students in Robe Town, Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zelalem Tolosa Geda, Ketema Zerihun Asefa, Asefu Nigussie Gonfa, Mesfin Assefa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOral health is crucial for overall well-being and quality of life. Poor hygiene can lead to dental issues like cavities, gum disease, and bad breath, negatively impacting students’ daily activities and health. However, many students, especially in low-income areas, lack adequate knowledge, hold negative attitudes toward oral care, and practice insufficient oral hygiene. This study aimed to assess the oral health knowledge, attitudes, practices, and related factors among high school students in Robe Town, Bale Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, in 2024.MethodsFrom June 1 to July 30, 2024, a cross-sectional survey was conducted with 422 high school students selected via simple random sampling. Data were collected using a structured interviewer questionnaire and analyzed with SPSS version 24. Results were presented in tables and charts, with demographic data summarized descriptively, and Binary logistic regression were calculated to examine relationships between dependent and independent variables.Results50% demonstrated strong oral health knowledge, 47% had a positive attitude, and 54% adequate oral health habits. Male students had lower odds of strong knowledge (AOR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.39–0.93), while Grade 11 students had higher odds. Key factors associated with strong knowledge included proper brushing (AOR: 2.34), using a toothbrush (AOR: 2.35), regular dental visits (AOR: 1.24), and a positive attitude toward dental care (AOR: 1.93). Males also had lower odds of a positive attitude while Grade 11 students had higher odds (AOR: 1.98; 95% CI: 1.03–3.82). Additionally, Grade 11 students were nearly three times more likely to engage in good practices (AOR: 2.89), with factors like visiting the dentist only when in pain (AOR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.1–4.05), regular visits (AOR: 1.38), a positive attitude (AOR: 2.49), and strong knowledge (AOR: 2.71; 95% CI: 0.47–0.96) contributing to better oral health.ConclusionOral health-related knowledge, attitude, and practice among adolescents in Robe Town were found to be suboptimal. Female students, those in higher grades, students with educated parents, and those with prior exposure to oral health information showed significantly better outcomes. Strengthening school-based oral health education, integrating oral health into the curriculum, and engaging parents and teachers are crucial to improve adolescent oral health behaviors in Ethiopia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25169-8.Keywords: Knowledge, Attitude, Practices, Oral health, High school students

Background

Oral health is a critical component of general health, affecting individuals physically, psychologically, and socially. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), oral health is “a state in which any illness or condition, and does not limit an individual’s ability to bite, chew, smile, talk, and maintain psychosocial wellbeing" [1]. Also, Federation Dentaries International (FDI) define oral health is “the ability to confidently speak, smile, smell, chew, swallow, and convey a range of emotions through facial expressions without pain, and discomfort [2]. Oral-related problems, however, may be predisposed to ignorance, a bad attitude, and inadequate dental hygiene habits [3].

Poor oral hygiene can result in pain, discomfort, deformity, and even death, which can lower someone’s quality of life and reduce productivity at school [4, 18], also raises the risk of absenteeism from work and school, which can impair learning and reduce productivity [19, 20]. Ensuring that schoolchildren have good oral health is crucial because it guards against disease, and poor oral health can negatively impact an individual’s everyday life by causing discomfort, suffering, and shame [5].The daily activities reported to have been impaired among the domains of living quality associated with dental health include eating, speaking, maintaining social contact, and schoolwork [6].

Understanding oral health is thought to be essential for creating good habits, and research has demonstrated a link between higher knowledge and improved oral health [7]. Oral health is just as vital as any other aspect of the body and has a significant impact on people’s daily lives and general wellbeing [8].To increase schoolchildren’s awareness of oral health and cleanliness, it is crucial to promote and practice good oral hygiene [9].

Practicing good oral hygiene is essential for preventing issues like ulcers, gum disease, and cavities, which significantly impact overall health [3].Well-being and promoting good oral hygiene practices among high school students are crucial for preventing oral health problems and maintaining good oral health. They are also major determinants of general well-being and life happiness [4].

Despite global, oral diseases is significant public health problem and present a significant economic burden and high health costs [21, 22]. Oral health issues are among the most common non-communicable diseases affecting an estimated 3.5 billion people. Furthermore, Oral hygiene poor practices are linked to gingivitis, periodontal disorders, foul breath, respiratory and chronic renal illnesses [10]. Evidence shows, 2.5 billion people have untreated dental disease [11].

In low-resource settings country particularly in Africa the awareness of oral diseases is significant public health problem. About 400 million individuals suffer from oral illness with 47 nations bear a disproportionate burden of oral health issues in Africa [14]. For instance, oral health problem in Nigeria 73.3% of the students had poor periodontal health and 4.7% had dental cavities [13]. The prevalence of dental caries is notably high, with rates of approximately 40.98% in Ethiopia, 52.4% in Sudan, and 50.3% in Kenya, resulting in an overall pooled prevalence of 45.7% in East Africa [12].

In Ethiopia, studies indicate a 40.98% prevalence of oral health problems, highlighting a significant challenge that requires targeted interventions to enhance oral hygiene and dental care access [12], oral health receives little attention, with mouthwash used infrequently alongside toothbrushes, plant stems, and chewing sticks [15]. Study conducted on oral health in Debre tabor although 61.6% of the students had poor oral hygiene habits [16]. Also, in Addis Ababa that oral disease and dental health problem were both quite common, with rates of 35.4% and 47.4%, respectively [17].

There is a noticeable shortage of oral health education, attitude and cultural practices of oral health in our country, Ethiopia, particularly among high school students. Few studies conducted in Ethiopia on assessing knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) towards oral health. So, our study addresses this gap by assessing students’ oral health knowledge, attitude, and practice and identifying associated sociodemographic factors. This study aims to explore oral health knowledge, attitudes, practices, and associated factors among high school students at Robe Town High School in Bale Zone, Ethiopia, in 2024.

Methodology

Study design and ethical considerations

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to July 3, 2016 E.C among high school students in Robe Town, Bale Zone, Oromia Region, Southeastern Ethiopia. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Salale University Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee (SIU-IRERC). The research protocol received ethical clearance from the SIU-IRERC under reference number SIU-IRERC-267/2016, dated 10 December 2016 E.C. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Confidentiality and anonymity of the participants were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Exclusion and exclusive criteria

All students enrolled in the selected high schools in Robe Town during the study period were eligible to participate. Students who are critically ill, unable to communicate, or have hearing impairments, as well as those who are blind, were excluded from the study. Additionally, students who do not provide informed consent and those who have not attended school regularly during the study period were also excluded.

Data collection methods, tools and data collectors

Data were collected using prepared interview-administered questionnaires that were adapted and modified from different related literature prior to data collection [17, 32–34]. The data collection process involved structured interviews using questionnaires. Structured interviews were conducted to gather data on students’ understanding of oral hygiene, perceptions of oral health, and barriers to maintaining good oral health. The questionnaires were administered by six Bachelor of Science (BSc) nurses fluent in Afan Oromo, who received training on the study objectives and ethical considerations. Two Master of Science (MSc) supervisors in adult health nursing oversaw the data collection process, providing guidance and regularly reviewing the data to address any inconsistencies. This structured approach aimed to ensure accurate and reliable insights into the KAP regarding oral health among high school students in Robe Town and necessary modifications were made based on their feedback.

The developed questionary is conducted in two ways; the first is KAP assessments tool (supplementary I). Oral Health knowledge Assessment Tools using 10 items: Response options were 1 = correct and 2 = incorrect/don’t know. The final scores of oral health-rated knowledges ranged from 1 to 20. Oral Health attitude assessment tools using 5 items; The possible response was three options, whereby 1 = correct and 2 = incorrect/don’t know. The final scores of attitudes toward oral health ranged from 1 to 10. Higher scores represented more positive attitudes toward oral health. Oral health practice assessment tools using 8 items. Dichotomic answers: ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Each of the items was scored 1 if conducted by the participant. The final scores of oral health practice ranged from 0 to 8. higher scores indicated better oral health practices. Based on the participants response who gave more than 50% correct answers to the total questions were categorized as belonging to ‘good knowledge [22] Positive attitude [22], or Good practice [16] otherwise, they were categorized as belonging to ‘poor knowledge, attitude, or practice.

The second one is developed for assessing factor associated factor with oral health related KAP (supplementary II). The questionnaire was structured into four sections: sociodemographic, knowledge, attitude and oral health practice associated factors.

Questionnaire validity and reliability

The questionnaire was adapted from previously published and validated instruments used in similar studies. To ensure content validity, the tool was reviewed by two dental public health experts and one education specialist, and modifications were made based on their feedback. A pretest was conducted on 5–10% of the sample (not included in the final study) to check clarity, comprehension, and cultural appropriateness of items. The reliability of the knowledge, attitude, and practice scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded coefficients of 0.78, 0.81, and 0.76 respectively, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were visually checked for completeness, coded, and entered into EpiData version 4.2.0. A random sample of 10% of the responses was verified for consistency before being exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Results were summarized using frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, and presented in figures, tables, and text. Binary logistic regression was performed to assess the relationships between independent and dependent variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 during Bivariable analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression using the backward likelihood ratio method. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to evaluate the strength of associations, with a p-value ≤ 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Handling of missing data

All questionnaires were checked for completeness during data collection. Questionnaires with substantial missing responses (more than 5% of items unanswered) were excluded from the analysis. For cases with ≤ 5% missing values within a domain (knowledge, attitude, or practice), the missing values were handled by mean imputation within that domain.

The binary logistic regression results for knowledge response

In this study, males demonstrate lower odds of possessing poor oral health knowledge compared to females, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.39, 0.93). Notably, students with a Grade 11 education are over twice as likely (AOR: 2.71; 95% CI: 1.21, 6.10) to exhibit good oral health knowledge compared to those with only a Grade 9 education.

Individuals maintaining proper brushing habits are 2.34 times more likely to have good oral health knowledge (AOR: 2.34; 95% CI: 0.12, 0.94). Additionally, using a toothbrush increases the likelihood of high oral health knowledge by 2.35 times (AOR: 2.35; 95% CI: 1.03, 5.36).

Regular dental visits also correlate with enhanced oral health, indicated by a modest increase in odds (AOR: 1.24; 95% CI: 0.08, 0.75). The critical role of fluoride emerges, as those not using it face increased odds (AOR: 1.59; 95% CI: 1.00, 2.54) of having poor oral health knowledge.

Furthermore, a positive attitude towards dental care significantly correlates with improved oral health knowledge, evidenced by an AOR of 1.93 (95% CI: 1.14, 3.05). Overall, these findings powerfully underscore the vital roles of education, dietary choices, and attitudes in shaping oral health knowledge among students, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions and awareness programs.

Discussion

The aim of the study was to assess the knowledge, attitudes, practices and its associated factors towards oral health among High School students in Robe, 2024. The study found that half (50%) of students had good oral health knowledge. Relatively low level of knowledge observed may be explained by limited access to oral health education in schools and minimal parental guidance. This finding is supported in India indicated that 49% of students had good knowledge regarding oral health [25]. study conducted among high school students in Nigeria reported that approximately 48% had good knowledge of oral health [26]. The similarity might be is due to similar study design conducted in current study in the previous studies.

However, higher than a study conducted in Huye district, Rwanda, found that only 1.8% of students had good oral health knowledge [27]. The disparity in findings may be attributed to differences in study design, sample size, and regional variations in oral health education.

But lower than study in Kathmandu, Nepal, found that 77.2% of students reported good oral health knowledge [28], in Malaysia 97.9% of participants exhibited good knowledge regarding oral health [29]. Conclusions

This study revealed that only half of the high school students in Robe Town demonstrated good oral health knowledge, while less than half held a positive attitude and just over half practiced adequate oral hygiene. The findings indicate a clear gap between knowledge, attitude, and practice, suggesting that awareness alone is insufficient to ensure healthy oral behaviors. Gender and grade level were significant predictors, with male and lower-grade students showing poorer knowledge, attitudes, and practices, whereas Grade 11 students consistently demonstrated stronger outcomes.

Key factors positively associated with good oral health practices included regular dental visits, proper brushing habits, use of fluoride, and a positive attitude toward oral care. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive oral health education and preventive strategies tailored to students’ age, gender, and educational level.

Overall, the results highlight an urgent need for school-based interventions, increased access to preventive dental care, and community engagement to promote lasting improvements in oral health among adolescents. Addressing these gaps will contribute to better health outcomes and improved quality of life for Ethiopian students.

Recommendations

Schools should incorporate structured oral health lessons into science and health education to foster knowledge and lifelong healthy practices. Since male and Grade 9 students were more vulnerable to poor outcomes, tailored awareness campaigns and mentorship programs should specifically address these groups. Oral health promotion should extend beyond schools, involving parents, teachers, and local health workers to create consistent support for healthy behaviors at home and in the community. Longitudinal and interventional studies are recommended to examine causal relationships between knowledge, attitudes, and practices, and to evaluate the effectiveness of school- and community-based interventions over time.

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations, primarily stemming from its cross-sectional design, which captures data at a single point in time and may not reflect changes in knowledge, attitudes, and practices. The exclusion of lower-grade students limits insights into early determinants of oral health behavior, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, self-reported data may introduce bias, impacting the accuracy of the reported knowledge and practices.
