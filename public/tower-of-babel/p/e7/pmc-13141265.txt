Epidural cell therapy reduces neurological sequelae severity in canine distemper survivors
Authors: Jaqueline Cardoso do Prado Bayma, Jonathan Edwin Baracho Trindade Hill, Ana Beatriz Molina, Amanda Baracho Trindade Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanine distemper has a high mortality rate in the acute phase, and survivors typically suffer irreversible, progressive neurological sequelae, due to demyelinating leukoencephalitis, under current treatment standards. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are found in the adult organism and represent a promising alternative for treating central nervous system disorders by secreting trophic and immunomodulatory factors that reduce inflammation, protect neurons, and promote regeneration. The therapeutic action of MSCs is largely mediated by the secretion of trophic and immunomodulatory factors, including anti-inflammatory cytokines, anti-apoptotic proteins, and neurotrophins.MethodsThe objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of three epidurally administered doses of 1 million MSCs per kilogram of body weight at 30-day intervals in four dogs suffering neurological sequelae of canine distemper. Each animal was evaluated using an adapted application of the modified neurodisability scale (0–15 scale) and a quality-of-life questionnaire ranking behaviors (0–7 scale).ResultsStatistical analysis confirmed the significant effect of using multiple doses, and regression analysis revealed a significant influence of age and weight on treatment outcome. Additionally, a logistic regression predicting improvement over time was extrapolated from these results to help clinicians and owners make better-informed decisions regarding cellular therapy. Complete clinical remission of four of the five subcategories of the neurodisability score was achieved in all subjects, with one subject achieving full remission of myoclonus.ConclusionsThese results indicate that epidural MSC administration progressively reduced sequelae severity and improved patient quality of life of the four canine distemper survivors that participated in this study, supporting the translation of cellular therapies into clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04875-5.Keywords: Mesenchymal stromal cells, Mesenchymal stem cells, Canine distemper, Neurology, Dog, Cell therapy, Veterinary regenerative medicine

Introduction

Distemper is a globally distributed infectious disease caused by a morbillivirus of the Paramyxoviridae family, with all families of order Carnivora considered hosts [1]. The canine distemper virus (CDV) is an enveloped, single strand antisense RNA virus, and it infects numerous cell types, including epithelial, mesenchymal, neuroendocrine, and hematopoietic cells of various organs and tissues [2]. It affects animals of all ages, though young, unvaccinated animals are infected most commonly and severely [3].

While infection may result in a variety of clinical signs affecting the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, as well as the skin and other organs and tissues, the provoked immunosuppression and demyelinating leukoencephalitis lead to the gravest sequelae in dogs that survive the acute phase [4–7]. Mortality in the acute phase typically occurs due to dehydration and systemic collapse, as well as secondary infection due to immunosuppression [8], with approximately 50% of dogs succumbing to the disease [9]. Dogs that survive the acute phase of the disease enter the chronic phase, in which a variety of progressive neurological signs may present. In fact, approximately half of the dogs that survive later show neurological signs of distemper [10], including myoclonus, nystagmus, ataxia, postural reaction deficits, and tetraparesia and tetraplegia [11]. Treatment for these sequelae is supportive, including nursing care, fluid replacement, nutrition, and, very importantly, treatment or prevention of secondary infections [8]. Even though some antiviral compounds have shown promise in in vitro studies, translating this into effective treatments for live animals has proven challenging [12–15]. As such, there is currently no recognized effective and safe antiviral therapy for canine distemper [16], and prognosis worsens with increased severity of neurologic signs [8]. In this context, mesenchymal stromal cell therapy has been investigated for animals with distemper and has proven to be the most promising option for treating the neurological sequelae of the disease, as it is safe and allows for either complete remission of clinical signs or partial, temporary improvement [17]. This underscores the importance of studying clinical protocols, dosing, and delivery routes to optimize outcomes. While any improvement is beneficial, refining these factors could further enhance the effectiveness of MSC therapy in treating distemper and similar conditions.

Cell therapy is based on cells’ ability to secrete bioactive, trophic, and anti-inflammatory factors [18]. In the context of CDV-induced demyelinating leukoencephalitis, mesenchymal stromal cells offer a promising therapeutic alternative due to their multifaceted biological effects. In canine distemper, the persistence of neuroinflammation characterized by activated microglia and sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β leads to progressive oligodendrocyte damage, axonal degeneration, and demyelination. MSCs can mitigate these effects by modulating immune responses, particularly by inducing a shift in microglial activity toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby ameliorating the neurotoxic environment that perpetuates central nervous system (CNS) injury [19, 20]. Additionally, MSCs exert significant neuroprotective effects through the secretion of neurotrophic factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which collectively promote neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, axonal regeneration, and remyelination [19, 20]. The therapeutic action of mesenchymal stromal cells is largely mediated by the secretion of trophic and immunomodulatory factors, including anti-inflammatory cytokines, anti-apoptotic proteins, and neurotrophins. These molecules modulate the inflammatory milieu, protect neurons, and stimulate synaptic plasticity and endogenous neurogenesis, particularly under chronic injury conditions [21–23]. Consequently, MSCs hold significant potential for managing the chronic, progressive neurological sequelae of CDV infection, providing a strong rationale for their inclusion in therapeutic strategies for canine distemper survivors.

Despite its potential, there are significant challenges to implementing cell therapy in clinical practice. One major hurdle is the lack of established guidelines regarding dose-response curves, administration routes, dose frequencies, length of treatment course, and expected outcomes [19]. The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of the effect of 1.0 ∙ 106 mesenchymal stromal cells per kilogram, administered epidurally, on the severity of neurological signs in spontaneously occurring canine distemper survivors.

Methods

Patient selection

Four mixed-breed dogs presenting with neurological sequelae as a result of canine distemper, previously diagnosed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and that were no longer considered to be in the acute phase of the disease were selected for treatment.

This was a prospective clinical study that did not involve untreated or placebo animals, as it focused solely on dogs with naturally occurring neurological distemper sequelae. All four patients began presenting signs more than six months prior to treatment. Neurological signs included myoclonus, paralysis, tetraparesis, postural and balance defects, ataxia, and head tilt, among others. An initial clinical examination, complete blood count, and serum biochemistry were performed to evaluate the general state of each animal’s health. As inclusion criteria, we used three assessments: animals were required to have a prior diagnosis, to not have received previous treatment specifically for their neurological signs, and to not be undergoing treatment for any condition at the time of the study. Patients who did not meet these criteria were excluded. All patients were within normal limits with regards to their physical examinations and blood work. All dog owners signed a written consent form before initiation of the experimental procedure, and were fully informed of the study methods, risks, and expected outcomes.

Mesenchymal stromal cell treatment

Three doses of one million allogeneic cells per kilogram of body weight were administered epidurally at 30-day intervals. The dose and interval were adapted from previous studies using 1 × 10⁶ MSCs/kg with modifications to extend the observation period between injections [24]. The cells were collected and characterized as previously described [25, 26] and donated by the CellTech - Tecnologias com Células Tronco research center in Brazil. The cells can be observed in supplementary Fig. 1. All the procedures followed ethical guidelines and were performed in compliance with relevant regulations. The procedure was performed using an anatomical approach, with trichotomy of the lumbosacral region, placement of a sterile surgical drape, and rigorous antisepsis over the L7–S1 intervertebral space. Prior to induction, an intravenous catheter was placed in one of the patient’s cephalic veins, and the anesthetist monitored the patient’s cardiorespiratory status during the procedure. Emergency drug doses were prepared and the surgical suite was equipped and prepared for oxygen supplementation, as well as the potential necessity of endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (4 mg/kg) without premedication, as the procedure was brief and minimally invasive. This protocol was selected to minimize handling and residual anesthetic effects. The epidural technique was performed by the attending clinical veterinarian, with anesthesia induction conducted by the hospital’s anesthesia resident. Epidural MSC transplantation was carried out using a 25/7-gauge needle inserted into the epidural space between L7 and S1. The space was identified by palpation and confirmed using both the “loss of resistance” and “hanging drop” techniques. After priming the catheter with sterile saline, the stromal cells were administered.

No animals were euthanized in this study. All clinical procedures were performed at the Veterinary Teaching Hospital of Centro Universitário de Jaguariúna by a veterinarian.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for reporting animal experiments.

Patient follow up and safety

The patients were evaluated at four time points which were denominated T1 (immediately prior to the first cell injection), T2 (30 days after the first cell injection and immediately before second cell injection), T3 (30 days after the second cell injection and immediately before third cell injection) and T4 (30 days after the third cell injection), as demonstrated in Fig. 1. Three of the patients were monitored for one year after the initial injection to assess safety; contact was lost with the fourth patient after six months.

Health-based quality of life score

Quality of life was assessed via responses to a questionnaire that asked owners to rate the patient’s behaviors on a scale of 1 to 7 (Table 3) at T1 and T4 only. This scale was adapted from the canine scale validated by Giuffrida et al., 2018 [28] and investigated a number of topics related to how well the animal was able to engage in its normal routine, such as on walks, at meals, during play, and at rest, as well as lethargy and propensity to seek out interactions with the owner. The aspects on which each patient was rated in this way are the following: lack of energy, decreased appetite, vomiting, diarrhea, reluctance to get up, disorientation, shortness of breath, pain or discomfort, enjoys being near the caregiver, playfulness, affection towards the caregiver, acceptance of being touched, perfoms favorite activities, good sleep at night, tail wagging, normal behavior, difficulty getting up or lying down, difficulty walking, accidents indoors, falls or loses balance, feeding behavior, comfort, treatment interferes with quality of life [28].

Statistical analysis

The neurodisability scale scores and quality of life questionnaire responses were compiled from the clinical evaluations. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data was analyzed using a one way ANOVA for repeated measures, with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test and Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction. Non-normally distributed data was analyzed using Friedman’s test with Conover’s multiple comparisons test, adjusted by the Benjaminyi-Hochberg FDR method, except where otherwise noted. Results were considered statistically significant if the p value was lower than 0.05. All data categories were compared as means of the four subjects across time points.

A Kruskal-Wallis test was also performed on the change in each measurement of the neurodisability score in reference to its most recent measurement, excluding measurements which had already reached zero (complete remission or absence) in order to better investigate the effect of each dose on the animals’ remaining sequelae, as opposed to their clinical condition as a whole. This test was chosen due to its tolerance of non-normal distributions and missing values.

Regression analysis

A logistic regression of neurodisability score vs. time was calculated with the aim of establishing a predictive model of how much improvement could be expected over the course of the treatment, providing a tool for clinicians interested in forecasting the improvement that the treatment would likely cause in a given patient, and how long that improvement would take to occur given doses at monthly intervals. The logistic equation family was chosen due to its characteristic sigmoid shape, which conceptually fit with the data due to the large differences seen between the first two time points and the second two time points, as well as the fact that a logistic curve approaches a baseline value without exceeding it, which is appropriate given the impossibility of scoring less than 0 on the neurodisability scale.

Linear least-squares regressions were also calculated using the difference in score, from T1 to T4, for each animal using age, weight, and initial score as explanatory values, with the aim of gleaning insight into whether these factors might affect prognosis, in order to help veterinarians make decisions about what treatment plan to pursue for a specific patient.

Discussion

Epidurally administered mesenchymal stromal cells reduced the severity of neurological sequelae and increased quality of life in the four canine distemper survivors evaluated in this study. Given the fact that viremia was absent in all patients prior to participation in this study, it is likely that these results are not specific to distemper survivors and that neurological sequelae may respond similarly well when they result from other demyelinating and inflammatory conditions. Further investigation of this mesenchymal stromal cell therapeutic protocol is warranted in other neurological sequelae resulting from mechanical trauma or other common encephalitides in domestic animals, for this reason. Although distemper does not affect humans, the genetic similarities between dogs and humans, coupled with the comparable size and complexity of their brains and shared physiological characteristics such as weight and metabolism, make dogs an invaluable large animal model for investigating neurodegenerative disorders and evaluating stem cell therapies [30].

The clinical improvements observed in this study align with the multifactorial mechanisms by which mesenchymal stromal cells promote central nervous system recovery. Rather than acting solely through direct tissue regeneration, MSCs primarily exert their effects by reshaping the chronic inflammatory microenvironment that underlies the neurological sequelae of CDV infection. The persistent activation of microglia and the elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β are known to exacerbate demyelination and inhibit endogenous repair mechanisms. MSCs help counteract this process by promoting the polarization of microglia toward the M2 phenotype, which is associated with immunomodulation, tissue repair, and neuroprotection [19, 20]. Additionally, MSCs secrete a wide array of neurotrophic and anti-apoptotic factors, including IGF-1, BDNF, and GDNF, that enhance synaptic plasticity, stimulate axonal regeneration, and support remyelination. These paracrine effects are particularly critical in the chronic phase of CDV-induced leukoencephalitis, where the damage to oligodendrocytes and axons is extensive. The observed reduction in neurodisability scores and improvement in quality of life in treated dogs are likely the result of this integrated immunomodulatory and neuroprotective action. These findings reinforce the rationale for using MSC-based therapies in the management of CDV-associated CNS damage, especially in cases where conventional treatments fail to promote functional recovery [19, 20].

Intravenous (IV) delivery has historically been the most commonly used route for CDV MSC treatments [31–33]. While these studies have contributed valuable insights to the field and demonstrated some improvement in symptoms, the clinical outcomes have not consistently reached statistical significance when comparing results before and after treatment. It has been discussed that intravenous administration poses notable challenges for treating central nervous system conditions, as it may not effectively deliver cells across the blood-brain barrier to reach the CNS due to the inability of the cells to pass the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in most situations [34]. Additionally, cells administered intravenously may become trapped in non-target organs like the lungs, liver, or spleen, reducing the number of viable cells available to treat CNS disorders [34]. Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies in rodent models have shown that less than 1% of MSCs reach the brain parenchyma within one hour after infusion [35]. These limitations often lead researchers to explore alternative delivery routes, such as intrathecal or epidural approaches, which offer more direct access to the CNS [34, 36]. It has been reported that approximately 10–15% of epidurally delivered canine MSCs reached the lesion site within 24 h, which is significantly higher compared to the IV route [39]. Furthermore, real-time in vivo tracking studies using fluorescence-based methods such as Q-tracker® labeling and multimodal imaging (including MRI and positron emission tomography- PET scan) have confirmed the rapid accumulation of MSCs in CNS lesion areas within hours of administration. These studies show that cell migration is guided by injury-induced chemotactic gradients and cytokine signals [37, 38]. Taken together, these findings support the epidural route as a clinically viable alternative for MSC delivery in neuroinflammatory diseases like canine distemper, due to its procedural safety, efficient CNS homing, and compatibility with repeated dosing protocols.

The homing of canine MSCs transplanted via the epidural route in an experimental model of acute spinal cord injury in rabbits has been demonstrated, highlighting the ability of these cells to pass through the dura mater and reach the injury site [39]. The success observed in the case of the present study is likely partially due to the epidural administration route chosen. This route has great potential for use in cellular therapy because it is minimally invasive and the dura mater is supplied by meningeal arterioles and venules, which may favor the homing of transplanted cells [40]. Although epidural administration adds a layer of complexity, the potential clinical benefits justify its use. In veterinary medicine, epidural procedures require general anesthesia to ensure proper positioning, which introduces procedural complexity and inherent risks [41]. Propofol, commonly used for sedation, induction, and maintenance of anesthesia in dogs, is widely regarded as a safe and effective agent for this purpose [42].

An important finding in this study is that administering three doses of stromal cells was found to be more effective than a single dose. A previous study investigating the effects of MSC therapy for neurological sequelae in dogs with CDV reports a significant improvement between the first and second applications and even greater improvement between the first and third applications [24]. In our study, no further significant improvement was observed between T3 and T4, via ANOVA, likely due to the substantial progress already achieved by T3. This interpretation is supported by the results of the Kruskal-Wallis test (Table 4), conducted at each time point only on the clinical signs for which the subjects had not already achieved complete clinical remission, which showed a similar effect size for all three doses, as well as the drastic reduction already seen by T3 (average score of 1.5/15 as opposed to 7.75/15 at T1). This impressive effect size and rapid progression may have been facilitated by the epidural administration route chosen for this study. This approach likely enabled faster or more efficient therapeutic outcomes by directly targeting the CNS, bypassing the limitations associated with systemic delivery methods and ensuring a higher concentration of viable cells reached the injury site.

This dose-dependent response was further highlighted by a logistic regression of mean neurodisability scores. The sigmoid curve described the marked improvement after the second dose and the diminishing overall effect size as animals approached clinical normalcy. This regression model shows potential as a predictive tool for clinicians to estimate and communicate the number of doses likely needed for significant improvement.

Additionally, our analysis, subdivided into the individual categories of the neurodisability scale, was able to demonstrate that the animals’ improvements were not distributed evenly across these categories. By T4, complete remission was achieved in all subjects but one for ambulatory status, and in all subjects for cerebellar function, postural abnormalities, and cerebral function, while only one animal achieved complete remission of myoclonus. This suggests that more than three doses may be beneficial for treating persistent sequelae like myoclonus. Ambulatory status presented significant improvement between T1 and T2, as well as T2 and T3. This suggests that this critical functional element of quality of life may be the first improvement seen in cellular therapy of neurological sequelae. Cerebellar function improved significantly from T2 to T3, at which point it had completely normalized and remained at 0 for all patients through T4. This finding implies that persistence with multiple doses, even when improvement is minimal or not observed after a single dose, is an advisable course of action. While no significant differences were observed between time points for postural abnormalities, this category had normalized by T3 in all patients. Non-epileptic myoclonus improved significantly from T1 to T4, with only one animal achieving full remission. This resistance to improvement relative to the other categories supports the assertion that myoclonus is the most difficult sequela to treat with cell therapy, as corroborated by other studies with similar protocols such as Gonçalves et al. [33], which showed a slight reduction in myoclonus in distemper survivors over a 180 day period, and Brunnel et al. [25], which saw a reduction in myoclonus in 8 of their 12 subjects over the course of treatment. It has been suggested that myoclonus reflects deeper and more refractory neuronal damage [43, 44].

The analysis of the Health-Based Quality of Life questionnaire provides additional support for the efficacy of this therapeutic protocol, revealing a significant improvement between the initial and final measurements. This change aligns with the observed reductions in neurodisability scores, emphasizing the potential of MSC therapy to address both specific neurological sequelae and broader aspects of overall well-being. This result strongly suggests that the animals’ well-being improved commensurately with their clinical condition, which is another important factor for veterinarians and owners to consider in their decision making processes.

Another important observation that may help decision making processes is provided by the linear regression analysis which demonstrates that age and weight score are predictive factors for the success of cellular therapy in CDV survivors. Younger animals and those with lower weights showed greater improvement. This is likely due to the decline in stem cell function with age, which impedes tissue regeneration [45, 46]. Factors like oxidative stress and physical damage contribute to stem cell senescence and decreased regenerative capacity [47]. MSCs have been shown to stimulate endogenous progenitor cells for better regeneration [48], but aging may reduce therapy efficacy, potentially requiring more frequent treatments. Regarding weight, obesity negatively affects cell therapy by reducing mesenchymal stromal cells and impairing their functions [49]. Unlike conventional drugs, MSCs undergo processes like apoptosis and senescence that impact their therapeutic potential [50].

This study has some limitations, including the small sample size, although the substantial improvement in all four subjects’ scores and absence of any adverse reactions after one year of follow up are promising. A lack of a control group is noted, but this is not impactful to the interpretation of the results given the well-documented permanent nature of canine distemper neurological sequelae. For instance, all patients had neurological symptoms with no signs of improvement for at least six months prior to cell therapy. Additionally, although magnetic resonance imaging of the central nervous system was not performed, clinical and neurological evaluations were consistently applied to monitor therapeutic responses. The use of epidural administration also presents practical challenges, including the need for general anesthesia and trained personnel. Another consideration is the availability of MSCs, which may limit broader application. Future studies should incorporate larger sample sizes and control groups to clarify the mechanisms underlying clinical improvement and enhance the predictive power of the regression models. Furthermore, the potential influence of sex and breed on treatment outcomes warrants investigation.

Conclusion

The epidural administration of mesenchymal stromal cells to canine distemper survivors significantly reduced the severity of neurological signs and significantly increased quality of life in this group of subjects, achieving complete clinical remission on the neurodisability scale in all categories except myoclonus, for which only one animal achieved remission, and ambulatory status, for which all but one animal achieved remission.
