Avatrombopag in thrombopoietin receptor agonist-naïve patients with immune thrombocytopenia: real-world treatment patterns and clinical outcomes from the REAL-AVA 3.0 study
Authors: Sandhya Panch, Abiola Oladapo, Scott Kolodny, Michael Vredenburg, Sarah Lucht, Emily Bland, Prathamesh Pathak, Bruce Feinberg, Debbie Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAvatrombopag (AVA) is a thrombopoietin receptor agonist (TPO-RA) approved for patients with immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), but real-world data is limited in TPO-RA-naïve patients.ObjectivesTo describe treatment patterns and outcomes in TPO-RA-naïve adult patients with primary ITP treated with AVA.MethodsThis retrospective chart review study included TPO-RA-naïve adults with primary ITP who initiated AVA on or after July 1, 2019, with ≥ 6 months of follow-up. Patients were followed from AVA initiation until the earliest of either last contact, death, or study end (January 20, 2025). Response was defined as achieving a platelet count (PC) ≥ 30, ≥ 50, or ≥ 100 × 109/L at least once. Response durability was calculated as the percentage of time on AVA with PCs above a response threshold among responders. PCs during or directly after rescue therapy were excluded from response assessments.ResultsAmong 200 patients, 84.0% initiated AVA with baseline PCs < 30 × 109/L. Of these, 98.8% achieved PCs ≥ 30 × 109/L, 94.0% achieved PCs ≥ 50 × 109/L, and 74.4% achieved PCs ≥ 100 × 109/L. Median response durability was 93.2% for ≥ 30 × 109/L, 88.3% for ≥ 50 × 109/L, and 70.2% for ≥ 100 × 109/L. Most patients (66.5%) remained on their initial AVA dose. Median follow-up and treatment duration were 8.5 months (IQR, 7.0-13.5) and 8.0 months (IQR, 6.4-10.9), respectively. At last follow-up, 74.5% of patients remained on AVA. Discontinuation reasons included target PC achievement (51.0%), death (7.8%), and adverse events (2.0%). Most living patients who discontinued AVA (59.6%) received no further ITP treatment.ConclusionAVA is an effective treatment option for TPO-RA-naïve patients with ITP, demonstrating a high real-world rate of sustained PC response.Keywords: avatrombopag; platelet count; purpura, thrombocytopenic, idiopathic; retrospective studies; treatment outcome

Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by platelet counts (PCs) < 100 × 109/L due to decreased platelet production and increased platelet destruction [1,2]. Primary ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion and, unlike secondary ITP, is not associated with another condition [[1], [2], [3]]. ITP is classified by duration of disease, with newly diagnosed ITP defined as < 3 months, persistent ITP as ≥ 3 to < 12 months, and chronic ITP persisting for ≥ 12 months from diagnosis [2]. ITP is associated with recurrent bleeding, hospitalizations, fatigue, and reduced quality of life [[4], [5], [6]], and treatment guidelines from the International Consensus Report and the American Society of Hematology recommend initiating therapy when PCs fall < 20 to 30 × 109/L in the absence of bleeding [7,8]. For newly diagnosed patients with low PCs and minimal symptoms, first-line treatments include corticosteroids and immunoglobulins [7,8]. However, long-term corticosteroid use is discouraged due to adverse effects, and many adults become corticosteroid-dependent or unresponsive, necessitating further treatment [7,8].

Several thrombopoietin (TPO) receptor agonists (RAs) are approved in the United States for second-line treatment of ITP, including romiplostim (ROMI), eltrombopag (ELT), and avatrombopag (AVA) [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Currently, ROMI is approved for ITP regardless of duration, ELT is approved for persistent or chronic ITP, and AVA is approved for chronic ITP [[15], [16], [17]]. While all 3 TPO-RAs target the thrombopoietin receptor (TPOR) to stimulate platelet production, they differ in administration and safety profiles [18]. ROMI, which binds the extracellular portion of TPOR and competes with endogenous TPO [19], is administered via weekly subcutaneous injections typically delivered in a clinical setting [16]. ELT binds in the transmembrane domain of TPOR, is administered orally, and proper absorption requires timing around meals and certain concomitant medications [17]. ELT also carries a boxed warning for hepatotoxicity, necessitating regular liver function monitoring [17,18]. Like ELT, AVA binds the transmembrane domain of TPOR and is orally administered, but it has neither food-type restrictions nor a hepatotoxicity warning [15,18]. As such, patient/provider preferences as well as patient comorbidities both play a role in TPO-RA selection after failure of first-line therapies.

An increasing body of real-world evidence (RWE) supports AVA’s effectiveness; however, because AVA was approved more recently than ROMI and ELT (2019 vs 2008), most patients included in these studies had switched to AVA from other TPO-RAs [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Consequently, RWE on patients with ITP who initiate AVA as their first TPO-RA remains limited due to small sample sizes, as seen in the REAL-AVA 1.0 study (n = 9) [22], the REAL-AVA 2.0 study (n = 60) [24], and a recent study conducted in Spain (n = 62) [23]. Additionally, few real-world studies have characterized the level of dose titration needed to achieve a stable PC when treating with AVA, with limited reporting on the incidence of interruptions [23,28], increases [25], and reductions [25,27]. While AVA’s effectiveness among TPO-RA-naïve patients is expected to be similar to, or better than, results observed in patients switching to AVA after prior TPO-RA exposure, a more precise understanding of the treatment patterns and clinical outcomes associated with AVA is needed to help inform physician selection of first-line TPO-RA treatment. Additionally, limited evidence exists on the use of AVA for newly diagnosed and persistent ITP [27]; given the recommendations for use of TPO-RAs as second-line treatment for ITP [8,29], further research is needed regarding AVA’s effectiveness among patients with newly diagnosed or persistent ITP.

Given these gaps, the overall objective of this study was to describe real-world treatment patterns and outcomes in TPO-RA-naïve adult patients with primary ITP treated with AVA across US community-based practices.

Methods

This study was a retrospective physician-abstracted chart review study. Physicians were recruited from the Cardinal Health Oncology Provider Extended Network, which is comprised ∼350 actively participating oncologists/hematologists across the United States [30]. Prior to data collection, the study protocol and case report form were reviewed by a central institutional board (WCG Institutional Review Board) and granted a waiver for obtaining informed patient consent.

Participating physicians identified eligible patients within their practice and abstracted deidentified patient data into an electronic case report form. Each provider was able to contribute up to 20 patient charts and were instructed to abstract charts in chronological order starting with the first eligible patient. Patients had a healthcare provider-confirmed diagnosis of primary ITP, initiated AVA (ie, index therapy) as their first TPO-RA for primary ITP on or after July 1, 2019, were at least 18 years of age at time of initiation of AVA, and had a minimum of 6 months of follow-up data available after initiation of AVA (unless deceased prior to 6 months). Follow-up was defined as continued management of the patient by the provider, and patients were not required to remain on AVA for the duration of follow-up. Patients were not eligible for inclusion if they had a diagnosis of secondary ITP, had received AVA for treatment of a non-ITP condition, or had participated in a clinical trial for ITP while receiving AVA. Demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), and PC response were collected for each patient. No detailed data was collected on adverse events. Patients were followed from the date of AVA initiation until date of death, last encounter, or study end date (January 20, 2025), whichever occurred earliest (Supplementary Figure 1).

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, HCRU, and PC responses were analyzed descriptively for all patients as well as by baseline PC category, which was defined as the PC closest to but prior to initiation of AVA and stratified into 3 categories: < 30, ≥ 30 to < 50, and ≥ 50 × 109/L. PCs obtained during or directly after rescue therapy were not eligible to be considered a response. PCs were excluded if measured during rescue therapy or within the following postdiscontinuation windows: steroids (8 weeks), immunosuppressants (8 weeks), intravenous immunoglobulin (4 weeks), anti-Rhesus D immunoglobulin (4 weeks), and platelet transfusion (1 week).

Response to AVA was defined as achieving a PC above the following PC response thresholds in the absence of rescue therapy: ≥ 30, ≥ 50, or ≥ 100 × 109/L, among patients who initiated AVA with a baseline count below the respective threshold. Durability of response was calculated at various thresholds (≥ 30, ≥ 50, or ≥ 100 × 109/L) as the percentage of time on AVA with PC above the response threshold in the absence of rescue therapy, among patients with ≥1 PC reported above the threshold while receiving AVA. Intervals between PC reports were categorized as a response or nonresponse based on the preceding PC, with loss of response being defined as ≥2 consecutive PC values ≤ 30 × 109/L after ≥1 prior PC ≥ 30 × 109/L. Patients who experienced cyclical dose modifications on AVA were defined as patients who had ≥1 dose increase and ≥1 reduction on AVA. Patients with ≥2 dose reductions but no dose increases were also described. For HCRU events, designation of an event as “ITP-related” was based on provider report. All analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Medical charts were abstracted for patients (N = 200) with primary ITP who were treated with AVA as their first TPO-RA. Thirty-six US-based treating physicians participated in the patient chart abstraction and abstracted a median of 5.0 charts (IQR, 1.0-8.5) (Supplementary Table 1). Most practiced in a community setting (88.9%) and were board-certified in hematology (88.9%). Participating physicians had a median of 16.0 years (IQR, 11.0-20.0) in practice and estimated treating a median of 25.0 patients (IQR, 19.0-34.0) with primary ITP in the past year.

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

Overall, patients included in this study were 59.5% female, had a mean age of 57.5 (SD 15.0) years at AVA initiation, and were geographically distributed across the United States (Table 1). While patients were predominantly White (67.0%), 16.5% identified as Black/African American, and 21.0% were Hispanic. Most (84.0%) patients-initiated AVA at a baseline PC < 30 × 109/L, with few (1.5%) initiating AVA at PC levels ≥50 × 109/L. There was an approximately equal distribution of patients with newly diagnosed, persistent, and chronic ITP at time of AVA initiation (30.5%, 36.0%, and 33.5%, respectively). The majority of patients (65.5%) had ≥1 comorbidity at AVA initiation, with diabetes (20.5%) and hypertension (13.0%) most commonly reported. Median follow-up after AVA initiation was 8.5 months (IQR, 7.0-13.5).

Treatment patterns

Prior to AVA initiation, 93.5% of patients had treatment with corticosteroids, 46.5% with immunoglobulins, and 27.0% with rituximab (Table 2). Nearly all patients (97.0%) initiated AVA at the label-recommended dose of 20 mg orally once daily; 5 patients (2.5%) initiated AVA for short-term use at 60 mg orally once daily for 5 days. At 30 and 90 days after initiation, 96.0% and 87.5% of patients remained on AVA, respectively. All patients receiving concomitant steroids at AVA initiation discontinued (6/9) or reduced (3/9) those steroids while on AVA, and few patients (8.5%) required rescue therapy while on AVA (4.0% dexamethasone, 2.0% intravenous immunoglobulin, 1.0% cyclophosphamide, and 1.5% platelet transfusion). Most recent AVA dosing was 20 mg once daily for the majority of patients (66.0%), with 10.0% on AVA doses < 20 mg once daily and 24.0% on AVA doses > 20 mg once daily.

At last follow-up, median AVA treatment duration was 8.0 months (IQR, 6.4-10.9), and 25.5% (n = 51/200) of patients had discontinued AVA. Most common reasons included physician-determined achievement of target PC levels (51.0%; n = 26/51), patient preference (15.7%; n = 8/51), and lack of efficacy (15.7%; n = 8/51). Four patients (7.8%; n = 4/51) discontinued AVA due to death unrelated to ITP, and 1 patient (2.0%; n = 1/51) discontinued due to adverse events/toxicity. All patients who received AVA (n = 5) for short-term use (ie, transient crises or procedural needs) discontinued AVA after achieving their target PC. The range of last recorded PC on AVA for these patients was 42.0 to 55.0 × 109/L. Of the patients who discontinued AVA for reasons other than death, 21.3% (10/47) went on to subsequent ITP therapies, including n = 2 who initiated the alternative TPO-RA ROMI, and 19.1% (9/47) underwent splenectomy.

Among patients who discontinued AVA after achieving target PC levels (n = 26; 13.0% overall), duration of ITP at AVA initiation was mixed (42.3% newly diagnosed ITP, 42.3% persistent ITP, 15.4% chronic ITP). In this group, median follow-up after AVA discontinuation was 7.7 months (IQR, 6.0-12.6), and most patients (24/26) received no subsequent ITP treatment (1/26 initiated subsequent ITP therapy; 1/26 underwent a splenectomy). All 5 patients who received AVA at 60 mg once daily for 5 days were included in this group, including 1 who went on to receive subsequent treatment with steroids. Excluding the 5 patients with short-term AVA use, the median for the last recorded PC on AVA for patients who discontinued after achieving target PCs was 109.0 × 109/L (IQR, 100.0-178.0).

Dose modifications

The majority of patients (66.5%) had no dose modifications while on AVA, with 25.5% reporting one dose modification and 8.0% reporting ≥2 modifications (Table 3). Among patients who had dose modifications, median time between modifications, including from initiation to first modification, was 1.4 months (IQR, 0.7-3.3). Dose increases were the most common modification reported (22.0% of all patients had at least one increase; in total, 53 increases were reported). The most commonly reported reason for dose increases was to achieve a stable PC (94.3%), while dose reductions were primarily attributed to reaching (72.2%) or exceeding (11.1%) target platelet levels. Reasons for treatment interruptions included toxicity (33.3%), insurance/financial factors (22.2%), and patient preference (22.2%). Median duration of AVA therapy was 9.6 months (IQR, 6.5-13.6) among patients who underwent ≥1 dose modification.

Overall, 3 patients (1.5%) reported ≥1 dose increase and 1 dose reduction while on AVA (Supplementary Figure 2). Five patients (2.5%) underwent ≥2 dose reductions and no dose increase (Supplementary Figure 3). Reasons for discontinuation among these 5 patients included achieving target PCs (4/5), patient preference (2/5), and adverse event/toxicity (1/5).

Clinical outcomes

Patients had a median of 7.0 PC values (IQR, 5.0-10.0) available in the absence of rescue therapy (Table 4). PC responses to AVA were observed across all baseline subgroups. Overall, 98.8% of patients achieved a PC response ≥ 30 × 109/L; 94.9% achieved a response ≥ 50 × 109/L; and 75.4% achieved a response ≥ 100 × 109/L. Among patients with baseline PCs < 50 × 109/L, 75.1% achieved a response ≥ 100 × 109/L. Few patients (3/200; 1.6%) reported a PC ≥ 400 × 109/L in the first 6 weeks after AVA initiation. Responses were generally sustained in the absence of rescue therapy (Figure), with patients initiating AVA at baseline PCs < 30 × 109/L spending a median of 92.9% of treatment time with PCs ≥ 30 × 109/L. Loss of response on AVA was rare, with 4 patients (2.0%) reporting 2 consecutive PCs < 30 × 109/L after a previous PC ≥ 30 × 109/L. Three of these 4 patients later regained a response on AVA (ie, reported a PC ≥ 30 × 109/L); ultimately, 3 of the 4 patients discontinued AVA, and 2 went on to subsequent treatment with rituximab (n = 1) or fostamatinib disodium (n = 1). Median last PC on AVA among all patients who discontinued AVA was 100.0 × 109/L (IQR, 55.0-180.0).

Healthcare resource utilization

Most patients reported no ITP-related emergency room visits, hospitalizations, or unplanned outpatient visits while on AVA (97.0%, 97.5%, and 98.0%, respectively; Table 5). For patients hospitalized for ITP while on AVA (n = 5), median length of hospitalization was 4.0 days (IQR, 3.0-5.0). Planned ITP-related office visits most commonly occurred on a monthly schedule (59.5%); only 16.5% of patients were seen more than once a month.

Discussion

The high rate of PC responses ≥ 30 × 109/L and durable PC responses observed in this real-world study support the use of AVA as a first-line TPO-RA among adult patients with primary ITP. The majority of patients on AVA were able to stay on treatment at their initial prescribed dose, indicating a low level of dose titration was needed. Nearly all patients who discontinued AVA after achieving target PC levels received no further ITP treatment, suggesting response may remain durable beyond AVA discontinuation for some patients.

This real-world study includes the largest reported number of TPO-RA-naïve patients who received AVA as their first TPO-RA to date, and the positive clinical outcomes observed are consistent with those in prior studies reporting on real-world treatment of ITP with AVA [[22], [23], [24],27]. The REAL-AVA 1.0 study, a claims-based study linked with PC laboratory data, included few TPO-RA-naïve patients (n = 9) but saw similar effectiveness after receipt of AVA as the current study (89% achieved a PC response ≥ 30 × 109/L; 67% achieved a PC response ≥100 × 109/L) [22]. The more recent REAL-AVA 2.0 chart review study included a subgroup of n = 60 TPO-RA-naïve patients for whom strong responses after AVA initiation were observed (93.3% and 86.7% had PC responses ≥ 30 × 109/L and ≥ 100 × 109/L, respectively) [24]. Similar to the current study, all patients in the REAL-AVA 2.0 study were able to reduce or discontinue steroids after AVA initiation. A recent study in Spain, which included both primary and secondary ITP patients, also described high levels of response to AVA among 62 TPO-RA-naïve patients with baseline PCs < 50 × 109/L (98.4% achieved PC response ≥ 50 × 109/L) [23]. In conjunction with the results of prior studies, the high response rate and durable PC response observed in the present study supports AVA’s effectiveness among TPO-RA naïve patients with ITP in diverse community patient populations.

Among patients who discontinued AVA after achieving target PC levels, most received no subsequent ITP therapy or splenectomy (24/200 [12.0%] overall). Previously published literature for ELT and ROMI supports that TPO-RA use may result in immunomodulatory changes, including increased regulatory T-cell activity [31], and ultimately lead to sustained response off treatment (SROT) for approximately one-third of patients [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Studies evaluating SROT after AVA discontinuation are limited compared with other TPO-RA treatments, with SROT estimates reported for 15.4% to 25.8% of patients who discontinued AVA [27,36]. While SROT was not a primary endpoint of this study, the low incidence of subsequent treatment initiation among patients who discontinued AVA after achieving their target PC level provides support that AVA may also induce immunomodulatory effects. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted cautiously, as achievement of target PCs was based on physician determination (ie, no specified PC threshold), and physician goals for AVA use may have varied. Furthermore, the group of patients who discontinued AVA after achieving target PCs in this study had a median follow-up after discontinuation of only 7.7 months, included several patients who had received AVA for short-term use, and included patients with newly diagnosed or persistent ITP at time of AVA initiation. To evaluate whether immunomodulatory changes are present, further studies with longer follow-up and PC data after AVA discontinuation are needed to better estimate the rate of SROT observed for AVA.

Most patients required no or minimal dose titration in this study, with approximately two-thirds of patients remaining on their initial AVA starting dose throughout the entirety of their treatment and <10% of patients undergoing multiple modifications. Overall, AVA dosing in this study, with most patients on a 20 mg once daily dose throughout, was slightly higher but remained broadly similar to that observed in prior RWE studies [21,23,26,27,37]. The higher percentage of patients that remained on a daily dose ≥ 20 mg compared with some prior studies (eg, 90.0% at most recent AVA dose vs 54.5% [26] or 50.0% [21] or 64.5% for maintenance dosing [23]) may reflect differences in patient populations (eg, exclusively TPO-RA-naïve population; high proportion of patients with newly diagnosed or persistent ITP; majority of patients with low baseline PCs) and/or differences in clinical practice patterns by geography (US- vs European-based care). Prior work by Pascual-Izquierdo et al. [23] observed higher initial and maintenance AVA dosing among patients with baseline PCs < 50 × 109/L vs those who initiated AVA with PCs ≥ 50 × 109/L (eg, 73.9% vs 46.6% on maintenance dosing ≥ 20 mg once daily, respectively). However, further studies are needed to better understand optimal maintenance dosing of AVA, as most studies to date that include dose information are relatively small (eg, n = 60 [21]; n = 8 [21]). Of the 3 patients who experienced cyclical dose modifications, 2 were able to continue on AVA, indicating that it was possible for these patients to titrate to an effective dose despite the wide swings observed at initial treatment doses. Among patients who underwent multiple reductions and no increases, 3 discontinued AVA after 2 dose reductions. The dose titration scheme in the AVA label includes 6 dose levels with 3 lower doses below the standard 20 mg orally once daily [15], suggesting at least one further dose reduction was available when physicians elected to discontinue treatment. Wide fluctuations in PCs while on TPO-RAs have been observed previously [38], and although identifying patient characteristics associated with cyclical modifications or repeated dose reductions is an area of interest for future research, the overall low rate of dose modifications in this study support AVA as a convenient treatment option for ITP on which most patients can achieve a sustained response without requiring a prolonged titration period.

Although AVA is currently approved only for chronic ITP use [39], approximately two-thirds of patients in this study had newly diagnosed or persistent ITP at AVA initiation. As ELT is approved for persistent ITP and ROMI is approved for both newly diagnosed and persistent ITP, off-label use of AVA for newly diagnosed or persistent ITP within this study is unsurprising and has been observed in other real-world studies [23,26,27]. One prior study comparing outcomes on AVA by disease stage (newly diagnosed or persistent vs chronic) observed that response to AVA was similar regardless of ITP disease duration at AVA initiation [27]. Off-label use of AVA was also reported in this study for 5 patients who received AVA for a short duration prior to undergoing a procedure; periprocedural use of AVA is currently only approved for patients with chronic liver disease [15]. The PC response achieved by almost all patients in this study suggests AVA may be an effective option for newly diagnosed or persistent ITP and transiently prior to surgery, although additional clinical trials may be required to support regulatory approval for AVA in these areas.

This study had several strengths, including leveraging physician-abstracted patient chart review, which provides comprehensive insights from the treating physician, including reasons for treatment initiation, modification, and discontinuation. Additionally, this study included a large number of TPO-RA-naïve patients who received treatment with AVA, a population of patients that has been studied previously only in small subgroups. The patient population in the current study also included 16.5% of patients who were African American/Black and 21.0% patients who were Hispanic, patient demographics underrepresented in prior studies [14,22,23,26]. Furthermore, comprehensive information on PC values was collected on a large population of patients treated in predominantly community-based settings, allowing insights into ITP management, PC fluctuations, and AVA use in real-world settings where most patients receive care.

This study also has limitations. These results represent only patients from hematologists/oncologists who elected to participate in the study and may not be representative of the broader patient population with primary ITP treated with AVA. While participating physicians were instructed to abstract patients in chronological order based on AVA initiation date, study members did not have direct access to patient charts to confirm; this may introduce selection bias if physicians selected patients according to an alternative scheme. Events occurring outside of the treating physician’s practice or that were not captured in the medical record may have been missing. PCs assessed in this study were collected as part of routine medical care and not at standardized time intervals, which may lead to underestimation or overestimation of time-based outcomes such as response durability. Discontinuation of AVA due to “achievement of target PC” was physician-determined and did not reflect reaching a specific PC threshold; as such, target PC may have differed between physicians. As this study focused on treatment patterns and response outcomes, data on specific adverse events were not collected. However, adverse events were included as a response option for AVA dose modifications and discontinuation. Furthermore, the safety profile of AVA is well documented in previously published literature, encompassing both clinical trials [14,28,40] and real-world studies [23,26,27]. Finally, a minimum of 6 months of follow-up, defined as provider management and not time on AVA, was required for eligibility. Patients who died within 6 months of AVA initiation remained eligible to mitigate immortal time and survivorship bias. However, patients lost to follow-up before 6 months (eg, due to changing providers) may differ systematically from those who remained under care for a longer duration.

Conclusion

In this real-world study evaluating the effectiveness of AVA as a first-line TPO-RA in primary ITP, the results demonstrate a high PC response rate in adult patients with low PCs at AVA initiation. Responses were maintained throughout the majority of the follow-up period at multiple PC thresholds, demonstrating AVA’s durability. Combined with AVA’s oral administration, absence of food-type restrictions, and no requirement for additional liver monitoring, the minimal dose titration observed in this study supports AVA as a convenient first-line TPO-RA option for patients with ITP.
