Globo‐H diagnostic stratification and identification of DUSP14 as a candidate target in colorectal cancer
Authors: Keren Zohar, Marco Strecker, Thomas Wartmann, Wenjie Shi, Frederike Stelter, Maximilian Doelling, Mihailo Andric, Or Kakhlon, Christian Täger, Denny Schanze, Michael Linnebacher, Michael Naumann, Daniel E. Stange, Dörthe Jechorek, Roland S. Croner, Michal Linial, Ulf D. Kahlert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide, underscoring the urgent need for precise and personalized therapeutic strategies. Globo‐H has emerged as a clinically relevant glycan target with promising diagnostic and therapeutic utility across multiple cancer types. In this study, we stratified colorectal cancer patients into Globo‐H‐high and Globo‐H‐low groups using a histology‐based classification, followed by RNA‐sequencing analyses to elucidate the key signaling pathways associated with Globo‐H overactivation. Among the 31 genes that were identified to meet the Globo‐H histology criterion, DUSP14 (dual specificity phosphatase 14) emerged as a promising pharmacological target associated with Globo‐H abundance. DUSP14 is an underexplored but pharmacologically actionable therapeutic target. DUSP14 protein in colon cancer cells is inversely correlated with total Transforming growth factor‐β‐activated kinase 1 (TAK1) protein. The druggability of DUSP14 was demonstrated through in vitro using cell lines and patient‐derived organoids (PDO). These results enhance current diagnostic frameworks and provide a foundation for developing novel targeted therapies. Further, in vivo studies are warranted to evaluate the potential of Globo‐H targeting in combination with standard treatment regimens. Overall, our work highlights the value of integrating PDO‐based functional assays with molecular profiling to uncover and validate actionable targets for CRC theranostics.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a major global health challenge, ranking among the most common malignancies and standing as the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide.1, 2 Despite advancements in screening and treatment, CRC continues to impose a heavy burden on healthcare systems, particularly in high‐ and middle‐income countries, which account for over 88% of global cases.3, 4 Another challenge in CRC oncology is the increase in the occurrence of drug‐resistant cancers that limit the effectiveness of anticancer agents. 5 The drug resistance mechanisms can vary between colon cancer patients and even change within the same patient over time. Thus, personalized and tailored combination therapies are urgently needed in clinical settings. Several innovative strategies were proposed to improve therapeutic outcomes and reduce disease burden. 6

Aberrant glycosylation is a hallmark of cancer cells that frequently alters tumor properties. 7 Among the resulting altered glycans, Globo‐H has emerged as a prominent tumor‐associated carbohydrate antigen (TACA).7, 8 It is abundant in several epithelial cancers, including breast, ovarian, prostate, colon, and lung tumors, while largely absent in normal adult tissues. 9 This observation was recapitulated in several human cancer cell lines. 9 Immunohistochemical analyses have consistently confirmed its expression across malignancies, with minimal presence in normal tissue, primarily limited to the luminal surface of glandular epithelium. 10 The selective expression and correlation with tumor progression and metastasis make Globo‐H an appealing target for immunotherapy. 11 The in vitro and biopsy‐based observations led to the development of therapeutic approaches such as Globo‐H based vaccines (e.g., OPT‐822/OPT‐821), which demonstrated promising immunogenicity in clinical trials for breast cancer 12 and other cancer types.13, 14, 15

Globo‐H is also found on the cell surface of cancer stem cells (CSCs), offering the potential to target the origin of tumor growth and reduce recurrence. 10 In vitro studies have shown that cells with high Globo‐H expression exhibit increased proliferation and tumorigenic potential compared to low‐expressing cells. 16 Furthermore, Globo‐H has been identified as a promoter of angiogenesis and an immune checkpoint molecule, influencing immune and endothelial cell properties. 10 It was proposed that Globo‐H promoted immunosuppression by downregulating NOTCH1 signaling, thereby impairing immune cell proliferation. 11 Another promising approach that benefited from the presence of Globo‐H and other antigens on the cell surface (e.g., SSEA3, SSEA4) is that they serve as a basis for antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs). 17 The recent clinical evaluation of OBI‐999, a Globo‐H‐targeting ADC, in patients with advanced solid tumors further underscores its therapeutic potential.18, 19

This study aims to test the potential of patient heterogeneity while seeking druggable therapeutic targets. We have utilized patient samples to establish an in vitro drug testing platform using two‐dimensional (2D) cultures, and short‐term primary patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) generated from resected colon tumors and adjacent non‐tumor tissues. This system enabled the functional evaluation of the anti‐Globo‐H antibody VK9. Correlating mRNA transcriptome profiling with histological assessment of Globo‐H expression in matched tumor samples, we identified DUSP14 (Dual specificity phosphatase 14) as a key transcript correlated with Globo‐H expression. Pharmacological inhibition of DUSP14 in vitro reduced cell growth, and both baseline activation levels and blockage experiments identified TAK1 (Transforming growth factor‐β‐activated kinase 1) as a putative downstream signal transduction avenue. This work highlights the value of combining patient‐derived models and bioinformatic approaches to uncover candidate antigens and potential therapeutic targets for CRC treatment and diagnosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample acquisition

Tissue samples utilized for the establishment of patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) and subsequent tissue analysis were procured from patients who underwent surgical procedures at Otto von Guericke University in Magdeburg. Patient‐derived tissue specimens were meticulously examined and categorized into tumor and non‐tumor tissue by a board‐certified pathologist affiliated with the Department of Pathology, Otto von Guericke University, Magdeburg.

Immunohistochemistry

All procedures were performed in the histology lab of medical pathology. Globo H‐expression was determined using the VK9 primary antibody staining. Detailed protocols for immunohistochemistry (IHC) are provided in Text S1. Globo‐H expression was analyzed on transmural sections of carcinoma for tumor epithelium in general, of the tumor center, and at the invasive margin, as well as for non‐tumorous colon mucosa. For each compartment, five power fields (×40; 0.23 mm2 per field) were evaluated, and the mean values were calculated. Expression was evaluated as the percentage of positive cells at each of the following intensity categories: 0 to 3+ (0 = negative, 1+ = weak, 2+ = moderate, and 3+ = strong). The H‐score was calculated as follows: H‐score = [fraction of cells with intensity grade 1 (%)] + [fraction of cells with intensity grade 2 (%) × 2] + [fraction of cells with intensity grade 3 (%) × 3], with a possible maximum of 300 points. The H‐score threshold for defining high Globo‐H expression was set at VK9‐TT ≥30, with values <30 classified as low. Using this cutoff, 17 patients were assigned to the low group, and the remaining samples were categorized as high. All samples passed pathologist‐verified quality control for IHC.

Transcriptome analysis of patient specimens

CRC patients from the hospital at Otto von Guericke University in Magdeburg were enrolled. The baseline clinical information of these patients is summarized in Table S1.

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) with RNA‐seq analysis was conducted in contract‐based cooperation at the genome analytics lab at Helmholtz‐Center for Infection Research (HZI), Brunswick, Germany. The mRNA expression levels of all genes were determined by pairwise analysis of cancerous and healthy tissues obtained from the same patient. A total of 33 patients, each of whom provided at least one tumor (T) and one healthy (H) sample. Altogether, we analyzed 36 and 37 cancerous and healthy samples, respectively. The Limma R package (Version 4.2.0) was used for differential expression gene (DEG) analysis.

Bioinformatic analysis

Analyses of CRC patients from public resources were based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data and Genotype‐Tissue Expression (GTEx) for cancerous and healthy tissues, respectively. We applied the visualization websites of GEPIA2 20 and TNMplot 21 to create box plots, violin plots, and scatter plots for comparative visualization of selected DEGs. TIMER2.0 (Tumor Immune Estimation) was used for assessing the purity and infiltration of immune cells. 22 TIMER2.0 provides the correlation of any gene against 22 immune cell types. The TIDE platform reports any gene (or gene sets) across a large number of samples for the T cell dysfunction and exclusion signatures. 23

STRING platform was utilized with protein–protein interaction (PPI) confidence score of 0.4. The collection of gene set that are filtered for colons is extracted from the literature and dedicated resources. Specifically, the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in colon used the comprehensive database EMTome. 24 A gene collection of stem cells colon was based on filtration of colon expression from the BCS (Biomarkers of cancer stem cells) database. 25 The gene collection of angiogenesis in CRC was based on 26 as a cross reference of STRING platform.

Establishment of PDOs and other in vitro models from CRC and healthy colon

Freshly resected tissue samples obtained during surgery were transported on ice to the Institute of Pathology, where they underwent processing by a pathologist. Detailed reagents' information used to culture the cells can be found in Strecker et al. 27 as well as Table S2. For crypt isolation and the establishment of healthy colon organoids, see details in Text S1.

Primary CRC 2D cell lines were kindly provided by Michael Linnebacher (University Medicine Rostock). The cells were cultured in primary CRC cell medium (DMEM/Ham's F12 with L‐glutamine, supplemented with 3 mM L‐glutamine and 10% Fetal bovine serum) and harvested using TrypLE cell dissociation reagent.

Flow cytometric assessment of Globo‐H levels on primary CRC cellular models, and a detailed protocol for the in vitro pharmacology are available in Text S1.

CRC cell lines Colo320, DLD‐1, HCT116, SW480, SW620 were cultured with Dulbecco modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) + 10% FBS, 1% P/S and 1% Sodium Pyruvate. Main genetic alterations in these CRC cell lines are summarized in Text S1. Inhibition experiments were based on the addition of DUSP14 inhibitor using the indicated molecular concentrations. Cells were treated with H2O2 (1 mM) and PTP inhibitor IV (25 μM or 75 μM) (Calbiochem, San Diego, CA) and harvested at the indicated time points.

Immunoblot analysis

Cells were treated with H2O2 (1 mM) and protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTP) inhibitor IV (25 μM/75 μM) (Calbiochem (San Diego, CA)) and harvested at the indicated time points. The cells were washed with cold PBS and then incubated with RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 10 mM K2HPO4, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X‐100, 0.05% SDS) supplemented with 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM Na2MoO4, 20 mM NaF, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 20 mM Glycerol‐2‐phosphate, and 1× EDTA‐free protease inhibitor mix (PI) (cOmplete™, Mini, Roche) for 30 min on ice. Lysates were obtained after centrifugation (13,000 × g, 4°C, 10 min). The total protein amounts of lysates were measured by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method using PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (cat. no. 23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, 10 μg of lysates was mixed with Laemmli buffer, heated at 95°C for 5 min, separated on SDS‐polyacrylamide gel, and blotted onto 0.45 μm Immobilon®‐P PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% dry skim milk in TBS‐T (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 137 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween20) at room temperature (RT) for 1 h. After overnight incubation with a specific primary antibody at 4°C, the membrane was probed with a secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Proteins were visualized using chemiluminescent substrate (WBKLS0500, Millipore) and ChemoCam Imager (Intas). The following antibodies were used: p‐TAK1 (Cell Signaling #4508, 1:1000 in BSA), p‐JNK1/2 (Invitrogen # 44‐682G, 1:1000 in BSA), DUSP14 (Invitrogen #PA5‐103288, 1:1000 in BSA), and GAPDH for loading control (Millipore MAB374, 1:5000 in milk).

Data quality and availability

The data have been uploaded to GEO under accession code GSE305337. The sequencing quality and mapping for each of the 73 samples are detailed in Table S3. The high quality of the libraries is shown by the with expression profile efficiency (84%) across samples. Related data are available from the corresponding authors upon request.

RESULTS

Immunohistochemistry analysis of Globo‐H expression in CRC patients

Figure 1 shows examples of immunohistochemistry by Globo‐H antibodies indicating the expression in colon carcinoma (CRC) and non‐tumorous colon mucosa. Based on VK9 Globo‐H staining, different values were calculated for the invasion front (IF) and tumor center (TC) (Figure 1A). The average staining values for IF and TC were significantly different (p‐value <5e‐04), with 66.97 and 44.24 as mean values, respectively. A coarse granular staining pattern with apical or luminal localization in the tumor glands was present for VK9. Compared to the tumor staining, the H‐score in adjacent normal tissue was much lower (Figure 1B). The VK9 mean value in the TT (total tumor) group is about 6.5 times higher than in the TNT (total non‐tumor) group (49.09 vs. 7.58; p < 3.5e‐03).

Figure 1C,D shows examples of the immune‐stained sections from colon carcinomas using VK9 Globo‐H antibodies. The figures emphasize the high staining variability, with 13 of 33 carcinomas (40%) being completely VK9 negative. A zonal expression with an increase from the tumor center to the IF was detected (Figure 1C). A zoom‐in of a small section with high levels of Globo‐H staining is shown (Figure 1C, right). This observation was primarily shown in tumors with high‐VK9 staining but was also found to some extent in tumors with relatively low levels of VK9 staining. As VK9 staining increased, a granular, apically and luminal staining pattern was detected. The non‐tumorous colonic mucosa was completely VK9 negative in 25 of 33 cases (76%, Figure 1E). In positive tumors, the mucosa originated exclusively from the proximal colon and also showed a granular apical pattern (Figure 1E).

We conclude that Globo‐H immunostaining showed high variability with detailed spatial patterns. These patterns should be assessed at a patient‐specific level for molecular characterization and clinical utility.

Clinical assessment of 33 CRC patients with respect to Globo‐H

We evaluated Globo‐H surface expression in a cohort of 33 CRC samples using the VK9‐based H‐score classification (see Methods). When stratifying Globo‐H levels across clinical parameters, including tumor stage, lymph‐node involvement, metastasis status, and overall UICC (Union for International Cancer Control) classification (Figure 2A), the mean Globo‐H scores ranged between 40 and 60 (but with a relatively large standard deviation). The limited number of samples within each class prevented detection of statistically significant enrichment for T (Figure 2B), N (with the exception of N2; Figure 2C), M (Figure 2D), or grading (Figure 2E). The overall mean H‐score suggests that UICC‐I cases exhibit substantially lower values (Figure 2A). Notably, among the 33 samples, no cases of T1, G1, or G4 were represented. Overall, data from our CRC cohort are consistent with higher Globo‐H VK9 TT staining in advanced tumors, although larger studies will be required to determine whether more subtle correlations with TNM or UICC categories can be resolved.

To correct for inter‐patient variability, the analysis was conducted for pairs of cancerous and healthy tissues obtained from the same patient, for a total of 33 patients. Altogether, we analyzed 36 and 37 cancerous and healthy samples, respectively (note that for some patients we had multiple samples). Figure 3A shows a principal component analysis (PCA) of the differentially expressed genes (DEG) performed on 73 samples (Table S4). The results show a very good separation of tumor (T) and healthy (H) samples, with two PCs accounting for ~32% of the variance. Samples were also classified according to Globo‐H staining (see examples in Figures 1 and 2). The H‐score for labeling samples as high Globo‐H was set to VK9‐TT ≥30. Values of VK9–TT <30 were considered low levels (see Section 2).

The Venn diagram (Figure 3D) shows the DEG partitioned according to the Globo‐H levels for the downregulated (top) and upregulated genes (bottom). The rest of the analysis focuses on the subset of 31 upregulated DEG that are characterized by high Globo‐H levels and thus are candidates for Globo‐H dependent therapy. First, we tested whether this set of 31 unique genes (Figure 3D, bottom) carries clinical predictive power for patients' survival. These 31 genes Globo‐H‐High and upregulated DEG (hereafter referred to as GHH‐DU). Listed in alphabetical order, they are: ANXA3, CCL20, CCND1, CXCL1, CXCL2, DBNDD1, DUSP14, ECT2, FASN, FGFRL1, HOMER1, IER3, IFFO2, IBANK1, NECTIN4, PMEPA1, RIPK2, RPGRIP1L, S100A11, S100P, SCD, SLC7A11, SLCO4A1, SNTB1, SOX4, SPINDOC, STRIP2, TAF1A, TNFRSF12A, TRIM29, and UHRF1. Note that while several of these genes are known genes that induce tumorigenesis, it is evident that the list includes genes that play a role in cytokine signaling pathways (CCL20, CXCL1, CXCL2; GO_MF: 0008009, Adj. p‐value <1.9e‐02) and others involved in cell–cell junction (GO_CC: 0005911, Adj. p‐value <7.7e‐03). Notably, focusing on the GHH‐DU does not imply better survival, but highlights genes for which Globo‐H specificity (e.g., antibody‐drug conjugates) predicts promising therapy. We then compiled 14 large‐scale colon cancer cohorts from different resources (e.g., TCGA, MSK, DFCI) and restricted the analysis to resource that reported on ≥100 samples (Figure 4B). For increasing interpretation on the nature of the gene alterations, we created a subset of samples included only alterations that affect the reported genes through mutations and ignored the contribution of cony number variations (CNVs) structural variants (SVs) (Figure 4C). A survival analysis was performed on the non‐overlapping samples (4739 samples from 4639 patients). The data from Figure 4B shows that 15% of the samples were marked with at least one alteration among the GHH‐DU genes. Figure 4D shows that the overall survival (OS) is statistically significant for alterations among GHH‐DU 31 genes relative to samples where these genes are unaltered (p‐value = 1.36e‐07). For these combined data, the progress free survival (PFS) based on 471 patients was also significant (p < 1.85e‐02). The median survival was 83.2 and 46.7 months for the altered vs. non‐altered group, respectively.

Figure 4E shows that a similar impact on OS was observed for the samples limited to genetic mutations (i.e., excluding other genomic structural alteration). While only 9% of the samples were included in the analysis, the OS remained significant (p < 9e‐06) with hazard ratio (HR) of 0.498 and confidence interval (CI) is 0.392 to 0.633. We conclude that as a gene set, alternations in GHH‐DU tend to be protective.

We applied Gene set cancer analysis (GSCA) analysis 28 with respect to 25 immunological signatures. Figure 5B highlights 4 examples from the GHH‐DU genes with strong correlation (Spearman correlation ≥0.4) with a specific immune cell type. A strong correlation of nTReg was shown for STRIP2 and UHRF1. STRIP2 (striatin‐interacting protein 2) is known to regulate cell growth, migration, and cytoskeletal organization, but it was not identified as a COAD prognostic marker. 29 The UHRF1 (Ubiquitin‐like with PHD and ring‐finger domain 1) promotes carcinogenesis through interaction with DNA methyltransferases and HDAC1 affecting the methylated promoters of tumor‐suppressor genes. Immunohistochemistry analysis of CRC samples revealed that UHRF1 is highly expressed in 66% of the samples, but no expression was detected in normal mucosa. In line with our goal to advance clinical application, we focused on DUSP14, a member of the dual‐specificity phosphatase family, which regulates MAPK signaling and was poorly studied in the context of clinical utility. DUSP14 expression was higher in cancerous samples and undetected in healthy matched samples (Table S4). It exhibits a strong sign of T‐cell exhaustion and also a strong positive correlation with monocytes (Figure 5B, left panels). Figure 5C presents two representative examples of GHH‐DU genes with strong negative correlations (Spearman correlation ≤ −0.4) to specific immune cell types. SPINODC expression was inversely associated with MAIT cell infiltration, a T cell population involved in antimicrobial defense and antitumor responses. Similarly, RIPK2 (receptor‐interacting serine/threonine‐protein kinase 2), a central player in innate immunity and inflammatory signaling, showed a strong negative correlation with Th17 cells, known for their IL‐17‐mediated pro‐inflammatory activity in tumors. The list of the most significant genes of GHH‐DU set in view of the immune cell profile and composition is available in Table S6.

Functional network analysis of upregulated genes with high Globo‐H levels

We further tested the connectivity of GHH‐DU candidate genes in view of the annotated gene sets. We selected four major cancer‐related processes and sought connectivity of the genes in any of the colon expressing genes and the list of GHH‐DU. Figure 5D shows the results for colon‐centric cell cycle. The preselected set of cell cycle genes, including genes that led to cell cycle arrest by anti‐colon‐cancer chemotherapy agents. 30 Similar analysis was performed to identify connection of GHH‐DU genes and stem cells (Figure 5E), angiogenesis (Figure 5F), and EMT (Figure 5G). We utilized the STRING network platform, with protein–protein interaction (PPI), and identified genes that support PPI connectivity (marked yellow). The GHH‐DU set indicated that many of the cancer‐related colon genes were involved in cell cycle and angiogenesis and, to a lesser extent, also stem cells and EMT. We conclude that GHH‐DU genes include candidates that contribute to major processes of CRC cancer hallmarks.

Expression of surface markers in CRC models and culture systems

We further tested the relevance of our candidate genes in the context of CRC patients; we established two distinct cell culture models for investigating the expression of surface markers in primary CRC specimens. The 2D cell culture models were derived from two patients (HROC277 and HROC300, Table S7), each providing one primary tumor specimen and matching synchronous metastatic counterparts (one for HROC277 and two for HROC300). Another in vitro model is the patient‐derived organoids (PDOs), which were established from four patients who underwent surgery at our hospital. These PDOs included three primary CRC specimens and one metastatic specimen. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted to evaluate the abundance of CD44, CD133, and Globo‐H on the cell surface. Clinical and molecular details of the samples are summarized in Table S7.

Flow cytometric analysis revealed distinct patterns of CD44, CD133, and Globo‐H expression across 2D‐cultured CRC cell lines, PDOs, and a comparative analysis of both culture systems. In 2D‐cultured CRC cell lines, CD44 expression was consistently high across all analyzed samples from HROC277 (primary and metastatic specimens). In HROC300, CD44 levels were generally higher overall, with elevated expression observed in the metastatic sample (HROC300Met1) compared to the primary tumor (Figure 6A). CD133 expression demonstrated individual level variability. The metastatic samples from HROC277 (HROC277Met1 and HROC277Met2) exhibited higher levels of CD133 compared to the primary tumor (HROC277T0). Conversely, in HROC300, CD133 was more abundant in the primary tumor than in its metastatic counterpart (HROC300Met1). Globo‐H was the only marker that consistently showed elevated expression levels in metastatic samples compared to primary tumors in both patients. The highest levels of Globo‐H were detected in HROC277Met1 and HROC277Met2.

In PDOs, CD44 expression varied across all samples. The highest levels were observed in the metastatic sample TM162, while the lowest expression was detected in T1719 (Figure 6B). CD133 also exhibited variability among PDOs, with T151 showing the highest levels. Globo‐H expression was consistently high across all PDOs but peaked in T86 and T1719, followed by TM162 and T151.

The comparison between PDOs and 2D cultures revealed significant differences in surface marker expression (Figure 6C). CD44 expression was markedly higher in 2D cultures compared to PDOs (p = .0412). Similarly, CD133 showed a trend toward lower expression in 2D cultures compared to PDOs (p = .0579). In contrast, Globo‐H expression was significantly elevated in PDOs relative to 2D cultures (p < .01), suggesting that culture conditions strongly influence antigen expression and may favor the selection of stem cell‐like phenotypes, such as Globo‐H.

These findings highlight differential surface marker profiles across CRC models and suggest that Globo‐H may serve as a novel cancer stem cell (CSC) marker. Its significantly higher expression under CSC‐selective PDO culture conditions underscores its potential role in identifying stem‐like tumor cells and advancing therapeutic strategies targeting CSC populations.

Therapeutic potential of DUSP14 inhibition in CRC PDOs and cell lines

Inspecting the potential of GHH‐DU genes in driving CRC tumors, along with their manipulability by small molecules, prompted the selection of Dual‐Specificity Phosphatase 14 (DUSP14) as a therapeutic target for deeper inspection. DUSP14, a cysteine‐based protein phosphatase, regulates key signaling pathways by dephosphorylating both tyrosine and serine/threonine residues. 31 Furthermore, it was signified by substantial fold change relative to other GHH‐DU genes (4.2 folds, Table S4). Unlike canonical mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) phosphatases (MKPs), DUSP14 lacks the N‐terminal CH2 domain but retains the catalytic domain critical for modulating MAPK activity, including ERK, JNK, and p38 pathways. Given the absence of clinically approved small‐molecule inhibitors for DUSP14, we utilized ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin (Figure 7A), a competitive inhibitor that binds the catalytic site of DUSP14 and effectively inhibits its phosphatase activity. 32 This inhibition prevents DUSP14‐mediated dephosphorylation of MAP kinases, thereby sustaining ERK, JNK, and p38 activation.

Our findings demonstrate ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin's robust anti‐growth effects on CRC cells, with a significant reduction in viability across three of the CRC PDO models (Figure 7B). The histograms of the dose response results for each of the tested PDOs are shown (Figure 7B). The samples are labeled by the clinical origin of the PDOs. The T151 and two metastatic tumor samples (labeled TM149 and TM162) are shown relative to control values (Figure 7B). Analyzing the IC50 of DUSP14 inhibitor revealed consistent and robust values of 47.73, 46.40 and 50.90 μM for TM149, TM151, and TM162, respectively (Figure 7C), underscoring its potential as a therapeutic agent for CRC subtypes driven by DUSP14 dysregulation. We concluded that despite differences in the expression level of each PDO, the ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin reagent demonstrated acting with identical efficacy.

To substantiate the potential of ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin to inhibit DUSP14, we tested the clinical properties of DUSP14 expression across COAD collection from TCGA. Figure 7D shows that the upregulation of DUSP14 remains and signifies across all tumor stages among COAD samples. There was no significant difference among any of the stages (I to IV). To further support the observation that DUSP14 increase in expression is coupled to tumorigenesis, we tested the change in expression of DUSP14 also in the transition to the metastatic phase and across tumors by their lymph nodal states (Figure S1A). The statistical significance from the normal samples to any of the N groups ranges from 1.6e‐12 to 7.2e‐15 for the N0 (no axillary lymph nodes affected), N1 (1–3 axillary lymph nodes), and N2 (metastases in 4–9 axillary lymph nodes) groups. A similar observation was shown for a partition of tumors according to the status of TP53 mutation (Figure S1B). We conclude that manipulating DUSP14 remains valid in any of the major clinical classifications (i.e., the TNM states), which are only statistically different relative to normal colon tissue. Thus, similar to PDO results, all COAD samples are expected to be sensitive to ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin and to attenuate proliferation across a range of clinical parameters.

To test the molecular mode of action of the DUSP14 inhibitor, we screened 2D CRC cell lines for TAK1 (Transforming growth factor‐β–activated kinase 1) pathway activity, previously shown to regulate DUSP14 potency.33, 34, 35 Among CRC‐derived cell lines (Text S1), we identified the Colo320 cell line as highly DUSP14‐dependent while the other CRC‐originated cell lines showed lower protein expression of DUSP14 (Figure 8A). Remarkably, there was an inverse correlation between DUSP14 expression and phospho‐TAK1/JNK1/2 signal intensity (Figure 8A). Furthermore, treatment with the DUSP14 inhibitor, which blocks its phosphatase activity, slightly reduced TAK1 protein levels (likely by affecting its stability, due to loss of dephosphorylation control) and substantially increased phospho‐TAK1 intensity in Colo320 cells in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 8A). To place these findings in a cancer‐relevant cellular context, we present a simplified schematic (Figure 8B) illustrating how DUSP14 functions as a negative regulator of stress‐ and cytokine‐activated MAPK signaling in cancer cells. Chemotherapy‐induced oxidative stress activates TAK1 and downstream JNK/p38 pathways, promoting pro‐apoptotic signaling. DUSP14 binds and dephosphorylates TAK1 (at Thr187, as detected by the antibody used) and deactivates JNK/p38, thereby dampening stress responses and supporting tumor cell survival. We propose that inhibition of DUSP14 by small molecules preserves sustained MAPK phosphorylation, amplifies apoptosis, and sensitizes tumor cells to cytotoxic treatment (Figure 8B).

DISCUSSION

The importance of glycosphingolipid antigens in cancer biology and the potential of utilizing them as targets for the development of novel therapies are well‐accepted. 36 A prominent member of a family of antigenic carbohydrates is Globo‐H, a hexasaccharide glycosphingolipid formed from the pentasaccharide precursor and SSEA3 (stage‐specific embryonic antigen 3). As reported in different contexts, Globo‐H is widely described as being expressed on the surface of cancer cells as a glycolipid and significantly less abundant in matched non‐cancerous organs and non‐tumor cell lines. 37 Studies from different cancer types emphasize the importance of glycosphingolipid (GSL) biosynthesis pathways to cancer progression. 38 In this study, we examined the tumor‐associated carbohydrate antigen Globo‐H as a new target in CRC. IHC on samples from 33 CRC patients using the VK9 antibody showed Globo‐H expression in tumors, with minimal detection in adjacent non‐tumorous mucosa. Transcriptomic data integrated with histological analysis identified co‐regulated candidate genes. We demonstrated for the first time that high Globo‐H tumors possess a unique set of differentially expressed genes. We emphasize that a substantial fraction of them not only recapitulate previously known phenomena, such as linkage to stem cells, cell cycle, and angiogenesis (Figure 5), but also predicts therapy outcomes in forthcoming immunotherapy using our signature genes.

By applying an unbiased approach to identify a unique set of genes that are upregulated in tumors conditioned by high levels of Globo‐H, we defined 31 candidate genes (coined GHH‐DU). The affected and potentially damaged genes lead to improved survival when considering any genomic alterations or mutations alone (Figure 4). For some of the genes, the connection to colon cancer treatment and progression has been established. Based on our results, it would be beneficial to implement Globo‐H immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining into current clinical standards already established (e.g., MSI staining and mutation analysis).

From a drug development perspective, our study presents novelty as it is one of the first to combine mRNA data and cell surface glycolipid profile data from matching patient tissue samples. This field, particularly correlating GSL levels with mRNA sequencing data analysis, is largely unknown due to the challenges of quantifying GSL levels. Our project focused on the elevated expression of DUSP14 in CRC samples. This gene remains poorly characterized in CRC, although a related gene, DUSP4, is known to be upregulated in CRC and may drive tumor progression by enhancing proliferation, migration, and survival through activation of the PKA/cAMP signaling pathway. Experimental evidence supports DUSP14's role in CRC development, identifying it as a potential therapeutic target. 39 We also analyzed pairwise correlations among all GHH‐DU genes (Figure S2). When DUSP14 was tested for maximal correlation, RIPK2 emerged as one of the top hits (Spearman r = 0.48). Notably, RIPK2 is also part of the GHH‐DU set. While DUSP14 and RIPK2 act at distinct points in the NF‐κB and MAPK signaling pathways, their functions are complementary; RIPK2 promotes inflammation upon activation, whereas DUSP14 downregulates MAPKs and inhibits NF‐κB activation, thereby dampening the inflammatory response.

To assess clinical relevance of DUSP14 activity in CRC, we established 2D cultures and 3D patient‐derived organoids (PDOs) from tumor and non‐tumor tissues to validate gene‐marker specificity. We propose that inhibiting DUSP14 with small molecules offers a sensitive strategy for enhancing CRC diagnosis and treatment.

DUSP14 emerged as a key transcript associated with Globo‐H expression, suggesting it as a promising pharmacological target. We propose that inhibiting DUSP14 with small molecules (Figure 7) offers a sensitive strategy for enhancing CRC diagnosis and treatment. Our results of DUSP14 inhibition provides a mechanistic basis for re‐sensitizing CRC cells to standard chemotherapy regimens like FOLFOX or FOLFIRI, especially in Globo‐H‐high subgroups characterized by DUSP14 upregulation. DNA‐damaging chemotherapeutic agents such as oxaliplatin and irinotecan induce oxidative stress (e.g., reactive oxygen species, ROS) that activates pro‐apoptotic MAPK pathways, including JNK1/2, p38, and upstream TAK1. However, DUSP14 rapidly dephosphorylates these major kinases, specifically p‐TAK1 at position Thr‐187, p‐JNK, and p‐p38, attenuating the stress response and promoting survival in resistant tumor cells, including cancer stem cell‐like populations with high Globo‐H.40, 41 By competitively inhibiting DUSP14's catalytic site with ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin, as evidenced by sustained MAPK phosphorylation (p‐JNK1/2, p‐TAK1) (Figure 8A), this negative feedback is blocked, preserving prolonged kinase activation that amplifies intrinsic pro‐apoptotic signaling (e.g., via TAK1/JNK‐driven apoptosis under chemotherapy stress). This sensitization may overcome chemoresistance mechanisms commonly observed in CRC (Figure 8B). Ultimately, DUSP14 blockade could convert transient chemotherapy‐induced stress into sustained cytotoxic apoptosis by maintaining elevated p‐TAK1/p‐JNK/p‐p38 levels, enhancing therapeutic outcomes in DUSP14‐driven tumors (Figure 8B). Recently, it was shown that DUSP14 is highly expressed in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) and predicts poorer relapse‐free survival. Its knockdown or inhibiting DUSP14 function reduces tumor progression. 42

Our findings emphasize the value of combining PDO‐based functional assays with molecular profiling to identify and validate new ADC targets in CRC. Given the feasibility of downregulating DUSP14 using inhibitors including recently discovered small molecule 4299–0718 using DUSP14 protein‐ligand interaction fingerprinting. 42 Our findings emphasize the value of combining PDO‐based functional assays with molecular profiling to identify and validate new ADC targets in CRC. Given the feasibility of downregulating DUSP14 activity using inhibitors, we advocate further exploration of ADCs as precise therapeutic options for selected antigens. As demonstrated (Figure 7), small molecules can modulate tumor progression and are essential for evaluating the clinical utility of ADCs and targeted therapies in CRC.

Despite the novel insights gained from these studies, we recognize several limitations. Although we analyzed a modest number of patients screened for Globo‐H expression with concomitant mRNA‐seq analysis, larger cohorts, ideally through multicenter collaborations, will be essential to confirm the reproducibility of these early‐stage findings for clinical translation. Furthermore, while the DUSP14 inhibitor ethyl‐3,4‐dephostatin effectively reduces PDO growth and we demonstrate its effect on TAK1‐network activation using protein analyses, it is likely to inhibit other DUSPs, potentially causing off‐target effects that may compromise specificity. We plan to address this in future studies using more selective approaches, including genetic silencing or small‐molecule inhibitors (e.g., 4299–0718, 42 ). Overall, while this project represents an early‐stage biomarker and drug‐discovery effort and findings will require in vivo validation, the correlations between DUSP14 expression and other understudied GHH‐DU druggable genes such as RIPK2 and SCD (stearoyl‐CoA desaturase), highlight attractive candidates for future investigation.
