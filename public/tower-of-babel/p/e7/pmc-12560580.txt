Predicting COVID-19 patient recovery or mortality using deep neural decision tree and forest
Authors: Mohammad Dehghani, Mohadeseh Zarei Ghobadi, Mobin Mohammadi, Diyana Tehrany Dehkordy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIdentifying patients at high risk of mortality is crucial for emergency physicians to allocate hospital resources effectively, particularly in regions with limited medical services. This need becomes even more pressing during global health crises that lead to significant morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of deep neural decision forests and deep neural decision trees in predicting mortality among COVID-19 patients. To achieve this, we utilized patient data encompassing COVID-19 diagnosis, demographics, health indicators, and occupational risk factors to analyze disease severity and outcomes. The dataset was partitioned using a stratified sampling method. Nine machine learning and deep learning methods were employed to build predictive models.ResultsAmong the models, the deep neural decision forest outperformed others. Results indicated that using only clinical data yielded an accuracy of 80.7%, recall of 80.7%, precision of 75.7%, and F1-score of 74.8% by deep neural decision forest, demonstrating it as a reliable predictor of patient mortality. The model differs from other machine learning approaches for COVID-19 mortality prediction by combining the representational power of deep neural networks with the structured decision-making of decision forests, enhancing interpretability and performance using only clinical data without reliance on imaging or laboratory tests.

Introduction

Healthcare system inequality has long been a critical challenge, particularly in regions with low socioeconomic status where access to quality healthcare resources is limited [1]. The emergence of health crises, such as pandemics or sudden surges in disease prevalence, has amplified concerns about the strain on healthcare systems. In these circumstances, there is an urgent need to optimize resource allocation [2], as the potential for shortages of healthcare professionals, hospital capacity, and medical supplies becomes more pronounced [3, 4]. Predicting patient outcomes, such as mortality or recovery, plays a central role in enabling public health experts and hospital administrators to allocate resources more effectively [5].

Traditional expert scoring systems, which are frequently used in clinical settings to assess in-hospital mortality, provide valuable insights but also present limitations [6]. These systems often rely on a fixed set of variables, which reduce their accuracy and adaptability in complex or rapidly evolving medical situations [7]. This indicates the need for systems that can help prioritize patients and predict the outcomes of various treatments.

Artificial intelligence has emerged as a promising tool to address these challenges across a range of diseases to predict outcomes, support diagnosis, and optimize treatment strategies [8–12]. In the context of infectious diseases, AI approaches have been used to predict mortality, identify risk factors, and assist in clinical decision-making [13, 14].

The effectiveness of Computed Tomography (CT) scan and Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) for Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) detection has been compared in several studies. Waller et al. reviewed diagnostic methods and found that CT scans are less sensitive and specific for COVID-19 compared to RT-PCR [15]. They recommend using chest CT as a supplementary tool, particularly for patients who shows symptoms. Al-Momani proposed that RT-PCR remains the recommended first-line diagnostic method for COVID-19, whereas chest CT is not reliable enough for primary screening [16]. Based on these studies, RT-PCR should be conducted in all cases with suspicious symptoms. However, it is important to note that a negative RT-PCR result does not always rule out COVID-19, and repeated testing may be necessary.

Existing mortality prediction models, such as clinical scoring systems and logistic regression, have some limitations in predicting COVID-19 mortality. For example, Queipo, et al. explored seventy-one features using machine learning techniques. The variables that had the greatest influence on predicting mortality in this population were lymphocyte count, urea levels, FiO2, potassium, and serum pH [17]. The XGBoost method demonstrated the highest accuracy, achieving a precision of over 95%. Similarly, a study was conducted by Talkhi et al. on 26,867 hospitalized individuals who tested PCR positive for COVID-19 [18]. The results showed that the artificial neural network model had a slightly higher accuracy (90.27%) for predicting mortality compared to the linear regression model (90.15%). However, most of these studies focus on patients with specific characteristics [19] or use of CT imaging features [20], thereby limiting their applicability in certain circumstances. Moreover, there is no comparison between the importance of collected features and the impact of their existence or absence in prediction. Most deep learning algorithms proposed in studies lack interpretability, which is a crucial aspect of medical applications.

Our study presents potential of deep neural decision forest model to improve prediction accuracy and provides clear insights into how different factors influence patient outcomes in association with improving interpretability. Unlike many AI solutions, our framework emphasizes understanding why decisions are made, which is especially valuable during health crises when quick adjustments and informed resource allocation are crucial. By using readily available clinical data, our system can be effectively implemented even in settings with limited resources, making it accessible across various healthcare environments.

In the following, we explain data, the preprocessing steps, and feature selection in the Materials and Methods. Further, we evaluate the proposed models in the Results. Next, we discuss the outcomes in Discussion.

During preprocessing, we applied Min-Max normalization to the age column to scale the data to a range of 0 to 1. Categorical features were converted to numerical data using one-hot encoding. Appropriate values were used to replace the missing data through KNN-based imputation.

With 32 features in the dataset, a two-step feature selection process was applied. First, features with a minimum frequency rate above 10% were retained, as higher repetition of a feature indicates greater effectiveness. Next, features with a correlation of ≥ 0.1 or ≤-0.1 with the target variable, ‘Disease_outcome,’ were selected. The relationships between the features are illustrated in Fig. 2.

A stratified sampling method was employed to partition the dataset, allocating 80% for training and 20% for testing the models. Stratified sampling involves selecting samples in proportions that reflect their distribution in the overall population by dividing it into groups, or “strata,” based on specific characteristics [2]. This approach enhances the flexibility of sampling methods across different strata and provides more accurate estimates of target parameters.

The implementation was carried out in four distinct stages. In the first stage, all features listed in Table 1 were included for training the model. In the second stage, we removed the Test-Result and Conformation-Method features from the dataset and built models using the remaining features. The Conformation-Method feature contains two values: Clinical and RT-PCR. In the third stage, we focused solely on the Clinical data, excluding RT-PCR from the dataset. Finally, in the fourth stage, we used only the RT-PCR data for predictions. After preparing the data, we applied seven machine learning methods and two deep learning techniques to train the model for predicting patient recovery or mortality at each stage. The popular traditional machine learning including Decision Tree, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Gaussian Naive Bayes (Gaussian NB), Support Vector Machine (SVM) [21–23], and some deep learning algorithms including Deep Neural Decision Tree [24] and Deep Neural Decision Forest [25] were used to build classifiers which are explained in the following. Traditional machine learning models face several limitations, including dependence on data quality and manual feature engineering, limited interpretability, and challenges handling unstructured, sequential, and missing data [26, 27].

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the proposed deep neural decision tree consists of several layers, starting with the input layer, which includes selected features organized into four stages; in stages one, three, and four, there are nine input features, while the second stage contains seven features. Following the input layer, multiple hidden layers are added to identify and analyze complex patterns within the data, incorporating processes such as dimensional expansion, concatenation, and batch normalization. This structure enables the selection of a random subset of input features, followed by the computation of probabilities for input instances to reach the tree leaves. Finally, the output layer is represented by the neural decision tree, which produces the final results using a Sigmoid activation function.

Deep neural decision forest is a model that combines the strengths of decision forests with the non-linear transformations learned by deep neural networks [28]. Instead of a single decision tree, deep neural decision forest uses an ensemble of decision trees to make predictions [29]. The combination of deep learning with the interpretability and ensemble nature of decision forest models, makes it possible to extract complex patterns from data [25]. In neural decision forests, several neural decision trees are simultaneously trained. In a forest model, the output is the average output of the trees. As shown in Table 3, we utilized 25 trees with depth of 10, and the hyperparameters equal to 16, 30, and 0.001 for the batch size, epochs, and learning rate. Moreover, the Adam optimization algorithm was used and the loss function was Binary Cross Entropy. We optimized these parameters through automated hyperparameter tuning using Ray Tune, a Python-based distributed framework that efficiently searches the parameter space to identify optimal values.

Discussion

Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing healthcare through improved predictive analytics, optimized decision-making, and enhanced patient outcomes [30]. AI models, including deep neural models, are increasingly used for disease prognosis due to their ability to analyze large clinical datasets accurately. However, challenges related to data bias, model interpretability, and computational costs require careful consideration [31].

Predicting patient recovery or mortality is crucial during disease outbreaks, particularly when medical resources are scarce. This study evaluated the effectiveness of machine learning and specifically deep neural decision forest and tree models in predicting outcomes for COVID-19 patients across four stages, focusing on the roles of clinical data. This highlights the potential of clinical assessments as reliable predictors of patient outcomes, even surpassing the predictive capabilities of RT-PCR testing.

As medical diagnosis has progressed, nucleic acid detection-based methods have become an essential tool for detecting viral infections. COVID-19 has prompted the development of more than seven types of nucleic acid testing kits [32]. Since RT-PCR is a specific and simple qualitative test, it has gained increasing popularity [33]. However, RT-PCR kits are insufficient, experts are in high demand, accredited laboratories are required, infrastructure is costly, and qualified workers are necessary [34]. On the other hand, RT-PCR results negatively affect computattional model performance due to high false-negative rates, long processing times, and variability in test accuracy [34]. Numerous COVID-19 patients with typical clinical symptoms and CT findings initially receive negative test results, which can introduce variability into the model and decrease its predictive accuracy. Clinically, studies suggest that repeated testing or combining RT-PCR with clinical data improves accuracy. This aligns with clinical findings that emphasize the limitations of RT-PCR and the need for alternative diagnostic approaches, such as machine learning models based on clinical and laboratory data [34].

Recent studies on COVID-19 mortality prediction have leveraged advanced machine learning techniques and imaging data. One study utilized 201 radiomic features and 16 neural network features from chest X-rays of 1,816 patients, applying algorithms like AdaBoost and random forest to forecast mortality [35]. Another research employed convolutional neural networks on data from 1,170 patients with 19,685 labeled CT slices to predict outcomes. Additionally, a multivariable logistic regression model analyzed risk factors in 1,663 hospitalized patients, identifying key variables such as age and pre-existing medical conditions that influence mortality rates [36]. Consistent with theses study, we found that the deep neural decision forest achieved an accuracy of 80.7%, demonstrating advantages such as reduced overfitting and improved robustness to noise. The deep neural decision forest outperformed the deep neural decision tree due to its ensemble learning approach, using multiple decision trees to capture complex patterns and reduce overfitting, along with improved feature selection [37]. This structure enhances model robustness and performance, while hyperparameter optimization may have further contributed.

The deep neural decision forest demonstrated strong predictive performance using only clinical data, making it a valuable tool for real-world clinical applications. Its ability to accurately predict mortality risk can assist physicians in early identification of high-risk patients, enabling timely interventions and optimized resource allocation, especially in emergency and intensive care settings. This is particularly crucial in resource-limited regions where access to advanced diagnostic tools may be restricted. By integrating such models into clinical workflows, hospitals can enhance patient triage, prioritize critical cases, and improve overall treatment outcomes.

The proposed model, while promising for predicting COVID-19 mortality, faces limitations. While our dataset provides valuable insights into local healthcare challenges, potential biases such as sampling bias, selection bias, measurement bias, temporal bias, class imbalance, and sociodemographic bias must be carefully considered. These biases can compromise the model’s generalizability, robustness, and ethical integrity. By employing mitigation strategies such as data augmentation, stratified sampling, and rigorous validation, we can enhance the model’s ability to perform reliably across diverse populations and settings. These issues emphasize the need for ongoing refinement, diverse training data, and optimization for practical deployment in various clinical environments. On the other hand, while deep neural decision forest offers promising capabilities for clinical applications, its deployment in real-time settings faces significant computational challenges. These include high training and inference costs, resource demands, scalability issues, and trade-offs between accuracy and speed. By employing strategies such as model compression, approximate inference, and edge computing, these challenges can be resolved. However, careful consideration must be given to balancing computational efficiency with clinical requirements to ensure practical usability in real-world scenarios.

Future research directions should focus on enhancing the model’s effectiveness by addressing several key areas. The model’s generalizability could be improved by incorporating more diverse and representative patient populations, addressing potential biases in the data sources. Incorporating imaging data, such as chest X-rays or CT scans, could improve predictive accuracy by capturing disease severity more comprehensively. Multi-modal AI approaches that integrate clinical, laboratory, genomic, and imaging data could provide a more holistic assessment of patient risk, potentially improving both sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, federated learning techniques for privacy-preserving AI in healthcare could enable decentralized training across multiple hospitals, facilitating multi-hospital collaborations without the need for data sharing. Future research can also focus on integrating the proposed AI model into electronic health records (EHR) systems and hospital triage processes. This integration would streamline clinical workflows and provide enhanced real-time decision support. By developing interoperable interfaces and utilizing standardized data formats, the model can be seamlessly embedded into existing healthcare infrastructures. This would enable clinicians to access actionable insights directly within their workflow, improving efficiency and decision-making. Furthermore, exploring transfer learning techniques could enable the deep neural decision forest framework to adapt quickly to new pandemics or emerging healthcare challenges, leveraging pre-trained models to reduce training time and improve performance in novel scenarios. This approach would allow the model to generalize more effectively across different diseases or populations while minimizing the need for extensive retraining with limited data. Future work should also investigate multi-objective optimization strategies to balance predictive accuracy and model interpretability, ensuring that models are both reliable and trustworthy in clinical settings. By employing techniques such as regularization methods, it may be possible to develop deep neural decision forest models that achieve an optimal trade-off between these competing objectives. Although the deep neural decision forest model holds promise for predicting mortality across various diseases beyond COVID-19 due to its reliance on clinical data, which often shares similarities across conditions, successful adaptation requires retraining with disease-specific data, as clinical indicators and risk factors can vary significantly between diseases. The model’s performance may also be influenced by unique disease characteristics and data quality. To ensure effectiveness in other medical conditions, domain-specific feature engineering, preprocessing, and validation are essential.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the utility of deep neural decision forest and deep neural decision tree models for predicting mortality among COVID-19 patients, addressing the critical need for effective risk stratification, particularly in resource-limited settings. The results also reveal the advantage of using clinical data over RT-PCR for mortality prediction in COVID-19 patients. While RT-PCR is essential for diagnosing infection, its high false-negative rate and reliance on availability and timely processing limit its effectiveness in risk stratification. In contrast, the deep neural decision forest model demonstrated strong predictive performance using only clinical data, highlighting its potential as a more accessible and reliable tool for assessing patient mortality risk. Moreover, our model’s ability to learn complex patterns from clinical data while offering some interpretability is crucial for clinical adoption, enabling healthcare professionals to understand the factors driving predictions. By leveraging routinely collected clinical indicators, the model reduces dependency on expensive or delayed laboratory tests, making it particularly valuable in resource-limited settings. Given the reliance of proposed model on readily available clinical data and its potential for real-time analysis, it could be integrated into hospital information systems for real-time mortality prediction, aiding clinical decision-making and improving patient outcomes through early risk identification.
