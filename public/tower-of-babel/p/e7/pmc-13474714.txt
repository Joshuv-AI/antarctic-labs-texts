The effect of nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices on medical error tendency: a descriptive and correlational study
Authors: Merdiye Sendir, Cevriye Ozdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses are expected to apply the best available evidence in their clinical decision-making processes to enhance patient safety and reduce the risk of medical errors.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the effect of nurses’ attitudes toward EBP on their tendency to commit medical errors.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional, and correlational design was used with a sample of 412 nurses. To reach nurses in public hospitals, we adopted a census sampling approach. Data were gathered through face-to-face interviews using a Google Forms questionnaire—accessible via QR codes and email—which incorporated the Attitude Toward Evidence-Based Nursing Scale and the Medical Error Tendency Scale in Nursing. Descriptive statistics and structural equation modeling (SEM) were conducted to test the hypothesized model in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.ResultsNurses reported moderately positive attitudes toward EBP and comparatively low tendencies to commit medical errors. Postgraduate education was significantly associated with more positive EBP attitudes and lower error tendencies. The SEM results revealed a significant association between more positive EBP attitudes and reduced medical error tendencies (β = 0.41, p < 0.001). The proposed model showed acceptable fit indices.ConclusionPositive attitudes toward evidence-based nursing were significantly associated with a reduced tendency to commit medical errors.RecommendationsNursing leaders and policymakers should implement strategies to strengthen nurses’ EBP attitudes and mitigate the risk of errors through targeted education, mentorship, and supportive organizational policies and practices.Clinical trialNot applicable.Keywords: Evidence-based nursing, Nursing, Clinical practice, Medical error

Background

Evidence-based practices (EBPs) play a critical role in enhancing patient safety and improving the quality of healthcare services. By grounding clinical decision-making in scientific evidence, EBP directly influences both the effectiveness and safety of care delivery [1, 2]. Recent studies have demonstrated that nurses who effectively implement EBP achieve higher standards of safety and quality in clinical practice, leading to improved patient outcomes [3, 4].

Medical errors may occur in various domains, including medication administration, patient monitoring, infection control, and communication, and they pose a substantial threat to patient safety [5, 6]. Preventing such errors is essential for enhancing healthcare quality. Positive attitudes toward EBP may help reduce the likelihood of errors by promoting scientifically informed and safer clinical decision-making [7, 8].

Understanding how EBP attitudes influence medical error tendencies is crucial not only for individual nurse behaviors but also for healthcare management and patient safety policies [9]. From a theoretical perspective, this relationship can be explained by the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model, which conceptualizes behavior as the result of interactions between individuals’ capabilities, opportunities, and motivational processes. In this context, nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices may be considered a motivational factor influencing clinical decision-making and, consequently, medical error tendencies.

The relationship between EBP and clinical decision quality may help healthcare institutions improve error management systems and standardize patient care processes [10]. However, the mechanisms through which nurses’ EBP attitudes affect their tendency toward medical errors and the factors shaping this relationship remain insufficiently explored in the literature [11]. Particularly in the Turkish context, research directly examining nurses’ attitudes toward EBP and their tendencies to commit medical errors is limited [12]. This gap highlights the significance and potential contribution of the present study. Despite the increasing emphasis on patient safety, limited empirical evidence exists regarding how nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices influence their tendency to commit medical errors. Clarifying this relationship is essential for developing targeted educational strategies, strengthening clinical decision-making, and improving patient safety outcomes. Therefore, examining the direct effect of EBP attitudes on medical error tendency is both clinically and administratively necessary.

Demographic factors such as age, education level, marital status, and professional experience are also believed to influence nurses’ attitudes toward EBP. Previous research has indicated that nurses with postgraduate education tend to have greater EBP attitudes, and younger or single nurses may be more receptive to evidence-based approaches [7, 12]. These findings suggest that educational and professional development processes are key factors in enhancing patient safety and healthcare quality.

The present study aims to investigate the effects of nurses’ attitudes toward EBP on their tendency to commit medical errors while also assessing the role of demographic variables in this relationship. The findings may inform nursing management and policy development by guiding the design of strategies and educational programs that promote evidence-based practices. Moreover, the results may provide insight into how adopting an evidence-based approach in clinical decision-making can effectively reduce medical errors and enhance patient safety. Methods

Aim

The aim of this study was to examine the effects of nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices on their tendency to commit medical errors and to evaluate the role of demographic characteristics in this relationship.

Design

This study was conducted as a descriptive, cross-sectional, and correlational research. The theoretical framework of this study is grounded in the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model, a well-established behavioral framework in health sciences (Michie et al., 2011; Advani et al., 2023). According to the COM-B model, behavior is the result of the interaction between individuals’ capability, opportunity, and motivation.

In the context of this study, nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices (EBP) are conceptualized as a key component of motivation, which influences clinical decision-making behaviors. These behaviors, in turn, are directly related to the tendency to commit medical errors. Positive attitudes toward EBP may enhance motivation to apply evidence-based knowledge, leading to safer clinical practices and reduced error tendencies.

Within this theoretical framework, EBP attitude was defined as the independent variable, medical error tendency as the dependent variable, and demographic characteristics (age, education level, marital status, and professional experience) as control variables. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to test the hypothesized relationships derived from this theoretical model. The COM-B model was selected a priori during the study planning phase and served as a guiding theoretical framework for the development of the study hypothesis, selection of variables, and interpretation of the structural relationships tested in the analysis. It was not retrospectively applied during manuscript preparation.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of approximately 500 nurses working in a university hospital and public hospitals in Kayseri. The study was conducted in Kayseri, a city located in central Türkiye. A census sampling method was used, and all nurses working in these hospitals across all clinical departments were invited to participate. Of these, 500 nurses were targeted, and 412 nurses voluntarily participated and completed the study forms in full. Nurses who did not respond or who submitted incomplete forms were excluded from the analysis. The adequacy of the sample was assessed via power analysis due to the known scope of the study. The mean score of the Evidence-Based Nursing Attitude Questionnaire (EBNAQ) was used to calculate the effect size, which was determined to be 0.62 (a medium-to-large effect size according to Cohen’s classification). Using this effect size, with n = 412 and α = 0.05, the post hoc power of the study was found to be 0.99 (99%) via G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf). This result indicated that the sample size was sufficient to ensure reliable statistical analysis. The study’s flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. Participant eligibility criteria, sampling strategy, and sample size determination were reported in accordance with STROBE guidelines.

Data collection

Data were collected during face-to-face encounters. During these encounters, participants completed the questionnaire themselves using a Google Forms survey, which was accessed via a QR code or sent by email upon request. The researcher facilitated the process but did not influence responses. The questionnaire included both a descriptive information form for demographic data and standardized measurement scales.

Three data collection tools were used to collect the data, which are explained below.

Descriptive Information Form

The form includes 10 questions about nurses’ descriptive information (gender, age, years of employment, institution of employment, etc.) [13–16].

Evidence-Based Nursing Attitude Questionnaire (EBNAQ)

The original version of the scale was presented in the literature by Ruzafa-Martinez et al. in 2011 [17]. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted in 2015 [14]. The scale consists of three subscales and fifteen items. The first subscale is Beliefs and Expectations toward Evidence-Based Nursing ‘BES’. The second subscale is evidence-based practice intention (IS), and the third subscale is emotions related to evidence-based nursing (ES). Since items 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 15 of the scale contain negative expressions, these items were coded by reversing them. The scale has no cutoff point, and a high score indicates that the attitude is high. The Cronbach alpha value of the original scale is 0.93. In this study, the Cronbach alpha value of the scale was determined to be 0.86.

Medical Error Tendency Scale in Nursing (METSN)

This scale was developed in 2009 to measure the tendency of nurses toward medical errors [18]. The scale has five subscales and 49 items in total. The subscales of the scale consist of drug and transfusion practices (DTPS), hospital infections (HIS), patient monitoring and material safety (PMMSS), falls (FS) and communication (CS). The scale is scored as 1: never, 2: very rarely, 3: occasionally, 4: usually, and 5: always. As the score increases, the tendency of nurses toward medical errors decreases. The scale assesses self-reported behaviors associated with medical error tendency rather than the actual frequency of medical errors. Although the scale was developed earlier, it was selected because it is a nursing-specific instrument with established psychometric properties and has continued to be used in recent studies conducted with nurses in Türkiye. The internal consistency reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.95. The Cronbach alpha value of the original scale was 0.93. In this study, the Cronbach alpha value of the scale was 0.97.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and SPSS AMOS 21.0. Descriptive statistics (n, %, mean ± standard deviation) were used to summarize participant characteristics. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to assess normality. Independent-sample t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for comparisons of more than two groups. Post hoc tests were applied where necessary. SEM was conducted to test the hypothesized model. The analysis was guided by theoretical assumptions regarding the relationships between nurses’ evidence-based attitudes and their tendency toward medical errors. Model fit was evaluated via several indices: the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), chi-square/df (CMIN/DF), normed fit index (NFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), and comparative fit index (CFI). A significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted for all analyses. All statistical analyses and model assumptions were reported following STROBE recommendations for observational studies.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kayseri University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 43918). Institutional permission was also obtained from the administrations of the public hospital where the research was conducted. All participants were informed about the study’s purpose and procedures and provided informed consent before participation.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement. All recommended STROBE items, including study design, setting, participants, variables, bias, study size, statistical methods, and limitations, were addressed in the manuscript.

Discussion

Main findings

This study examined the relationship between nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices (EBP) and their tendency to commit medical errors. The findings revealed that more positive attitudes toward EBP were significantly associated with lower medical error tendencies. Additionally, demographic variables, particularly education level, were found to influence both EBP attitudes and medical error tendencies. These findings support the hypothesis of the study and emphasize the importance of evidence-based approaches in enhancing patient safety.

Interpretation within theoretical framework

The findings of this study are consistent with the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model, which highlights the role of motivation in shaping healthcare professionals’ behaviors [7]. In this context, positive attitudes toward evidence-based practice can be interpreted as a motivational driver that promotes safer clinical behaviors and reduces the likelihood of medical errors. Because the COM-B model informed the conceptualization of the study from the outset, the observed relationship between EBP attitudes and medical error tendency was interpreted within a pre-specified behavioral framework rather than through a post hoc theoretical explanation.

Comparison with previous literature

The results of this study are consistent with previous research indicating that evidence-based practices improve patient outcomes and healthcare quality [3, 10]. Evidence-based decision-making enables nurses to rely on scientifically validated knowledge, which reduces uncertainty in clinical practice and minimizes the likelihood of preventable errors [1, 2].

Similarly, previous studies have demonstrated that the integration of evidence-based approaches into clinical practice contributes to improved patient safety and reduced medical error rates [4, 13]. Nurses who adopt evidence-based practices are more likely to follow clinical guidelines and standardized procedures, thereby enhancing the safety and quality of care.

In addition, studies have shown that nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and implementation of evidence-based practice are closely related, and higher EBP competence is associated with better clinical performance [11, 19]. These findings support the present study’s results, indicating that positive attitudes toward EBP play a critical role in reducing medical error tendencies.

Role of demographic and professional factors

The findings of this study also revealed that demographic and professional characteristics influence nurses’ attitudes toward EBP and their tendency to commit medical errors. Nurses with postgraduate education demonstrated more positive EBP attitudes and lower error tendencies. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that higher education levels enhance nurses’ ability to interpret and apply evidence in clinical practice [12, 20].

Furthermore, working conditions were found to influence medical error tendencies. Nurses working continuous daytime shifts exhibited lower error tendencies compared to those working in shifts. This finding aligns with studies indicating that workload, fatigue, and working conditions are important determinants of patient safety and medical error occurrence [21, 22].

Implications for practice

The findings of this study highlight the importance of strengthening nurses’ attitudes toward evidence-based practices to improve patient safety. Healthcare institutions should prioritize the implementation of structured educational programs, continuous professional development opportunities, and mentorship initiatives to enhance EBP competencies [23, 24].

Additionally, organizational support mechanisms such as access to updated clinical guidelines, supportive leadership, and improved working conditions may further contribute to reducing medical error tendencies and improving the quality of care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of structural equation modeling to examine complex relationships between variables and a relatively large sample size. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, the study was conducted in a single city, and the use of self-reported measures may introduce response bias. Additionally, organizational factors such as workload and safety culture were not included in the model and should be considered in future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides empirical evidence that positive attitudes toward evidence-based practices are associated with reduced medical error tendencies among nurses. Enhancing EBP attitudes through education, organizational support, and leadership strategies may play a critical role in improving patient safety and healthcare quality.
