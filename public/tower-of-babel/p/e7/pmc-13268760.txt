Racial and ethnic disparities in GLP-1 prescriptions among veterans with obesity across direct and community care settings
Authors: Rebekah I Stein, Jessica Phelan, Austin B Frakt, Steven D Pizer, Melissa Garrido, E John Orav, Jose F Figueroa, Thomas C Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObesity is an increasing chronic medical condition facing the veteran population. Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1s) have emerged as an optimal treatment for obesity- and related-medical disorders. As the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) continues to expand community-based care purchased under the MISSION Act, it is unknown whether veterans receiving community-based primary care experience comparable access to GLP-1 prescriptions.MethodsIn this national analysis of VHA data, we assessed differences in GLP-1 prescribing among veterans with overweight or obesity in VHA direct care compared with community care.ResultsVeterans with 3 or more primary care visits receiving VHA-direct primary care had 1.2 percentage points (pp) higher adjusted probability of GLP-1 prescriptions (20% higher odds) than veterans receiving mostly community primary care. Black and Asian veterans had 1.8-pp and 2.2-pp lower probabilities of GLP-1 prescriptions (26% and 34% lower odds, respectively) compared with White counterparts across both settings.ConclusionResearchers and policymakers should continue to monitor whether differences in care between the direct and community settings contribute to disparities in GLP-1 access.

Introduction

Obesity and chronic disease are prevalent and increasing among the US veteran population.1,2 In 2014, 37% of veterans were estimated to be overweight, while 41% were estimated to be obese.2,3 Because veterans are known to have a higher prevalence of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and cancer than nonveterans,1 finding ways to effectively treat obesity and related medical conditions in this population is of primary importance for Veterans Health Administration (VHA) leadership and the veteran community.

Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1s) have emerged as highly effective treatments for obesity and related medical conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and liver disease.4-7 Nonetheless, given the high cost of GLP-1 medications, many payers—including Medicare and several state Medicaid programs—do not cover the use of GLP-1s for weight loss in patients without diabetes.6,8-12 The VHA, however, has covered the use of GLP-1s for the purpose of weight loss among overweight/obese veterans since 2021.

The privatization of the VHA has prompted concern among policymakers on whether medical care and prescribing patterns are comparable across VHA-based direct care and community care settings. The Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 authorized veterans to receive care from private-sector providers paid for by the VHA.13 Access to this community care program was expanded by the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Maintaining Internal Systems and Strengthening Integrated Outside Networks (MISSION) Act of 2018, which created the Community Care Network (CCN) of community clinicians from whom veterans could receive care.14 Prescriptions sent in by community providers to VHA pharmacies are covered at the same rate as when sent in by VHA direct care providers.15 While the community care program has made more providers and appointments available to veterans, fragmented care for veterans receiving primary care in both the direct and community settings could lead to lower uptake of GLP-1 medications.16

Whether access to GLP-1 medications for treatment of obesity varies across race and ethnicity and source of primary care is of importance for policymakers. Of particular concern for the shift towards community care, there is growing evidence of racial/ethnic disparities in access to high-quality primary care at the VHA, including racial/ethnic minority veterans experiencing longer wait times for appointments with both community and VHA physicians than White counterparts.17,18 Prior work has found that racial and ethnic minority veterans were less often prescribed GLP-1 medications for the treatment of diabetes and chronic kidney disease than their White counterparts.19-22 Emerging data suggest similar racial disparities in prescription for 1 specific GLP-1 medication (semaglutide) among veterans with obesity receiving care directly from the VHA.23,24 However, evidence is needed on racial disparities in the use of all available GLP-1s by veterans with overweight/obesity, which have not been investigated among veterans across the community care and direct care settings.

This evaluation aims to answer the following questions. First, to what extent are prescribing patterns for GLP-1 medications different between veterans with overweight/obesity receiving primary care directly at the VHA vs through community care providers? And second, do racial/ethnic minority veterans with overweight/obesity have decreased access to GLP-1s compared with White veterans with obesity?

Data and methods

Data

Our primary datasets were the electronic medical records (EMRs) and the Integrated Veterans Care Consolidated Data Set from the VHA's Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) for direct and community care, respectively. Inpatient and outpatient data from both direct and community care as well as height and weight EMR measurements exclusive to VHA direct care were collected from the CDW for the years 2022 and 2023. The presence/absence of each of the 38 Elixhauser comorbidities as well as bariatric surgery in the years 2022 and 2023 were additionally extracted from these sources. We used 2023 and 2024 CDW prescription records for fills sent to VHA pharmacies to identify veterans who received at least 1 GLP-1 prescription within the given study year.

Study population

This study leverages 2 separate qualification periods (2022 and 2023) and study periods (2023 and 2024) pooled together for final analysis. This design was chosen due to the intermittent nature of GLP-1 prescribing during this period, including some GLP-1 shortages during early 2023. To be included in analysis for a given study year, veterans must have been obese or overweight in the prior qualification period, as determined by either the presence of an International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), code associated with overweight or obesity on any claim or, for veterans with any direct care appointments in which height/weight records were available, a calculated body mass index (BMI) of 27 kg/m2 or above. In this article, when we refer to the term “obese,” this includes overweight as well.

Our main exposure was receiving primary care through community care. We define care purchased by the VHA and delivered by non-VHA providers as “community care” and health care services delivered directly at VHA facilities by VHA providers are referred to as “direct care.” Following prior methodology, we identified direct and community care primary care visits using Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System/Current Procedural Terminology (HCPCS/CPT) codes and clinician taxonomy codes in outpatient claims in both settings, as well as Stop Codes (VHA-specific identifiers of where a visit takes place) for direct care claims and Place of Service Codes for community care claims.18

Veterans’ primary care settings were determined during the 2 qualification periods separately based on where more than 50% of the veteran's primary care visits took place during that year. We created a third group of veterans with equal numbers of primary care visits in each setting that year. To account for potential variation in the likelihood of GLP-1 prescription by a veteran's volume of primary care visits, we stratified analysis into 3 groups: veterans with 1 total visit, 2 total visits, and 3 or more total visits across both community and direct care settings in the qualification period (for the distribution of primary care visit count in our sample, see Appendix Table S1) (to access the Appendix, click on the details tab of the article online). Only veterans who had at least one primary care visit during their qualification period were included in the sample for the subsequent study period.

Our primary outcome was whether a veteran with obesity received a GLP-1 prescription during the given study year, and our secondary analysis was whether receipt of GLP-1 prescriptions varied by race and ethnicity. Race/ethnicity was defined by veterans’ race/ethnicity as recorded by the VHA Office for Health Equity, which compiles several data sources across the VA to create the most complete, accurate race/ethnicity variable.25 The included racial categories are American Indian/Alaskan Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian/other Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic White, more than 1 race, and unknown race/ethnicity. Due to the study design, analysis was at the veteran-year level. Veterans appear twice in the dataset if they qualified for both study years. We use the terms veteran-years and veterans interchangeably.

Statistical analysis

We first compared characteristics of veteran-years in the direct care, equal, and community care primary care setting groups. Comparisons included age, gender, race, year of potential fill, number of primary care provider (PCP) appointments during the qualification period, and VHA priority group—an indicator of what degree of cost-sharing veterans are responsible for when receiving VHA health care in either setting. We calculated pairwise standardized mean differences between each of the 3 groups. In addition, we compared the number/proportion of veteran-years in which a veteran had a diagnosis of diabetes during the qualification period, as prior research has demonstrated significantly higher rates of GLP-1 prescriptions among individuals with diabetes and obesity than obesity alone.26 We also compared the number/proportion of veteran-years in which a veteran had undergone bariatric surgery in the qualification period due to differing clinical indications (ie, weight regain) for veterans prescribed GLP-1 prescriptions in the 1 to 2 years following bariatric surgery compared with veterans prescribed GLP-1 primarily for obesity without undergoing metabolic bariatric surgery.27

We then examined the probability of GLP-1 prescription and differences by primary care setting and race/ethnicity. We first compared the unadjusted rates of GLP-1 prescriptions between primary care settings and by race/ethnicity, considering veterans with 1, 2, and 3 or more primary care visits separately.

Finally, we conducted binary logistic regression analyses to identify the association between veterans’ primary care setting or race/ethnicity and likelihood of receiving a GLP-1 prescription. Our primary model was among veterans with 3 or more primary care visits, as this constituted the majority of the sample. The regression outcome was whether a veteran received a GLP-1 prescription during the study year. The main explanatory variables were veterans’ primary care setting (direct care and equal vs community care) as well as race/ethnicity, and additional covariates included study year, gender, age group, VHA priority group, closest VHA primary care site (to serve as a geographic control), Elixhauser comorbidities (including diabetes), and bariatric surgery during the qualification period. We additionally included a categorical covariate to adjust for primary care visit volume (3, 4, 5, and 6+ visits). For all models, we calculated robust SEs clustered by individual veterans to adjust for repeated observations. Results include adjusted predicted marginal probabilities (subsequently referred to just as “probabilities”) as well as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) of GLP-1 prescriptions by primary care setting and race/ethnicity.

We conducted several additional secondary and sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the primary analysis among veterans with 1 and 2 primary care visits during the qualification period separately. All model specifications remained the same except for the exclusion of a covariate to adjust for number of primary care visits. To determine whether racial and ethnic disparities in GLP-1 prescriptions differed between VHA direct and community care, we conducted additional models with added interaction terms between race and primary care setting. Finally, to assess whether our primary findings on differential rates of GLP-1 prescribing by primary care setting hold among veterans who are most likely receiving GLP-1s for the purpose of weight loss and not diabetes management, we conducted sensitivity analyses among the subset of our sample with a diagnosis of overweight or obesity alone without a diagnosis of diabetes. This evaluation was conducted as a quality-improvement activity for VHA and was deemed by the R&D Committee at the VA Boston Healthcare System not to be research; therefore, it was not subject to institutional review board review.

Limitations

Our study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, we were unable to identify GLP-1 prescriptions filled through non-VHA pharmacies. However, expert opinion from the VHA Office of Integrated Veteran Care has suggested that fills of GLP-1s at non-VHA pharmacies would be very limited, as the VHA only covers emergency medication fills at non-VHA pharmacies and non-VHA coverage of GLP-1s for obesity is relatively rare.15 While fills through private-pay or secondary insurance (Medicare/Medicaid or employer-sponsored insurance) are possible, analysis (data not shown) of observed GLP-1 fills through Medicare among our veteran cohort showed no difference between the community and direct care groups, which may extrapolate to other secondary insurance coverage as well. Second, we divided veterans into primary care, equal, and direct care groups by observed primary care utilization. There is a potential for GLP-1 prescriptions to come from non-PCPs. However, in a sensitivity analysis (results not shown) evaluating multiple provider types, we found limited prescribing by non-primary care physician providers of GLP-1s in this population. Endocrinologists, who may manage care coordination and treatment for veterans with obesity/diabetes, prescribed only 5% of the observed GLP-1 fills in our sample. Third, Elixhauser comorbidities—used as covariates in our models as well as for excluding veteran-years with a diagnosis of diabetes from sensitivity analyses—are defined by ICD-10 codes. Undercoding is known to be present in the VHA, as payment does not rely on coding, and as a result, comorbidities may be undercounted and our sensitivity analyses may include some veterans with diabetes who do not have any ICD-10 code indicating this condition.28,29 Fourth, as with any descriptive analyses, unobserved confounding differences between groups may not be adjusted for in our models, and we are unable to determine causal relationships between primary care setting and GLP-1 prescription rates. Finally, some racial/ethnic groups, such as Asians, may have different BMI thresholds for being considered clinically overweight or obese.30 We adopted standard ICD-10 definitions for overweight or obesity in our study to allow for comparison, but clinically appropriate indications for GLP-1 may vary across different patient groups.

Results

In total, 3 423 933 (49.7%) veterans qualified during 2022 for the study period in 2023 and 3 461 183 (50.3%) qualified during 2023 for the study period in 2024. Compared with veterans in the community care group, the direct care group was more often Black (18.3% vs 11.8%), male (90.9% vs 87.3.%), older (53.0% vs 46.8% ≥65 years), had fewer total primary care appointments (47.5% vs 79.1% 3+ visits), and in priority groups associated with greater cost sharing (50.9% vs 43.4% priority 2 through 8) (Table 1). Additionally, fewer veterans in the direct care setting had a diagnosis of diabetes in the prior year than in community care (28.4% vs 33.4%). Veterans with an equal number of primary care appointments in both settings had more similar characteristics to the community care group than the direct care group. Rates of bariatric surgery were extremely low (<0.5%) across primary care settings.

GLP-1 prescriptions in VHA direct care vs community care

In total, across PCP settings, 421 415 (6.5%) veteran-years had a GLP-1 fill during the 2 study periods combined (Table 1). Among veterans with just 1 visit during the qualification period, the unadjusted rate of GLP-1 prescriptions in the direct care group was 2.91% compared with 4.01% in the community care group (Figure 1). Among veterans with 2 PCP visits during the qualification period, the rate of GLP-1 prescriptions was 5.10% in the direct care group compared with only 4.44% in the community care group and 4.12% in the group of veterans who had an equal number of PCP visits in each setting. Among veterans with 3 or more PCP visits during the qualification period, 8.54% of veterans in the direct care group were prescribed a GLP-1, 7.98% of veterans in the community care group were prescribed a GLP-1, and 8.79% of veterans with equal numbers of PCP visits in both settings were prescribed a GLP-1.

In our primary analysis of veterans with 3 or more primary care visits, a greater likelihood of GLP-1 prescribing was still present in the direct care group after adjustment. Veterans in the direct care group had 1.18-percentage points (-pp) higher predicted probability (P < .001; OR: 1.20, P < .001) of GLP-1 prescription than those in the community care group (Figure 3, Appendix Tables S3 and 4, Appendix Figure S1). Veterans with an equal number of visits in both settings had just 0.73-pp higher predicted probability (P < .001; OR: 1.13, P < 0.001) of GLP-1 prescription than the community care group.

Results differed somewhat in secondary analyses among veterans with 1 and 2 primary care visits (Appendix Tables S3 and 4, Appendix Figure S1). Among veterans with 2 visits, effect size was comparable for the direct care group, although no differences were observed between veterans with equal numbers of visits across settings and the community care group. Surprisingly, for veterans with just 1 primary care visit, those receiving care at the VHA directly were less likely than those receiving primary care in the community to be prescribed a GLP-1 (0.49-pp lower predicted probability, P < .001; OR: 0.84, P < .001).

Racial and ethnic disparities in GLP-1 prescriptions

In primary unadjusted analysis of veterans with at least 3 primary care visits, racial and ethnic minority veterans experienced lower rates of GLP-1 prescriptions than White veterans. Notably, 8.7% of White veterans received a GLP-1 prescription compared with only 7.5% of Asian veterans, 8.1% of Hispanic veterans, and 8.4% of Black veterans (Figure 3). Results were qualitatively similar among veterans with 2 primary care visits but varied among veterans with just 1 primary care visit (Appendix Table S5).

In the primary adjusted analysis of veterans with at least 3 primary care visits, racial/ethnic disparities in GLP-1 prescriptions remained present (Figure 2, Appendix Tables S3 and 4, Appendix Figure S1). Compared with White veterans, Asian and Black veterans experienced 2.23-pp and 1.80-pp lower predicted probabilities (ORs: 0.70 and 0.76) of GLP-1 prescription, respectively (all P <.001). Although with smaller effects, American Indian/Alaskan Native and Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander veterans also experienced lower likelihoods of receiving GLP-1 prescriptions than White veterans (0.78-pp [P < .001] and 0.55-pp [P = .005] lower predicted probability; ORs: 0.89 [P < .001] and 0.92 [P = .006]).

In secondary analyses among veterans with fewer than 3 primary care visits, results were qualitatively similar, with some smaller effect sizes (Appendix Tables S3 and 4, Appendix Figure S1). In particular, in some models, significance was no longer present for probability/odds of GLP-1 prescription for American Indian/Alaskan Native and Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander veterans compared with White veterans. In models with added interaction terms between race/ethnicity and primary care setting, we observed no significant differences in racial/ethnic disparities in GLP-1 prescribing between primary care settings across veterans with 1, 2, and 3 or more primary care visits (Appendix Table S6).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses among veterans with obesity without a diagnosis of diabetes yielded results consistent with those from the main analysis in directionality and significance (Appendix Figure S2). Even greater differences in odds of GLP-1 prescriptions between veterans receiving a majority of primary care at the VHA directly vs in the community were present.

Discussion

In our national study, we found that veterans receiving a majority of primary care at the VHA directly often have a higher likelihood of being prescribed GLP-1s than those receiving primary care in the community. Among veterans with 3 or more primary care visits, those in the direct care group had 1.2-pp higher predicted probabilities and 20% higher adjusted odds of GLP-1 prescriptions than the community care group.

Across primary care settings, significant racial disparities existed. Black and Asian veterans with at least 3 primary care visits had 1.8-pp and 2.2-pp lower predicted probability (24% and 30% lower adjusted odds) of receiving GLP-1 prescriptions, respectively, than White counterparts, and disparities persisted among veterans with fewer primary care visits. Some groups of American Indian/Alaskan Native and Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander veterans were also less likely to receive GLP-1 prescriptions than White veterans. While Hispanic veterans had lower unadjusted rates of GLP-1 prescriptions than White counterparts; differences were no longer observed after adjustment. Racial disparities did not differ significantly between the community and direct care settings.

Although our observational study focused on GLP-1 prescription patterns and did not determine why differences by primary care setting emerged, our finding that veterans with 2 or more primary care appointments who received a majority of this care at the VHA directly were more likely to be prescribed GLP-1s than those receiving community primary care may be explainable be several factors. First, underlying differences between veterans in the community care and direct care groups that we were unable to adjust for, such as patient preference for using GLP-1s, may well cause differential use of GLP-1s between these 2 groups.

More systemically, physicians practicing at the VHA may be more aware of the VHA formulary coverage for GLP-1s and the processes for sending prescriptions to VHA pharmacies than community physicians.31 Similarly, community clinicians have reported difficulty and substantial burden associated with paperwork required for submitting prescriptions to VHA pharmacies, which may disincentivize community clinicians from prescribing medications for the purpose of weight loss to veterans.31,32 Additionally, prior research has found that providers with a patient mix subject to several different formularies may experience spillover effects from the most restrictive formulary, resulting in more limited prescribing even to patients with access to broader formularies.33 Thus, unlike community physicians, VHA prescribers may be less likely to be influenced by these disincentives to prescribe GLP-1 medications.

Conversely, we found that, among veterans with just 1 primary care appointment in the prior year, those who received this care in the community were more likely to be prescribed GLP-1s than those who had this appointment directly at the VHA. This finding suggests that the increased prescribing of GLP-1s among VHA direct care veterans seems to accrue only for patients who have higher primary care utilization.

Similarly to prior research conducted in the VHA and other health care delivery systems in the United States, we found significant evidence that racial/ethnic minorities were less likely than White counterparts to be prescribed GLP-1s for weight loss in both community and direct care settings.23,24,34,35 Such racial disparities outside the VHA have often been attributed to differential coverage of GLP-1s among commercial and public payers, such as Medicare and Medicaid, and the considerable costs of GLP-1s for uninsured individuals and those with high cost-sharing.34-36 In the VHA, in which GLP-1s have been approved for treating obesity since 2021 with minimal prescription copayments across the United States, our findings of racial and ethnic disparities in both VHA direct and community care suggest that other mechanisms beyond insurance coverage may be contributing to disparities in GLP-1 utilization more broadly.

Recent research has found significantly longer wait times for community primary care and specialist visits in both the community and direct care settings among racial minority veterans.17,18 Wait times thus present 1 possible mechanism for disparities in obesity treatment between White and racial/ethnic minority veterans. Additionally, implicit biases among clinicians have been documented to affect care decision-making, and compared with White patients, racial/ethnic minority patients are less likely to receive diagnostic testing and treatment than White counterparts with the same presentation.37 Racial/ethnic minority veterans may also have different medical decision-making than White veterans, including increased hesitancy to try newer medications, due to greater distrust in the health care system.38,39

Our findings lead to several important policy and research implications. First, policymakers may consider education initiatives among community care network physicians regarding the VHA's GLP-1 coverage for obesity and the process for submitting prescriptions to VHA pharmacies as well as reducing administrative burden on community physicians prescribing to VHA pharmacies to ensure appropriate GLP-1 access for veterans receiving primary care in the community. Additionally, research to elucidate whether observed differences in GLP-1 prescribing rates by primary care setting are due, in part, to care quality differences between direct and community care should be prioritized. Further research identifying patient and provider characteristics and practices both within the VHA and in other health care systems in the United States driving racial and ethnic disparities in obesity care will be important for advancing health equity across direct and community care settings.

Conclusion

Among veterans with obesity and multiple primary care visits per year, veterans receiving primary care directly from the VHA were more likely to be prescribed GLP-1 medications than those receiving community primary care. Black and Asian veterans were less likely than White veterans to be prescribed GLP-1 medications across both care settings. For the management of veterans with obesity, our findings suggest that veterans may experience lower barriers to accessing GLP-1 treatment through VHA direct care than community care.
