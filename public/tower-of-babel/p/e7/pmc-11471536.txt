Impact of overweight and obesity on radiation dose and outcome in patients undergoing pulmonary vein isolation by cryoballoon and pulsed field ablation
Authors: Christiane Jungen, Manuel Rattka, Jan Bohnen, Evangelos Mavrakis, Dimitra Vlachopoulou, Sebastian Dorna, Isabel Rudolph, Christina Kohn, Dobromir Dobrev, Tienush Rassaf, Shibu Mathew
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary vein isolation (PVI) using pulsed field ablation (PFA) or cryoballoon ablation (CBA) are commonly used single-shot techniques for the treatment of patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). The number of overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m2) and obese (BMI>30 kg/m2) patients undergoing PVI is increasing, but data on this patient population is limited.MethodsConsecutive AF patients with a BMI ≥25 kg/m2 undergoing PFA- or CBA-PVI were included in this retrospective analysis. Baseline characteristics, procedural parameters and 1-year AF-freedom were retrospectively analyzed and compared for both ablation modalities.ResultsOf 115 patients (66 % men, 64 years [IQR: 58–71 years], 57 % overweight and 43 % obese) PFA- was performed in 68 % and CBA-PVI in 32 %. Contrast-dye volume (PFA: 80 ml [IQR: 60 − 117 ml] vs. CBA: 130 ml [IQR: 95 − 200 ml], P=0.001) and radiation exposure (PFA: 2196 cGy·cm2 [IQR: 1398 − 2973 cGy·cm2] vs. CBA: 3239 cGy·cm2 [IQR: 1288 − 5062 cGy·cm2], P=0.009) was lower in patients undergoing PFA-PVI. Logistic regression analysis identified obesity (OR: 5.58, 95 % CI: 1.63–19.06; P=0.006) and CBA-PVI (OR: 12.93, 95 % CI: 3.51–47.68; P<0.001) to be associated with increased radiation exposure. Both techniques were comparably safe (PFA: 4 % vs. CBA: 0 %; P=0.3). The median follow-up time was 145 days [IQR: 103 − 294 days]. AF-freedom after 1-year was similar in overweight (82 %) and obese patients (67 %) (HR: 0.61; 95 % CI: 0.29–1.28; P=0.19) as well as in PFA- and CBA-PVI patients (76 % vs. 76 %, HR: 1.37; 95 % CI: 0.63–2.99; P=0.42).ConclusionOverweight and obese patients undergoing PFA-PVI had lower contrast-dye volume compared to CBA-PVI. Obesity was associated with increased radiation exposure. Both techniques were comparably safe. The 1-year AF-freedom was similar in overweight and obese patients.Keywords: Pulmonary vein isolation, Pulsed field ablation, Obesity, Catheter ablation, Atrial fibrillation

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) has become the cornerstone therapy in atrial fibrillation (AF) treatment [1]. Overweight (body mass index (BMI) 25–30 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI>30 kg/m2) are known risk factors for AF and are associated with an increased incidence and prevalence of AF [2], [3], [4]. Thus, many patients with an indication for PVI are overweight or obese. With increasing BMI, patients experience more AF recurrences following PVI and have higher radiation exposure during the intervention compared to patients with normal weight [5], [6]. Previous studies did not show increased complication rates in the presence of obesity [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], although some reported higher procedure-associated complication rates in morbidly obese patients with a BMI>40 kg/m2 [6], [12]. Thus further refinement of PVI is required in this patient population in order to reduce radiation exposure and increase AF-free survival.

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) for PVI is a promising single-shot non-thermal, tissue selective treatment approach. A recent study has shown that efficacy and safety of PFA and cryoballoon ablation (CBA) for PVI are comparable [13]. Most studies focus on AF-recurrences after PVI without stratification of the patients according to their BMI [14]. For PFA there are no data describing procedural parameters such as radiation dose or efficacy in relation to body weight [15], [16]. Thus, the aim of this study was to compare procedural parameters and 1-year AF-recurrence in the growing cohort of overweight and obese AF patients undergoing PFA- or CBA-PVI.

Methods

This retrospective study was performed based on our institutional registry database. Overweight and obese patients with symptomatic AF (paroxysmal AF and persistent AF), who underwent PFA- or CBA-PVI between July 2020 and March 2023 at the Department of Cardiology and Vascular Medicine at the West German Heart and Vascular Center, University Hospital Essen, Germany were analyzed. The AF type was categorized as paroxysmal AF (lasting < 1 week or episodes that were cardioverted within 7 days) and persistent AF (episodes lasting > 1 week and episodes that were terminated by cardioversion, either with drugs or by direct current cardioversion after > 7 days) [1]. Patients who underwent ablation beyond PVI, except for cavotricuspid isthmus ablation (CTI), were excluded. All patients were categorized into either overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m2) or obese (BMI>30 kg/m2) depending on their BMI at the time of procedure. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before the procedure. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee (registration number: 23–11279-BO).

Preprocedural management and ablation procedure

Preprocedural management was performed as described before [17], [18]. In brief, transesophageal echocardiography was performed in all patients prior to PVI to rule out left atrial thrombus. Patients treated with non-VKA oral anticoagulants were advised to stop the intake 24 h prior to the ablation procedure. Vitamin K antagonists were administered uninterruptedly to a target INR of 2.0 – 2.5 at the time of procedure. All procedures were performed under heparin administration during the procedure with the goal of an activated clotting time of > 300 ms.

In all patients a diagnostic decapolar catheter was placed in the coronary sinus, and an additional quadripolar catheter in the right ventricle or right atrium for phrenic nerve (PN) capture if required. A single trans-septal puncture (TSP) (BRK-1 transseptal needle, 8.5F SL1 transseptal sheath; SJM Abbott, Illinois, United States) followed by PV angiograms were performed.

In patients undergoing PFA the transseptal sheath was exchanged for the FaradriveTM sheath using a guidewire. Subsequently the FarawaveTM ablation catheter was introduced to the left atrium (FarawaveTM, Farapulse Inc., California, United States). The catheter was navigated with a guidewire to achieve a stable position at the PV ostia using either a flower or basket configuration. PVs were treated in a clockwise direction with energy applications being delivered as a biphasic waveform unsynchronized to cardiac rhythm using bipolar energy in a set of microsecond-scale biphasic pulses of 1800–2000 Volts using five pulse applications. Each PV was treated with 8 energy applications, 4 in flower and 4 in basket configurations, with repositioning and/or rotation of the catheter every two applications to ensure circumferential PV ostial and antral coverage. The procedural endpoint was confirmed by bidirectional conduction block after PFA applications.

The cryoballoon PVI was performed using a 28-mm cryoballoon catheter (POLARxTM, Boston Scientific, Massachusetts, United States) under fluoroscopic guidance. Local PV occlusion was assessed by contrast injection through the distal cryoballoon lumen. A freeze with a targeted duration of 180 s was delivered to each PV. While freezing at the septal PVs, the right PN was constantly paced from the superior caval vein and the PN function was monitored by the tactile feedback of diaphragmatic contraction. In case of reduction of PN function, energy delivery was immediately terminated. Chronic PN palsies (PNPs) were defined as PN injury (PNI) not resuming before discharge (<48 h). After PVI, entrance and exit block was confirmed. Additionally, pre- and post-ablation three-dimensional ultra-high-density mapping was used at the discretion of the operator.

Procedural parameters, such as fluoroscopy time, radiation dose and contrast dye volume were recorded for each procedure. The German Federal Office for Radiation Protection recommends a maximum radiation dose of 4900 cGy·cm2 as diagnostic reference level for complex fluoroscopic interventions such as the implantation of a cardiac resynchronization therapy device, however there no reference levels for ablation procedures [19].

Postprocedural management and clinical follow-up

Hemostasis was reached via a suture, followed by 6 h of bed rest. Echocardiography was performed in every patient immediately after the procedure and before hospital discharge to rule out pericardial effusion or pericardial tamponade. All patients underwent a neurological examination directly after the procedure to detect potential focal neural deficits.

Oral anticoagulants were reintroduced in the evening after the procedure in the absence of bleeding complications. Antiarrhythmic drug treatment was interrupted after ablation. During hospitalization a 12-lead ECG and 24 h Holter ECG were obtained to exclude early arrhythmia recurrence. Outpatient visits included clinical assessment, transthoracic echocardiography, and at least a 24 h Holter, scheduled 1, 3, and 6 months after the procedure. Patient information from phone contact, and arrhythmia records from other hospitals and referring physicians were collected. Any documented sustained atrial fibrillation on 12-lead ECG or any AF episode of 30 s or longer on Holter ECG were counted as AF recurrence. The follow-up period started at the day after ablation and a blanking period of 3 months was taken into account.

The primary endpoint was defined as documented recurrence of AF or the need for repeat ablation during the 3-month blanking period. Secondary endpoints were defined as major procedure related adverse events. These have been defined as death or complications requiring intervention for treatment, or prolonging hospitalization for more than 48 h, as recommended in the consensus statement for catheter ablation of AF [20].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative values were presented as mean ± SD or median and interquartile range (25th-75th IQR), as appropriate with regard to normal distribution. For comparison, the student‘s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test were used, as appropriate. Normally distributed data were tested for significance using the Fisher-exact test. For identification of independent predictors of increased radiation dose, baseline characteristics were analyzed by univariate logistic regression. The effects of parameters with a P≤0.1 were further tested by multivariate logistic regression. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to assess the time to AF recurrence and groups were compared using the Cox proportional hazard model. All tests were performed with a 2-sided significance level of 5 %. Statistical calculations were performed using the software SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS, Inc, an IBM Company, Chicago, IL).

Results

Patient population and baseline characteristics

Among the 115 patients male sex was more prevalent (PFA 62 %, CBA 73 %, P=0.21). Median age was 64 years [IQR: 28–71 years] in patients undergoing PFA- and 65 years [IQR: 58–74 years] in those receiving CBA-PVI (P=0.12, Table 1). AF was classified as paroxysmal in 49 % of PFA- and in 51 % of CBA patients (P=0.98). Other baseline characteristics did only differ in presence of arterial hypertension (PFA 80 %, CBA 61 %, P=0.047), as summarized in Table 1.

Procedural data and safety

Procedural data are summarized in Table 2, Table 3. Patients undergoing PFA-PVI had lower radiation exposure than patients undergoing CBA-PVI (PFA: 2196 cGy·cm2 [IQR: 1398 − 2973 cGy·cm2] vs. CBA: 3239 cGy·cm2 [IQR: 1288 − 5062], P=0.009). Additionally, less contrast dye volume had to be applied in PFA- compared to CBA-PVI (PFA: 80 ml [IQR: 60 − 117 ml] vs. CBA: 130 ml [IQR: 95 − 200 ml], P=0.001). The fluoroscopy time, as well as the rate of additional CTI ablations did not differ between groups (Table 2).

Periprocedural adverse events did not differ between groups (PFA: 4 % vs. CBA: 0 %; P=0.3); in PFA-PVI two pericardial tamponades, which were both treated with pericardiocentesis without surgery, and one infected groin haematoma, that prolonged the hospital stay over 48 h, occurred. No periprocedural deaths and no switch to invasive ventilation or general anaesthesia were recorded.

AF recurrence after 1-year

The median follow-up time was 145 days [IQR: 103 − 294 days] for all patients, with a median of 136 days [IQR: 103 − 207 days] in PFA- and 185 days [IQR: 121 − 456 days] in CBA-PVI.

Using a blanking period of twelve-weeks, 76 % of all patients were free from AF recurrences after 1-year. There was no difference in 1-year AF recurrence rate regarding the mode of ablation (PFA: 76 % vs. CBA: 76 %, HR: 1.37; 95 % CI: 0.63–2.99; P=0.42, Fig. 1). For overweight (82 %) and obese patients (67 %) the 1-year AF-freedom was similar (HR: 0.61; 95 % CI: 0.29–1.28; P=0.19). Freedom from AF after 1-year was better in patients with paroxysmal AF (84 %) compared to persistent AF (67 %) (HR: 2.45; 95 % CI: 1.1–5.4; P=0.03). Overall results did not change using an eight-week blanking period: 75 % (29 of 115) patients were free from AF recurrence after 1 year. Neither ablation mode (PFA: 74 % vs. CBA: 76 %, HR: 1.44; 95 % CI: 0.66–3.11; P=0.35) nor BMI (overweight: 82 %, obese: 65 %, HR: 0.61; 95 % CI: 0.29–1.27; P=0.19) showed a difference in 1-year AF recurrence. In patients with paroxysmal AF (84 %), freedom from AF after 1-year was better compared to persistent AF (66 %) (HR: 2.59; 95 % CI: 1.18–5.69; P=0.02) after an eight-week blanking period.

Discussion

This study compared procedural parameters and 1-year freedom from AF of overweight and obese patients undergoing PFA- or CBA-based PVI. The main findings are as follows: 1) Contrast dye volume was lower in PFA-PVI patients, 2) obesity was identified to be associated with increased radiation exposure, 3) both techniques were comparably safe and 4) the 1-year AF-freedom was similar in overweight and obese patients.

Procedural parameters

In this cohort of overweight and obese patients the evaluation of procedural parameters revealed that the amount of contrast dye was higher in patients undergoing CBA-PVI compared to PFA-PVI. This finding is in line with a study comparing CBA-PVI with radiofrequency-PVI and is rather expected, because during CBA-PVI contrast dye is essential to assess the grade of pulmonary vein occlusion by the cryoballoon [21]. It is well known that high doses of contrast medium potentially impair renal function [22]. As there are numerous links between obesity and chronic kidney disease, the lower amount of contrast dye applied during PFA-PVI might be beneficial in obese patients [23]. Additionally, it has been shown that PFA-PVI can significantly increase the risk of acute kidney injury, due to energy delivery in the blood stream resulting in haemolysis and even haemoglobinuria [24], [25].

Interestingly, our study found that an increased radiation dose was only present in CBA-PVI patients, which was not the case for those undergoing PFA-PVI (Table 2). This is likely due to the required monitoring of PV occlusion and phrenic nerve capture which is needed in patients undergoing CBA-PVI, but not in PFA-PVI. Thus, cine imaging is used more frequently in CBA, which might explain the similar fluoroscopy times with increased radiation dose [26]. In contrast, a recent study reported similar radiation doses in patients undergoing CBA- vs. PFA-PVI [27]. Further, current fluoroscopy times are nowadays shorter and have also been reported to be shorter for CBA-PVI compared to PFA-PVI in a recent publication [28]. However, these studies only reported the mean BMI so that their results cannot be compared to the overweight and obese patient cohort of this study.

In this study, radiation dose was increased in obese compared to overweight CBA-PVI patients. An increased BMI has been associated with a higher radiation exposure in patients undergoing non-single-shot device procedures [29]. It is well accepted that in obese patients a higher radiation dose is necessary to generate adequate images, which is especially important during transseptal puncture [30]. In studies employing CBA-PVI only, an increase in radiation dose was noted in overweight and obese patients [8], [21], [26], [31]. However, in PFA-PVI patients only the mean BMI has been reported [15], [16], [32], so to the best of our knowledge our study is the first to assess radiation dose and other procedural parameters of patients undergoing PFA-PVI in relation to BMI.

Our data also show that obesity and CBA-PVI are independent predictors of increased radiation exposure. Of course, it is relevant to reduce the radiation dose received by patients undergoing medical procedures. However, higher radiation exposure while treating obese patients is also harmful for the investigator, as exposure to ionizing radiation has been associated with an increased risk of developing cataracts and left-hemisphere brain tumours in healthcare professionals [33], [34]. Since in overweight and obese patients the operator’s exposure is particularly high due to the increased scattering of radiation, it appears reasonable to develop strategies to reduce radiation in this population [35].

Noteworthy, both ablation techniques were comparably safe in obese and overweight patients. The two pericardial tamponades might be attributable to an initial learning curve and match the early literature for PFA-PVI [36]. A recent study reported an incidence for pericardial tamponade of 1.6 %, however neither the ablation technique nor the body weight has been taken into account [37].

AF recurrence rates 1-year after PVI

This study found no difference regarding 1-year AF-recurrence rates between CBA- and PFA-PVI (76 % vs. 76 %) in this selected cohort of obese and overweight patients. Results stayed similar for a twelve-week as well as an eight-week blanking period. However, these findings are only hypothesis-generating as there has been no continuous arrhythmia monitoring. Similarly, a recent study by Schipper et al. found freedom from any atrial arrhythmia in 72 % of CBA- and 74 % of PFA-PVI patients after 273 ± 129 days [28]. Another study reports a 1-year freedom from AF in 78 % of CBA- and 75 % of PFA-PVI patients [27].

In this study, AF-freedom after 1-year was higher in patients with paroxysmal compared to persistent AF (85 % vs. 67 %) after a blanking period of twelve weeks. These findings are in line with recent literature, which report higher 1-year freedom from AF recurrences in paroxysmal (CBA: 83 %, PFA: 80 %) compared to persistent AF (CBA: 71 %, PFA: 67 %) [13] and a recent multi-center study analyzing results from PFA-PVI reporting a 1-year arrhythmia-free survival of 80 % for paroxysmal AF and 66 % for persistent AF [38].

We found a similar 1-year AF-freedom in overweight as well as obese patients independent of the ablation mode and independent of a blanking period of eight- or twelve-weeks. Up to now obesity has been associated with an increased AF recurrence, however these findings all result from AF ablation studies using radiofrequency [39], [40]. For overweight and obese patients undergoing CBA-PVI, literature reports efficacy outcomes after 1-year similar to the here presented results (overweight: 72 % and 79 %, obesity: 78 % and 74 %) [8], [9].

Our results imply that the choice of ablation technique (PFA or CBA) does not appear to impact the maintenance of sinus rhythm in a cohort of overweight and obese patients. However, PFA was associated with lower radiation exposure and contrast dye volume, emphasizing the favourable characteristics of the novel ablation modality in this patient subpopulation. Since the number of overweight and obese patients undergoing PVI will increase, outcome studies related to BMI are clearly warranted.

Limitations

This is a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small numbers of patients, so that some trends may not have reached statistical significance. Fluoroscopy times in this study were higher as the currently reported ones, which might be attributable to the distinct patient population, a learning curve and to the often used three-dimensional ultra-high-density mapping pre- and post-ablation. Follow-up data were obtained using a 24 h-Holter, medical history and outpatient visits including a 12-lead ECG, which do not capture asymptomatic AF. This study focused on single-shot techniques only, no additional non-PVI applications such as linear ablation or epicardial PVI have been performed [41]. As no continuous rhythm monitoring device was used, findings on 1-year AF-freedom are only hypothesis-generating and overestimation of 1-year success rate is possible.

Conclusion

Overweight and obese patients undergoing PFA-PVI had lower contrast dye volume compared to CBA-PVI. Obesity was associated with increased radiation exposure. Both techniques were comparably safe. The 1-year AF-freedom was similar in overweight and obese patients.
