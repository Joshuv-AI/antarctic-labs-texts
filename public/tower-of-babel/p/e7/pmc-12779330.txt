Haploinsufficiency of brain‐specific kinase BRSK1 causes epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorders
Authors: Qi Zhang, Hiroshi Yamanaka, Ping Li, Yingfeng Li, Tian Luo, Miao Xia, Ting Liu, Xueyan Liu, Qing Lu, Bin Yang, Fan He, Kaixian Du, Yousheng Shu, Bo Peng, Yong‐hui Jiang, Yi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe BRSK1 gene encodes brain‐specific serine/threonine kinase 1 (also known as SAD‐B kinase), which is almost exclusively expressed in the brain and plays critical roles in neuronal polarization, neurotransmitter release, mitochondrial dynamics, and neuronal maturation. This study aimed to investigate the pathogenicity of BRSK1 variants in epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorders.MethodsTrio‐based exome sequencing was performed in 394 probands with epilepsy. Expression analyses and functional validation were conducted using a newly generated Brsk1 exon 4–7 knockout mouse line (Brsk1 Δ4–7), with subsequent behavioral, electrophysiological, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic assessments.ResultsSix novel BRSK1 variants were identified in seven probands, including four single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and two indels; five were de novo, one inherited, and one recurrent. Frameshift or nonsense variants led to complete loss of detectable BRSK1 protein, whereas one missense variant reduced protein levels. Both heterozygous and homozygous Brsk1 Δ4–7 mice exhibit increased seizure susceptibility, neuronal hyperexcitability, and neurobehavioral impairments—recapitulating key clinical features associated with BRSK1 haploinsufficiency in humans. Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses revealed dysregulation of pathways critical for axonal development and synaptic function.SignificanceOur genomic and functional studies strongly support BRSK1 haploinsufficiency as a pathogenic mechanism in a human epilepsy syndrome. To our knowledge, this is the first study to implicate the brain‐specific serine/threonine kinase BRSK1 in epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorders.Keywords: BRSK1, epilepsy, loss of function, neurodevelopmental disorders

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy accounts for a significant proportion of the global disease burden, affecting ~50 million people worldwide. 1 Genetic etiology is strongly implicated in epilepsy, particularly in children. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Large‐scale genomics studies have led to the discovery of over 1000 novel epilepsy‐related genes, which have provided insights into disease mechanisms. 6 , 7 However, definitive genetic causes could be found in only 30%–50% of children with epilepsy in clinics. 7 , 8 Thus, the molecular basis of epilepsy remains unresolved for a substantial proportion of affected children.

BRSK1 (brain‐specific kinase 1), also known as SAD‐B, is a brain‐specific serine/threonine kinase in the AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK)‐related kinase family. 9 BRSK1 together with family member BRSK2 (SAD‐A) plays a critical role in neuronal polarization, synaptic transmission, mitochondrial dynamics, and neuronal maturation. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Its kinase activity is specifically mediated by liver kinase B1 (LKB1), dependent on phosphorylation at a conserved threonine residue in the activation loop. 16 BRSK1 maintains structural and functional stability in neurons by regulating microtubule dynamics, presynaptic proteins, and energy metabolism, making it a critical molecule for brain development and function. 17 , 18 , 19 However, BRSK1 has not previously been implicated in any genetic disorder.

In this study, through the trio‐based exome sequencing (trio‐ES) of 394 families with unexplained epilepsy, we identified six novel single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indel heterozygotes in the BRSK1 gene, five de novo and one inherited variant from affected parent, in eight affected individuals including seven probands. Three variants were frameshift or stop gain that resulted in no detectable BRSK1 protein in cellular assay. One missense variant led to reduced BRSK1 protein. We generated and characterized a new line of Brsk1 exon 4–7 deletion (Brsk1 Δ4–7) mice that resulted in a complete knockout of Brsk1. Both heterozygous and homozygous Brsk1 exon 4–7 knockout mice displayed increased seizure susceptibility to pentylenetetrazol (PTZ), hyperexcitability of neurons, and neurobehavioral impairments. Proteomic analyses have revealed that BRSK1 deficiency leads to dysregulation of proteins involved in axonal development and synaptic function, which may underlie the phenotypic deficits observed in Brsk1‐deficient mice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

A total of 394 unrelated trio families were recruited from epilepsy centers in the following hospitals from 2018 to 2024: Department of Pediatrics, Children's Hospital of Fudan University; Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University; Epilepsy Center, Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University; Department of Neurology, Anhui Hospital; and Department of Pediatric Neurology, Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Clinical information was collected, including age at seizure, type, frequency, progression, treatment response, family history, and neurological examination findings. Clinical assessments including brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and video‐electroencephalography (EEG) recordings with the protocol of hyperventilation, photic stimulation, and sleep were reviewed by two investigators with experience in these assessments. The diagnosis and classification of epileptic seizures and syndromes followed the criteria proposed by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE; 1989, 2001, 2010, and 2017). 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of the National Children's Medical Center, Fudan University Children's Hospital ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parents.

Exome sequencing and analysis

Blood samples were collected from the probands, their parents, and other available family members. Genomic DNA was extracted from the blood samples using the QIAamp DNA Blood Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The exome was captured from fragmented genomic DNA samples using the xGen Exome Research Panel V2.0 (IDT, USA) for enrichment. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was performed with DNBSEQ‐T7 (MGI, China). Data were generated through massively parallel sequencing, achieving an average depth of over 100x and capturing more than 98% of the targeted regions. The raw sequencing data were then aligned to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Genome build 37 (GRCh37) using the Burrows–Wheeler alignment algorithm. Following this alignment, single‐nucleotide variants and insertion/deletion variants were identified and annotated using the Genome Analysis Tool Kit.

A case‐by‐case analysis approach was employed to systematically identify potential pathogenic variants for each trio. A comprehensive list of potential pathogenic variants was generated through a systematic stepwise screening process. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 (1) Population‐based filtration retained variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) <0.005 in the 1000 Genomes Project, Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC), ExAC‐East Asian Population (ExAC‐EAS), Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), and Chigene in‐house database. (2) Functional prediction‐based filtration retained missense, nonsense, indel, frameshift, and splice variants based on computational and predictive data. Potential pathogenic variants were flagged if predicted as damaging by Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT), Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (PolyPhen‐2), Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD), and Mutation Taster (3) The known epilepsy‐associated genes were set aside, and inheritance‐based filtration retained variants consistent with inherited models. (4) Several unrelated sporadic cases harbored the identical gene variation, particularly those sharing the same locus. These variations represented the genetic difference between the patients and normal individuals in a family and potentially explained the occurrence of disease, and they were selected for further studies to define the gene–disease association.

The pathogenicity of variants was interpreted according to American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) 32 and modified based on ClinGen recommendations. Validation of the identified variants was performed using Sanger sequencing. For all identified de novo variants, variant reads (vR) to total reads (tR) ratios in parental samples were manually reviewed using the raw BAM files to assess the possibility of low‐level mosaicism.

Copy number variation (CNV) analysis

To evaluate whether haploinsufficiency due to large‐scale deletions or duplications contributes to BRSK1‐associated neurodevelopmental phenotypes, we performed copy number variation (CNV) analysis based on exome sequencing data from the entire cohort. Two independent algorithms, XHMM (eXome‐Hidden Markov Model) and CoNIFER (Copy Number Inference From Exome Reads), were utilized to ensure robustness. Raw data were quality controlled by FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), and adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed using fastp. 33 The sequences were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37/UCSC hg19 using BWA‐MEM, 34 followed by CNV calling according to each tool's default pipeline. Copy number variants (CNVs) overlapping the BRSK1 locus were manually inspected. No deletions or duplications involving the BRSK1 genomic region were detected in any individual. These results support the conclusion that all pathogenic alterations identified in this study are single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) or small insertions/deletions (indels). It is noted, however, that exome‐based CNV detection has reduced sensitivity for complex or noncoding structural variants. Further studies incorporating whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), which covers the entire genome and provides more comprehensive detection of structural variants than whole‐exome sequencing (WES), will be necessary to fully assess the structural disruptions of BRSK1.

BRSK1 protein structural modeling analysis

The structure of BRSK1 was modeled using AlphaFoldDB (https://alphafold.com/entry/Q8TDC3) to predict the effect of missense variants on protein structure. Hydrogen bonding and clashes were analyzed and visualized using UCSF ChimeraX software (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax/). 35 , 36

Constructs of wild‐type and BRSK1 variants

Human BRSK1 cDNA was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using SH‐SY5Y cDNA as the template, with the primers Kpn1‐Hsa‐BRSK1‐F (CCGCTAGAGATCTGGTACCatgtcgtccggggccaagg) and Not1‐Hsa‐BRSK1‐R (atccCTCGAGCGGCCGCGagggcagaggggtcccgtt). The amplified fragment was then cloned into the AdTrack plasmid using KpnI and NotI restriction sites. The BRSK1 wild‐type (WT) plasmid served as the template for generating mutant constructs via site‐directed mutagenesis. All plasmids were validated by Sanger sequencing. The primers used for mutagenesis are provided in Table S4.

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Experimental Animals at National Children's Medical Center, Fudan University, in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines. WT and Brsk1 knockout mice, on a C57BL/6 background, were kept in a 12‐hour light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. All experimental animals were male.

Generation of Brsk1 mutant mice

We used CRISPR‐Cas9 technology to edit the Brsk1 gene. The brief protocol was as follows: guide RNAs (gRNAs) were transcribed in vitro. The gRNAs targeted the exon 4–7 (5′‐TAGAGGCTTGGGCTATGGGT‐3′ and 5’‐GACACCCTTGGCCTATCCGT‐3′) of Brsk1. Cas9 and gRNAs were microinjected into fertilized C57BL6/J mouse embryos. We targeted exon 4–7 of Brsk1 transcript (ENSMUST00000048248.8). Exons 4–7 contain a 361 bp coding sequence, and the deletion resulted in a frameshift and was predicted to disrupt BRSK1 protein function. The expression levels of BRSK1 protein were analyzed in 8‐ to 10‐week‐old mice of the Brsk1 Δe4–7+/+ , Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− , and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− genotypes.

Immunostaining

Hippocampal slices (100 μm) from WT C57BL6/J mice were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 minutes and then kept in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. Following cryoprotection with 15% and 30% sucrose, 30‐μm‐thick coronal sections were prepared using a freezing microtome (Leica, Germany). The sections were washed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized, and blocked for 2 hours at room temperature in the dark with 0.2% Triton‐X‐100 and 10% normal donkey serum (NDS). The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (rabbit anti‐BRSK1, 1:300; guinea pig anti‐NeuN, 1:500; guinea pig anti‐GFAP, 1:300; guinea pig anti‐IBA1, 1:300; and guinea pig anti‐MBP, 1:300) and diluted in 10% NDS. After washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature in the dark. Finally, sections were stained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, 1:1000) for 15 min and rinsed for at least 30 min.

Quantitative immunoblot

To evaluate BRSK1 protein expression in C57BL6/J mouse brains or plasmid‐transfected SHSY5Y cells and HEK293Tcells, tissue or cell samples were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (PIC, Beyotime, China). The protein samples were then separated by 4%–20% SDS‐PAGE and subjected to analysis using anti‐GFP (Cell Signaling Technology, 2956S, USA), anti‐BRSK1 (Proteintech, 12 673‐1‐AP, China), anti‐β‐Actin (Proteintech, 66 009‐1‐Ig, China), and anti‐GAPDH (Servicebio, GB15004‐100, China) antibodies. The bands were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Electrophysiology

Mice (2–3 weeks old) were deeply anesthetized and subjected to transcardial perfusion with oxygenated ice‐cold NMDG cutting solution (in mM: 93 NMDG, 93 HCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 25 D‐glucose, 20 HEPES, 5 Na‐ascorbate, 2 thiourea, 3 Na‐pyruvate, 10 MgSO4, 0.5 CaCl2, and pH 7.35). Following brain extraction, the tissue was quickly immersed in the same solution. Horizontal hippocampal slices (250 μm) were then prepared using a Leica VT1200s vibratome and incubated in NMDG solution at 34°C for 10–13 min. Afterward, the slices were maintained at 25°C in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) for 1 hour before electrophysiological recordings. The slices were transferred to the recording chamber, continuously superfused with oxygenated ACSF (3 mL/min), and observed under an upright microscope fitted with a 40× water‐immersion lens (Examiner. A1). CA1 hippocampal neurons were identified based on their characteristic morphology and location. Spontaneous action potentials (sAPs) were recorded via whole‐cell patch‐clamp using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier, Digidata 1440A converter, and pClamp 10.4 software. The AP frequency was analyzed over a 10‐min period. The definition of epileptiform bursting activity is that at least five consecutive APs are overlaying the large depolarization shift (≥300 ms). The criterion to define a bursting neuron is at least two repeated bursting activities occurring during 10 min of recording. 37 , 38 , 39

Assessment of seizure susceptibility

A dose‐escalation experiment with pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) was conducted to assess seizure susceptibility. The mice were transferred to a transparent cage and habituated therein for at least 30 min before the initial PTZ (30 mg/kg, i.p.) injection. PTZ was administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg every 10 min until the mice achieved a Racine score of 4/5. Seizure severity was classified according to the Racine scale: 0, normal behavior; 1, facial and mouth movements; 2, head nodding; 3, forelimb clonus; 4, rearing with forelimb clonus; and 5, rearing and falling with forelimb clonus. Seizure‐onset latency, duration, and frequency were recorded for each group. No spontaneous seizures were observed in any of the tested mice. 40

Behavioral tests

The Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− , Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− , and WT mice were littermates from Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− breeding. Mice were grouped and housed in a facility with a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle. The light turned on at 8:00 a.m. Mice were given unrestricted access to food and drink. Male mice, aged 8–12 weeks, were subjected to behavioral tests conducted from 8:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. Prior to conducting the test of each animal, every device was cleaned using a diluted sodium hypochlorite solution to minimize potential bias from olfactory cues. Behavioral tests included the Morris Water‐maze test, Y‐maze test, Open‐field test, Elevated Plus maze. To prevent any bias in behavioral assessments, the experimenters were blinded to the genotype throughout the testing.

Video‐EEG monitoring in mice

Four screws with electroencephalography (EEG) recording wires were implanted into the bilateral frontal and suboccipital bones and then secured with dental cement. One week later, continuous video‐EEG monitoring was conducted for 48 hours. EEG coherence analysis data regarding EEG basal activity as well as Global and Local Aspect Recognition were included for analysis. EEG data were analyzed using the software Seizure Pro 2.1.6.

Protein digestion

Protein of each sample from hippocampus of Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and WT mice was adjusted to the same concentration and volume based on the measured protein content. To each protein solution, 25 mM DTT was added, achieving a final concentration of ~5 mM, and the mixture was incubated at 55°C for 30–60 min. Afterward, iodoacetamide was introduced to a final concentration of about 10 mM, and the solution was kept in the dark at room temperature for 15–30 min. Pre‐chilled acetone (6 times the volume of the protein solution) was then added to precipitate the protein, and the mixture was stored at −20°C for a minimum of 4 h, or overnight. Once the precipitation was complete, the sample was centrifuged at 8000g for 10 min at 4°C to collect the protein pellet. The volume of enzyme digestion buffer was added according to the protein amount (protein: enzyme ratio of 50:1 (m/m), with 2 μg of enzyme per 100 μg of protein), and the solution was incubated at 37°C for 12 h. The enzymatic digestion was terminated by adjusting the pH to ~3 with phosphoric acid. Finally, the samples of proteomics were desalted on SOLA SPE, and the peptides of phosphoproteomics were desalted using a Sep‐Pak C18 cartridge.

The enrichment of phosphorylated peptides

For the phosphoproteomic analysis, the peptides were extracted from the frozen sample after trypsin digestion using the methods described. The tryptic peptides were then enriched with High‐Select Fe‐NTA Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's recommendations. Briefly, the peptides were suspended with binding/wash buffer (provided in the enrichment kit), mixed with the equilibrated resins, and incubated at 21–25°C for 30 min. After incubation, the resins were washed thrice with binding/wash buffer and twice with water. The enriched peptides were eluted with elution buffer (contained in the enrichment kit) and dried in a vacuum drier at 30°C.

LC–MS/MS analysis

The proteomic data analysis was performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All analyses were performed on a Nanoelute2 system (Bruker) that was coupled to a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer equipped with CaptiveSpray source (Bruker Daltonics). An aliquot of the solution described at the end of the preceding paragraph containing ~300 ng peptide was injected onto a 15 cm × 100 μm column (1.9 μm C18, NanoRes). Mobile Phases A and B were 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in ACN, respectively. The flow rate was 300 nL/min, and the linear gradient was set as follows: 0–1.5 min, 5–7% B; 1.5–22 min, 7–24% B; 22–25 min, 24–35% B; 25–26 min, 35–90% B; 26–27 min, 90% B; 27–27.5 min, 90–2% B; and 27.5–30 min, 2% B. For the timsTOF HT settings, we used the instrument control software (Bruker timsControl, v6) to define quadrupole isolation windows as a function of the TIMS scan time (diaPASEF). Capillary voltage was 1.5 kV, dry gas temperature was 180°C, and dry gas flow rate was 3.0 L/min. The full MS scan range was set from 100 to 1700 m/z. Ion mobility is set from 0.75 to 1.4 Vs/cm2, and the collision energy range from 20 to 59 eV. All proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses were performed using a label‐free quantification strategy based on data‐independent acquisition (DIA) with diaPASEF mode on the timsTOF Pro platform (Bruker).

The phosphoproteomics data analysis was performed by Shanghai Luming Biological Technology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). All analyses were performed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Thermo, Bruker) equipped with an Easyspray source (Thermo, USA). Nanoflow reversed‐phase chromatography was performed on an EASY‐nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile Phases A and B were 0.1 vol% formic acid solution and 80:20:0.1 vol% ACN: water: formic acid, respectively. The total run was 60 min (0–48 min, 5–22% B; 48–53 min, 22–35% B; 53–56 min, 35–90% B; 56–57 min, 90–3% B; 57–60 min, 3% B). Capillary voltage was 1.5 kV, dry gas temperature was 180°C, and dry gas flow rate was 3.0 L/min. The full MS scan range was set from 100 to 1700 m/z. The ion mobility range was 0.7–1.3 Vs/cm2, and the collision energy range was 20–59 eV.

Database search and data analysis

MS/MS spectra were searched using the Spectronaut Pulsar 18.4 (Biognosys, Swiss) against the Uniprot Mus Musculus database. Search database specific parameters are set as follows: Fixed modifications: Carbamidomethyl (C); Variable modifications: phospho (STY) (only for phosphoproteomics), Oxidation (M), and Acetyl (Protein N‐term); digestion: trypsin; Precursor Qvalue cutoff: 0.01; Protein Qvalue cutoff: 0.01; Missed cleavage: 2; Quantity MS‐Level: MS2.

The raw data were processed using Spectronaut Pulsar (version 18.4, Biognosys), with the search database set to the UniProt Mus musculus reference proteome (uniprot‐Mus musculus‐10 090‐2024.2.1.fasta). Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and phosphosites were defined as those with a log2‐fold change (log2FC) >1 or <−1 and p < 0.05. Functional annotation of identified proteins was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) (http://geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). DEPs were subjected to GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were analyzed using STRING (https://string‐db.org/).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To compare independent or paired samples, the Student's t test was used. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed and multiple comparisons with Dunnett's post hoc test applied to examine pairwise differences. For non‐parametric data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized, and Fisher's exact test (two‐tailed) was employed to compare allele frequencies between groups. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 7.00 and SPSS 20, using a significance level of .05.

RESULTS

Genetic and clinical characteristics associated with BRSK1 variants

All 394 probands diagnosed with epilepsy were born to non‐consanguineous Chinese parents. Clinical trio‐ES of the 394 probands did not identify any clinically significant SNVs that explain the cause of epilepsy. CNV analysis using both XHMM and CoNIFER algorithms did not identify any clinically significant CNVs involving the BRSK1 locus. Through the research analysis of trio‐ES, we identified four novel SNVs and two indels in the BRSK1 gene (NM_032430.2) from seven unrelated probands (Table 1). These included two frameshift variants (c.191_c.192insCGTGAAC; p. Lys 69Profs*51 and c.674_c.675insCG; p. Val226Alafs*19), one nonsense (stop gain) variant (c.2032C>T; p. Arg678Ter), and three missense variants (c.457T>C; p. Cys153Arg, c.574G>A; p. Gly192Arg, and c.1609C>A; p. Pro537Thr). The c.191_c.192insCGTGAAC, c.674_c.675insCG, c.574G>A, and c.2032C>T variants were de novo. The c.457 T>C (p. Cys153Arg) variant was inherited from the mother with epilepsy. The c.1609C>A variant was identified in two families: one1 was de novo and the other was inherited from the mother who was lost to follow‐up during the study (Figure 1A). For five de novo variants, we reviewed the parental sequencing reads; the vR/tR ratios showed no evidence of low‐level mosaicism.

The detailed clinical characteristics of seven affected individuals with BRSK1 variants were summarized in Table 1. Except for one case with an age at onset of 6 years (Case 4), the age at seizure onset of the remaining six cases was within 1 year of age. The seizure types include generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS), childhood absence epilepsy (CAE), epileptic spasms (ES) and focal epilepsy (FE). Six cases experienced diurnal seizure, one had nocturnal seizure. Case 1 could be precipitated by a specific camera flash. The representative traces of EEG were shown in Figure S1. Abnormal EEG findings with epileptiform discharges were observed in Cases 1, 2, 3, and 4, with representative patterns (such as spikes, slow waves, and generalized spike‐and‐wave discharges) summarized in Table 1. Abnormal MRI findings were described in three cases. These abnormalities include T2 patches (Case 3), thin corpus callosum and slight widening of the extracerebral space (Case 6), and bilateral ventricular signal abnormalities with impaired white matter myelination (Case 7). Behavioral and developmental assessments revealed variable presentations in these cases ranging from normal to global developmental delay (GDD) and with or without autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Case 1 exhibited delayed gross motor and language development, ASD, and ADHD. Case 3 presented with delayed language development and social impairment. Case 6 demonstrated development delay, with developmental quotient (DQ) of 75 and a mental index (MI) of 58 from Developmental Screening Test (DST). These observations support the contribution of the BRSK1 variants to broader but variable neurodevelopmental phenotypes. More neurobehavioral investigations are warranted in future.

Altered expression of BRSK1 variants in SH‐SY5Y and HEK293T cells

To investigate the effect of BRSK1 variants on protein expression, we expressed the plasmids containing six variants and normal BRSK1 gene together with a control GFP plasmid in SH‐SY5Y and HEK293T cells. As expected, two frameshift variants (p. Lys69Profs*51 and p. Val226Alafs*19) and the nonsense variant (p. Arg678Ter) showed no detectable BRSK1 protein, indicating mRNA decay or unstable protein product. For three missense variants, only p. Pro537Thr variant resulted in a noticeable reduction of BRSK1 protein compared to normal BRSK1, suggesting a partial protein instability or increased degradation (Figure 2B–E). The normal level of BRSK1 protein for other missense variants may suggest that these variants may affect the protein function dynamics but not the protein level. The protein expression data further support the loss of function as a potential pathomechanism associated with BRSK1 variants.

The temporal and spatial expression of BRSK1 in the mouse and human brain

Previous studies revealed neuron and brain specific expression of Brsk1 in mouse and rat embryonic and adult brain. 15 , 18 , 41 We confirmed that Brsk1 expression was detected across multiple mouse brain regions, with significantly higher levels observed in the hippocampus and brainstem compared to the olfactory bulb, cortex, striatum, and cerebellum (Figure 3A). Mouse Brsk1 is expressed predominantly in neurons in the hippocampus while at lower levels in astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes (Figure S2A–D). Developmental analysis revealed Brsk1 was expressed at embryonic Day 14 and reached the peak P14 to adult in mice (Figure 3A). To examine the expression of BRSK1 in human brains, we analyzed expression of BRSK1 via in silico expression data extracted from Brain Span (www.brainspan.org) (Figure 3B). Overall, the expression pattern of BRSK1 in different brain regions during development in humans was similar to that in mice.

Haploinsufficiency of Brsk1 leads to increased susceptibility to seizures in mice

To examine the functional impact of BRSK1 haploinsufficiency, we generated a new Brsk1 mutant mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 method. We targeted the exons 4–7 (361 bp) deletion of Brsk1 transcript (ENSMUST00000048248.8/NM_001003920.3 in C57BL6/J strain) (Figure 3C). The deletion of exons 4–7 was identified by PCR genotype (Figure S3A,B). The exon 4–7 deletion was predicted to result in a frame shift for BRSK1 protein (Figure 3C). Germline transmission of targeted exon 4–7 deletion (Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− ) was bred to C57BL/6J for F2–3 generations for the molecular and F4–F9 generations for the behavioral experiments. Brsk1 protein in heterozygous (Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− ) and homozygous (Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− ) mice was analyzed by immunoblot (Figure 3D,E). The reduced and absent Brsk1 protein was observed in Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice, respectively, indicating that exon 4–7 deletion resulted in a complete knockout of the Brsk1 gene. The expression level of Brsk2 was not altered in Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice (Figure S4). Both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice did not have any apparent developmental defect. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve showed no significant differences in survival among WT, Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− , and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice. No significant difference was observed for the body weight among WT, Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− , and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice from 3 to 8 weeks of age (Figure 3F,G).

No spontaneous seizure activity was observed in both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice in home cages. In 48 h cortical EEG recording, the epileptiform activity was observed only occasionally in Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− or Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− mice (Figure S5A–C). To assess seizure susceptibility, a PTZ seizure induction protocol was employed. To assess seizure susceptibility, we employed a PTZ dose‐escalation protocol as described previously. 39 Both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice exhibited significantly increased seizure severity and susceptibility compared to WT, as indicated by higher maximum Racine scores across cumulative doses (Figure 4A), a greater proportion of mice reaching Stage 4/5 seizures (Figure 4B), and lower cumulative doses required to induce Racine 4/5 seizures (Figure 4C). During the initial 10‐min phase following the first 30 mg/kg injection, WT mice showed no Stage 4/5 seizures, whereas many Brsk1‐deficient mice progressed to severe seizures (Figure 4D). In addition, Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice exhibited significantly longer durations of Racine 5 seizures (Figure 4E), further supporting heightened seizure susceptibility. Patch‐clamp recordings revealed increased synchronized bursting activity in hippocampal neurons of Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice of 2–3 weeks. The bursting frequency and the number of APs over a 10‐min recording period were significantly higher in both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice compared to the WT group. In the WT group, 8.3% (1 of 12) of recorded neurons displayed bursting activity. In contrast, 50% (6 of 12) of neurons in the Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− group and 66.7% (8 of 12) of neurons in the Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− group exhibited bursting activity (Figure 4F–I).

Neurobehavioral phenotypes of Brsk1Δe4–7−/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− mutant mice

To investigate whether Brsk1 deficiency recapitulates the neurobehavioral impairments described in patients with BRSK1 variants, we conducted a battery of behavioral tests in Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice. Male mice were used primarily because of potential concern for significant variability for certain behavior in female mice related to the estrous cycle. Both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice appeared healthy in the home cage. In the open field test, the total distance traveled and movement speed were significantly higher in both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice than in WT mice (Figure 5 A,B,E). Both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice spent significantly less time in the center and longer time in peripheral space accordingly (Figure 5C,D). The rearing frequency was comparable between Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− , Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− , and WT mice (Figure 5F). These results indicated the anxiety‐like and hyperactivity behavior of Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk 1Δe4–7−/− mice. In the Morris water maze test, both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice had significantly increased path length (Figure 5H) and longer escape latencies (Figure 5I) during training compared to WT. In the probe trial, both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice showed significantly longer path lengths (Figure 5G), spent less time in the target quadrant (Figure 5K), and crossed the former platform location fewer times (Figure 5J) compared to WT mice. There was no significant difference in swim speed between groups. (Figure 5L). The Y‐maze test revealed a significantly reduced percentage of correct alternation but a comparable number of total entries in both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice compared to WT (Figure 5N,O). Both Morris water maze and Y‐maze indicated the impaired working and spatial memory of Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice. In the elevated plus maze (EPM), both Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice spent significantly less time in the open arms (Figure 5P) and fewer entries into open arms (Figure 5Q) compared to WT. Together, these findings indicated hyper‐locomotion, anxiety‐like behavior, and impaired working and spatial memory in Brsk1 deficiency mice.

Proteomic and phosphoproteomic dysregulation in the hippocampus of Brsk1‐deficient mice

BRSK1 is a brain‐specific kinase. To further elucidate the biochemical mechanisms resulting from Brsk1 gene deficiency, we performed proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling of hippocampal tissues from Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− and WT mice. PCA demonstrated a clear separation between WT and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice of both proteomic and phosphoproteomic datasets (Figure S6A). Significantly positive correlations among biological replicates further validated the robustness of the datasets (Figure S6B).

At the proteomic level, 98 proteins were significantly upregulated, whereas 37 were downregulated in Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice (Figure 6A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated proteins were associated primarily with axon development, neuron migration, and synaptic transmission, particularly chemical synaptic transmission (Figure 6B–D). Representative proteins contributing to the GO term “modulation of chemical synaptic transmission,” including TUBB2B, HRH3, HTR1A, HTR1B, and TMEM108, were significantly upregulated in Brsk1‐deficient hippocampi and are highlighted in the volcano plot (Figure 6A), indicating enhanced synaptic signaling activity. In contrast, downregulated proteins were enriched in processes such as mRNA processing, autophagy, and the regulation of oxidative stress responses (Figure S6C,D). Representative proteins associated with the GO terms “oxidative stress response” and “autophagy” including CHRNA4, PENK, MYD88, MT1, and MSRB1, were significantly downregulated and are also highlighted in the volcano plot (Figure 6A), suggesting impaired cellular stress response and autophagic processes.

Phosphoproteomic analysis identified 142 phosphosites significantly upregulated and 151 downregulated in Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mouse brains (Figure 6E). Phosphoproteomic analysis identified MAPT (tau), a known BRSK1 substrate, with significantly reduced phosphorylation at the S500 site in Brsk1‐deficient mouse brains. Although S500 has not been reported previously as a BRSK1 target, the significant downregulation observed here suggests it may be a new phosphosite regulated by BRSK1. These results highlight the potential of BRSK1 to regulate tau phosphorylation in a site‐specific manner and underscore the relevance of our dataset. GO analysis indicated that elevated phosphoproteins were enriched in pathways involved in synaptic organization, axonal transport, and neuron projection development (Figure 6F,G), whereas downregulated phosphoproteins were associated with actin filament‐based processes, cell junction organization, and regulation of membrane potential (Figure S6E,F). Among the upregulated phosphoproteins, six phosphosites—CACNA1E_S2097, PCDH8_Y1051, CACNA1C_S815, CACNA1A_S1038, CAMK4_T196, and CAMK1D_T180—were also annotated under the GO term “modulation of chemical synaptic transmission,” suggesting enhanced synaptic activity in Brsk1‐deficient hippocampi (Figure 6E). Conversely, representative downregulated phosphosites—ANK2_S1936, PEX5L_S172, CACNA1A_S2303, GRIN2D_S1049, and ANK2_S1900—were annotated under the GO term “regulation of membrane potential,” with some also associated with cytoskeletal organization and neuronal polarity (Figure 6E). Notably, kinase‐substrate enrichment analysis (KSEA) identified Rps6kb2 and Lrrk2 as key activated kinases in Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice, highlighting their potential role in regulating downstream signaling (Figure 6H–J).

Network analysis of enriched GO terms demonstrated that upregulated proteins formed functional modules related to chemical synaptic transmission, axon guidance, and neuron migration (Figure 6C), whereas downregulated phosphoproteins were linked to disrupted cytoskeletal organization, cell polarity, and regulation of membrane potential (Figure S6E,F). These findings suggest that Brsk1 deficiency perturbs synaptic signaling, axonal development, cytoskeletal dynamics, and membrane excitability via widespread proteomic and phosphoproteomic dysregulation.

DISCUSSION

Our genomics and functional studies provide convincing evidence to support BRSK1 as a novel gene for epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorder. Our in vitro and in vivo functional studies support the pathogenicity of four BRSK1 variants (c.191_192insCGTGAAC; p.Lys69Profs51, c.674_675insCG; p.Val226Alafs19, c.1609C>A; p.Pro537Thr, and c.2032C>T; p.Arg678Ter). Our analyses suggest the pathogenicity of other two missense variants (c.457T>C; p.Cys153Arg and c.574G>A; p.Gly192Arg) but additional evidence is needed. It is important to note that both heterozygous and homozygous Brsk1‐mutant mice display increased neuronal hyperexcitability, seizure susceptibility, and neurobehavioral phenotypes. These findings support the causative role of BRSK1 in epilepsy syndrome and the loss of function as the molecular mechanism for the pathogenesis of BRSK1 deficiency. Although our current CNV analysis using XHMM and CoNIFER did not identify any deletions or duplications at the BRSK1 locus, we acknowledge that the resolution of exome‐based CNV detection is limited. Future studies by WGS may be warranted to fully capture potential structural variants that may contribute to BRSK1 haploinsufficiency and broaden the mutational spectrum beyond SNVs and indels.

As with many recently identified neurogenetic disorders, variants in BRSK1 are associated with a broad spectrum of neurodevelopmental phenotypes. Although the limited number of reported cases makes it premature to establish a definitive genotype–phenotype correlation, current data suggest that individuals harboring null mutations—such as nonsense or frameshift variants—tend to present with more severe clinical features than those with missense variants. Furthermore, the variable expressivity of epilepsy—ranging from GTCS to ES—also appears to be associated with these null mutations. These findings imply that BRSK1 haploinsufficiency may be influenced by additional genetic, epigenetic, or environmental modifiers, contributing to the heterogeneity of the neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

It is worth noting that Brsk1‐mutant mice of an exon 1 deletion (Brsk1 Δe1 ) were described previously. 15 However, the focus of the initial study was on molecular and cellular phenotypic analysis of Brsk1 and Brsk2 double mutant mice because of no apparent phenotype in single mutant and perinatal lethality of double mutant. 15 , 41 A subsequent study reported abnormal behavior in open field and fear conditioning and impaired synaptic function in Brsk1 Δe1 homozygous mice. 17 Alvarado‐Kristensson et al. report that the mouse Brsk1 gene has multiple short mRNA. 42 One isoform (EU586326, name SadB SI ) has a transcriptional starting site in intron 1 and terminates at intron 10 of the full‐length transcript (NM_001003920) (Figure 3C). This short transcript encodes a shortened BRSK1 protein that was not disrupted in Brsk1 Δe1−/− mice. 42 , 43 This finding indicates that Brsk1 Δe1−/− mice are an isoform‐specific knockout of Brsk1. The isoform‐specific functions have not been fully elucidated but the short isoform is shown to contribute to the phosphorylation of γ‐tubulin, regulating centrosome duplication. 42 In contrast, our Brsk1 Δe4–7 mice disrupt all known Brsk1 isoforms and are a complete knockout of the Brsk1 gene. This difference may explain the phenotypic difference between Brsk1 Δe4–7 and Brsk1 Δe1 mice.

Our findings demonstrate that Brsk1 Δe4–7+/− and Brsk1 Δe4–7−/− mice exhibit a range of behavioral abnormalities, including hyperactivity, increased anxiety‐like behavior, and impaired learning and memory. These phenotypes recapitulate the clinical presentations observed in patients harboring BRSK1 variants, such as intellectual disability, ASD, and ADHD. This phenotypic convergence between the mouse model and human cases underscores the translational relevance of BRSK1 in neurodevelopmental disorders.

Family serine/threonine kinases play a role in the regulation of cell proliferation, programmed cell death, cell differentiation, and embryonic development. Many serine/threonine kinases are known to be implicated in cancers. 44 , 45 Recently, germline mutations in a list of serine/threonine kinases such as CDKL5, mTOR, and CamKII have been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders with epilepsy. BRSK1 is a special kinase because of its almost exclusive expression in brain. It has been shown that deficiency of both Brsk1 and Brsk2 together in mice results in abnormal neuronal polarization. 15 , 41 Future study is warranted to examine whether Brsk1 deficiency alone is sufficient to cause the same defect. The discovery of BRSK1 as a novel causative gene for epilepsy highlights the expanding role of family serine/threonine kinases in the neurodevelopmental disorders.

Our findings appear to contrast with those of a previous study by Li et al., 46 which reported that acute siRNA‐mediated knockdown of SAD‐B (BRSK1) in the adult rat hippocampus reduced seizure susceptibility in a PTZ‐induced model. Several key distinctions may account for this discrepancy. First, the Brsk1 deficiency in our study is caused by a germline mutation, whereas in the study by Li et al., Brsk1 deficiency was induced by shRNA somatically. There are many examples in medical genetics where germline and somatic mutations in the same gene result in markedly different phenotypes. For instance, germline mutations in K‐RAS cause Noonan syndrome, which is associated with little to no increased cancer risk, whereas somatic mutations in K‐RAS are well known to contribute to cancer development. Second, Brsk1 deficiency in our model affects all cell types, whereas in the PTZ‐induced seizure model, both the increased Brsk1 expression and its knockdown via shRNA are likely limited to specific cell populations. This difference in cellular context may contribute to the contrasting findings. Finally, it is possible that Brsk1 is a dose‐sensitive gene, and that increased expression of Brsk1 contributes to the phenotype observed in the PTZ‐induced model.

How the deficiency of Brsk1 leads to neuronal hyperexcitability and epilepsy is unknown. The results from our initial proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses offer some initial insights. We identified a large set of differentially regulated proteins when comparing the proteome of BRSK1 deficiency and WT brains. These proteins are implicated primarily in axon development and chemical synaptic transmission. In addition, phosphoproteomic KSEA suggested that Rps6kb2 and Lrrk2 are the kinases with upregulated activity following Brsk1 knockout, although further studies are needed to determine whether they are directly regulated by Brsk1.

RPS6KB2 is a key component of the mTOR signaling pathway. 47 , 48 Notably, the mTOR pathway has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of epilepsy, particularly in genetic disorders such as tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) and focal cortical dysplasia. 49 Furthermore, it is worth noting that a family gene, RPS6KA3, is known to cause a well‐characterized neurodevelopmental disorder, Coffin–Lowry syndrome. 50 , 51 Although no report indicates that LRRK2 is implicated in epilepsy, LRRK2 is a multifunctional kinase highly expressed in the nervous system, playing an important role in the regulation of vesicle trafficking, neurite outgrowth, receptor localization, and synaptic function. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 Together, further investigation into the link between BRSK1 and the RPS6KB2 axis and LRRK2 may provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying BRSK1‐related epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorders.

CONCLUSION

In summary, our findings support a pathogenic role of BRSK1 haploinsufficiency in epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorders, highlighting it as a promising target for future therapeutic interventions.
