Non-destructive testing of steel-lined concrete structure using multiple agents with deep prior
Authors: Abdulrahman M. Alanazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-destructive testing is a cornerstone of structural integrity assessment that enables internal evaluation of materials without inflicting damage. Among various imaging methods, Ultrasonic Model-Based Iterative Reconstruction (UMBIR) has gained attention for its ability to enhance ultrasonic imaging by incorporating physical and statistical priors. Notably, its extension, Multi-Frequency Ultrasonic Model-Based Iterative Reconstruction (MF-UMBIR) to process multi-frequency datasets have shown improved accuracy over traditional techniques. However, its performance has some limitations in highly complex structures. This paper presents Deep Multi-Agent Consensus Equilibrium (Deep-MACE) method, a novel reconstruction framework that integrates multi-frequency forward model agents with a deep learning prior using the consensus equilibrium formulation. By combining data consistency across different excitation frequencies with the expressive power of a learned U-Net prior, Deep-MACE enables high-fidelity imaging in acoustically heterogeneous environments. The primary objective is to reconstruct images that reveal structural damage, such as corroded rebars and delaminations, thereby supporting non-invasive Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) of steel-lined concrete structures. Experimental results demonstrate that both UMBIR and MF-UMBIR suffer from limitations in defect visibility and robustness when applied to steel-lined concrete structures. In contrast, Deep-MACE consistently produces clearer reconstructions, successfully identifying all internal rebars with fewer artifacts and improved spatial resolution. These results highlight the potential of integrating deep priors into multi-agent frameworks for advanced ultrasonic non-destructive testing.

Introduction

Non-destructive testing (NDT) is an essential technique for evaluating the integrity and safety of materials and structures without causing damage. It plays a critical role in various industries, including construction, aerospace, and manufacturing, by enabling the detection of internal defects, cracks, and other anomalies that could compromise structural performance such as corroded rebars. Rebars, short for reinforcing bars, are steel rods embedded within concrete to provide tensile strength and enhance structural integrity. Their corrosion or degradation poses significant safety concerns in civil infrastructure. Traditional NDT methods, such as ultrasonic testing, radiography, and magnetic particle inspection, have been widely adopted due to their effectiveness in identifying surface and subsurface flaws. However, these conventional approaches often rely on manual interpretation and are limited in their ability to provide quantitative assessments, especially in complex structures or materials with heterogeneous properties. The increasing demand for more accurate, efficient, and automated inspection techniques has spurred research into advanced NDT methodologies that leverage computational modeling and data-driven analysis1.

SHM refers to the process of implementing a damage detection and characterization strategy for engineering structures. In the context of civil infrastructure, SHM plays a vital role in ensuring the safety and longevity of reinforced concrete systems, pipelines, and containment vessels2,3. Ultrasonic NDT techniques serve as an essential tool within SHM frameworks, particularly for detecting internal damage such as corrosion, cracking, and delamination that may not be visible on the surface. The inspection of steel-lined concrete structures falls naturally within the domain of SHM, where regular assessment is critical to detect early signs of degradation and prevent structural failure.

Model-based regularized inversion methods have emerged as powerful tools for enhancing the capabilities of NDT. These techniques formulate the inverse problem of reconstructing material properties from measured data as an optimization task4,5, incorporating prior knowledge and physical models to stabilize the solution and mitigate the effects of noise and incomplete data. For instance, Full Waveform Inversion (FWI) has been applied to ultrasonic imaging to achieve high-resolution reconstructions by minimizing the difference between observed and simulated waveforms, accounting for complex wave propagation phenomena6. Similarly, Model-Based Iterative Reconstruction (MBIR) approaches have been utilized to improve image quality in ultrasonic B-scan imaging by incorporating spatially variant priors and advanced forward models7,8. Other notable contributions include the development of tensor-based models for the Total Focusing Method (TFM)9, inverse approaches for ultrasonic imaging from full matrix capture data10, and regularized inversion techniques for ultrasonic B-scan image reconstruction11. These advancements have significantly improved the accuracy and reliability of NDT, particularly in challenging scenarios involving complex geometries and heterogeneous materials.

Building upon these foundations, the Ultrasonic Model-Based Iterative Reconstruction (UMBIR) method was introduced to address specific challenges in one-sided ultrasonic NDT. UMBIR leverages a linear forward model and incorporates spatially variant priors to account for depth-dependent attenuation, enabling the reconstruction of reflectivity images from ultrasonic measurements12,13. This approach has demonstrated superior performance over traditional techniques like the Synthetic Aperture Focusing Technique (SAFT)14, particularly in scenarios with limited access and complex material properties. However, UMBIR’s reliance on a single frequency limits its ability to capture frequency-dependent behaviors and may result in suboptimal reconstructions when dealing with materials exhibiting dispersive characteristics.

To overcome these limitations, the Multi-Frequency UMBIR (MF-UMBIR) approach was developed, extending the UMBIR framework to incorporate multiple frequency components in the reconstruction process15. By simultaneously considering data from various frequencies, MF-UMBIR enhances the resolution and contrast of the reconstructed images, capturing a broader range of material responses. This multi-frequency strategy allows for better characterization of defects and inhomogeneities, particularly in materials where different frequencies interact differently with structural features. MF-UMBIR has shown promising results in simulated and experimental studies, outperforming single-frequency methods in terms of defect detectability and image fidelity.

In recent years, deep learning has gained significant attention in the field of NDT due to its ability to learn complex patterns and features from large datasets. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been employed for automated analysis of ultrasonic B-scan images, achieving high accuracy in defect classification tasks16,17. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been utilized to generate synthetic UT data for training robust defect detection models, addressing the scarcity of labeled datasets18. Additionally, deep learning models have been applied to other NDT modalities, such as thermographic signal reconstruction for weld inspection19, and radar-based defect detection in tree trunks20. These studies demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of deep learning in enhancing NDT capabilities across various applications.

Recent studies have explored the use of guided waves in combination with deep learning to improve damage localization and characterization in stratified structures and composite materials. These approaches leverage the dispersive nature of guided waves and their sensitivity to structural anomalies, while using neural networks to extract latent features or estimate damage indicators from wavefield data21–23. Such efforts have advanced the capabilities of SHM systems, especially in complex or multilayered media. The present work extends this direction by integrating a learned prior within a model-based reconstruction framework, specifically tailored for steel-lined concrete geometries where scattering and impedance mismatches pose significant challenges.

The integration of model-based and data-driven approaches has led to the development of plug-and-play (PnP) methods, which incorporate deep learning priors into iterative reconstruction algorithms. PnP frameworks allow for the seamless integration of learned denoisers or regularizers into traditional optimization schemes, enhancing reconstruction quality without the need for explicit modeling of complex priors24. Building upon this concept, the Multi-Agent Consensus Equilibrium (MACE) framework was introduced to coordinate multiple agents, each representing different priors or data fidelity terms, to achieve a consensus solution through iterative updates25. MACE provides a flexible and modular approach to image reconstruction, accommodating various sources of information and promoting convergence to high-quality solutions13.

The proposed Deep-MACE is a novel reconstruction framework that synergistically combines the strengths of model-based and data-driven approaches within the MACE paradigm. Deep-MACE integrates multiple frequency-dependent forward models with a deep learning-based prior, facilitating the reconstruction of high-fidelity images from UT data in complex NDT scenarios. By leveraging the complementary information provided by different frequencies and the powerful representation capabilities of deep neural networks, Deep-MACE aims to overcome the limitations of existing methods in detecting and characterizing defects in challenging materials, such as steel-lined concrete structures. The proposed framework offers a promising direction for advancing the state-of-the-art in ultrasonic NDT, enabling more accurate and reliable assessment of structural integrity.

Multi-frequency physical model agents

In ultrasound NDT, each excitation frequency offers a distinct trade-off between penetration depth and spatial resolution. Lower frequencies can penetrate deeply into highly attenuating media such as concrete, but with lower imaging resolution. Conversely, higher frequencies offer finer detail but are more sensitive to attenuation and scattering. To utilize the advantages of different frequency bands, each frequency is treated as a distinct forward model agent, each enforcing data consistency with its respective measurement.

To this end, each forward model agent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_i$$\end{document} is implemented as a proximal operator that minimizes a data fidelity term while remaining close to the consensus estimate w provided by the global system. Specifically, the update for the i-th forward agent is given by:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_i(w) = \arg \min _{\textbf{x}} \frac{1}{2} \left\| \textbf{A}_i \textbf{x} + \textbf{D}_i \textbf{g} - \textbf{y}_i \right\| _2^2 + \frac{\beta }{2} \left\| \textbf{x} - w \right\| _2^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} is a tuning parameter that balances data fidelity against proximity to the shared variable w. To efficiently compute the update in Eq. (7), iterative coordinate descent (ICD) is employed as used in prior work on multi-frequency UMBIR15, which enables scalable and memory-efficient optimization even with large forward matrices and dense signal coupling.

By modeling each excitation frequency as an independent agent, the framework can reconcile differing spatial sensitivities and propagation characteristics without forcing a unified model. Each agent contributes a complementary view of the subsurface structure, and the consensus equilibrium ensures that all agents converge toward a mutually consistent solution. This modular architecture is particularly useful in complex NDT scenarios, where both resolution and penetration are critical for reliable defect detection.

This work assumes a perfectly bonded steel-concrete interface, consistent with established practices in ultrasonic NDT for SHM. This assumption provides a reliable baseline model to detect deviations caused by internal defects such as corroded rebars or voids. While interfacial modeling methods like the Quasi-Static Approximation (QSA) exist, see26,27, they are more commonly applied when the objective is to characterize interface-specific phenomena such as delamination. Incorporating such models is a promising direction for future studies focused on interface integrity.

Deep learning prior agent

Traditional priors used in regularized inversion such as total variation, wavelet sparsity, or Markov random fields rely on handcrafted assumptions that often fail to capture the complex patterns and high-order structures present in ultrasound images. In contrast, deep learning-based priors can be trained directly from data to learn both local features and global contextual patterns that represent physically plausible reconstructions. These learned priors have shown strong empirical success in imaging inverse problems28,29.

In the proposed framework, the prior agent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H: \mathbb {R}^N \rightarrow \mathbb {R}^N$$\end{document} is defined as a deep convolutional neural network that maps a noisy or artifact-laden image to a denoised, structurally accurate reconstruction. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(\cdot )$$\end{document} is implemented as a U-Net architecture trained to act as a projection operator onto the manifold of plausible images. This enables the agent to enforce high-level image priors that account for shape, texture, and boundary information beyond pixel-wise smoothness.

The U-Net used in this work is a convolutional neural network designed to act as a denoising agent. A schematic of the architecture is shown in Fig. 1. It receives a single-channel input image that corresponds to the current estimate of the acoustic reflectivity distribution obtained from MF-UMBIR. The U-Net outputs a denoised image of the same size. This output acts as the prior-consistent update in the consensus equilibrium iteration. The U-Net is composed of an encoder-decoder structure with skip connections to ensure that the architecture combines features from different scales effectively.

The prior agent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(\cdot )$$\end{document} is trained on a dataset of simulated phantoms representing steel-lined concrete blocks with embedded defects such as rebars and voids. Each training pair consists of an initial reconstruction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_0$$\end{document}, computed using a back-projection or adjoint model (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{A}^T \textbf{y}$$\end{document}), and a corresponding ground truth speed-of-sound map. The network is optimized using mean squared error (MSE) loss:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}(\theta ) = \frac{1}{T} \sum _{t=1}^{T} \left\| H_\theta (\textbf{x}_0^{(t)}) - \textbf{x}_{\text {true}}^{(t)} \right\| _2^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_\theta$$\end{document} is the neural network with parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}, and T is the number of training examples. Training is performed using stochastic gradient descent with a batch size of 1, a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document}, and momentum of 0.5. Data augmentation (flips, shifts, and noise injection) is used to improve generalization.

The dataset consists of 1000 synthetically generated 2D slices of ultrasonic reflectivity images. These slices simulate various target configurations and levels of subsurface structure complexity. The dataset was split as follows: 700 slices were used for training, 150 for validation, and 150 for testing. This split ensures separation between training and evaluation, and the validation set was used to monitor training progress and prevent overfitting.

Once trained, the network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(\cdot )$$\end{document} is embedded into the consensus equilibrium framework as a prior agent. Unlike traditional optimization-based regularization terms, this agent is applied directly in operator form. Within CE, the agent contributes a reflected estimate:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w_H^{(k+1)} = 2H(z^{(k)}) - z^{(k)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z^{(k)}$$\end{document} is the current consensus estimate across all agents. This formulation treats the network as a projection-reflection operator that helps guide the iteration toward the manifold of learned feasible solutions.

At equilibrium, the CE framework ensures that the output of the deep prior agrees with the average of all agents, i.e.,10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H(w_H^*) = \sum _{i=1}^{p} \mu _i F_i(w_i^*) + \mu _H w_H^*, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _H$$\end{document} are the weights assigned to the forward and prior agents in the consensus update. Because H is used in operator form and not as part of an optimization objective, its gradients or internal structure are not required during CE iterations. This allows for flexible plug-and-play integration of deep priors without needing end-to-end differentiability or re-training.

By embedding a data-driven prior into the CE framework, we are able to recover reconstructions that are not only consistent with the measurements, but also exhibit qualities that are difficult to impose through classical regularization terms alone.

Experimental results

In this section, a simulation-based evaluation of the proposed Deep-MACE method is presented for inspecting steel-lined concrete structures. The presence of a surface-bonded steel liner introduces substantial challenges to traditional ultrasound imaging: the large acoustic impedance mismatch between steel and concrete causes strong reflections at the interface, significant reverberations, and masking of deeper echoes from corroded rebars. As a result, conventional methods often fail to detect early-stage corrosion beneath such liners, especially when using single-frequency or heuristic-based processing.

To address this, we exploit a multi-frequency acquisition strategy combined with learned priors within a consensus equilibrium formulation, which fuses data across complementary frequency bands. This approach enhances structural resolution while ensuring robustness to the nonlinear and dispersive wave behavior in concrete and across the steel-concrete interface.

A steel liner of 2 cm thickness is placed along the top surface and is assumed to be perfectly bonded to the concrete. Four steel rebars are embedded within the concrete at a depth of roughly 10-12 cm, each exhibiting a different corrosion level represented by a progressively weaker reflectivity. Methods

The proposed Deep-MACE framework is evaluated against two established approaches: UMBIR and its multi-frequency extension MF-UMBIR, both introduced and validated in the prior work by Alanazi et al.15. To ensure a fair and direct comparison, the same parameter settings reported in their study is adopted.

The UMBIR method is a single-frequency Bayesian reconstruction algorithm that incorporates a prior model to regularize the inverse problem. MF-UMBIR extends this framework to jointly fuse measurements from multiple excitation frequencies through a linear combination strategy. While both methods provide significant improvements over classical delay-and-sum approaches, they are inherently limited in their ability to leverage more expressive priors and nonlinear multi-frequency fusion.

The proposed Deep-MACE algorithm integrates multiple physical model agents–each representing a distinct excitation frequency–with a learned prior agent encoded via a deep convolutional neural network (CNN), specifically a U-Net architecture. These agents interact within the consensus equilibrium framework described earlier. The equilibrium updates are performed using the Mann iteration scheme with a relaxation parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = 0.9$$\end{document}. To emphasize the contribution of the deep prior in guiding the reconstruction process, we set the balancing weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu = 1$$\end{document}, effectively prioritizing the learned prior during consensus formation.

This setup enables us to directly assess the contribution of deep priors and consensus equilibrium to the quality and robustness of ultrasound-based defect imaging, especially under the challenging conditions imposed by a steel liner and multi-scale scattering.

UMBIR: The conventional UMBIR reconstruction (Fig. 4b) fails to localize any of the four rebar defects embedded within the concrete. This failure can be attributed to several factors. First, the single-frequency nature of UMBIR limits its ability to capture multi-scale features; particularly when imaging through a highly reflective medium such as the steel liner. The strong reflection from the steel boundary dominates the reconstruction, suppressing subtle echoes from deeper rebar structures. Additionally, the highly attenuative nature of concrete combined with the rebar depth exacerbates this limitation, causing UMBIR to converge to a trivial solution where structural details are completely obscured.

MF-UMBIR: The MF-UMBIR results (Fig. 4c) demonstrate a clear improvement over UMBIR. The multi-frequency fusion allows the algorithm to extract more robust information from depth-varying components of the measurements. As a result, one or two of the rebar defects begin to appear in the reconstructed image. However, the recovery remains incomplete. Not all rebars are detected, and the corrosion gradient is not faithfully represented. Furthermore, the reflection from the steel liner remains highly dominant in the image, creating artifacts near the surface. These high-intensity reflections may cause the forward model agents to misallocate information, reducing the accuracy of deeper reconstructions. The method shows promise in mitigating some challenges introduced by the liner, but its fidelity is still compromised.

Deep-MACE: The Deep-MACE reconstruction (Fig. 4d) provides the clearest and most faithful image among all methods. The learned prior successfully regularizes the inversion by embedding rich contextual features into the CE framework. Three rebars are clearly identified, and the reconstructed positions align reasonably well with the ground truth in terms of depth, there is a noticeable overestimation of the lateral extent (i.e., width) of the rebar structures. This effect is attributable to the limited spatial resolution of the imaging system in the lateral direction, which is constrained by the aperture geometry and the angular sampling density. Notably, Deep-MACE effectively suppresses the surface reflection artifacts caused by the steel liner, thanks to the powerful denoising and feature-extraction capabilities of the U-Net prior. However, while the geometric structure is well-recovered, two limitations are worth noting. First, the intensities of the rebars do not accurately reflect the true corrosion levels, which suggests that the network prioritizes structural consistency over precise reflectivity. Second, the rightmost rebar is not detected and the one beofore it is slightly misaligned laterally. This misallocation may be due to residual ambiguity in the learned prior or interactions between neighboring agents in the consensus iteration. Convergence

A key strength of the proposed Deep-MACE framework is its convergence behavior, which is governed by the consensus equilibrium (CE) dynamics. Unlike traditional optimization methods that minimize a single composite cost function, CE reaches a fixed point where all agents, where each is enforcing either a data consistency or a prior constraint to agree on a common solution.

To evaluate the robustness of convergence behavior, the Deep-MACE algorithm was executed in 10 independent trials, each initialized with a different random seed. These seeds affect both the initial estimate of the acoustic reflectivity (used to start the iterative process) and the internal behavior of the deep prior (U-Net), which contains stochastic components such as batch normalization. While dropout is not used during inference in this implementation, batch normalization still introduces slight variability due to its dependence on learned statistics and runtime mode behavior.

Each trial ran for a fixed number of iterations (50), and the consensus error was recorded at each step. Figure 5 illustrates the mean convergence trajectory over all runs, along with a shaded region denoting the 95% confidence interval. As expected, the error consistently decreases over iterations, and most runs reach practical convergence (defined as consensus error below a small threshold) between 30 and 40 iterations. However, the early stages of the convergence curves exhibit notable variability, which is attributed primarily to differences in initial reconstructions and the non-deterministic response of the learned prior.

This analysis reveals several important observations. First, the choice of initial reconstruction affects not just the speed of convergence but also the path taken through the consensus equilibrium iterations. Some runs begin closer to the equilibrium point (especially when the initial MF-UMBIR estimate is closer to the denoised truth), resulting in faster stabilization. Others may initially oscillate or drift slightly due to unfavorable early priors but still recover. Second, although the U-Net operates in inference mode, batch normalization introduces slight random behavior if the training statistics are not perfectly representative of the test data. This means that even for fixed input, the output may exhibit small variations, which then propagate into the iterative consensus updates. Nevertheless, these effects are limited and do not prevent convergence. Third, the observed variance highlights the importance of convergence monitoring in real applications. While Deep-MACE is not highly sensitive to initialization or network stochasticity, practical deployments may benefit from diagnostics (e.g., tracking residuals or applying early stopping). Conclusion

This work presented Deep-MACE, a novel multi-agent reconstruction framework for one-sided ultrasound NDT of steel-lined concrete structures. The method integrates frequency-dependent physical model agents with a powerful learned deep prior, unified through the Consensus Equilibrium (CE) framework. Unlike traditional inversion strategies, which rely on global cost functions or handcrafted regularization, Deep-MACE enables modular fusion of physics-based and data-driven components, while maintaining provable convergence through fixed-point iterations.

The simulation results demonstrated the limitations of conventional UMBIR and its multi-frequency extension (MF-UMBIR) when applied to scenarios with strong acoustic impedance mismatch and complex propagation environments such as the presence of a steel liner. UMBIR failed to reveal any internal defects due to its reliance on a single frequency model and weak prior regularization. MF-UMBIR showed improvements in depth penetration and contrast but remained susceptible to masking effects from the steel surface and failed to resolve all rebar targets. In contrast, Deep-MACE provided clearer reconstruction of subsurface features, showing resilience to the steel liner’s reflection and recovering all four corroded rebars. Although the learned prior improved structural continuity and noise suppression, minor localization errors and limited specificity in corrosion quantification highlight the need for more advanced or task-specific learning strategies.

The modularity and flexibility of Deep-MACE open new possibilities for integrating richer priors such as segmentation-aware networks or temporal models. Future work will explore training on larger-scale experimental datasets, adaptive agent weighting, and real-time deployment scenarios. Overall, Deep-MACE represents a promising direction for robust, data-efficient imaging in challenging NDT environments.
