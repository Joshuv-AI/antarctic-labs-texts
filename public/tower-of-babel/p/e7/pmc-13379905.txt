Sports Cardiology Takes Center Stage at the Copa Del Mundo ‘26
Authors: Craig Basman, Jonathan H Kim
Published: 2026

This summer, billions of viewers will watch elite athletes perform at the outer limits of human cardiovascular physiology at the Fédération Internationale de Football Association (International Federation of Association Football; FIFA) World Cup (Copa Del Mundo). We, as cardiologists, will marvel at the athletics, but we also cannot help but think about the young athletes who have succumbed to sudden cardiac death during sports. Few of us soccer fans will forget Christian Eriksen's cardiac arrest during the European Championship in 2021, witnessed live by millions: the swift compressions, the shielding wall of teammates, the defibrillation that restarted both a heart and a career. Eriksen's subsequent return to elite competition with an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, including a goal scored minutes into his return to international play, reframed what survivorship and shared decision making can look like in the modern era. His story stands in hopeful contrast to that of Marc-Vivien Foé, who died on the pitch during the 2003 Confederations Cup, in an era before emergency preparedness was what it is today.

Recently, JACC: Case Reports published a second edition of sports cardiology cases.1 Numerous submissions were still under review at the time of that publication, which are now included in Part 2 of this edition, titled, “The World Cup and Sports Cardiology.” The cases collected here continue to span the breadth of contemporary sports cardiology. Readers will encounter the diagnostic dilemmas of the gray zone, where multimodality imaging, exercise testing, and genetics are marshaled to distinguish adaptation from disease. They will find cases addressing sudden cardiac arrest and its aftermath: the resuscitation, the diagnostic work-up, and the increasingly individualized conversation about return to play.

There is a reason the collapse of an athlete strikes us so deeply. Sport is our culture's celebration of the body at its best. Yet recent cases—Bronny James (basketball), Damar Hamlin (football), and Christian Eriksen (soccer)—have brought optimism because each of these sudden cardiac arrest survivors has since returned to play. The modern story of sports cardiology is, increasingly, one of redemption: of arrests survived, diagnoses refined, careers resumed, and systems that work when seconds count. But many young athletes who never made headlines have had less fortunate outcomes. We still have work to do, including optimization of best practices for screening and improvements to on-field preparedness and the chain of intervention from emergency medical services to hospital.

As the world gathers this summer to watch, we offer this issue as a reminder that the field of sports cardiology must continue to evolve.
