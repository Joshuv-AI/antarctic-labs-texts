ABSTRACT
Central Illustration

Heart failure (HF) is a deadly form of cardiovascular disease affecting more than 63 million people worldwide.1 The annual economic burden of HF was reported to exceed $30 billion U.S. dollars (USD) in 2012 in the United States alone — this is projected to increase to $70 billion USD by 2030.2 HF is a clinical syndrome defined as adverse changes in cardiac structure and function, leading to a reduced ability for the heart to fill and pump sufficient blood to effectively meet systemic demand.3 Symptoms include fatigue/weakness, shortness of breath, lower limb edema and reduced exercise tolerance.4,5 Common risk factors for developing HF include hypertension, obesity, diabetes, coronary heart disease, inflammation, and chronic pulmonary disease.3

The human heart has a substantial energy requirement, and is critically dependent on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to supply the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) required for myofibrillar contraction, ion pumping, and other functional demands.6,7 Measured noninvasively using phosphorus-31 nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, the myocardial phosphocreatine (PCr)–to–ATP ratio (PCr/ATP) is an index of energetic reserve.6,7 A low PCr/ATP is common in human HF and is a predictor of mortality, indicating a central role for metabolic alterations in the disease progression.7, 8, 9 Alterations in mitochondrial substrate metabolism, bioenergetic function, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation have all been identified as key drivers contributing to the development and progression of HF (Figure 1).10 A major source of cellular ROS formation originates from singlet electron leakage from the electron transport chain within mitochondria.11 Conventional treatments lack specificity to target the underlying disease mechanisms that induce the cardiac remodelling observed in HF.12 Thus, targeting the mitochondria has emerged as an exciting avenue as a potential therapy to treat HF.10 In this review, we discuss the intricate role of mitochondrial alterations and oxidative stress in the pathological development of HF. Overview of the cardiac phenotype of HF: clinical and preclinical contexts

Clinical presentation of HF

Current guidelines classify human HF based on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) at the time of diagnosis.13 HF has been categorized as heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction, and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (LVEF ≥50%, 41% to 49%, and ≤40%, respectively).14 In HFpEF, the predominant feature is impaired left ventricular (LV) diastolic function with impaired relaxation, leading to restrictive filling but relative preservation of contractile function.13,15,16 HFpEF is accompanied by high filling pressure leading to pressure overload,17 largely attributed to LV stiffness as a result of cardiac remodelling.13 In contrast, HFrEF is characterized by impaired LV contractile function resulting in an LVEF ≤40% and decreased systemic perfusion.18 Precursors to HFrEF include overactivation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and sympathetic nervous systems, leading to fluid retention which exacerbates hemodynamic overload.19 The overlapping phenotypes and comorbidities of the 2 major forms of HF challenge clarity regarding whether HFpEF is a distinct pathological entity or a precursor for HFrEF.20

Structural and functional changes in the failing heart

Structural alterations to the myocardium have been extensively explored in the failing heart,3 with fibrosis,21 cardiomyocyte hypertrophy,22 and apoptosis23 all observed following cardiac injury,24 hemodynamic overload, and/or in inherited cardiomyopathies.25, 26, 27 Apoptosis plays a pivotal role in initiating cardiac remodelling following tissue damage in cardiac etiologies such as myocardial infarction.28,29 Apoptosis is predominantly activated by proteolytic enzymes called caspases30 following cytochrome c release from mitochondria.31,32 The resulting loss in cardiomyocytes can trigger an inflammatory response to replace apoptotic cardiomyocytes with collagen to prevent myocardial rupture.33 However, in HF, excess collagen deposition from activated cardiac fibroblasts results in cardiac fibrosis, causing myocardial stiffness and decreased myocardial tissue compliance.34

After cardiac injury, surviving cardiomyocytes undergo hypertrophy to accommodate increased hemodynamic workload.35 This compensatory response thickens LV mass and temporarily preserves cardiac output.35 Cardiac hypertrophy is a key phenotype observed in human HF, and the main pathophysiological function is to maintain peripheral organ perfusion.36 Although this initially preserves cardiac output, recurring injury and/or chronic stress likely override this adaptive response resulting in decompensated hypertrophy, ventricular dilatation, and reduced contractile function in the failing heart.37 Further, perturbations in mitochondrial energy metabolism during compensated hypertrophy can lead to cardiomyocyte death, exacerbating pathological hypertrophy and ultimately leading to HF.37,38

Mitochondrial alterations evident in HF

Metabolic alterations are a key feature of human HF, with extensive evidence from patients and animal models supporting a role for mitochondrial alterations leading to myocardial energetic impairment.39,40 A low energetic reserve (PCr/ATP) is common in individuals with HF and strongly predicts mortality.7, 8, 9 In myocardial biopsy samples from humans with HF, low ATP was associated with diastolic dysfunction.41 Moreover, in a study of patients harboring mutations associated with familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), a low PCr/ATP preceded the development of hypertrophy, suggesting that energetic impairment can be an early pathological feature associated with the development of the disease phenotype.42

Alterations in mitochondrial biogenesis have been implicated in myocardial energy depletion and HF progression, with a focus on the transcriptional coactivator and master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC1α).43 In rats, pressure-overload hypertrophy decreased myocardial expression of PGC-1α, resulting in impaired mitochondrial oxidative capacity.44 Suppressed PGC-1α expression and/or activity has also been reported in failing human hearts45; however, a clinical study has reported decreased expression in individuals with HFrEF but not HFpEF.46 PGC-1α−/− mice exhibit impaired mitochondrial oxidative capacity and lower myocardial ATP.47 These mice show progressive decline in cardiac function47,48 and a rapid progression to HF when subjected to pressure-overload.49 Collectively, this shows the critical role of mitochondrial biogenesis in preserving myocardial energetic reserve and resilience.

Further metabolic and bioenergetic features associated with HF pathology include alterations in myocardial substrate metabolism50 and bioenergetic coupling.51 The healthy heart is metabolically flexible, with ATP requirements met through oxidation of fatty acids, glucose, ketone bodies, and lactate, depending on substrate and oxygen availability, workload, and hormonal status.52 Alterations in substrate use are commonly observed in patients and animal models of cardiac pathology, often alongside a suppression of overall oxidative capacity.52 Impaired myocardial fatty acid oxidation is a feature of advanced HF52; however, impaired glucose uptake and oxidation have also been reported, particularly in diabetic cardiomyopathy.53 Instead, a greater reliance on glycolysis and ketone oxidation is evident in end-stage HF.53 Although the nature of the metabolic alteration may depend on the specific underlying pathology and disease stage, and could be considered adaptive, a loss of metabolic flexibility may further limit the capacity of the failing heart to optimize the means of ATP production under different circumstances to meet demand. Additionally, uncoupling of substrate oxidation from ATP synthesis by the mitochondria has been implicated as a mechanism potentially impacting on myocardial energetic reserve in HF.54,55 In fact, mild uncoupling may play a protective role against oxidative stress55 and/or lipid overload56 but at the cost of a lower ATP yield per oxygen or substrate consumed.

Alterations in mitochondrial calcium handling have also been implicated in the progression of HF.57 Excess mitochondrial calcium uptake can trigger apoptosis by initiating the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, releasing cytochrome c to activate caspase and mediate cell death.58 Mitochondrial calcium overload can also lead to increased production of ROS and damage key components such as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).59

Altered mitochondrial dynamics in HF impact mitochondrial cristae and the preservation of the mitochondrial genome.60 Increased mitochondrial fusion results in accumulation of mtDNA,61 supporting maintenance of mitochondrial respiratory complexes, whereas mitochondrial fission promotes the removal of damaged components.60,62 The balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion is severely impacted in the failing heart,6 with LV biopsy specimens from individuals with HFpEF exhibiting mitochondrial fragmentation and a reduction in cristae density. These changes were even more pronounced in individuals with HFrEF,46 where increased cardiac expression of the fission regulatory protein, dynamin-related protein-1, and decreased mitochondrial complex IV activity were also observed.46 Overall, multiple aberrations in the mitochondrial phenotype are seen in the failing heart, and together these represent possible therapeutic targets.

Oxidative stress in HF — mitochondrial and nonmitochondrial mechanisms

ROS play important physiological roles in healthy tissues (Figure 1)63; however, myocardial oxidative stress can arise from an imbalance of prooxidant and antioxidant factors, resulting in oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA.64, 65, 66 Under physiological conditions, ROS play a signaling role to modulate several transcriptional factors63,67 as well as a protective antimicrobial role in phagocytosis for immune defence.68 Endogenous antioxidants maintain redox balance through the removal of excess ROS.69 In the failing myocardium, ROS production exceeds the capacity for ROS clearance via endogenous antioxidant mechanisms, leading to oxidative stress.39,70 Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidases (NOXs), xanthine oxidase (XO), nitric oxidase synthases (NOS), and mitochondria are all major sources of ROS in the failing myocardium (Figure 1).71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78

NOX2 and NOX4 are constitutively expressed NOX isoforms, with NOX1 inducible in response to pathologies such as diabetes, atherosclerosis, and myocardial infarction.79,80 NOX1 and NOX2 generate ROS, such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, and are the predominant isoforms in cardiomyocytes.81 NOX4 generates hydrogen peroxide but not superoxide.79 NOX1 and NOX2 primarily reside at the plasma membrane and can translocate into intracellular membranes,79 whereas NOX4 is mainly located at the endoplasmic reticulum, nucleus, and mitochondria.82,83 Elevated expression and activity of XO leads to overproduction of XO-derived superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, contributing to oxidative stress.84 Excessive levels of ROS can uncouple endothelial NOS (eNOS, also known as NOS3) and activate inducible NOS (iNOS, or NOS2), both of which further exacerbate oxidative stress.76,85 Oxidation and depletion of tetrahydrobiopterin, an essential cofactor for physiological eNOS activity, uncouples eNOS, causing it to generate superoxide instead of nitric oxide (NO).76,79 Activation of iNOS is detrimental under oxidative stress, as it leads to considerable overproduction of NO, which readily reacts with superoxide to form the powerful oxidant, peroxynitrite.85

Crosstalk between compartments can activate ROS-induced ROS release, whereby ROS released in the cytosol can trigger ROS production in the mitochondria, and vice versa (Figure 1).86 Mitochondrial-derived ROS molecules, particularly superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, have been implicated in the development and progression of HF.87 Singlet electrons reduce oxygen molecules, forming the highly unstable and reactive superoxide, which can damage cellular components (Figure 1).88,89 This has been observed in various animal models of HF exhibiting excessive superoxide levels, lipid peroxidation, fibrosis, apoptosis, and LV dysfunction.39,88,90 Moreover, lower mitochondrial electron transport activities were associated with overproduction of superoxide, suggesting that singlet electrons leak out rather than flowing through the respiratory chain.39,91

Because of their unstable nature, superoxide molecules are rapidly converted into other forms of ROS.92 For example, superoxide can be converted into hydrogen peroxide through a reaction mediated by superoxide dismutase (SOD), the key defense mechanism against excess superoxide.92,93 Isoforms of SOD are present in 3 distinct locations to provide antioxidant defence.93,94 SOD1 is localized in the cytoplasm and neutralizes cytosolic NOX2, NOX4 and XO-generated cellular superoxide, whereas SOD2 is present in the mitochondria and neutralizes superoxide generated from mitochondrial respiratory chain electron leakage and mitochondrial-localized NOX4.93,95 NOX2-generated extracellular superoxide is converted to hydrogen peroxide by SOD3.94 Once superoxide is oxidized into hydrogen peroxide, it can then be further converted into another reactive and unstable ROS known as hydroxyl radical, mediated by the Fenton reaction.96,97 Mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide emission has been observed in cardiac biopsy samples from individuals with HFpEF but has yet to be reported in individuals with HFrEF.98 Oxidative damage to mtDNA in LV tissues evident in human end-stage HF is accompanied by a decline in mtDNA volume and replication rate, leading to reductions in mtDNA-encoded protein expression including mitochondrial electron transfer chain complex I and III subunits.99,100 In human type 2 diabetes, LV systolic dysfunction and oxidative stress are accompanied by significantly lower levels of LV SOD.101 Deficiencies in SOD isoforms, particularly SOD2, are associated with exacerbated mitochondrial ROS production and cardiac dysfunction.102,103 In line with clinical studies, various animal models of HF have increased levels of ROS, leading to oxidative damage in the myocardium.104, 105, 106 This increased ROS generation was also associated with decreased expression of antioxidant defense enzymes SOD and glutathione peroxidase in the myocardium.107

Current therapies for the treatment of HF

Traditional HF therapies

The main objectives in the management of HF are to alleviate symptoms, improve quality of life, and reduce mortality.12 Several pharmacologic agents have served to meet these objectives, typically by reducing fluid retention and improving cardiac function.13 Vasoactive drugs that target the RAAS such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors are primarily used in the clinic to limit the symptoms of HF.108,109 However, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors are also associated with increased risk of hypotension and hyperkalemia, as reported in a recent large meta-analysis of 378 randomized controlled trials.110,111 A meta-analysis of randomized controlled HF trials of 47,662 individuals showed angiotensin II receptor blocker treatment was not found to provide significant benefits in cardiovascular mortality, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality.112 In fact, individuals with HF on angiotensin II receptor blocker therapy may also be at a greater risk of acute kidney damage.113 Similar to RAAS inhibitors, both β-adrenoreceptor antagonists (or β-blockers) and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) have also been long considered cornerstones of treatment for HF. A recent meta-analysis has suggested potential β-blocker benefits on reduced all-cause mortality; however, without reduction in rehospitalization for HF.114 Aldosterone receptor agonists and loop diuretics are used clinically to treat HF by reducing sodium and water retention.115 There is also evidence of loop diuretics being associated with an increased risk of death and hospitalization.116 MRAs are now second-line therapy (after diuretics) in the most recent guidelines for management of HF117 but are associated with side effects such as renal dysfunction and hyperkalemia (avoided by the more recently developed nonsteroidal MRA finerenone).118,119 In essence, morbidity and mortality remain high in individuals with HF despite the current medical guideline-directed traditional therapeutics,120,121 necessitating the need for precision medicine approaches for HF.

Newer, nontraditional HF therapies

Here we consider antidiabetic drugs developed over the last decade, particularly sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) As recently reviewed,122 SGLT2i are recommended therapy in the clinical management of human HF for both HFrEF and HFpEF.122,123 The mechanisms responsible for SGLT2i cardioprotection in HF remain to be resolved because of the pleiotropic (off-target) actions of this class of drug (beyond their glucose-lowering actions).124,125 Cardiac (and cardiomyocyte) SGLT2 (Slc5a2) expression is very low-negligible,126 but SGLT2i are reported to exert direct cardiac effects via inhibition of other solute carrier transporters, particularly the Na+/H+ exchanger NHE-1 (Slc9a1) and possibly also the glucose sensor sodium-myoinositol cotransporter-1 (Slc5a3).127,128 Lowering cytosolic Na+ levels via NHE-1 inhibition indirectly impacts mitochondrial Ca2+ and metabolism.125,129 There is also evidence supporting the cardioprotective effect of glucagon-like peptide-1 by decreasing blood pressure, increasing coronary blood flow, and improving LVEF.130,131 However, although these approaches reduce HF hospitalizations in human HFpEF,132,133 their benefit is predominantly evident on hospitalizations not mortality, leaving a treatment gap in the HFpEF space. Taken together, the limitations of the current treatments for HF in terms of mixed reports and outcomes suggest that these therapies may be suboptimal due to the complexity and heterogeneity of human HF. Moreover, current HF therapies were not originally designed to specifically target underlying mechanisms driving the progression of HF, which may partially explain the mixed cardiovascular outcomes.134 Therefore, developing therapies that target the specific underlying mechanisms of HF, and in particular energy metabolism, may provide advantages over current treatment strategies to take us one step closer to mechanism-guided, precision medicine for arresting HF progression.

Treating mitochondrial alterations in HF

Mitoprotective therapies in HF

Mitochondria have emerged as a promising therapeutic target because of a growing number of studies indicating a causal link between mitochondrial alterations and oxidative stress in the development and progression of HF.6,87 Antioxidant therapy has been proposed to combat ROS-induced oxidative damage in the progression of HF,135 with coenzyme Q10 and tempol under investigation in a range of cardiac diseases including HF.135,136 Hydrogen sulfide donors such as anethole dithiolethione have also gained traction as a cardioprotective agent in HF, as hydrogen sulfide levels are severely diminished in individuals with HF.137,138 Further, HF symptoms worsen as hydrogen sulfide levels progressively decline.137 Interest in mitochondrial uncoupling has recently been revitalized as a therapeutic strategy for obesity-associated HF with the emergence of HU6 (a modified form of 2,4-dinitrophenol [DNP]), which circumvents toxicity concerns surrounding unmodified DNP. Here we discuss various therapeutic avenues to correct mitochondrial alterations evident in HF.39

Coenzyme Q10

Coenzyme Q10 is a quinone antioxidant and a component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain where it plays a crucial role in transporting electrons from either mitochondrial complex I or complex II to complex III.139 Coenzyme Q10 deficiency has been described in individuals with HF, leading to respiratory impairments and increased ROS production.140,141 Thus, restoring endogenous levels of coenzyme Q10 may provide beneficial effects. Coenzyme Q10 has exhibited cardioprotective effects in several preclinical models of HF, attenuating LV diastolic dysfunction, hypertrophy, fibrosis and myocardial infarct size.142, 143, 144, 145 In clinical settings, results from the Q-SYMBIO (Coenzyme Q10 as Adjunctive Treatment of Chronic Heart Failure: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Multicentre Trial With Focus on Symptoms, Biomarker Status [Brain-Natriuretic Peptide (BNP)], and Long-term Outcome [Hospitalisations/Mortality]) trial suggested that coenzyme Q10 treatment for 106 days reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in individuals with HF.146 However, this trial did not reach the predefined 550 participant enrolment plan,146 and, of 420 individuals enrolled, only 41% reached the primary endpoint.146 A meta-analysis has reported that coenzyme Q10 supplementation in individuals with HF led to lower mortality and increased exercise capacity compared with the HF group receiving placebo.147 However, because of its hydrophobicity and large molecular weight, coenzyme Q10 exhibits poor bioavailability and is unable to passively cross the mitochondrial double membrane to sufficiently replenish endogenous levels in the mitochondria respiratory chain.148,149 Thus, large doses are required (2,400-3,000 mg/d) to achieve beneficial effects in adult human HF.150,151 Overall, supplementation of coenzyme Q10 has provided beneficial outcomes in HF; however, its inability to accumulate in the mitochondria where it is required presents important limitations.

Anethole dithiolethione

Anethole dithiolethione is a slow-release hydrogen sulfide donor that has attracted much attention in the therapeutic application of HF.152 Hydrogen sulfide is an endogenously produced gas that plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammation,153 blood pressure,154 and metabolism.155 It is widely recognized to be cardioprotective in preclinical models of HF, arrhythmia, cardiac hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and ischemic/reperfusion injury.138,156, 157, 158 However, hydrogen sulfide treatment alone is limited because of a lack of cellular targeting and short half-life in vivo (∼6 minutes in rats when administered intravenously).159,160 To improve bioavailability, anethole dithiolethione was developed, an agent that releases hydrogen sulfide at a slower rate, increasing the half-life to up to 4.4 hours in vivo compared with hydrogen sulfide treatment alone.161 Anethole dithiolethione has yet to be clinically tested in human HF; however, it has been approved for topical application by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to restore salivation and treat dry mouth with good safety and tolerability.162,163 Overall, anethole dithiolethione has been successful in exhibiting cardioprotection in preclinical settings; however, despite evidence of clinical safety, its clinical translation for targeting human HF has been lacking.

Tempol

Promising evidence of SOD therapy in preclinical models of HF164, 165, 166 has led to increased interest in expanding its therapeutic potential.167,168 SOD is an antioxidant enzyme found in the extracellular matrix, cytoplasm, and mitochondria, which functions to detoxify the highly unstable and damaging superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide.169 A prominent feature of HF is reduced levels of endogenous SOD, leading to excess levels of superoxide; thus, restoring SOD functionality may be a promising therapeutic avenue.142,170, 171, 172 Studies in human HF have reported lower SOD activity is associated with overt HF, including LV remodelling and LVEF ≤45%.171,172 SOD knockout mice are more susceptible to cardiac ischemic reperfusion injury compared with wild-type mice, confirming the key role of SOD in cardiac defence.173,174 Preclinical investigation of tempol, an SOD mimetic agent, has been promising in the space of cardiac pathology.175,176 Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α knockout mice treated with tempol exhibited improved LVEF, normalized superoxide levels, and increased endogenous antioxidant glutathione peroxidase/glutathione reductase activities.177 Oral administration of tempol for 8 weeks ameliorated LV pressure and cardiac hypertrophy, which was associated with normalized matrix metalloproteinase-2 and transforming growth factor β levels in hypertensive rats.136 Tempol reduced myocardial cell death in association with decreased markers of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in mice subjected to streptozotocin-induced HF,178 with similar benefit in insulin-resistant glucose cotransporter type 4–deficient mice in vivo.179 However, to date, little progress has been made in the clinical progression of tempol. Although tempol has not been tested in individuals with HF, it is currently under clinical investigation for vascular dysfunction (NCT03680638; NCT03680404; NCT03376555). Preclinical evidence strongly suggests that restoring physiological levels of SOD can attenuate cardiac dysfunction in HF. However, due to the lack of clinical investigation, it is still unclear if these findings can be recapitulated in human HF.

HU6 (DNP)

HU6 is a metabolic accelerator that is metabolized in the liver to DNP, a mitochondrial uncoupling protonophore, and is being investigated as a potential treatment for obesity-associated HFpEF.180 Because of its hydrophobic properties, DNP is able to freely cross the mitochondrial membrane.181,182 DNP acts to shuttle protons across the mitochondrial inner membrane independent of the F1Fo-ATP synthase, resulting in increased mitochondrial substrate use, increased whole-body energy expenditure, and enhanced weight loss.180,183 DNP has been reported to be protective in preclinical models of acute myocarditis, myocardial infarction, and obesity.184, 185, 186 Although therapeutic development declined due to toxicity concerns first reported in the late 1930s, interest has been reinvigorated, stemming from a recent safety trial.183,187 It was reported that HU6 was well tolerated in 80 participants (39 women and 41 men) taking oral daily doses of 150, 300, or 400 mg for 2 months in a phase 2a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial.187 Excitingly, the HuMAIN (Hepatic Uncoupler Mitochondrial Accelerator In HFpEF)-HFpEF phase 2 trial, designed to investigate the cardioprotective potential of HU6 in individuals with obesity-associated HFpEF,180 including examination of the effects of 134 days (oral daily dose) of HU6 treatment, evaluated for safety, tolerability, exercise capacity, weight loss, body composition, and cardiac structure/function in individuals with obesity exhibiting HFpEF.180 Due to report soon (NCT05284617), a press release has suggested that it has met its primary endpoint (weight loss) and a number of secondary endpoints around safety/efficacy as well as cardiovascular function.188 We await release of the full report, and subsequent peer-reviewed publication, of the full results dataset.

1-Deoxynojirimycin

1-Deoxynojirimycin is an iminopyranose alkaloid naturally synthesized in mulberry leaves. Although it inherently exhibits poor lipophilicity, a functional group can be linked to the N-atom of 1-deoxynojirimycin to improve its lipophilicity, enhancing its ability to cross cellular and mitochondrial membranes.189 This molecule has been identified as an effective α-glucosidase inhibitor and exhibits anti-obese and anti-hyperglycemic properties.190 1-Deoxynojirimycin has shown mitochondrial protection in an angiotensin-II–treated mouse model of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, attenuating LVEF, LV fibrosis, LV cardiomyocyte size, and restoring mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP levels.191 1-Deoxynojirimycin has also shown promise in treating mitochondrial HCM, a condition caused by mitochondrial genomic point mutations in genes such as MT-TL1, MT-RNR2, Mto1, Gtpbp3, and ELAC2.192, 193, 194, 195, 196, 197 1-Deoxynojirimycin restored mitochondrial cristae construction by promoting oligomerization via optic atrophy protein-1 in human stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes with mutant mtDNA (MT-RNR2).191

Mitochondrial-targeted therapies for the treatment of HF

The recognition of mitochondria as a pharmacological target in HF has led to the development of therapies that specifically target and accumulate in the mitochondria (Figure 2).39,198 Within the last decade, various mitochondrial-targeted therapies have been synthesized and developed both in vitro and in vivo.198 Murphy et al199 developed a method of delivering therapies into the mitochondrion by conjugating a lipophilic cation known as dodecyl-triphenylphosphonium (dTPP)to a bioactive therapy. dTPP is pulled into the mitochondria by the mitochondrial membrane potential and can accumulate up to ∼1,000-fold compared to the cytosolic concentration (Figure 2).200 Therapies that have been conjugated to dTPP include coenzyme Q10, anethole dithiolethione (hydrogen sulfide donor), tempol (SOD mimetic), methylglyoxal scavenger, and plastoquinone (Figure 2, Central Illustration).201 An alternative mitochondrial targeting therapeutic known as Szeto–Schiller 31 (SS31) is a tetrapeptide first synthesized by Schiller et al.202 This cationic agent inherently exhibits a +3 formal charge and is also pulled into the mitochondria via the charge differences created by the mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure 2).203 Here we discuss the success, challenges, and impacts of these agents in HF.Figure 2Successful and Effective Mitochondrial-Targeting Delivery Systems to Treat Mitochondrial Alterations in Heart Failure(A) Cationic peptide-based targeting strategy and (B) lipophilic cation dodecy.l-triphenylphosphonium (dTPP)–conjugated based therapies. Mitoquinone mesylate

The mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ) has exhibited promising therapeutic effects in HF. MitoQ was synthesized by conjugating the coenzyme Q10 antioxidant moiety to a mitochondrial-targeting lipophilic cation, dTPP, using a 10-carbon alkyl chain.204 The mitochondrial membrane potential allows highly positively charged cations such as dTPP to travel against its concentration gradient and accumulate in the mitochondrial matrix, thus making more efficient and selective delivery of coenzyme Q10 into the mitochondria (Central Illustration).205,206 MitoQ accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix up to 1,000-fold higher than in the cytoplasm.204,207,208 The highly positive cation also increases the bioavailability of MitoQ by reducing the hydrophobicity of the coenzyme Q10 moiety and thus circumventing the limitations of coenzyme Q10 treatment alone.207,209 MitoQ can be orally administered clinically with 10% bioavailability.208 A phase 1 clinical trial reported that a daily oral dose of MitoQ (80 mg/d) can be safely administered for up to 1 year.210 The benefits of MitoQ supplementation (20 mg/d for 4 weeks) are currently being evaluated in an ongoing clinical trial for cardiac function and mitochondrial respiration in individuals with HFpEF.211 MitoQ treatment for 6 weeks improved endothelial function and atrial stiffening by reducing mitochondria-induced oxidative stress in elderly subjects.212 It has been suggested that oral administration of MitoQ (20 mg/d for 4 weeks) improves immune cell mitochondrial function in individuals with HFpEF, albeit the work has only been published in abstract form at the time of writing.213 MitoQ has also exhibited therapeutic benefits by attenuating chronic hepatitis C–induced liver damage in a large phase 2 clinical trial214 and has been clinically tested in other oxidative stress-associated pathologies including Parkinson’s disease, peripheral artery disease (NCT03506633), and asthma (NCT04026711).210 MitoQ has also exhibited cardioprotective benefits in preclinical models of HF including improved LV contractile function and mitochondrial dynamics, blunted oxidative stress, attenuated cardiac hypertrophy, and reduced systolic blood pressure.215, 216, 217 Cardioprotection exerted by MitoQ in both clinical and preclinical settings indicates its potential as an effective therapy for individuals with HF.

10-(6'-Plastoquinonyl) decyltriphenylphosphonium

10-(6'-Plastoquinonyl) decyltriphenylphosphonium (SkQ1) is another mitochondrial-targeting dTPP-conjugated antioxidant molecule that is similar to MitoQ but incorporates a chloroplast-derived plastoquinone antioxidant moiety rather than coenzyme Q10.218 Similar in structure to coenzyme Q10, plastoquinone is localized in the plantae chloroplast and shuttles electrons in the process of photosynthesis.219 It has been proposed that plastoquinone exhibits more effective antioxidant properties than coenzyme Q10 given that the chloroplasts are exposed to significantly higher oxidative challenge compared to the mitochondria.220, 221, 222 SkQ1 acts as an ROS scavenger to reduce oxidative stress and can be regenerated at mitochondrial complex I or complex III (Central Illustration).223 One advantage of SkQ1 is a wider antioxidant action window, the concentration range in which it exerts antioxidant activity (between 0.9 and 850 nM) compared with MitoQ (between 350 and 550 nM).224 It has been proposed that SkQ1 may also prevent mitochondrial cytochrome c–mediated oxidative stress by binding to cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane to inhibit cardiolipin/cytochrome c hydrophobic interactions and reduce peroxidase activity of cytochrome c.225 The cardioprotective effects of SkQ1 have been documented in a range of experimental models of cardiac-associated diseases.226 For example, SkQ1 treatment for 20 weeks prevented cardiac hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and oxidative stress in mice fed a high-fructose diet.226 Another study reported that SkQ1 treatment (0.02-250 nM/kg/d) for 3 weeks in rats subjected to ischemia-reperfusion injury significantly reduced myocardial infarct size and cardiac arrhythmia.227 SkQ1 prevented cardiac lesions, myocardial fibrosis, and hypertrophy in aged C57BL/6 mice.228 In a mouse model of premature ageing using the PolgAmut mtDNA mutant mouse model showed that mice administered SkQ1 for 100 days showed improvements in cardiac mitochondrial ultrastructure and lived significantly longer than nontreated mice.229 A stable topical form of SkQ1, Visomitin (eye drops), was developed in 2009; the safety and efficacy has been reported in a randomized multicenter double-blind and placebo-controlled clinical study.230 Daily administration of Visomitin for 6 weeks in individuals with dry eye syndrome resulted in a significant reduction in eye dryness and blurred vision compared to placebo.230 Visomitin ophthalmic solution was approved and marketed in Russia in 2011230 and is currently approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration as an orphan drug to treat Leber hereditary optic neuropathy.231 Preclinical findings suggest that SkQ1 provides promising cardioprotective effects. The success of SkQ1 as a safe and effective therapy for dry eye syndrome and Leber hereditary optic neuropathy may pave the way for further clinical studies to investigate SkQ1 in the context of human HF.

SS31

SS31, also known as elamipretide and Bendavia, is a mitochondria-targeting and highly permeable antioxidant tetrapeptide that has been widely investigated in numerous cardiac pathologies including HF.198,232,233 As mentioned, this is a cationic compound with a strong formal charge of +3 and uses the charge differences created by the mitochondrial membrane potential to penetrate and accumulate within mitochondria (1,000- to 5,000-fold) compared to cytosolic concentrations.203 The dimethyltyrosine component of SS31 is thought to be responsible for its antioxidant effects.233 It has been reported that SS31 provides beneficial effects by stabilizing cardiolipin (and thereby the mitochondrial respiratory complexes) and blunting cytochrome-c/cardiolipin complex peroxidase activity, thus preserving electron transport chain function and ATP synthesis (Central Illustration).234,235 The beneficial effects of SS31 have been widely reported in various animal models of cardiac pathology.236, 237, 238 Longer-term treatment of SS31 over 3 months in a dog model of HF improved LV systolic function, decreased oxidative stress, and restored mitochondria bioenergetics and ATP synthesis.236 Mice subjected to angiotensin II–induced cardiomyopathy with 4 weeks of SS31 administration showed improvements in diastolic function as well as a reduction in cardiac fibrosis and hypertrophy.237 In models of ischemia/reperfusion injury, SS31 treatment decreased myocardial infarct size (by 50%), reduced myocardial lipid peroxidation, and resulted in less frequent and severe arrhythmic events.238,239 SS31 intervention in high-fat/high-fructose–fed pigs improved myocardial mitochondrial organization, mitochondria-sarcoplasmic reticulum interaction, restored nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate levels, mitochondrial complex IV activity, ATP output, mitochondrial calcium handling, and cardiolipin profile.240

However, translating the beneficial therapeutic effects of SS31 into the clinical setting has proven challenging. For example, in a multicenter phase II clinical trial, EMBRACE STEMI, individuals with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction treated with SS31 (0.05 mg/kg/h for 1 hour) exhibited no significant improvement in LV function or myocardial infarct size.241 A successful phase 1 trial showed that a 4-hour infusion of SS31 (25 mg/kg/h) improved LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volume in individuals with HF compared to placebo.242 However, these positive outcomes were not observed in a successive phase II trial where daily subcutaneous injection of SS31 (4 mg or 40 mg) in individuals with HFrEF did not significantly improve either of the primary endpoint measures mentioned above.243 SS31 treatments in these clinical trials were well tolerated and safe among HFrEF individuals.242,243 It has been proposed that these disappointing results may be due to the rapid clearance and proteolysis of the SS31 peptide, leading to a short half-life and reduced bioavailability.242 SS31 has been successful in clinical trials focusing on other diseases including adults with skeletal muscle primary mitochondrial myopathy and visual dysfunction in age-associated macular degeneration.244, 245, 246 Taken together, although the therapeutic benefits of SS31 in HF were clear in preclinical studies, further evaluation is required in clinical settings, with judicious selection of both time of onset and duration of treatment, especially in the space of cardiac pathologies.

Mito-tempol

Mito-tempol is another well-known dTPP-conjugated antioxidant which contains a mitochondrial SOD mimetic tempol as the bioactive compound (Central Illustration).218,247 Cardiac mitochondria, in particular, are a major source of superoxide, thus, specific delivery of SOD into the mitochondria has been proposed to be an effective therapeutic strategy to combat cardiac dysfunction.170,248 Mito-tempol has shown clear cardioprotective benefits in numerous preclinical studies.248,249 Obese mice exhibited improvements in diastolic dysfunction and a reduction in mitochondrial oxidative stress with Mito-tempol treatment for 8 weeks.250 Mito-tempol limited the progression of HF and reduced mortality in transgenic mice with adverse myocardial remodelling induced by Calpain overexpression.251 In guinea pigs subjected to ascending aortic constriction-induced HF, Mito-tempol improved fractional shortening and contractility and prevented sudden cardiac death, which was associated with decreased ventricular arrhythmias, mitochondrial ROS, and suppressing the HF-associated proteome/phosphoproteome.77 Mito-tempol therapy for 30 days improved cardiac function, reduced mitochondrial ROS, apoptosis and myocardial hypertrophy in db/db type 2 diabetic mice.252 Unfortunately, little progress has been made in the clinical translation of Mito-tempol. Its therapeutic potential is currently being tested in the context of microvascular endothelial function (NCT06003855). Although therapeutic targeting of mitochondrial superoxide with Mito-tempol has been successful in treating several preclinical models of HF and various other cardiac pathologies, clinical data are lacking.

10-Oxo-10-[4-(3-thioxo-3H-1,2-dithiol-5-yl) phenoxy]decyl]triphenyl-phosphonium

10-Oxo-10-[4-(3-thioxo-3H-1,2-dithiol-5-yl) phenoxy]decyl]triphenyl-phosphonium (AP39), a novel mitochondrial-targeting hydrogen sulfide donor was synthesized by conjugating the mitochondrial-targeting dTPP to anethole dithiolethione.218,253 It has been proposed that AP39 improves bioenergetics by increasing electron flow at the site of the mitochondrial respiratory chain (Central Illustration).160 Once inside the mitochondria, AP39 releases hydrogen sulfide and the sulfide-quinone oxidoreductase catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen sulfide, feeding 2 electrons into the electron transport chain.160 This has been shown in bovine hearts ex vivo where AP39 treatment increased mitochondrial complex II and complex III activity.160 A recent study has reported that AP39 attenuated doxorubicin-induced apoptosis and oxidative stress in Sprague–Dawley rats by increasing adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase phosphorylation and expression of mitochondrial uncoupling protein-2.254 AP39 treatment reduced the infarct size of mice subjected to myocardial ischemic-reperfusion injury, and this cardioprotection was proposed to be due an inhibitory effect on the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pore, resulting in increased mitochondrial calcium retention.255 Similarly, in rats subjected to myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, intravenous administration of AP39 (0.01-1 μmol/kg) before reperfusion significantly reduced infarct size in a dose-dependent manner, reduced mitochondrial ROS production, and inhibited mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening.256 In an experimental model of cardiac arrest, AP39 treatment (100 and 1,000 nmol/kg) 2 minutes before cardiopulmonary resuscitation improved neurological function, reduced neurological degeneration, inhibited mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening, and blunted ROS production.257 Moreover, AP39 treatment protected cardiac tissue when subjected to prolonged ischemia in a mouse model of a heart transplant.258 Although the cardioprotective action of AP39 has been reported in numerous preclinical studies, these findings have yet to be clinically translated.

MitoGamide

MitoGamide (represented in the Central Illustration) is a novel methylglyoxal scavenging agent that is conjugated to dTPP and was developed as an analogue of MitoG, a compound previously used to measure the levels of mitochondrial methylglyoxal.259 Produced as a by-product of glycolysis, the overproduction of methylglyoxal (a highly reactive carbonyl species) found in the failing heart can react with and damage nucleic acids, lipids, and proteins leading to the formation of advanced glycation end-products.260,261 Both methylglyoxal and advanced glycation end-products have been implicated in several pathologies including neurodegenerative diseases, ageing, diabetes, and HF.261,262 Pilot data has shown the cardioprotective properties of MitoGamide in the diabetic heart.259 Moreover, T1D Akita mice treated with MitoGamide for 10 weeks showed improvements in several markers of diastolic dysfunction including E/è and è/à ratio.259 Further work has confirmed that MitoGamide treatment for 12 weeks significantly improved E/A ratio, a marker of diastolic dysfunction, in a mouse model of HFpEF.263 However, the lack of advanced glycation end-product accumulation in this and other models of HF, such as db/db mice, presents a challenge when assessing the underlying cardioprotective mechanism of MitoGamide in HF.263,264 Preclinical investigation of MitoGamide was also performed in a mouse model of diabetic kidney disease; however, no improvements were observed in renal function or renal mitochondrial respiration.265 Overall, MitoGamide has shown strong therapeutic potential to improve cardiac function in preclinical models of HF despite the lack of mechanistic insights underlying the benefits.259,263

Promising mitochondrial targets for HF therapy on the horizon

Mitochondrial gene editing and replacement therapies

Genetic mitochondrial cardiomyopathy, a condition affecting myocardial structure and function resulting from mutations in cardiac mtDNA, can lead to hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, and HF.266,267 Traditional management strategies include supplementation with coenzyme Q10, thiamine, folate, and vitamins C and E (which only offer modest and incomplete protection).268 However, there have been recent advances in mtDNA base editing technology as a therapeutic for mtDNA-associated multisystemic disorders, including cardiomyopathy. A groundbreaking genome engineering method developed in 2020 enabled a targeted base conversion of cytosine to thymine in mtDNA via double-stranded DNA deaminase toxin A, an enzyme guided by transcription activator-like effector proteins.269,270 Mitochondrial transplant and replacement approaches are also in development. This has been most promising in the space of cardiac-related disorders, improving cardiac function in Zucker diabetic fatty rats,271 sepsis-induced rats,272 and several models of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity.273, 274, 275 Replacing defective mitochondria carrying mtDNA mutations by transferring mitochondria from healthy to diseased cells in cardiac-mitochondria–associated disorders, including dilated cardiomyopathy, HCM, and cardiac conduction defects, has also been proposed as a promising therapeutic strategy.276, 277, 278, 279 However, further studies are required to confirm its therapeutic benefits.

Mitochondrial quality control targets

Therapeutically targeting quality control mechanisms to preserve mitochondrial structure and function may also offer potential utility in HF.280 Developments in the study of cardiac mitochondria have emphasized the importance of cardiac mitochondrial quality control to maintain bioenergetic output and contractile function.280,281 Targeting alterations in mitochondrial regulation and dynamics exhibited in HF including proteostasis282 and mitophagy283 as well as fusion and fission mediators dynamin-related protein-1,46 Fission-1,284 Mitofusin-2,285 and Optic Atrophy-1,286 may offer the most benefits to preserve cardiac bioenergetic and contractile function. These leads provide promising therapeutic applications against HF but are still in development.

Conclusions and perspectives

The global economic burden, hospitalization and mortality of HF have exponentially grown proportional to the increase in the aging population.287 The current therapeutic focus is to alleviate the symptoms of HF by reducing the cardiac workload rather than correcting the pathophysiological mechanisms.13 The recognition of mitochondrial alterations and oxidative stress as key pathological driving forces has led to an increased interest in mitochondria as a therapeutic target to treat HF.288 This targeted strategy to reverse the pathological development of HF is proposed to restore cardiac bioenergetics in the failing heart and offers considerable potential to attenuate cardiac dysfunction, improve quality of life, and increase survival among individuals with HF.10,288

The therapies considered here in the context of HF have various targets in the mitochondria, including ROS scavenging, cardiolipin stabilizing, and electron supplementing.160,198 The development of mitochondrial-targeted therapies in HF is still in its infancy despite substantial understanding of mitochondrial alterations in HF.289 Despite evidence of preclinical success, clinical translation has been lacking. Preclinical models of HF do not capture the full complexity of human HF. In this regard, therapeutically targeting the mitochondria alone may not effectively address all aspects of the condition. Another consideration is potential therapeutic intervention discrepancies between preclinical models and human HF. Timing is crucial as these mitochondrial-targeted therapies may be more appropriate for various stages/subcategories of HF, which can be intimately linked with the age of individuals with HF. Careful consideration to correctly align the treatment timeline reported in preclinical models of HF into the clinical settings may assist in designing appropriate clinical trials.

Given that there are multiple mitochondrial aberrations exhibited in HF,288 is targeting one aspect sufficient to attenuate the condition clinically? Perhaps a more holistic approach in exploring a combination therapy to correct multiple mitochondrial targets in HF (alone or on top of standard care) may yield better outcomes.165 The clear associations between myocardial energetic impairment, function, and outcome in the context of HF, as well as evidence for altered redox homeostasis, make mitochondria appealing targets for novel therapies. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that mitochondrial functions extend well beyond ATP synthesis and therefore recognize a modern framework that integrates mitochondrial physiology within the wider cellular context.290 The concept of mitochondrial dysfunction itself is poorly defined, and frequently misused in reference to mitochondrial alterations that may in fact be adaptive in response to circumstance. Mitochondria are dynamic organelles that undergo changes in morphology within a reticular network, and in turn interact with other cellular components, including the endoplasmic reticulum. Moreover, mitochondria act as signaling hubs, interacting with cellular and nuclear pathways (eg, AMP-activated protein kinase and hypoxia-inducible factor signaling), regulating apoptosis, and playing key roles in intracellular ion homeostasis, which are intertwined with mechanisms of energy transduction. There is also significant crosstalk between pathways of mitochondrial and cytosolic ROS production. This broader perspective need not dampen enthusiasm for targeted mitochondrial approaches, which are still in their infancy, but could instead accelerate the translation of such approaches by recognizing the complexity of how such strategies act within the wider cellular context, and alongside existing, successful therapeutic approaches, particularly SGLT2i (whose mechanisms of action in HF remain unresolved despite their clear benefits in both major HF phenotypes).

Any new therapy developed for HF must improve on current clinical options, including both traditional therapies outlined earlier (under the heading Traditional HF Therapies) as well as SGLT2i. Given that 5-year survival in HFrEF remains high, and that no current therapies reduce mortality in HFpEF, optimal pharmacotherapy for human HF in all its remains one of the greatest unmet needs in medicine.291 We propose a call to action for accelerated translation of mitochondrial-targeted therapies for HF, both alone or in combination with current therapeutic options, including SGLT2i. The evolution of the concept of precision medicine for HF may incorporate a further step away from a “one size fits most” approach to incorporating newer, mitoprotective approaches and/or combination therapies to take us a step closer to mechanism-guided, precision medicine for arresting HF progression, particularly in settings associated with cardiac mitochondrial alterations.
