Safety and Effectiveness of Pain Medications for Migraine Management During Pregnancy and Lactation
Authors: Liza Smirnoff, Nicolas Garzon Mancera, Tayina Hyppolite, Daniela Lozano, Alexandra Cocores
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The management of migraine during pregnancy and lactation poses a major challenge owing to the paucity of safety data. While very little safety data are available for new targeted migraine therapies such as calcitonin gene receptor protein (CGRP) blocking medications, including the CGRP monoclonal antibodies and gepants, well-established migraine therapies such as topiramate, valproate, and dihydroergotamine have known risk for teratogenicity. Likewise, the safety evidence for commonly used therapies for migraine in pregnancy such as propranolol and verapamil is of low quality, and the level of evidence for efficacy of verapamil is limited. Meanwhile women experience migraine at the highest rates during their reproductive years necessitating an effective and balanced approach that considers maternal and fetal risk as well as optimal benefit to the patient to ensure adequate and appropriate treatment during pregnancy and lactation. The purpose of this review is to provide an overview of the available data on the safety of available and efficacious medications in the management of migraine during pregnancy and lactation. Based on more recent studies, there is increased evidence for use of more specific and efficacious medications for migraine, such as onabotulinumtoxinA, local nerve blocks with lidocaine, and triptans during pregnancy and lactation.

Introduction

Migraine is a common primary headache disorder characterized according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), by recurrent episodes of headache lasting 4–72 hours [1]. Attacks are typically unilateral and pulsating in quality, of moderate-to-severe intensity, and are aggravated by routine physical activity. Migraine headaches are frequently associated with nausea and/or photophobia and phonophobia. Migraine episodes can be preceded by transient, fully reversible focal neurological symptoms, which are called auras [1].

Migraine disproportionately affects women, particularly during their reproductive years, with higher prevalence in the third and fourth decades of life [2–5]. Pregnancy is often associated with improvement in migraine symptoms, with studies reporting remission or significant improvement in up to 90% of pregnant individuals [6, 7]. However, women with migraine with aura are less likely to experience symptom improvement [8]. Additionally, a retrospective study showed that women who continue to have headaches beyond the first trimester are unlikely to improve later in pregnancy [4].

Consistent with clinical improvement, migraine medication use has been shown to decrease to approximately a third during the first trimester compared with the 3 months prior to pregnancy [9]. This reduction is thought to be related, at least in part, to rising estrogen levels in pregnancy [4]. In contrast, the postpartum period has been shown to be associated with migraine exacerbation which occurs around 3–6 days postpartum as the estrogen levels fall. This worsening can potentially be owed to other factors postpartum such as poor sleep, diet, and stress in addition to hormonal reduction [10, 11]. Some studies have shown that even more than half of their subjects will present with worsening migraine frequency after the first month of childbirth in comparison with their pregnancy state [6]. Poorly controlled migraine has been associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and higher risk of preterm delivery [12–14]. Migraine is also a risk factor for the development of cerebral venous thrombosis, a condition that occurs more commonly in pregnancy [15]. Meanwhile, given significant known disability burden of migraine itself during pregnancy, achieving adequate migraine control during pregnancy is essential, while carefully balancing maternal benefits against potential fetal risks. The aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive overview of medications that have known evidence in the management of migraine, and the known data surrounding their safety when used during pregnancy and lactation, to enable clinicians to make evidence-based decisions during this critical period in a woman’s life.

Literature Search and Article Scope

We conducted a review of the literature using PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and the University of Miami Calder Library to identify primary research articles and guidelines pertaining to the management of migraine throughout pregnancy and lactation. The National Institute of Health (NIH) Drugs and Lactation Database (LactMed) and Mother to Baby repositories were also reviewed for each medication. Articles were selected for inclusion based on their quality. It is noted throughout the manuscript where certain medications or interventions have limited evidence, and where large-scale data sets exist. Unfortunately, given the ethical considerations for treatment of pregnant patients, few high-quality studies such as randomized controlled trials exist, so our data largely reflects high quality systematic reviews, and other large scale prospective and cross-sectional studies, with focus on more recent literature utilizing studies conducted in the previous 10–15 years when possible. For lactation data, pharmacokinetic studies were included. Medications included in this review include those currently recommended for the management of migraine by the American Academy of Neurology (AAN), the American Headache Society (AHS), and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and available to the US market.

Abortive Migraine Medications

Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen (paracetamol) has traditionally been considered safe to use in all stages of pregnancy and is consistently considered a first line and “preferred” option for acute treatment of migraine in pregnancy. According to the AAN level of evidence guideline, acetaminophen is a “more safe” option in pregnancy [16]. The ACOG 2022 clinical practice guideline likewise recommends acetaminophen 1000 mg orally as initial therapy for the treatment of acute migraine in pregnancy, ranked as a “strong recommendation” with “very low-quality evidence.” [17]. Notably, there is risk of maternal and fetal hepatic toxicity with overdosage so caution should be taken when explaining dosing to patients. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends a maximum daily dosage of 4 g while some manufacturers recommend a lower 24-h maximum exposure of 3–3.25 g of acetaminophen during pregnancy [17].

The use of acetaminophen in pregnancy has been studied extensively, and the overall evidence is reassuring. A 2021 summary of evidence and consensus statement and the 2020 Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality systematic review raised concerns about its use and called for precautionary action [18, 19]. Several studies have likewise raised concern about adverse neurodevelopmental effects [20]. However, the two highest qualities studies on the subject represent the most reassuring and clinically useful evidence we have to date: they both found no significant associations between use of acetaminophen during pregnancy and children’s risk of autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), or intellectual disability [21, 22]. Evaluations by pharmacovigilance teams and multiple professional organizations conclude insufficient evidence to support the association between prenatal exposure and subsequent negative neurodevelopmental outcomes [23].

The Society for Maternal and Fetal Medicine in 2017, ACOG, and the US FDA continue to consider acetaminophen a reasonable and appropriate medication choice for the treatment of migraine pain in pregnancy [24, 25]. As is the case with all acute medications during pregnancy, acetaminophen should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible duration to minimize any possible risk [26].

In terms of lactation, the AAN suggests that acetaminophen is a “more safe” option and “moderately effective.” [16]. Hales risk category classifies the medication as L1 (compatible); with rare adverse events [27]. One study concluded that an infant who ingested 90 mL of breastmilk every 3 h would receive an average of 0.14% (0.04–0.23%) of the mother's absolute dosage. Using data from this study, an infant would receive a maximum of about 2% of the maternal weight-adjusted dosage [28].

Triptans

Triptans are serotonin 5 HT1B/1D agonists with known long-standing efficacy in the management of migraine [29]. The use of triptans during pregnancy has traditionally been approached with caution due to concerns regarding their potential effects on placental circulation because of their vasoconstrictive properties [30].

Studies have shown a marked decrease in the use of triptans during pregnancy in comparison with antepartum, likely owing to a combination of improving migraine during pregnancy, as well as patients switching to less effective, but more well-established medications [31, 32]. While this substitution may feel safer, it can lead to poorly controlled migraine, which can be associated with increased migraine associated disability [12–14]. The ACOG guidelines recommend sumatriptan and other triptans as second line options for pregnant individuals with persistent migraine that is not controlled by first line agents such as acetaminophen. They report it as a conditional recommendation with very low quality evidence [17]. Large registries and cohort studies have shown that the risk for major congenital malformations with triptan use were similar to the general population [32–36]. It was also seen that low birth weight has not been associated with triptan use in pregnancy [33, 36]. Beyond congenital outcomes, previous research demonstrated a possible greater risk of externalizing-type behavior such as emotionality and activity, but this study only evaluated infants until the age of 36 months [37]. More recent studies have shown no significant increase in neurodevelopmental outcomes in children that were exposed prenatally to triptans of different doses and duration [38, 39].

In the setting of lactation, ACOG suggests that triptans may be used, but advised that a shared decision-making model is needed in considering avoiding breastfeeding for a specified timeframe after their use [17]. Specifically, ACOG recommends avoiding breastfeeding for 8–12 h after the use of sumatriptan [40, 41]. Meanwhile, a previous study demonstrated low levels of triptan excretion into breast milk, with mean relative infant dose values ranging from 0.6–0.7% for eletriptan and sumatriptan to 5.0% for naratriptan [2].

Dihydroergotamine

Dihydroergotamine (DHE) was one of the first medications developed for the acute management of migraine, and its use has persisted due to efficacy both at the beginning and later in the course of the migraine in contrast to other commonly used migraine medications [42]. However, DHE products have significant effects on uterine contractility, making them incompatible with pregnancy. Known DHE use during pregnancy has resulted in increased rates of prematurity, low birth weight, atrial septal defects, and potential increased risk of spontaneous abortion, although studies did not show increased risk of major congenital abnormalities [33, 36]. The 2022 ACOG guideline on pregnancy and lactation recommended avoiding use of DHE during pregnancy given the associated risks of inducing uterine contractions; it should not be recommended, especially during the first trimester [17].

Although no studies have been done to assess the passage of DHE into breastmilk, due to hypothetical risks of reducing milk supply and potential risks to the infant, DHE is not recommended during breastfeeding. The manufacturer recommends disposing of any milk produced during and for 3 days after nasal DHE administration [43].

Gepants

The safety profile of gepants for abortive migraine management during pregnancy and lactation remains largely unknown owing to insufficient clinical data available on their safety in these populations, necessitating careful risk–benefit assessment in this population. The currently approved gepants for acute treatment include ubrogepant, rimegepant, and zavegepant, with rimegepant also holding dual indication for prevention. Pregnancy data are largely absent; however, the FDA labels for all three medications recommend against their use in pregnancy based on animal data and theoretical concern of CGRP blockade in placental development [44–46]. Notably, there are ongoing pharmaceutical company sponsored studies to evaluate the safety of ubrogepant and rimegepant during pregnancy and infant outcomes through 12 months of age [47].

Data on use in lactation is more reassuring. With regard to rimegepant, the mean estimated relative infant dose was 0.51%, indicating that oral administration of rimegepant 75 mg to lactating women results in a < 1% RID in human milk [48]. The review from LactMed recommended pausing breastfeeding for 8–12 h following a dose [49]. With regard to ubrogepant, the mean relative infant dose was 0.15% after 24 h [50]. A recently published phase one study on zavegepant during lactation supported its safety in lactation given very low infant exposure with an relative infant dose (RID) of 0.04% [51].

NSAIDs

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are commonly used in the management of migraine. However, NSAID exposure during the first trimester has been associated with increased risk of miscarriage and developmental defects [26, 31, 52–56]. Meanwhile, the use of NSAIDs in the third trimester has been associated with premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, neonatal intraventricular hemorrhage, impaired renal function, persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn, necrotizing enterocolitis, and cerebral palsy [57]. Therefore, ACOG reports that use of NSAIDs is considered safe only during the second trimester [26]. Among NSAIDs, indomethacin has been associated with a comparatively higher risk of fetal ductal constriction and oligohydramnios, particularly with prolonged use or exposure later in gestation, and therefore warrants greater caution relative to other agents [26, 58]. Aspirin use during pregnancy has been associated with an increased risk of fetal/neonatal hemorrhages and increased intrauterine growth restriction with medium-to-high doses; however, the use of low-dose aspirin in pregnancy may be medically indicated for the treatment of other conditions during pregnancy such as preeclampsia prevention [26, 59].

During lactation, NSAIDs are regarded as acceptable for use and ibuprofen is generally preferred, since it has been shown to be undetectable in breast milk with no neonatal side effects [60–62]. Pharmacokinetic studies demonstrate that low-dose aspirin undergoes rapid hydrolysis, resulting in undetectable or minimal concentrations in breast milk, suggesting limited exposure to the nursing infant [63, 64]. However, there have been reports of thrombocytopenic purpura in infants who are exposed to aspirin via breast milk so caution is advised for using aspirin during lactation [57].

Dopamine Antagonists

Dopamine antagonists such as metoclopramide, promethazine, and prochlorperazine have been shown to be efficacious not only in the management of nausea associated with migraine, but head pain as well [65]. In a registry-based cohort study, metoclopramide use in pregnancy was not associated with increased risk of major congenital malformations overall, in any of the 20 individual malformation categories assessed, spontaneous abortion, or stillbirth [66]. Because evidence for metoclopramide use is more robust, other dopamine agonists are considered second-line [26]. Prochlorperazine, based on expert opinion, is considered “moderately safe” and “moderately effective” with no known risks aside from neonatal extrapyramidal symptoms [17]. Promethazine, which is commonly used to treat hyperemesis gravidarum in pregnancy, has not been shown to cause teratogenic effects in animals at 2–4x the toxic concentration in humans [67, 68]. There is recommended caution with use of dopamine antagonists around delivery given possibility for infant jaundice, platelet aggregation, reflex changes, withdrawal, extrapyramidal signs, and methemoglobinemia in neonates [26, 69].

Data during lactation for promethazine and prochlorperazine are based on other phenothiazides which suggest little risk to the breastfed infant [70, 71]. Excretion of metoclopramide is inconsistent with infant serum levels, ranging from 10% of the maternal weight-adjusted dosage to pharmacologically active serum levels, with potential risk for intestinal discomfort [72]. It is notable that promethazine decreased prolactin secretion, while metoclopramide and prochlorperazine increased prolactin levels, which may actually improve lactation.

Opioids and Barbiturates

Opioids are not recommended by the AHS in the treatment of migraine, except in refractory headache in women for whom standard first-line and second-line treatment are ineffective or contraindicated [73]. The ACOG clinical practice guideline also recommends against the use of opioids to treat migraine in pregnancy [17]. Opioid use during pregnancy is not only associated with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome but also with cognitive and behavioral neurodevelopmental effects [74].

Similar to opioids, barbiturates carry their own set of risks and therefore, their use is discouraged during pregnancy and lactation. Butalbital in particular is a barbiturate commonly found in combination medications with caffeine and analgesics approved in the USA for treatment of tension-type headaches [17, 75]. However, even outside of pregnancy, it is no longer recommended as a first-line treatment given risks of dependence and tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, medication overuse, headache, and risk of conversion to chronic migraine even with limited use [76, 77]. Because of the absence of significant benefit and documented associated risks, many countries have banned butalbital-containing drugs. ACOG does not recommend its use during pregnancy [17]. In the National Birth Defects Prevention Study, use of butalbital-containing products during pregnancy was linked to statistically significant increases in congenital heart defects (tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary valve stenosis, secundum-type atrial septal defect) [75].

In lactation, it is important to note that opioids can diffuse into breast milk and thus pose risk for adverse effects in the breast fed infant, mainly sedation, which can be life-threatening and lead to respiratory depression [78]. Thus, opioid use during breastfeeding should be avoided, and if necessary, proceeded with extreme caution, close monitoring, and adherence to specific guidelines for dosing and frequency that is considered safe. Butalbital can also pass into breast milk and lead to sedation in the nursing infant, and therefore should be avoided [79].

Lidocaine

There is growing evidence for the safety and efficacy of occipital nerve blocks during pregnancy, which can be used in situations of status migrainosus or as an intermittent treatment for periods of significant worsening during pregnancy in patients who may not be appropriate for preventive therapy, or when there is concern that preventive therapy may take too long to reach efficacy [80, 81]. In pregnancy, lidocaine, without the concomitant use of steroids, is the preferred agent of use for nerve blocks over other anesthetic agents given its safety profile, that confers no known increased risk for spontaneous abortion, or major congenital defects [82]. While data on noninvasive sphenopalatine ganglion blocks in migraine are more limited, the known safety of lidocaine combined with topical administration also suggests an additional treatment strategy in pregnancy [83].

In lactation, the data are likewise reassuring, with known poor absorption by the infant even in cases of large intravenous administration of lidocaine to the mother [84].

Preventive Migraine Medications

Beta Blockers

Beta blockers may be considered for use of migraine prevention during pregnancy, based on long-term data of use of beta blockers for other indications in pregnancy, and known level of efficacy for migraine treatment. Beta blockers are among the most commonly used antihypertensive agents during pregnancy for blood pressure control: observational studies show some concern with intrauterine growth restriction, neonatal bradycardia, hypoglycemia, and possible metacystic renal dysplasia risk, although a large population-based study found use was not associated with a higher risk of spontaneous abortion or major congenital anomalies in the offspring [85–88]. For their use in migraine, the ACOG clinical practice guideline recommends a shared-decision discussion balancing the benefits and potential risks [17]. In another review, expert neurologic opinion considered propranolol “more safe” and “most effective” for migraine prevention in pregnancy [16]. The International Headache Society (IHS) Global Practice Recommendations consider propranolol “the best oral option compared with others in terms of safety.” They recommend propranolol can be used throughout pregnancy until the last part of the third trimester to avoid fetal and neonatal bradycardia and hypoglycemia [89].

Propranolol and metoprolol are rated L2 (probably compatible) according to the Hale’s Lactation Categories [90]. At the very low levels passed into breastmilk, exposure to the infant is low and typically not expected to cause adverse effects. A prospective real-life survey study found no serious neonatal adverse effects and infrequent cases of reversible bradycardia or hypoglycemia [91]. Overall, infants should be monitored for symptoms of beta blockade including bradycardia and hypoglycemia [92].

Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors and Angiotensin Receptor Blockers

Both angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors (such as lisinopril) and angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) medications (such as candesartan) should be avoided during pregnancy. These classes of medications were reported among the “least safe options” in a large review [16]. Major congenital malformations, cardiovascular malformations, oligohydramnios, and stillbirth may occur [93]. For this reason, it is recommended to stop ACE-I and ARB prior to conception [17, 89].

In terms of lactation, both lisinopril and candesartan are rated per Hale’s Lactation Category as L3 (no data—presumed compatible) [90]. Only low levels of lisinopril pass into breastmilk; therefore, amounts ingested by an infant are small and are unlikely to cause adverse effects. Similarly, candesartan is barely detectable in the plasma of breastfed infants, due to poor passage into breastmilk [94]. The IHS practice recommendations cite use of candesartan and lisinopril can be considered while monitoring side effects [89].

Calcium Channel Blockers

Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) may be considered for migraine prevention during pregnancy. The ACOG clinical practice guidelines consider this class of medications as “first line,” noting no potential fetal or maternal associated risks [17]. However, their efficacy in migraine prevention is generally considered to be low-to-moderate, limiting the potential benefit of this class of medications. For this reason, expert neurologic opinion considered CCBs “least effective” in review of options during pregnancy [16]. Multiple systematic reviews found no increased risk of maternal (low strength of evidence) or fetal/child adverse effects (low strength of evidence) [19, 95]. Of note, verapamil may carry risk of fetal bradycardia, heart block, and hypotension [26, 96].

In lactation, both amlodipine and verapamil are rated per Hale’s Lactation Category as L2 (probably compatible) [90]. Even at higher doses, verapamil only passes to breast milk in low levels, and would not be expected to cause any adverse effects in the breastfed infant [97]. Similarly, limited information indicates limited passage of amlodipine in breastmilk, and plasma levels in breastfed infants are undetectable; no adverse effects have been reported [98].

Antiseizure Medications

The safety profile of antiseizure medications (ASMs) for migraine prevention during pregnancy and lactation is characterized by significant known teratogenic and neurodevelopmental risk, limiting options despite their noted efficacy [17]. Topiramate is associated with spontaneous abortion, fetal growth restriction, cleft lip or palate deformities, autism, and other major congenital anomalies. Valproic acid likewise has been associated with fetal death, neural tube defects, craniofacial defects, spontaneous abortion, major congenital malformations, cleft lip or palate deformities, developmental delays, autism and dyspraxia, with risk increased when used in the first trimester [99]. Both carry the recommendation of “not recommended in pregnancy” by ACOG [17, 100]. Given the significant known risk of teratogenicity of valproate in pregnancy, extensive counseling should be done with women and girls of child-bearing potential regarding use of, and adherence to, effective birth control methods prior to prescribing. Gabapentin has low level of efficacy evidence for the treatment of migraine and is associated with congenital cardiovascular anomalies, hypospadias, and psychomotor developmental delay and carries the recommendation of “not recommended for episodic headache prevention” from ACOG [101, 102]. Lamotrigine likewise has low efficacy data and has been associated with dose-dependent cardiac abnormalities plus autism/dyspraxia and carries the recommendation of “balance risks and benefits” per ACOG guidelines [100, 103].

In lactation, topiramate is considered Level 3 (L3), given association with infant watery diarrhea in one case report, though maternal doses of up to 200 mg daily are thought to produce low levels in infant serum [104, 105]. Valproic acid is L4, potentially hazardous, with studies suggesting the need to monitor infants for jaundice, hepatotoxicity, and hematologic abnormalities, although levels in breastmilk are low and infant serum levels range from undetectable to low [99, 106]. Gabapentin is L2, probably compatible, with suggested need to monitor infants for sedation and poor feeding [26, 107].

Antidepressants

Antidepressants with known evidence for efficacy in migraine prevention include tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) such as amitriptyline and serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) such as venlafaxine or duloxetine, while no such evidence exists for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) [102]. Therefore, ACOG states that SSRIs are not recommended for the prevention of primary headaches in pregnancy [17]. Venlafaxine has been shown to have comparable efficacy in the treatment of migraine to amitriptyline [108]. Although there is moderate evidence showing that venlafaxine can increase the risk of preterm birth, other studies have reported no increased risk of spontaneous abortion with use of venlafaxine or duloxetine [17, 95, 109]. Meanwhile, TCAs may be linked to an increased risk of major congenital and cardiovascular anomalies, small-for-gestational-age infants, neonatal seizures, and respiratory distress, though they do not appear to increase the risk of low birth weight [110]. Amitriptyline and nortriptyline have been associated with potential neonatal withdrawal symptoms in the third trimester [111]. ACOG recommends balancing the risks and benefits of using both SNRIs and TCAs during pregnancy [17].

In terms of lactation, most antidepressants are transferred into breast milk in minimal or negligible amounts. Amitriptyline and nortriptyline for one are rated L2 (probably compatible) per Hale’s lactation risk categories, with amitriptyline having the most established safety profile [90]. In another study, amitriptyline levels were undetectable in infants exposed to amitriptyline through lactation [112]. Venlafaxine was detectable in infants’ serum, with infants receiving an average of 5.2% of the maternal dosage; however, no clear adverse effects have been reported [113]. Duloxetine was present in infant serum at 0.14–0.25% of the weight-adjusted maternal dosage, with only known side effect of potential somnolence in one infant [114]. It was additionally noted that serotonin reuptake inhibitors may delay milk secretory activation with a small potential delay to breastfeeding [113, 114].

CGRP Inhibiting Medications

Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeting therapies—monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab) and small-molecule gepants (rimegepant, atogepant)—are migraine specific therapies that have been shown to have notable efficacy in the management of migraine [115]. However, given their novelty, they lack robust human pregnancy and lactation safety data, so current guidance advises avoidance during pregnancy and cautious, individualized consideration in lactation [101]. The ACOG guidelines recommend discontinuing CGRP monoclonal antibodies 6 months before planned conception given their long half-lives and theoretical concern for adverse effects on feto-placental growth and development. Although animal studies have not shown teratogenicity of the CGRP monoclonal antibodies, there is some concern in terms of safety given CGRP’s role in the normal development of the placenta, with CGRP blockade potentially contributing to the development of preeclampsia [16, 116]. Thus far, assessments of the pharmacovigilance databases on the above-mentioned class of monoclonal antibodies has not shown signals of fetal or maternal toxicity, or increased rates of spontaneous abortion [117, 118]. Data on gepant safety in pregnancy is further discussed in the abortive medications section. Overall, further data are needed to fully evaluate the safety of this class of medications during pregnancy, and clinical studies to evaluate the safety of erenumab, galcanezumab, and atogepant are ongoing by their parent pharmaceutical companies [47]. In the meantime, given the pathophysiological risks, this class should be avoided in pregnancy [119].

Owing to their large size, it is thought that the monoclonal antibodies minimally pass into breast milk, with likely minimal subsequent absorption, lowering concern that they can be passed through lactation, although no formal studies dedicated to their safety during lactation exist, and they do not currently hold a Hale’s safety classification due to their novelty [16]. In terms of gepant use in lactation, levels of atogepant in breastmilk are low and almost absent from milk at 16 h after a dose [120]. Rimegepant safety during lactation is discussed in the abortive gepant medications section of this review.

OnabotulinumtoxinA

OnabotulinumtoxinA currently has several FDA-approved indications, including treatment of chronic migraine. There are no published clinical trials evaluating its use in pregnant or lactating women; however, evidence is available from observational studies and case series. A comprehensive 29-year analysis of the Allergan Global Safety Database reviewed outcomes in 397 pregnancies with onabotulinumtoxinA exposure across all indications and found no increased risk of major congenital malformations or spontaneous abortion [121]. Furthermore, a prospective observational cohort study of 126 pregnant women with chronic migraine found that continuation of onabotulinumtoxinA in 97 of the participants was associated with sustained migraine control and no observed adverse fetal outcomes [122]. While the FDA labeling suggests that despite local administration, the rare spread of toxin beyond the injection site is possible, a recent narrative review looking at human data from available case reports and observational studies also suggests that onabotulinumtoxinA use during pregnancy at typical therapeutic doses does not appear to increase the risk of major fetal malformations or adverse outcomes [123, 124].

In terms of lactation, the large molecular size of onabotulinumtoxinA and its mechanism of action suggest that it does not transfer into breast milk [123]. The single prospective observational cohort study by Wong and colleagues also analyzed 94 women treated with onabotulinumtoxinA while breastfeeding and found no adverse effects in the infants over the first year of life. This is further supported by case reports of mothers affected by botulism poisoning breastfeeding their infants, with no evidence of symptoms in the infants despite the high levels of maternal serum botulinum toxins [125, 126]. Given the limited but generally reassuring data available, the use of onabotulinumtoxinA during pregnancy and lactation should involve a thorough discussion with the patient regarding the absence of high-quality evidence and limited data currently available and weighed against its notable benefit in the management of chronic migraine.

Memantine

Memantine is a noncompetitive glutaminergic NMDA receptor antagonist and is currently FDA approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe Alzheimer’s dementia [127]. It has also been studied as a potential preventive treatment for migraine. A 2024 systematic review and network meta-analysis found that memantine demonstrated the second-largest reduction in migraine days and the highest 50% response rates among 15 other commonly used interventions, although prospective studies evaluating evidence are limited [128]. However there are no published studies evaluating maternal or fetal outcomes from use of memantine during pregnancy. It is therefore not currently recommended by the most recent ACOG guidelines [17]. Animal studies have shown decreased fetal weight and skeletal ossification at doses higher than those used clinically [127, 129]. Its use in breastfeeding is unsupported as there have been no studies assessing whether memantine is excreted in breast milk or affects nursing infants or milk production [127].

Cyproheptadine

Cyproheptadine, an antagonist at the 5-HT2, histamine H1, and muscarinic cholinergic receptors, has been widely used for the prevention of migraine in children/adolescents. Small clinical studies have also demonstrated its efficacy for migraine prevention in adults [130, 131]. In one study, Rao and colleagues reported cyproheptadine to be equivalent to propranolol in reducing migraine frequency and severity [131]. Two human studies demonstrated no increased risk of major congenital abnormalities or teratogenic effects when cyproheptadine was administered during any trimester of pregnancy [132]. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 37 human studies found that first-trimester exposure to H1 antihistamines was not associated with increased risk of major malformations, spontaneous abortion, prematurity, stillbirth, or low birth weight [133].

Regarding lactation, it is unknown if cyproheptadine is transferred into breast milk. There are no direct studies to date on its effect on breastfeeding at low doses but there is theoretical concern that it may interfere with lactation, as high doses have been used to lower prolactin levels for the treatment of amenorrhea-galactorrhea syndrome [134]. As an antihistaminergic agent, sedation is also a potential risk for the breastfeeding infant. Therefore, cyproheptadine should generally be avoided during lactation.

Conclusions

While data continues to be limited for the management of migraine in pregnancy and lactation, current evidence provides clinicians with a framework for management that focuses on the least teratogenic and most efficacious strategies at the frontline (see Table 1). In terms of abortive management, acetaminophen and triptan medications continue to hold the highest evidence of safety, with triptans continuing to show higher efficacy. Occipital and sphenopalatine ganglion blocks with lidocaine are safer options for the management of status migrainosus and as a bridge therapy to preventive management, in comparison with more conventional treatment strategies for status migrainosus such as steroid tapers. For preventive treatments, amlodipine, cyproheptadine and onabotulinumtoxinA show the highest promise of safety; however, beta blockers continue to be used most given a long-term history of relative safety and known efficacy. Meanwhile there is a significant paucity of data surrounding the newer CGRP blocking medications, with current literature recommending against their use due to hypothetical risk of preeclampsia. At the same time, multiple preventive therapies with known efficacy hold significant teratogenic risk, such as topiramate, valproic acid, and candesartan, necessitating appropriate counseling of patients and discussion of effective contraceptive use when starting these agents in women of childbearing age. In helping patients with family planning, it is important to discuss available safe options, timelines for family planning, and plans for transitions of medications when patients are planning to attempt to conceive. Meanwhile in patients using either inconsistent or less effective birth control methods such as period tracking, it is recommended to avoid known teratogenic therapies in case of unplanned pregnancies. If unplanned pregnancy occurs while on a teratogenic therapy, the therapy should be removed as soon as known, and nuanced discussions regarding potential risks and mitigation measures to the potential pregnancy should take place, involving the patient’s obstetrician and maternal fetal medicine specialist when needed.

In lactation, data are likewise limited, but reassuringly many of the agents previously mentioned have limited or no known passage into breastmilk. As for many women, migraine frequency may increase around this time to prepregnancy levels, we are able to provide reassurance to patients by offering comprehensive treatment options at this crucial time. It is important to note that in recent years there has been increased focus on the management of migraine in pregnancy and lactation. Ongoing registry data will help contribute to long-term evidence surrounding safety of new agents during pregnancy and lactation. At the same time, more large-scale cohort studies and pharmacovigilance are needed to ascertain the risk of new pharmaceutical agents, with improved knowledge surrounding the safety of CGRP medications. When managing our pregnant patients with migraine, we must remember to balance risk with benefit to the patient who may face significant migraine-related disability during pregnancy and lactation. A comprehensive personalized approach combining pharmacological agents, lifestyle modifications, and complementary therapies is needed to address each patient’s individual symptoms and disease course.
