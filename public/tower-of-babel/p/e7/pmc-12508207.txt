Contrasting roles of KMT2C and KMT2D in breast cancer
Authors: Emily Tinsley, Philip Bredin, Sinead Toomey, Bryan T Hennessy, Simon J Furney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKMT2C and KMT2D are paralogous lysine-methyltransferases that primarily monomethylate lysine residues at enhancer regions promoting an active transcriptional state. KMT2C and KMT2D are frequently mutated in multiple cancer types and in breast cancers mutations collectively occur at frequencies of 10–20%. However, the specificity and unique functions of the paralogues in breast cancer development are unclear. Using a combination of mutation, transcriptome, ChIP-seq, chromatin accessibility, and cell proliferation data from breast cancer patient and cell line samples we have investigated the roles of these proteins in tumour development. We identified a dependency of oestrogen receptor (ER) positive breast cancer cell lines on wildtype KMT2D. KMT2D ChIP-seq peaks show greater enrichment and interaction with ER and its binding partners FOXA1 and GATA3 compared to KMT2C in MCF-7 cells and uniquely bind enhancers of a set of genes enriched for oestrogen response pathways. Both KMT2C and KMT2D mutant luminal patient samples show significant downregulation of oestrogen response pathways compared to wildtype which, for KMT2C mutants, correlates with reduced chromatin accessibility around enhancers. Our results suggest that KMT2C and KMT2D have contrasting and subtype-specific roles in breast cancers and reveals KMT2D in particular as a potential target in ER + subtypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19027-1.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer with around 2.3 million cases globally and the cause of ~ 7% of cancer deaths1. It is a highly heterogeneous disease often characterised into one of three main subtypes; luminal, HER2 + and basal representing the presence of receptor biomarkers: oestrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and Human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2)2,3. Luminal/Oestrogen receptor positive (ER+) breast cancers make up the majority (70–80%) of all breast cancers and show over expression of the ERα transcription factor (TF) and upregulation of proliferative, oncogenic pathways4. Despite relatively good treatment options for this subtype, many breast cancer deaths occur as a result of therapy resistance. In recent years, epigenetic dysregulation via histone modifications and chromatin remodelling have been shown to play important roles in breast cancer development and endocrine therapy resistance5,6 making histone modifying proteins major targets for the development of new therapies7,8. Whilst the language of histone modification and gene expression is complicated9,10generally, active transcription can be characterised with histone 3 lysine 4 (H3K4) methylation around enhancers and promoters11 with additional lysine 27 (H3K27) acetylation. Methylation of H3K4 is catalysed by the histone lysine-N-methyltransferase (KMT2) family which comprises three sets of gene paralogues: KMT2A, KMT2B, KMT2C, KMT2D, SETD1A, SETD1B12. This family are frequently mutated in multiple cancers12,13 including breast.

The most frequently mutated genes within the KMT2 family in solid cancers are KMT2C and KMT2D which specifically mono-methylate H3K4 at enhancer regions of genes, promoting an active transcriptional state14. These genes evolve from a single gene, Trithorax related (TRR) in drosophila15and differ slightly in their protein recognition domains16 though functional differences between the two are not well understood. Previous cell line experiments have shown that KMT2C/D individually act as co-regulators of ER and monomethylate enhancer regions of ER target genes to prime them for transcription16–19. In breast cancer, KMT2C is one of the most frequently mutated genes20–22 and despite its role as an ER regulator, it is an established tumour suppressor20,23. Loss of function of KMT2C has been linked to oestrogen independency18 and the promotion of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT)24. In contrast, the role of KMT2D in breast tumorigenesis is ambiguous and it is generally mutated around three times less frequently than KMT2C with only ~ 20% of these mutations being truncating20,23. The differences in mutation frequencies and landscapes of these genes highlights potential differences in the benefit of their mutation.

In this study we sought to investigate whether KMT2C and KMT2D, have similar or different roles in breast cancer development and to determine the downstream effects of their mutations in patient samples. We used cell line data from the Dependency Map (DEPMAP) to identify the importance of KMT2C/D in cancer cell growth. We then utilised ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data from the Encyclopaedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) and further studies to determine binding patterns of KMT2C/D in breast cancer cells and identify their shared and independent roles in gene regulation. Finally, we used multi-omic patient data from two large breast cancer data sets; The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) to analyse the specific downstream effects of KMT2C or KMT2D mutation in patient samples and how these differ between the proteins and across breast cancer subtypes.

Methods

Computational analyses

All analyses, unless otherwise specified, were carried out using R version 4.4.125. Full details of packages and versions are specified in Supplementary Table 1.

Data sources

CRISPR dependency data were analysed for cell lines in the DEPMAP consortium using the 23Q2 public dataset which contains data from CRISPR knockout screens from ﻿project Achilles, as well as genomic characterisation from the CCLE project26–30. ChIP-seq peaks For ER related TFs, histone modifications and ATAC-seq for MCF-7 were downloaded directly from ENCODE31,32. Fastq files for all other ChIP-seq experiments were downloaded using SRAtoolkit33 from the following GEO datasets: GSE8171434, GSE12075635, GSE17144724, GSE10032818. RNA-seq, mutation calls, and GISTIC2.0 copy number calls for the TCGA-BRCA and METABRIC cohorts were extracted from cBioportal36–38 and clinical information extracted using the TCGAbiolinks package in R39–41. All TCGA and METABRIC data was downloaded on 06/08/2024. Samples were filtered to those where mutation, RNA-seq, and copy number information was available (TCGA n = 994, METABRIC n = 1866). ATAC-seq counts for the available breast TCGA samples were extracted directly from the source paper42.

DEPMAP analysis

Differences between mean dependencies were calculated using a Wilcoxon test. Logistic regression models were used to show the change in dependency of KMT2C/D mutants. In this analysis, all cell lines with a dependency value of 0 were removed. KMT2C values were log10 transformed to account for the heavy positive skew across the data and for both genes, dependency values more than 3 standard deviations above or below the mean were removed. KMT2C/D status, including mutation, copy number and expression individually, was modelled for each dependency independently in both the pan-cancer and breast cancer cohorts adjusting for tumour mutational burden, TP53 and PIK3CA status, and either cancer type or subtype.

Mutational summaries and survival plots

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the survminer and survival packages in R43–45 and adjusted for age of diagnosis and AJCC pathologic stage. Deleterious mutations were classed as mutations that were either truncating (frameshift, nonsense, splice site), missense with a “probably damaging” PolyPhen score, or homozygous deletion.

ChIP-seq analysis

Fastq files downloaded through the SRA toolkit were quality checked and trimmed using fastp46 and aligned with bwa-mem47. Duplicates were removed with Picard and peaks were called with MACS248 with default settings. Peaks were filtered for those on primary assembly chromosomes and not within ENCODE blacklist regions49. For KMT2C peaks, the overlapping peak set was used for all further analyses. Identification of peaks overlapping other peak sets was carried out using FindOverlapsOfPeaks from the ChIPpeakAnno package50. Specific analyses and visualisations of ChIP-seq data were carried out using a combination of ChIPpeakAnno and ChIPseeker51.

ChIP-seq peak annotation

Chromosome regions were assigned to peaks using the assignChromosomeRegion function in ChIPpeakAnno50. Specific enhancers were obtained from GeneHancer52 using the elite (identified by more than one source) MCF-7 specific enhancers for peaks. All enhancer-gene associations used were those considered elite (association observed with more than one method) in GeneHancer. MCF-7 cells were also further annotated with cis-regulatory elements and ATAC-seq peaks from ENCODE31,32.

Motif enrichment analysis

Motif enrichment for all peak sets was carried out using homer find motifsGenome.pl function with default settings53.

Gene set enrichment

Gene sets from ChIP-seq peaks were selected as genes with elite associations with specified enhancers within the GeneHancer database52. Gene sets were then inputted into Enrichr54–56 and results from enrichment in the molecular signatures database57,58 were extracted. For analysis in patient samples, all gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) were carried out using the fgsea package59 using the hallmark gene set from the Molecular Signatures Database 57,58.

Differential expression analysis

For TCGA data, differential expression analysis of KMT2C or KMT2D mutation vs. wildtype was carried out using the edgeR package60–62. Mutation was specified as those with either a non-silent, exonic mutation or a deep copy number deletion (GISTIC value = -2). Normalisation factors using the ‘TMM’ method were used to scale the gene library and lowly expressed genes were removed. For METABRIC microarray data, differential expression analysis was carried out on the log-normalised z-scores using the limma package63. All analyses were adjusted for age at diagnosis (binned into ten year categories), AJCC pathologic stage for TCGA or Grade for METABRIC. P-values were adjusted for false discovery rate using Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Immune cell fraction analysis

Immune cell fractions for each TCGA-BRCA patient have previously been determined64. The 21 immune cells were grouped into six immune cell categories and mean fraction of each cell type was calculated for KMT2C/D mutants and wildtype individually and collectively. P-values for differences between mutant and wildtype were calculated with a Wilcoxon test.

Differential accessibility analysis

Raw counts for all ATAC-seq peaks were normalised to account for library size and GC content using the EDAseq package65. Differential accessibility analysis of KMT2C mutants vs. wildtype was carried out using edgeR60–62. The analysis was adjusted for subtype, age of diagnosis (binned into ten-year categories), and stage. P-values were adjusted for false discovery rate using Benjamini-Hochberg correction. After the differential accessibility analysis, peaks were filtered to include only the peaks that were significantly correlated with expression of a gene within 500 kilobases in a pan-cancer cohort42. For genes with multiple peak correlations, mean logFC was calculated.

Results

ER + breast cancer cell lines show dependency on wildtype KMT2D but not KMT2C

First, we sought to assess the relative importance of KMT2C/D and COMPASS related proteins on cell growth in breast cancer cell lines using the DEPMAP dataset with breast cancer receptor biomarkers as positive controls. We identified a large variance in cell line dependency on different members of the KMT2C/D COMPASS complex (Fig. 1a). Breast cancer cell lines show a consistent lack of dependency on KMT2C consistent with its established roles as a tumour suppressor in breast cancer20,23. In contrast, KMT2D dependency differs across cell lines with dependent lines also showing dependence on either ESR1 or ERBB2 (genes encoding ER and HER2). The mean dependency on KMT2D was significantly higher in both ER+ (p = 7 × 10− 5) and HER2+ (p = 0·015) cell lines compared to their negative counterparts though this was not observed for KMT2C (Fig. 1b-e). This was also observed when looking at the intrinsic subtype level as both luminal (p = 5.9 × 10− 5) and HER2+ (p = 2·6 × 10− 6) subtypes show significantly higher dependency on KMT2D than the basal subtype (Fig. 1f). Interestingly, HER2 + subtypes also showed significantly increased dependency on KMT2C compared to basal (p = 0·028) though the majority of values still fell well below the 0·5 threshold considered to be dependent within DEPMAP (Fig. 1g).

We then assessed whether aberrations in KMT2C/D would affect cell line dependency on the proteins. We developed a multivariate logistic regression model assessing the contributions of mutation, expression and copy number of KMT2C and KMT2D on their individual dependency.

In a pan-cancer analysis, we identified that KMT2D mutants have significantly decreased dependency on KMT2D (p = 0.033) whilst KMT2D copy number (ABSOLUTE copy number quantification) is positively associated with KMT2D dependency (p = 1.43 × 10− 6). This indicates the cell lines are dependent on the wildtype KMT2D protein. We also identified an increased dependency on KMT2D in KMT2C mutants within the pan-cancer cohort (p = 0.009) indicating some level of redundancy between the two proteins. Most analyses were not significant at the breast specific level, likely impacted by low sample size however we did observe decreased dependency on KMT2D in KMT2D mutants in the breast cancer cohort (p = 0.031) (Fig. 1h). As KMT2C/D are responsible for mono-methylation of H3K4, we took advantage of the global chromatin profiling screens from CCLE and regressed global levels of H3K4me1 on KMT2C/D mutation and dependency. On a pan-cancer level, KMT2D mutations show significantly reduced levels of global H3K4me1 (p = 0.027) particularly when focusing on truncating mutations (p = 2·98 × 10− 8). These results were not replicated in breast cancer likely due to the small sample size at this level (n = 44). However, in breast cancer cell lines, H3K4me1 did show a positive correlation with KMT2D dependency (Pearson correlation = 0·129, p = 0·0014) (Fig. 1i). Collectively, this data suggests an oncogenic role of wildtype KMT2D in receptor positive breast cancers that involves its role in H3K4 methylation but that is not evident with KMT2C.

ChIP-seq analysis identifies a unique set of ER target genes regulated by KMT2D but not KMT2C

Based on the difference in dependency observed, we next sought to investigate whether KMT2C/D show different gene regulation patterns in ER + cell lines. We used KMT2C and KMT2D ChIP-seq data from the ER+, KMT2C/D wildtype, MCF-7 cell line. For KMT2C, we used the overlapping peaks of ChIP-seq data from two independent studies34,35 to ensure accuracy of the calls resulting in a total of 14,106 peaks (Supplementary Table 2). For KMT2D, peaks from only one study24 were available with a total of 16,452 peaks (Supplementary Table 3). In line with their role as H3K4 mono-methyltransferases, over 90% of peaks in both sets showed overlap with H3K4me1; 91.5% (n = 12,912) in KMT2C, 91.6% (n = 15,075) in KMT2D (Figs. 2a-b). Additionally, 52.0% of KMT2C peaks overlapped with H3K27ac, a signal of active enhancers, initiated post H3K4 mono-methylation, however this was only observed for 37.7% of KMT2D peaks (Figs. 2c-d). To remove potential false positive peaks, the following analyses focused on the peak sets overlapping H3K4me1 peaks. KMT2C/D peaks showed similar distribution across genomic features, primarily occurring in intergenic and intronic regions distal to transcription start sites and in regions of open chromatin (Fig. 2e) though, those that were closer to promoter regions (within 5 kb) were clustered around transcription start sites (Figs. 2f-h). At the enhancer level, 29.3% and 34.2% of KMT2C and KMT2D peaks respectively mapped to ENCODE-labelled, MCF-7specific enhancer-like regions with the majority being distal rather than proximal in both peak sets. When expanding to MCF-7 enhancers within the GeneHancer database, 63.0% and 59.7% of KMT2C and KMT2D peaks respectively mapped to either Enhancer or Promoter/Enhancer sites (Fig. 2e). Consistent with previous analyses18,34 we identified a high overlap of KMT2C/D peaks with ER and its well-established binding partners FOXA1 and GATA3. Binding motifs of all TFs were significantly enriched in the full peak set (Figs. 2i-k) (Supplementary Tables 4–5) though enrichment showed much greater significance in KMT2D peaks. Furthermore, genes previously associated with the GeneHancer enhancer regions overlapping both KMT2C and KMT2D peaks showed the highest enrichment with the oestrogen response early pathway (p = 1.79 × 10− 28 for KMT2C & p = 7.35 × 10− 44 for KMT2D) (Fig. 2l).

Whilst these results corroborate what is currently known about KMT2C/D, we were interested in identifying differences between the paralogues that could aid in explaining the discrepancy in dependency data amongst ER + subtypes. We used elite MCF-7 specific enhancers from the GeneHancer database to identify enhancers that were unique to KMT2C/D or shared by both proteins. We identified 3,111 KMT2C unique enhancers, 3,283 unique KMT2D enhancers and 4,891 enhancers that showed overlap with both proteins (Fig. 3a). These enhancers were integrated with MCF-7 ChIP-seq for H3K4me1 and H3K27ac and ATAC-seq data from ENCODE31,32 to generate an active enhancer map for KMT2C and KMT2D (Fig. 3b). In both the unique and shared groups, the majority of enhancers were active (overlapping H3K4me1 and H3K27ac peaks) (Fig. 3b), though a subset of active enhancers across all sets were also potentially insulated by CTCF (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Using ChIP-seq data for H3K4me1 and H3K27ac in MCF-7 cells pre and post shRNA KMT2C knockdown, we confirmed that 88% of enhancers that showed altered status upon KMT2C knockdown, did so via loss rather than gain of a histone modifications and this was primarily through loss of H3K27ac (Supplementary Fig. 1b-g). We then looked at enhancer associated genes that were unique to the three gene sets (Supplementary Tables 6–8). Consistent with results from the full peak sets (Fig. 2l), shared genes showed enrichment of oestrogen response pathways (padj early oestrogen response = 9·63 × 10− 31; padj late oestrogen response = 9·07 × 10− 16) however these pathways were also significantly enriched in genes unique to KMT2D (padj early oestrogen response = 0·013; padj late oestrogen response = 0·013). These pathways showed the highest enrichment when filtering for genes associated with active or active/poised enhancers only, though they were not significant after adjusting for multiple testing in the active only set (Supplementary Fig. 1h-i). This highlights a set of oestrogen response genes that are bound by KMT2D but not KMT2C in MCF-7 cell lines. To further highlight the regulation of ER pathways in the KMT2D unique peaks, 79·8% of KMT2D unique peaks overlapped with ER or one of its binding partners whereas this was only 63·2% for KMT2C unique peaks (Fig. 3d-e). Additionally, KMT2D unique peaks were significantly enriched for oestrogen response elements (EREs) (p = 1 × 10− 87) whilst this was not significant in KMT2C unique peaks (Fig. 3f). Other motifs with higher enrichment in KMT2D unique peaks include TGASTCA motifs associated with members of the AP-1 transcription factor whereas GRHL2 motifs showed the greatest enrichment in KMT2C unique peaks (Supplementary Tables 9–11).

As KMT2D dependency also differed between ER + and ER- cell lines, we further filtered these unique peaks to those that were not overlapping with KMT2D ChIP-seq peaks in the ER-, KMT2C/D wildtype, MDA-MB-231 cell line. Of the 8,025 peaks that were unique to KMT2D in MCF-7 cell lines, 6,391 were also unique to the MCF-7 cell line (Fig. 3g). We filtered all elite enhancers within these peaks to those which did not overlap enhancers in the KMT2C peaks in MCF-7. Genes with ‘elite’ associations with these 1,983 enhancers in MCF-7 cell lines were filtered to exclude any gene that also linked to an enhancer in the KMT2C peaks. This resulted in a final set of 486 genes that are uniquely regulated by KMT2D in the MCF-7 cell line (Supplementary Table 12). This final unique gene set still showed positive enrichment of oestrogen response early (padj = 0·0057) and oestrogen response late pathways (padj = 0·001), and specifically 38 are ChIP-seq confirmed targets of ESR131,32,66 and 22 are found in the oestrogen response hallmark pathways (Supplementary Table 12). These gene sets provide potential contributors to the dependency on KMT2D observed in ER + cell lines that is not seen in KMT2C. Collectively our ChIP-seq analysis supports the known roles of KMT2C/D in H3K4 mono-methylation primarily at oestrogen receptor associated enhancer sites yet shows a much stronger enrichment of these sites in KMT2D and identifies a set of ER regulated genes that are regulated by KMT2D and not its paralogue in MCF-7 cell lines. These KMT2D unique genes present potential targets contributing towards the dependency of ER + cell lines on KMT2D.

KMT2C and KMT2D mutations show differential pathway dysregulation in patient samples

We then sought to determine whether results from breast cancer cell lines translate to what is observed in patient samples. Initially, we compared mutation frequencies of KMT2C and KMT2D across subtypes in the TCGA-BRCA and METABRIC patient cohorts. We focused on intrinsic, molecular subtypes as this information was available for a higher number of patients compared to specific ER/HER2 status and closely reflects receptor status. In TCGA, KMT2C mutations occur 6·2x and 5·25x more frequently than KMT2D mutations in luminal A and luminal B subtypes respectively however the disparity between the mutation frequencies is much lower in basal (2·4x) and particularly HER2+ (1·2x) subtypes (Fig. 4a). This pattern was also observed in METABRIC though at a lower level with KMT2C mutations occurring 3·5 and 3·1 times more frequently in luminal A and luminal B subtypes respectively but only 1.6 times more frequently in HER2 and 2·2x more frequently in basal subtypes. Unsurprisingly, this pattern still stands when looking specifically at receptor status (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We then assessed the effects of these mutations on survival. In a collective luminal cohort, deleterious mutations of KMT2C in luminal subtypes was associated with significantly poorer survival (p = 0·043) which was not observed for KMT2D (Figs. 4b-c) though admittedly this could be affected by the small sample size. In METABRIC, KMT2C deleterious mutations show a trend towards poorer survival compared to the wildtype, particularly in luminal A breast cancers. Conversely, KMT2D mutations seem to show a trend towards better survival than the wildtype though neither of these results were statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 2b-e). Mutation frequencies and survival data from breast cancer patients supports a hypothesis whereby KMT2C loss provides an oncogenic benefit to luminal/ER + breast cancers whereas they are more dependent on the wildtype version of its paralogue KMT2D.

This led us to investigate which pathways are significantly differentially expressed upon KMT2C/D mutation within the luminal subtype to identify differences between mutations in the individual genes. We carried out a differential expression analysis in TCGA luminal samples (n = 696) comparing KMT2C or KMT2D mutants to wildtype individually. We identified 53 and 13 significantly differentially expressed genes (padj < 0.05) for KMT2C and KMT2D mutants respectively, none of which were overlapping between both analyses. Upon gene set enrichment, the downregulation of ER pathways was identified in patient samples as both early oestrogen response (KMT2C padj = 0·013; KMT2D padj = 0·00053) and late oestrogen response (KMT2C padj = 0·032; KMT2D padj = 0·031) pathways were significantly downregulated in mutants compared to wildtypes of both genes. Common upregulated pathways between KMT2C and KMT2D include Interferon Alpha (IFN-α) response (KMT2C padj = 2·3 × 10− 16; KMT2D padj = 2·9 × 10− 16), Interferon Gamma (IFN-γ) response (KMT2C padj = 1·8 × 10− 21; KMT2D padj = 3·0 × 10− 18), IL2 STAT5 signalling (KMT2C padj = 9·2 × 10− 5; KMT2D padj = 0·0024), and inflammatory response (KMT2C padj = 0·0052; KMT2D padj = 0·01) all which are linked to an immune response. Based on the upregulation of inflammatory and interferon pathways, we speculated whether KMT2C/D mutants show increased fractions of tumour infiltrating lymphocytes but this was not found to be significant (Supplementary Fig. 3). Nine pathways showed significant dysregulation in opposite directions between KMT2C and KMT2D mutants. G2M checkpoint, MYC and E2F targets and DNA repair genes were significantly upregulated in KMT2D mutants but downregulated in KMT2C mutants whereas apical junction, coagulation, UV response and epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways were significantly upregulated in KMT2C mutants and downregulated in KMT2D (Fig. 4d). Gene set enrichment results of micro-array RNA expression data from the METABRIC cohort (n = 1175 luminal BC samples) (Fig. 4d), validated some of the TCGA results, in particular supporting a downregulation of oestrogen response pathways and upregulation of inflammatory response in KMT2C mutants and also the downregulation of EMT in KMT2D mutants (Fig. 4d).

In TCGA we also compared expression profiles of KMT2C/D mutation across different molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 4). In the HER2 + subtype, KMT2D mutation showed significant downregulation of both oestrogen response pathways (early padj = 1·4 × 10− 9; late padj = 0·0040). KMT2C mutation, on the other hand, actually showed significant upregulation of oestrogen response early (padj = 0.00071). In the HER2 + subtype, significant enrichment of all immune response pathways at the same level seen in the luminal subtype was not observed for HER2 + subtypes for either KMT2C or KMT2D mutation. However, in KMT2C mutants, both IFN-γ (padj = 0.00071) and inflammatory response (padj = 0.010) were significantly upregulated. Notably, in line with the luminal subtype, KMT2C mutants showed significant upregulation of the EMT pathway (padj = 1·4 × 10− 22) which was significantly downregulated in KMT2D mutants (padj = 8·6 × 10− 30). Additionally, E2F targets and G2M checkpoint genes showed significant downregulation in KMT2C mutants (padj = 2·4 × 10− 11, padj = 0·00038 respectively) and G2M showed significant upregulation in KMT2D mutants (padj = 0.010). In the basal subtype, KMT2D mutants also showed significant downregulation of both oestrogen response pathways (padj = 2·8 × 10− 7, padj = 5·2 × 10− 6 in early and late pathways respectively). In line with results observed in the luminal subtype, KMT2C mutants showed significant upregulation of immune response pathways such as IFN-α response (padj = 5·2 × 10− 29), IFN-γ response (padj = 2·0 × 10− 38) and inflammatory response (padj = 1·6 × 10− 11). KMT2D mutants, however, showed significant downregulation of these pathways (IFN-α response (padj = 2·5 × 10− 15), IFN-γ response padj = 1·0 × 10− 30, inflammatory response padj = 6·7 × 10− 7). In the basal subtype, the EMT pathway also showed contrasting dysregulation with KMT2C mutants showing significant downregulation of the pathway (padj = 6·5 × 10− 8) and KMT2D mutants showing significant upregulation (padj = 1·2 × 10− 10). These results emphasise that whilst KMT2C and KMT2D should be considered independent considering the different expression profiles of their mutant samples, there effects are also subtype specific.

Next, we took advantage of ATAC-seq data available for 74 of the TCGA breast cancer samples to carry out a differential accessibility analysis to determine whether changes in expression profiles are linked to changes to chromatin accessibility. There were 46 luminal samples with ATAC-seq data (including an individual with no intrinsic subtype but immunohistochemistry confirmed ER+/HER2 status), five of which had a KMT2C mutation (Supplementary Table 13). We carried out a differential accessibility analysis between the mutant and wildtype luminal samples. Of the 551,918 genome-wide ATAC-seq peaks in our analysis, 13,515 were found to be significantly differentially accessible between KMT2C mutants vs. wildtype with 2,685 showing significantly decreased accessibility and 10,830 showing significantly increased accessibility in the mutant samples. The majority of differentially accessible peaks were found in intronic (52%) and distal (39%) gene regions with a small number around promoter regions (5%) (Fig. 4e). This is characteristic of enhancer gene regions and also closely reflects the regions of KMT2C binding observed in MCF-7 cell lines (Fig. 2e). We filtered the peaks to gene-linked enhancer peaks previously identified by Corces et al.42 which resulted in 755 ‘enhancer’ peaks with higher accessibility that correlated with 802 genes and 346 ‘enhancer’ peaks with lower accessibility that correlated with 401 genes. Gene set enrichment of the collective gene set identified significantly decreased accessibility of peaks associated with genes involved in both late oestrogen response (padj = 0·027) and early oestrogen response (padj = 0·021) (Figs. 4f-g) corroborating with the differential expression analysis. Intriguingly, enhancer peaks associated with G2M checkpoint (padj = 0·0029) and E2F target (padj = 0·021) genes showed significantly increased accessibility in mutant samples despite expression of these pathways being significantly lower in mutant samples. Between the differential expression and differential accessibility, we identified five genes (ERBB2, GRB7, MALL, FGFR1, PAPPDC1B) that were significantly dysregulated in both analyses (Fig. 4h). ERBB2, GRB7 and PPAPDC1B showed significant downregulation in both analyses whilst, MALL was upregulated in both and FGFR1 enhancers showed increased chromatin accessibility but overall decreased gene expression. ERBB2 and GRB7 are established ER targets31,32,66. A 2250 bp intronic region was found to be significantly less accessible in KMT2C mutant samples compared to wildtype (padj = 4·6 × 10− 6) and was associated with expression of both genes. This was also significant when adjusting for IHC-confirmed HER2 + status (padj = 0·00057). This peak region fell within the GeneHancer enhancer GH17J039673 which overlapped ChIP-seq peaks of KMT2C in MCF7 cell lines and showed elite associations with both ERBB2 and GRB7 in MCF-7 cell lines (Fig. 4i). However, this enhancer did not appear to lose activation status upon shRNA knockdown of KMT2C in MCF-7 cells18. As far as we are aware, this is the first time downstream effects of KMT2C/D aberrations in human breast cancer samples have been analysed. Our data show that in line with results in cell lines, KMT2C/D mutation causes a decrease in expression of oestrogen response genes in luminal patient samples, which, at least in KMT2C mutants, results from reduced accessibility around enhancer regions of these genes. EMT pathways are significantly upregulated in KMT2C mutant luminal samples and those involved in the regulation of cell cycle and growth are significantly downregulated providing beneficial changes to KMT2C mutant samples which are regulated in the opposite direction in KMT2D mutant samples.

Discussion

KMT2C and KMT2D both derive from a single gene (Trithorax related (TRR) in Drosophila15 and while the presence of two separately evolved paralogues indicates the requirement of slightly different functions, there have been few studies investigating this, particularly in the context of breast cancer. Though our analyses reflect a clear link between both paralogues and ER driven pathways, this relationship appears to be essential in ER + breast cancers for KMT2D but dispensable for KMT2C. Dependency data from CRISPR knockdown assays showed that whilst KMT2C knockout did not affect proliferation of any breast cancer cell lines, KMT2D knockout reduced proliferation of ER + and HER2 + cell lines. This is somewhat contrasting to previous shRNA KMT2C knockdown experiments which showed that KMT2C knockdown did significantly affect proliferation of ER+/HER2- cell lines18. However, this study did highlight a reduction of cell proliferation in all cell lines except the triple-negative MDA-MB-231 line for KMT2D knockdown, which is consistent with our dependency results.

An oncogenic benefit of KMT2C loss to ER + breast cancer is supported by the mutation frequencies in luminal subtypes, most of which (~ 60%) are truncating. Our analyses further support a role of KMT2C in oestrogen response pathways as ChIP-seq peaks for KMT2C show significant enrichment for ER binding sites and oestrogen response related genes, and patient samples with a KMT2C mutation show significantly decreased expression of oestrogen response genes. Considering the crucial role of ER in luminal breast cancer development, the loss of KMT2C and subsequent reduction of ER regulated pathways seems counterintuitive. However, multiple studies have linked the loss of KMT2C with a shift in oestrogen dependence and development of endocrine resistance. Gala et al., identified that whilst loss of KMT2C in ER + cell lines led to decreased ERα activity, in hormone-depleted conditions it also promoted proliferation18highlighting a role in promoting oestrogen independence and potential resistance to selective oestrogen receptor modulators and aromatase inhibitors. It has also been shown that loss of KMT2C in ER + cell lines can alter the binding profile of ERα towards a more endocrine resistant transcriptome and promote a reliance on non-ER-regulated mechanisms such as SP1 transcription67. Additionally, these cell lines with altered ERα and SP1 binding profiles showed resistance to both tamoxifen and Fulvestrant67. Multiple studies have highlighted the increase of KMT2C mutations or decrease in KMT2C expression in therapy resistant and metastatic populations18,67–69 though one has also highlighted a link between increased KMT2C expression and tamoxifen resistance70. Interestingly, ER + cell lines which have developed endocrine resistance via either tamoxifen exposure or long-term oestradiol deprivation (reduced reliance on ER driven proliferation) showed remarkably similar differential expression profiles to KMT2C mutant patients71. Specifically, the strongest results in both cases included the down regulation of E2F targets and G2M checkpoint related genes. Interestingly these were significantly upregulated in KMT2D mutant samples in TCGA and in fact increased KMT2D expression has been associated with tamoxifen resistance72. The reduction of these cell cycle regulatory genes could aid in allowing cell proliferation independent of ER function. MCF-7 ChIP-seq peaks unique to KMT2C showed enrichment for enhancers associated with E2F target genes though this enrichment was not seen in either the KMT2D unique or shared peak sets. This highlights that KMT2C loss could aid in driving ER independent proliferation via reduced enhancer activation and subsequent downregulation of proliferative regulatory genes which are not also KMT2D targets. In contrast to this we did identify significantly increased accessibility around E2F and G2M genes in patient samples with a KMT2C mutation which you would expect to correlate with increased gene expression. It is possible that increased accessibility around these regions could allow access for proteins that inhibit gene expression though the complicated nature of chromatin accessibility and gene expression is hard to elucidate with the data available. Additionally, the data could be impacted by the small sample size available for the DAA and would benefit from a larger cohort of KMT2C mutant profiles.

Another oncogenic benefit of KMT2C loss that has been established in cell lines is the upregulation of the EMT pathway related to metastasis24,73. We were able to identify significant upregulation of this pathway in luminal and HER2 + KMT2C mutant breast cancer samples in TCGA though it was significantly downregulated in the basal samples. In the ER + MCF-7 cell line, the top motif enriched in KMT2C peaks was the Grainyhead like 2 (GRHL2), also identified by the previous authors34and was one of the most differentially enriched motifs between the unique peak sets (Supplementary Tables 8–9). GRHL2 has been shown to suppress the expression of EMT in epithelial cells74 and specifically regulate epithelial structure with the help of the KMT2C/D complex75. Loss of KMT2C could potentially promote EMT pathways through the reduced activation of GRHL2 target gene enhancers and the downregulation of the genes supressing EMT and regulating epithelial stability. On the contrary, this pathway showed significant downregulation upon KMT2D mutation in luminal and HER2 + samples with upregulation in the basal subtype. Gene set enrichment of genes regulated by KMT2D showed enrichment of the EMT pathway for all KMT2D regulated genes (Fig. 2l) and genes in peaks unique to KMT2D (Fig. 3c). Specific activation of EMT genes by KMT2D could explain the downregulation of this pathway upon KMT2D mutation again highlighting less oncogenic benefit to its loss. These pathways that show contrasting patterns of dysregulation upon mutation in KMT2C or KMT2D highlight areas where only a KMT2C mutation is beneficial to ER+/luminal cancer growth.

It could also be possible that the loss of KMT2C provides an oncogenic benefit via increased dependence on KMT2D. Regression analyses with the DEMAP data showed that KMT2C mutants do have increased dependency on KMT2D in a pan cancer setting. The lack of significance at the breast cancer level could potentially be a result of small sample size. The dependence on KMT2D in the ER + vs. ER- cell lines is striking, and we have identified a set of genes associated with enhancers that are unique to KMT2D peaks and show potential to contribute to this dependency. KMT2C loss could increase reliance on KMT2D to compensate for any necessary, shared regulatory networks and subsequently increase the ER related benefits that are unique to KMT2D. An increased dependency on wildtype KMT2D upon KMT2C loss provides a potential therapeutic pathway that could be exploited and deserves further investigation. Homozygous deletions of KMT2D are not observed at all in breast cancer16 and KMT2D knockout mice are embryonic lethal76further highlighting the essential nature of this protein aligning with the lower frequency of mutations that is observed. Additionally, KMT2D has been previously been shown to bind ER directly via an LXXLL motif17 and also aid in the assembly of the ER-FOXA1-PBX1 regulatory network19. Dependency on KMT2D was also observed in HER2 + cell lines, including those with ER- status so it is likely that the essential functions of KMT2D stem further than just unique regulation of ER pathways however, as previously mentioned16the field would benefit from further analyses focusing on the HER2 + subtype for both KMT2C and KMT2D.

Our analyses in patient data sets support the results observed in cell line experiments as described above. Whilst the frequency of KMT2D mutations was too small, we were able to assess the effects of KMT2C mutation on chromatin accessibility profiles as well as gene expression profiles in luminal breast cancer samples. Our results identified a change in chromatin accessibility upon KMT2C mutation though interestingly we observed a much higher number of loci with increased chromatin accessibility upon KMT2C mutation compared to those with decreased accessibility. One explanation for this could be that genes that have reduced regulation promote open chromatin accessibility around oncogenic genes such as EMT genes which showed increased chromatin accessibility, just not significant after correction. Finally, for oestrogen response genes, the decreased expression of these pathways observed was supported by reduced accessibility around their enhancer regions. Considering that ChIP-seq data showed that KMT2C bound enhancers had the greatest enrichment of oestrogen response early genes (Fig. 2l), it is understandable that these pathways would show significant downregulation upon KMT2C loss. Our patient data supports a model in which KMT2C loss leads to reduced accessibility around enhancer regions of oestrogen response genes and subsequent reduced expression. It is possible that whilst the main direct effect of KMT2C mutation is a reduction of oestrogen gene expression via reduced enhancer accessibility, the loss of these specific genes drives other changes in chromatin accessibility and transcription which contribute to the overall expression profiles we observed. This could also in part explain the differences in profiles observed in luminal vs. basal subtypes upon a KMT2C mutation.

This study has some clear limitations, most of which stem from the reliance on publicly available data that was not generated specifically for comparison between KMT2C and KMT2D. The use of KMT2C/D ChIPseq data from multiple independent sources allows for experimental differences between the experiments however does provide higher confidence in overlapping peak calls. To minimise the differences from these experiments, all fastq files from KMT2C/D ChIP-seq experiments were inputted into the same pipeline for quality control, alignment and peak calling differing only slightly based on single or paired end sequencing. Our analysis would be improved by replication experiments for all proteins and multiple ER + cell lines for validation. Our differential accessibility analysis was also impacted by a low number of mutant samples. To better identify specific enhancers that have altered chromatin accessibility profiles in KMT2C and KMT2D mutant samples, a larger sample size would be required. It would also be beneficial to have ChIP-seq data for H3K4me1 and H3K27ac in patient samples to integrate with ATAC-seq and expression data to further elucidate the histone-methylation related mechanism behind changed expression profiles. All analyses in patient samples will be impacted by sample differences which cannot all be controlled. Despite these limitations, this study also emphasises the invaluable nature of open and accessible data. Whilst more refined and specific analyses will require experiments set up for the purpose of results required, we have been able to elucidate different regulatory mechanisms between the KMT2C and KMT2D paralogues and identify leads for further investigation which would not have been possible without access to the multiple datasets that we utilised.

In conclusion, we have shown that luminal patient samples with a KMT2C mutation show gene expression profiles consistent with decreased cell cycle regulation, similar to those in endocrine resistant cell lines, and increased EMT supporting oncogenic mechanisms that have been elucidated in ER + cell lines. Whilst KMT2C mutation/loss is beneficial to ER + breast cancers, these cell lines show dependency on the wildtype version of its paralog KMT2D which has a set of oestrogen response genes that it uniquely regulates in MCF-7 cells. This analysis reveals that in the context of ER + breast cancers, KMT2C and KMT2D should be considered to have contrasting roles.
