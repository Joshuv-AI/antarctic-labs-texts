Phase 1/2, open-label study of oral bacterial supplementation (EDP1503) plus pembrolizumab in participants with advanced or metastatic microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, and checkpoint inhibitor–relapsed tumors
Authors: Judy S. Wang, Edward R. Arrowsmith, J. Thaddeus Beck, Jennifer Friedmann, Rahima Jamal, Duncan McHale, Humphrey Gardner, Michael J. Chisamore, Susanna V. Ulahannan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We report a phase 1/2 study evaluating EDP1503 (capsule containing Bifidobacterium animalis lactis) ± pembrolizumab in participants with microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer (MSS CRC), triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), or other tumor types that relapsed after responding to immunotherapy (KEYNOTE-939/EDP1503-101; NCT03775850). Participants (≥ 18 years) had confirmed advanced/metastatic tumors and progressive disease (PD), were intolerant/nonresponsive to recommended treatment, and had measurable disease (RECIST v1.1). Cohorts were: MSS CRC (Cohort A), metastatic/locally advanced TNBC (Cohort B), and PD following partial response/stable disease for ≥ 6 months during anti‒PD-(L)1 therapy (≥ 8 months during anti‒PD-(L)1 therapy plus chemotherapy for non–small-cell lung cancer) (Cohort C). Participants received oral EDP1503 (2 or 4 capsules twice daily [BID]) for 2 weeks, then EDP1503 (2 or 4 capsules BID) plus intravenous pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks until PD, participant withdrawal, investigator decision, intolerable toxicity, or completion of 35 cycles. Primary endpoints were safety/tolerability, objective response rate (RECIST v1.1), and immune-response rate. Secondary endpoints included duration of clinical benefit, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). Of 69 participants, objective responses were observed in 3 (2 in Cohort B and 1 in Cohort C with partial responses received 4 capsules BID). For participants receiving 4 capsules BID, median duration of clinical benefit (95% CI) was 8.7 months (5.5 months‒not evaluable), median PFS was 1.8 (1.7‒1.9) months, and median OS was 7.8 (2.5‒13.5) months. Grade ≥ 3 adverse events occurred in 28 participants (40.6%), with no new safety signals. EDP1503 plus pembrolizumab had manageable safety but limited clinical activity.Trial registration: KEYNOTE-939, EDP1503-101 trial: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03775850.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10637-025-01573-0.

Background

Despite significant improvements made by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to improve clinical outcomes for cancer [1], a subpopulation of patients remain either unresponsive to treatment or develop resistance. There are multiple resistance mechanisms for cancer progression following immunotherapy, including the influence of the microbiome [2]. Composition of the gut microbiota has been demonstrated to systemically influence immune responses impacting health [3]. Therefore, one approach to increase the proportion of responders would be augmentation of the microbiome via administration of defined bacteria with immunomodulatory properties and manipulation of the immune microenvironment with live biotherapeutic strains of bacteria [4].

Enrichment of specific intestinal bacterial strains can enhance the antitumor response induced by immunotherapies in both murine models and cancer patients. In mice, fecal transplants from ICI responders but not nonresponders successfully engendered an antitumor response [5]. In patients with metastatic melanoma before receipt of immunotherapy, a significant association was observed between beneficial bacterial strains and clinical response, which was recapitulated in mouse melanoma models [6, 7]. These studies provide evidence that manipulation of the microbiome could potentially improve therapeutic efficacy over administration of immune checkpoint blockade therapy alone.

Multiple studies have associated certain bacterial strains in the microbiome—and their sustained oral supplementation—with an immunotherapy response [8]. The benefits of bifidobacteria on stimulating an antitumor immune response were shown in mice, with additive or potential synergistic effects demonstrated when combined with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1)–specific antibody therapy [9]. In mice, oral administration of live bifidobacteria alone demonstrated a similar degree of tumor control as anti–PD-(L)1 therapy, and the combination was significantly more effective than each therapy alone [10]. In clinical studies, antitumor activity of live bacterial agents was demonstrated in a phase 1 study of metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) treated with nivolumab (a PD-1 inhibitor) plus ipilimumab (a cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 inhibitor) with a supplemental live bacterial agent (CBM588, containing Clostridium butyricum). Participants receiving CBM588 experienced significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) versus those who did not (12.7 versus 2.5 months; hazard ratio [HR], 0.15; 95% CI, 0.05–0.47; P = 0.001) [11]. Furthermore, a phase 1/2 study of MRx0518, a bacterial supplement containing Enterococcus gallinarum [12], combined with pembrolizumab (a PD-1 inhibitor) showed an objective response rate (ORR) of 22% and 33%, respectively, in participants with mRCC and metastatic non–small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who had progressive disease (PD) after initial response to ICIs [13].

EDP1503 is a live bacterial biotherapeutic containing Bifidobacterium animalis lactis. In preclinical studies, administration of oral EDP1503 activated effector lymphocyte populations (natural killer, CD4+, and CD8+ cells) and induced a proinflammatory gene signature, with elevations in C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10, interleukin-12p70, interferon-gamma, interleukin-1 beta, tumor necrosis factor, and interleukin-6 [14, 15]. Additionally, in tumor models, EDP1503 inhibited tumor growth when administered alone and had greater effect when combined with anti–PD-1 therapy [14, 15]. These data provide the rationale for KEYNOTE-939/EDP1503-101, a phase 1/2 study of EDP1503 with/without pembrolizumab to treat microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer (MSS CRC), triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), or tumors that progressed after initially responding to immunotherapy (KEYNOTE-939, EDP1503-101; NCT03775850).

Methods

Study participants

KEYNOTE-939/EDP1503-101 was a phase 1/2, open-label study of EDP1503 with pembrolizumab (KEYTRUDA®) conducted at 8 sites across the United States and Canada between December 19, 2018 (first participant, first visit), and May 25, 2021 (last participant, last visit). Participants were adults with histologically/cytologically confirmed advanced/metastatic solid tumors with PD after unsuccessful treatment with available therapies or who refused or were intolerant to treatment and who had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1.

Participants were enrolled in 3 cohorts. Cohort A comprised participants with MSS, histologically/cytologically confirmed CRC with adenocarcinomas who received prior treatment with fluoropyrimidine-, oxaliplatin-, or irinotecan-based chemotherapies (eg, FOLFOX and FOLFIRI) and an anti–epidermal growth factor receptor therapy if indicated by National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines. Cohort B comprised participants with metastatic/locally advanced TNBC without progesterone receptor, estrogen receptor, or human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 expression, and radiologic/objective evidence of recurrence or PD after ≥ 1 line of chemotherapy or checkpoint inhibitor for metastatic breast cancer. Cohort C comprised participants with NSCLC, bladder cancer, gastroesophageal cancer, RCC, or any cancer with microsatellite instability; with PD; and with previous partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD) for ≥ 6 months after receiving anti–PD-(L)1 therapy (≥ 8 months for participants with NSCLC after anti–PD-(L)1 therapy plus chemotherapy). PD was defined as PD documented within 12 weeks after the last of ≥ 2 doses of an approved anti–PD-(L)1 therapy, confirmed by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Initial evidence of PD was confirmed by a second assessment ≥ 4 weeks from the first documented PD, in the absence of rapid clinical progression. The study protocol and its amendments were approved by applicable institutional review boards. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study design and treatment

Participants received an initial run-in dose of oral EDP1503 monotherapy (each capsule containing ≥ 7.5 × 1010 colony-forming units [CFU] B animalis lactis) twice per day (BID) for 2 weeks. Participants who did not experience a dose-limiting toxicity and were clinically stable went on to the main part of the study, where they received a combination of oral EDP1503 BID plus intravenous pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks for up to 35 cycles (~ 2 years). Participants enrolled under an earlier version of the protocol (version 2.0; November 16, 2018) received 2 capsules of EDP1503 (≥ 1.5 × 1011 CFU) BID; participants enrolled after this under the next protocol iteration (version 3.0; July 30, 2019) received 4 capsules (≥ 3.0 × 1011 CFU) BID, following review by the safety monitoring committee. Treatment with EDP1503 and pembrolizumab continued until PD confirmed by RECIST version 1.1 or Immune Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (iRECIST) [16], participant withdrawal, investigator decision, intolerable toxicity, or completion of 35 cycles of pembrolizumab.

Tumor imaging occurred at baseline using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, every 6 weeks until week 36, then every 12 weeks until final study follow-up. Participants with a confirmed objective response or SD continued treatment, with reevaluations every 6 weeks, until confirmed PD by iRECIST or intolerable toxicity. Toxicity was assessed on an ongoing basis.

Study endpoints

Primary objectives of KEYNOTE-939/EDP1503-101 were safety, tolerability, and antitumor activity of EDP1503 alone and in combination with pembrolizumab, as measured by primary endpoints including ORR by RECIST version 1.1 and immune-response rate (iRR) by iRECIST. Secondary endpoints were distribution and elimination of EDP1503, duration of response (DOR), clinical benefit rate (CBR), duration of clinical benefit, PFS, and overall survival (OS).

ORR was defined as the proportion of participants with a best objective response (BOR) of either PR or complete response (CR) per RECIST version 1.1 by investigator assessment. PR was confirmed by a second assessment ≥ 4 weeks after the first assessment. SD was determined ≥ 8 weeks after the start of treatment. BOR did not incorporate responses after the first receipt of subsequent anticancer therapy or the first instance of PD. CBR was defined as the proportion of participants with CR, PR, or SD per RECIST version 1.1. PFS was defined as the time from beginning study medication to first documentation of PD per RECIST version 1.1 or death due to any cause. Participants without documented PD were censored on the date of the last adequate tumor measurement. OS was defined as the time from beginning study medication to death.

Adverse events (AEs) were assessed from treatment initiation through ≤ 30 days after treatment discontinuation (≤ 90 for serious AEs) and were graded using National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Treatment relatedness of AEs was based on investigator decision.

Exploratory biomarker analyses were conducted, including the presence of EDP1503 in the stool (polymerase chain reaction); tumor inflammation signature (TIS), an expression profile of genes predictive of anti–PD-1 efficacy [17]; and changes in the percentages of infiltrated immune cell populations within the tumor before/after treatment. Fecal samples were collected and the microbiome (both the microbial composition and Shannon Diversity index through 16S sequencing) was analyzed at baseline, day 4 of the EDP1503 run-in, day 1 of cycles 1 and 2 of pembrolizumab treatment, at the end of treatment, and 1 to 4 weeks after treatment termination.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculations varied depending on cohort. For Cohort A, an admissible 2-stage design was used. For the first stage, 23 participants were enrolled; if ≥ 1 participant(s) achieved successful treatment (defined as SD or better for ≥ 4 cycles of combination therapy), an additional 7 participants would be enrolled in the second stage (total of 30 participants). If ≤ 1 participant(s) achieved successful treatment, the study treatment would be rejected. The null hypothesis was that the true response rate (RR) is ≤ 1% (RR of nonpromising treatment for MSS CRC) versus the alternative of ≥ 10% (RR of a promising treatment for MSS CRC), yielding a type I error rate of 0.10, a power of 80%, and an expected sample size of 25.

For Cohort B, Simon’s 2-stage minimax design was used. For the first stage, 16 participants were enrolled; the protocol specified that if ≥ 1 participant(s) achieved successful treatment (ie, SD or better for ≥ 4 cycles of combination therapy), an additional 15 participants would be enrolled (total of 31 participants). If ≤ 5 participants achieved successful treatment, the study treatment would be rejected. The null hypothesis was that the true RR is ≤ 10% (nonpromising) versus the alternative of ≥ 25% (promising), yielding a type I error rate of 0.10, a power of 80%, and an expected sample size of 24.

For Cohort C, a 1-stage design was used. Given the multiple therapeutic indications in this cohort, the total sample size was 50 participants (≤ 15 participants for any 1 indication). If ≥ 10 participants respond, then the RR of 10% will be rejected. For an RR of 25%, the type I error was 0.025 and the power was 85%. If no responses are observed in the first 25 enrolled participants with ≥ 3 indications, further recruitment may have been discontinued. If ≥ 2 responses were seen in any indication, this cohort may be expanded to enroll only within that indication.

For ORR and CBR, 2-sided 95% CIs were calculated based on the Clopper–Pearson method for binomial proportions. DOR, duration of clinical benefit, PFS, and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with medians and 2-sided 95% CIs calculated using the Brookmeyer–Crowley method after applying the log–log transformation. Data from participants who had documented PD during the run-in period (those who only received EDP1503 monotherapy) were excluded from efficacy analyses.

Results

The study was conducted between December 19, 2018, and May 25, 2021. Of the 91 participants who consented for the study, 70 were assigned to treatment and 69 entered the run-in period with EDP1503 monotherapy (Fig. 1). There were 33 participants in Cohort A, all of whom received 2 EDP1503 capsules BID; 27 were diagnosed with colon cancer and 6 with rectal cancer. Cohorts B and C were stopped for futility. Nineteen participants were enrolled in Cohort B; 1 participant received no study treatment, resulting in 18 participants with data available for analysis (3 received 2 capsules BID and 15 received 4 capsules BID), 15 of whom were diagnosed with TNBC. Safety analysis

A total of 66 participants (95.7%) experienced ≥ 1 treatment-emergent AE (TEAE), 28 (40.6%) of whom experienced ≥ 1 grade ≥ 3 TEAE (Table S1). Overall, 4 participants (5.8%) discontinued any study treatment due to TEAEs; all 4 participants discontinued EDP1503, and 2 of the 4 participants also discontinued pembrolizumab. The 2 participants who discontinued both study treatments experienced treatment-related AEs of decreased appetite and diarrhea (n = 1) and colitis (n = 1). The most common TEAEs overall were fatigue (31.9%), nausea (29.0%), and vomiting (26.1%), and the most common grade ≥ 3 TEAEs were anemia (8.7%) and dyspnea (5.8%). AEs considered to be related to study treatment by the investigator occurred in 41 participants (59.4%), which were classed as grade ≥ 3 in 4 participants (5.8%).

Fourteen participants (20.3%) experienced a TEAE leading to a dose interruption of EDP1503, and 9 (14.5%) experienced a TEAE leading to a dose interruption of pembrolizumab. TEAEs led to dose reductions of EDP1503 in 2 participants (2.9%); dose reductions for pembrolizumab were not allowed. A total of 46 participants (62.3%) died during the study; no deaths were determined to be related to the study treatment, and 43 deaths were due to PD under investigation. There was 1 reported death due to a pulmonary embolism of a participant who had discontinued treatment of 2 capsules BID of EDP1503, which was deemed unrelated to study treatment by the investigator. The other 2 reported deaths were due to COVID-19 and unknown causes, respectively, and occurred in participants who had discontinued treatment of 2 capsules BID of EDP1503 after PD.

Additional analyses

EDP1503 was detectable in the stool samples of participants during the dosing period and undetectable during follow-up, confirming stool clearance. Among participants in Cohort A, the colonic microbiome composition was not significantly altered by EDP1503, both during and after cessation of treatment. Microbiome composition was not examined in Cohort B or C.

At day 14, the end of the monotherapy period, there was no significant overall change in the ratio of CD8 + T cells to regulatory T cells and in the percentage of activated CD8 + T cells compared with baseline for participants overall and by cohort (Figure S3). There was no significant trend toward changing TIS gene expression across participants overall (Figure S4).

Discussion/conclusion

This phase 1/2 study investigated the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of EDP1503 alone and in combination with pembrolizumab in advanced MSS CRC (Cohort A), TNBC (Cohort B), and checkpoint inhibitor–relapsed tumors (Cohort C). EDP1503 was generally safe and well tolerated alone and in combination with pembrolizumab but demonstrated limited clinical activity in combination with pembrolizumab.

The modest beneficial effects observed with EDP1503 plus pembrolizumab were slightly more pronounced in participants with longer time on combination therapy, including in the subset of participants with TNBC with confirmed objective responses (Figure S1B). Initially, the treatments administered to the study participants, all of whom had advanced disease and had received prior therapy, were well tolerated with emerging clinical activity. However, by data cutoff, more than half of participants had PD. Possible explanations for the limited clinical efficacy observed with EDP1503 plus pembrolizumab may include that this study enrolled participants generally considered to be difficult to treat as they had progressed despite prior treatment. Particularly, those in Cohort C had PD despite prior treatment with ICIs. As such, it is difficult to compare these findings in the advanced setting or line of therapy with trials evaluating live bacterial biotherapeutics plus cancer treatments in treatment-naïve populations. Interestingly, there was no significant change in the TIS after 14 days of daily monotherapy EDP1503 treatment in participants by cohort, although some individual participants seemed to have uniform increased or decreased signature expression across the TIS biomarkers. Therefore, further evaluation of EDP1503 monotherapy is warranted to determine the role of monotherapy efficacy versus combined with the known clinically active anti-PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab [18–20] from a contribution of components perspective. While overall EDP1503 pretreatment impacted the predicted sensitivity to immunotherapy expression profile, it is not known if the impact is clinically beneficial or detrimental.

Patients with MSS CRC do not typically respond to immunotherapy [21], and findings from Cohort A, in which no participants responded to combination treatment, indicate that the addition of 2 capsules BID of this bacterial supplement does not improve response to pembrolizumab in this population; whether 4 capsules BID would be beneficial for MSS CRC remains unknown. There were 2 participants (11.1%) from Cohort B who had a PR to EDP1503 plus pembrolizumab. Although previous evidence has demonstrated low response to pembrolizumab in patients with metastatic TNBC, data from the phase 2 KEYNOTE-086 study suggest a greater benefit in participants who are PD-L1 positive versus those who are PD-L1 negative (disease control rate, 9.5% versus 4.7%) [22]. Because data describing PD-L1 expression were not collected throughout this study, it is unknown whether the subset of responders had confirmed PD-L1 expression.

Comparing studies of live bacterial biotherapeutics can be challenging because of the variability of bacterial strains tested, treatment dosage, and treatment duration. In addition, bifidobacteria can be frequently combined with other bacterial strains such as lactobacilli [23], making it difficult to compare outcomes with single-strain bifidobacteria-only biotherapeutics, such as EDP1503. Regardless, ongoing studies are evaluating oral bacterial supplementation to improve immunotherapy response. A phase 1 study showed a significant increase in PFS in mRCC treated with nivolumab plus ipilimumab and CBM-588 versus nivolumab plus ipilimumab (12.7 versus 2.5 months; HR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.05–0.47; P = 0.001) [11]. CBM-588 is being investigated in combination with nivolumab and the multikinase inhibitor cabozantinib in mRCC (NCT05122546) [24]. In addition, treatment with MRx0518 with pembrolizumab resulted in an ORR of 25% in participants with mRCC and NSCLC who had PD despite prior immunotherapy [13]. Another area of interest is fecal microbiota transplant, in which the microbiome of responders transplanted into nonresponders has demonstrated limited efficacy in preliminary studies [25].

Overall, EDP1503 was well tolerated in this population, although the combination of EDP1503 and pembrolizumab demonstrated limited clinical efficacy. Future studies are needed to determine whether oral bacterial supplementation can reliably increase sensitivity to immunotherapy in patients with solid tumors and under what conditions.
