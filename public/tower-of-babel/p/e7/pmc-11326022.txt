The future of living organ donation: confronting the challenges and embracing the opportunities of an aging donor population
Authors: Yongguang Liu, Runtao Feng, Jianrong Chen, Hongyan Yan, Xiaoyou Liu
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We recently read the article titled ‘Older living liver donors can enlarge the donor pool: a systematic review and meta-Analysis’ by Ter Burg et al., published in the International Journal of Surgery 1. The study focuses on elderly organ donors and explores the possibility of including the elderly population as potential donors, which is of significant value in alleviating the shortage of organ sources and expanding the organ donor pool. With the aging population, an increasing number of elderly potential organ donors are requesting to donate. How to view and respond to this phenomenon is worthy of in-depth reflection by the transplantation community. We aim to offer some perspectives on this article and discuss the opportunities and challenges brought about by elderly organ donors.

Organ transplantation is a crucial treatment for end-stage organ failure; however, organ shortage has always been a bottleneck restricting the development of transplantation. Due to the scarcity of donated organs, many patients unfortunately die while waiting for transplantation. With the aging population, the number of potential elderly organ donors is increasing. By pooling data from 17 studies, Ter Burg et al.1 found that compared to young donors under 50 years old, elderly donors over 50 years old showed no significant differences in the overall incidence of postoperative complications, the incidence of severe complications, and mortality. This suggests that under strict screening and management, the safety of elderly donors can be guaranteed. It is worth noting that the incidence of postoperative biliary complications in elderly donors is even lower than that of young donors1. Further research is needed. At the same time, the study also found that the postoperative quality of life of elderly donors is comparable to that of young donors1. This indicates that elderly donors can recover well to their pre-donation functional status. This finding confirms that elderly living liver donors do not lead to higher complication rates and mortality, which undoubtedly provides strong support for clinical practice.

Current research indicates that organ donation from elderly individuals provides a new source of organs for transplantation, offering hope for a new lease on life to more patients. For instance, Toyoda et al.2 reported that the kidney function and 10-year survival rate of living kidney donors over 60 years old were similar to those of younger donors (<60 years). Reese et al.3 found no significant difference in postoperative mortality and cardiovascular disease incidence between living kidney donors over 55 years old and healthy non-donor elderly individuals. Gerbase-DeLima et al.4 discovered that when the donor age was ≥60 years, there was no difference in graft survival between young and elderly recipients. Moreover, elderly organ donors can provide other potential benefits, such as shortening patient waiting times and increasing organ utilization rates. These research findings collectively support Ter Burg et al.’s perspective that elderly organ donors should be included in screening programs to expand the donor pool. Houben et al.5 reported that in a 12-month follow-up of liver donors aged ≥70 years, the graft survival rate reached 62.5%, while the 1-year graft survival rate for liver donors aged ≥65–69 years was 73.1%. Furthermore, researchers have also attempted to use elderly hearts6 and lungs7 for clinical transplantation, achieving positive outcomes. These findings significantly expand the scope of organ donors.

Despite this, there are still several limitations in the research on elderly organ donors. Firstly, most studies have relatively small sample sizes, and the level of evidence is limited. Secondly, existing studies mainly focus on the short-term outcomes of transplant recipients, while the long-term prognosis of transplanted kidneys from elderly donors remains unclear. Thirdly, there is currently insufficient data to support the safety of surgery for elderly donors (such as those over 70 years old). At the same time, elderly individuals often have more comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis, and their organ functions also decline to a certain extent, resulting in slower postoperative recovery. Furthermore, ethical issues, obtaining informed consent, and willingness to donate to elderly organ donors need to be further explored. Lastly, due to the anatomical and physiological peculiarities of elderly organs, the processes of procurement, preservation, and repair also face greater challenges.

In light of the opportunities and challenges faced by organ donation from elderly donors, we propose that future research should focus on the following aspects: (1) Establishing a standardized screening process for elderly organ donors, clarifying the upper age limit and organ function requirements for suitable donors; (2) Dynamically monitoring the postoperative recovery of elderly organ donors and recipients, and optimizing perioperative management; (3) Investigating the molecular mechanisms of aging in different organs, and identifying targets for reversing aging and improving organ quality; (4) Applying multi-omics approaches to map individualized ‘biological age profiles’ and precisely assess the status of elderly organs; (5) Integrating artificial intelligence algorithms to assist in selecting suitable organs and predicting transplantation outcomes; (6) Expanding research sample sizes and conducting multicenter prospective cohort studies to obtain higher-level evidence-based medicine; (7) Exploring new organ preservation and repair technologies, such as machine perfusion and stem cell therapy, to improve the quality and utilization of organs donated by the elderly.

In summary, organ donation from elderly donors is an important approach to alleviate the shortage of organ sources. On the basis of rigorous evaluation and optimized screening, organ donation from the elderly can expand the donor pool and benefit more patients. As transplantation professionals, we should actively conduct relevant research and make new contributions to the field of organ transplantation. We sincerely look forward to the near future, when organ donation from the elderly can benefit more patients and write a new chapter in the development of organ transplantation.
