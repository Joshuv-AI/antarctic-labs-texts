Prevalence of Anemia in Reproductive-age Females (15–49 Years) from Indian Indigenous Population: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Vaibhavkumar Shrivastav, Yogesh Murugan, Arya Ramachandran, Disha Shrivastav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anemia remains a critical public health challenge in India, disproportionately affecting indigenous populations. This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of anemia among reproductive-age females (15–49 years) from indigenous communities in India and identified associated risk factors. Four databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science) were systematically searched from 2000 to 2024 following PRISMA guidelines. Two independent reviewers conducted study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Random-effects meta-analysis with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to calculate pooled prevalence estimates, with comprehensive subgroup and meta-regression analyses. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test. Analysis of 41 studies (131,267 participants) revealed a pooled anemia prevalence of 73% (95% CI: 64–81%). Notable regional variations emerged, with the Eastern region showing the highest prevalence (84%, 95% CI: 68–93%) and the Northeastern region the lowest (53%, 95% CI: 33–72%). Areas with IFA supplementation coverage greater than 50% paradoxically showed higher observed prevalence (91%), likely reflecting targeting of interventions toward high-burden areas. At the ecological level, tropical monsoon climate zones showed the highest pooled prevalence (87%), while hot semi-arid regions had the lowest (44%). Key risk factors included labor-intensive occupations (OR = 3.18, 95% CI: 1.52–6.64), low monthly income (OR = 2.38, 95% CI: 1.42–3.98), and lower educational attainment (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.15–2.72). Köppen Climate Classification explained the greatest proportion of between-study variance in meta-regression (R² = 18.5%), though substantial heterogeneity remained unexplained (residual I² = 99.3%). The pooled anemia prevalence of 73% among reproductive-age women from Indian indigenous communities substantially exceeds the national average. Regional disparities, climatic variation, and socioeconomic factors—particularly occupation, income, and education—emerged as key determinants. These findings highlight the need for targeted, culturally appropriate interventions and region-specific programmatic strategies for anemia control in indigenous communities.

INTRODUCTION

A prevalent health condition termed anemia is defined by a lack of red blood cells, either in quantity or quality, which hinders the body’s capacity to provide enough oxygen to its tissues. It is a serious public health concern that affects people of all ages worldwide, and its prevalence and causes vary according to demographic characteristics like age, gender, and ethnicity.[1] We must step up our efforts to prevent and control anemia to improve the health of the nation’s citizens. An effective plan to treat this public health issue is urgently needed because of the morbidity and mortality consequences linked with anemia.[2] Improved maternal and infant survival rates, as well as better health outcomes for other population groups, will result from the decrease in the prevalence of anemia. In children, anemia hinders cognitive and motor development; in adults, it lowers workability and productivity and increases disease susceptibility. This increases the risk of obstetrical complications, low birth weight, fetal development impairment, and increased maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity throughout pregnancy.[3]

India hosts the world’s second-largest tribal population, with 705 ethnic groups officially recognized as Scheduled Tribes, constituting 8.6% (104 million) of the total population. These indigenous communities, predominantly concentrated in Central India and North-Eastern states, face significant socioeconomic challenges, with 45.3% living below the poverty line. Their distinct cultural practices, traditional healing systems, and geographic isolation often result in limited access to modern healthcare facilities, compounded by high illiteracy rates, particularly among tribal women (41%).[4]

Anemia is defined as hemoglobin concentration below established cut-off levels in the blood. Usually is <11 g/dL in pregnant women and <12 g/dL in non-pregnant women.[5]

Approximately 24.8% of the world’s population, or 1.62 billion people, suffer from anemia, making it a serious global health concern (WHO, 2021). Geographically, the prevalence of this illness varies significantly; South Asia has the highest incidence (48.2%), followed by Africa (44.3%), and Europe has the lowest prevalence (14.6%). The prevalence rates of 40.1% and 41.7%, respectively, indicate that pregnant women and children under five are the two demographic categories most at risk. Reproductive-age women are still at high risk; 32.9% of non-pregnant women between the ages of 15 and 49 are impacted worldwide. With yearly worldwide gross domestic product (GDP) losses estimated at $50.4 billion, the economic consequences are as significant and mostly stem from decreased worker productivity and stunted kid cognitive development.[6]

Anemia is currently a major public health concern in several nations, including India. According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS)-5 (2019–21), India’s chronically high anemia prevalence is a serious public health concern. According to the Survey, the prevalence of anemia is alarmingly on the rise across all demographic categories, affecting 57% of women aged 15–49, 67.1% of children aged 6–59 months, and 25% of men. Compared to NFHS-4 (2015–16), when the prevalence was 53.1% in women and 58.6% in children, this indicates a significant increase.[7] Significant regional and socioeconomic differences exist in the incidence of anemia; for example, West Bengal reports a prevalence of 71.4% among women, whereas Ladakh reports a startling 92.8% among children.

With rural areas having a greater burden (58.5% women, 68.3% children) than urban areas (54.1% women, 64.2% children), the rural-urban difference is as important.[8] The rising prevalence of anemia, especially among vulnerable groups like pregnant women (52.2%) and adolescent girls (59.1%), highlights the urgent need for more efficient strategies and focused interventions to address this serious health issue, even in the face of multiple national health programs and interventions.[9] Despite prevalence rates much greater than the national average, anemia among India’s tribal groups continues to be a crucial public health concern. The NFHS-5 (2019–21) found that anemia rates among tribal women and children are concerning, with up to 80% of women and 85% of children in various tribal regions suffering from the condition.[10] This high burden, which is especially common in states with major Indigenous populations, highlights the urgent need for focused interventions in these vulnerable communities and is a result of the intricate interactions between socioeconomic limitations, geographic isolation, and restricted access to healthcare.[11]

Study outcome

The prevalence of anemia in Reproductive-Age Females (15–49 years) from the Indian Indigenous Population was the outcome variable in this systematic review and meta-analysis.[14] A hemoglobin level was used to define anemia: <13.0 g/dL, women aged 15 and up who are not pregnant: <12.0 g/dL, and pregnant women: <11.0 g/dL.[15]

Information sources

We systematically searched four major databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception until November 2024. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to “anemia,” “Indigenous populations,” “India,” and “reproductive age women.” The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Additional relevant studies were identified by manually searching reference lists.[16]

Study selection and quality assessment

The retrieved papers from this systematic review and meta-analysis were imported into Rayyan software to compile and organize search results and remove duplicate articles. Two reviewers (V.S. and A.R.) independently screened the publications according to their abstracts and titles. After lengthy debates, the two reviewers agreed. A third reviewer who participated in cases of conflict was (Y.M). Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for prevalence studies. Each study was evaluated on nine domains, including sampling methods, measurement reliability, and statistical analysis. Studies were categorized as high quality (≥7 points), moderate quality (4–6 points), or low quality (≤3 points)[17] [Supplementary Table 2].

Studies were excluded for ‘unclear methodology’ if they failed to report at least three of the following four core JBI domains: (Q1) an appropriate sample frame for the target population; (Q4) a description of study subjects and setting in sufficient detail to enable reproducibility; (Q6) a valid and documented method for identifying anemia (i.e., a stated hemoglobin cut-off with measurement method); and (Q8) appropriate statistical analysis. This threshold was determined a priori and applied independently by two reviewers (V.S. and A.R.) during full-text screening, before formal JBI scoring.

No studies were excluded solely based on the JBI total score. The quality categories (high, moderate, low) were used descriptively and for sensitivity analysis purposes only. The ‘unclear methodology’ exclusion criterion operated independently at the full-text screening stage, before formal JBI scoring, based on the pre-specified domain threshold described above.

Statistics

We statistically evaluated data on the prevalence rate and risk variables of anemia in Reproductive-Age Females (15–49 years) from the Indian Indigenous Population using R version 4.2.0 with the “meta” and “metafor” packages. We also evaluated research heterogeneity using the I2 value. High heterogeneity is indicated if I2 >50% and P < 0.1. Due to anticipated heterogeneity, we used Random-effects models with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation for prevalence data, over fixed effects, as it accounts for both within-study sampling error and between-study variation, providing more conservative estimates that better reflect the true uncertainty in our findings. This approach is particularly appropriate for observational studies with diverse populations and settings, as confirmed by significant Q-statistics and tau² values indicating meaningful between-study variance.

Back-transformation of pooled estimates and confidence intervals from the arcsine scale to the probability scale was performed using the method of Miller (1978), as implemented in the meta package in R via the transf.ift function. Under this approach, the pooled arcsine-transformed estimate and its confidence limits are each individually back-transformed to proportions using the inverse Freeman-Tukey transformation. Harmonic mean sample size across included studies was used as the back-transformation parameter, as recommended for prevalence meta-analyses with heterogeneous sample sizes.

If there is a lot of variation among the included study outcomes, the subgroup analysis method was employed to try to pinpoint the evident reasons for heterogeneity; however, further investigation into the origins of heterogeneity is necessary.

This article’s subgroups are as follows: Region, anemia Mukt Bharat (AMB) situation,[18] AMB index iron-folic acid (IFA) coverage,[19] Köppen climate classification,[20] AMB key performance indicator (KPI) index,[21] Sample Range, Pregnancy Status, Study design, and Publication year [Table 1]. The P value in all statistical analyses is set at 0.05. To address the high heterogeneity among the included studies, sensitivity analyses, and influence diagnostics have been conducted. We used an Egger test and funnel plots to look for publication bias. The Egger test revealed statistically significant publication bias when the P value was less than 0.05. For risk factors, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using random-effects models.

Subgroup analyses were conducted using the following pre-specified classification systems, each defined below at first use:

The Anemia Mukt Bharat (AMB) Situation classification categorizes Indian states and districts by their prevailing anemia burden: Severe (anemia prevalence ≥ 40%) and Moderate (anemia prevalence 20–40%).[18]

The AMB Index Iron-Folic Acid (IFA) Coverage represents the percentage of the target population receiving IFA supplementation in each state or district under the AMB programmed, reported in decile ranges (≤11–20%, 21–30%, 31–40%, 41–50%, and > 50%).[19]

The AMB Key Performance Indicator (KPI) Index ranks Indian states and union territories as High Performing, Medium Performing, or Low Performing based on composite programmed delivery metrics under the AMB strategy.[21]

The Köppen Climate Classification is an internationally recognized standard system for categorizing regional climates based on temperature and precipitation patterns. In this review, study locations were assigned Köppen climate zones, including Tropical Monsoon (Am), Tropical Savanna (Aw), Hot Desert (BWh), Hot Semi-Arid (BSh), Humid Subtropical (Cfa), and Warm-Summer Humid Continental (Dfb).[20]

The meta-regression of subgroup analyses was conducted using a random-effects model in R (version 4.4.2) with the “metafor” package. Effect sizes were calculated using proportions of anemia prevalence, with variance computed based on sample sizes. For each moderator, R² values were calculated to quantify the proportion of heterogeneity explained, while I² statistics were computed to assess residual heterogeneity. The significance level was set at P < 0.05. Confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for all effect estimates. Multiple methods were used to verify R² value robustness, including bootstrap confidence intervals with 1,000 iterations.

Two complementary regression analyses were conducted. The first was a univariable meta-regression using Freeman-Tukey double arcsine-transformed proportions, which quantified the proportion of between-study variance (τ²) explained by each subgroup variable, expressed as R². The second was a mixed-effects moderator analysis using logit-transformed proportions, which tested whether individual moderator levels were statistically significant predictors using z-tests and compared overall model fit using Akaike Information Criterion/Bayesian Information Criterion (AIC/BIC). Because these two analyses operate on different effect-size scales (arcsine vs. logit), their β coefficients are not directly comparable in magnitude; however, the direction and significance of substantive findings are interpreted jointly.

RESULTS

Literature search and identified results

The initial search identified 4,007 records across databases (PubMed = 1,429; Scopus = 838; Embase = 932; Web of Science = 808). After removing 870 duplicates, 3,137 records were screened by title and abstract.[22] Full-text assessment of 58 potentially eligible articles led to the inclusion of 41 studies. The main reasons for exclusion were non-reporting of prevalence (n = 4), review articles (n = 2), irrelevant outcomes (n = 4), inappropriate age groups (n = 3), and unclear methodology—specifically, absence of hemoglobin measurement method and sampling description (n = 2). Features of the included research

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 41 publications.[23] The study encompasses research from various regions of India, with the following distribution: Northeastern (seven studies), Southwestern (seven studies), Central (six studies), Eastern (eight studies), Northwestern (four studies), Western (four studies combined), Southeastern (two studies), India-wide studies (one study), Northcentral (one study), and Southern region (one study). The sample size for the included studies ranged from 48 to 1,14,089 participants. The selected studies were predominantly cross-sectional studies (41 studies) with one prospective cohort study. The 41 studies involved 1,31,267 Reproductive-Age Females (15–49 years) from the Indian Indigenous Population. The studies were conducted across different geographical locations, incorporating various Köppen climate classifications and indigenous populations, focusing on different age ranges and pregnancy status. The research presents comprehensive data on anemia prevalence with associated 95% confidence intervals across these diverse populations and settings. The included studies represented a wide range of Indigenous populations across India. Specific tribal groups identified included Sahariya, Gond, Santhal, Baiga, Tharu, Koraga, Chakhesang, Tangkhul Naga, Koya, Bhil, and several particularly vulnerable tribal groups (PVTGs). However, some studies reported only a general “tribal” population without specifying the exact ethnic group, limiting detailed subgroup analysis by tribe. [Table 1].

The overall pooled prevalence of anemia among Indigenous populations shows significant regional variations. The Eastern region had the highest prevalence at (84%, 95% CI: 68–93%), followed by Central region at (83%, 95% CI: 52–95%) and Southwestern region at (82%, 95% CI: 60–93%), which are substantially higher than the (NA Study-National Family Health survey and Multicentric Study) National prevalence of (57%, 95% CI: 16–90%). The lowest prevalence was observed in the Southeastern region at (63%, 95% CI: 58–67%). This substantial geographic variation suggests the influence of regional factors like climate, diet, healthcare access, and socioeconomic conditions.

When analyzed by the AMB situation classification, areas with severe anemia (≥40%) showed a prevalence of (74%, 95% CI: 64–82%), while moderate anemia areas (20–40%) had (73%, 95% CI: 44–91%) both exceeding the national prevalence of 57%. The AMB Index IFA Coverage analysis revealed that regions with >50% coverage had the highest prevalence at (91%, 95% CI: 22–100%), suggesting that high-burden areas may be receiving more focused interventions. A notable finding is that areas with IFA coverage between 11% and 20% showed the lowest prevalence of anemia at 52% (95% CI: 27–76%), This is an interesting and counterintuitive finding because we would generally expect higher IFA coverage to be associated with lower anemia rates. This unexpected result suggests that there may be other factors influencing anemia prevalence in these regions, such as dietary patterns, socioeconomic conditions, or access to other nutritional interventions, beyond just IFA supplementation coverage.

Climate classification analysis showed that tropical monsoon regions had the highest prevalence at 87% (95% CI: 70–95%), while hot semi-arid regions had the lowest at 44% (95% CI: 36–53%). This pattern suggests potential environmental and ecological influences on anemia prevalence.

Sample size analysis revealed that studies with 501–1,000 participants showed higher prevalence (87%, 95% CI: 62–97%) compared to larger studies with > 1,000 participants (51%, 95% CI: 26–75%), indicating possible sampling variations. The prevalence was similar between pregnant (70%, 95% CI: 37–90%) and non-pregnant women (73%, 95% CI: 61–83%), suggesting that anemia is a widespread problem regardless of pregnancy status.

Publication year analysis showed consistent findings between older (2010–2018) and recent (2019–2024) studies, both reporting around 73% prevalence, indicating persistent high levels of anemia over time. The high heterogeneity (I² >90%) across most analyses suggests substantial variability in study findings, likely due to methodological differences and true variations in population characteristics [Table 2].

Univariable meta-regression of subgroup variables (arcsine-transformed prevalence)

The meta-regression of subgroup analysis reveals several important findings regarding anemia prevalence among Indigenous populations, with varying degrees of explanatory power across different categories.

Climate and Geographic Impact stand out as the strongest predictors, with Köppen Climate Classification having the highest R² among examined subgroup variables (18.5%), though this explained only a modest fraction of total between-study variance (τ² = 1.627).

Iron Supplementation Influence emerges as the second most important factor, with AMB Index IFA Coverage having the second highest R² (11.4%), explaining a small proportion of between-study variance, with 88.6% remaining unexplained (residual I² = 99.3%).

Regional Variations showed interesting patterns, with Eastern regions displaying the highest coefficient (β = 0.864), though the relationship was not statistically significant. The Northeastern region showed the lowest coefficient (β = 0.554), suggesting substantial geographic disparities in anemia prevalence.

Sample Range explained a small proportion of variance (R² = 7.1%), leaving 92.9% unexplained. All remaining subgroup variables explained less than 1% of between-study variance individually. Collectively, all examined variables explained only a small fraction of total heterogeneity, consistent with the high residual I² (99.3–99.7%) observed across all models.

However, note that all β coefficients in this analysis are on the arcsine scale and are not numerically comparable to β coefficients reported in the moderator analysis below, which uses a logit scale [Supplementary Table 4].

Collectively, the nine subgroup variables examined in the meta-regression explained only a modest cumulative proportion of between-study variance, with the highest individual R² being 18.5% (Köppen Climate Classification). The persistently high residual I² (99.3–99.7%) across all models indicates that most of the between-study heterogeneity remains unexplained by the study-level variables available in this review. This is consistent with the overall τ² = 1.627 and I² = 98.7%, reflecting true heterogeneity in anemia prevalence driven by individual-level factors—including dietary patterns, specific infectious disease burdens, household socioeconomic conditions, and intra-tribal cultural practices—that cannot be captured by aggregate study-level moderators. These findings underscore the limitations of ecological-level subgroup classification as a tool for heterogeneity explanation and highlight the need for individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis to more fully decompose sources of variance in future work.

The risk factors of anemia in reproductive-age females (15–49 years) from the Indian indigenous population

The systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that anemia among reproductive-age females (15–49 years) from the Indian Indigenous Population is predominantly influenced by socioeconomic. Significant risk factors include occupation, where women in labor-intensive roles have a higher susceptibility to anemia (OR = 3.18, P < 0.05), likely due to increased physical demands and limited access to adequate nutrition or healthcare. Low monthly income also emerged as a major determinant (OR = 2.38, P < 0.05), highlighting the impact of poverty and resource constraints on health outcomes. Women aged 25–39 years had twice the odds of being anemic compared to other age groups (OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 0.99–4.06, P = 0.042). Women with lower education levels had significantly higher odds of anemia (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.15–2.72, P = 0.009). Conversely, factors such as BMI, place of residence, and wealth index were not significantly associated with anemia, suggesting that its occurrence is shaped more by systemic socioeconomic than individual demographic variables. Addressing these underlying issues is essential for effectively mitigating anemia in these populations [Supplementary Table 6].

Mixed-effects moderator analysis (logit-transformed prevalence)

The meta-analysis reveals substantial heterogeneity in anemia prevalence among reproductive-age Indigenous women (15–49 years) in India, influenced by multiple factors. Temporal analysis showed significant patterns from 2010 to 2018 (intercept = 1.5171, P = 0.0068), particularly in regional variations. The Northeastern region (−2.2668, P = 0.0063) and India overall (−2.1261, P = 0.0009) demonstrated significantly lower prevalence than other regions during this period.

Notably, Köppen Climate Classification—which had the highest R² (18.5%) in the univariable meta-regression—showed no individually significant subgroup contrasts in the moderator analysis. These findings are compatible: R² measures the overall variance explained by the variable, while the z-tests in the moderator analysis test each climate category against the reference level. A variable can explain substantial between-study variance while having no single contrast reach significance when the effect is distributed across many categories rather than concentrated in one.

IFA (Iron-Folic Acid) coverage emerged as a critical determinant, with areas having 11–20% coverage showing significantly lower prevalence (−3.0557, P = 0.0121) compared to areas with >50% coverage (intercept = 3.0031, P = 0.0007). The AMB_KPI Index analysis revealed significant findings in the NA category (−1.5188, P = 0.0684) which included NFHS survey data, suggesting important insights from national-level surveillance.

Other factors including Köppen Climate Classification, Sample Range, Study Design, AMB_Situation, and pregnancy status did not show statistically significant associations with anemia prevalence in the meta-moderator analysis.

These findings emphasize the need for strengthened IFA supplementation programs and region-specific healthcare strategies, particularly focusing on areas identified through national surveys. Addressing these disparities through targeted interventions and improved healthcare access is crucial for reducing the anemia burden among Indigenous women in India [Supplementary Table 5].

Influence diagnostic analysis

Based on the influence diagnostic analysis of the meta-analysis examining anemia prevalence among reproductive-age females (15–49 years) from Indian Indigenous populations, no severely influential cases were identified. The diagnostic plots revealed balanced study weights and consistent leverage values across all 41 included studies, indicating robust meta-analytic results. While there is heterogeneity in the findings (I² = 98.7%), it is not driven by individual influential studies, as evidenced by stable Cook’s distances and DFFITS values. The covariance ratios remained close to 1, suggesting reliable precision estimates. A slight increase in influence was noted for later studies, but none exceeded critical thresholds that would warrant exclusion. This comprehensive influence analysis supports the reliability of the pooled prevalence estimate of 73% (95% CI: 64–81%) and suggests that the findings are not disproportionately affected by any single study [Supplementary Figure 11].

DISCUSSION

Anemia is highly prevalent among reproductive-age women (15–49 years) in Indian Indigenous communities, associated with socioeconomic, nutritional, and environmental factors. The 41 studies involved 2,79,796 Reproductive-Age Females (15–49 years) from Indian Indigenous Cultures, revealing a pooled prevalence of 73 (95% CI: 64–81), which was more than the national prevalence of anemia in India, which was (60%).[58] The pooled prevalence observed in the present study (73%) is substantially higher than estimates reported in broader populations from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as 42.7% among pregnant women reported by Rahman et al.[65] Global estimates suggest that anemia prevalence in LMIC populations generally ranges between 30% and 50%, depending on demographic and regional factors. This difference is expected, as prior studies primarily represent general populations, whereas the present analysis focuses specifically on Indigenous communities in India, who experience disproportionate socioeconomic disadvantage, nutritional deficiencies, and limited access to healthcare.

Therefore, this comparison is intended not to establish equivalence, but to highlight the magnitude of disparity. The substantially higher prevalence observed in Indigenous populations underscores the presence of structural inequities, and indicates that these communities bear a disproportionately higher burden of anemia compared to general LMIC populations.

The average prevalence of anemia was found to be 46.5% with a range of 18.1% to 75% in a review article by Ali et al.[66] in developing countries.

The extremely wide prediction interval (17%–97%) reflects the high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 98.7%) and indicates that the true prevalence of anemia may vary markedly across different Indigenous populations in India. This suggests that the pooled estimate should not be interpreted as a single underlying “true” prevalence, but rather as a summary of a highly heterogeneous distribution. Such variability is expected given the diversity of included studies in terms of time, pregnancy status, hemoglobin cut-offs, sample size, and ecological contexts, including multiple climate zones. The persistence of high residual heterogeneity despite subgroup, and meta-regression analyses further indicates that unmeasured individual-level and contextual factors contribute substantially to this variation.

The Subgroup reveals a high overall anemia prevalence of 73% among reproductive-age females in Indian indigenous cultures, with significant regional disparities. The Central (83%) and Eastern (84%) regions reported the highest prevalence, while the Northeastern and Western regions had lower rates (53%). Heterogeneity across studies was consistently high (I² >98%), reflecting the diverse geographic and socio-cultural contexts. Similar regional variations have been documented in other indigenous populations globally, where geographic isolation often correlates with higher anemia rates (Khambalia et al.).[68] The consistently high heterogeneity (I² >98%) across regions suggests that local factors may be correlated with anemia risk, though causal directionality cannot be established from cross-sectional data.

Another notable finding of the subgroup of studies that used random sampling showed a prevalence of 22% (95% CI [17–27], 22 studies), whereas, in those with non-random sampling, the prevalence was 27% (95% CI [23–30], 61 studies) by Macena et al.,[67] Brazilian women. The burden of anemia is overwhelmingly higher among Indigenous groups compared to the general population and represents a moderate (20–39.9%) to severe (≥40%) public health problem to Khambalia et al.[68] in Multiple countries.

The observed variation in anemia prevalence across Köppen climate zones suggests potential ecological-level associations between environmental context and anemia burden. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The climate classification was assigned at the study level based on geographic location rather than measured at the individual level. Therefore, these results do not imply a direct causal relationship between climate and anemia risk.

Instead, climate zones may serve as proxies for broader contextual factors, including food availability, agricultural patterns, infectious disease burden, and socioeconomic conditions, which may vary systematically across regions. These ecological associations highlight potential areas for further investigation but should not be interpreted as evidence of individual-level effects.

Our analysis of the AMB program indicators revealed an unexpected pattern: areas with higher IFA coverage (>50%) showed higher anemia prevalence (91%, 95% CI: 22–100%). This counterintuitive finding suggests that while intervention programs may be targeting high-burden areas, implementation effectiveness requires attention. The AMB KPI analysis showed minimal differences between high-performing (72.3%) and low-performing (70.7%) states, indicating that current performance metrics may not adequately capture program impact.

The risk factor analysis identified several significant socioeconomic determinants. Women in labor-intensive occupations showed higher susceptibility (OR = 3.18, P < 0.05), while low monthly income emerged as a major risk factor (OR = 2.38, P < 0.05). Educational status was also significantly associated with anemia prevalence, with lower education levels associated with significantly higher odds of anemia (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.15–2.72). These findings align with broader research on social determinants of health in indigenous populations (Subramanian et al., 2006).

The meta-regression analysis revealed a slight but concerning upward trend in anemia prevalence, with an annual increase of 0.03 percentage points. This trend, despite ongoing national health programs, suggests the need for more targeted and culturally appropriate interventions. The consistently high prevalence across different periods (2010–2018: 73%, 2019–2024: 73%) indicates the persistence of underlying structural barriers to anemia prevention and control.

The strengths of our analysis include comprehensive database coverage, robust statistical methodology including sensitivity analyses, extensive subgroup and meta-regression analyses, assessment of temporal trends, and rigorous quality assessment of included studies.

The burden of anemia is likely to vary considerably across different Indigenous groups due to variations in dietary practices, cultural norms, genetic predispositions, environmental exposures, and access to healthcare. In the present review, we identified substantial diversity in tribal populations, including groups such as Sahariya, Santhal, Gond, Baiga, Bhil, Tharu, and others. However, the available data did not permit robust subgroup analysis at the level of individual tribal groups.

Furthermore, several studies did not specify the exact tribal affiliation and instead reported broadly on “tribal” populations. This aggregation may mask important intra-group heterogeneity, and limit the ability to draw tribe-specific conclusions. Future research should prioritize detailed reporting of tribal identity to enable more targeted and culturally appropriate public health interventions.

Limitation

We acknowledge limitations, including high statistical heterogeneity (I² =98.7%) despite subgroup analyses, potential publication bias (though adjustment did not substantially alter findings), limited data from some geographic regions, English language restriction, and variation in anemia definitions across studies. The use of Freeman-Tukey double arcsine back-transformation may produce misleading confidence intervals in subgroups with extreme pooled prevalences or small numbers of contributing studies. For example, the 95% CI for the IFA coverage >50% subgroup (91% [95% CI: 22–100%]), the national prevalence subgroup (57% [95% CI: 16–90%]), and the pregnant women subgroup (70% [95% CI: 37–90%]) are implausibly wide, reflecting the instability of FT-DAS back-transformation at extreme values under high heterogeneity. Readers should exercise caution when interpreting these specific subgroup estimates.

The analysis of the Köppen climate classification is based on study-level ecological assignment rather than individual-level exposure. As such, the observed associations are subject to ecological fallacy, and causal inferences at the individual level cannot be made.

The restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language bias, particularly in the Indian context where relevant public health research is also published in regional language journals. This may have led to the exclusion of studies conducted in remote or underserved Indigenous communities, which are often reported in local or non-indexed sources. As a result, the pooled estimate may not fully capture the diversity of anemia prevalence across all Indigenous populations.

The direction of this bias is difficult to determine with certainty; however, it is plausible that studies published in regional languages may represent more localized and underserved populations with potentially higher disease burden. Their exclusion may therefore have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence. Conversely, the inclusion of smaller or methodologically heterogeneous studies could also have increased variability in the estimates. These considerations highlight the importance of cautious interpretation and the need for more inclusive future evidence synthesis efforts.

Additionally, incomplete reporting of specific tribal affiliations in some studies limited the ability to conduct subgroup analyses at the level of individual Indigenous groups.

CONCLUSION

Our systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence on anemia prevalence and its determinants among reproductive-age females from indigenous communities in India. Given the very high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 98.7%), the pooled prevalence estimates of 73% (95% CI: 64–81%) should be interpreted as a broad descriptive indicator rather than a precise population estimate. Addressing this burden will require targeted interventions that account for regional, climatic, and socioeconomic determinants, alongside strengthened implementation of the existing National Anemia Mukt Bharat Programmed. Future research should priorities longitudinal designs, standardized outcome measurement, and qualitative investigation of cultural and behavioral factors to better inform evidence-based policy.
