Patterns and determinants of mitogenomic evolution in Bilateria
Authors: Ivan Jakovlić, Yi-Wen Ma, Tong Ye, Rui Han, Xiang Liu, Chuan-Yu Xiang, Hong Zou, Jin Zhang, Rui Song, Wen-Xiang Li, Gui-Tang Wang, Yindong Tong, Fei Liu, Benhe Zeng, Jinsong Chen, Dong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial genomes of some animals remained almost unchanged architecturally for over half a billion of years, while other lineages exhibit conspecific variability. The drivers underlying this disparity remain unknown. We analyse over 10,000 bilaterian mitogenomes to test whether locomotory capacity and parasitic lifestyle can explain the variability in architectural evolutionary rates, and attempt to answer multiple other evolutionary questions. Double-stranded (genes on both strands) architecture is the most likely ancestral state for most major radiations (phylum and above). We identify over twenty transitions to single-stranded architectures, and the evidence of state-reversals (in Annelida and Mollusca). Gene order rearrangement and sequence evolution rates are positively correlated (r = 0.69), and both are increased in single-stranded mitogenomes, parasites, and species with low locomotory capacity. The latter two categories exhibit increased prevalence of single-strandedness and GC skew inversions. Mitogenome size is negatively correlated with evolutionary rates, whereas the effective population size is decoupled from all tested evolutionary variables. Endotherms exhibit slower evolutionary rates than ectotherms in Bilateria, but faster in Chordata. Here, we provide evidence that purifying selection mediated by locomotory capacity and species ecology is a major driver of mitogenomic architectural evolution, but other variables are required to fully explain the observed patterns.

Introduction

Mitogenomic architectural rearrangement rates, i.e. changes in the order and strand distribution of genes encoded by the animal mitochondrial genomes, vary dramatically among bilaterian lineages, but factors underlying this disparity in architectural evolutionary rates remain poorly understood1–3. On the conserved end of the spectrum, most species of Chordata exhibit a mitogenomic architecture that remained almost unchanged over half a billion years1,4,5. In contrast to this, some lineages of Mollusca, Isopoda, and Nematoda exhibit within-family/genus architectural variability2,6–9, and nematodes from the family Mermithidae even exhibit conspecific architectural variability7. Such stasis in the architectural evolution over long time spans is an indicator that architectural rearrangements are commonly deleterious, and likely disrupting mitogenomic replication, transcription, and gene expression co-regulation1–3. However, no known models explain this apparent disparity in the stringency of selective constraints among lineages.

Recently, we have shown that parasitism and locomotory capacity have the power to explain a major proportion of variability in mitogenomic sequence evolutionary rates across the Bilateria10. We proposed that reduction in energy demands allows reduced (ATP) production, which results in relaxation of purifying selection pressures—i.e. increased accumulation of deleterious mutations and accelerated evolutionary rates. Reduction in locomotory capacity and high metabolic dependence on the host (“auxotrophy”) characterise many parasitic animals, resulting in disproportionately elevated evolutionary rates, particularly in endoparasites10. Based on that finding, and the fact that isolated parasitic lineages exhibit highly unusual, “disrupted”, rapidly evolving mitogenomic architectures7–9,11–13, we hypothesised that parasitism and locomotory capacity variability may also have the power to explain the variability in architectural evolution.

As this hypothesis posits purifying selection pressure as the central mediator, it implies that architectural rearrangement rates (commonly quantified as gene order rearrangement rates—GORR) and sequence evolution (quantified as branch length) should exhibit a positive correlation. While some studies found support for this hypothesis9,14–18, others found no correlation in isolated lineages1,19,20. Some studies proposed that GORR and branch length are correlated only in lineages with high rearrangement rates, and not in those with low rearrangement rates1,14, but this hypothesis remains untested statistically.

As regards parasitism, studies of its putative association with mitogenomic architecture evolution are scarce and results inconsistent: no correlation in Diptera (Insecta)21, inconsistent results in Hymenoptera (Insecta)22,23, no correlation in Annelida18, and a recent study of Platyhelminthes found decreased GORR rates in parasites20. Aside from a few sporadic mentions of this putative association1,24–26, no other study had attempted to test it systematically. Our own studies of mitogenomes of parasitic animals (Supplementary Table 1) indicate that isolated lineages of nematodes and crustaceans do exhibit rapidly evolving architectures8,9,11,27, but parasitic flatworms (Neodermata) exhibit comparatively slow-evolving architectures28. As regards the hypothesis that species with low locomotory capacity should exhibit increased GORR rates, to our knowledge, it remains untested.

Several other questions regarding the correlates and patterns of mitogenomic evolution remain open. The observation that some species exhibit disproportionately rapid architectural evolutionary rates has produced a hypothesis that mitogenomic architecture evolution is “discontinuous”; i.e. that once a rearrangement event has destabilized the mitogenomic architecture, this is often followed by a cascade of rearrangements11. Conversely, several comparative analyses suggested that architectural evolution is often re-stabilised after a burst of rearrangements9,18,20, indicating an “episodic” evolutionary pattern, where purifying selection pressure is restored. Overall, the dynamics of architectural evolutionary rates remain poorly understood.

Further, mitogenomes can have genes encoded on both strands (“double-stranded”) or on only one strand (“single-stranded”)2. Boore4 proposed that evolutionary transitions from double-stranded to single-stranded architectures may be irreversible if they are accompanied by the loss of regulatory elements on the noncoding strand (“ratchet mechanism”). The evolutionary history of mitogenomic strandedness has never been systematically studied in animals, so the total number of state switches remains unknown, and the “ratchet” hypothesis untested. Even the ancestral state for bilaterians is debated, with some studies proposing a single-stranded ancestral architecture2,29, whereas both studies that attempted to systematically infer the ancestral bilaterian (ur-bilaterian) architecture settled on a vertebrate-like double-stranded architecture5,30.

A correlation between single-strandedness and highly rearranged mitogenomic architectures was proposed2, but not tested statistically. We also noticed that some of the fastest mitogenomic DNA sequence evolutionary rates are exhibited by parasitic lineages that have exclusively or predominantly single-stranded architectures, such as Neodermata (Platyhelminthes), Chromadorea (Nematoda), and Acanthocephala10. This is not a rule, as parasitic Orthonectida and Enoplea (Nematoda) exhibit both highly elevated evolutionary rates and double-stranded architectures2,10,31. We hypothesise that single-strandedness may be an accidental consequence of relaxed purifying selection pressures, allowing a wider range of rearrangement types, thus increasing the likelihood of a rearrangement producing a single-stranded architecture, which may then become fixed via the ratchet mechanism. This implies that single-stranded lineages should exhibit elevated sequence and architectural evolutionary rates, and be more common in parasitic and low-locomotory species.

Compositional asymmetry between strands (GC/AT skew) is linked to mitogenomic architecture via asymmetrical mutational pressures32. Metabolic rate should be positively associated with GC skew magnitude via mutagenic reactive oxygen species33, but crustaceans exhibited a negative correlation between these two variables, mediated by purifying selection driven by locomotory capacity variability34. This suggests the existence of a positive correlation between GC skew and both GORR and branch length, as well as increased average GC skew values in single-stranded mitogenomes, parasites, and low-locomotory lineages. Aside from the association between GC skew and GORR in Annelida18, and elevated GC skews in parasitic crustaceans34, the universality of these hypotheses remains untested. Certain types of architectural rearrangements may change the direction of replication and transcription, thus changing the direction of mutational pressures, and eventually cause skew inversions35. These have been identified in several phyla, and associated with increased branch lengths1,35–37 and increased dN/dS ratios in crustaceans36. Along with other arguments outlined herein, this suggests that skew inversions should be more likely in lineages evolving under relaxed selection pressures, i.e. parasitic and low-locomotory species.

Mitogenomic size is another variable that exhibits pronounced variability among bilaterian species for unknown reasons. Most animal mitogenomes evolve under a strong selection for small size, putatively driven by the “race for replication”, wherein smaller mitogenomes are replicated at higher rates due to their shorter replication time38,39. This selection pressure should be more pronounced in species with high metabolic rates due to their faster mitogenomic metabolism and higher mitogenomic replication rates39, implying that mitogenomes should be larger in species evolving under relaxed purifying selection pressures, and mitogenome size thus positively correlated with GORR, branch length and GC skew. Indirect support for the negative correlation between size and purifying selection was observed in some geckos and frogs40. However, increased metabolic rates were not associated with smaller mitogenomes in selected tetrapod lineages41. Aside from the supporting evidence for positive correlation between GORR and mitogenomic size in flatworms20, correlates of mitogenomic size have not been tested statistically on a comprehensive dataset.

The above hypothesis also implies that mitogenomic size should be affected by the thermoregulation type: endotherms should have smaller mitogenomes and reduced size variability compared to ectotherms via their higher energy expenditure, driving higher metabolic rates and producing a stronger race for replication39,42. Support for this hypothesis was found on a dataset comprising mostly vertebrates and a small number of invertebrates39. Further, two different models proposed that ectotherms should exhibit lower sequence mutation rates: the “functional constraints” hypothesis proposes that variations in the thermic environment may restrict physiologically acceptable amino acid substitutions42,43, and the “metabolic rate” hypothesis links the reduction in energy expenditure in ectotherms to a reduction in metabolic rates, production of mutagenic reactive oxygen species, and finally DNA mutation rates33,42. Indeed, there is strong evidence that ectotherms exhibit significantly lower evolutionary rates than endotherms among vertebrates33,42–44, and there is some evidence that the effect of thermoregulatory type remains consistent when invertebrates are included45. However, this contradicts our central premise that high energy expenditure (as in endotherms) strengthens purifying selection. Indeed, vertebrates (which comprise all endothermic animals) exhibit some of the shortest branches among all bilaterians10. Therefore, our working hypotheses contradict the proposals of reduced evolutionary rates in ectotherms and instead propose reduced rates in endotherms, expected to be reflected in lower branch lengths, GC skew, and GORR.

Finally, a reduction in the effective population size (Ne) weakens the purifying selection46–48, so we hypothesised that lower Ne should be reflected in higher average branch lengths, GORR values, GC skews, and mitogenome sizes3,10,34,47. However, previous studies produced contradictory results34,40,49–54, so the effects of Ne on mitogenomic evolution remain debated.

Herein, we rely on a mitogenomic dataset comprising almost 11,000 bilaterian species, categorised by locomotory capacity and lifestyle and assembled for our recent study10, to test the hypotheses concerning the correlates of mitogenomic evolution outlined in this section, and provide evidence that purifying selection mediated by locomotory capacity and species ecology is a major driver of mitogenomic architectural evolution. However, analyses indicate that other variables must be invoked to fully explain observed patterns.

Ancestral mitogenomic architectures and strand distribution of genes

Hypothesis 1: On the basis of our own reconstruction of ancestral mitogenomic architectures and previous findings5,30, the architecture identical to the one exhibited by most chordates, including humans (only PCGs and rRNAs were considered), was selected as the most likely ancestral architecture of all bilaterian animals (hypothesis 1a; Fig. 1). This also implies that double-stranded architecture and negative GC skew on the plus strand are the ancestral bilaterian states. There was some instability in the exact position of nad6 across different analyses, but its location on the minus strand was inferred with high confidence (more details in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figs. 1–2). Ancestral double-strandedness was further corroborated by the Maximum Likelihood ancestral state reconstruction for the two architecture types. This analysis included tRNA genes (eight on the minus strand in the human mitogenome), and resolved the ancestral state for Bilateria as double-stranded with a probability of 0.9999 (hypothesis 1b). The above ur-bilaterian architecture was used as the reference for subsequent gene order rearrangement analyses, and to infer the evolutionary history of mitogenomic strandedness shifts (double-stranded/single-stranded).

Hypothesis 2: a double-stranded architecture was inferred as the most likely ancestral state for Deuterostomia/Xenambulacraria and all belonging phyla with high confidence. We inferred one architectural evolutionary transition from genes encoded on both strands to all genes encoded on a single strand (henceforth “double-to-single”) in Xenacoelomorpha and at least one in Tunicata (Chordata), likely in the common ancestor of classes Thaliacea+Ascidiacea, but not Appendicularia. Details of these analyses are provided in Table 2 and Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Figs. 2–38. BayesTraits reconstruction produced double-stranded as the ancestral state for the superclade Protostomia and both of its superphyla, Ecdysozoa and Lophotrochozoa, with high probability (>0.999). Within the Ecdysozoa, double-stranded was inferred as the ancestral state for six out of seven phyla included in the dataset (Kinorhyncha was the outlier), and we inferred five putative double-to-single transitions. The superphylum Lophotrochozoa exhibited by far the most rearranged mitogenomic architectures and the most complex scenario of strandedness transitions. Likely due to the highly rearranged mitogenomic architectures of most lineages, we failed to reconstruct of the ancestral architecture for this superphylum with precision, but multiple sources of evidence indicate that a double-stranded architecture was the ancestral state. These comprise most results of the ancestral architecture reconstruction (Fig. S8), BayesTraits reconstruction (probability of double-stranded > 0.999), and the fact that double-stranded was the most likely ancestral state for 11 out of 13 individual phyla, with Brachiopoda and Syndermata as the only exceptions. Syndermata comprise sensu-stricto phyla Acanthocephala (all single-stranded) and Rotifera (mostly single-stranded) (Figs. S9–S12). We found indications of single-stranded to double-stranded transitions in several species of Rotifera, but due to their fragmented mitogenomes, within-species strandedness variability, and strand switches comprising only tRNA genes, these are not confirmed. Another double-to-single transition was inferred in Platyhelminthes after Catenulida branched-off. Three lophotrochozoan phyla exhibited particularly complex scenarios: Bryozoa, Annelida, and Mollusca. In Annelida, we identified a double-to-single evolutionary shift after Palaeoannelida branched-off, followed by a state reversal (i.e. single-to-double shift) in a single derived Sedentaria species, Sabella spalanzani (Sabellidae). GORR values distribution

Hypothesis 3: GORR values in comparison to the ancestral bilaterian architecture ranged between 0 and 12 in the complete dataset (Figs. 1 and 2). The distribution of GORR values across individual phyla could be roughly classified into three basic, albeit not necessarily mutually exclusive, patterns of architectural evolution. (1) Discontinuous: in most phyla, the ancestral architecture (disregarding volatile tRNA genes) for the phylum is highly conserved in a majority of species, with exponentially elevated rearrangement rates in a small proportion of species (e.g. Arthropoda, Chordata, Nematoda, and Echinodermata). (2) Episodic: mitogenomic architecture is re-stabilised after an evolutionary period of increased levels of architectural rearrangements, commonly occurring deep in the evolutionary history (e.g. Nematoda and Arthropoda). (3) Continuous: GORR values exhibit an approximately linear distribution, characterised by the absence of statistical outliers. Most phyla exhibited a combination of episodic and discontinuous patterns, whereas Mollusca and Bryozoa were the only phyla exhibiting a continuous pattern. In addition, Platyhelminthes exhibited a unique pattern, with parasitic Neodermata exhibiting a strongly episodic pattern (exceptionally highly rearranged, but mostly stable architecture: GORR = 9), and predominantly free-living “turbellaria” exhibiting much less rearranged, but fast-evolving in an apparently continuous manner, pattern of GORR distribution (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 39).Fig. 2The distribution of gene order rearrangement rates (GORR) in comparison to the ancestral bilaterian architecture across Bilateria and selected phyla.Statistically identified outliers (InterQuartile Range) are highlighted in red. GORR values, branch length, and strand distribution of genes

Hypothesis 4: Mitogenomic architectural evolutionary rates (GORR) strongly varied among the superphyla: highly conserved in Deuterostomia (0.24), intermediate in Ecdysozoa (3.29), and high in Lophotrochozoa (6.86). In support of the working hypothesis. branch lengths followed this pattern, but with less pronounced differences (Supplementary Fig. 40). Congruent patterns across the two variables were also observed across the individual phyla, with the slowest evolution in Chordata, Hemichordata, and Echinodermata, and fastest in Platyhelminthes, Orthonectida, and Mollusca/Acanthocephala (GORR/branch length respectively) (Figs. 1 and 2, Supplementary Table 2). GC skews and Ne

Hypothesis 6: contrary to the working hypothesis 6a, GC skew (│value│>0.1) was negatively weakly correlated with GORR (r = −0.25), with a negligible explanatory power (R2 ≈ 3%). However, the working hypothesis was weakly-moderately supported when we separately analysed Chordata and the remaining Bilateria (both r = 0.38; all p = <0.001), with higher explanatory power values (R2 ≈ 26–28% and 16–17% respectively). Hypothesis 5b was also rejected in the Bilateria dataset, where the correlation between GC skew and branch length was negligible (r = 0.13, R2 < 1%), but supported when we separately analysed Chordata (r = 0.37, R2 ≈ 20%) and the remaining Bilateria (r = 0.50; R2 ≈ 28%; p = <0.001 in all cases; Supplementary Fig. 48).

Hypothesis 7: the Ne dataset comprised 202 species, belonging to four phyla: Chordata (182 species), Mollusca (9), Arthropoda (8), and Echinodermata (3). All were free living, but all three locomotory capacity categories were represented: 180 species in high, 14 in intermediate, and 9 in low. There were four single-stranded mitogenomes in the dataset. Contrary to the working hypotheses 7a-c, Ne was not correlated with GORR (r = 0.07, p = 0.30; R2 = 0.010), branch length (r = 0.03, p = 0.66; R2 < 0.006), and GC skew (r = −0.14, p = 0.05; R2 ≈ 0.030). It was also not associated with strandedness (R2 < 0.030) and with skew inversions (R2 < 0.005) (Supplementary Fig. 49 and Data 2).

Parasitism, locomotory capacity, and thermoregulatory type

Hypothesis 8: In agreement with the working hypothesis 8a, endoparasitic species exhibited by far the most highly rearranged mitogenomic architectures, followed by ectoparasites, parasitoids, micropredators, and free-living species (Fig. 5B). In pairwise comparisons, all differences were statistically significant (Tukey HSD tests; details in Supplementary Data 3), and Bayes factors were huge (Figs. 6A, S50). Statistical power was good and effect sizes were very large or huge for key pairwise comparisons: endoparasites vs. free-living (Cohen’s d = 2.6), and ectoparasites vs. free-living (1.5) (Fig. 6B). The results were replicated with Chordata removed from the dataset and across the Ecdysozoa and Lophotrochozoa (Supplementary Fig. 45). The patterns across the individual phyla were congruent in Platyhelminthes, and partially congruent in Arthropoda (elevated GORR in ectoparasites but not in endoparasites) and Nematoda (GORR was nonsignificantly increased in endoparasites) (Supplementary Fig. 51), and mixed across lower taxonomic levels (class and order): consistent in Insecta, Chromadorea, Malacostraca, Hexanauplia, and Rhabditida, but not in Arachnida, Enoplea, and Diptera (Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53). Hypothesis 9: in agreement with the working hypothesis 9a, GORR values were significantly increased in the low locomotory capacity category (Fig. 5E). The class Hexanauplia was the only major lineage where the hypothesis was rejected (Supplementary Figs. 45, 55, and 56). In support of hypothesis 9 d, GC skews were significantly increased in low-locomotory species (Fig. 5H). In support of hypothesis 9e, there was an increased likelihood (>99×) of skew inversions (positive GC skew) in low-locomotory category compared to the high-locomotory category species (p < 0.001, Fisher’s test, one-sided; Figure S54), and locomotory capacity explained 47% of the variability in skew direction. In support of hypothesis 9 f, we found an increased likelihood (>3×) of single-stranded mitogenomes in species classified into the low locomotory capacity category, and decreased among the high-locomotory species (<0.4×, Fig. 7, Supplementary Data 3). There was a highly significant association between the locomotory capacity and strandedness (p ≈ 0.0, Chi-square test of independence), with locomotory capacity explaining ≈33% of the variability in strand types across lineages (Table 3).

Hypothesis 10: in the bilaterian dataset 8498 species were ectotherms, and 2362 were endotherms (all Chordata and almost all free-living). Among the endotherms, >99% were classified in the high locomotory capacity category (none were low-locomotory). Among the ectotherms, ≈75% were high-locomotory, and ≈14% were low-locomotory. In agreement with the working hypotheses 10a/b, ectotherms exhibited significantly higher GORR (hypothesis 10a, Fig. 5I), and branch lengths (hypothesis 10b, Fig. 5J) than ectotherms, but Chordata exhibited an inverted pattern (Supplementary Fig. 45). The explanatory power of thermoregulatory type on these two variables was small: ≈2% and ≈5%, respectively (Table 3). Contrary to the working hypothesis 10c, endotherms exhibited significantly higher average GC skew value than ectotherms (both across the Bilateria and only Chordata; Fig. 5K and Supplementary Fig. 47).

Mitogenome size patterns and correlates

Hypothesis 11: Aside from hypothesis 11i, all other hypotheses associated with correlates of mitogenomic size were rejected. Mitogenomes were the smallest (≈12.6 Kb) in Longidoridae (Nematoda: Enoplea), and the largest in two Bivalvia (Mollusca) species: Anadara sativa (48.2 Kb) and Dreissena rostriformis (46.7 Kb). Only eight species exhibited mitogenomes >30 Kb (5 Mollusca and 3 Arthropoda). Multiple Chordata species (Squamata and Anura) were among the largest mitogenomes (>25 Kb) (Supplementary Data 1). The correlation between GORR and mitogenome size was weakly/moderately negative in Bilateria (hypothesis 11a; r = −0.39, Fig. 3B), and size explained ≈10% of the GORR variability (Table 3). In individual phyla, correlation between GORR and size was highly variable: positive in Annelida, Echinodermata, and Nematoda (weak—moderate); negative in Platyhelminthes and Mollusca (weak); and negligible in Arthropoda, Chordata, and BilateriaremovedChordata (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 57). The correlation between mitogenome size and branch length was weakly negative across the bilaterian dataset (hypothesis 11b, r = −0.33, Fig. 3C). It also varied across the individual phyla: positive in Mollusca, Nematoda, Echinodermata, and Annelida (weak—moderate); negative in Arthropoda and Platyhelminthes (negligible—weak); and negligible in Chordata and BilateriaremovedChordata (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. 57). Ne values were not correlated with size (hypothesis 11c, r = −0.09, p = 0.21, R2 < 0.013, Supplementary Fig. 49). GC skew was also negligibly correlated with size across all tested datasets (hypothesis 11 d, r ≈ −0.07−0.17; R2 ≈ 0.005 − 0.030; Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 48). Mitogenomes were the largest in free-living, and the smallest in ectoparasitic and endoparasitic lineages (hypothesis 11e, Fig. 5L). Mitogenomes were larger in high-locomotory than in low-locomotory categories (hypothesis 11 f, Fig. 5M). Double-stranded mitogenomes were larger than single-stranded ones (hypothesis 11 g, Fig. 5N). Endotherms had larger mitogenomes than ectotherms (hypothesis 11 h, Fig. 5O). Finally, in agreement with hypothesis 11i, variability in size was lesser in endotherms than in ectotherms (SD = 537 vs. 1340, respectively, Fig. 5O).

Discussion

We offer an in-depth analysis of the evolutionary forces influencing mitogenomic architecture across Bilateria. It builds upon our previous study, where we used a similar dataset to show that parasitism and locomotory capacity have the power to explain the variability in sequence evolution rates10, but did not include architectural rearrangement rates, size, thermoregulatory type, Ne, and GC skew among the studied variables. While confirming that purifying selection mediated by locomotory capacity and species ecology plays a central role in architectural evolution as well, our findings highlight a multifaceted evolutionary landscape, characterized by interactions among lineage-specific constraints, genomic mechanisms, and historical contingencies.

Ancestral architectures and deep evolutionary history

Our results support the hypothesis that the ancestral bilaterian mitogenome was double-stranded and architecturally very similar to the typical vertebrate mitogenome, also found in humans5,30, albeit with some instability noted in the specific positions of nad6 and cytb genes. However, alternative ancestral reconstructions did not alter our fundamental conclusions (Supplementary Note 2 and Fig. 45). The inferred ancestral architectures were also double-stranded for all superphyla and most phyla. Whereas our results support previous reconstructions of the ancestral architecture for Ecdysozoa55,56, they contradict the proposal that the ancestral state for Lophotrochozoa, Annelida, and Platyhelminthes was single-stranded29. Overall, Lophotrochozoa exhibited the most highly rearranged mitogenomic architectures, often exhibiting few or no plesiomorphies with the ur-bilaterian architecture, so we failed to infer the ancestral architecture for this superphylum, which also prevented us for inferring one for Protostomia. However, multiple lines of evidence strongly support a double-stranded arrangement as the ancestral one, albeit probably with a small number of genes on the minus strand. In both Annelida and Platyhelminthes (flatworms), most of which have single-stranded architectures, the “basal” lineages (Palaeoannelida and Catenulida, respectively) exhibit double-stranded architectures57–59, and their sister phyla exhibit double-stranded architectures (Orthonectida and Gastrotricha, respectively). Therefore, a single-stranded ancestral state of Lophotrochozoa and/or these phyla would require multiple single-to-double transitions deep in the evolutionary history of Lophotrochozoa. As this type of transition is evolutionarily extremely rare and we observed it only at relatively shallow phylogenetic levels, double-stranded as the ancestral architecture for Lophotrochozoa and all belonging phyla aside from Brachiopoda and Syndermata is the most parsimonious scenario (Supplementary Note 5).

The role of locomotory capacity and parasitism in purifying selection pressure variation

We found multiple lines of evidence supporting the hypothesis that variation in purifying selection pressures is a major factor underlying the variability in architectural evolutionary rates, and that this variability is mediated by locomotory capacity and species ecology. The underlying logic is that relaxed selection for efficient ATP production, consequent to low locomotory capacity or parasitic auxotrophy, permits the accumulation of both sequence mutations and structural rearrangements that would otherwise be deleterious. These predictions were supported by a wide range of analyses and variables, comprising the positive correlation between GORR and branch length, elevated GORR in parasitic and low-locomotory species, intermediate rates in parasitoids (undergoing selection for locomotory capacity in adults10), decreased GORR rates in ectoparasites with high locomotory capacity, and strongly increased likelihoods of single-stranded mitogenomes and GC skew inversions in parasitic and low-locomotory species. Controlling for the variability in locomotory capacity (only low-locomotory) and life history (only free-living species) failed to fully exclude the impact of either of these two variables, but it did show that the impact of parasitism was limited to endoparasites (ectoparasites had lower GORR than free-living). This was also reflected in a stronger predictive power of locomotory capacity than parasitism on architectural evolution, whereas an opposite pattern was observed on sequence evolution10.

Despite the support for our hypotheses, the explanatory power of parasitism and locomotory capacity on variability in architectural evolutionary rates among bilaterians was much lower (6% and 14%, respectively, combined 17%) than that on variability in sequence evolution rates in the same dataset (≈45%, ≈39%, ≈56%, respectively)10. First, it should be emphasised that these values cannot be considered low. For example, thermoregulatory type and Ne both explained less more than 2% of the GORR variability in our results, whereas body size explained 6% of the branch length variability in mammals60, and generation time explained ≈13% of this variability in both vertebrates and invertebrates60,61. Intriguingly, the predictive power of locomotory capacity was very strong within the Protostomia alone (≈56%). This suggests that the extreme architectural conservation in Deuterostomia, which dominated the full dataset, may be governed by different mechanistic constraints (e.g., replication or repair machinery).

Nevertheless, our model lacks the power to fully explain the long-term stasis in architectural evolution in several lineages marked by high prevalence of low-locomotory and parasitic species: Neodermata (Platyhelminthes), Acanthocephala, and many Chromadorea (Nematoda). In agreement with our model, all three lineages exhibited some of the most highly rearranged mitogenomes in the dataset on average, but most of these rearrangements took place deep in their evolutionary history, representing an “episodic” evolutionary pattern2,20,62,63. While there are isolated taxa within Neodermata and Nematoda that exhibit elevated architectural evolution at shallow phylogenetic levels9,11,20,64, this indicates that rearrangements are strongly selected against even in some low-locomotory and parasitic lineages. An intriguing observation is that all three lineages have single-stranded architectures. This suggests that a factor underpinning this unexplained evolutionary architectural stabilisation may be strong purifying selection pressures against most rearrangements that cause strand inversions in single-stranded architectures. In other words, the initial shift to single-strandedness, once fixed, may itself impose a new structural constraint via the “ratchet” mechanism4, drastically reducing the range of permissible rearrangements and re-stabilizing the mitogenomic architecture. This hypothesis remains to be tested statistically in the future.

Other potential candidates explaining the reduced explanatory power of locomotory capacity and parasitism on architectural evolution comprise Ne, selective sweeps, genetic draft/hitchhiking, recombination, heteroplasmy, etc.47,49,65,66. For most of these, our current understanding is insufficient to include them and further discuss in this study, but we did include Ne analyses, albeit at a price of a strongly reduced dataset, limited to the variability in locomotory capacity, but not life history (i.e. parasites were absent). Contrary to the expected central role of Ne in the interlineage variability of evolutionary rates46–48, it exhibited negligible corelation/regression values with all other variables tested herein. This aligns with our previous work10,20,34, as well as findings of several other studies49,67. We hypothesise that temporal fluctuations in Ne and selective sweeps in non-recombining mitogenomes49 could explain this decoupling between the contemporary Ne values and cumulative evolutionary variables like GORR and branch length. This may also explain the synchronised episodic patterns of architectural and sequence evolutionary rates in Neodermata and Chromadorea9,20 (Supplementary Note 6).

GC skews

GC skew patterns behaved in a less predictable manner than GORR and branch lengths, offering only partial support for the working hypotheses. This was expected, as GC skew magnitude is strongly affected by multiple types of rearrangement events35,36, which are likely to strongly affect the signal to noise ratio. A major confounding factor was the disparity between Chordata and other bilaterians, with Chordata exhibiting the slowest evolutionary rates (both sequence and architecture) and most pronounced (negative) GC skews in the dataset. A factor contributing to this may be the highly conserved ancestral bilaterian mitogenomic architecture in most chordates, whereas the common ancestor of Protostomia underwent multiple architectural rearrangements that comprised strand switches, and multiple lineages underwent full inversions in the direction of mutational pressures35,36. As GC skew on the plus strand is a mirror image of GC skew on the minus strand, strand switches invariably cause a reduction in the GC skew magnitude on the plus strand. Therefore, chordates are the only lineage that was consistently evolving over half a billion of years under unidirectional mutational pressures unaffected by rearrangement events causing a reduction in skew magnitude. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that variability in replication/transcription mechanisms across different bilaterian lineages may also contribute to this finding3,68–70 (Supplementary Note 6). Additional intriguing findings comprise the strongly increased likelihood of skew inversions in parasitic and low-locomotory lineages, with a vast majority of endoparasitic species exhibiting inverted (positive) GC skews, and a strong association between the evolutionary transitions to single-strandedness and GC skew inversions. This further indirectly links skew inversions to elevated rearrangement rates under relaxed selection.

Ectotherms exhibit higher evolutionary rates

We found support for our hypothesis that locomotory capacity/species ecology-mediated impacts of purifying selection pressure outweigh the impacts of “functional constraints” and” metabolic rate”, with endotherms exhibiting slower evolutionary rates. While this contradicts previous findings, those were mostly based on comparisons of different vertebrate lineages33,42–44. Therefore, thermoregulatory type is another variable in relation to which vertebrates and invertebrates do not exhibit congruent patterns of mitogenomic evolution. Once again, GC skews were an outlier, with higher values in endotherms. However, the explanatory power of thermoregulatory type on evolutionary rates was lower than that of locomotory capacity and parasitism (Supplementary Note 6).

The evolution of strandedness

We inferred over twenty putative shifts in the strandedness type (single vs. double-stranded) in 11 phyla across the available bilaterian dataset, with a strong association between species ecology/locomotory capacity and single-strandedness. While a majority of these were double-to-single shifts, we also found compelling evidence for rare reversals to double-strandedness, challenging the absolute nature of the “ratchet” hypothesis4. While some putative reversals involving only tRNAs could be artefacts caused by pseudogenization, the conserved patterns in some Bivalvia and Annelida lineages provide strong evidence for true evolutionary reversals. Nevertheless, these events are exceedingly rare, confirming that single-strandedness is a predominantly irreversible state at macroevolutionary scales. Intriguingly, the class Bivalvia, which exhibited by far the most complex evolutionary history of shifts in strandedness, also exhibits high recombination rates66,71. Aside from underscoring the unique and dynamic nature of mitogenomic evolution in Mollusca3,6, we speculate that these two phenomena may be interrelated.

Mitogenome size is negatively correlated with architectural and sequence evolutionary rates

All of our working hypotheses related to the mitogenome size, and based on the assumed central mediating role of purifying selection, were rejected, aside from the decreased variability in endotherms. Major variables that may be invoked to explain these unexpected results comprise reversibility of sequence duplications and gene loss. As a consequence of selection for small size39, sequence duplications are removed from mitogenomes over time, and become decoupled from rearrangement rates and branch lengths, both of which accumulate over time (mostly) irreversibly. Gene losses are common in most major parasitic lineages, e.g. Nematoda, Acanthocephala, Neodermata, parasitic Isopoda, etc., but very rare in Chordata, free living Arthropoda, and Mollusca2,3,8,11–13,72. This explains why endotherms, which evolve under strong, consistent purifying selection, have larger and less variable mitogenomes than parasitic ectotherms: they rarely experience gene losses or large deletions (Supplementary Note 7). While these explanations do have a sufficient power to explain the observed patterns, we cannot exclude that other variables, such as the replication mechanism, repair machinery, recombination, etc., may also play a role.

Outliers and rejects

These large-scale analyses allowed us to contextualize previous findings and identify informative outliers. For example, we did not find support for the proposal that GORR and branch length correlate only in lineages with high rearrangement rates1. The correlation was strong across Bilateria, with Mollusca being a notable exception, possibly due to its high recombination rate66,71 decoupling these two variables. Despite this, locomotory capacity still produced predicted effects on mitogenomic evolution in Mollusca – with high-locomotory lineages exhibiting shorter branches10 and lower GORR values. In Nematoda, parasitism had a nonsignificant impact on branch length10 and GORR (limited to Enoplea in this study), which can be attributed to an evolutionarily complex mix of parasitic and free-living life histories73 that exhibit many similarities, comprising the locomotory capacity10. However, nematodes exhibit further unique evolutionary patterns for which we do not have full explanations currently. Locomotory capacity can be also invoked to explain the outliers in two arthropod lineages: Diptera—comprising high-locomotory ectoparasitic lineages (fleas), and Arachnida—exhibiting similar locomotory capacity across ectoparasitic (ticks) and free-living (mites) life histories10. An outlier where selection for locomotory capacity failed to explain the pattern is Hexanauplia (Arthropoda): low-locomotory species exhibited lower GORR values and branch lengths10. This class comprises sessile crustaceans, barnacles (subclass Cirripedia), which have been identified as a major outlier in the relationship between locomotory capacity and mitogenomic evolution among all bilaterian animals10,34: despite their predominantly sessile lifestyle, mitogenomes of this lineage are evolving under some of the most stringent purifying selection pressures among all Arthropoda (Supplementary Note 8 and Figs. 58–62).

Concluding remarks

Patterns of mitogenomic architectural evolution strongly vary across bilaterian lineages: Deuterostomia exhibited the most conserved architectures on average, followed by intermediate rearrangement rates in Ecdysozoa, whereas Lophotrochozoa exhibited highly rearranged architectures. Many phyla exhibited an episodic-discontinuous evolutionary pattern: de-stabilisation deep in the evolutionary history, followed by re-stabilisation, and then again strong elevation of rearrangement rates in a small proportion of lineages. Mollusca and Bryozoa were outliers, exhibiting a “continuous” (clocklike) pattern of mitogenomic evolution. These two phyla also exhibited the highest likelihood of switches in mitogenomic strandedness types (single-stranded vs. double stranded). We found consistent support for our hypothesis that reduced locomotory capacity allows a reduced stringency of selection for mitochondrial metabolic efficiency, which in turn relaxes the purifying pressures on mitogenomic repair and maintenance mechanisms, and finally results in increased rates of both sequence mutations and architectural rearrangements. This was also reflected in strongly increased likelihoods of single-stranded mitogenomes and skew inversions in low locomotory and parasitic species. This should not be misconstrued as evidence for a direct causal relationship among these variables. Rather, via stochastic rearrangement processes, a small proportion of events may randomly lead to single-stranded architectures or inversions of the mutational pressure (i.e. replication direction). As relaxed selection for mitogenomic efficiency allows a wider range of architectural rearrangements, these events are statistically more likely in parasitic and low-locomotory species. While the central role of purifying selection in mitogenomic architecture evolution in bilaterian animals is not a novel finding in itself3, the underlying reason for the variability in its strength remained elusive until now. However, there are also multiple lines of evidence that impacts of some variables on mitogenomic evolution differ between vertebrates and invertebrates53,74, which indicates that it is necessary to consider other variables, such as molecular machinery in charge of repair, transcription and replication, as well as selection sweeps and draft, transient population bottlenecks, etc., in order to fully explain the mitogenomic architectural evolutionary patterns across bilaterian animals.

Methods

Dataset, categorisation, phylogeny, branch lengths, and Ne

Features of all mitogenomes of bilaterian animals available in the NCBI’s manually curated RefSeq database were extracted, calculated, and comparative tables generated using PhyloSuite75,76. The dataset was categorised according to the lifestyle (endoparasites, ectoparasites, parasitoids, micropredators, and free-living species), locomotory capacity (high, intermediate, and low), and thermoregulatory type (endotherms and ectotherms). Duplicated genes were resolved using PhyloSuite following the methodology described previously10,75. To infer branch lengths, we used the phylogeny from our previous study10, reconstructed using concatenated amino acid sequences of 12 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (atp8 removed) of all mitogenomes in the dataset, and mtZOA+G4 + F + C50 model in IQ-TREE v2.2.0.7.mix77. We constrained the phylum-level topology according to the bilaterian phylogeny in Laumer et al.78, with Xenacoelomorpha as the sister lineage to all other bilaterians79,80 (“Nephrozoa” topology henceforth). The molecular sequence evolution rate was defined as the root-to-tip branch lengths, which were extracted using the TreeSuite function in PhyloSuite. More details about the dataset, including the limitations of these classifications, and methodology are available in Supplementary Methods and in Jakovlić et al.10. The effective population size (Ne) analyses were extracted by overlapping the species in our dataset with those in the dataset recently compiled by Clarke et al.81. When there were multiple populations per species, we inferred average Ne values.

GC skews

To exclude the confounding factor of variability in the noncoding region size, GC skews were calculated82 by PhyloSuite using only coding sections of the plus strand (GC skew is more pronounced than AT skew and thus more suitable for testing these hypotheses). To attempt to reduce the amount of noise in the GC skew dataset stemming from inversions in mutational pressures caused by inversions of the origin of replication35,36 and facilitate the analysis of magnitude irrespective of skew direction, all GC skews were converted to their absolute values. To further reduce the amount of noise, the lower end of distribution (GC < 0.1) was removed, as such low GC values commonly suggest ORI inversions or otherwise disrupted replication mechanisms, so they poorly reflect the strength of purifying selection pressure36. In addition, we attempted to remove all annotation artefacts caused by the authors submitting the wrong mitogenomic strand to the GenBank via a manual inspection of the dataset (removed 65 species).

Mitogenomic architecture analyses

Mitogenomes of bilaterian animals commonly encode 37 genes (13 protein-coding genes - PCGs, 2 rRNAs, and 22 tRNAs) distributed in varying arrangements across both DNA strands, by convention named “plus” and “minus”. This arrangement is referred to as mitogenomic architecture, and it can be quantified as the gene order rearrangement rate (GORR). For the ancestral architecture inference, we relied on previous proposals of ancestral architectures for different lineages (Supplementary Table 3), and conducted the ancestral mitogenomic architecture inference using TreeREx83 and MLGO84 using a linearised gene order dataset from which tRNAs were removed due to their highly variable content and difficulties in annotation. To minimise the noise and reduce the size of dataset (362 species after the reduction), we used only the ancestral architecture for a phylum when it was inferred with confidence by previous studies (e.g. only the mitogenome of Homo sapiens for Chordata5,30 and Limulus polyphemus for Arthropoda85), and only the “basal” lineage of large phyla exhibiting high rearrangement rates (e.g. only Catenulida for Platyhelminthes57). As the phylogeny of Bilateria remains debated, we tested two topologies, both constrained at the phylum level. Aside from the “Nephrozoa” topology, described in the first section, we also tested the topology where Xenacoelomorpha is the sister group to Ambulacraria (Hemichordata+Echinodermata)86,87, with paraphyletic Deuterostomia88–named “Xenambulacraria” henceforth. As this topology implies that the common ancestor of Chordata and Xenambulacraria (Ambulacraria+Xenacoelomorpha) was the last common ancestor of all Bilateria, and characters common to these two clades would be bilaterian plesiomorphies88, we removed the highly rearranged Protostomia (Ecdysozoa and Lophotrochozoa)5,30, and tested a dataset comprising only the outgroups, Xenambulacraria, and Chordata, constrained according to the relationships proposed previously86,88. The outgroups were Cnidaria, Porifera, and Placozoa. Further methodological details are provided in Supplementary Methods. The inferred ur-bilaterian mitogenomic architecture (tRNAs excluded) was then used to calculate mitogenomic GORR values using the Breakpoint Distance measure in CREX89. As TreeREx and CREX cannot handle missing genes, for these analyses, atp8 was removed from all mitogenomes, and species with further missing PCGs or rRNAs were removed from these analyses, which included the entire phylum Chaetognatha–lacking atp62,90. In addition, all mitogenomes were classified into two categories according to the strand distribution of genes: double-stranded (genes encoded on both strands) and single-stranded (all genes encoded on a single strand), and then the Maximum Likelihood method in BayesTraits v4.0.191 was used to infer the ancestral states of strand distribution of genes. To get a better resolution for strandedness analyses, we further analysed complete GenBank mitogenomic datasets (as opposed to only RefSeq) for all poorly represented major lineages and for all lineages with complex evolutionary histories.

Statistical analyses

As GORR values equalled 0 for more than half of the dataset, mostly due to a majority of Chordata having a perfectly conserved ancestral bilaterian architecture and being overrepresented in the dataset (>50% of all species), we recoded GORR = 0 values to 0.0000001 for all statistical analyses. To further confirm that Chordata did not skew our statistical analyses, we additionally tested all major hypotheses using a bilaterian dataset from which all Chordata were removed. Outliers in GORR distributions were identified using the IQR method (InterQuartile Range), selected due to the non-normality of GORR distribution. Pairwise comparisons of between different groups were conducted using Tukey HSD tests in the R package agricolae v1.3.592. As the limitations of the traditional p-value-based statistics are well-documented, we further assessed the reliability of statistical analyses by employing multiple recommended alternative tests93. Effect sizes were inferred using the R package effsize. Statistical power analyses were conducted for each effect size measure using the pwr.t2n.test method in the R package pwr. Herein, power refers to the probability of detecting a statistically significant effect if one exists, and the commonly accepted cut-off value for a good statistical power is ≥ 0.8. Bayesian hypothesis testing was conducted using BayesFactor94. As the dataset violated the assumption of independence of data, two algorithms designed to account for phylogenetic relatedness were used to conduct multilevel linear regression analyses: linear fixed-effect models accounting for kinship implemented in the lmekin function in coxme v2.2.1695, and phylogenetic multilevel Bayesian models implemented in brms v2.16.15096. For these analyses, we used a matrix of phylogenetic distances extracted from the phylogenetic tree described above using the ape v5.5 package97. The R2 value of lmekin models was calculated using the r.squaredLR method available in the MuMIn v1.46.0 package in R. The Bayesian R2 value was inferred using the bayes_r2 function of brms package in R. For brms and lmekin analyses, we log-transformed the obtained GORR data to reduce the non-normality of distribution. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were inferred using R. Correlation values were classified as follows: negligible (<0.2), weak (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), strong (0.6–0.8), very strong (>0.8). Due to the very large number of samples in our study (>10,000), we did not rely on p-values to assess the support for the working hypotheses in correlation tests, as statistical tests are practically always highly significant on such high numbers of samples98. Unless if specified otherwise, statistical tests were inherently two-sided.
