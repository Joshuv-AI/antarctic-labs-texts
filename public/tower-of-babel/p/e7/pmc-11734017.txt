Healthy Plant-Based Diet, Genetic Predisposition, and the Risk of Incident Venous Thromboembolism
Authors: Jing Guo, Yang-Yang He, Xiang-Ying Suo, Yang Liu, Li-Li Xiao, Yan-Jun Zhou, Zhen-Xuan Hao, Rui Liang, Ya-Cong Bo, Quan-Jun Lyu, Yong-Jian Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlant-based dietary patterns are becoming increasingly popular due to environmental and health impacts, yet there are few studies exploring the relationship between plant-based dietary patterns and venous thromboembolism (VTE) in different genetic backgrounds.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to investigate how plant-based dietary pattern and genetic susceptibility independently or jointly affect VTE and its subtypes of pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis.MethodsA total of 183,510 participants who were White British ethnicity background and free of VTE at baseline in the UK Biobank were recruited, in consideration that the selection of genetic variants for VTE was based on results of White European individuals. The healthy plant-based diet index (hPDI) was calculated based on the 17 foods in the Oxford webQ. Genetic risk was quantified by a polygenic risk score (PRS) consisting of 297 single-nucleotide polymorphisms. Incident VTE was identified through hospital inpatient and death databases records from the UK Biobank. Cox regression analyses were conducted to estimate the associations between hPDI and PRS with the incident risk of VTE.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 13 years, a total of 4,587 cases of VTE were recorded. Compared to individuals in the lowest tertile of hPDI, multivariable-adjusted HRs of VTE in the highest tertile were 0.86 (95% CI: 0.80, 0.92; P trend = 0.02). And high PRS was associated with a 47% increase in VTE risk (adjusted HR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.37, 1.58). No significant interaction between hPDI and PRS (P interaction = 0.540) was observed, even though participants with low hPDI an high PRS had the highest risk of VTE compared to those with high hPDI and low PRS (HR: 1.72, 95% CI: 1.52, 1.95).ConclusionsAdherence to a healthy plant-based dietary pattern could reduce the risk of developing VTE independent of genetic background, lifestyles, sociodemographic features, and multiple morbidities. Our findings underline the importance of diet in VTE prevention interventions.Key words: deep vein thrombosis, plant-based diet, pulmonary embolism, venous thromboembolism

As the third most common vascular disease after myocardial infarction and stroke, venous thromboembolism (VTE) affects approximately 10 million people worldwide each year.1 Pulmonary embolism (PE) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) are 2 clinical entities of VTE.2 VTE causes substantial healthcare costs due to its high morbidity, frequent recurrences, and poor survival rates without treatment.3 The incidence of VTE increases exponentially with age and is 4 times more common in high-income countries compared to low-income countries. It has been estimated that the total healthcare costs associated with VTE range from $7 to $10 billion per year in the United States and from €1.3 to €2.2 billion in Europe.3,4 VTE is a chronic condition characterized by frequent recurrences that can lead to severe consequences, including death, long-term disability, and the risk of hemorrhage associated with anticoagulants during treatment.5 VTE is a multifactorial condition caused by genetic and environmental risk factors. The genetically driven risks play pivotal role in thrombosis.6,7 Recent large-scale gene-wide association studies (GWAS) study have identified new multiple risk loci in VTE development.8 While 50% to 60% of VTE risk was attributed to genetic effects,9 significant provoking factors such as obesity, cancer, hospitalization, surgery and chronic kidney disease, also remarkably increase the VTE risk. Despite the fact that independent risk factors have been identified, the incidence of VTE has remained fairly constant or even increasing.10 In recent years, the focus on VTE prevention has expanded from primarily hospital-based risk factors to the adoption of a heart-healthy lifestyle.11

Several studies have linked dietary patterns to the incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and found that people who consumed predominantly plant-based foods had lower risk of CVD compared to those who consumed animal-based foods.12, 13, 14 However, less is known about the relationship between healthy plant-based dietary patterns and VTE risk, especially at different genetic risk scales. Thus, we conducted a large-scale cohort study based on the UK Biobank to assess the association between healthy plant-based dietary pattern and the risk of incident VTE and its 2 subtypes (PE and DVT), and explored the potential modifying effects of genetic predisposition defined by polygenic risk scores (PRSs). These findings contribute to deepening the understanding of the food and nutrients combinations that may influence the development of VTE.

Methods

Study population

UK Biobank is a detailed, long-term prospective health research study in the world, which has recruited half a million participants aged 40 to 60 years old between 2006 and 2010.15 UK Biobank provides substantial information about their demographic, lifestyle, physical activity (PA), genetic profiles, proteomics, laboratory tests, and health-related records. Written informed consents were signed by all UK Biobank participants before being recruited. The approval of all procedures involving human participants was obtained from the Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (approval number 11/NW/0382).

The procedure of participant selection is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. In brief, we excluded participants without information on dietary assessment (n = 291,421), withdrew from UK Biobank (n = 569), diagnosed with VTE at baseline (1,398), and non-White British descent (n = 9,651) in consideration that the selection of genetic variants for VTE was based on results of white European individuals. In addition, we further excluded individuals who had no genetic data (n = 16,822). As a result, a total of 183,510 participants for the final analyses.

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was incident VTE, which was a composite outcome of PE and DVT. The incident VTE was ascertained from the linked hospital and death databases. PE was defined as ICD-10 codes: I26 and O88.2; DVT was defined as ICD-10 codes: I80.1-I80.9, I82.8-I82.9, O22.3, O22.8-O22.9, O87.1 and O87.8-O87.9;16 VTE was defined as total PE and DVT. The hospital admissions/visits data were available up to October 31, 2022 in England, August 31, 2022 in Scotland, and May 31, 2022 in Wales, respectively. The death data were available up to November 30, 2022. Therefore, follow-up was censored at the date of death, loss to follow-up, or the last date with available health data, whichever occurred first.

Assessment of healthy plant-based diet indices

The dietary information was collected using the Oxford WebQ, which is a web-based, self-administered 24-hour dietary assessment tool, validated for use in large-scale observational studies,17 collected more than 200 common foods and 30 beverages from the previous 24 hours. The 24-hour online dietary recall questionnaire was administered 5 times, with the initial assessment occurring from April 2009 to September 2010 and the final one from April 2012 to June 2012. A previous study demonstrated a strong correlation between the “baseline healthful plant-based diet index (hPDI)” and the “averaged hPDI” from repeated measurements between 2009 and 2012 (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.88; P < 0.0001).18 Consequently, this study utilized the earliest dietary intake data to maximize follow-up time for participants who completed the dietary assessment multiple times.

We developed a “healthful” version of the PDI, referred to as hPDI, based on methods outlined in previous studies.18,19 The hPDI emphasizes the consumption of healthy plant foods, assigning positive scores to healthier plant foods and negative scores to less-healthy plant foods and animal products. The criteria for categorizing foods as healthy or less healthy were derived from existing data on their associations with cardiovascular outcomes and metabolic abnormalities.20 In UK Biobank, a total of 17 food groups were selected for calculating the hPDI, including whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes and vegetarian protein alternatives, tea and coffee, fruit juices, refined grains, potatoes, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets and desserts, animal fat, dairy, egg, fish or seafood, meat, and miscellaneous animal-based foods (Supplemental Table 1). The 17 food groups were categorized into consumption quintiles, with each quintile given positive scores (Q1 = 1 to Q5 = 5) or reverse scores (Q5 = 1 to Q1 = 5). The scores of the 17 food groups for each individual food were summed to calculate the hPDI. A higher hPDI indicates better dietary quality, reflecting greater consumption of healthy plant foods and reduced intake of less-healthy plant foods and animal products.

Construction of PRS

We developed a PRS for VTE using 297 independent single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with VTE (P < 5 × 10−5).21 Detailed information about the included SNPs is provided in Supplemental Table 2. PRS is the representation of an individual’s risk of developing a specific disease, which is determined by the cumulative effect of many common DNA sequence variants. The weight of each gene variant was assigned based on the strength of the association of each gene variant with disease risk (β), individuals were then scored in a weighted manner for the number of risk alleles they carried for each variant in the PRS. The calculation equation is as follows: PRS = (β1 × SNP1 + β2 × SNP2 + ... + β297 × SNP297) × (297/sum of the β coefficients). The PRS was categorized into tertiles, thus indicating low to medium to high genetic risk, with higher PRS representing greater genetic susceptibility to VTE.

Assessment of covariates

The covariates in this study included demographic variables such as sex (male/female), age (<65 years/≥65 years), education level (college or university/others), employment (employed/unemployed), and other potential confounders that may be associated with VTE, including body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), PA (low/moderate/high), alcohol drinking (never/previous/current), smoking status (never/previous/current), and history of chronic disease such as CVD (yes/no) and cancer (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics are summarized as percentages for categorical variables, mean ± SD for normal continuous variables, and median (IQR) for non-normal continuous variables. The Cox proportional hazards models are used to derive the HR and 95% CI for evaluating the associations of hPDI and PRS with VTE risk. Three models were gradually adopted: Model 1: crude model without adjusting for any variables; Model 2: adjusted for age and sex; Model 3: further adjusted for townsend deprivation index, PA, educational level, employment status, smoking, drinking, BMI, CVD, and cancer. Additionally, we used restricted cubic spline to assess the potential nonlinear relationship between hPDI and PRS risk. The proportional hazards assumptions were examined using a log-minus-log plots. Missing values were imputed using the multiple imputation.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess potential interaction effects among subgroups of sex (male, female), age (<65 years, ≥65 years), smoking status (never, ever), drinking status (never, ever) PA (low, moderate-high), and BMI (<25 kg/m2, ≥25 kg/m2), respectively. In addition, we also conducted 3 sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of these associations: 1) we repeated the main analysis by excluding participants diagnosed as VTE within 2 years after baseline; 2) restricted the analysis among participants without CVD and cancer at baseline; and 3) adjusted the frequency of alcohol consumption (daily or almost daily, 3-4 times per week, 1-2 time per week, 1-3 times per month, special occasions only, never, and prefer not to answer) instead drinking status (never, previous, current). To examine whether the relationship between hPDI and VTE might be affected by genetic risk, we performed joint analyses by cross-classifying participants by hPDI (tertile1, tertile2, tertile3) and PRS (tertile1, tertile2, tertile3). All analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2-sided P < 0.05 in each test was considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 183,510 participants were included in this study, of which 100,831 (54.9%) were female. The mean age of the participants was 56.80 ± 7.90 years. During a mean follow-up of 13 years, 4,587 participants developed VTE including 2,509 cases of PE and 2,479 cases of DVT. Participants who developed VTE were more likely to be male, older, less educated, and had higher BMI and Townsend Deprivation index than those who did not develop VTE. In addition, they had adverse health factors such as low levels of PA, current smoking, and a history of cancer or CVD (Table 1). We also compared the baseline characteristics of the participants according to the tertiles of the hPDI (Supplemental Table 3) and PRS (Supplemental Table 4). Participants with higher hPDI were more likely to be female, with a lower BMI and have healthy habits: high levels of PA and never smoking. Participants with higher PRS had a higher risk of developing VTE, PE, and DVT.

Association of hPDI with the risk of VTE

Compared with the lowest tertile of hPDI, the highest tertile of hPDI was significantly associated with a lower risk of VTE (HR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.73-0.84), PE (HR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.72-0.87), and DVT (HR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.71-0.86), respectively. After adjusting for a series of covariates, these relationships remain consistent (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 1A). In addition, the distribution of hPDI scores in the VTE population differs from non-VTE, with the VTE population accounting for a lower proportion of the top quartile of hPDI scores (Figure 1B). We also performed a cumulative risk analysis to validate the cumulative risk of VTE over time, which showed that participants with low hPDI had a higher cumulative incidence than those with high hPDI (Figure 1C), and the results for PE and DVT were consistent with VTE (Supplemental Figures 2A and 2D).

Association of PRS with the risk of VTE

We constructed the VTE-PRS to assess the incidence of VTE in populations with different genetic risks. The results showed that individuals with high PRS had a 47% increased risk of incident VTE compared to those with low PRS. The results remained consistent after adjusting for a series of covariates with adjusted HR of 1.47 (95% CI: 1.37-1.58), 1.56 (95% CI: 1.41-1.72), and 1.40 (95% CI: 1.27-1.54) for VTE, PE and DVT, respectively (Tables 2 and 3). The distribution of PRS scores in the VTE population differs from non-VTE, with the VTE rate increased with PRS increasing (Figure 1B). Furthermore, the cumulative risk of VTE increased gradually with increasing PRS, suggesting that VTE-PRS could provide effective risk stratification of the VTE population (Figure 1D), which could be verified equally in PE and DVT (Supplemental Figures 2B and 2E).

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

In stratified analyses for hDI, the results showed that the correlations between hPDI and VTE did not vary by age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, and PA (Supplemental Figure 3), so as to the associations regarding to PE and DVT (Supplemental Figures 4 and 5). The stratified analysis for PRS suggested that male participants were more sensitive to the effects of PRS on incident VTE, male participants and individuals with higher PA were more sensitive to the effects of PRS on incident DVT. No significant modifying effects of age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, or PA were observed for the association between PRS and risk of PE. Sensitivity analysis showed similar results (Supplemental Tables 5 to 7).

Joint effect of hPDI and PRS on incident VTE

We also explored the impact of hPDI on the risk of VTE in each genetic risk category (Figure 1E), with the highest hPDI scores and lowest genetic risk groups as reference. Among participants with higher hPDI scores, the risk of VTE was lower in different genetic risk groups. The risk of VTE was highest in participants with the lowest hPDI scores and the highest PRS (adjusted HR: 1.72, 95% CI: 1.52-1.95). However, we did not find significant interaction between hPDI and PRS with the subsequent risk of VTE (P for interaction = 0.540). These results were also observed in the associations between hPDI, PRS, and subsequent risk of PE (adjusted HR: 1.78, 95% CI: 1.50-2.12, P for interaction = 0.901) and DVT (adjusted HR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.40-1.96, P for interaction = 0.507) (Supplemental Figures 2C and 2F).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the independent and joint associations between hPDI and polygenic risk with the risk of VTE. We found that adherence to a healthy plant-based dietary pattern based on a reduced intake of refined grains, potatoes, sugary beverages, desserts, and animal fats, is associated with a lower risk of VTE independent of genetic background, lifestyles, sociodemographic features, and multiple morbidities.

Even though no previous studies explored the associations between hPDI and risk of VTE, our findings are generally in line with those from previous studies on hPDI in relation to CVD. Higher adherence to hPDI was associated with reduced risk of CVD in National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey study,22 Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study,23 and in 2 cohorts of health care professionals (Nurses’ Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study).24 The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study also suggested that dietary patterns with high consumption of fish, fruit, and vegetable are associated with lower risk of VTE.25 The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension, which is high in fruits, vegetables, and low-fat dairy products, but low in saturated and total fat, has also been demonstrated to be associated reduced the risk of VTE.26 Although the food groups included in these dietary patterns partially overlap with the plant-based dietary patterns developed empirically for this study, most of these dietary patterns ignore the intake of animal foods and some unhealthy plant food. Compared with animal foods, plant foods are low in saturated fat, less energy dense, and rich in dietary fibre, plant proteins, unsaturated fatty acids, and a variety of micronutrients.27 Until now, the concept of a plant-based diet still varies greatly, and it is more importantly to understand the heterogeneous health effects of gradually increasing plant-based foods and decreasing animal-based foods than to completely abandon animal-based foods in favor of a vegetarian diet. Satjia et al. has developed a healthy version of the Plant-Based Diet Index to assess plant food intake without excluding animal food intake, which is considered to be a healthy cultural dietary pattern.20 As proven by our study, this healthy plant-based dietary pattern is associated with the risk of incident VTE, which supplements the new evidence in this area.

Our findings are also consistent with the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Task Force on Clinical Practice Guidelines for primary prevention of CVD, which state that adherence to a healthy dietary pattern reduces the risk of CVD, with a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains being the optimal, as well as a reduction in added sugars (sugary beverages), and red meat.28 On the other hand, plant-based diet patterns are conductive to environmental development due to their lower resource intensity, reduced environmental impact, and lower greenhouse gas emissions compared to animal-based foods.29, 30, 31 Thus promoting hPDI benefits both human health and ecosystems.

The beneficial effects of plant-based diet could be explained by different potential mechanisms. The healthy plant-based dietary pattern consists of whole grains, fruits, vegetables, tea, coffee, and nuts, which are rich in dietary fiber, unsaturated fatty acids, antioxidants, and polyphenols. These are all have been shown to have protective effect on vascular health and prevention of VTE events.32, 33, 34, 35, 36 Foods rich in folate and vitamin B6 (eg, fish, carrots, legumes, and bananas), which are effective in reducing the incidence of VTE events.37,38 Finally, diets actually consist of a variety of compounds, and there may be synergistic effects of the various components of food, so that the sum of the dietary components has a greater impact on health than the individual effects of a single food or nutrient.

PRS provide a comprehensive assessment of an individual's genetic susceptibility to disease by considering multiple genetic variants across the genome. An increasing number of studies using PRS have investigated the combined effects of different dietary patterns on adverse health outcomes, including type 2 diabetes,39 cancer,40 and CVD.41 Our study did not observe significant interaction between hPDI and polygenic risk for VTE, which suggest that higher adherence to a healthy plant based diet was associated with lower VTE risk, independent of genetic risk, underlining the importance of diet in VTE prevention interventions. This combined approach, which takes into account both genetic susceptibility and modifiable lifestyle factors, may yield better results in providing prevention of VTE. Further research is needed on the mechanisms of plant-based dietary patterns that reduce the risk of VTE incident in different genetic backgrounds.

The strengths of our study included a large-scale sample, a long follow-up period, and sensitivity analyses for potential confounders, which demonstrate the robustness of the findings. In addition, we considered the complexity and multidimensionality of the diet and calculated dietary scores rather than single nutrients. Meanwhile, there are some limitations. First, some bias may exist due to self-reporting dietary data, but these may decrease with cumulative measurements of the diet, reflecting dietary habits over time.19 The Oxford WebQ, a self-administered 24-hour recall method, was used to derive the hPDI. This method yields results broadly comparable to interviewer-administered 24-hour recalls.17 The self-administered 24-hour recalls may miss crucial dietary details, as well as limitations in representing habitual and long-term dietary habits, compared to a comprehensive assessment of total dietary intake (eg, food frequency questionnaire), potentially affecting the representativeness of the hPDI scores. However, 20,348 participants repeated the touchscreen questionnaire with a median of 4.4 years after the initial assessment. Analysis of the data indicated moderate to substantial agreement between responses at baseline and at the follow-up visit.42 Second, while this study controlled for many factors, residual confounders could not be completely excluded. We have further performed subgroup and sensitivity analyses which could partially reduce the possible bias. Finally, as the participants were all of European ancestry, our findings may only be applicable to populations with similar demographic characteristics, which may limit the generalization to the general population.

Conclusions

In this large cohort study based on the UK Biobank, we found that adherence to a healthy plant-based dietary pattern based on a reduced intake of refined grains, potatoes, sugary beverages, desserts, and animal fats could reduce the risk of VTE regardless of genetic background, lifestyle sociodemographic features and multiple morbidities (Central Illustration). These results underline the importance of dietary interventions in future VTE prevention strategies regardless of genetic predisposition. Further studies based on different populations are needed to validate these findings in the future.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Future efforts are needed to encourage people to follow the healthy plant-based diet to prevent VTE.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The healthy plant-based diet could reduce the risk of venous thromboembolism irrespective of genetic background, lifestyle, sociodemographic characteristics, and multiple morbidities.
