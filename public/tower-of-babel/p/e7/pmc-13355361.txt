Systemic immunosuppression limits NK cell therapy efficacy in pancreatic cancer

ABSTRACT
Nature killer (NK) cell plays a critical role in cancer immunosurveillance and is considered a potent immunotherapeutic tool for many cancers, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDACs). Increasing evidence suggests that cancer occurs with systemic immune perturbations. However, the effects of PDAC tumor burden on systemic NK cells remain poorly understood. PDAC tumor-bearing mice display decreased frequency and dysfunction of NKs the spleens. We identified an increase in Gr-1+ myeloid cells within the spleens, which negatively impacts both endogenous and adoptively transferred NK cell frequency and function. Apolipoprotein E (ApoE), a lipid metabolism regulator, is upregulated in Gr-1+ myeloid cells of tumor-bearing mice, promoting lipid oxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Genetic knockout of Apoe in Gr-1+ myeloid cells abrogate their suppressive effects on NK cell function. Furthermore, treatment with lipid metabolism inhibitors restores endogenous and adoptively transferred NKs’ effector function in the spleens and tumor microenvironment. These studies underscore the importance of understanding preexisting systemic alterations in PDAC patients before applying NK cell-based immunotherapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02340-8.

Background

Despite the remarkable progress of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in treating various cancers, its efficacy against pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains limited. This underscores an urgent need for novel immunotherapeutic approaches to combat this aggressive malignancy effectively. Natural Killer (NK) cell-based immunotherapies are rapidly emerging as effective modalities for cancer treatment due to their intrinsic ability to recognize and eliminate tumor cells independently of antigen presentation, alongside their capacity to produce cytokines that modulate adaptive immune responses [1]. NK cell-based therapies, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-transduced NK cells, have shown promising results in some cancer models [2]. However, these approaches have shown limited efficacy in PDAC. In the PDAC tumor microenvironment (TME), NK cell numbers are very low and often display attenuated function compared to their healthy counterparts [3]. Notably, the PDAC tumor microenvironment exhibits hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and the accumulation of metabolic waste products, all of which can impact NK cell function. PDAC cells can upregulate the expression of indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), which catalyzes the conversion of tryptophan to kynurenine. IDO-1 driven tryptophan depletion in the TME impairs NK cell function. [4]. Our recently published study shows that PDAC cells preferentially utilize vitamin B6 (VB6) to enhance one-carbon metabolism for their proliferation, which culminates in reduced availability of the pyridoxal phosphate (an active form of VB6) in PDAC TME. The resulting VB6 deficiency hinders glycogen breakdown, a critical process supporting glycolysis during NK cell activation, thereby impairing their cytotoxic capabilities [5].

Cancer is a systemic disease characterized by complex interactions between malignant and non-malignant cells within the local tumor microenvironment (TME) and the distant microenvironment [6, 7]. To date, the understanding of the PDAC-driven factor in shaping systemic anti-tumor immunity and immunotherapy response remains poorly understood [8, 9]. Multiple studies have shown that tumor cells can cause systemic immune perturbations, which include the expansion of immunosuppressive myeloid populations in lymphoid organs [10, 11]. Thus, understanding the implication of these systemic perturbations on anti-tumor immunity is crucial for developing effective immunotherapies against PDAC.

The spleen is the largest peripheral lymphoid organ and plays a crucial role in T cell development. The spleen regulates systemic immune responses and displays significant hematopoiesis, particularly in various cancers [7, 12]. Earlier, a study demonstrated the splenic expansion of myeloid cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) of PDAC [13]. However, the study did not interrogate the impact of expanded immunosuppressive myeloid populations on splenic lymphocyte (particularly NK cell) function, and these questions remain unexplored. Multiple studies have shown that the adoptively transferred CAR T/NK cells primarily locate in the peripheral organs such as the liver and spleen [14–18]. Thus, the immunosuppressive splenic microenvironment may influence the cytotoxic capabilities of these transferred NK and T cells. Given the ongoing clinical trials investigating the efficacy of CAR-T/NK cell therapies in PDAC patients (NCT04390399, NCT02839954, NCT03634501, NCT03941457, NCT03323944, NCT05275062, and NCT05277987 a comprehensive understanding of how systemic immune perturbations regulate the effector function of adoptively transferred immune cells is paramount.

In the present study, we utilized mouse models to investigate systemic immunosuppression in PDAC and evaluated the functional, transcriptional, and metabolic profiles of splenic NKs in mouse models. We explored the underlying mechanisms of splenic NK cell dysfunction in PDAC tumor-experienced mice. Our findings highlight the importance of considering pre-existing systemic immune alterations in cancer patients before implementing immune therapies.

Methods

Mice and orthotopic tumor models

Eight- to ten-week-old Apoe-/- (strain# 002052) and wild-type C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Nebraska Medical Center and the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center. The KPC cell lines were provided by Dr. David Tuveson (KPC1245) and Dr. Michael A. Hollingsworth (KPC8069). Cancer cells were cultured in RPMI medium (ThermoFisher, Cat# 22400089) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Orthotopic implantations of tumor cells (3000) were carried out as previously described [19]. Treatment with Trimetazidine (Cayman Chemical, Cat# 18165, 10 mg/kg/day, orally), orlistat (Cayman Chemical, Cat# 10005426, 50 mg/kg/day, orally), vitamin E (Sigma, Cat# T3634, 10 mg/kg/day, subcutaneous injections), or BMX-001 (0.5 mg/kg/day, subcutaneous injections) begun from post ten days of tumor cell-implantation. In adoptive NK cell transfer experiments, 5 × 108 NKs were infused through the tail vein. Recombinant murine IL-15 (Peprotech, cat# 210 − 15, 2 µg/mouse) was used to stimulate NKs.

In vitro culture assays

Murine NKs and Gr-1+ cells were isolated from the spleens. Briefly, spleens were processed as per the published protocol [20]. NKs and Gr-1+ cells were isolated using an NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-115-818) and an MDSC isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-094-538). To generate MDSCs from bone marrow collected from femur and tibia bones in 5- to 6-week-old mice, bone marrow cells were cultured in 100 mm dishes (2 × 10) cells in RPMI medium with 10% FBS, recombinant GM-CSF (PeproTech, cat# 315-03, 10 ng/mL), G-CSF (PeproTech, cat# 250-05, 10 ng/mL) for six days.

For the co-culture assay, Gr-1+ cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were cultured in 12-well plates with NKs at a density of 5 × 105 cells in the culture inserts (Millipore, cat#PICM03050) for respective time points. Post co-culture, ROS measurement (DCFDA assay, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D6883), lipid staining (BODIPY 493/503, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Cat# D3922), lipid peroxidation measurement (C11-BODIPY 581/591, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Cat# D3861) were performed in the harvested MDSCs or NK cells. NK cell killing activity against pre-labeled (CellTrace Violet, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Cat# C34557) target cells was determined using the propidium iodide staining method as previously described [21]. Additionally, we assessed the NK cell activity by checking the levels of IFNγ, Granzyme B (GZMB), and CD107a after target cell stimulation for 4 h using flow cytometer analysis.

Immune cell depletion

For regulatory T cell depletion, mice received 400 µg anti-CD25 antibody (BioXcell, Cat# BE0012) three days per week. Rat IgG1 (BioXcell, Cat# BE0088) was used as isotype control. For macrophage depletion, mice received 500 µg anti-CSF1R antibody (BioXcell, Cat# BP0213). Rat IgG2a (BioXcell, Cat# BP0089) was used as isotype control. For the depletion of MDSCs, mice received 300 µg anti-Ly6G/Ly6C (Gr-1) antibody (BioXcell, Cat# BP0075). Rat IgG2b (BioXcell, Cat# BP0090) was used as isotype control. These antibodies were administered five days after orthotopic tumor cell implantation and continued (3 times/week) till the end of the study.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

The single-cell RNA-seq data of human PDAC tumors, healthy control, and PBMCs were collected from previous studies [22–24]. The data for Fig. 1A were obtained from a study by Junya Peng et al. [23]. The UMAP analysis plots and metabolic pathway enrichment signature in Fig. 1A were generated using the tools from Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub (TISCH) at tisch.comp-genomics.org [24]. The data for Fig. 1B were obtained from the study by Nina G. Steele et al. [22]. The raw data of the expression profiles for APOE, GPX1, A2M, and G0S2 in 12 main cell types in human PDAC tumors were obtained from the study by Junya Peng et al. [23]. The raw data was obtained from The Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) with Accession number CRA001160 and the NIH Gene Expression Omnibus database under the accession number GSE155698.

Flow cytometry analysis and immunofluorescent staining

For flow cytometry analysis, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 250 µl Brefeldin A (1 mg/mL) six hours before euthanization. Blood, spleens, and tumors were collected and processed into single cells according to a previously established protocol [5]. The cells were blocked with FC anti-CD16/32 (BioLegend, Cat # 101320) and incubated with LIVE/DEAD dye (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat# L34966). Following this, the cells were labeled with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting cell surface and intracellular markers. Flow cytometry analysis was carried out using an LSRII-Yellow/Green Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the resulting data was analyzed utilizing FlowJo V10 software (TreeStar).

In the immunofluorescent staining assay procedure, the mice were initially perfused with 30 ml of PBS, followed by perfusion with a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 0.5% methanol in PBS. The collected tissues were then stored in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 48 h. Subsequently, the tissues were transferred to a PBS solution containing 25% sucrose until they sank and then embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT to prepare eight µm sections. The anti-APOE (Proteintech, cat #: 66830, 1:75 dilution), anti-LDLR (Proteintech, cat #:10785, 1:150 dilution), anti-Gr-1 (BioLegend, Cat #: 108401, 1:100 dilution), and anti-CD45 (BioLegend, Cat #: 157602, 1:75 dilution) antibodies were used to detect the target proteins.

LC-MS/MS analysis of polar metabolites and non-polar lipids

Polar and non-polar metabolites were analyzed using a Waters Acquity UPLC system connected to a Xevo TQ-S mass spectrometer according to a previously established protocol [25, 26]. The metabolite peak areas were integrated using Skyline (MacCoss Lab Software) and normalized to the respective cell numbers. Further analysis of the relative quantification of the resultant peak areas and principal component analysis, heatmap analysis, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Metaboanalyst 5.0 [27].

Statistical analysis

The Unpaired student’s t-test was used to compare the two groups. A one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed to compare multiple groups. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was used for multiple groups with two independent variables. Gene FPKM was utilized to reflect the gene expression levels. The |fold change|>2 with adjusted p-value < 0.05 was considered for further analysis. The GESA analysis was conducted using GSEA_4.0.2 and KEGG datasets. Mouse survival comparisons were made using Kaplan-Meier curves and the Mantel-Cox log-rank test. The statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, USA). p and adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significant.

Additional detailed materials and methods are provided in the Supplementary material section.

Results

Pancreatic tumor induces dysfunction of endogenous and adoptive transferred NK cells in the spleen

While PDAC is often characterized as an “immunologically cold” cancer [9], analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing datasets [22, 23, 28] revealed a notable presence of anti-tumor effector cells, including CD4 + T, CD8 + T, and NKs, within the TME and in the circulation of PDAC patients (Figure S1A). Furthermore, we have shown that NK cell infiltration is a predictive prognostic factor in PDAC [5]. We have recently demonstrated that enhancing NK cell-based immunotherapies presents promising approaches to improve the clinical outcomes for PDAC [5]. However, studies have shown that adoptively transferred NKs primarily accumulate in liver and spleen organs [14–18]. Spleen regulates innate and adaptive immunity; hence, any aberration in the splenic microenvironment could modulate the cytotoxic capacity of transferred NKs in a tumor-bearing host.

To investigate the systemic effects of PDAC on the NKs in spleen, we orthotopically implanted immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice with syngeneic pancreatic tumor cell lines (KPC1245 or KPC8069) derived from LSL-KrasLSL.G12D/+; LSL-p53R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) PDAC mouse model. The KPC tumor-bearing mice (called PDAC tumor-bearing mice henceforth) exhibited splenomegaly (Figure S1B), a common feature in advanced PDAC patients [29]. Severe enlarged white and red pulp compartments were observed in the spleens of PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 2A), indicating that PDAC burden induced significant immune reaction in the spleen. Interestingly, splenic NKs from PDAC tumor-bearing mice showed reduced cytotoxicity against PDAC tumor cells and two established murine MHC class I-deficient target cells (YAC-1 [30] and B16F10 [31]) in ex vivo studies (Figure S1C & D). These NKs also showed reduced granzyme B (GZMB), IFNγ, and CD107a expression as compared to splenic NKs from healthy mice (Figure S1E). Transcriptomic analysis identified over 1500 genes with significant changes in expression in splenic NK cells from PDAC mice (Fig. 2B). Gene set enrichment analysis highlighted alterations in pathways related to cytokine signaling, autoimmune responses, infections, and cytotoxicity (Fig. 2C). Several genes crucial for NK cell killing function, such as Gzma, Prf1, Gzmb, Ncr1, Fasl, and Ifng, were downregulated in splenic NKs from PDAC mice (Fig. 2D). In alignment with these findings, tumor-infiltrating NK cells and circulating NK cells from PDAC patients demonstrated reduced expression of effector function genes, alongside increased expression of inhibitory checkpoint genes such as PCDC1, LAG3, and TIGIT (Figure S1F& G). These results indicate that PDAC tumor induces systemic immunosuppressive effects on endogenous circulating and splenic NKs.

To assess the immunosuppressive effects of PDAC on adoptively transferred NKs within the splenic microenvironment, we infused orthotopic PDAC mice with syngeneic CFSE-labeled NKs (from healthy mice) and treated with IL15 (Fig. 2E). Herein, we noted the increased accumulation of transferred NKs in spleens compared to tumors. Moreover, the frequency of both endogenous and adoptively transferred NKs was significantly reduced in the blood and spleens of PDAC mice compared to healthy mice (Fig. 2F & G). Furthermore, adoptively transferred NKs (CFSE+) isolated from the spleens of PDAC tumor-bearing mice exhibited reduced cytotoxicity against target cells in vitro compared to endogenous and adoptively transferred splenic NKs from healthy mice (Fig. 2H). This diminished cytotoxicity was accompanied by a significant decrease in the expression of GZMB, IFNγ, and CD107a (Fig. 2I). These results demonstrate that PDAC-induced immune alterations within the spleen suppress the effector function of both endogenous and adoptive transferred NKs.

Depletion of Gr-1+ cells rescues the effector functions of splenic NKs in PDAC mice

To understand how the PDAC tumor burden affects NK cell function in the spleen, we examined the proportions of different immune cell types in the spleens of PDAC mice. Consistent with previous reports [32], the macrophage (CD11b+, CD11c-, F4/80+) and dendritic cell (CD11b+, CD11c+, F4/80-) population was significantly increased in the spleen of PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Figure S1H & I). In addition, the frequency of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, including granulocytic-MDSCs (G-MDSCs, CD11b+, Ly6G+) and monocytic-MDSCs (M-MDSCs, CD11b+, Ly6C+), were also drastically increased (Figure S1J & K). While the CD8 + T and CD4 + T cell populations were significantly decreased, the regulatory T cells (Tregs; FOXP3+, CD25+, CD4+) proportion was notably increased in PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Figure S1L). We also noted a significant decrease in NK cell proportion in the spleens of PDAC tumor-bearing mice compared to the healthy control counterparts (Figure S1M). Together, these findings indicate that orthotopic PDAC tumor remodels the immune environment in the spleen.

To investigate the role of expanded splenic Tregs and myeloid cells in NK dysfunction, we performed depletion studies using anti-CD25 (eliminating Tregs), anti-CSF1R (eliminating macrophages), or anti-Gr-1+ (eliminating MDSCs) antibodies in PDAC mice. Neither anti-CD25 nor anti-CSF1R antibody treatment restored the frequency and effector function of splenic NKs from PDAC mice (Fig. 3A–F). However, anti-Gr-1 treatment fully restored the effector function of splenic NKs (Fig. 3D–F), indicating that MDSCs are required for PDAC tumor-induced systemic NK cell dysfunction. To corroborate these findings, we cultured splenic NKs from healthy mice with splenic Gr-1+ cells from PDAC tumor-bearing mice. Splenic Gr-1+ cells from PDAC mice significantly inhibited NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, degranulation, and cytokine-producing capabilities (Fig. 3G & H). These findings suggest that Gr-1+ cells mediate splenic NK cell dysfunction in PDAC mice.

Splenic NKs exhibit altered lipid metabolism in PDAC mice

Earlier studies showed a significant connection between metabolism and the activity of immune cells, including NKs, in the context of tumor burden [33, 34]. We conducted a metabolomic analysis to identify the metabolic pathways involved in the impaired splenic NK cell function observed in PDAC mice. The principal component analysis (PCA) and heatmap analysis revealed that tumor burden primarily affected non-polar metabolic pathways in splenic NKs (Figure S2A & B). Specifically, there was an overall increase in lipid metabolites, including free fatty acids (FAAs) and triacylglycerides (TAGs), in splenic NKs cells from PDAC tumor-bearing mice compared to healthy NKs (Figure S2C). BODIPY staining further confirmed the increased lipid levels in splenic NKs from PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 4A). However, the increase in lipid metabolites was reversed upon depletion of Gr-1+ cells (Fig. S2C). Also, NKs showed significant increase in lipid levels upon co-culture with splenic Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 4B). These results indicate a potential role of Gr-1+ cells in driving metabolic changes in splenic NKs of PDAC mice.

Splenic NK cells from PDAC tumor-bearing mice showed increased levels of genes involved in lipid synthesis (Figure D). Diacylglycerol O-acyltransferases (Dgat1/2) are enzymes that play a key role in the production of TAGs. We then investigated whether blocking TAG synthesis with the DGAT inhibitor T863 [35] could improve the function of NK cells. While T863 treatment (100mM in DMSO) attenuated lipid content in NKs (Figure S2E), it failed to restore the functional profile of co-cultured NKs (Figure S2F& G), suggesting that Gr-1+ cell-induced TAG synthesis and lipid accumulation does not directly contribute to the dysfunction of splenic NKs in PDAC mice.

Oxidative stress is a known driver of lipid accumulation in cells [34–36]. Splenic NKs from PDAC tumor-bearing mice exhibited elevated levels of lipid peroxidation (measured by C11-BODIPY (581/591) probe) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), but depletion of Gr-1+ cells significantly reduced these oxidative stress markers (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, increased ROS levels and lipid peroxidation were observed in NKs upon co-culture with Gr-1+ cells from PDAC mice (Fig. 4D & E). To determine whether oxidative stress contributes to NK cell dysfunction, alpha-tocopherol (vitamin E; VitE) was utilized in co-culture assays. VitE blocked Gr-1+ cell-induced ROS elevation and lipid peroxidation in NKs, and Gr-1+ cells failed to suppress the NK cell effector function in the presence of VitE (Fig. 4F–I). Moreover, VitE prevented lipid accumulation, ROS elevation, and lipid peroxidation in splenic NKs in vivo (Fig. 4J–L). Moreover, VitE treatment rescued the killing ability of splenic NKs from PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 4M & N). Thus, Gr-1+ cell-induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation contribute to NK dysfunction in PDAC mice.

APOE underlies the immunosuppressive effects of Gr-1+ cells on splenic NKs

Myeloid cells are abundant in both PDAC tumor microenvironment (TME) and the peripheral blood and have been shown to exert immunosuppressive effects in PDAC animal models and patients [36–39]. However, the mechanisms underlying the immunosuppressive effect of myeloid cells in the splenic niche remain unclear. GSEA of a single-cell RNA-seq dataset from 24 PDAC tumor samples [23] revealed a significant enrichment of transcriptional signatures associated with lipid metabolism in the myeloid lineage subsets (Fig. 1A). Similarly, analysis of another single-cell RNA-seq dataset comprising 16 PDAC patient samples and four healthy subjects [22], demonstrated a significant upregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolic pathways in both tumor-infiltrating and circulating myeloid populations (Fig. 1B). Among the top altered genes (top 10 upregulated and top 10 downregulated) related to lipid metabolism, APOE, GPX1, and A2M were upregulated.

In contrast, the expression of G0S2 was downregulated in myeloid cells from PDAC compared to the control cohort in both scRNA-seq datasets analyzed [22, 23] (Fig. 1C). Among these altered genes, APOE showed the highest expression in PDAC-associated myeloid cells (Fig. 1D).

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) is involved in lipid transport and metabolism [40, 41]. We observed elevated levels of APOE in the spleens of tumor-bearing mice, particularly concentrated in areas enriched with Gr-1 positive cells (Figure S3A). This finding suggests a potential role for APOE in regulating lipid metabolism that may underlie Gr-1+ cell-induced dysfunction of NKs. To investigate this hypothesis, tumor cells were implanted into Apoe-knockout (Apoe−/−) mice. APOE was undetectable in the serum of Apoe−/− mice. However, compared to healthy controls, APOE was significantly elevated in wild-type (WT) mice with PDAC tumors (Figure S3B). Intriguingly, the presence of PDAC tumors impaired effector functions of splenic NKs from WT mice, while splenic NKs from Apoe-/- tumor-bearing mice retained the killing ability, which was comparable to NKs from Gr-1+ cell-depleted PDAC mice (Figure S3C-E). This data indicates that APOE plays a crucial role in attenuating the function of splenic NKs in PDAC mice. However, recombinant murine APOE did not directly inhibit the effector function of NKs (Figure S3F–H). Apoe-/- NKs didn’t demonstrate impaired killing ability. However, WT and Apoe-/- NKs exhibited reduced cytotoxicity upon co-culture with Gr-1+ cells (Figure S3I). These results showed that APOE is required for PDAC to suppress splenic NK cell function, but APOE does not directly induce NK cell dysfunction. Our data suggests that the expression of Apoe in Gr-1+ cells might play a critical role in their immunosuppressive activity. Thus, we pre-cultured NK cells with either Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells or WT Gr-1+ cells. As anticipated, NKs pre-cultured with Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells exhibited enhanced cytotoxic activity compared to those pre-cultured with WT Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 1E–I). These results indicate that the absence of APOE in Gr-1+ cells significantly reduces their immunosuppressive effects on NK cells.

Our in vivo data showed that the depletion of Gr-1+ cells prevents lipid peroxidation in NKs. Also, we showed that Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells fail to induce NK cell dysfunction in vitro. Hence, we proposed that the absence of APOE disrupts the capacity of Gr-1+ cells to generate oxidative stress that contributes to systemic dysfunction in NKs. In contrast to the WT Gr-1+ cells, Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells failed to increase lipid content, ROS levels, and lipid peroxidation in NKs (Fig. 1J–L). Cell division assays showed that NKs co-cultured with Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells exhibited a significantly faster proliferation rate compared to those co-cultured with WT Gr-1+ cells (Figure S3J). The absence of APOE also reduced their ROS production (Figure S3K). Overall, APOE is critical for immunosuppressive functions of Gr-1+ cells against NKs.

PDAC tumor burden reprograms lipid metabolism in Gr-1+ cells

To uncover the mechanisms that govern APOE-regulated ROS production in Gr-1+ cells, we performed RNA sequencing analysis of WT and Apoe-/- splenic Gr-1+ cells derived from PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 5A). The GSEA analysis revealed a significant downregulation of genes associated with lipid metabolism, including lipid transport, cholesterol metabolism, and glycerolipid metabolism in Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 5B). Among the significantly altered genes involved in lipid metabolism, we observed decreased transcript levels of genes related to lipid transport (such as Apoe, Apoc1, Lrpap1, Abcb4, Npc1, Npc2, Tspo, Scarb1, Sort1, and Cd36), lipid synthesis (Plpp1, Lpcat1, Lpcat2, Lpcat4, Agpat2, Mogat2, Soat1, Soat2, Gk, and Dgat2), and lipolysis (Lipa, Lipe, Lipg, Mgll, and Lpl) in Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 5C).

Next, we examined the lipidomic profile of Gr-1+ cells isolated from the spleens of WT and Apoe-/- mice with PDAC tumors. The absence of Apoe led to a significant reduction in neutral lipids (such as triglycerides, diacylglycerols, and monoacylglycerols) and free fatty acids in Gr-1+ cells. However, the levels of most structural lipids, including sphingomyelin, ceramide, lysophospholipids, and glycerophospholipids, remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). BODIPY staining indicated that Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells had lower overall lipid content (Fig. 5E), suggesting the absence of Apoe may impair lipid uptake in Gr-1+ cells. To investigate the role of APOE in immune reaction in PDAC, we examined the expression of Apoe and its receptors in primary bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs) cultured with serum from healthy or PDAC tumor-bearing mice. WT BMMCs exhibited increased expression of Apoe and its receptors (Apoer2, Lrp1, and Ldlr) when stimulated with serum (10%) from PDAC tumor-bearing mice. However, Apoe-/- hindered PDAC serum-induced expression of Apoer2 and Lrp1 in BMMCs (Fig. 5F–I). These results indicate that PDAC tumor induces the upregulation of Apoe and its receptors in BMMC-derived Gr-1+ cells.

APOE can bind with cell surface receptors like LDLR and LRPs to facilitate the uptake of LDL and VLDL. We observed increased lipid content in splenic cells from PDAC mice compared to healthy control. Notably, cells with higher LDLR expression exhibited increased lipid accumulation (Figure S4A). To further determine the role of APOE-mediated lipid utilization, we utilized siRNA to knock down Ldlr, a major receptor for APOE in Gr-1 cells (Figure S4B-C). Knockdown of Ldlr reduced the lipid content in Gr-1+ cells (Figure S4D). Furthermore, knocking down Ldlr or blocking lipid oxidation (by Etomoxir) eliminated the suppressive effects of Gr-1+ cells on NK cell proliferation (Figure S4E & F). Compared to undifferentiated BMMCs, Gr-1+ cells displayed significantly higher levels of neutral lipids and FFA. However, the knockdown of Ldlr impaired the lipid increase in Gr-1+ cells (Figure S4G). Contrastingly, most structural lipids remained at similar levels in control Gr-1+ cells and Ldlr-knockdown Gr-1+ cells (Figure S4H). In addition, the knockdown of Ldlr significantly impaired the suppressive effect of MDSCs on NKs (Figure S4I-J). These findings demonstrated that the loss of APOE/LDLR /-mediated lipid utilization weakens the immunosuppressive functions of Gr-1+ cells.

Knockout of Apoe disrupts fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in MDSCs

Mitochondrial FAO is a major source for ROS production [42, 43], and single-cell RNA-seq data [22, 23] showed that myeloid cells in PDAC have increased expression of some genes related to FAO compared to myeloid cells from healthy PBMCs (Fig. 5J). To investigate the effect of Apoe-/- on mitochondrial FAO in Gr-1+ cells, the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in splenic Gr-1+ cells isolated from PDAC mice. We found that Apoe-/- significantly impaired OCR in splenic Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 5K). Specifically, Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells exhibited reduced basal respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity (SRC) compared to WT Gr-1+ cells. These results suggest that the absence of Apoe weakens the mitochondrial oxidation within splenic Gr-1+ cells in PDAC mice.

Although the knockout of ApoE significantly reduced OCR in splenic Gr-1+ cells, GSEA analysis revealed an upregulation of mitochondrial translation and oxidative phosphorylation in Apoe-/- Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 5L), suggesting a compensatory mechanism. Thus, we hypothesized that ApoE might impair mitochondrial FAO in Gr-1+ cells. To investigate this, we measured OCR in WT and Apoe-/- splenic Gr-1+ cells before and after treatment with etomoxir, an irreversible inhibitor of mitochondrial FAO. Etomoxir significantly reduced OCR in wild-type cells but had minimal effects on Apoe-/- cells (Fig. 5M). These results indicate that Apoe-/- impairs FAO in Gr-1+ cells.

Restricting lipolysis and FAO in Gr-1+ cells hamper their immunosuppressive ability

Neutral lipids are a primary energy source for cells during periods of high energy demand and environmental stress. Lipolytic enzymes, or lipases, break down neutral lipids into fatty acids (FAs), which are then utilized by the cell for biological processes through fatty acid catabolism [44]. Our data connected the lipid metabolic profiles and immunosuppressive functions within Gr-1+ cells in PDAC mice. APOE, along with its receptors, facilitates lipid uptake in Gr-1+ cells. These intracellular lipids are subsequently converted into FAs to support FAO and generate ROS, leading to lipid peroxidation and functional impairments in systemic NKs. Thus, we posited that inhibiting the FAs synthesis or oxidation can rescue NK cell function (Fig. 6A). Single-cell RNA-seq data [22, 23] showed that myeloid cells in human PDAC tumors exhibit elevated expression of lipases, including LIPA, LIPE, MGLL, and PNPLA2, compared to the myeloid cells in PBMCs from healthy individuals (Fig. 6B), suggesting that myeloid cells in PDAC tumors may have increased lipolysis activity (to provide FAs which can be used for beta oxidation) compared to their counterparts in healthy individuals.

To investigate the role of lipid metabolism in MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, we employed a panel of small molecule inhibitors targeting ROS (BMX-001, mito-TEMPO, and Vitamin E), lipolysis (Orlistat and Lalistat 1), and FAO (Etomoxir and Trimetazidine) (Fig. 6A). Treatment with BMX-001, mito-TEMPO, and Vitamin E effectively restored the effector function of NKs co-cultured with Gr-1+ cells (Figure S5A–F). Additionally, blocking FAO with Etomoxir reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation levels in co-cultured NKs (Figure S5G & H). Furthermore, blocking lipolysis (with Orlistat, Lalistat 1) and lipid oxidation (with Trimetazidine) reduced GM-CSF/G-CSF (GM/G) and tumor serum (TS)-induced ROS production in Gr-1+ cells (Figure S5I & J). These results suggest that targeting lipolysis and FAO may represent a novel strategy to attenuate Gr-1+ cell-mediated immunosuppression.

To investigate the effects of targeting lipolysis, FAO, and ROS in vivo, we administered Orlistat, Trimetazidine, and BMX-001 into PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Figure S5K). The PDAC burden increased the population of Gr-1+ cells in the spleens, accompanied by elevated expression of Arginase 1 (ARG1) and IL10, known mediators of immunosuppression (Fig. 6C & D). Treatment with Orlistat, Trimetazidine, or BMX-001 showed modest effects on tumor growth and overall spleen weight in pancreatic tumor-bearing mice. Also, these treatments did not reduce the proportion of Gr-1+ cells in the spleens compared to vehicle treated control tumor cohort (Fig. 6C, S5L & M). However, these treatments decreased the ARG1, IL10 protein levels and ROS abundance within splenic Gr-1+ cells (Fig. 6D & E). These findings suggest that targeting lipolysis, FAO, and ROS production can attenuate the immunosuppressive activity of Gr-1+ cells in the spleens of PDAC mice.

We then investigated whether inhibiting lipolysis, FAO, or ROS production improves NK cell effector function at systemic sites. Inhibiting lipolysis and FAO, as well as scavenging ROS, increased the population of splenic NKs in PDAC mice (Fig. 6F). Treatment with Orlistat (lipolysis inhibitor), Trimetazidine (FAO inhibitor), and BMX-001 (ROS scavenger) resulted in a significant increase in splenic NK cell population in PDAC mice (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, these treatments reduced ROS levels and decreased lipid peroxidation stress in splenic NKs (Fig. 6G & H). Notably, the expression of PD-1, LAG3, and TIGIT inhibitory receptors was significantly reduced in NKs following the treatments (Fig. 6I–K). Consistently, ex vivo assays demonstrated enhanced cytotoxic function in splenic NK cells from treated groups (Fig. 6L–O). These findings suggest that targeting lipid uptake, FAO, and ROS generation may restore the immunosurveillance role of systemic NKs in PDAC.

Inhibiting lipid utilization enhances NK cell immunotherapy

Our results demonstrated that pancreatic tumor increases Gr-1+ cell population within the spleen. Previous studies have established the spleen as a primary site for the accumulation of adoptively transferred NKs [38, 45], suggesting that splenic Gr-1+ cells may hinder the function of these NKs. We hypothesized that inhibiting the APOE-mediated utilization of lipids could potentially eliminate the immunosuppressive effects of Gr-1+ cells on NKs. To investigate this, we implanted pancreatic tumor (KPC1245) cells into control and Apoe-/- mice (Fig. 7A). Apoe-/- exhibited a moderate impact on the overall fraction of Gr-1+ cells in the spleen, tumor, and blood in PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Figure S6A), but a significant reduction in lipid content and ROS levels was observed in these Gr-1+ cells (Figure S6B & C). Furthermore, the fraction of both adoptively transferred and endogenous NKs was significantly increased in the spleen, tumors, and blood of Apoe-/- PDAC mice compared to the wild-type controls (Fig. 7B & S6D). Notably, adoptively transferred NKs exhibited accelerated proliferation in Apoe-/- PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 7C & S6E). Notably, both adoptively transferred and endogenous NKs displayed decreased lipid peroxidation stress and increased GZMB expression in Apoe-/- PDAC tumor-bearing mice compared to WT PDAC tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 7D, E, S6F, & G). These findings suggest that of Apoe impairs the immunosuppressive effects of PDAC tumor on both endogenous and adoptively transferred NKs at systemic sites.

To investigate the therapeutic potential of interrupting FAO in enhancing the efficacy of adoptively transferred NKs in PDAC. We administered trimetazidine (TMZ) into PDAC mice both before and after adoptive transfer of NK cells (Figure S7A). TMZ treatment significantly reduced ROS production in Gr-1+ cells residing in the spleens, tumors, and blood (Figure S7B). TMZ administration to PDAC-bearing mice also enhanced IL15-induced NK cell expansion and GZMB expression in both endogenous and adoptively transferred NKs at systemic sites (Fig. 7F, G, & S7C). Notably, PDAC tumors increased oxidative stress in NKs in the spleen and blood (Figure S7D). However, TMZ treatment effectively mitigated this oxidative stress in both endogenous and transferred NK cells (Figure S7D). These findings suggest that blocking FAO diminishes the immunosuppressive effects of PDAC tumor and promotes the preservation of effector functions in NKs systemically.

We then investigated the efficacy of a combined approach utilizing TMZ (to inhibit FAO) and NK cell therapy (IL15 to stimulate NKs) in the PDAC tumor model (Figure S7E). Our results demonstrate that the combination of TMZ and IL15 significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 7H). TMZ reduced the presence of immunosuppressive Gr-1+ cells and promoted IL15-induced expansion and activation of NKs (Fig. 7I, J, S7F & G). Moreover, the TMZ and IL15 combination therapy resulted in significantly prolonged survival compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 7K). To further investigate the role of NK cells in tumor reduction within the TMZ + IL15 treatment cohort, we conducted an NK cell depletion study alongside the treatment. The results indicated that NK cell depletion significantly reduced the efficacy of TMZ + IL15 on tumor growth (Figure S7H), implying a crucial role for NK cells in mediating the tumoricidal effects of this combination therapy. Additionally, to elucidate whether the beneficial effects of combined treatment on NK cell activity are primarily result from Gr-1+ cell metabolic disruption, we isolated Gr-1+ cells from the spleens of treated subjects and co-cultured them with healthy NK cells. Our data revealed that the suppressive effect of MDSCs following TMZ treatment on NK cell activity was markedly diminished (Fig. 7L). These findings suggest that TMZ’s ability to support NK cell function is at least partially mediated through the metabolic modulation of Gr-1+ cells. These findings suggest that targeting lipid utilization to overcome systemic immunosuppression holds promise for developing novel NK cell-based immunotherapies for PDAC.

Discussion

NK cells are key immunosurveillance players in cancer. However, these cells demonstrate poor presence in pancreatic cancer [46]. Past studies have shown limited NK cell infiltration, which may, in part, be governed by reduced expression of the CXCR2 receptor [46]. In the past, a positive correlation between plasma TGF-β level and NK cell dysfunction was also noted in PDAC [47]. Very recently, Muthalagu et al. showed that MYC and KRAS-mediated repression of type I interferon (IFN) pathway inhibits NK cell infiltration and function in pancreatic tumors [48]. Repression of the type I interferon (IFN) pathway attenuates the expression of CXCL13 chemokine, which is critical for B and NK cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. To overcome these challenges, some studies focus on developing genetically engineered NKs expressing CXCR2 chemokine receptors to enhance their tumor migration [49, 50]. Besides the inhibitory action on NK cells in the local environment, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) can also control NK cell immune surveillance in the peripheral system. A recent study showed that CTCs upregulate HLA-E expression (an immune checkpoint) that interacts with its corresponding receptor, NKG2A, on NK cells in pancreatic cancer [51]. These findings suggest that pancreatic tumors control the immunosurveillance function of NK cells inside and outside the tumors. To date, the study has not interrogated the activity of NK cells in the spleen, and hence, this is the first study that showed the incompetency of NK cells in the splenic niche of pancreatic tumor-bearing mice.

Prolonged inflammation is a significant factor affecting tumor growth [52]. Tumors induce a systemic inflammatory response, contributing to the dissemination of cancer cells and the formation of distant metastases [6, 52]. This inflammatory milieu was characterized by the expansion of myeloid lineage cells, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs, Gr-1+ cells in this study), within the TME and distant organs such as the bone marrow and spleen [6, 7, 37]. Our study demonstrates that PDAC tumors selectively promoted the expansion of Gr-1+ myeloid suppressor cells in the spleen. The spleen is a primary site for cancer-induced extramedullary hematopoiesis [12, 53], and splenectomy has been shown to enhance tumor growth while reducing the formation of inflammatory niches at pre-metastatic and metastatic sites, thus inhibiting lung metastases in PDAC mouse models [54]. Furthermore, genetically modified T and NK cells accumulate in the spleen following transfusion [14–18], suggesting that the expansion of splenic Gr-1+ cells may impair the function, survival, and persistence of T and NK cells. Our study reveals that the PDAC-induced expansion of Gr-1+ cells in the spleen suppresses NK cell function by creating an oxidative stress environment. These findings highlight the potential of targeting the systemic immunosuppressive environment of the spleen as a novel strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

Metabolic pathways can regulate immune responses. Our findings revealed that the APOE/LDLR axis regulates the lipid metabolism in Gr-1+ myeloid suppressor cells. Gr-1+ cells lacking Apoe exhibited diminished lipid content, impaired FAO, reduced ROS generation, and a loss of immunosuppressive function. Furthermore, targeting lipolysis or FAO impaired the immunosuppressive function of Gr-1+ myeloid suppressor cells and restored NK cell cytotoxicity in the spleen and tumor of PDAC tumor-bearing mice. Combining NK cell therapy with FAO inhibition effectively suppressed PDAC tumor growth and prolonged animal survival. While there could be additional Gr-1 cell-independent pathways that contribute to the rescue of NK cells function in TMZ (FAO inhibition) and IL 15 treated tumor bearing mice, our findings highlight the potential of targeting lipid metabolism to enhance the efficacy of NK cell immunotherapy.

The role of APOE in cancer outcomes remains a subject of ongoing investigation. Our findings, and recent studies [55, 56], highlighted a potential link between APOE/LDLR pathway and PDAC. Our study demonstrated that PDAC mice have elevated serum APOE compared to healthy controls, corroborating a previous study, in which Kemp et al. observed a correlation between higher APOE levels and poorer overall survival in PDAC patients [55]. Mechanistically, our study revealed that APOE deficiency restricts lipid availability for supporting FAO and ROS production, thereby diminishing the immunosuppressive function of splenic Gr-1+ cells. Furthermore, Guillaumond et al. recently demonstrated elevated APOE and LDLR in PDAC tissue compared to control pancreas [56]. Inhibition of LDLR-mediated cholesterol uptake enhances sensitivity to chemotherapy and augments the efficacy of chemotherapy in PDAC [56]. These studies underscore the reliance of PDAC cells and tumor-associated Gr-1+ cells on lipid metabolism, suggesting that targeting lipid utilization pathways may represent a promising novel therapeutic strategy, potentially enhancing the efficacy of existing treatments.

Numerous studies investigating oxidative stress in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and atherosclerosis have utilized Apoe-/- mice to elucidate the impact of Apoe deficiency on these pathological conditions. These studies indicate that the absence of Apoe correlates with heightened oxidative stress in both the cerebral environment associated with AD and vascular tissues involved in atherosclerosis [57, 58]. Contrastingly, our data showed that Apoe deletion is associated with decreased lipid levels and ROS (oxidative stress) within the splenic, peripheral, and intratumoral Gr-1+ cell population. This contrasting outcome suggests that ApoE may exert divergent roles in different disease contexts. In atherosclerosis studies, Apoe-/- mice are fed chow and high-fat diets. ApoE-/- mice fed on HFD show poor LDL clearance, increased cholesterol accumulation in blood, as well as increased macrophage oxidation, plaque formation, and vascular oxidation. Here, the clearance of lipids is limited in the absence of Apoe, which triggers vascular oxidation. Similarly, perturbation in cholesterol and fatty acid clearance in the murine model of AD leads to oxidative stress in ApoE-/- mice. In contrast, in PDAC, excessive circulating cholesterol is not an issue. Increased cholesterol (dyslipidemia) is associated with an increased risk of developing pancreatic cancer [59, 60]. Pancreatic tumor cells depend more on fatty acids and cholesterol to increase fatty acid oxidation and thus support their uncontrolled growth [61–63]. In parallel, PDAC zTME also displays increased expression of Apoe lipoprotein[55], which can help carry the lipids to the PDAC TME. In our model, myeloid cells (GR-1 cells) also demonstrate enhanced expression of Apoe. The presence of a tumor leads to a systemic proliferation of Gr-1+ cells, a process contingent upon lipid oxidation for both energy production and metabolic support. Per the earlier report, intracellular lipid stores provide an essential energy source for mitochondrial respiration during neutrophil differentiation and maturation in the bone marrow. We also noted a significant expansion of granulocytes in the splenic niche of the PDAC tumor model. Thus, the increased Apoe expression with concurrent lipid presence in the Gr-1+ could be behind these myelopoesis. Thus, Apoe is crucially important for Gr-1+ expansion and its loss may obstruct the influx of lipids into these myeloid cells, thereby limiting the availability of crucial fatty acids necessary for lipid oxidation. Hence, Apoe could impair the generation of ROS associated with lipid metabolism, which can reduce the immunosuppressive capabilities of Gr-1+ cells.

Conclusions

Taken together, our study elucidated the critical role of the APOE/LDLR axis in mediating the systemic immunosuppressive effects of Gr-1+ cells. We demonstrate that inhibiting FAO impairs immunosuppressive functions of Gr-1+ cells and enhances the efficacy of NK cell-based immunotherapy against PDAC. These findings also underscore the importance of considering the systemic immune microenvironment in cancer patients when utilizing CAR T/NK-based therapies.
