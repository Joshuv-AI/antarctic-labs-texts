Differences in Performance in the Trail Making Test Part B Between Adoptees With High and Low Genetic Risk for Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders: The Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia
Authors: Toni Myllyaho, Mika Niemelä, Ritva Rauhala, Helinä Hakko, Karl‐Erik Wahlberg, Sami Räsänen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe current study uses data from the Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia to investigate the association of genetic risk status and diagnosed psychiatric disorders with executive functioning, measured by Trail Making Test Part B (TMT‐B), in adoptees with high and low genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders. The results showed no statistically significant differences in TMT‐B completion times between adoptees with high and low genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders, although adoptees with high genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders as a group exhibited slightly slower performance. This contradicts previous studies reporting poorer TMT‐B performance in unaffected first‐degree relatives of individuals with schizophrenia. However, it aligns with studies that did not find differences in TMT‐B performance between first‐degree relatives of individuals with schizophrenia and controls. Significant differences in TMT‐B completion times were observed among adoptees with psychiatric disorders, indicating that psychiatric morbidity associates with TMT‐B performance, regardless of genetic risk. These findings indicate that various forms of psychopathology may hinder TMT‐B performance, potentially due to reduced processing speeds or increased propensity towards a higher error rate. This highlights the link between psychopathology and cognitive impairment, underscoring this relationship as a critical focus for neuroscience research. Although TMT‐B performance did not differ between adoptees with high and low genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders, our results do not rule out the existence of other cognitive deficits in adoptees with high genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders.Keywords: adopted children, adoptive families, executive functioning, psychiatric disorders, schizophrenia spectrum disorders, Trail Making Test Part B (TMT‐B)

Introduction

Accumulating research evidence shows that cognitive and neurocognitive deficits are a prominent feature of schizophrenia (Karrer et al. 2019; Kępińska et al. 2020; Laere et al. 2018; Mosiołek et al. 2016; Thompson et al. 2005) and schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Habtewold et al. 2020; Sánchez‐Torres et al. 2013). The reported cognitive deficits associated with schizophrenia include verbal memory, executive functioning, processing speed, cognitive flexibility and attentional dysfunctions (Habtewold et al. 2020; Laere et al. 2018; Mukkala et al. 2011; Sánchez‐Morla et al. 2009; Sánchez‐Torres et al. 2013). These deficits can significantly impact upon social and occupational functioning, contributing to a substantial socioeconomic burden (Charlson et al. 2018; Crespo‐Facorro et al. 2021; Olesen et al. 2012). Furthermore, deficits in cognitive functioning have also been reported to be present even before the onset of psychotic episodes (Bora et al. 2010; Bora and Murray 2014) and to be relatively stable in various courses of the disease process (Laere et al. 2018; Metsänen et al. 2005; Mosiołek et al. 2016), indicating that they are not only a consequence of schizophrenia but a fundamental element of the disorder itself (Kahn and Keefe 2013).

Cognitive deficits have also been reported among first‐degree relatives of patients with schizophrenia, including children, parents or siblings (Agnew‐Blais and Seidman 2013; Cella et al. 2015; Hou et al. 2016; Kuha et al. 2007; Sitskoorn et al. 2004; Snitz et al. 2006; Szöke et al. 2005; Tuulio‐Henriksson et al. 2002; Üçok et al. 2013). These impairments are documented as being similar to those of the proband subjects, although substantially less severe (Snitz et al. 2006; Szöke et al. 2005). For example, in their study Kuha et al. (2007) observed that healthy siblings of patients with schizophrenia had similar but milder deficits in performance speed and executive functioning. Conversely, some studies have failed to find differences in different measures of cognitive functioning between first‐degree relatives, including parents, siblings and children, of patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls (Laurent et al. 2001; Mukkala et al. 2011; Zalla et al. 2004).

Studies have used different test methods to assess cognitive functioning, with Trail Making Tests Part A (TMT‐A) and Part B (TMT‐B) often utilized (Reitan and Wolfson 1985). The Trail Making Test (TMT) is a well‐established test, which is sensitive to impairments in multiple cognitive domains. Performing TMT‐A requires complex visual scanning, motor processing speed, whereas performing TMT‐B also demands more complex cognitive processes such as divided attention and cognitive flexibility (Gaudino et al. 1995; Kortte et al. 2002; Laere et al. 2018; Lezak et al. 2012). Accordingly, impaired performance in the TMT‐B has been documented among individuals with schizophrenia, compared to healthy controls (Laere et al. 2018; Periáñez et al. 2007; Quiñones et al. 2009; Sánchez‐Morla et al. 2009; Yurgelun‐Todd and Kinney 1993). Poorer levels of performance in the test have also been reported in individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Sánchez‐Torres et al. 2013).

Studies have also reported moderately poorer performance in TMT‐B among first‐degree relatives of individuals schizophrenia, compared to controls (Gochman et al. 2004; Egan et al. 2001; Kuha et al. 2007; Szöke et al. 2005; Thompson et al. 2005). For example, a meta‐analysis by Sitskoorn et al. (2004) and Snitz et al. (2006) and the study by Thompson et al. (2005) reported that the difference between relatives and controls was most prominent in TMT‐B scores in comparison to alternative tests, such as The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) and Stroop. Although moderate deficits in TMT‐B have been reported among the relatives of individuals with schizophrenia, some studies have failed to find these associations (Yurgelun‐Todd and Kinney 1993; Zalla et al. 2004). Furthermore, research findings have varied depending on the specific relatives included in the analyses. For example, in the study of Ma et al. (2007), the siblings performed significantly worse than controls in TMT‐B, but the parents did not.

As cognitive deficits, measured on different tests, are widely reported among the relatives of individuals with schizophrenia, studies have debated whether these impairments are influenced by genetic or environmental, including family environment related, factors (Aukes et al. 2008; Bigdeli et al. 2020; Greenwood et al. 2007; Quiñones et al. 2009; Sitskoorn et al. 2004). While previous studies have implicated genetic factors as the primary drivers of cognitive deficits among relatives, studies have seldom been able to control the influence of the rearing environment in impaired cognitive functions. Nevertheless, there is also a growing understanding that environmental factors, such as the rearing environment, and gene–environment interactions may contribute to the cognitive deficits (Roisko et al. 2015; Tuulio‐Henriksson et al. 2002). For example, Quiñones et al. (2009) argued, based on their findings, that the comparatively low heritability estimates observed for TMT performance imply a more prominent role of environmental, rather than genetic, factors in influencing an individuals' performance in the task. Similarly, Zalla et al. (2004) have postulated that TMT results are more likely to be driven by environmental, rather than genetic factors.

In order to investigate the impacts of genetic and environmental factors to the TMT‐B scores, there is a need for research within settings where the rearing environment is comparable between study subjects. The current study utilizes nationwide data from the Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia, consisting of adoptees with and without genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003). The high‐risk adoptees had a biological mother who was diagnosed with schizophrenia or a schizophrenia spectrum disorder. The data available offers an opportunity to investigate the influence of inherited genetic factors on TMT‐B scores in cases where children with a high genetic risk are not being raised by their biological parents.

The objectives of the current study are as follows: 1) to examine whether there are differences in TMT‐B performance scores between the adoptees with high and low genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders; 2) To analyse whether the percentile distribution of adoptees' TMT‐B completion scores differs from that of the normative Finnish population; 3) To analyse the predicitive ability of the TMT‐B completion time to discriminate the adoptees both by their psychiatric disorder status and their genetic risk status.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

The present study utilizes data from the Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia, originally designed to investigate the roles of genetics, rearing environment, and their interaction on adoptees' mental health and cognitive functioning. The research design originated as the hospital records of all adult female patients (n = 19,447) admitted to Finnish psychiatric hospitals between the years 1960 and 1979 were examined. The primary objective was to identify women who had received a diagnosis of schizophrenia or paranoid psychosis, as documented in their medical records (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003). Women diagnosed with other psychiatric conditions, such as bipolar disorder or reactive psychosis, were excluded from further investigation. Subsequently, a thorough analysis of national census and parish registers was conducted to identify which of these women had placed a child or children for adoption. In this study, these women are referred to as biological index mothers and their child (ren) as index adoptees. Children who were adopted by relatives, adopted internationally, or adopted after the age of 4 years were excluded from further scrutiny (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003).

After applying the exclusion criteria, 170 biological index mothers with 186 index children adopted by 185 adoptive families were identified (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003). These index adoptees and their adoptive families were then paired with control adoptees and their adoptive parents. Records from a national adoption agency were examined to find a suitable control for each index adoptee and their adoptive family. The demographic matching criteria encompassed factors such as the sex and age of the adoptee, the adoptee's age at the time of placement, and the socioeconomic status of the adoptive family (Tienari et al. 2000, 2004). To be eligible for the study, the biological control mothers needed to have no record of hospitalization for a psychotic disorder. Of the control biological mothers, 41 mothers had a psychiatric diagnosis other than a broad schizophrenia spectrum disorder and 151 had no psychiatric disorder. Initially, 206 control adoptees and families were identified. This number was later reduced to 203 because of insufficient data on three biological mothers (Tienari et al. 2000, 2004).

At the initial phase, the hospital diagnoses of biological mothers were based on ICD‐8 or ICD‐9 diagnostic criteria (World Health Assembly 1966, 1978). In later phases of the study, the research diagnoses of biological mothers were defined following the DSM‐III‐R (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3rd Edition, Revised) diagnostic criteria (American Psychiatric Association 1987). Extensive measures were taken, including reviewing hospital and clinic records, conducting research interviews with available biological index and control mothers (as well as adoptees), and cross‐checking relevant sources, such as hospital discharge records and health insurance files, to confirm psychiatric diagnoses of the study participants. In addition to the interviews conducted and other measures, the final evaluation of biological mothers was based on public and private hospital discharge records in 2003 (Tienari et al. 2003). Following the diagnostic reassessment, three index mothers were moved to the control group due to having a nonschizophrenia spectrum diagnosis, 14 control mothers were reclassified as index as they were found to have a research diagnosis of a schizophrenia spectrum disorder, and seven index adoptees were excluded due to diagnostic uncertainties. Ultimately, the final study population comprised 190 HR adoptees and 192 LR adoptees (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003, 2004).

Based on Kendler et al.' (1996) recommendations, the DSM‐III‐R (American Psychiatric Association 1987) diagnoses of biological mothers were classified into two categories: (1) broad schizophrenia spectrum disorders, encompassing diagnoses of schizophrenia, odd‐cluster personality disorders (schizotypal, schizoid, and paranoid personality disorders, plus avoidant personality disorder), nonschizophrenic nonaffective psychoses (schizoaffective, schizophreniform, delusional disorders, and psychotic disorder not otherwise specified), and affective psychoses (bipolar and depressive disorders with psychotic features); and (2) psychiatric disorders other than broad schizophrenia spectrum disorder, including alcohol abuse, eating disorders, anxiety disorders, major depressive disorder, dysthymic disorder, depressive disorder not otherwise specified, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, somatoform disorder, antisocial personality disorder, borderline personality disorder, narcissistic personality disorder, histrionic personality disorder, dependent personality disorder, and personality disorder not otherwise specified (Tienari et al. 2003).

Adoptees whose biological mothers were diagnosed with broad schizophrenia spectrum disorders were considered high‐risk (HR) for broad schizophrenia spectrum disorders. Adoptees whose biological mothers had nonschizophrenia‐spectrum diagnoses, or no history of psychiatric diagnoses were considered low‐risk (LR) for broad schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Tienari et al. 2003). The biological mothers of control adoptees were initially unscreened diagnostically, assuming that they represented a typical range of psychiatric disorders found in the general population (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003, 2004).

Adoptees, their adoptive parents, and families were assessed in their homes through a comprehensive and standardized evaluation protocol, typically spanning 2 days. The assessment battery comprised structured observations, semistructured interviews, diagnostic personality tests, intelligence tests, and spouse and family interaction tests. All procedures were administered by trained psychiatrists who were blinded to the adoptees' genetic risk status. Interviews and test sessions were tape‐recorded, and the use of standardized tests was intended to yield objective, quantifiable data to complement observational and interview findings. Evaluations were conducted at the individual, parental‐couple, and whole‐family levels. Approximately 6.8% of families declined participation (Tienari et al. 2000, 2004).

The study design of the Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia was approved by the Ethics Committee of Oulu University Hospital to ensure that the study design followed ethical practices. All Finnish authorities, from which the register data were obtained, have given official permissions for use of their data. In other words, they have authorized access and usage for a specific purpose within legal and ethical frameworks. The data given for researchers for their study purposes were anonymized so that no individual or family can be identified from the data. Only a few key persons (i.e., leader of the project and database administrators) had access to information, which could be used for identification of a study participant. This personal information was stored securely, both physically and digitally, with measures like strong passwords and restricted access to secure locations (locked rooms).

Trail Making Test

Trail Making Test is a widely used and easily administered neuropsychological test used to measure visual scanning, visuomotor tracking, divided attention, and cognitive flexibility (Lezak et al. 2012). TMT was originally developed in 1938 as “Partington's Pathways” (Partington and Leiter 1949) and was designed as part of the Army Individual Test Battery 1944. With further development by Reitan (1958), it gained acceptance in civilian clinical neuropsychological practice as an indicator of organic brain damage. It was also included in many other neuropsychological test batteries (collection of tests). The best known is the Halstead–Reitan test Battery (Reitan and Wolfson 1985). Nowadays, it is generally accepted as being applicable to other patient groups, in addition to those with brain injuries.

The Trail Making Test has two parts, A (TMT‐A) and B (TMT‐B). In TMT‐A the subject must draw lines to connect consecutively numbered, 25 circles on one worksheet. In TMT‐B the subject has to connect a series of 25 encircled numbers (1–13) and letters (A–L), which are pseudo‐randomly arranged on a page in ascending order, alternating between number and letter (e.g., 1‐A‐2‐B), as quickly as possible. In the test procedure, the examiner identifies and alerts the examinee to any errors made and instructs the examinee to make corrections. The timing of the test continues throughout the error identification and correction process. The TMT‐B is then scored by measuring the time it takes for an individual to connect characters in a sequential pattern (Chan et al. 2008; Gaudino et al. 1995; Lezak et al. 2012; Mahurin et al. 2006).

Both TMT‐A and TMT‐B evaluate scanning, visuomotor tracking and motor speed, whereas TMT‐B also measures divided attention and cognitive flexibility. TMT‐B is frequently employed as a measure of executive functioning as it requires from participants mental flexibility, involving the alternating connection of both numbers and letters. TMT‐B demands cognitive abilities such as flexibly shift attention and exhibit task‐switching abilities (Gaudino et al. 1995; Kortte et al. 2002; Lezak et al. 2012).

In this study, adoptees were exclusively assessed using TMT‐B, leaving out the administration of TMT‐A due to its less demanding cognitive requirements (Kortte et al. 2002). TMT‐B, known for its heightened demand on cognitive processing, has been demonstrated to be efficient in distinguishing individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia from healthy controls (Quiñones et al. 2009). Moreover, TMT‐B has been shown to yield distinguishable differences between first‐degree relatives of individuals with schizophrenia and control groups, when compared to other cognitive assessments such as the WCST and the Stroop test (Sitskoorn et al. 2004; Snitz et al. 2006; Thompson et al. 2005). In addition, compared to, for example, WCST, the Stroop test, Intra‐Extra Dimensional Set Shift (IED; CANTAB) and the Tower of London, TMT‐B offered a clear advantage in terms of administration efficiency, as it could be conducted and scored relatively quickly during home visits in adoptive families. Therefore, the use of TMT‐B was a well‐justified choice for this study, both by its strong empirical association with schizophrenia‐related cognitive deficits and by its methodological advantages as a brief yet sensitive measure of cognitive functioning and impairment.

The TMT‐B tests were conducted during the follow‐up phase of the study. Of the initial study population of 382 adoptees, a total of 131 (34.3%) adoptees completed the TMT‐B (HR = 69 [36.3%]; LR = 62 (32.3%), p = 0.407). TMT‐B was conducted under the supervision of experienced psychiatrists, who were trained in the method according to Reitan's procedure. A short introduction sample, consisting of 8 items, was completed by the subjects prior to the testing session.

Normative Population Matched Values on Trail Making Test

In this study, adoptees' completion times for the TMT‐B were analyzed using both raw TMT‐B scores as well as TMT‐B scores, standardized into percentiles based on the normative population values (Poutiainen et al. 2010). Earlier studies have considered using normative values useful, because it enables the establishment of representative baseline values and compares performance across different demographic and cultural groups (Fernández and Marcopulos 2008; Periáñez et al. 2007; Tombaugh 2004).

Psychiatric Disorders of the Study Subjects

In the current study, the adoptees' psychiatric status was taken into consideration in analyses, because psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia (Laere et al. 2018; Periáñez et al. 2007; Quiñones et al. 2009; Sánchez‐Morla et al. 2009; Yurgelun‐Todd and Kinney 1993) and major depressive disorder (Mahurin et al. 2006), have been documented as associating with impaired cognitive performance in TMT. This also provides an opportunity to explore the potential influence of both the adoptees' genetic risk status and their psychiatric status on TMT‐B scores (Snitz et al. 2006; Thompson et al. 2005).

The adoptees' psychiatric disorders were evaluated with personal interviews, conducted using semistructured methods, and the utilization of various data sources, such as hospital records, national registers, and other diagnostically relevant sources (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003). A total of 346 adoptees participated in personal interviews, either initially, during follow‐up, or at both study phases. Of the high‐risk (HR) adoptees, 89.1% (n = 170) and of the low‐risk (LR) adoptees, 91.5% (n = 176) participated in the interviews. The interviews were conducted by psychiatrists who were unaware of the adoptees' genetic status (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003, 2004). Interrater reliability tests for the diagnostic evaluations were conducted separately for the initial and follow‐up diagnoses, each time using 40 randomly selected cases. The kappa coefficient values for these assessments were 0.80 for the initial diagnoses and 0.71 for the follow‐up diagnoses (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003). Furthermore, following the follow‐up phase of the study, the adoptees' diagnostic status was reassessed using hospital discharge records that encompassed data until the end of 2006.

The diagnostic criteria used for the adoptees were based on the DSM‐III‐R (American Psychiatric Association 1987) criteria. These criteria were applied by considering all available sources, including hospital records, personal interviews, and other relevant diagnostic sources. The DSM‐III‐R (American Psychiatric Association 1987) diagnoses were assigned as the “best‐estimate” diagnosis, representing the most severe lifetime diagnosis based on a hierarchical order. The hierarchical order of diagnoses followed the following sequence: 1) schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder; 2) cluster A personality disorders; 3) schizophreniform disorder, delusional disorder, and psychosis not otherwise specified; 4) affective psychoses; 5) cluster B personality disorders; 6) nonpsychotic affective disorders; 7) cluster C personality disorders, alcohol disorders, and other psychiatric disorders; 8) absence of any psychiatric disorder.

Age of the Adoptees

The adoptive children's age at time of the follow‐up phase of the study was classified into the following three categories: age‐groups 15–39 years, 40–50 years, and 51–62 years. Age groups were selected based on those of normative values available for Finnish population (Poutiainen et al. 2010). The age at time of the TMT‐B assessment was controlled, because ageing is reported to associate with a decline in cognitive performance, such as in tasks requiring processing speed and divided attention (Murman 2015). Similar cognitive deficits associated with ageing have been documented both in patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls (Fucetola et al. 2000). Age‐related declines have also been reported in TMT performance in healthy participants (Periáñez et al. 2007).

Statistical Methods

Our study applied a conventional (traditional) statistical approach, which refers to the methods of analysing data that rely on mathematical theory and probability distributions. The choice whether use parametric or nonparametric tests in analyses was carefully considered, since parametric tests assume that the data follows a specific distribution, often a normal distribution, while nonparametric tests do not make such assumptions. Thus, statistical significance of group difference in categorical variables was assessed with the Pearson's chi‐square or Fisher's exact test, and in continuous variables with Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskall–Wallis test. The first phase prior statistical analyses of the TMT‐B completion time were that the TMT‐B completion time of each adoptee was transformed to percentiles, following the cutoffs of the normative age‐stratified Finnish population (Poutiainen et al. 2010). Then, the observed percentile distribution of the TMT‐B completion time of the adoptees was compared to expected quintile distribution of the normative Finnish population using the ordinary chi‐square test for multinomials (Wonnacott and Wonnacott 1990). The null hypothesis was that the observed distribution of the TMT‐B completion time is evenly distributed over the five quintiles (each quintile represents 20% of the values). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used as a global measure of ability, whether the variable under analysis (i.e., TMT‐B completion time) has the ability to discriminate the HR‐ and LR ‐adoptees and the adoptees with and without psychiatric disorders. A higher AUC value indicates better discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.5 representing random performance and an AUC of 1.0 representing perfect separation (Nahm 2022).

Discussion

In order to examine the influences of genetic and environmental factors on cognitive perfomance, the present study utilized nationwide data from the Finnish Adoptive Family Study of Schizophrenia (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003, 2004), comprising adoptees with high (HR) and low (LR) genetic predisposition for schizophrenia spectrum disorders. The data available provides an opportunity to examine the impact of inherited genetic risk, as the adoptees were raised in comparable adoptive family environments and not by their biological parents. This enables the separation of genetic factors from the rearing environment. The study examined the impacts of genetic risk status and diagnosed psychiatric disorders on the TMT‐B performance among HR and LR adoptees.

The current study showed no statistically significant differences in completion times for TMT‐B between HR and LR adoptees. Despite the HR adoptees performing slightly slower than the LR adoptees in the test, these differences were not found to be significant. Therefore, TMT‐B was not able to differentiate between HR and LR adoptees. This result contradicts earlier studies reporting small to moderately poorer TMT‐B performance in unaffected first‐degree relatives of individuals with schizophrenia compared to controls (Egan et al. 2001; Gochman et al. 2004; Kuha et al. 2007; Szöke et al. 2005; Thompson et al. 2005). However, this result is in line with some earlier studies that found no differences in TMT‐B performance between the first‐degree relatives and healthy controls (Yurgelun‐Todd and Kinney 1993; Zalla et al. 2004).

There are plausible explanations for this result. Firstly, in comparison to earlier studies, the high‐risk relatives (HR adoptees) in our study did not live with their biological parents/families, and this may have had an influence on their cognitive development. Several earlier studies have postulated that TMT performance may be influenced by both environmental and genetic factors along with their interaction (Quiñones et al. 2009; Tuulio‐Henriksson et al. 2002; Zalla et al. 2004). The study of Quiñones et al. (2009) reported low estimates of heritability for TMT performance, which indicates a prominent role for environmental factors in the development of the cognitive functions required in the study. Our result seems to be in line with this study because, despite the HR adoptees performing slightly (yet insignificantly) more slowly in the TMT‐B, their scores were, nonetheless, comparable to the LR adoptees. Consequently, it is reasonable to propose that our findings may have been impacted by the adoptive family environment, considering that the HR adoptees were not raised in an environment influenced by the genetic characteristics of their biological parents, but rather by their adoptive parents.

Secondly, in our study the LR adoptees represented a control group with a considerable number of psychiatric disorders, representing a typical range of psychiatric disorders found in the general population. It has been argued that the selection of a control group may have impacted many earlier studies examining the cognitive functioning in relatives of probands with schizophrenia (Egan et al. 2001). Several studies have examined the potential biases associated with employing a “supernormal” control group, where a more stringent exclusion criteria for psychopathology has been implemented compared to that used for relatives (Snitz et al. 2006; Thompson et al. 2005). As Thompson et al. (2005) have argued, this may have caused an overestimation of the cognitive deficits between the relatives and controls.

Accordingly, we examined whether the adoptees' psychiatric status influenced their performance scores in the TMT‐B, and whether these potential impacts of psychiatric morbidity differed between the HR and LR adoptees. Our results indicated that significant differences in completion times in the TMT‐B were observed between the psychiatric disorder groups, both in the total sample of adoptees and in the LR adoptees. Furthermore, the TMT‐B completion times of HR and LR adoptees showed no significant differences in median times between the two groups within each psychiatric disorder category. Overall, these findings suggest that adoptees with psychiatric disorders, regardless of their genetic risk, exhibit deficiencies in TMT‐B performance, compared to those with no psychiatric morbidity. In addition, no significant differences were observed in TMT‐B performance between HR and LR adoptees within the specific psychiatric disorder categories (not reported). It is likely that different forms of psychopathology may potentially hinder TMT‐B performance, leading to an increased response time or a higher frequency of errors (Mahurin et al. 2006).

Among the individuals with a schizophrenia spectrum disorder in particular, descriptive statistics showed slower performance times in the test. This is in line with earlier studies that have reported cognitive deficits being a prominent feature of schizophrenia (Karrer et al. 2019; Kępińska et al. 2020; Laere et al. 2018; Mosiołek et al. 2016; Thompson et al. 2005) and schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Habtewold et al. 2020; Sánchez‐Torres et al. 2013). This may be attributed, for example, to reduced processing speed, as assessed by the TMT, which can impede an individual's capacity to adequately process social interactions and restrain their behavioural responses (MacDonald 2017). However, as this subgroup of adoptees with a schizophrenia spectrum diagnosis included only nine individuals, the finding should be interpreted with caution.

Although the schizophrenia spectrum disorders were more common among the HR adoptees, our results indicate that distinctively deficient cognitive functioning may have a stronger link to the disease process itself, rather than to the genetic liability for schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders (Egan et al. 2001; Laurent et al. 2001). Consequently, although our findings do not support the hypothesis that TMT‐B performance may serve as an endophenotype of schizophrenia (impaired TMT‐B performance is more prevalent in nonpsychotic, first‐degree relatives of patients than in controls; Aydın et al. 2017; Gur et al. 2007), it remains plausible that other forms of cognitive deficits may exist in the HR adoptees.

Furthermore, our results showed that, based on the distribution of TMT‐B completion times, matched to the population, statistically significant skewness towards longer completion times was observed in the groups of all adoptees and LR adoptees. This may indicate that adopted children, regardless of their genetic risk status, represent a special demographic group in relation to their cognitive functioning. In addition, adoptees with any psychiatric disorder, irrespective of genetic risk, exhibited significantly slower completion times compared to the general population. This further indicates that psychiatric disorders associate with cognitive deficits.

One of the key strengths of the current study is the adoption study design, which facilitated the examination of adoptees with and without genetic susceptibility to schizophrenia spectrum disorders. These adoptees were raised by adoptive parents who did not share genetic relatedness, thereby enabling the separation of shared environmental and genetic correlations. In addition, the study benefited from the utilization of reliable diagnostics and a diverse range of methodological procedures, conducted by trained researchers, which further strengthens the validity and robustness of the study.

The present study is also subject to several limitations. Firstly, this study focused exclusively on performance in the TMT‐B among the study subjects, precluding the possibility of combining its completion time with TMT‐A scores. Calculating the difference (B‐A) or ratio (B/A) between the two tests would ignore the impact of psychomotor speed and primarily reflect cognitive flexibility (Kuha et al. 2007; Periáñez et al. 2007; Quiñones et al. 2009). However, it is important to note that impaired psychomotor speed/processing speed has been consistently observed in studies involving individuals at ultra‐high risk for psychosis (Kelleher et al. 2013) and relatives of individuals with schizophrenia (Agnew‐Blais and Seidman 2013; Keshavan et al. 2010). Moreover, the analysis of error rates among the study participants could have provided insights into potential disparities, such as higher error frequencies exhibited by HR adoptees as compared to LR adoptees (Mahurin et al. 2006).

Secondly, the cross‐sectional nature of the TMT‐B data, collected during the follow‐up phase, limits our ability to investigate whether diagnosed psychiatric disorders are associated with an increased risk of slower performance on the test, or whether impaired cognitive functioning is a risk factor for developing psychiatric disorders. Consequently, the temporal relationship between the onset of diagnosed psychiatric disorders and the administration of the TMT‐B test remains uncertain, although it has been argued that individuals with significant cognitive deficits may be more prone to the development of psychotic disorders (Baker and Morrison 1998; Niemi et al. 2003). It remains unclear whether the diagnosed psychiatric disorders manifested prior to the administration of the TMT‐B test, or in which phase of the disease progression the diagnosed adoptees were at the time of their testing. However, this limitation applies equally to both the HR and LR adoptees and should not affect the comparison of TMT‐B performance between the two groups.

Thirdly, the TMT scoring method (Reitan 1958) utilized in this study has inherent limitations. In this procedure, the examiner identifies errors as they occur, which means that the measured time encompasses the examiner's reaction time in detecting errors, pointing them out, and the examinee's speed in comprehending and rectifying them (Lezak et al. 2012). Although this may introduce a potential bias, this method represents the prevailing approach for administering and scoring the TMT in contemporary practice, including the Finnish manual of the test.

Fourthly, the sample size utilized for statistical analyses was relatively small, but mostly in accordance to the guidelines of Mitrushina et al. (2005), who have recommended sample sizes of at least 50 subjects per group for TMT studies. In several subgroup analyses conducted in our study, the sample sizes fell below these recommended guidelines. Fifthly, our study did not assess the potential influence of levels of education on TMT performance. Therefore, we cannot ascertain whether lower performance in certain cases resulted, for example, from insufficient familiarity with the alphabet, rather than attention deficits (Mahurin et al. 2006). Sixthly, as the study protocol did not include measures of brain structure or function, and, thus, analyses addressing potential neurofunctional correlates of TMT‐B performance could not be completed. Finally, the HR adoptees included in our study were at an elevated risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders due to their biological mother. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether the findings would have differed if the HR adoptees had been specifically at risk for schizophrenia alone. However, it is important to note that biological high‐risk mothers were initially included in the study, based on a diagnosis of schizophrenia or paranoid psychosis (Tienari et al. 2000, 2003), indicating a predisposition towards the more severe end of the schizophrenia spectrum.

Conclusions

In conclusion, despite previous evidence suggesting TMT‐B as a differentiating measure, our findings did not reveal statistically significant differences in TMT‐B completion times between HR and LR adoptees. Although this contrasts with some previous studies, it aligns with others that have reported similar TMT‐B performance in first‐degree relatives of individuals with schizophrenia and controls. However, significant differences in TMT‐B completion times were observed among adoptees with psychiatric disorders, indicating that psychiatric morbidity associates with TMT‐B performance, regardless of genetic risk. Future studies are recommended to consider the potential influences of specific psychiatric disorders on TMT‐B performance. Future research should explore alternative cognitive measures and tests, as well as the inclusion of environmental variables, such as family environment, to further elucidate the cognitive profiles of individuals at genetic risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders and their potential association with psychiatric outcomes.
