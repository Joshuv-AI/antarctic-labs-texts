Shared decision-making and medication adherence among community adults with chronic diseases: a cross-sectional study in Hubei Province, China
Authors: Da Feng, Zhe Jin, Jian Zou, Zhongxin Dong, Qiao Zong, Yangfan Sun, Chengxu Long, Zhanchun Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShared decision-making has been shown to improve self-management of chronic diseases, however whether it improves the medication adherence of chronic disease patients and the magnitude of the effect remains unclear. Therefore, our objective was to evaluate whether shared decision-making is associated with better adherence and to estimate the magnitude of this association.MethodsWe conducted a community-based cross-sectional questionnaire survey in four areas of Hubei, China (April–June 2021) using cluster sampling. Medication decision-making was assessed with the Control Preferences Scale–Post, and medication adherence with the 4-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-4). We estimated associations using propensity-score weighting and logistic regression. We investigated three types of medication decision-making models: paternalistic model, shared decision-making model, and self-decision-making model. The predictor of interest in this study is the models of medication decision-making, and the dependent variable was patients’ medication adherence. We applied the propensity score weighting approach to adjust for observed differences in characteristics between those with different types of medication decisions, and explored the association between medication decision-making and medication adherence through logistic regression.ResultsPatients with self-decision-making were less likely to be adherent to medication (OR = 0.33, 95%CI:0.27–0.41) compared to those with shared decision-making, and the corresponding marginal effect was − 0.278 (OR = 0.331, 95%CI: -0.292,-0.203). There was no significant difference in medication adherence between patients with shared decision-making and patients with a paternalistic model. Patients over 80 years old (OR = 2.19, 95%CI: 1.44–3.39), with two or more diseases (OR = 1.24, CI: 1.03–1.49), with high medication knowledge (OR = 1.32, 95%CI: 1.09–1.63), who have not experienced adverse drug reactions (OR = 1.89, 95%CI:1.53–2.33), or often visit non-primary medical institutions (OR = 1.93, 95%CI: 1.58–2.34) were more likely to be adherent to medication. While patients in bad health status (OR = 0.73, 95%CI: 0.61–0.87), who smoke (OR = 0.77, 95%CI: 0.60–0.99), seldom do exercise (OR = 0.83, 95%CI: 0.70–0.98), taking five or more medicines (OR = 0.63, 95%CI:0.51–0.78), have difficulty in taking medication (OR = 0.65, 95%CI: 0.48–0.86), seldom communicate with doctors (OR = 0.79, 95%CI: 0.64–0.95), and lack trust in doctors (OR = 0.79, 95%CI: 0.66–0.95) were less likely to be adherent to medication.ConclusionShared decision-making may be included in intervention measures designed to reduce non-adherence in patients with hypertension or diabetes in China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03646-3.Keywords: Shared decision-making, Medication adherence, Chronic diseases

Introduction

Chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) have always been a global threat to public health, which are responsible for approximately 71% of the deaths worldwide [1]. A study in 2019 has shown that nearly 20% and 10% of global deaths are caused by hypertension and diabetes, respectively, and that the top five risks for attributable deaths worldwide were both related to hypertension and diabetes [2]. in China, the prevalence of ncds has been increasing in recent years, [3, 4] with the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes estimated at approximately 31.6% and 13.7% [5]. according to the China Health Statistics Yearbook 2023, the latest available data (from 2018) show that the prevalence of chronic diseases among individuals aged 65 years and older is 62.33%. as population aging intensifies, NCDs will pose more challenges to public health.

A meta-analysis of 66 Asian studies estimated that 48% of hypertensive patients were non-adherent to BP-lowering therapy, and among older adults with hypertension in China, the pooled “high adherence” was only 43% [6, 7]. For type 2 diabetes, a recent meta-analysis found 55.5% adherence to oral agents globally, while a Chinese hospital study reported 63.1% adherent patients, underscoring sizable gaps in medication taking across China [8, 9].

Medication adherence among patients with chronic diseases was defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the extent to which a person’s behavior with respect to taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes, corresponds with agreed recommendations from a healthcare provider [10]. Considered an essential component of managing the health of NCDs patients [1], medication adherence is essential for the effective management of chronic diseases [11]. In addition, non-adherence to medication has been proven to be associated with significant adverse events, such as poor treatment, progressive illness, and decreased quality of life, which will bring additional disease economic burdens and even deaths [12].

The reasons for medication adherence can be multifactorial. It was reported that age, information, perception, duration of disease, complexity of dosing regimen, polytherapy, psychological factors, safety, tolerability, and cost all affect medication adherence [13]. In addition to these individual and environmental factors, patients’ engagement in clinical decision-making also has a bearing on medication adherence. Some studies have shown that doctor-patient interactions could improve patient uptake and adherence to antidepressants [14]. What’s more, involvement in treatment decisions and time spent with physicians were considered to be the predictive factors associated with a high ability to self-manage chronic diseases [15]. In most treatment processes, doctors play the role of decision-maker, while patients rarely participate in the decision-making process, which might lead to lower patient satisfaction and even affect the effectiveness of treatment [16].

Many studies have discussed factors that improve medication adherence in patients with NCDs; however, few studies have measured factors influencing patient medication adherence from the perspective of patient-physician communication. Whether patients’ medication adherence can be improved through shared decision-making (SDM) with prescribers and the prevalence of the effect remains unanswered. Our study aims to discuss the relationship between medication decision-making models and medication adherence in patients with NCDs and to quantify its effects.

Methods

Study participants and procedure

This community-based cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted between April and June 2021 in four areas of Hubei Province, China, namely Qianjiang, Zhijiang, Wuhan, and Yichang. Participants were recruited from both rural and urban communities using cluster sampling. A multicenter randomized controlled trial in adults with poorly controlled asthma reported higher one-year controller medication refill adherence with shared decision making [17]. The adherence rate was 67% in the shared decision-making group, compared with 46% in the usual-care group and 59% in the clinician-directed group. These contrasts correspond to absolute increases of approximately twenty-one and eight% points in adherence rates when shared decision-making was used compared to paternalistic or self-decision-making models. On this basis, we prespecified a conservative ten-percentage-point improvement for sample-size planning [17]. In addition, recent reviews conclude that SDM can improve medication adherence in chronic conditions, with heterogeneous effect sizes across settings—consistent with using 10% points as a conservative a priori effect size [18]. Using a two-group comparison with a two-sided alpha of 5% and 80% power, the total sample under simple random sampling is about 714. With a conservative design effect of 1.5 for cluster sampling, this is about 1,071, and allowing 10% nonresponse yields a planned total of 1,190.

We first randomly selected 3 communities in each city (a total of 12) and then collected 100 patients in each community. Each patient needs to fill in the informed consent form to participate in the survey before being investigated to make sure that they participate in this study voluntarily. A total of 1205 questionnaires were collected, of which 10 samples were removed due to a lack of information, and finally, 1195 valid samples were obtained. Data were collected through structured, interviewer-administered questionnaires conducted by trained investigators in community health service centers. The questionnaire collected sociodemographic information, clinical and treatment characteristics, decision-making role (CPSpost), medication adherence (MMAS-4), medication knowledge, and depressive symptoms (CES-D10).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were recruited using a cluster sampling method, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients who meet at least one of the clinical diagnostic criteria for hypertension or diabetes; (2) Long-term medication patients who have been taking medication for 3 months or more due to chronic diseases; (3) Adults over the age of 18; (4) Patients who are willing to cooperate with this study after reading the informed consents; (5) Patients with clear consciousness and the ability to communicate with others. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients are severely ill and unable to complete the questionnaire; (2) Patients cannot express their wishes correctly; (3) Patients have a history of mental illness; (4) Patients are unwilling to participate in our survey; (5) Patients have acute complications.

Ethics

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. 2020[S223]).

Measure

The predictor of interest in this study was the models of medication decision-making. We used the Control Preferences Scale-Post (CPSpost) to evaluate doctors and patients’ actual control over medical decisions [19, 20], with five statements describing the perceived role of the patient in the physician-patient encounter: “1. I made my decision alone”, “2. I made my decision alone considering what my doctor said”, “3. I shared the decision with the prescriber”, “4. The prescriber decision considering my preferences”, and “5. The prescriber made the decision”. According to the choices of patients, the types of decision-making were divided as self (option “1” or “2”), shared decision (option“3”), and Paternalistic (option “4” or “5”) [21]. Good validity and reliability evidence has been reported for the CPSpost [20]. Participants were instructed to answer CPSpost with reference to decisions about their current long-term medication plan for chronic disease management (i.e., the regimen they were currently taking).

The dependent variable was patients’ medication adherence, which was examined using the 4-item Morisky medication adherence scale (MMAS-4) [22]. Patients were asked to answer the following 4 questions based on their real behavior in the last three months: (1) ‘‘Do you ever forget to take your medicine?”; (2) ‘‘Are you careless at times about taking your medicine?”; (3) ‘‘When you feel better, do you sometimes stop taking your medicine?”; (4) ‘‘Sometimes when you feel worse when you take the medicine, do you stop taking it?” [23]. Responses of ‘‘no’’ were coded as 1, while responses of ‘‘yes’’ were coded as 0. The total score ranges from 0 to 4, which is commonly used scores 0–3 reflecting “non-adherence” and 4 reflecting “adherence” [24]. The MMAS-4 was widely used to measure medication adherence among patients with chronic diseases and has established reliability and validity, including Chinese populations [25].

Confounding variables included patients’ demographic characteristics, clinical conditions, medical treatment behavior, health information, medication knowledge, and depression symptoms. Demographic characteristics include patients’ age, domicile, gender, education level, annual income, and medical insurance type. Clinical conditions include the number of chronic diseases, adverse drug reactions, self-perceived health status, number of medicines, and difficulty of taking medicines alone. Medical treatment behavior data include whether the patient has been in the hospital in the past year, the frequency of communication with doctors, whether they trust doctors, and the type of medication institution they always visit. Health information includes patients’ smoking status, drinking status, physical exercise status, and eating habits.

Depressive symptomatology was assessed according to the Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D10), which supports the identification of clinically important depression. The overall score ranges from 0 to 30, with a score ≥ 10 suggesting current depressive illness, [26, 27] we use the score of 10 as the cut-off point.

Patient knowledge about medication was evaluated through a questionnaire adapted from the study by McPherson et al. [28] Participants answered five items: (1) “Can you tell me the name of the medication you are taking?”, (2) “Can you tell me why you are taking this medication?”, (3) “Do you know how and when to take your medication?”, (4) “Can you tell me what side effects your medication may cause, and what to do if they occur?”, and (5) “Do you know what to do if you miss a dose of your medication?” Responses were scored according to predefined criteria, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 8 [29]. Based on the sample median, participants were categorized into two knowledge levels: low medication knowledge (< 5) and high medication knowledge (≥ 5). This dichotomization was used to distinguish participants with relatively insufficient versus sufficient practical knowledge required for day-to-day medication use. The full item wording, scoring criteria, and response distributions are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in this study. Patients were not invited to comment on the study design or the study findings, and no patients were invited to contribute to the writing or editing of this document.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 24.0 and R 4.1.1, and we used a two-tailed test with a p-value of < 0.05 as a cutoff for significance. Since all variables are categorical, we used the Chi-square test to perform univariate analysis, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to estimate their association with medication adherence. Odds ratios (OR), marginal effects, and their 95% confidence intervals were reported to indicate the relationship between variables. The propensity score weighting approach was applied to adjust for observed differences in characteristics between those with different types of medication decisions, it could better tease out the net influence of decision types on patients’ medication adherence. To fit the propensity score, we used the mnps function in the twang package based on Generalized Boosted Modelling (GBM) in R 4.1.1 [30]. While propensity-score weighting helps balance observed covariates across decision-making groups, it cannot address unmeasured confounding or biases inherent to self-reported scales.

Several other variables also showed a significant relationship with medication adherence. Patients above 80 years old (OR = 2.191, 95%CI: 1.444–3.378), patients with two or more diseases (OR = 1.236, CI: 1.028–1.488), patients who have not experienced adverse drug reactions (OR = 1.889, CI:1.533–2.330), patients with high medication knowledge (OR = 1.322, CI: 1.086–1.634), or patients who often visit non-primary medical institutions (OR = 1.925, CI: 1.583–2.344) were more likely medication adherent. While patients who smoke (OR = 0.767, CI: 0.596–0.987), patients who seldom do exercise (OR = 0.832, CI: 0.704–0.983), patients taking five or more drugs (OR = 0.627, CI: 0.507–0.775), patients who have difficulty taking medication (OR = 0.647, CI: 0.484–0.856), patients with bad health status (OR = 0.725, CI: 0.608–0.865), patients who seldom communicate with doctors (OR = 0.793, CI: 0.644–0.947), or patients who lack of trust in doctors (OR = 0.791, CI: 0.661–0.945) were less likely medication adherent.

Discussion

Using shared decision-making as the reference category, patients who reported making medication decisions alone had significantly lower adherence; the marginal effect indicates a 27.8%-point decrease in the probability of good adherence for self-decision-makers relative to SDM, holding other covariates at their mean levels. By contrast, patients in the paternalistic model did not differ significantly from SDM in the probability of good adherence.

Lower adherence among patients who decided alone may reflect a defensive stance consistent with psychological reactance theory described by Brehm in 1966 [34]. Notably, the MMAS-4 includes items that capture both unintentional non-adherence (e.g., forgetting or being careless) and intentional non-adherence (e.g., stopping medication when feeling better or worse). The association between decision-making style and adherence may therefore vary depending on which type of non-adherence predominates. Gérard Reach reported similar autonomy-driven refusal in 2009 when comparing diabetes medication use with seatbelt use [35]. Studies using the Social, Psychological, Usage, and Rational (SPUR) behavioral diagnostic survey also highlight reactance as a driver of non-adherence [36]. This perspective suggests that reactance, rather than information, may underline the gap and supports strengthening genuine shared decision-making to reduce defensive responses.

According to the results, the majority of patients’ medication decisions are made exclusively by their physicians, which belongs to paternalistic models, and this is consistent with the previous studies [37]. In this model, information transfer is one-way, and doctors only provide medical information about the chosen treatment to the patient and decide the medication alone [38]. Although the paternalistic model may make patients feel more assured about the medication, our study shows that it does not significantly increase the possibility of adherence. Several studies showed that patients’ participation in the decision-making process will reduce the decisional conflict, improve the health outcome, satisfaction, and quality of life, enhance self-management and self-efficacy in the long-term, and help them live acceptably with the illness [39–42]. Generally, compared to being entirely decided by doctors, patients’ involvement will not lead to non-adherence but contribute to other improvements in medication.

A few patients make the medication decision themselves, which we have described as “the self model”. The doctor provides only the information the patient needs about the various treatment options, and the deliberations are carried out by the patient alone. In this model, the information transfer is also one-way [38]. Our study found that a self-model of decision-making is more likely to lead to non-adherence. In addition, it has also been proven to bring risks such as adverse drug reactions, inappropriate drug use, and making a wrong diagnosis in previous studies [43, 44].

We encourage both doctors and patients to participate in medication decision-making, which will not only lead to an improvement in adherence. The SDM of chronic diseases can be divided into the following steps: establishing an ongoing partnership, exchanging information, deliberating on options, and deciding and acting on the decision [45]. The information transfer in this process is two-way, with the doctor providing all the medical information needed to make a decision, the patient providing information about their personal situation and the outcomes that are most important to them, and the doctor and patient reaching a joint decision after deliberating together [37].

However, the incidence of shared decision-making is not optimistic; only 20.55% of patients get their medication through SDM in this study, and paternalistic decision-making remained the primary means by which patients received their medication. Since research has shown that patients want to participate in SDM but that SDM provision is not ideal, [46] healthcare providers may need to review their models of provision. Sufficient information exchange can promote the symmetry of information between doctors and patients, which is an effective way to promote SDM. In addition, a long-term cooperative relationship helps to establish patient loyalty, improve patients’ trust in doctors, [47] and eliminate the distance of doctors in patients’ minds, [48] which also helps to improve patients’ adherence [49].

Practical implications extend beyond the mere introduction of shared decision-making tools and point to the need for their systematic integration into routine care. This includes implementing brief, evidence-based decision aids for commonly prescribed chronic disease medications, strengthening clinicians’ SDM communication skills through targeted training, and embedding SDM prompts, such as option grids and preference-elicitation checklists, into everyday clinical workflows to support value-concordant treatment discussions. At the same time, team-based care models involving pharmacists or nurses can provide sustained medication counseling, address concerns about adverse effects or regimen complexity, and reinforce agreed treatment plans over time. By distributing SDM-related tasks across the care team and clinical encounters, these approaches may enhance continuity, strengthen patient–provider trust, and contribute to more durable improvements in medication adherence.

The effects of other variables on medication adherence are roughly consistent with previous studies, which indicated that age, unhealthy habits, some factors related to disease, and medical treatment all showed a significant impact on medication adherence. Generally speaking, our study provides new ideas for improving the medication adherence of patients with NCDs. Compared to previous studies on medication adherence, we have controlled other confounders to tease out the net impact of SDM on adherence, and we divide decision-making into three specific forms rather than simply sharing decisions or not, so that the effects may seem clearer.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this community-based survey was conducted in four areas of Hubei Province, China; therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the findings to other regions and healthcare settings. Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow for the determination of the temporal ordering between decision-making style and medication adherence, and causal inference is not possible. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify directionality, and multi-regional validation studies would help strengthen external validity. Third, medication adherence was assessed using the 4-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale. This measure reflects self-reported medication-taking behaviors and mainly captures forgetfulness or carelessness as well as stopping medication when feeling better or worse, but it provides limited coverage of other key phases and dimensions of adherence, including treatment initiation and primary non-adherence, timing deviations, longer-term persistence and discontinuation, and refill-based adherence. In addition, self-reported measures are susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. The decision-making role was also self-reported and referred to participants’ current long-term medication plan, which limited our ability to distinguish between initial prescribing decisions and subsequent regimen adjustments. Moreover, the questionnaire was anchored to participants’ overall current long-term medication regimen rather than being restricted to a single condition or drug class; therefore, we could not link “self-decision-making” to a specific disease or to specific medication classes. Future studies should incorporate disease- or drug-class–specific anchoring items and capture decision timing and treatment duration to better disentangle these processes. Finally, although propensity-score weighting improves balance on observed covariates across decision-making groups, it cannot eliminate unmeasured confounding and does not correct self-report biases. There may also be some construct overlap between self-reported decision-making and adherence measures. Future studies should collect richer clinical and psychosocial determinants and incorporate more objective adherence metrics when feasible, such as pharmacy refill data or electronic monitoring.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study found that the medication adherence of patients with chronic diseases is related to the decision-making of their medication plans. Lack of doctors’ participation in decision-making is prone to non-adherence, but patients who share decisions are no more likely to be non-compliant than patients with paternalistic decisions. Combined with the improvement of patient experience and treatment effectiveness by shared decision-making, as proven in previous studies, we suggest that when formulating a chronic disease medication plan, both doctors and patients should be involved in decision-making.
