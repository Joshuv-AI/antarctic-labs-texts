Effect of genetic polymorphisms of aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 on the efficacy of intermittent parathyroid hormone treatment and bone mineral density: A retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Hinako Obara, Takafumi Tajima, Manabu Tsukamoto, Yoshiaki Yamanaka, Hitoshi Suzuki, Masato Nagashima, Satoshi Nishida, Satoshi Ikeda, Kazumichi Maekawa, Akinori Sakai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn a mouse model, aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) knockout resulted in lower bone mineral density; however, higher parathyroid hormone receptor expression than wild-type mice. This study aimed to investigate whether ALDH2 polymorphisms influence efficacy of intermittent parathyroid hormone therapy and bone mineral density changes in humans.MethodsEighty-two patients with primary osteoporosis treated with parathyroid hormone for > 1 year were divided into wild-type ALDH2 (ALDH2∗1) and variant (ALDH2∗2) groups. Bone mineral densities were measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Changes in bone mineral density, treatment response, bone turnover markers, and new fracture incidence were evaluated. Furthermore, bone mineral density was analyzed using a mixed-effects model.ResultsFemoral neck bone mineral density increased by 1.0 ± 7.4% in the ALDH2∗1 group and 4.3 ± 8.1% in the ALDH2∗2 group (P < 0.05), whereas lumbar spine bone mineral density increased by 5.7 ± 8.2% and 9.4 ± 9.1% without significance. Treatment success rates were higher in ALDH2∗2 group (femoral neck 38.7%, lumbar spine 68.8%) compared with ALDH2∗1 (16.3%, 51.0%). Statistical significance was observed only at the femoral neck. Bone turnover markers and fracture incidence were comparable between groups. Mixed-effects analysis adjusting for confounders showed a significant ALDH2 genotype × duration interaction for femoral neck, indicating genotype-related differences in the rate of bone mineral density increase over time. For lumbar spine, the genotype main effect was significant, whereas the interaction was not.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that ALDH2 polymorphisms may influence the therapeutic response to PTH treatment and highlight the need for larger future studies.Keywords: Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 polymorphism, Bone mineral density, Parathyroid hormone, Teriparatide

Introduction

Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) is the primary enzyme responsible for the breakdown of acetaldehyde produced by alcohol dehydrogenase during alcohol metabolism. ALDH2 converts acetaldehyde into nontoxic acetic acid. This enzyme is expressed in the mitochondrial matrix and plays a critical role in the oxidation and detoxification of aldehydes in various tissues and cells, particularly in the liver [1]. Additionally, ALDH2 is crucial for metabolic detoxification of 4-hydroxynonenal and malondialdehyde, both of which are aldehydes generated by lipid peroxidation in cells [2,3].

There are clear racial differences in the frequency of ALDH2 polymorphisms worldwide. Mutant ALDH2 is common among Mongoloid populations, including Japanese, Korean, and Chinese populations, whereas it is extremely rare among Caucasoid and Negroid populations. In Japan, 48.3% of the population carries the wild-type ALDH2 gene (ALDH2∗1/∗1 genotype), 43.6% has the heterozygous ALDH2 gene (ALDH2∗1/∗2 genotype), and 7.7% has the homozygous mutant ALDH2 gene (ALDH2∗2/∗2 genotype) [4]; regional differences have also been observed [5].

Regarding the relationship between polymorphisms in ALDH2 and disease frequency, the risks of esophageal cancer [6], Alzheimer disease [7], coronary artery disease [8], Parkinson disease [9], and lumbar disc herniation [10] are higher with the ALDH2∗2 genotype than with the other genotypes. Regarding bone metabolisms, individuals with the ALDH2∗2 genotype have a higher incidence of hip fractures and osteoporosis than those with the other genotypes [11].

In our laboratory, we investigated the relationship between ALDH2 polymorphisms and bone metabolism in Aldh2 knockout (KO) mice. In studies related to bone metabolism and mechanical stress, the increase in trabecular bone mass with exercise in wild-type (WT) mice was not observed in Aldh2 KO mice [12], and cortical bone loss due to skeletal unloading was more pronounced in Aldh2 KO mice than in WT mice [13]. We found that Aldh2 KO mice exhibited higher gene expression of parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptors in osteocytes and osteoblasts than WT mice. We administered PTH (teriparatide) to ovariectomized Aldh2 KO mice and observed that its effect on bone formation, particularly in the cortical bone, was more pronounced than that in WT mice [14]. As aforementioned, ALDH2 is a mitochondrial enzyme involved in the detoxification of aldehydes, and individuals carrying the ALDH2∗2 variant exhibit reduced enzymatic activity, which has been associated with increased oxidative stress [[15], [16], [17]]. Oxidative stress affects the signal responsiveness of bone cells and may influence the expression and sensitivity of parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptors [18]. Aldh2 KO mice are considered a model that reflects this altered metabolic state, and the enhanced PTH responsiveness observed in these mice suggests that a similar trend may also occur in humans.

In clinical practice, we have observed that responses to osteoporosis treatment vary; some patients experience increased bone mineral density (BMD), whereas others do not. However, the factors predicting treatment response remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effects of PTH treatment on the BMD in individuals with different ALDH2 polymorphisms. On the basis of our findings in Aldh2 KO mice, we hypothesized that individuals with the ALDH2∗2 genotype would experience a greater increase in BMD following PTH treatment than those with the ALDH2∗1 genotype, similar to the response observed in Aldh2 KO mice.

Methods

Study design and population

This study included men and postmenopausal women who were diagnosed with primary osteoporosis and had been treated with PTH (Forteo® 20 μg, daily teriparatide manufactured by Eli Lilly or Teribone® 56.5 μg, weekly teriparatide manufactured by Asahi Kasei Pharma) for > 1 year at the collaborating hospitals. Patients were categorized into three groups based on their genotype: ALDH2∗1/∗1, ALDH2∗1/∗2, and ALDH2∗2/∗2. However, the presence of even a single ALDH2∗2 allele results in a marked reduction in ALDH2 enzymatic activity [19]; therefore, many previous studies have classified participants into two groups based on the presence or absence of the ALDH2∗2 allele. Similarly, in our study, the ALDH2∗1/∗2 and ALDH2∗2/∗2 genotypes were combined into a single group, leaving two study groups, ALDH2∗1 and ALDH2∗2, for analysis.

Outcome measurements

We collected data on patient background characteristics, including height, weight, body mass index (BMI), history of osteoporosis treatment, grip strength, and previous osteoporotic vertebral fractures. Additionally, the following parameters were retrospectively obtained from patients’ medical records.

We adopted the concept of least significant change (LSC) to define treatment response to account for measurement error in BMD assessments. The LSC was calculated on the basis of the coefficient of variation (CV), which reflects measurement precision, using the following formula: LSC = 2.77 × CV.

As this was a multicenter retrospective study, it was not feasible to obtain site-specific CVs for dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry measurements. Therefore, in accordance with the precision assessment guidelines of the International Society for Clinical Densitometry, we adopted a representative assumed CV of 2%, resulting in an LSC of 5.54%.

Based on this threshold, the treatment response was classified as failure (< −5.5%), success (≥ +5.5%), or borderline (−5.5% to +5.5%) for FN and LS BMD.

ALDH2 genotyping

Saliva samples were collected, and total DNA was extracted using an Oragene DNA kit (DNA Genotek). Purified DNA was used for ALDH2 SNP genotyping via TaqMan polymerase chain reaction (PCR), which was performed using the QuantStudio® 5 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The SNP genotypes were confirmed on the basis of the amplification curves generated by the StepOne Plus™ Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems).

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation or percentage. Statistical methods were selected on the basis of the results of normality assessment using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables that satisfied the assumption of normality (height and BMI), the Student's t-test was applied; for variables that did not satisfy the assumption of normality (age, weight, and grip strength), the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. For the mean BMD and the percent increase in BMD, the same procedures (Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test) were used for analysis. Treatment success rates were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. For the values and trends in bone turnover markers over time, the Mann–Whitney U test, paired t-test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as appropriate. All of these statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0 for Mac; IBM Corp.), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Mixed-effects models were performed with JMP Pro 19 (SAS Institute Inc.), also with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Post-hoc power analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0; R Development Core Team).

Sample size

At the study design stage, we considered the distribution of ALDH2 genotypes in the Japanese population and anticipated that a two-group comparison (wild-type vs ALDH2∗2 allele carriers, including heterozygotes and homozygotes) might allow detection of significant differences. Assuming a between-group difference in BMD of 0.045 g/cm2 and a standard deviation of 0.08 g/cm2, the estimated effect size was Cohen's d ≈ 0.56, suggesting that 50 participants per group (total N = 100) would be sufficient. However, in practice, the final dataset comprised 49 participants in the ALDH2∗1 group and 33 participants in the ALDH2∗2 group, resulting in a total of 82 participants.

Ethics statements

This research was approved by the institutional review board of theauthors’ affiliated institutions and conducted in accordance with theDeclaration of Helsinki. All patients provided informed consent.

Results

Nineteen patients had the ALDH2∗1/∗2 genotype and 14 had the ALDH2∗2/∗2 genotype, with a mean age of 76.8 years, including three men and 30 women. Twelve months after the initiation of treatment, the percent increase in FN BMD in the ALDH2∗1 group was 1.0 ± 7.4%, whereas a higher increase of 4.3 ± 8.1% was observed in the ALDH2∗2 group. The percent increases between the two groups after 12 months of treatment were statistically significantly different. Similarly, the percent increase in LS BMD was greater in the ALDH2∗2 group than in the ALDH2∗1 group. After 12 months of treatment, the percent increases in LS BMD were 5.7 ± 8.2% and 9.4 ± 9.1% in the ALDH2∗1 and ALDH2∗2 groups, respectively. However, the difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, post-hoc power analysis revealed that the statistical power for comparing the mean FN BMD between the two groups ranged from 66% to 85.1%, whereas that for LS BMD ranged from 5% to 7%. For the percent increase in FN BMD, the statistical power ranged from 82% to 97%, while for LS BMD, it was 58%. Notably, bootstrap analysis of the P-value distribution indicated that only for the percent increase in FN BMD did the P-values, in some iterations, tend to cluster around 0.05 or 0.95.

Treatment response rate

With regard to the treatment response rate to PTH, the success rates in the FN and LS were higher in the ALDH2∗2 group than in the ALDH2∗1 group. In the FN, the success rates were 16.3% (N = 8) and 38.7% (N = 12) in the ALDH2∗1 and ALDH2∗2 groups, respectively. In the LS, the success rates were 51.0% (N = 25) and 68.8% (N = 22) in the ALDH2∗1 and ALDH2∗2 groups, respectively. A statistically significant difference was observed only in the FN. When the outcomes were further categorized into success, borderline, and failure, the distribution in the FN was 16.3% success, 71.4% borderline, and 12.2% failure in the ALDH2∗1 group, compared with 38.7% success, 48.4% borderline, and 12.9% failure in the ALDH2∗2 group. In the LS, the distribution was 51.0% success, 40.8% borderline, and 8.2% failure in the ALDH2∗1 group, compared with 68.8% success, 28.1% borderline, and 3.1% failure in the ALDH2∗2 group (Supplementary Table 3).

Examination of confounding factors: mixed-effects model analysis

A mixed-effects model was constructed with patient ID as a random effect and baseline age, sex, type of PTH preparation used, prior osteoporosis treatment, concomitant vitamin D use, treatment duration (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months), ALDH2 genotype, the interaction between treatment duration and ALDH2 genotype, and the interaction between PTH preparation and ALDH2 genotype included as fixed effects. The analysis showed that for FN BMD, the main effect of ALDH2 genotype was not significant (P = 0.891), whereas the interaction between ALDH2 genotype and treatment duration reached statistical significance (P = 0.009). In contrast, for LS BMD, ALDH2 genotype was significantly associated with BMD (P = 0.012), and the interaction between ALDH2 genotype and treatment duration demonstrated a trend toward significance (P = 0.074). Regarding differences by PTH preparation type, no significant interaction with ALDH2 genotype was observed for either FN or LS BMD, indicating that the type of PTH preparation did not modify the association between ALDH2 genotype and treatment response (Table 3a, Table 3b).

New osteoporotic fractures

During the follow-up period, 10 individuals in the ALDH2∗1 group experienced a total of 11 osteoporotic fractures (incidence, 20.4%): seven spinal compression fractures, two distal radius fractures, one proximal femur fracture, and one proximal humerus fracture. In the ALDH2∗2 group, five individuals experienced five fractures (incidence, 15.2%): three spinal compression fractures and two proximal femur fractures. There was no significant difference in the incidence of fractures between the groups.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effect of ALDH2 genotypes on BMD increase following PTH treatment. We first conducted an exploratory analysis without adjusting for potential confounding factors to directly assess genotype-specific trends in BMD response. This analysis revealed that individuals in the ALDH2∗2 group had significantly lower baseline FN BMD than those in the ALDH2∗1 group. However, over the treatment course, the mean BMD difference between the groups narrowed, and the ALDH2∗2 group exhibited a greater tendency toward responsiveness to PTH treatment than the ALDH2∗1 group. Notably, at 12 months, the increase in FN BMD was significantly higher in the ALDH2∗2 group than in the ALDH2∗1 group, suggesting a potential influence of ALDH2 genotype on treatment efficacy.

After adjusting for confounding factors using a mixed-effects model, no significant main effect of ALDH2 genotype was observed for FN BMD (P = 0.891). However, the interaction between ALDH2 genotype and treatment duration was significant (P = 0.009). This finding indicates that the effect of ALDH2 genotype on FN BMD is dynamic and depends on treatment duration, and therefore cannot be fully captured by simple group comparisons. The larger FN BMD increase observed in the ALDH2∗2 group in the unadjusted analysis was likely attributable to a combination of this interaction effect and the influence of confounding factors. In contrast, for LS BMD, ALDH2 genotype independently exerted a significant effect (P = 0.012), and the interaction between genotype and treatment duration showed a trend toward significance (P = 0.074). These results suggest that, in the LS as well, ALDH2 genotype may modulate responsiveness to PTH treatment, with its influence potentially varying over the course of therapy. These findings differ in part from those of previous murine studies, which reported pronounced effects in cortical bone. In our human data, although genotype-related differences were observed in the cortical-rich FN site in the unadjusted analysis, the main effect of genotype disappeared after adjustment, leaving only the interaction with treatment duration as significant. This discrepancy may reflect the influence of various human-specific factors—such as lifestyle, comorbidities, and medication history—that were not fully accounted for in the present analysis.

Although the results after accounting for confounding factors varied by skeletal site, individuals with the ALDH2∗2 genotype tended to exhibit a stronger bone-forming response to PTH treatment. In the FN, the significant interaction between genotype and treatment duration suggests that the bone-forming response may become more pronounced in the ALDH2∗2 group as treatment progresses. In contrast, in the LS, the significant main effect of genotype indicates that ALDH2 genotype may regulate treatment responsiveness in a site-specific manner.

Several studies have examined the association between the efficacy of osteoporosis treatments and various genetic polymorphisms; however, these studies have focused on genes directly involved in bone metabolism. For example, polymorphisms in low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 5 [20], sclerostin [21], and vitamin D receptor [22] genes have been examined in relation to BPs, whereas polymorphisms in vitamin D receptor genes have been studied in relation to SERMs [23]. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, no reports have examined the relationship between PTH treatment and genetic polymorphisms despite the high prevalence of ALDH2∗2 in the Japanese population. Therefore, this study is considered important and may assist in the development of treatment strategies for osteoporosis. Investigating these associations could lead to personalized approaches for managing osteoporosis on the basis of genetic profiles.

Previous studies have reported that daily teriparatide treatment increases BMD and reduces the risk of osteoporotic fractures in Japanese patients [24,25]. Additionally, weekly teriparatide treatment has been confirmed to demonstrate comparable efficacy in reducing fracture risk [26]. A study comparing the effectiveness of daily teriparatide treatment over a 12-month period between Japanese and non-Japanese populations (primarily Westerners) demonstrated that the percent increases in BMD were 8.25 ± 6.10% for Western participants and 10.43 ± 5.61% for Japanese participants [27]. Although this difference was not statistically significant, these findings suggest that PTH treatment may be more effective in increasing the BMD in Japanese individuals than in their Western counterparts. Approximately half of the Japanese population carries the ALDH2∗2 genotype. In contrast, although direct frequency data on ALDH2 genotypes in Western cohorts have not been reported, the ALDH2∗1 genotype is presumed to be predominant among individuals of European descent [28]. These genetic differences may play a crucial role in understanding the varying efficacies of osteoporosis treatments across different populations.

Concerning the bone turnover markers, a significant correlation has been reported between an increase in the P1NP concentration and the percent increase in LS BMD after 12 months of PTH treatment [29]. A similar trend was observed in our study, in that the mean P1NP concentration of the ALDH2∗2 group, which exhibited a higher rate of percent increase in BMD, was higher than that of the ALDH2∗1 group. This result further supports the association between P1NP and LS BMD and indicates differences in the effects of PTH treatment based on ALDH polymorphisms.

This study has several limitations. First, because this was a retrospective study, prior treatments were not standardized. In particular, the efficacy of PTH treatment in increasing BMD is diminished when bone resorption inhibitors, such as BPs, are used prior to treatment [30]. To address this issue, we performed additional mixed-effects model analyses. These analyses showed that prior treatment did not significantly affect FN BMD (P = 0.0987), suggesting that the major finding at the FN site—the significant interaction between ALDH2 genotype and treatment duration—was unlikely to be attributable to differences in pretreatment medication use. In contrast, prior treatment had a significant effect on LS BMD (P = 0.0080), indicating that non-standardized pretreatment may have influenced the estimation of genotype effects at this site. Second, this study included both daily and weekly teriparatide regimens, which may have introduced variability into the observed effects on BMD. Consistent with this possibility, the type of PTH preparation had no significant effect on FN BMD (P = 0.9389) but showed a significant main effect on LS BMD (P = 0.0013). However, the interaction between ALDH2 genotype and PTH preparation was not significant at either site (FN: P = 0.1074; LS: P = 0.2178), indicating that differences in dosing regimens did not modify the association between ALDH2 genotype and treatment responsiveness.

However, it should be noted that in human studies, these limitations, as well as various confounding factors—such as lifestyle, comorbidities, and environmental influences—may have affected the outcomes. As noted above, these factors may partially explain why the extent of the PTH treatment response observed in humans was not as pronounced as that seen in the mouse model. Additionally, while the mouse model used complete Aldh2 KO animals, the ALDH2∗2 group in this study included heterozygotes (ALDH2∗1/∗2), who are not completely deficient although they have markedly reduced ALDH2 enzymatic activity. These factors should be considered when interpreting the differences in PTH treatment responsiveness between mice and humans. Finally, the sample size was small, and the follow-up period was short. At the study design stage, we assumed a between-group difference in BMD of 0.045 g/cm2 and a standard deviation of 0.08 g/cm2, based on values reported in large-scale studies of Japanese populations and international datasets. Under this assumption, the estimated effect size was Cohen's d ≈ 0.56, indicating that approximately 50 participants per group (total N = 100) would provide sufficient statistical power. However, the final dataset was smaller than initially anticipated, comprising 49 participants in the ALDH2∗1 group and 33 participants in the ALDH2∗2 group, for a total of 82 participants. The results of the post-hoc power analysis indicated that, although the statistical power for comparing the mean FN BMD and its percent increase was relatively good, the bootstrap analysis of the P-value distribution suggested that variability in the data may have influenced the statistical outcomes. The statistical power for detecting differences in LS BMD was below 10%, reflecting the limitations of the sample size and suggesting an increased risk of type I and type II errors. Therefore, future studies should incorporate sample size calculations based on anticipated effect sizes and desired statistical power to ensure more robust and reliable conclusions.

Despite these limitations, statistically significant differences were observed in some outcomes, indicating the potential for clinical relevance. Notably, this study is the first to investigate the association between ALDH2 genetic polymorphisms and the efficacy of PTH treatment in humans. Consistent with findings from Aldh2 KO mouse models, our results suggest that individuals with the ALDH2∗2 genotype may exhibit a greater therapeutic response to PTH treatment than those with the ALDH2∗1 genotype. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate these findings and further clarify the role of ALDH2 polymorphisms in PTH treatment responsiveness.

Conclusions

In this study, we examined the association between ALDH2 genotypes and BMD increases following PTH treatment. In unadjusted analyses, individuals with the ALDH2∗2 genotype had significantly lower baseline FN BMD but showed greater increases in both FN and LS BMD over the 12-month treatment period, with a significant difference particularly at the FN.

In contrast, in mixed-effects models adjusting for major confounders and incorporating repeated measurements, no significant main effect of ALDH2 genotype was observed for FN BMD. However, the interaction between ALDH2 genotype and treatment duration was significant, suggesting that individuals with the ALDH2∗2 genotype may experience greater FN BMD gains as treatment progresses. For LS BMD, ALDH2 genotype was independently associated with treatment effects, and the interaction with treatment duration showed a trend toward significance, indicating that responsiveness may also vary over time.

These findings indicate that ALDH2 genotype may influence responsiveness to PTH treatment in a site-specific and time-dependent manner. Although the interaction with treatment duration should be interpreted cautiously, it suggests a potential influence of ALDH2 polymorphisms on osteoanabolic treatment responses.

To our knowledge, this is the first study in humans to investigate the relationship between ALDH2 gene polymorphisms and the efficacy of PTH treatment, offering preliminary insight into the potential relevance of genetic background in osteoporosis therapy. Future large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and further elucidate the role of ALDH2 gene polymorphisms in PTH treatment responsiveness.
