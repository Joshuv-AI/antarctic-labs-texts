On the Two-Step Hybrid Design for Augmenting Randomized Trials Using Real-World Data
Authors: Jiapeng Xu, Ruben P. A. van Eijk, Alicia Ellis, Tianyu Pan, Lorene M. Nelson, Kit C. B. Roes, Marc van Dijk, Maria Sarno, Leonard H. van den Berg, Lu Tian, Ying Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hybrid clinical trials, which borrow real-world data (RWD) from patient registries, claims databases, or electronic health records (EHRs) to augment randomized clinical trials, are of increasing interest. Hybrid clinical trials are especially relevant for rare diseases, where the recruitment of large sample sizes may be challenging. While these trials may better use available information, they assume that the RWD and randomized control arm are exchangeable. Violating this assumption can induce bias, inflate Type I error, or adversely affect statistical power. A two-step hybrid design first tests the exchangeability between randomized control arm and external data sources before incorporating RWD as a comparator for statistical inferences (Yuan et al. 2019). This approach reduces the chance of inappropriate borrowing but may simultaneously inflate the Type I error rate. We propose four different methods to control the Type I error rate under the exchangeability assumption. Approach 1 estimates the variance of the overall test statistic and rejects the null hypothesis based on a Z-test. Approach 2 uses a numerical method to determine the exact critical value for Type I error control. Approach 3 splits the Type I error rates according to the equivalence test outcome. Approach 4 adjusts the critical value only when equivalence is established. We illustrate these methods using a hypothetical scenario in the context of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). We evaluate the Type I error and power under various clinical trial conditions in comparison with the Bayesian power prior approach (Ibrahim et al. 2015). We demonstrate that our proposed methods and Bayesian power prior control Type I error and increase power under the exchangeability assumption, whereas the method proposed by Yuan et al. (2019) results in an increased Type I error. In the scenario where the exchangeability assumption does not hold, all methods fail to control the Type I error. Our proposed methods, however, limit a maximum Type I error inflation ranging from 6% to 8%, which compares favorably to 10% for Yuan et al. (2019) and 16% for the Bayesian power prior. All methods increase statistical power under the exchangeability condition but may lead to a loss of statistical power when the exchangeability assumption is violated.

Introduction

A rare disease is defined as a condition with a prevalence of fewer than 200,000 cases in the United States of America. The rarity of these diseases poses challenges in conducting clinical trials and developing pharmacologic treatments (Burton et al. 2021). The use of real-world data (RWD) offers new opportunities to enhance randomized clinical trials (RCTs) (Liu et al. 2022). To use RWD as an additional comparator in a randomized clinical trial, however, it is necessary to assume exchangeability between patients who randomized to the control arm and those who originated from RWD sources. When this assumption is violated, borrowing RWD can introduce bias into the estimated treatment effect, thereby inflating Type I error or adversely affecting statistical power. Several dynamic borrowing methods using Bayesian and frequentist approaches have been developed to mitigate these risks and improve inference. These methods dynamically discount information from external data based on the level of exchangeability between external and trial data (Viele et al. 2013). Bayesian dynamic borrowing methods include the power prior (Ibrahim et al. 2015), commensurate prior (Hobbs et al. 2011), meta-analytic-predictive (MAP) prior (Neuenschwander et al. 2010), and robust MAP prior (Schmidli et al. 2014). Frequentist approaches, such as the test-then-pool strategy (Yuan et al. 2019; Li, Liu, and Snavely 2020; Wied, Posch, and Hilgers 2024), offer alternative ways to regulate borrowing and ensure robust inference.

To facilitate exchangeability, matching methods, including the use of propensity scores, reduce baseline differences between two populations (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). However, these methods do not address the potential differences in outcomes caused by, for example, the occurrence of intercurrent events such as early study termination due to adverse events, withdrawal of consent or disease progression. Hence, despite matching of baseline covariates, it remains necessary to additionally determine the equivalence of outcomes. The two-step hybrid clinical trial design proposed by Yuan et al. (2019) combines participants in the RCT control arm with their propensity-matched external controls only when the equivalence criteria for the outcomes are met. Yuan et al. (2019) further demonstrated that outcome exchangeability was necessary to avoid the introduction of bias. The value of this approach was recently illustrated in a case study on amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (Van Eijk et al. 2023); using a hybrid approach improved precision and reduced study duration compared to that of the original clinical trial.

While the two-step hybrid design reduces bias in borrowing, it uses two statistical tests that are conditioned on exchangeability, which leads to inflation of Type I error (Yuan et al. 2019). The primary aim of this study, therefore, is to propose alternative statistical tests for two-step hybrid designs to more robustly and efficiently control Type I error and reduce bias in the estimated treatment effect. Second, we compare the operating characteristics of our proposed tests with those of Yuan et al. 2019) and the Bayesian power prior approach (Ibrahim et al. 2015). Comparisons will be made by conducting an extensive simulation study based on a hypothetical trial for Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) using a disease registry as the RWD source.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Section 2 describes the statistical model of the two-step hybrid design; Section 3 introduces four new methods; Section 4 describes the simulation study design and results; and Section 5 provides the discussion and conclusions.

A Two-Step Hybrid Design for RWD Augmentation

The Statistical Model

Consider a randomized trial in which patients are assigned to either the treatment or control arm. The primary objective is to determine whether the means differ between the two arms in a continuous outcome variable. The statistical hypotheses are as follows (1) H0:Δ=μt−μc=0vsH1:Δ=μt−μc≠0 where μt and μc represent the mean responses of the treatment and control arms, respectively.

We focus on scenarios in which an external dataset is available to augment the control arm of a clinical trial. We assume that the external data align perfectly with the baseline characteristics of trial participants (i.e., no selection bias) and that the outcome variable is measured identically to that of the trial. Van Eijk et al. (2023) provided an example of using concurrent registry data to supplement a randomized phase II trial after propensity score matching. Yuan et al. (2019) also provided an example using data from historical control arms of a similar disease and the same control condition after matching baseline characteristics to augment a new randomized phase III trial.

Without loss of generalizability, we assume that X∣S=s∼N(μs,σs2), for s=t,c,r. Here, X represents the random continuous outcome variable, S represents the data source, “t” represents treatment arm; “c” represents control arm, and “r” represents external data, “μ” represents the mean, and “σ2” represents the variance.

Let nt, nc, and nr be the sample sizes, and let X¯t, X¯c, and X¯r denote the sample means for the treatment arm, control arm, and external data, respectively. The test statistic for the clinical trial without the external data is (2) Y1=X¯t−X¯c∼N(μt−μc,σt2∕nt+σc2∕nc)=N(Δ,σY12).

Similarly, we can compare the outcomes between the control arm and external data as follows (3) Y2=X¯r−X¯c∼N(μr−μc,σr2∕nr+σc2∕nc)=N(δ,σY22).

To augment the trial with the external data, we compare the treatment arm with a weighted average of the observed sample means of the control and external data under the assumption that they are exchangeable, that is δ=E(X¯r−X¯c)=0. The augmented test statistic is Y3(w)=X¯t−(1−w)Xc−wX¯r=Y1−wY2.

The optimal weight that minimizes the variance of Y3 is the least square regression coefficient, that is, w∗=cov(Y1,Y2)σY22=σc2ncσr2nr+σc2nc.

Thus, (4) Y3=Y3(w∗)=Y1−w∗Y2∼N(Δ−ρσY1σY2δ,σY12(1−ρ2)) where ρ=cov(Y1,Y2)σY1σY2=σc2∕ncσt2∕nt+σc2∕ncσr2∕nr+σc2∕nc is the correlation coefficient between Y1 and Y2. Therefore, the variance of Y3 is always less than that of Y1. When δ=0, Y3 is a consistent and more efficient test statistic for the null hypothesis (1).

A Two-Step Hybrid Test

The validity of using Y3 as a test statistic depends on the assumption that δ=0. Yuan et al. (2019) proposed a two-step hybrid test that first tests the equivalence of means between external RWD and the control arm under the null hypothesis of H0,EQ:δ≠0. If this null hypothesis is rejected, equivalence is confirmed, and RWD is then pooled to augment inference using Y3. Otherwise, standard RCT analysis using Y1 is conducted. This two-step hybrid test is also known as the test-then-pool approach (Viele et al. 2013).

The equivalence test is typically conducted via the Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) (Schuirmann 1987), using a pre-specified equivalence margin δEQ reflecting the largest acceptable difference based on clinical significance. The null and alternative hypotheses for the TOST are as follows: (5) H0,EQ:∣μr−μc∣≥δEQvsH1,EQ:∣μr−μc∣<δEQ.

Let αEQ denote the significant level of the equivalence test. The null hypothesis is rejected if (6) −δEQ+Z1−αEQσ^Y2≤Y2≤δEQ−Z1−αEQσ^Y2 where Z1−α is the (1-α) quantile of the standard normal distribution. Let θ=δEQ−Z1−αEQσ^Y2; then H0,ET is rejected if ∣Y2∣≤θ. Yuan et al. (2019) proposed a two-step procedure for test H0:Δ=0 conditioning on whether ∣Y2∣≤θ. Specifically, the rejection rule is as follows: (7) {when∣Y2∣>θ,rejectH0if∣Y1∣≥Z1−α2σ^Y1when∣Y2∣≤θ,rejectH0if∣Y3∣≥Z1−α2σY3.}

Alternative 2-Step Tests

To ensure proper control of the Type I error rate under the exchangeability assumption, we propose four alternative two-step tests for the null hypothesis.

Approach 1: A Z-Test

In this approach, although Y no longer follows a normal distribution, we still estimate the variance of Y and use a Z-test to reject the null hypothesis when ∣Y∣≥Z1−α2σ^Y. The variance σ^Y can be derived by plugging in the estimations of the sample means and variances from Section 2 using the following formula: (9) σY2=V(Y)=V(Y1)+V(w∗Y21{∣Y2∣≤θ})−2cov(Y1,w∗Y21{∣Y2∣≤θ})=σY12−w∗2(E(1∣Y2∣≤θY22)+[E(1∣Y2∣≤θY2)]2)+2w∗2δE(1∣Y2∣≤θY2).

The derivation of (9) is provided in Appendix A.2.

For a fixed δEQ, with a large sample size from both the control arm and RWD, the power for the equivalent test βEQ=P(∣Y2∣≤θ∣δ=0) approach to 1. Thus, Y approaches to Y3, which follows a normal distribution. However, when δEQ is chosen according to a fixed probability of borrowing, the power for the equivalence test will be fixed, and Y will not approach to a normal distribution. In this case, the Z-test is only an approximation of the normal distribution.

The advantage of Approach 3 over Approach 2 is the pre-specified splitting of the Type I error rate under the two conditions. However, like Approach 2, Approach 3 does not guarantee Type I error control when δ≠0.

Conditional and Overall Type I Error and Statistical Power for the Proposed Approaches

Approaches 2 and 4 are special cases of Approach 3 with specific choices of the weighting parameter v. The selection of v reflects the degree of confidence in exchangeability. However, v should not be excessively large or small. If v is too large, no power gain will be achieved under the exchangeability condition, undermining the purpose of the hybrid design. Conversely, if v is too small, it may unnecessarily reduce the power when the data are not exchangeable. Ideally, v should be close to βEQ, aligning the Type II error allocation with the likelihood of the pooling decision.

(2019) and the proposed approaches, Table 1 presents the numerical results for conditional Type I error rates under scenarios in which exchangeability is either rejected (i.e., no borrowing) or accepted (i.e., borrowing), and the overall Type I error. These results are shown as functions of the equivalence margin, Type I error rate for the equivalence test, and power of the equivalence test (i.e., probability of borrowing) under realistic sample sizes of 100 per arm and 200 RWD patients. Statistical power was also reported for a true treatment effect size of 0.3962, which corresponds to the effect size that yields 80% power in a standard t-test without access to RWD.

From Table 1, we observe that the conditional Type I error without borrowing for Approaches 1 and 4 is comparable to that derived by Yuan et al. (2019). Approach 1 uses the same critical value as Yuan et al. (2019) but a different variance estimation than theirs. Approach 4 uses the same critical value only without borrowing and a higher critical value with borrowing, compared to Yuan et al. (2019). Therefore, both approaches have significantly lower conditional Type I error rates with borrowing than those derived by Yuan et al. (2019), resulting in overall Type I error control under the exchangeability condition. Approach 2 shows a lower conditional Type I error in the no-borrowing scenarios than in Approaches 1 and 4. Approach 3 provides flexibility through the weight assignment. All proposed approaches controlled the overall. Type I error at the 5% level when δ=0.

For δ=0, most proposed tests achieve statistical power above 80% only in a few scenarios with smaller v’s and low borrowing probabilities. Choosing a small v when βEQ is large may result in power loss. The power increases with the equivalence test power.

Application beyond Sample Mean of Normally Distributed Data

Although the statistical tests proposed above are based on normally distributed samples, they can be extended to generalized linear models (GLMs) and Cox regression models, in which differences in outcomes from the data sources are estimated via regression coefficients. In these models, two binary indicators can be introduced to denote the data sources: one for the treatment group and one for the RWD, with the control group serving as a reference.

For GLMs in the exponential family, let g(⋅) be the link function of the GLM for an outcome variable from different data sources, denoted as Xs for s=t,c, and r. A GLM used to represent data is (17) g(E(Xs))=β0+β11{s=t}+β21{s=r} where, β1 represents the difference between the treatment and control arms, and β2 represents the difference between the RWD and the control arm. Let β=(β1β2) be the vector of regression coefficients of interest. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) β^=(β^1β^2) asymptotically follows a bivariate normal distribution N(β,I−1(β)), where I is the Fisher information matrix (see sec. 4.2.4 in Agresti 2015). In this case, we can choose Y1=β^1, Y2=β^2, and Y3=Y1−w∗Y2=(1−w∗)β^1+w∗(β^1−β^2), where w∗=corr(β^1,β^2), which can be derived from I−1(β). The procedures in Sections 3.1–3.4 can be used for these models.

A special example is the binary outcome, where g(⋅) is a logistic link function, β1 is the log of odds ratio (OR) between the treatment and control arms, and β2 is the log-OR between the RWD and control arm. The equivalence margin was defined based on β2, with β2=0 corresponding to an odds ratio of 1 between the RWD and control arm. Y3 is the optimal weighted average of the log-ORs between the treatment and control arms, and between the treatment arm and RWD.

Similarly, we can extend our approach to survival data within the framework of the Cox regression model. Let Xs=(Ts,δs) be the observed follow-up time and death indicator for s=t,c and r; and h0(t)=hc(t) denote the hazard function for the control arm. Under the proportional hazards and non-informative censoring assumptions, the hazard function from all data sources is specified as follows: (18) hs(t)=h0(t)eβ11{s=t}+β21{s=r} where β1 represents the log hazard ratio (HR) for treatment, and β2 represents the log-HR for RWD relative to the randomized control arm.

According to Andersen and Gill (1982), the Cox regression model can be formulated within a counting process and martingale framework, under which the partial likelihood estimator β^=(β^1β^2), corresponding to the log-HR, is consistent and asymptotically normal, even in the presence of censoring and time-dependent covariates. Lin and Wei (1989) further demonstrated that the estimated regression coefficients in a Cox regression model can be represented as a sum of independent and identically distributed (iid) terms under mild regularity conditions. This property holds for both the estimated treatment effect and the estimated survival difference between the RWD and control arm. Consequently, the pair of estimated log-HRs can be expressed as the sum of iid bivariate random vectors. According to the multivariate central limit theorem, the joint distribution of these estimators converges weakly to a bivariate normal distribution after an appropriate standardization (Billingsley 1995). While the normal approximation remains valid under standard conditions, we empirically assess the adequacy of the bivariate normality assumption in Figure 2 using Q–Q plots for the standardized coefficients β^1 and β^2 from the simulation study in Section 4, with sample sizes of nt=nc=100, and nr=200. Again, we use Y1=β^1, Y2=β^2, and Y3=Y1−w∗Y2, where w∗=corr(β^1,β^2). All formulas derived in this section remain valid in this regression framework, where equivalence is defined by the log-HR β2 being close to zero.

Simulation Studies

Comparison of Methods

In this section, we conduct simulation studies to evaluate the operating characteristics of our proposed approaches and compare them the method proposed by Yuan et al. (2019) and the Bayesian power prior method proposed by Ibrahim et al. (2015). The Bayesian power prior method (Ibrahim et al. 2015) is a common approach to augment the control arm of RCT data (denoted as D, i.e., s=t or c) with external RWD (denoted as D0, s=r). The power prior method incorporates D0 into the analysis by combining an initial non-informative prior of treatment effect π0(μ) with the likelihood function of the D0 raised to a power α0∈[0,1]. Specifically, the power prior is defined as (19) π(μ∣D0,α0)∝L(μ∣D0)α0π0(μ) where L(μ∣D0) is the likelihood based on the historical data, and α0 is set based on the heterogeneity of D0 and D (dynamic borrowing) that controls the influence of historical information. In our case, if the external registry data are inconsistent with the control arm, α0 tends to be small, and vice versa. We choose α0 to maximize the marginal likelihood function, defined as (20) m(α0)=∫L(μ∣μc,σc2)L(μ∣μr,σr2)π0(μ)dμ∫L(μ∣μr,σr2)π0(μ)dμ=∫ϕ(μ−μcσc)ϕα0(μ−μrσr)ϕ(μ−μ0σ0)dμ∫ϕα0(μ−μrσr)ϕ(μ−μ0σ0)dμ where μ is the random variable of treatment effect with a prior N(μ0,σ02).

Once we obtain α0 that maximizes the above marginal likelihood function, the posterior estimation of the pooled treatment effect is X¯t−(1−α0w∗)X¯c−α0w∗X¯r=Y1−α0w∗Y2. We reject the null hypothesis based on the Z-test of the posterior distribution of the pooled treatment effect. To use the power prior for our simulation, we used Y1=β^1 and Y2=β^2.

Simulation Settings

The simulation study was motivated by a hypothetical 1:1 randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial of ALS (Van Eijk et al. 2023). The primary outcome was survival, defined as time to death from any cause. A synthetic RWD dataset was generated according to the distribution of the Netherlands ALS registry used by Van Eijk et al. (2023). The simulation study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University Medical Center Utrecht and Stanford University.

In each iteration of our simulation study, we used a set of common eligibility criteria in ALS (Van Eijk et al. 2023) to define eligible patients, that is, those with a symptom duration ≤ 24 months, age at enrollment between 18 and 80 years, and percentage predicted vital capacity ≥ 65%. Eligible patients were enrolled in the trial based on their probability of trial participation based on a logistic regression model described by Van Eijk et al. (2023); enrollment was stopped after randomly selecting 200 eligible patients from the registry as trial participants. Patients were then randomly allocated to active or placebo treatment in a 1:1 ratio. The survival distribution of the control arm was derived from a Cox regression model with the Breslow baseline survival curve according to the observed survival time and baseline covariates of the eligible patients (Breslow (1972)). The estimated survival distribution was used to generate random survival times for each patient in the trial cohort. Covariates (U→ used in the Cox regression model included the site of symptom onset, El Escorial classification, % predicted vital capacity, age at enrollment, symptom duration, revised ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS-R) total score, and body mass index (BMI). A patient with covariates U→i in the control arm has the survival function (21) Sc(t;U→i)=(S0(t))ea′Ui where S0(t) is the Breslow baseline survival function and a→ is the vector of regression coefficients from the Cox proportional hazards model for eligible patients in the synthetic registry data.

Survival time of patients randomized to active treatment were simulated based on the HR θT. Specifically, the survival time for patients in the treatment arm with covariates U→i followed a survival distribution given as (22) ST(t;U→i)=(Sc(t;U→i))θT=(Sc(t;U→i))exp(β1).

Patients who were eligible but not enrolled in the trial formed the external RWD population. To identify concurrent matched external controls from the registry, logistic regression was used to generate a simulation-specific propensity scores for the 200 patients enrolled in the simulated trial, who were matched to 200 patients in the external RWD population using nearestneighbor matching, as described by Van Eijk et al. (2023). The survival time for the RWD external controls was generated according to the pre-specified difference between the control arm and RWD measured by HR θR. Thus, the survival time for patients in the RWD with covariates U→i had the following survival function: (23) SR(t;U→i)=(Sc(t;U→i))θR=(Sc(t;U→i))exp(β2).

Since our primary endpoint was event-driven, the trial ended when 89 events were reached in the clinical trial, resulting in approximately 81% power to detect β1=log(θT)=−0.4. We administratively censored the trial participants after the completion of the trial. Additionally, matched external control patients were administratively censored according to the length of follow-up in the registry or trial follow-up time, whichever occurred first. The simulation was repeated 10,000 times, leading to a 95% confidence interval for a 5% Type-I error rate of (4.57%, 5.43%), and a 95% confidence interval for 80.74% power of (80.00%, 81.51%). Figure 3 shows a flowchart of the simulation.

In the simulation study, the equivalence boundary δ between the randomized control arm and RWD external controls was set at 0.3 for β2, corresponding to an HR within the range of 0.74–1.35. A significance level of 10% was chosen for assessing the equivalence, under which the borrowing probability was 70%–80% when the control arm and the RWD were equivalent (i.e., β2=0). This reflects the principle that borrowing should not be overly restrictive when RWD and the control arm are similar. The significance level for the primary efficacy test was set at 5% (two-sided).

The hybrid control design was studied under multiple scenarios by varying β1 from 0 (HR=1, under the null hypothesis) to −0.4 (HR= 0.67, under the alternative hypothesis) and varying β2 from −0.7 (HR=0.50) to 0.7 (HR=2.0) in increments of 0.1. This allowed for the assessment of scenarios in which there was no difference between the treatment and control arms (β1=0), and the difference between the control arm and RWD was either zero (β2=0), within the equivalence margin (0<∣β2∣<δ), or beyond the equivalence margin (∣β2∣≥δ); as well as scenarios where there was a treatment effect (β1=−0.4) and varying degrees of difference between RWD and the control arm.

Simulation Results

Figure 4 presents the probability of rejecting the null of equivalence test, which is the probability of borrowing from RWD to augment RCT data, and the power prior α0 under various values of log(θR). The figure shows that maximum borrowing occurs when β2=log(θR)=0, meaning that the RWD and control arm are equivalent. The probability of borrowing decreases as log(θR) moves away from 0.

In the ideal case of log(θR)=0, the test-then-pool approach had a 74.94% chance of borrowing, corresponding with the power of the equivalence test. The power prior was also below 1 in this setting, reflecting the influence of random variations on borrowing RWD.

When ∣log(θR)∣=δEQ=0.3, the test-then-pool approach resulted in approximately 10% borrowing, consistent with the prespecified Type I error rate of the equivalence test. For ∣log(θR)∣≥0.5, borrowing using the test-then-pool approach was nearly eliminated. However, the power prior continued to borrow RWD even when ∣log(θR)∣≥0.5, indicating its lack of sensitivity to the degree of difference. One advantage of the 2-step hybrid approach is that it allows explicit control over the amount of borrowing by adjusting the equivalence boundary δEQ, thereby bounding the potential bias introduced by nonequivalent external data.

Figure 5 displays the bias introduced for both 2-step hybrid approach and Bayesian power prior in two scenarios: when the exchangeability assumption held and when it was violated. This demonstrated that the bias introduced in 2-step hybrid approach in treatment estimation was 0 when log(θR)=0. As log(θR) deviated from 0, the bias gradually increased and peaked at approximately ∣log(θR)∣=0.2. Subsequently, the bias diminished because of the reduced chance of borrowing. When log(θR) was greater than 0, the bias introduced to an underestimation of log(θT) whereas when log(θR) was less than 0, the bias led to an overestimation of log(θT). When using the Bayesian power prior according to Ibrahim et al. (2015), no bias was introduced when the exchangeability assumption was satisfied. However, as the value of log(θR) deviated from 0, the bias introduced surpassed that of the 2-step hybrid approach, reaching its peak at approximately ∣log(θR)∣=0.3. Additionally, bias persisted when ∣log(θR)∣≥0.5 due to continued borrowing.

The simulation results are summarized in Tables 2 and 3, which show the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis under null and alternative conditions, respectively. They are also presented in Figures 6 and 7.

Under the perfect null condition, Yuan et al. (2019) had a Type I error of 6.58%, a 1.58% inflation from the targeted 5%, and outside the 95% confidence interval. Among all methods, Approach 1 was the most conservative, with a Type I error of 3.82%, which was below the 5% threshold. Approaches 2, 3, and 4 were nearly identical and resulted in Type I error rates of 4.76%, 4.85%, and 5.07%, respectively. The power prior had a Type I error of 5.22%, within the 95% confidence interval. As the true difference between the control arm and external RWD increased, the Type I error inflated for all methods (Figure 6). Approach 1 was the most conservative method that maintained Type I error almost below 6% in all scenarios. The Type I errors of approaches 2, 3, and 4, were similar and were at their maximum when log(θR)=0.2 with Type I error rates of 8.13%, 7.93%, and 8.05%, respectively. Yuan et al. (2019) method had a Type I error of 9.92%, whereas the power prior led to a serious Type I error inflation of 16.78%. Overall, Type I error inflation could not be fully controlled by any of the methods, as information borrowing may introduce bias into the treatment effect estimation. However, our two-step approaches could provide an upper bound of type I inflation, which could be specified in the design. As the true difference increased further, Type I error inflation began to decrease because of the decreased likelihood of borrowing. It is worth noting that Approaches 2 and 3 lead to a deflated Type I error rate when ∣δ∣ is above 0.4. This occurs because, under substantial bias, the borrowing probability converges to zero, and the Type I error approaches the conditional Type I error of no borrowing with a critical value z1∗ more stringent than z1−α∕2. In contrast, Approach 4 ensures that the Type I error rate converges to the prespecified level when ∣δ∣ is above 0.4, thereby avoiding Type I error deflation.

Under the alternative condition when log(θT)=−0.4, all methods led to improved power compared to a no borrowing scenario when log(θR)=0 (Figure 7). Among them, Approach 1 had the least power gain of 82.73%, and the Bayesian power prior had the highest power of 92.51%. The statistical power was approximately 86% for Approaches 2–4 and 88% for the method by Yuan et al. (2019).

When log(θR)<0, all methods had decreased statistical power, which could be lower than the designed power without borrowing. This was primarily due to the bias introduced by the better survival of patients in RWD than that in the trial, leading to an underestimation of treatment effects by pooling RWD.

The power gain was less for Approaches 1–4 when log(θR)>0 because of a rapid drop in the probability of borrowing RWD and the more stringent critical values to reject the null. Similarly, owing to the use of a more stringent critical value for non-borrowing cases and the low probability of borrowing for substantial bias (∣δ∣≥0.4), we observed a reduction in power compared to the reference level of 80% in both Approaches 2 and 3. Both Yuan et al. (2019) and the power prior (Ibrahim et al. 2015) had greater power gains.

Conclusion and Discussion

Borrowing RWD is an attractive strategy for improving the efficiency of clinical trials without increasing the number of randomized participants. A fundamental assumption in this approach is the exchangeability between the external and randomized control arms. The two-step hybrid trial design incorporates an equivalence test to evaluate this assumption, allowing flexibility in the selection of an equivalence margin that reflects the maximum clinically acceptable difference. This margin plays a critical role in controlling the likelihood of borrowing and balancing efficiency gains against risk of bias.

In this study, we proposed four calibration methods within a test-then-pool framework to control the Type I error of the treatment effect, conditional on the outcome of the equivalence test. Through a comprehensive simulation study—based on the hybrid design by Yuan et al. (2019)—we evaluated operating characteristics under various scenarios. Our results show that the method proposed by Yuan et al. (2019) leads to inflated Type I error rates, even under conditions of exchangeability. Both this method and the Bayesian power prior approach by Ibrahim et al. (2015) showed greater Type I error inflation under nonexchangeable conditions compared to our proposed approaches.

Among the methods evaluated, Approach 1 was the most conservative, consistently maintaining Type I error control even in the presence of small differences between the randomized control and RWD data. Approaches 2, 3, and 4 adequately controlled Type I error only when the difference was close to zero, making them preferable when there is a strong a priori belief in exchangeability. When the difference exceeded the equivalence margin, Approaches 1 and 4 continued to control Type I error under the null and retained power under the alternative. Approaches 2 and 3 tended to yield lower Type I error under the null and exhibited lower power under the alternative.

The evaluation of conditional Type I error based on the equivalence test outcome is also informative. As shown in Table 1, Approaches 1 and 4 achieved substantially lower conditional Type I error rates compared to the original approach proposed by Yuan et al. (2019), thereby improving overall error control under exchangeability. Approach 1 achieves this by using the same critical value as Yuan et al. (2019), but with an improved variance estimation. Approach 4 adapts the critical value only when borrowing occurs. Approach 2 applies a consistent critical value regardless of borrowing status, providing stronger conditional Type I error control. Approach 3 introduces a weighting parameter, v, that flexibly allocates Type I error between borrowing and no-borrowing scenarios, allowing for a tunable tradeoff between the Type I error control and power. Approaches 2 and 4 can be viewed as specific instances Approach 3 corresponding to fixed values of v.

These conditional characteristics are important for assessing the robustness of hybrid designs, especially in the presence of uncertain exchangeability. Overall, Approach 4 offers a reasonable compromise, balancing modest Type I error inflation with adequate power across a range of non-exchangeability scenarios.

When implementing a Bayesian power prior, selecting the discount parameter α0 is a critical consideration. A conservative value can reduce Type I error inflation but may compromise power. In the presence of bias, Type I error inflation can worsen and estimation of α0 may fail to converge as bias increases. To address this, we use the marginal likelihood criterion proposed by Ibrahim et al. (2015) to select α0. Unlike the power priors, the test-then-pool approach stops borrowing beyond the equivalence margin, thereby limiting bias and narrowing the Type I error inflation window. Approach 4 enables prospective trial designs that maintain correct Type I error control under exchangeability, limit Type I error inflation when the exchangeability is violated and still achieve meaningful gains in statistical power.

In general, all external borrowing methods may lead to Type I error inflation in certain conditions. However, as noted by Kopp-Schneider, Calderazzo, and Wiesenfarth (2020), when external data are truly exchangeable, power gains can be achieved even under strict Type I error control—consistent with our findings. We support the use of concurrent registry data to augment randomized trials. In our framework, trial participants were sampled from a concurrent registry and matched to nonparticipating patients who are alive and diagnosed during the runtime of the trial. This enhances the likelihood of exchangeability after appropriate propensity score matching. With this design, and after confirming exchangeability, external data can be safely borrowed to augment clinical trials while maintaining Type I error control.

A challenge for the test-then-pool approach is that an adequate sample size of the randomized control arm is needed to power the equivalence test, which may be difficult for rare diseases. Yuan et al. (2019) proposed a two-stage design with an initial imbalanced randomized allocation favoring the treatment arm, reversing the allocation if the interim equivalence test fails to reject non-exchangeability. A more practical approach is to use equal randomization before the interim analysis to ensure a sufficiently sized control arm. Recently, Wied, Posch, and Hilgers (2024) proposed a sequential “fill-it-up” design, which further explores the choice of equivalence margin, sample size estimation, statistical power, and family-wise error control.

Although two-sided tests were used in our simulations, one-sided tests are often more relevant for evaluating new therapies. In our simulation study, the operating characteristics between the one- and two-sided tests were mostly similar, with the main difference being the Type I error curve, which changed from a bimodal to an S-shaped curve. When bias was positive, Type I error became inflated; otherwise, it was deflated, while power remained largely unchanged. To avoid redundancy, we did not discuss the one-sided results in detail, but they are available in the supplementary materials.

In summary, the two-step hybrid design that tests exchangeability prior to pooling offers a practical framework to integrate external data into randomized clinical trials. Proper statistical procedures are essential to maintain Type I error control—a key requirement for regulatory approval. While we focused on four frequentist approaches, other alternatives, including Bayesian methods that do not require pre-testing, are also available. In practice, sensitivity analyses using multiple borrowing strategies are recommended to ensure robustness.
