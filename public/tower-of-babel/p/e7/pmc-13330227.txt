Multidrug-resistant Bacillus cereus in retail poultry meat: molecular insights into toxigenicity and antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Reham M. El-Tarabili, Ahmed Alfifi, Mohammed Y. Behairy, Sherief M. Abdel-Raheem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBacillus cereus (B. cereus) is a spore-forming foodborne pathogen linked to poultry contamination. This study evaluated its prevalence, groEL phylogeny, toxigenic potential, and antimicrobial resistance in Egyptian retail poultry meat.MethodsA total of 110 retail poultry meat samples were collected from commercial supermarkets in Port Said, Egypt, between April and May 2025. Afterwards, samples underwent bacteriological examination. The isolated samples were subjected to groEL gene sequence analysis, antibiotic sensitivity testing, and PCR detection for toxigenic and resistance genes.ResultsThe total prevalence of B. cereus in the food samples examined was 14.5%. The groEL-based phylogeny revealed high genetic similarity between our isolates and human clinical strains, suggesting a potential for zoonotic transmission and highlighting the public health risk. Furthermore, the isolated B. cereus strains exhibited β-hemolytic and lecithinase activities, and PCR analysis demonstrated that the examined B. cereus strains usually harbored hbl complex (hblD: 81.3%, hblA:81.3%, and hblC: 75%), nhe complex genes (nheB: 62.5%, and nheA: 50%), pc-plc (100%), ent-FM (100%), cytK (50%), and ces (31.25%) virulence genes. In addition, all isolates encoded bla1, bla2, and tetA (100% each), and 75% carried ermA. Overall, 62.5% of isolates were multidrug resistant to seven agents from six antimicrobial classes and harbored bla1, bla2, ermA, and tetA.ConclusionThis study highlights the prevalence of multidrug-resistant B. cereus in poultry meat, underscoring the importance of molecular monitoring of MDR B. cereus strains. These findings indicate that toxigenic B. cereus persists in poultry meat and exhibits evolving resistance profiles, underscoring the need for multiple control strategies to reduce food contamination in poultry production and retail settings and highlighting the significant public health concern posed by this pathogen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05337-5.

Background

Bacillus cereus (B. cereus) is abundant in the environment and is often found in soil, vegetation, and a variety of foods, such as rice, pasta, dairy products, meat, chicken, and starchy foods [1]. Recent studies have established that B. cereus is widespread in both processed and raw food products worldwide, with contamination developing during food production and handling [2]. The ability of B. cereus to survive as spores allows it to withstand cooking and subsequently proliferate in food, particularly under inadequate storage conditions [3]. B. cereus can act as an opportunistic pathogen in immunocompromised or otherwise vulnerable hosts, even though gastrointestinal illness caused by this organism is typically mild. Serious extraintestinal infections, such as endophthalmitis, endocarditis, pneumonia, sepsis, osteomyelitis, and other invasive syndromes, may be linked to B. cereus [4, 5]. These severe symptoms are uncommon and should be differentiated from more common foodborne illnesses [5]. B. cereus produces a lecithinase (phospholipase C) that contributes to β‑hemolysis and dermonecrosis and is heat-stable and cation-dependent [6]. The enzyme exhibits heat resistance and demonstrates immunological cross-reactivity between B. cereus and B. mycoides strains [6].

B. cereus is a Gram-positive, spore-forming bacteria that is known for its association with foodborne infections. It is responsible for causing gastrointestinal illness that is self-limiting and presents as a mild form of diarrhea or emetic form that disappears after 24 h. In rare cases, more severe disease may occur, particularly in vulnerable individuals or after exposure to high levels of toxins [7]. B. cereus food poisoning presents as emetic or diarrheal syndromes resulting from toxin production. Emetic syndrome is caused by the heat-stable toxin cereulide, synthesized in food when B. cereus grows to high levels (typically ≥ 10⁵ CFU/g), resulting in nausea and vomiting, while the diarrheal syndrome is primarily associated with enterotoxins such as hemolysin BL (Hbl), non‑hemolytic enterotoxin (Nhe), and cytotoxin K (CytK), which are produced in the small intestine after ingestion of viable cells or spores [8, 9]. The primary virulence factors linked to B. cereus infection include Cytotoxin K, encoded by the cytK gene; non-hemolytic enterotoxin, encoded by the nheABC gene cluster; Hemolysin BL, encoded by the hblABCD gene cluster; emetic cereulide toxin, encoded by the ces gene; and phospholipase C, encoded by the pc-plc gene. Infection occurs due to the consumption of food contaminated with B. cereus, which adheres to the host intestinal mucosa, leading to colonization and enterotoxin production [10, 11]. The organism’s capacity to produce these toxin with its ability to survive and proliferate in prepared foods, underscores their importance for public health [7]. B. cereus is associated with serious infections in humans, such as pneumonia, bacteremia in neonates, gas gangrene, bacterial meningitis, and ocular infections [12].

The recent rise in multi-drug-resistant (MDR) B. cereus strains in meat, dairy, and low-moisture foods has raised concerns regarding food safety and clinical therapy [13, 14]. B. cereus possesses many antibiotic resistance genes that confer resistance to various types of antimicrobials [10]. B. cereus is intrinsically resistant to many β‑lactam antibiotics, mainly because it produces β‑lactamase enzymes encoded in its chromosome [12, 15]. B. cereus may exhibit acquired resistance to several drugs, including streptomycin, macrolides, tetracycline, clindamycin, and erythromycin [16, 17]. The concurrent presence of virulence genes and resistance markers raises hazards, particularly in food, as it can increase the survival and persistence of microorganisms [15, 18–20].

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of B. cereus in retail poultry meat in Port Said, Egypt, and to characterize the isolates phenotypically and genotypically, including groEL phylogeny, virulence gene content, antimicrobial susceptibility, and resistance determinants.

Materials and methods

Sampling

During the period from April to May 2025, ten sampling visits were conducted to many commercial supermarkets located in Port-Said Governorate, Egypt. During each visit, approximately 8–15 samples were collected, totaling one hundred and ten retail poultry meat samples. Consequently, samples were transported on icebox to the laboratory.

Isolation and identification of B. cereus

Approximately 25 g of each collected sample was homogenized in 225 mL of buffered peptone water (BD Difco, USA) using a stomacher, followed by serial dilution. Subsequently, 0.1 ml of each diluted specimen was uniformly applied with a spreader to Mannitol Egg-Yolk Polymyxin (MYP) agar plates (BD Difco, USA) and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h [21]. B. cereus was identified based on its culture and morphological characteristics, endospores, and results from biochemical tests, including catalase, indole, H2S production, oxidase, methyl red, sugar fermentation, and Voges-Proskauer tests, as outlined by Maturin and Peeler [22]. The identity of the isolates was genetically determined using specific primers for the groEL gene, as previously described [23].

Phenotypic virulence assessment of B.cereus

Hemolysis

Isolates were streaked onto 5% sheep blood agar (Oxoid, UK) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The presence of β-hemolysis surrounding the colonies was interpreted as a positive result [24].

Lecithinase production

The isolates were incubated on Mannitol Egg-Yolk Polymyxin (MYP) agar plates at 37 °C for 24 h, and the opaque zone on MYP indicates lecithinase activity [6].

groEL gene sequencing

The recovered B. cereus isolates exhibited comparable phenotypic traits; therefore, the PCR amplicons from two randomly selected isolates were subjected to one-directional groEL sequencing (QIAGEN Sciences Inc., Germantown, MD, USA). Gene sequencing was performed using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA version 11 [25] .

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The antibiotic susceptibility of the isolated B. cereus strains (Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:{10}^{8}$$\end{document} CFU/mL) was evaluated on Mueller-Hinton agar (BD Difco, USA) using the disc diffusion method at 35 ± 2 °C for 18–24 h, as described by Park et al. [26]. Ten antimicrobial discs were utilized, including β‑lactam agents (amoxicillin (AMX, 30 µg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AMC, 30 µg)), macrolides (erythromycin (E, 15 µg)), aminoglycosides (gentamycin (CN, 30 µg)), tetracyclines (tetracycline (TE, 30 µg)), sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SXT, 25 µg)), carpebenems (imipenem (IPM, 30 µg)), and fluroquinolones (ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg)) (Oxoid, UK). The antimicrobial agents evaluated are frequently used in the veterinary and health sectors in Egypt. The results were interpreted in accordance with CLSI [27]. Inhibition zones were measured according to CLSI procedures, in the absence of B. cereus–specific criteria, interpretive categories were derived from breakpoints for related Gram‑positive organisms, as described by park et al. [26]. The control strain used was S. aureus ATCC 29,213. The isolated B. cereus strains were categorized as multidrug-resistant (MDR) based on the criteria established by Magiorakos et al. [28]. Isolates were classified as multidrug‑resistant when they showed non‑susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories. The multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index was evaluated following the method described by Krumperman [29].

Detection of toxigenic and antibiotic resistance genes

Virulence-associated genes (hblACD, nheAB, cytK, ces, ent-FM, and pc-plc) and antibiotic resistance genes (tetA, bla1, ermA, and bla2) were detected using PCR. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA). PCR was performed in a 25 µL reaction mixture containing 5 µL of 10× buffer, 1 µL of dNTPs at a final concentration of 200 µM (10 mM stock), 3 µL of bacterial DNA, 0.4 µL of Taq DNA polymerase (2 U), 30 pmol of each primer (0.1–0.6 µM), and nuclease-free distilled water to reach a final volume of 25 µL. The purified DNA was eluted into a new collection tube using 200 µL Buffer AE. DNA-free reactions were included as negative controls, and reference ATCC 14,579 B. cereus strains obtained from the Animal Health Research Institute (AHRI; Dokki, Egypt) were used as positive controls. The nucleotide sequences of the primers used are shown in Table 1. Amplified PCR products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Statistical associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test implemented in R (version 4.0.2; https://www.r-project.org/), with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. A correlation matrix describing resistance phenotypes, resistance determinants, and antimicrobial resistance was generated in R (version 4.5.2; https://www.r-project.org/) using the “corr” function.

Results

Phenotypic characteristics and the frequency of B. cereus isolates

The prevalence of B. cereus in the inspected poultry samples was 14.5% (16\110). The isolates displayed the unique morphological characteristics of B. cereus, with isolates being Gram-positive, motile, short bacilli that contained non-bulging endospores. On MYP media, colonies exhibited pink coloration along with a distinct precipitation zone, indicating lecithinase positivity and β-hemolytic activity on blood agar plates. Furthermore, all isolates exhibited a biochemical profile consistent with B. cereus, which exhibited positive results for Voges-Proskauer, catalase, citrate utilization, glucose fermentation, and nitrate reduction tests. Similarly, the isolates showed negative results for oxidase, H2S production, methyl red, indole, and mannitol fermentation tests (Table 1S). Furthermore, all isolates obtained carried the groEL gene.

Phylogenetic analysis of the groEL gene

Phylogenetic analysis confirmed that the examined B. cereus strains (accession numbers PX458638 and PX458639) showed significant genetic similarity to other isolates from various sources across multiple geographic regions. For instance, B. cereus strain BCS 33 (98.7%) isolated from Taiwan (Accession number: AY112837), strain ATCC 9818 (98.1%) isolated from Taiwan (Accession number: AY112844), B. cereus strain 3a (98.5%) isolated from the USA (Accession number: CP009596), B. cereus strain J2 (97.7%) isolated from human blood of a bacteremia patient in Japan (Accession number: AP022927), B. cereus strain cifa_chp23 (97.7%) isolated from a hot spring in Japan (Accession number: KC895943), B. cereus strain 2–6 A (97.2%) isolated from the Pacific Ocean (Accession number: CP117421), B. cereus strain Beh10 HSP60 (96.6%) isolated from Iran (accession number: ON664924), as displayed in Fig. 1.

Hemolysis and lecithinase ability

All isolated B. cereus isolates (100%) exhibited β-hemolysis on sheep-blood agar (Fig. 1S). Lecithinase activity was indicated by the development of an opaque zone surrounding colonies on egg yolk agar. All the examined isolates demonstrated positive lecithinase activity.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of B. cereus strains

Resistance to antimicrobial agents among the B. cereus isolates was identified as follows: All B. cereus isolates were resistant to amoxicillin, amoxicillin‑clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone, and ceftazidime, consistent with intrinsic β‑lactam resistance. Beyond this intrinsic profile, 100% of isolates showed phenotypic resistance to tetracycline (100%, n = 16), gentamicin (68.75%, n = 11), erythromycin (62.5%, n = 10), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (31.3%, n = 5). Moreover, the recovered isolates were sensitive to ciprofloxacin and imipenem (100%, n = 16). The examined B. cereus strains showed statistically significant variations in their antimicrobial resistance (p < 0.05).

Presence of toxigenic and antimicrobial resistance genes

Virulence-related genes were identified in B. cereus isolates as follows: hbl complex (hblD: 81.3%, hblA:81.3%, n = 13, and hblC: 75%, n = 12), nhe complex genes (nheB: 62.5%, n = 10, and nheA: 50%, n = 8), pc-plc (100%, n = 16), ent-FM (100%, n = 16), cytK (50%, n = 8), and ces (31.25%, n = 5) virulence genes (Fig. 2a). Eight virulence genes were profiled, as illustrated in Table 2. PCR demonstrated that the tested B. cereus isolates carried the bla1, bla2, tetA, and ermA antibiotic resistance genes with prevalences of 100% (n = 16), 100% (n = 16), 100% (n = 16), and 75% (n = 12), respectively (Fig. 2b). Statistically, no significant differences (p > 0.05) in gene frequencies were observed among isolates. Overall, our data revealed that poultry B. cereus isolates combine extensive toxigenic with multiple resistance determinants.

Phenotypic and genotypic antimicrobial resistance patterns of B. cereus isolates

Among the 100% examined B. cereus isolates, 62.5% of the examined B. cereus strains met the MDR criteria by exhibiting non‑susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more clinically relevant antimicrobial categories and MDR to seven antimicrobial agents (six antimicrobial classes) and encoded bla1, bla2, ermA, and tetA genes. Besides, 12.5% (2/16) of the isolated B. cereus strains were MDR to six antimicrobial agents (five antimicrobial classes) and harbored bla1, bla2, ermA, and tetA genes. Additionally, 25% (4/16) of the recovered strains exhibited multidrug resistance to six antimicrobial agents (five antimicrobial classes) and encoded the bla1, bla2, and tetA genes (Table 3). In this study, the multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index values ranged from 0.6 to 0.7, indicating that the B. cereus strains were derived from high-risk contamination (Table 3).

The clustering and correlation of recovered isolates

Hierarchical clustering based on combined phenotypic and genotypic data segregated isolates into two major branches (I and II) and seven subclusters (Fig. 3), suggesting moderate heterogeneity. Isolates within the same cluster typically display similar patterns of antibiotic resistance along with virulence genes. Several significant positive correlations were detected between virulence genes and resistance characteristics (Fig. 4): hblD and hblA (r = 1); nheA, and nheB (r = 0.77); nheA and ermA (r = 0.58); nheA and E (r = 0.52); nheB and E (r = 0.47); nheB and ermA (r = 0.45); hblC and E (r = 0.15), linking macrolide resistance to specific enterotoxin genes. Additional positive correlations were found between cytK and hblD, hblA (r = 0.48); hblC and hblA, hblD (r = 0.46); hblC and ces (r = 0.39); ces and hblA, hblD (r = 0.32); ces and nheB (r = 0.24); hblC and nheB (r = 0.15). Emetic gene can occur with antibiotic resistance, ces and E (r = 0.24); ces and CN (r = 0.16). Macrolide phenotypic resistance correlated with its genotypic resistance: ermA and E (r = 0.75), as revealed in Fig. 4. Overall, these findings signify that many poultry B. cereus isolates possess linked virulence and resistance traits.

Discussion

B. cereus is a significant concern in poultry meat due to its role in foodborne illness, presence of toxigenic and antibiotic-resistant strains, and implications for food safety and public health. This study aimed to examine the prevalence, groEL gene sequence, and resistance profiles of multidrug-resistant B. cereus in poultry meat. This study emphasizes the presence of toxigenic and antimicrobial resistance genes.

This study revealed that all the isolates exhibited distinct phenotypic characteristics of B. cereus and shared similarities in their biochemical activities, as previously documented in Tunisia by Gdoura-Ben Amor et al. [37]. The total prevalence of B. cereus in the samples examined (14.5%) is comparable to that reported in a previous investigation for poultry products in Egypt (10%) [38]. Similar prevalence values were also documented in chicken samples by Zaki in Egypt [39]. These findings suggest that contaminated poultry meat may pose a risk to human health. previous studies have recorded a relative high prevalence of B. cereus, with rates of 21% in meat products in Egypt as documented by Algammal et al. [12], and 22.5% in beef burgers in Iran as documented by Soleimani et al. [40]. In Japan, approximately 25% of food samples tested positive for B. cereus, with particularly high rates in raw chicken and dairy products by Torii and Ohkubo [41]. A higher frequency of B. cereus (50%) has been documented in retail chicken by Osman et al. [42]. Inadequate hygiene practices during food processing and storage contribute to B. cereus contamination [42].

The occurrence of B. cereus in poultry products is concerning because it can produce diarrhea and emetic toxins, which are responsible for most foodborne outbreaks linked to meat consumption [43]. The results of this investigation indicated that all B. cereus isolates possessed the groEL gene. Wei [44] indicated that groEL serves as a reliable diagnostic biomarker when evaluated in comparison to the gyrB gene for distinguishing B. cereus from other pathogens in food products. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed that the examined B. cereus strains (accession numbers: PX458638 and PX458639) showed notable genetic similarity with other isolates from different sources from many geographical areas, such as the USA, Japan, Taiwan, and Iran, and from diverse origins, particularly humans, indicating zoonotic significance [12]. All retrieved B. cereus strains demonstrated β-hemolysis on sheep blood agar. The results agreed with those previous studies approved by [12, 45, 46] and Hwang and Park in the Republic of Korea, also reported that 27% of their B. cereus isolates showed weak or no hemolysis, whereas 18% exhibited strong hemolysis [46] which highlights variability in hemolytic activity among B. cereus isolates. Foodborne B. cereus strains usually displayed significant hemolytic activity, attributed to inherited factors [47].

Multiple investigations have documented the widespread occurrence of antibiotic-resistant B. cereus in various foods, including raw chicken, milk, rice, meat products, fish, and vegetables [10, 12, 16, 48]. Our findings align with those reported by Owusu-Kwarteng et al. [49], who recorded high resistance to ampicillin (98%), penicillin (100%), amoxicillin (100%), cefepime (100%), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (80%) in milk. Additionally, in German retail market, B. cereus strains from fresh vegetables demonstrated complete resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics, including penicillin G and cefotaxime and frequent resistance to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid and ampicillin [10]. In a previous study in Egypt, reported that B. cereus strains were resistant to ampicillin, amoxicillin, tetracycline, cefepime, ceftriaxone, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole by Algammal [12]. In Japan, B. cereus isolates show high resistance to ampicillin, oxacillin, penicillin, cefoxitin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, erythromycin, and tetracycline [48, 49]. A previous study in China documented that B. cereus isolates recovered from food products were resistant to penicillin, cephalothin, ampicillin, cefoxitin, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid [50]. This study demonstrates that ciprofloxacin and imipenem exhibit strong antibacterial effects against B. cereus strains isolated from chicken meat. These results align with those of [37], who reported that all B. cereus isolates in Tunisia were susceptible to ciprofloxacin. In addition, stated that B. cereus isolates are susceptible to imipenem. Fiedler et al. [10] in Germany indicated that 99.3% and 93.9% of B. cereus strains obtained were highly susceptible to ciprofloxacin and imipenem, respectively.

The primary virulence factors include genes encoding non-hemolytic enterotoxin (nheA, and nheB), hemolysin BL (hblA, hblC, hblD), cytotoxin K (cytK), enterotoxin FM (entFM), and emetic toxin encoded by ces [12], which play a fundamental role in its pathogenic potential and are the primary reason for its significance as a foodborne pathogen [51]. The combination of hblA and hblD was observed in 81.25% of the tested strains, which agrees with the results of Yu in China [50] and Fiedler [10] in Germany. The relatively low prevalence of nhe genes in our isolates is nearly similar to the findings of a previous study [52], which also reported a low prevalence of nhe genes (31.03%). Our study showed that genes linked to the NHE complex occurred less frequently than those findings from [43, 53]. Previous studies frequently use nheA and nheB as genetic markers of Nhe complex in Bacillus cereus, which is essential for hemolytic activity. Although nheC is commonly eliminated due to its lower expression [54, 55]. In contrast, the detection rates for entFM, cytk were 100%, 50%, respectively, which were nearly similar to those reported in previous investigations of B. cereus from food sources. Previous studies reported lower entFM prevalence (67%) and higher cytK prevalence (75%) and reported entFM gene was detected in all B. cereus strains, and lower cytK gene (68%) [49, 56]. The concurrent presence of ent-FM (100%) and cytK (50%) in MDR poultry isolates may increase the risk of severe diarrheal disease. Our results showed that 31.25% of ces gene was detected, which is consistent with findings reported in other previous studies [57, 58]. All the recovered B. cereus isolates carried two or more enterotoxigenic genes, underscoring their importance to public health as a cause of serious food poisoning in humans [49]. Discrepancies may indicate variations in sample type, geographic region, methodological approaches, or local selection pressures.

In this study, 62.5% of the examined B. cereus strains were MDR and exhibited non‑susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more clinically relevant antimicrobial categories. In this study, the multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index values ranged from 0.6 to 0.7, indicating that the obtained B. cereus strains were exposed to high-risk contamination. Phenotypically, B. cereus is intrinsically resistant to many penicillins and cephalosporins. Genomic studies have identified multiple chromosomally encoded β‑lactamase genes, including class A (e.g., bla1) and class B metallo‑β‑lactamases (e.g., bla2) [59]. Similarly, the resistance of B. cereus strains to tetracycline is mostly attributed to tetA gene, while their resistance to erythromycin is supported by the ermA gene [35]. The findings prove the presence of MDR B. cereus in poultry meat, indicating that food may serve as a primary means of transmission of foodborne MDR B. cereus to human consumers [60]. The indiscriminate use of antimicrobials in both the agricultural and healthcare sectors promotes the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains [16].

The correlation between genetic resistance and antibiotic resistance phenotypes has been observed across multiple studies and antibiotic classes. For example, Algammal [12] observed that predicted beta-lactam resistance corresponded with observed genetic resistance. Several studies have highlighted that virulence genes, such as nhe, hbl, cytK, and ces, often occur alongside resistance determinants in B. cereus genomes [61]. Resistance and virulence are commonly linked in Gram-positive bacteria to enable survival under antibiotic stress [62]. The strong correlation between ermA and phenotypic erythromycin resistance, as well as its relationships with nheA and other virulence genes, suggests that macrolide resistance is genetically connected to toxigenic circumstances. The link between ces and enterotoxin genes and resistance phenotypes suggests that some poultry isolates may have emetic potential and multidrug resistance, which poses concerns for food safety. These patterns suggest that B. cereus in poultry meat could serve as a reservoir of virulence and resistance factors, enabling the establishment of virulent strains by Algammal [12] .

In summary, this study highlights the increased prevalence of resistant B. cereus strains in poultry meat in Port Said, Egypt, underscoring the importance of monitoring food for pathogenic B. cereus. The genetic similarity between these foodborne strains and human clinical isolates highlights the potential for zoonotic transmission within the One Health framework. These findings necessitate the implementation of more stringent hygiene protocols in poultry processing and the establishment of a continuous molecular surveillance program to monitor the evolution of antibiotic resistance in foodborne pathogens. Our phenotypic and genotypic findings, in line with current treatment guidance for B. cereus infections, indicate that β‑lactams are not recommended. Imipenem and ciprofloxacin exhibit remarkable antibacterial activity against the isolated strains. Hierarchical clustering revealed moderate diversity across two main clusters and seven subclusters, with significant correlations between certain virulence/resistance genes and antimicrobial phenotypes.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. Sampling was restricted to local supermarkets in Port Said Governorate and may not be representative of poultry across Egypt. Future studies should expand to national/multi-season sampling, include whole-genome sequencing of a larger number of isolates to better understand population structure, virulence/resistance genes, and transmission dynamics, and include quantitative toxin assays and cytotoxicity testing.
