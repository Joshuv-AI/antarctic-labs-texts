Empirical estimate for the indirect healthcare costs of sickle cell disease from a retrospective cohort study in England between 2007 and 2019
Authors: Caoimhe T Rice, Martin Besser, Christine L Baker, Carmine Colavecchia, Jennifer A Davidson, Jeremy P Brown, Giovanna Tedesco Barcelos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSickle cell disease (SCD) imposes a considerable burden on patients and their families while also exerting economic pressure on high-prevalence countries. This retrospective cohort study estimated the societal cost of SCD in England, including productivity loss (PL) and loss of future income (LFI).MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, individuals aged ≥ 12 years with SCD were identified between 01 April 2007 and 31 March 2019 using linked healthcare data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink Aurum, Hospital Episode Statistics, and Office for National Statistics death registrations. PL was calculated based on time spent hospitalised, attending healthcare appointments, or on certified sick days, using average wages for England stratified by age for the same year the PL occurred. LFI due to unemployment, medical retirement, or death was calculated from the age at first occurrence until age 68 years. Average annual earnings for FY2021/2022 were applied by age decile and discounted at 3.5%. Expected income increased with age decile.ResultsOverall, 9,272 patients with SCD were included, with a mean age of 35.5 years and a median follow-up of 7 years. Mean annualised (per patient per year [PPPY]) productive time lost due to hospital stays was 5.5 days. Individuals spent 2.7 days PPPY in outpatient care, 3.6 days PPPY attending primary care appointments, 3.3 days PPPY in emergency departments, and 2.2 days PPPY sick at home. The mean annualised PL for these sickness and healthcare attendances was £1,821 PPPY, equivalent to 3.1 weeks of average UK weekly earnings in 2021. For the 6% of patients (555/9,272) who experienced an LFI event, they were estimated to lose an average of £511,236 over their lifetime.ConclusionsPatients with SCD and their families had an estimated PL equivalent to 3.1 weeks of average UK weekly earnings in 2021 per individual annually. For patients with SCD who died or were incapacitated before retirement age, lifetime LFI was more than half a million pounds.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14847-x.Keywords: Cost, Hemoglobinopathy, Indirect cost, Loss of future income, Productivity loss, Sickle cell disease, Societal burden

Background

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a group of hereditary haemoglobinopathies that results in considerable burden for patients and their families, while also exerting rising economic pressure worldwide [1, 2]. SCD is caused by β-globin gene mutations, resulting in sickle-shaped erythrocytes [3, 4]. The major clinical features of the disease are driven by haemolytic anaemia and vaso-occlusion with ischaemia-reperfusion injury [3]. In the SHAPE study, symptoms associated with anaemia, particularly fatigue, and vaso-occlusive crises (VOC), particularly pain crises, were most common in adults with SCD, followed by low mood or depression and headaches [5]. Acute VOC events, such as priapism and acute chest syndrome, require urgent intervention in hospital [1, 2, 6]. Over an individual’s life with SCD, patients may develop chronic complications such as osteonecrosis, pulmonary hypertension, or chronic kidney disease [1, 7, 8]. Regular transfusion therapy (RTT) and automated red cell exchange therapy (ARCET) are used to manage symptoms and prevent development of acute and chronic complications [9–11], with the latter having an estimated annual procedure cost of more than £40,000 [12].

Patients with SCD may miss workdays due to illness or the need to attend healthcare facilities for monitoring or treatment, resulting in lost productivity and lost wages [5, 13–15]. Frequent periods of sickness absence can lead to a reduced ability to obtain and retain regular employment and may lead to long-term unemployment [13, 16–18]. While the direct economic burden of SCD and its complications on healthcare systems has been reported in several countries [19–23], the indirect costs of this chronic, inherited disease for patients and wider society are less clear. Observational studies from the USA found individuals with SCD are less likely to be employed than their siblings without SCD [17], and that increased frequency of pain is associated with unemployment [24]. A qualitative study from the USA showed that 76% of patients at a single centre were unemployed [16], which is similar to estimates from a province in Brazil, where 72% of individuals with SCD were not in active employment.

Other chronic diseases affecting younger adults with flares in disease activity, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and multiple sclerosis (MS), and genetic conditions such as cystic fibrosis (CF) have substantial labour-market losses, though lower than reported for SCD. In Californian and Dutch SLE patient survey studies, more than half of patients reported being unemployed (55% and 59%, respectively) [25, 26]. A European multi-country registry study for MS reported unemployment to be between 47% (in Poland) and 80% in Sweden [27]. Finally, a UK registry-based study also reported 50% employment amongst patients with CF [28].

To our knowledge, only one previous estimate of lost future income (LFI) for SCD has been published to date and was estimated using simulated cohorts in the USA [18].

Productivity loss (PL) and LFI are important components in understanding the societal cost of SCD, particularly in the context of a genetic disease where lost income may have intergenerational impacts, such as reduced opportunities for education and diminished ability to own and transfer assets such as a home or business. Quantifying the societal impact of SCD can help inform evidence-based policies and initiatives to better support those affected by SCD.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study included patients with SCD between 1 April 2007 and 31 March 2019 and used data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum dataset [29, 30], linked with Hospital Episode Statistics (HES), the national secondary care activity dataset, including Admitted Patient Care (APC) [31], Outpatient (OP), and Accident and Emergency (A&E) datasets, the Office for National Statistics (ONS) death registrations [32], and the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD).

The CPRD Aurum database collates electronic health records of patient demographics, diagnoses (coded using Systemized Nomenclature of Medicine – Clinical Terms [SNOMED-CT]), laboratory tests requested in primary care, referrals to specialty care, and prescriptions from over 50 million patient lives across > 1,800 primary care practices (PCP) [30]; 7 million patients were actively registered in September 2018 [33]. HES contains clinical information about secondary care activity, including diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) [31], operations coded according to OPCS-4 [34], and dates and methods of hospital admissions, discharges, and attendances.

These data were obtained under licence from the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency following study protocol approval by the CPRD Independent Scientific Advisory Committee (Study Protocol Number: 21_000422). CPRD has ethics approval from the Health Research Authority to support research using anonymised patient data; therefore, further ethics approval was not required. Access to CPRD and linked datasets must be requested by application to CPRD. The analysis code is available upon reasonable request.

Study population

Patients were eligible if they had at least one record of SCD in either CPRD or HES during the study period and were aged 12 year or older on their first record of SCD in the dataset, defined as the index date (SCD clinical codes are provided in Additional file 1). Patients were excluded if they had a haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) prior to the index date (Fig. 1).

To derive healthcare resource use for the calculation of PL and to identify LFI events, eligible patients were followed from their first record of SCD (index date) to the earliest date of HSCT, death, administrative censor (transfer out of PCP or PCP ceased contributing to CPRD), or the end of the study period (31 March 2019).

Productivity loss

PL was estimated from time spent seeking healthcare or sickness at home requiring PCP certification during study follow-up. Productive time lost was summed and the monetary value was applied per the average hourly wage for the UK population, stratified by 10-year age bands, as reported by the ONS [35]. The average duration of a full-time working week of 37.5 h (March–May 2019) was applied to all patients [36], made up of five 7.5-hour workdays.

To calculate time seeking healthcare, proportions of a workday were assigned to each type of healthcare interaction, as per Choi and Lee 2019 [37]. One full productive day (7.5 h) per bed day for inpatient (IP) admissions or daycare attendances, and one-third of a productive day (2.5 h) was assigned per outpatient (OP) appointment [37]. In addition, two-thirds of a productive day (5 h) was attributed to attendance at emergency departments, and one-quarter of a productive day (1.875 h) to appointments with a PCP.

Non-healthcare-related sick days were counted through sick note certificates (Med 3 certificate) recorded by PCPs and captured in CPRD. In England, patients may self-certify illness for up to seven consecutive days (including non-working days), after which a sick note from a PCP is required to obtain employer or statutory sick pay. Thus sickness of fewer than seven days will not be captured for productivity loss. From available data, it was not possible to determine the exact number of days covered by a Med 3 and 10 working days of lost earnings were attributed to each Med 3 certificate. If the patient had a Med 3 reporting return to work with amendments (either to their working hours or their duties), five working days were attributed, assuming 50% productivity or 50% of normal hours.

All healthcare attendances were assumed to take place on working days; weekends were not accounted for within the analysis. Where IP admissions lasted more than 5 days, length of stay was divided by 5/7 to reflect the typical working pattern in the UK [38].

Patients aged 12 to 15 years were assumed to be accompanied by an adult caregiver to all healthcare events. This included IP admissions where parents are encouraged to stay with their children. Therefore, in this age group, time spent attending healthcare was costed as per the hourly wage for a 30–39-year-old, assuming that this time was representative of caregiver time lost [38].

The underlying rate of unemployment was accounted for by multiplying the product described above by the proportion of English adults in employment. The costs were inflated to 2021 UK pounds using the Consumer Price Inflation [39] rate of inflation (see Supplementary Materials Eq. 1, in Additional File 1, for further detail). A societal perspective was taken, assuming that society’s willingness to pay for a day of lost productivity is the same as the average societal daily wage in England.

Loss of future income

LFI consisted of earnings lost due to unemployment, medical retirement, or death. Death was identified from the ONS death register during the study period, and retirement due to medical reasons was identified from the primary care record in CPRD. Unemployment was similarly defined using any PCP record of unemployment, including applications for unemployment benefits. Patients with any record of unemployment were assumed to remain unemployed, as records of re-entry into the job market were unavailable. Although this is a strong assumption, previous work has shown that maintaining long-term employment is challenging, with 30%–80% of participants with SCD unemployed and/or not looking for work [16, 17]. LFI due to death was based on the observed patient deaths in the dataset during the study period.

A retirement age of 68 years—the predicted UK state retirement age for a 36-year-old in 2021—was applied to the whole cohort. Average yearly earnings by age and gender were used, assuming all patients would have worked full time at 37.5 h per week and 52 weeks per year (to include paid holiday) [38]. To avoid double counting, lost earnings were assigned to the earliest event (unemployment, retirement, or death).

The method was adapted from Choi and Lee 2019 [37]. For each patient, average annual income for each projected year of financially productive time lost was multiplied by the employment rate in 2021. This was summed for each year of age lost and then summed for all patients to provide the total for the cohort (see Supplementary Materials Eq. 2, in Additional File 1, for further detail). Costs were reported in 2021 UK pounds, and future lost earnings were discounted at an annual rate of 3.5% [40].

LFI sensitivity analysis

As 75% of patients in our study were aged less than 44 years (median age 33 years, interquartile range [IQR] 21–44) at index, LFI estimated based only on observed deaths during the 12-year study period would potentially lead to an overestimation of the true LFI per person experiencing an LFI event amongst this cohort. This is because most patients were not observed during the later life stages when they may be expected to die, and those who died young would have more life years lost. The life expectancy for the cohort was extrapolated, and the median survival was found to be 78 years, with 25% of patients dying before age 67 (see Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Fig. 1, in Additional File 1, for the extrapolated survival curve). Previous work estimated a median survival of 67 years amongst patients homozygous for HbSS or HbS/HbSβ0 thalassaemia [41].

In this sensitivity analysis, the potential LFI amongst the cohort was re-estimated based on patients dying at age 67 years—one year before the retirement age—unless they had an LFI event before this age or were known to survive beyond this in the study period. Rates of unemployment or medical retirement were not extrapolated in the sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary Tables 2–4, in Additional File 1, for code lists identifying SCD, sick notes, and unemployment).

The remaining (130/555, 23.4%) were due to unemployment or medical retirement, at a median age of 39 years (IQR 26–50). The projected total financially productive time lost for the cohort was 13,056 years, corresponding to £283,224,914 in total lost income. Amongst those who experienced an LFI event, the mean LFI was £511,236 (SD £218,753) per lifetime. The average LFI for the cohort was £30,546, which is approximately 1 year of earnings in 2021 (£31,437) [38].

A total of 9,290 financially productive years were lost due to deaths, resulting in an estimated £206,841,873 of lost income, with an average of £486,687 per person who died. Amongst those who died, 85% (362/425) had an SCD complication; these patients died at a median age of 48 years (IQR 37–57). Although fewer patients without SCD complications died compared to those with complications (2%, 63/4064 vs. 7%, 362/5208, respectively), the median age at death was 8 years younger (40 years, IQR 28 to 52) and the lifetime LFI per person was £75,000 higher amongst those with no SCD complications (£552,365 vs. £475,257 with complications) (Table 4).

Long-term unemployment and early medical retirement resulted in a total loss of 3,766 productive years and £76,383,041 in LFI amongst the cohort. Patients were unemployed or retired at a median age of 39 years (IQR 26–50). The mean LFI due to these events was £592,117 (SD £175,751) per lifetime. Amongst those without SCD complications, fewer patients were unemployed or retired (0.35%, 14/4064 vs. 2.2%, 116/5208) compared to those with SCD complications; however, where these patients were unemployed, the median age at first LFI event was 7 years younger (33 years vs. 40 years, respectively, Table 4).

In the sensitivity analysis (where patients were assumed to die at age 67 years unless they died or survived beyond this age during the study period), 4,529 patients (48.8%) had an LFI, including 4,399 deaths. The projected total financially productive years lost were 21,683, with a projected total LFI of £411,230,200. The mean LFI amongst those with an event was £90,799 per lifetime, 2.9 times the average annual income in 2021 (£31,437) [38].

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis, assuming patients died at age 67 years unless they were observed to die or survive beyond this age during the study period, estimated that 21,683 total productive years would be lost amongst the cohort, with a total discounted present value LFI of £411,230,200. In total, 4,529 patients (48.8%) had an LFI, including 4,399 deaths. The corresponding LFI per person with an LFI was £90,799, equal to 2.9 times the average income in 2021 (£31,437) [35].

Discussion

This study found that individuals with SCD and their households may face considerable indirect costs, including productivity losses from illness or attending healthcare and lost family income over time resulting from death or unemployment. A key strength of this study is the use of large, nationally representative healthcare datasets of high quality for research [33, 43] to derive estimates for PL and LFI. From primary and secondary healthcare data linked with death registrations, 9,272 patients with SCD were identified, similar to the 10,856 registered with the NHR in England in 2018/19 [44], however, the true number of patients with SCD in England is unknown. CPRD included approximately 13% of the English population as actively registered patients in 2019 [33]. As of 2024, the NHR has 16,228 active registrants with SCD; however, it does not yet have good coverage in all areas of England, with fewer than 10 registrants in many regions e.g., London South West and the English Midlands [45]. The CPRD-HES datasets do not contain genetic information, and this study cohort was therefore likely to be heterogenous, including those with HbSS, HbSC, and HbS/β0. While these results may be generalisable to all patients with SCD in England, this heterogeneity masks differences in disease severity amongst patients with different SCD genotypes. We acknowledge that stratifying the population by history of any or no SCD complications gives equal weight to all complications of SCD and that some complications may be associated with greater morbidity and mortality than others.

The use of a single diagnostic code to identify patients with SCD, may have introduced some misclassification, including the potential inclusion of individuals with sickle cell trait or miscoded diagnoses and is a potential limitation of this study. This approach is, however, consistent with prior UK studies using HES data [46] and is likely to include a sensitive and heterogenous SCD population. This may have led to a small degree of overestimation of the study population and should be considered when interpreting the results.

The gender imbalance in Table 2 is unexpected for an autosomal inherited genetic disease. We suspect this is due to routine maternal screening for SCD in the UK leading to identification of greater numbers of women. As the UK has had newborn screening for SCD since 2003, we checked gender balance amongst adolescent patients in the cohort and found it to be more balanced with 53% female.

The estimated annual number of days lost due to sickness or attending healthcare in this study (14.9 PPPY) is similar to days with ‘disability pension’ (15–23 days PPPY) from a Swedish national registry SCD cohort [41, 47]. Most other estimates of SCD-associated PL have been based on patient surveys, which relied on self-reported workdays missed [5, 13, 14, 48–50]. In the multi-country SHAPE study of 919 respondents [5], patients reported missing an average of 7.5 days per month from school or work, equivalent to 90 days per year, six times higher than reported in this study. Similarly, a US single-centre cross-sectional survey of 192 patients estimated a loss of 8 workdays per month [14]. These patient-reported estimates suggest that true PL may be significantly higher than the empirical estimates presented here.

Annualised productive time lost was approximately three times higher in patients with SCD complications than in those without (21 vs. 7 days PPPY, respectively), which were 2–3 times lower than findings from an analysis of the US IBM MarketScan Medicaid database [51], which reported 55–62 productive days lost PPPY amongst patients with SCD and end organ damage (EOD) and 21–25 days PPPY amongst those without EOD. However, the definition of EOD used in the MarketScan analysis was limited to chronic conditions associated with disability and high healthcare resource utilisation (stroke, pulmonary hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and end-stage renal disease), whereas a broader group of SCD complications were included in this study.

To further contextualise these results, annual productivity losses have been estimated at approximately $8,659 per working-age patient with SLE in California [26], 17% of the median household income of $51,815 in 2004 [52]. Mean annual excess productivity loss per person with MS in Sweden was estimated as €13,173 [53] which is 4.5-fold higher than the non-affected population, and the equivalent of 5 months’ salary (monthly salary 33,100 SEK in 2018 [54], $2,586 when converted to Purchasing Power Standard using Eurostat AIC PPP indexed on the EU27 [55]). The high productivity loss is consistent with high unemployment (77%) among patients with MS in Sweden [27].

PL may have been underestimated in this study for three reasons. First, the available data did not capture self-certified sickness; furthermore, the assumption that sickness requiring a sick note lasted 10 days’ duration is much shorter than the patient-reported loss of workdays discussed above. In addition, periods of sickness lasting fewer than 7 consecutive days will not have been captured by med 3 forms. Second, the hours assigned for attendances at primary care, specialist OP appointments, or emergency care may be an underestimate of actual time spent seeking healthcare in the English NHS. Third, estimation of caregiver PL for patients aged 12–15 years was costed at the wages of an adult aged 30–39 years. This may have led to an underestimation of lost earnings amongst parental caregivers, as the average age of parenthood increased to 31 for women and 34 for men in 2020, which would make a mother 43 when her child turned 12 [56] and average earnings increase with age.

By using the national average wages for England for both PL and LFI, this study may overestimate the actual costs, particularly as 59% of patients lived in areas within the two most deprived IMD quintiles. However, use of average wages reflects the opportunity cost for society, as the costs of lost productivity can fall on the individual, the employer, or the government. Other estimates of PL amongst patients with SCD based on the educational attainment of individuals had lower estimated annual PL ($586) than reported here [13]; although, SCD itself may disrupt education and affect highest educational attainment, biasing these PL estimates downwards. Furthermore, prevalence of SCD is not evenly distributed across regions of England [57], and regional differences in earnings have not been accounted for.

A minority of patients had an LFI event (6%, n = 555) and the estimated LFI per person with an LFI event (£511,236) is similar to Lubeck et al. 2019 [18], who reported a lifetime LFI of $695,000 from a simulated SCD cohort in the US [18], equivalent to £489,800 in 2021, based on purchasing power parity for 2019 [58] and inflated to 2021 pounds using Consumer Price Inflation [39]. Although more patients with SCD complications had an LFI event (9.2%, 478/5208 vs. 1.9%, 77/4064), patients with no SCD complications and an LFI event had a higher mean LFI. This may be explained by patients dying prior to developing complications as a lower median age of death was observed amongst those without complications (40 vs. 48 years), with a 6-percentage point difference in the proportion of LFI events due to death (82% vs. 76%, respectively).

Disease-specific lifetime earnings lost due premature death/ long term unemployment appears to be a major evidence gap for comparable conditions to SCD. Whilst we postulated that there may be intergenerational impacts of PL and LFI, the study was not designed to assess this; though most patients (59%) lived in the most deprived area quintiles, 19% points higher than expected if patients were equally distributed with respect to deprivation. Most primary studies use family-level or area-level socioeconomic proxies such as a paper by Taylor-Robinson et al. 2013 [28] which showed an association between deprivation category and employment in CF.

Identification of painful crises and acute chest syndrome was not possible in this study as there are no reliable International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes to identify these from HES [7, 46]. These are common VOC complications of SCD that can impair productivity at work and activities of daily living [14, 15] and this could have led to a misclassification of some patients with VOCs as having no SCD complications, partially explaining the earlier age of death amongst those with no SCD complications, which may have led to an underestimation of LFI and PL amongst those with SCD complications.

The sensitivity analysis, sought to address the lack of information on death, beyond the end of the study period, providing a range for the actual value of LFI per person with an event of between £90,799 for the sensitivity and £511,236 based only on observed events. An additional 45% of patients were assumed to die at 67 years, increasing all deaths to 49% of the total population, higher than the 25% estimated from the extrapolated survival analysis (see Supplementary Fig. 1 in Additional File 1). However, selecting which patients would have died would have introduced selection bias. By assuming that all eligible patients die at 67 and not earlier, one year per patient is added to the LFI when, some patients would have died earlier, with increased LFI at younger ages.

A minority of patients (1.4%, n = 130) were determined to be long-term unemployed or retired early during the study period. This is likely to be an underestimate as the validity of unemployment recording in CPRD data is unknown and the proportion was very low compared with the 31% estimated in the SHAPE survey [5] and a two-centre, cross-sectional survey from the US [48]. The proportion of patients with SCD out of work has been reported as high as 56% [14]. For longer-term employment, a sibling comparison between those with and without SCD showed a statistically significant difference between the number of participants with SCD (10%, n = 2) who had been employed for 2 years compared with the sibling group (75%, n = 11). Whilst cross-sectional estimates may not reflect long-term or permanent unemployment, it is likely that long-term unemployment has been underestimated in this study, despite inclusion of codes for disability living allowance where people may work while receiving it. In this study, the methodology of Choi and Lee 2019 [37] was extended to include the national unemployment rate. Given the chronic nature of SCD and its impact on the ability to stay in long-term employment, the national rate may have underestimated the actual rate of unemployment amongst patients with SCD. Patients with a record of unemployment in their primary care record were assumed to never return to work, as if or when patients returned to the job market was not possible to identify from CPRD. Whilst this is a strong assumption, and some patients may have returned to work; it is likely that this method captured a minority of patients who were unemployed as patients may have sought support from sources other than their PCP. Patient eligibility for unemployment benefits also depends on their immigration status [59], which could have been a factor in underestimating unemployment in this cohort. This descriptive study did not account for potential confounding, thus estimates of PL and LFI are reported amongst patients with SCD and are not presented as being wholly attributable to SCD.

Conclusion

This study showed that patients with SCD and their families may incur substantial indirect costs in terms of PL for illness or seeking healthcare as well as LFI due to death or unemployment (6%, n = 555). Estimated annual mean PL was £1,821 per patient, equivalent to 3 weeks’ average wages. Patients who experienced death, unemployment, or early medical retirement, and their dependents, were estimated to lose more than half a million pounds of future income over their financially productive lifetime.
