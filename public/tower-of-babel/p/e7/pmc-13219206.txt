Clinicopathological characteristics of a rare group of chronic active Epstein-Barr virus disease involving the gastrointestinal tract
Authors: Xiaodan Zheng, Yuanyuan Zheng, Jianlan Xie, Xiaowen Huang, Xiaojing Teng, Yanlin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastrointestinal involvement in chronic active Epstein-Barr virus disease (CAEBVD) is rare, and no large-scale case series has been reported in the literature to date. Due to lack of specific clinicopathological manifestations, this disease is prone to being missed or misdiagnosed as other intestinal disorders. The aim of our study was to describe the clinicopathological features of this disease and identify the key diagnostic and differential diagnostic criteria, in order to facilitate accurate and timely diagnosis and effective treatment.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 44 cases of CAEBVD with gastrointestinal involvement. We summarised the clinicopathological and molecular genetic characteristics of these cases using immunohistochemical staining, EBER in situ hybridisation, double-labelling staining, and TCR gene rearrangement.ResultsTwenty-six cases were male and 18 were female. Their mean age was 27 years, and the mean disease duration was 15 months. The main manifestations were abdominal pain and diarrhoea (75%), fever (68.2%), splenomegaly (88.6%), lymphadenopathy (59.1%), hepatomegaly (38.6%), and anaemia (72.7%). Most cases (88.6%) presented with erythema, erosion, or superficial ulcers characterised by multiple small lesions. The lesions were primarily confined to the mucosal layer. The tumour cells were mainly small to medium in size, with mild to moderate nuclear irregularity. All cases tested positive for CD3, with CD5 antigen loss observed in 45.5% of cases, and most cases (76.9%) predominantly exhibited CD4-positive T cells. Some cases (31.8%) were positive for CD56. All cases were EBER-positive, with 20–200 positive cells per high-power field. The majority of cases (77.3%) were T-cell infections. Six out of ten showed TCR monoclonal rearrangement. Of the 39 cases, a total of 20 (51.3%) died, with a mean survival time of 5.9 months. Patients who received allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) had a survival rate of 76.5%.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that gastrointestinal CAEBVD lacks distinctive clinical and pathological characteristics, and peripheral blood EBV DNA screening combined with EBER detection of biopsy tissues is the most important factor in avoiding misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis. Introduction

The Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a member of the gamma herpesvirus subfamily. It is a double-stranded DNA virus that was originally discovered by Epstein and Barr in a Burkitt’s lymphoma cell line in 1964 [1]. EBV is ubiquitous in the general population, with over 90% of adults testing seropositive for EBV antibodies. EBV can infect a wide range of cell types, primarily B lymphocytes. The EBV envelope glycoproteins GP350/220 and GP42 bind, respectively, to the B-cell surface CD21 receptor and to major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II, also known as HLA II), playing a crucial role in B-cell infection [2, 3]. EBV can also infect T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells, though the infection pathways and underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Current research suggests that haematopoietic stem cells can be infected with EBV; subsequent differentiation and proliferation of these infected stem cells give rise to T- and NK-cell proliferative disorders [4]. Additionally, some scholars have reported that NK cells can acquire the EBV receptor CD21 via synapse-mediated transfer from B cells, thereby enabling EBV to bind to NK cells [5]. EBV may also infect non-lymphoid cells, most frequently epithelial cells. In rare cases, it can infect mesenchymal cells in immunocompromised individuals. Following primary infection, the virus establishes a latent infection state in memory B lymphocytes, and the host remains a lifelong carrier. In instances where the immune system is compromised or triggered by specific risk factors, the latent EBV becomes reactivated, leading to the resumption of active replication and the manifestation of the associated disease.

Diseases associated with EBV infection are relatively complex, encompassing benign, borderline, and malignant lesions. Some present with prominent systemic symptoms and follow a self-limiting clinical course, such as infectious mononucleosis (IM) [6]. Some have a well-established association with malignancies, including Burkitt lymphoma, NK/T-cell lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, as well as nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer, and EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours. Others are linked to a spectrum of lymphoproliferative disorders that may progress to lymphoma, which is referred to as EBV-associated/positive lymphoproliferative disorders (EBV+-LPD)[7], such as lymphomatoid granulomatosis (LYG), lymphoid proliferations and lymphomas associated with immune deficiency and dysregulation (IDD-LPD/lymphoma), and chronic active EBV disease (CAEBVD), such disorders pose diagnostic challenges for pathologists.

CAEBVD is restricted to cases with T-cell and NK-cell phenotypes and does not include B-cell cases [7]. It predominantly occurs in Asia and Latin America, such as Japan, South Korea, China, and Mexico, and is rarely reported in Western countries and Africa [8, 9]. Clinically, it presents with chronic and recurrent IM-like symptoms, including fever, lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly, which persist for more than 3 months. The condition predominantly affects children and adolescents [10]; however, in recent years, an increasing number of adult cases have been documented, which tend to follow a more aggressive clinical course and carry a poorer prognosis [11–13]. Based on the extent of lesion involvement, CAEBVD can be classified into cutaneous and systemic types. The cutaneous type includes hydroa vacciniforme lymphoproliferative disorder (HV-LPD) and severe mosquito bite allergy (SMBA), most of which eventually progress to systemic CAEBVD; systemic CAEBVD can involve multiple organ systems, including the hematological, digestive, respiratory, mucocutaneous, cardiovascular, and central nervous systems, amongst others. It may also result in life-threatening complications, such as hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), massive gastrointestinal ulcer bleeding or perforation, aneurysms, and refractory lymphoma [14].

Gastrointestinal CAEBVD is rare, and most of the previous literature [15–17] consists of case reports. The disease can either present as systemic CAEBVD involving the gastrointestinal tract or primary gastrointestinal CAEBVD. Endoscopically, it lacks specific features, manifesting as multiple shallow, small, irregular ulcers, which may be accompanied by longitudinal ulcers, primarily affecting both the colon and small intestine simultaneously. It is often liable to be misdiagnosed as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), especially in cases where gastrointestinal symptoms are the initial presentation. Severe complications such as haemorrhage, perforation, and multiple organ failure are common [18]. In addition, the prognosis and treatment regimens of gastrointestinal CAEBVD and IBD are completely different. Therefore, it is both essential and of great significance to correctly recognise and diagnose this disease. The present retrospective study summarised the clinical and pathological characteristics and prognosis of 44 cases of gastrointestinal CAEBVD, with the objective of enhancing the comprehension of pathologists with regard to this rare group of cases, improving the accuracy of pathological diagnosis, averting misdiagnosis and missed diagnoses, and thus guiding clinicians to provide more precise and effective treatment for patients, thereby improving their prognosis.

Materials and methods

Cases selection

A total of 216 archived cases of gastrointestinal EBV-associated diseases, diagnosed between July 2018 and July 2025, were retrieved from the database of the Department of Pathology, Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University/Lymphoma Diagnosis and Research Center, Beijing Clinical Medical Research Institute. Ultimately, 44 patients with gastrointestinal CAEBVD were enrolled in the study from this initial cohort.

The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: ① Biopsy specimens were obtained from the gastrointestinal tract, including the oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, and colon; ② diagnosis of CAEBVD was confirmed in accordance with established diagnostic criteria; and ③ complete clinical data and follow-up records were available. The diagnosis of CAEBVD was based on the revised criteria issued by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare Study Group in 2022 [19]: ① persistent or recurrent IM-like symptoms lasting ≥ 3 months; ② elevated EBV load in affected tissues and/or peripheral blood (the updated guidelines recommend a cutoff value of ≥ 10,000 IU/ml for EBV DNA in whole blood); ③ presence of EBV-infected T cells or natural killer (NK) cells in affected tissues and/or peripheral blood; and ④ the chronic condition could not be attributed to other known disease entities.

The exclusion criteria included concurrent known immunodeficiency disorders or other underlying malignancies.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed manually on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue for immunophenotypic analysis. The EliVision two-step method was used. Sections were stained with monoclonal antibodies against CD21, CD20, cytoplasmic CD3, CD5, CD4, CD8, CD56, granzyme B, TIA1, Ki-67, and others; cytoplasmic CD3 was the product of Dako, and the rest of the antibodies used were from Maxim-Bio (Cat. No. KIT-5910/5931), Fuzhou, China. Positive and negative controls were analysed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

EBER‑in situ hybridisation

The EBV probe in situ hybridisation (ISH) kit (Triplex International Biosciences (China) Co. Ltd., Fuzhou, China) was used to detect EBERs. Details of this procedure were described in our previous report [20]. The positive signal was located in the nucleus and was brown in colour. EBV-positive extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma was used as a positive control, and EBV-negative tonsillar lymphoid tissue hyperplasia was used as a negative control. EBER-positive cells were counted: the area with the densest positive cells was first determined at low magnification, and then the positive cells were counted at high magnification.

Double-labelled ISH and IHC staining

An IHC plus EBER-ISH dual-staining technique was performed using the Leica Bond MAX autostainer (Leica, Melbourne, Australia). Sections (2-µm thick) were first stained for EBER with DAB staining (brown), then for CD3 (LN10, RTU; Lecia), CD20 (L26, RTU; Lecia), followed by visualisation of the red staining after application of amino-ethylcarbazole (Bond Polymer Refine Red Detection kit, Leica) as chromogen. Methods currently used to identify lymphocyte subtypes infected by EBV include lymphocyte sorting, EBV DNA PCR detection, and double-labelled ISH and IHC staining. Of these, double-labelled ISH and IHC staining is the most intuitive and accurate basis for diagnosis.

T-cell receptor gene clonality analysis

T-cell receptor (TCR) gene rearrangement analysis was performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) based on the “Biomed-2” primers (InVivo Scribe Technologies, San Diego, CA, USA) [21]. DNA was extracted from FFPE tissue samples using the TIANamp FFPE DNA Kit (DP331) (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). For the gene rearrangement assay, PCR was carried out in a 25-µl volume containing 22.5-µl of master mix, 0.13-µl of Ampli Taq Gold DNA polymerase, and 100 ng of genomic DNA. The cycling profile used for all reactions was as follows: 95 °C for 7 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, 60 °C 45 s, and 72 °C 90 s; and a 10-min final extension at 72 °C. After amplification, PCR products were denatured at 94 °C for 5 min followed by a quick chill to reanneal the PCR products at 4 °C for at least 60 min. The PCR products were electrophoresed in 6% polyacrylamide gels (BioRad) in 1 × TBE buffer at 120 V for approximately 65 min. The gel was then soaked for 20 min in 100 ml of 0.1 M NaCl solution containing 10 µl of 10 mg/ml Gel Red (Biotium, USA) and photographed using ultraviolet illumination. T-cell clonality was analysed by multiplex PCR targeting the TCR beta (TRB), TCR gamma (TRG), and TCR delta (TRD) gene rearrangements.

Results

Clinical features

A total of 44 patients with gastrointestinal CAEBVD were included in the study. Of these, 22 initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms, while the remaining 22 had systemic CAEBVD that subsequently involved the gastrointestinal system (systemic symptoms and signs related to systemic CAEBVD occur first, including common manifestations such as fever, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, anaemia, and abnormal liver function, followed by gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain and diarrhoea. Alternatively, gastrointestinal EBV infection may be detected during routine examinations of the gastrointestinal system for evaluating disease after a systemic CAEBVD diagnosis has been confirmed). There were 26 males and 18 females, with ages ranging from 1 to 71 years (mean age, 27 years; median age, 22 years). The duration of the disease from onset to biopsy was 3–120 months (mean, 15 months; median, 8 months). None of the patients had a history of immunodeficiency or malignancy. Regarding the sites of involvement, the colon was affected in 33 cases (75%), with no significant difference in incidence among the different colonic segments; the small intestine was affected in 28 cases (63.6%), with no significant difference among the segments; the stomach was affected in 18 cases (40.9%); and the oesophagus in 4 cases (9.1%). Most patients had simultaneous involvement of multiple sites (75%, 33/44), while 11 patients (25%) had involvement of a single site only. The main clinical symptoms and signs were as follows: abdominal pain and diarrhoea (75%, 33/44), fever (68.2%, 30/44), upper respiratory tract symptoms (sore throat, cough; 22.7%, 10/44), splenomegaly (88.6%, 39/44), lymphadenopathy (59.1%, 26/44), hepatomegaly (38.6%, 17/44), rash (9.1%, 4/44), and weight loss (4.5%, 2/44). Laboratory findings are as follows: anaemia was present in 32 cases (72.7%), including 20 cases of pancytopenia (45.5%), 8 cases of leukopenia and reduced haemoglobin (18.2%), and 4 cases of isolated reduced haemoglobin (9.1%). Eight patients had normal three-lineage haematopoiesis and four had incomplete or unknown complete blood count results. EBV DNA load was elevated in all 44 patients. Gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed that the majority of patients (88.6%, 39/44) presented with erythema, erosion, or superficial ulcers, which were characterised by multifocality and small size (typically < 2 cm) (Figs. 1A and 2A). Only a minority of cases (4.5%, 2/44) exhibited intestinal wall thickening and roughness, while three patients showed no obvious abnormalities on endoscopy. Biopsy-confirmed extra-gastrointestinal involvement was present in the following organs: bone marrow (18 cases), lymph nodes (16 cases), nasopharynx (9 cases), liver (7 cases), central nervous system (6 cases), and lung (1 case). Severe complications are as follows: HLH occurred in 28 cases (63.6%), gastrointestinal haemorrhage occurred in 5 cases (11.3%), and gastrointestinal perforation occurred in 2 cases (4.5%). Morphological features

All 44 cases of gastrointestinal CAEBVD were diagnosed as EBV+ T or T/NK-cell LPD, grade 1 or 2. Except for one case, which was a partial small bowel resection specimen due to small bowel perforation with lesions involving the entire thickness of the intestinal wall, the remaining 43 cases were biopsy specimens, and the lesions were mainly confined to the mucosa (Fig. 1B), with a few extending into the submucosa. Abnormal crypt architecture was observed in 21 cases (Fig. 2B), cryptitis in 13 cases (Fig. 2C), and crypt abscesses in 3 cases. Eight cases showed architectural destruction, characterised by crypt and gland destruction, reduction, and lymphoepithelial lesions. Mildly atypical cells arranged around blood vessels were observed in three cases, and small foci of necrosis were noted in two cases. All cases exhibited a mixed inflammatory cell infiltrate comprising mature small lymphocytes, plasma cells, histiocytes, eosinophils, and neutrophils, with prominent vascularity. The neoplastic cells were predominantly small to medium-sized with mild to moderate nuclear irregularity (mild in 17 cases; mild to moderate in 22 cases; and moderate in 5 cases), inconspicuous nucleoli, and visible mitotic figures (Figs. 1C and 2C). IHC and ISH findings

In all cases, the lymphocytes predominantly expressed T-cell markers: CD3 (100%, 44/44) (Figs. 1D and 2D), CD5 (54.5%, 24/44), CD4 (76.9%, 20/26), and CD8 (23.1%, 6/26). Cytotoxic molecules TIA-1 and granzyme B were expressed in 24 cases (88.9%; 24/27). CD56 was positive in 14 cases (31.8%) (Fig. 1F). The Ki-67 proliferation index ranged from 5 to 50%. All cases were negative for CD20 (Figs. 1E and 2E).

EBER was positive in all cases. Of the 43 biopsy specimens examined, the number of EBER-positive cells ranged from 20 to over 200 per high-power field (HPF): 2 cases had over 200 EBER-positive cells per HPF, 12 cases had 100–200 cells per HPF, 20 cases had 50–100 cells per HPF, and 9 cases had fewer than 50 cells per HPF. One case was a surgical resection specimen with 100–200 EBER-positive cells per HPF. In all cases, the EBER-positive cells were predominantly small to medium-sized (Figs. 1G and 2G).

Double-labelling IHC combined with EBER ISH and CD3, CD20, and CD56 showed that most EBER-positive cells were T cells in 34 cases (77.3%; 34/44) (Fig. 2H) and NK cells in 10 cases (22.7%; 10/44) (Fig. 1H). A summary of the pathological findings is provided in Table 2.

PCR for TCR gene rearrangements

TCR gene rearrangement analysis was performed in ten cases of this series. Six cases showed monoclonal TCR rearrangements, including both single-tube and multi-tube rearrangements, mainly involving TCRD and TCRG (Fig. 3). In cases of T-cell infection, proliferating T cells may exhibit monoclonal, oligoclonal, or polyclonal TCR gene rearrangement. Therefore, although monoclonal TCR rearrangement in T cells suggests a T-cell origin, the presence of polyclonal rearrangement or negative results does not necessarily indicate an NK-cell origin. Further confirmation by double staining is required to distinguish between the two.

Follow-up

Among the 44 cases of gastrointestinal CAEBVD, follow-up data were available for 39 cases, and five cases were lost to follow-up. Twenty patients (51.3%) died, with survival time ranging from 1 to 20 months. The mean survival time was 5.9 months, and the median survival time was 4 months. Of these 20 deceased patients, 12 (60%) had T-cell involvement, 7 (35%) presented initially with gastrointestinal symptoms, 14 (70%) were complicated with HLH, and 4 (20%) underwent allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Nineteen patients survived. Of these 19 survivors, 14 (73.7%, 14/19) had T-cell involvement, 12 (63.2%, 12/19) presented initially with gastrointestinal symptoms, 11 (57.9%, 11/19) were complicated with HLH, and 13 (68.4%, 13/19) underwent allo-HSCT. Discussion

The intestine is one of the sites most susceptible to EBV-associated diseases due to the abundance of lymphoid tissue within its mucosa [22]. When patients present with gastrointestinal symptoms and intestinal lesions that cannot be explained by other disorders, alongside clinical manifestations consistent with chronic active EBV-associated enteritis (CAEAE), a diagnosis of CAEAE should be made [23]. In recent years, CAEAE has attracted increasing attention.

CAEAE is an extremely rare entity. A comprehensive literature search across major databases reveals that almost all of the relevant publications are case reports, with fewer than 30 cases having been reported worldwide in total. The most recent study is a case report by Yajie Meng et al. from China, published in 2024, which summarises 27 CAEAE cases through a systematic literature review (including one case from their own institution and 26 previously reported cases) [24]. Due to its extreme rarity and the lack of specific clinical manifestations, CAEAE is highly prone to misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis in clinical practice, often being confused with other intestinal disorders such as IBD and intestinal tuberculosis [25]. Furthermore, CAEAE is associated with a poor prognosis due to its high propensity for severe complications, including HLH and gastrointestinal bleeding. Therefore, it is of great significance to provide a detailed elaboration of the clinicopathological features of CAEAE for accurate diagnosis and timely intervention. In the present study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 44 cases of gastrointestinal CAEBVD at our institution, summarising their clinicopathological characteristics and prognostic outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale case series to focus on gastrointestinal involvement in CAEBVD.

According to previous literature reports, CAEAE patients are predominantly young and middle-aged adults, and the proportion of males is higher than that of females. Common symptoms include gastrointestinal manifestations such as abdominal pain and diarrhoea, accompanied by systemic features including fever, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy. Liu et al. [26] reported 11 cases of CAEAE, including eight males and three females, with a mean age of 40 years and a median age of 40 years (range 11–72 years). Symptoms and signs included intermittent fever exceeding 39 °C (100%), diarrhoea (73%), abdominal pain (64%), lymphadenopathy (64%), splenomegaly (64%), and hepatomegaly (27%). Meng et al. [24] summarised 27 cases of CAEAE (including the 11 cases reported by Liu et al.), comprising 21 males and 6 females. The mean age was 40 years, with a median age of 12 years (range 20–40 years). The disease course was less than 6 months in six cases and more than 12 months in seven cases, with a median duration of 29.4 months (range 6 to 168 months). Symptoms and signs included fever (92.5%), diarrhoea (59.2%), abdominal pain (51.8%), haematochezia (48.1%), hepatosplenomegaly (14.8%), and lymphadenopathy (14.8%). The epidemiological characteristics and main clinical manifestations in our cohort are generally consistent with those reported in the literature.

The previously adopted diagnostic criteria for CAEBVD [27] required an increased EBV load in infected tissues or peripheral blood, or elevated antibody titres, with patients having to satisfy at least one of the following four criteria: detection of EBV DNA by Southern blot hybridisation; detection of EBER-positive cells; EBV DNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells > 102.5 copies/mg; and abnormally elevated serum EBV antibody titres, specifically anti-viral capsid antigen IgG (VCA-IgG) ≥ 640 (original: 5120) and anti-early antigen antibody (EA) ≥ 160 (original: 640). In 2022, the updated diagnostic criteria for CAEBVD proposed that elevated EBV-related antibody titres are not necessary for the diagnosis, as some patients do not exhibit this characteristic [19]. In our cohort, the peripheral blood EBV DNA load was significantly elevated in almost all cases (with the exception of four cases for which results were unavailable), while EBV-related antibody levels were not evaluated. Anaemia was present in over 70% of patients and almost half presented with pancytopenia. Previous literature [24, 26] has reported that anaemia is common in CAEAE patients; however, leukocytosis and thrombocytosis were observed in almost a third of cases. Elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were observed in over 90% of cases.

Endoscopic findings in CAEAE are characterised primarily by multiple gastrointestinal ulcers, which often affect multiple sites. These ulcers lack distinctive features, vary in shape, depth, and size, and other manifestations may include erythema, oedema, and mucosal erosion. As they are difficult to distinguish from IBD, these ulcers are frequently misdiagnosed as Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), or tuberculosis [24, 26]. The endoscopic manifestations of gastrointestinal CAEBVD in our cohort of 44 cases were generally consistent with previous reports in the literature, primarily consisting of multi-site involvement (oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, and colon) and multifocal erosions or small, shallow ulcers. Deep, large, fissured, or longitudinal ulcers were not observed. Furthermore, the characteristic “cobblestone” appearance of CD and the continuous lesions typical of UC were absent. These findings suggest that, although the endoscopic features of this disease can be easily confused with those of IBD, there are certain distinguishing differences. The specific differential diagnostic points are discussed below.

From a pathological morphological perspective, the presence of intestinal mucosal inflammation accompanied by EBV-positive lymphocytic infiltration is one of the necessary criteria for diagnosing CAEAE. The histomorphological features of the cases in this study were similar to those reported in previous literature, with non-specific morphological changes observed. Based on the EBV+ T/NK-LPD classification proposed by Ohshima et al. in 2008 [28], CAEBVD is graded according to morphological and molecular characteristics into the following categories: (1) grade 1 (proliferative), characterised by polymorphous lymphoproliferation and polyclonal proliferation of EBV-infected cells; (2) grade 2 (borderline), characterised by polymorphous lymphoproliferation and monoclonal proliferation of EBV-infected cells; and (3) grade 3 (neoplastic), characterised by monomorphous lymphoproliferation and monoclonal proliferation of EBV-infected cells. All cases in this cohort were classified as CAEBVD grade 1 or 2. If the lesion progresses to grade 3 (the neoplastic stage), it is necessary to differentiate it from EBV-positive T/NK-cell lymphoma, since the treatment strategies and prognosis for these two conditions differ. This series of cases showed no morphological features characteristic of either grade 3 CAEBVD or ENKTL (such as angiocentric growth, necrosis, and marked cellular atypia). Although the overall prognosis of CAEBVD involving the gastrointestinal tract is poor, it does not show a significant correlation with histological grade. Several studies have also demonstrated that grading is not associated with prognosis [29, 30]. The 2022 edition of the WHO Classification has therefore abandoned this system. However, after consulting with domestic clinical and pathological experts in related fields, some scholars argue that grading remains necessary, as it can effectively determine the extent of lesions in different sites and assist in selecting clinical treatment regimens. Therefore, the necessity of grading CAEBVD remains a subject of debate.

In CAEBVD, T lymphocytes express the CD3 receptor and cytotoxic molecules such as TIA-1 and granzyme B. These T cells are predominantly CD4-positive and rarely express CD8; only a minority of cases shows predominant CD8 expression [29, 31]. Similarly, of the 11 cases reported by Liu et al., the vast majority were dominated by CD4-positive cells, with only one case showing a predominance of CD8-positive cells. The findings of this study are consistent with those previously reported in the literature. In our cohort, the vast majority of cases were predominantly CD4-positive T cells (76.9%, 20/26), which also differs from the typical ENKTL, where CD4 and CD8 are usually negative. All cases reported by Liu et al. were negative for CD56, and no cases of CD5 loss were identified. By contrast, approximately half of the cases in our cohort showed loss of the CD5 antigen, and nearly one third expressed CD56. This discrepancy may be due to the small sample size in the Liu et al. study. It is worth noting that the loss of T-cell antigens is not significantly correlated with prognosis [32], a conclusion that is also supported by the results of this study. EBV infection can be detected using a variety of methods. The most intuitive and sensitive approach is to detect EBER in biopsy tissues using ISH, which has a sensitivity of almost 100% and is considered the gold standard for confirming EBV infection [33]. Currently, there is no clear standard regarding the number of EBER-positive cells required for the diagnosis of CAEBVD. Some studies adopt 10% as the threshold, while others use 20% or 10 positive cells per HPF [34–36]. A recent summary of 27 CAEAE cases reported EBER counts of over 30 cells per HPF in biopsy samples and over 100 cells per HPF in surgical specimens. Our results showed that the number of EBER-positive cells exceeded 20 per HPF in all biopsy specimens and exceeded 100 per HPF in one surgically resected specimen. These findings may provide partial reference for establishing criteria for the number of EBER-positive cells in CAEBVD in the future. Neither our study nor previous literature [30, 32] found a correlation between the number of EBV-infected cells and prognosis. Furthermore, this study did not confirm a correlation between the type of EBV-infected cells and prognosis. Kimura et al. [29, 37] suggested that in CAEBVD, EBV infects T cells in 60% of cases and NK cells in 40% of cases. Cases involving T cells and NK cells present with different clinical symptoms. The NK-cell type is associated with lower malignancy and a longer survival time. Five-year survival rates are 59% for T-cell infections and 87% for NK-cell infections. In our cohort, EBV infected T cells in 68% of cases and NK cells in 32% of cases, which is a similar distribution to that reported in the literature. Among T-cell-infected cases, 14 patients survived (53.8%, 14/26), whereas among NK-cell-infected cases, 5 patients survived (38.5%, 5/13). These results do not support the idea that cases involving NK-cell infection have a better prognosis than those involving T-cell infection, and further investigation involving a larger number of cases is warranted.

Clonal analysis of CAEBVD has revealed that T-cell proliferation can be polyclonal, oligoclonal, or monoclonal [38, 39]. Studies have shown that the prognosis for CAEBVD is not significantly correlated with T-cell clonality. Instead, it is primarily associated with the development of severe complications such as HLH and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [32, 34]. In our cohort, TCR gene rearrangement testing was performed on ten cases, six of which exhibited monoclonal TCR rearrangement. Of these six cases, four survived and two died, which supports the conclusions of previous studies.

The overlap in symptoms, endoscopic findings, and histopathology between gastrointestinal CAEBVD and IBD makes diagnosis difficult. Some cases of CAEBVD present primarily as steroid- and biologic-refractory IBD, which can easily lead to misdiagnosis. Therefore, CAEBVD should be included in the routine differential diagnosis of refractory colitis [23]. It is noteworthy that the treatment strategies for gastrointestinal CAEBVD and IBD conflict with each other. The inappropriate use of immunosuppressants or immunomodulators may accelerate the clinical deterioration and death of CAEBVD patients [40, 41]. Although CAEAE and IBD share certain similarities, they also exhibit distinct differences. The key differentiation points are summarised as follows: (1) CAEAE patients often present with systemic symptoms such as intermittent high fever (over 39 °C), hepatosplenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy, whereas these manifestations are rare in IBD patients; (2) the main lesions in CAEAE present as numerous small, irregular ulcers that are superficial and located in the colon and small intestine. However, the lesions are not uniformly continuous, do not have a cobblestone appearance, and do not have longitudinal ulcers, which are common in UC and CD [42]; (3) in CAEAE, atypical lymphocytic infiltration is more common [35], and the chronic structural changes typically seen in IBD, such as granulomas, muscularis mucosae hyperplasia, and neural hypertrophy, are usually absent. Diffuse crypt architectural distortion and crypt abscesses are also not present [26]. It is worth noting that a certain proportion of IBD patients also have EBV infection, but in these cases, the virus typically infects B cells.

In addition to requiring a differential diagnosis of IBD, a subset of adult cases, particularly those with nasopharyngeal disease of sCAEBVD, should be distinguished from other EBV-positive T/NK-cell neoplasms, such as extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL). The diagnostic criteria for CAEBVD in adults require patients to be aged 20 years or over. All the other criteria remain the same as mentioned above [19]. sCAEBVD and ENKTL are difficult to distinguish based on morphology, immunophenotype, or even molecular genetics, whereas clinical information is crucial. While sCAEBVD usually presents with systemic symptoms from the outset and progresses over a period of months or years, ENKTL often lacks systemic clinical manifestations at the outset and progresses rapidly. It begins with localised lesions and then spreads throughout the body. The nasal cavity, nasopharynx, paranasal sinuses, and palate are the most commonly affected areas, with the nasal cavity being the prototypical site of involvement [10]. While sCAEBVD is most frequently observed in children and adolescents, a notable number of cases have been reported in adults and the elderly [11–13]. Four patients (cases 2, 34, 35, and 42, respectively) in this group were elderly individuals aged approximately 60 years or over. CAEBVD diagnosis should be approached with caution in such patients and a differential diagnosis of ENKTL is necessary. Case 2 presented with gastrointestinal symptoms as the initial manifestation, whereas case 34 had a history of sCAEBVD and subsequently experienced gastrointestinal and nasopharyngeal involvement. Neither patient underwent chemotherapy or transplantation. They are both currently EBV DNA-negative and in good general health, which is inconsistent with a diagnosis of ENKTL. ENKTL has a poor prognosis, with rapid disease progression, and is unlikely to resolve spontaneously without prompt treatment. Cases 35 and 42 both presented with gastrointestinal symptoms as the initial manifestation. Endoscopic findings revealed small, superficial ulcers on the gastrointestinal mucosa. The disease durations were 36 and 10 months, respectively, with recurrent clinical manifestations. Neither case showed nasopharyngeal involvement and both exhibited an immune phenotype of CD4-positive and CD8-negative T cells. This also does not support a diagnosis of ENKTL, as primary gastrointestinal NK/T-cell lymphoma typically presents with aggressive, progressive features and endoscopic findings of large gastrointestinal masses. It is also often CD4- and CD8-negative [43]. However, as both patients died, subsequent progression to ENKTL cannot be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, apart from case 34, eight other cases in this cohort involved the nasopharynx. All of these cases had a history of sCAEBVD (see Supplementary Table 1.), with nasopharyngeal involvement developing subsequently. Two of these cases resulted in death, which may have been due to the progression of sCAEBVD to ENKTL. Additionally, aggressive NK-cell leukaemia (ANKL) should also be considered and excluded. ANKL is a systemic malignant proliferation of NK cells, characterised by an acute clinical presentation and a highly aggressive clinical course. It exhibits typical leukaemic manifestations and extensive involvement of the bone marrow and peripheral blood [10].

CAEBVD generally carries a poor prognosis. According to the largest and most recent global cohort study on CAEBVD [44], the disease can be classified into four clinical subtypes based on the primarily involved organ system: cutaneous, vasculitic, gastrointestinal, and unclassified. Patients with the cutaneous subtype have the highest survival rate, while those with the gastrointestinal subtype have the worst prognosis and require early intervention. Gastrointestinal CAEBVD progresses rapidly, with a mortality rate of up to 51% within 6 months [24]. Furthermore, in our cohort, we found that patients who presented with gastrointestinal manifestations as their initial symptom had a better prognosis, with a survival rate of 63.2% (12/19), whereas those who presented with gastrointestinal involvement following systemic CAEBVD had a survival rate of only 35% (7/20). The prognosis was slightly poorer for cases complicated by HLH, with a survival rate of 44% (11/25), compared to 57.1% (8/14) for cases without HLH. As no relevant data had previously been reported in the literature, our findings can serve as a reference for future research and to inform clinical prognostic assessments. Literature reports suggest that gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation are also severe complications of gastrointestinal CAEBVD that often indicate a poor prognosis. In our cohort, there were five cases complicated by gastrointestinal bleeding (four survived and one died), and two cases of perforation (one died and one was lost to follow-up). Due to the limited number of cases, no correlation with prognosis could be demonstrated, necessitating further research with a larger sample size in the future. According to literature reports [29], the overall survival rate after 15 years is 60.6% for patients who underwent allo-HSCT, compared to 25.7% for those who did not. In our cohort, follow-up data were available for 39 patients with gastrointestinal CAEBVD. The mortality rate was 51.3%, with a mean survival time of 5.9 months. The survival rate was 76.5% (13/17) for patients who received allo-HSCT, compared to 27.3% (6/22) for those who did not.

This study has certain limitations and shortcomings. Firstly, the classification of T-cell and NK-cell lineages in this cohort relied heavily on double staining (EBER/CD3 and EBER/CD56), which has certain limitations. For example, cytoplasmic CD3 (cCD 3/CD3ε) can be expressed in both T cells and NK cells, and CD56 lacks specificity for the NK-cell lineage. This marker is also expressed in other T-cell lymphomas, such as enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, and not all NK-cell tumours consistently express CD56. In addition, the vast majority of cases in this cohort were consultation cases and almost all were gastrointestinal endoscopic biopsy specimens. The limited amount of tissue available meant that TCR clonality testing could not be performed in most cases, which affected the reliability of cell lineage determination. Future work will focus on obtaining a larger volume of tissue samples and refining TCR and TCR protein expression testing (e.g. TCRβ and TCRγ by immunohistochemistry). This will provide more comprehensive and objective evidence, helping to distinguish sCAEBVD with gastrointestinal tract involvement from ENKTL.

Conclusions

CAEBVD involving the gastrointestinal tract is relatively rare. As the first large-scale report with a substantial sample size, this study retrospectively analysed and elaborated on its clinicopathological features, investigated prognostic factors, and summarised the key points for differential diagnosis from IBD. In clinical practice, a diagnosis of CAEAE should be considered for patients presenting with recurrent fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy, accompanied by abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and multiple intestinal ulcers, where a pathological diagnosis of IBD remains unclear. It is recommended that EBV DNA screening is employed in conjunction with EBER detection via endoscopic biopsy. Although the prognosis for CAEBVD involving the gastrointestinal tract is poor, allo-HSCT is a highly effective treatment for improving patient survival. Therefore, early identification and prompt administration of appropriate therapy are crucial. Furthermore, secondary involvement of the gastrointestinal tract in systemic CAEBVD, as well as cases complicated by HLH, suggest a poor prognosis.
