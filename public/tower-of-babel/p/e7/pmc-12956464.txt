White Matter Hyperintensities and Neuropsychiatric Symptoms in Neurodegenerative Diseases
Authors: Shigeki Katakami, Hideki Kanemoto, Daiki Taomoto, Yuto Satake, Takashi Suehiro, Shunsuke Sato, Kenji Yoshiyama, Tetsuo Kashibayashi, Ryuichi Takahashi, Kenji Tagai, Shunichiro Shinagawa, Kazunari Ishii, Hiroaki Kazui, Manabu Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeNeuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) are frequent in dementia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), yet their reported associations with white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) remain inconsistent. We examined item-level associations between WMHs and NPS across Alzheimer’s disease dementia (ADD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and amnestic MCI (aMCI).MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study involved 523 patients: 276 with ADD, 67 with DLB, and 180 with aMCI. Three-dimensional T1-weighted and T2–fluid-attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance images were processed with BAAD (Brain Anatomical Analysis using Diffeomorphic Deformation) software to quantify WMH volume (WMHV) and gray matter volume (GMV), normalized to total intracranial volume (TIV). NPS were assessed using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory. Multiple regression analyses were performed within each group, adjusting for GMV/TIV, age, sex, years of education, Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes score, and institution.ResultsIn the aMCI group, higher WMHV/TIV was positively associated with depression, apathy, and disinhibition, and negatively with delusions. Subregional analyses revealed NPS-specific topographic patterns. No significant associations were detected in the ADD or DLB group. GMV/TIV was positively associated with delusions in the ADD group, but negatively associated with hallucinations in the DLB group.ConclusionsAssociations between WMHs and NPS were evident in the aMCI group but absent in the ADD and DLB groups, suggesting a stage-dependent effect. WMHs may contribute to NPS primarily in prodromal stages, whereas their influence may be overshadowed by neurodegenerative pathology in dementia. Clarifying the mechanisms underlying WMHs is critical for evaluating their potential as intervention targets.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with dementia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) frequently develop neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS),1 which impose a substantial burden on both patients and caregivers and accelerate cognitive as well as functional decline.234567 Nonpharmacological strategies are recommended as the first-line treatment,8 whereas pharmacological options remain limited and are often associated with adverse effects.9 Identifying the biological substrates of NPS is therefore an urgent priority.

Cortical atrophy has consistently been associated with NPS across neurodegenerative conditions.101112 White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) appear as hyperintense lesions on T2–fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI),13 and represent another potential pathological substrate. Previous reports have linked WMH burden with delusions,1415 depression,1617 apathy,1819 aberrant motor behavior (AMB),2021 anxiety,21 and sleep behavior disorders21 in Alzheimer’s disease dementia (ADD). In dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), WMHs have been associated with hallucinations,22 delusions,23 elation,23 apathy,23 AMB,23 and sleep behavior disorders.23 In MCI, WMHs have been associated with depression,2425 apathy,19 and anxiety,2627 with WMH burden being greater in individuals exhibiting persistent NPS.28 Nevertheless, several studies have found no significant associations between WMHs and NPS in ADD,2930313233 or between WMHs and hallucinations in DLB,34 indicating inconsistencies in previous findings.

Methodological limitations may explain these discrepancies. Previous studies have often relied on small samples, visual WMH ratings, and insufficient adjustment for confounding variables. It is particularly notable that gray matter volume (GMV), which has been strongly associated with NPS,10 has rarely been considered. Only one longitudinal study35 and one cross-sectional mediation analysis25 have directly accounted for GMV, and most investigations have examined ADD, DLB, or MCI in isolation.

To our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively examined item-level associations between WMHs and NPS across ADD, DLB, and amnestic MCI (aMCI). Previous studies have been limited to specific Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) items (e.g., apathy in ADD and MCI19), have focused on NPI subsyndromes without including DLB,35 or have not addressed these associations within a cross-disorder framework. These three neurodegenerative groups should be evaluated simultaneously, as they represent distinct disease stages and phenotypes in which WMH-related processes—not limited to vascular mechanisms—and primary neurodegenerative pathology may contribute to varying degrees.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to identify associations between WMHs and individual NPS in patients with ADD, DLB, and aMCI. Automated voxel-based morphometry (VBM) was applied to a relatively large multicenter Japanese cohort to quantify WMHs and assess their associations with NPI scores, while adjusting for GMV/TIV (total intracranial volume) and other potential confounders.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted as part of J-BIRD-RN (Japan multicenter study: Behavioral and psychological symptoms Integrated Research in Dementia–Retrospective Neuroimaging part). Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committees of Kochi University Medical School Hospital (approval number: 2021-92), Tokyo Jikei University Hospital (approval number: 33-306), and The University of Osaka Hospital (approval number: K21253). Informed consent was obtained using an opt-out procedure.

Data collection

Data were collected at Kochi University Medical School Hospital (Institution A), Tokyo Jikei University Hospital (Institution B), and The University of Osaka Hospital (Institution C), all of which are multispecialty hospitals that participated in the J-BIRD-RN study. The study cohort included patients examined and diagnosed by dementia specialists between April 2015 and December 2021 at the three participating institutions.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosis of probable ADD,36 probable DLB,3738 or aMCI39 according to international criteria; availability of MRI data including three-dimensional T1-weighted (3D T1w) and T2-FLAIR images; and contemporaneous assessments using the NPI, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR). The exclusion criteria included conditions that could interfere with statistical image analysis, such as major cerebrovascular disease, cerebral contusions, brain tumors, or motion artifacts.

The following data were collected: diagnosis, MRI data, age, sex, years of education, MMSE score, CDR score, CDR–Sum of Boxes (CDR–SB) score, institution, and composite scores for the 12 items of the NPI. The NPI was used to assess the frequency (scored 0–4) and severity (scored 0–3) of the following 12 NPS: delusions, hallucinations, agitation/aggression, depression/dysphoria, anxiety, elation/euphoria, apathy/indifference, disinhibition, irritability/lability, AMB, sleep/nighttime behavior disorders, and appetite/eating changes. For each NPS, a composite NPI score ranging from 0 to 12 was calculated by multiplying the frequency and severity scores. When available, the Logical Memory subtest of the Wechsler Memory Scale–Revised40 was also administered, primarily to confirm memory impairment in aMCI.

When available, N-isopropyl-p-[123I]iodoamphetamine single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) was performed as part of routine diagnostic evaluation; however, the SPECT data obtained were not analyzed in the present study. In addition, information was collected on the presence or absence of the four core clinical features of DLB, as well as on indicative biomarkers when available. This information, together with the CDR score and other clinical data, was reviewed retrospectively to ensure that each case was consistent with its assigned diagnostic category (i.e., ADD, DLB, or aMCI).

MRI data acquisition

MRI was performed using a 3.0-T scanner (Philips Ingenia) at Institution A, a 1.5-T scanner (Siemens MAGNETOM Avant Fit) at Institution B, and two 1.5-T scanners (GE Signa Excite HD and GE Signa Explorer) at Institution C. Detailed MRI protocols are presented in Supplementary Table 1 (in the online-only Data Supplement).

MRI data processing

The WMH volume (WMHV), GMV, and TIV were calculated using BAAD (Brain Anatomical Analysis using Diffeomorphic Deformation) software (version 4.3.2; https://www.shiga-med.ac.jp/hqbioph/iBaad/BAAD_ja/), a VBM-based tool implemented in Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM12).41 The image processing procedures included skull stripping and segmentation of 3D T1w images into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid, using maximum a posteriori estimation with probability-weighting correction. This was followed by normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute space using the DARTEL (Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie Algebra; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/docs/manual/dartelguide/) algorithm. Voxel volume was preserved by modulation, and the images were smoothed using an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel.

T2-FLAIR images were integrated with 3D T1w images and processed using the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (LST) lesion prediction algorithm to automatically detect WMHs, calculate WMHV, and correct GMV for WMH-related contamination.42 WMHs were then segmented into bilateral frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital subregions using region-of-interest masks provided in the MarsBaR toolbox within SPM12. Total and subregional WMHVs were normalized to TIV by calculating the WMH-to-TIV ratio (WMHV/TIV).

Statistical analyses

Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared among patients diagnosed with ADD, DLB, or aMCI using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Dunn–Bonferroni method.

Associations between NPI scores and WMHV/TIV were investigated within each diagnostic group using multiple linear regression analyses, adjusting for GMV/TIV, age, sex, years of education, CDR–SB score, and institution. Because MRI field strength was fixed by institution (3.0 T at Institution A and 1.5 T at Institutions B and C), including both MRI field strength and institution as covariates in the model would result in perfect multicollinearity. Institution was therefore included as a covariate to adjust for differences in MRI field strength. The degrees of freedom were 268, 59, and 172 for ADD, DLB, and aMCI, respectively. Sensitivity analyses were performed using regression models without GMV/TIV to assess robustness. To address skewed or overdispersed distributions of NPI items, Poisson regression with overdispersion correction was also performed using the same covariates as in the primary analyses.

For NPI items that were significantly associated with total WMHV/TIV, post-hoc regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the contributions of subregional WMHV/TIV values (in the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital regions), while adjusting for the same covariates.

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 28.0; IBM Corp.). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value of less than 0.05. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.43

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Among the 526 patients enrolled in the J-BIRD-RN study (279 with ADD, 67 with DLB, and 180 with aMCI), 523 (276, 67, and 180, respectively) were included in the present analysis following MRI quality control using the BAAD system. Three patients were excluded: segmentation could not be performed in one case, and segmentation errors resulted in mislabeling portions of the white matter and lateral ventricles as gray matter in two cases. No significant differences in age or sex were observed among the ADD, DLB, and aMCI groups. Years of education were significantly longer in the aMCI group than in the DLB group, whereas disease duration was significantly longer in the ADD group than in the aMCI group. MMSE and CDR scores, as well as WMHV/TIV and GMV/TIV values, indicated lower impairment in the aMCI group than in the ADD and DLB groups (Table 1).

Relationship between WMHs and NPI scores

Fig. 1 summarizes the associations among WMHV/TIV, GMV/TIV, CDR–SB score, and individual NPI item scores in the ADD, DLB, and aMCI groups.

In the ADD group, none of the NPI items were significantly associated with WMHV/TIV (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). Delusions were associated with GMV/TIV (B=16.50, p=0.035), and scores for all NPI items except for elation and appetite changes showed significant positive associations with the CDR–SB score. In the DLB group, none of the NPI items were significantly associated with WMHV/TIV (Supplementary Table 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). However, hallucinations were associated with GMV/TIV (B=-60.94, p=0.047) and apathy showed a significant positive association with the CDR–SB score. In the aMCI group, WMHV/TIV was positively associated with depression (B=25.93, p=0.029), apathy (B=48.26, p=0.029), and disinhibition (B=6.55, p=0.045), and negatively associated with delusions (B=-34.37, p=0.005) (Supplementary Table 4 in the online-only Data Supplement). Additionally, apathy and appetite changes were significantly positively associated with the CDR–SB score.

In all multiple regression analyses, the variance inflation factors for the explanatory variables were all less than 2.1, alleviating concerns about numerical collinearity. However, because theoretical and interpretive collinearity might still have been present, multiple regression analyses excluding GMV/TIV were conducted (Supplementary Tables 5, 6, 7 in the online-only Data Supplement). The results confirmed that statistically significant associations with WMHV/TIV were robust across models, although effect sizes differed slightly on the model used.

In the ADD and DLB groups, Poisson models with overdispersion correction revealed no significant associations between WMHV/TIV and any of the NPI items, consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 in the online-only Data Supplement). In the aMCI group, the negative association with delusions and the positive association with depression remained significant, whereas no significant associations were found with apathy (p=0.050) or disinhibition (p=0.062) (Supplementary Table 10 in the online-only Data Supplement).

For the post-hoc analyses, multiple regression models were used to examine associations between subregional WMHV/TIV values and levels of delusions, depression, apathy, and disinhibition in the aMCI group—the NPS that were significantly associated with total WMHV/TIV—while adjusting for potential confounders (Fig. 2). WMHV/TIV in the left frontal and left temporal regions was positively associated with depression, whereas WMHV/TIV in the left frontal region was positively associated with apathy. WMHV/TIV in the bilateral frontal, right temporal, and bilateral parietal regions was positively associated with disinhibition, whereas WMHV/TIV in the bilateral frontal, parietal, and occipital regions was negatively associated with delusions.

DISCUSSION

This multicenter study identified a stage-dependent pattern: WMH burden was significantly associated with multiple NPS in the aMCI group, but not in the ADD or DLB groups. In the aMCI group, greater WMH burden was positively associated with depression, apathy, and disinhibition, whereas it was negatively associated with delusions. In contrast, no significant associations between WMHs and NPS were observed in the dementia groups. These findings suggest that WMHs influence NPS early in the disease course, with their contribution subsequently overshadowed by neurodegenerative processes, including amyloid, tau, and α-synuclein pathology.

Only one other study has examined associations between WMHs and all NPI items in patients with MCI, reporting a significant association with anxiety26; however, the authors interpreted this as a chance finding due to the small effect sizes. Moreover, although that study included a large sample,26 it relied on visual ratings and did not control for GMV. In contrast, WMHs were not associated with anxiety in our data. Our results are largely consistent with previous studies reporting that depression2425 and apathy19 are associated with more severe WMHs in patients with MCI. Thus, the association between NPS and WMHs in patients with aMCI appears to be reproducible. Longitudinal data also indicate that both gray matter atrophy and WMHs contribute to the progression of NPS in individuals with MCI and early-stage ADD, with WMHs exerting a stronger effect.35 These findings suggest that WMHs are associated with—and may contribute to—the progression of depression, apathy, and disinhibition in aMCI. Moreover, one study found that the association between depression, WMHs, and frontal gray matter in patients with MCI did not appear to be mediated by frontal gray matter; however, interpretative caution is warranted due to the cross-sectional design of that study.25

Depression was positively associated with WMHV/TIV in the left frontal and left temporal regions in patients with aMCI. Previous studies of patients with ADD have shown that depression involves dysfunction in frontostriatal and subcortical limbic circuits, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, subgenual cingulate, and inferior temporal cortex,4445 which are key components of the affective network.46 Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies have confirmed that late-life depression and depression comorbid with MCI are associated with disruption of these circuits.474849 One longitudinal study demonstrated progressive atrophy in the left frontal, parietal, and bilateral temporal white matter in patients with comorbid depression and MCI.50 Other investigative modalities—including electroencephalography and functional MRI515253—as well as lesion and stimulation studies have revealed an interhemispheric imbalance characterized by right-hemisphere hyperactivity and left-hemisphere hypoactivity.54 Our findings regarding left-hemisphere WMH correlates are consistent with this model.

Apathy was positively associated with WMHV/TIV in the left frontal region in the aMCI group, whereas no such association was observed in the ADD group. Patients with apathy and dementia typically perform poorly on frontal tasks such as semantic fluency, motor response inhibition, and abstract thinking, indicating prefrontal cortical dysfunction.55 However, previous findings have been inconsistent. Structural MRI studies have revealed associations between apathy and localized gray matter atrophy within frontosubcortical circuits, including the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), caudate, and putamen.1156 Associations between apathy and frontal WMHs or leukoaraiosis have also been reported,1819 although those studies relied on visual rating methods without adjustment for GMV, and one study included a relatively small sample.18 In contrast, a larger cohort study using the Fazekas scale reported no associations between WMHs or medial temporal lobe atrophy and any NPI symptoms, including apathy.31 DTI investigations in ADD have further indicated that apathy is associated with microstructural changes in the anterior cingulum, with evidence that this relationship persists even after controlling for gray matter density in the ACC.575859 In addition, WMHs in the anterior thalamic radiation have been associated with apathy in patients with MCI and amnesia.60

Taken together, these observations suggest that both macroscopic WMHs and microstructural white matter abnormalities are relevant to apathy, although their contributions and detectability61 may vary across disease stages. The present findings for aMCI support the possibility that macroscopic WMHs contribute more prominently during earlier stages, whereas their influence becomes less apparent in ADD, possibly because finer-grained regional gray matter or microstructural white matter changes play a greater role as dementia progresses. This interpretation is supported by the observed association between apathy and CDR score in our ADD group, as well as by previous reports of null associations between WMHs and apathy in other ADD samples.31 It should also be noted that our analyses were based on subregional WMHV and global GMV adjustment, which may have reduced sensitivity for detecting tract-specific or finer-grained regional effects. Future studies utilizing higher-resolution imaging techniques are needed to further clarify these associations.

Disinhibition in patients with aMCI was positively associated with WMHV/TIV in the bilateral frontal, right temporal, and bilateral parietal regions. The anterior temporal cortex underlies social cognition.62 Disinhibition in patients with frontotemporal dementia has been linked to reduced GMV in the right medial and superior temporal regions,63 as well as in the bilateral OFC, insular cortex, inferior frontal gyrus, and right middle temporal gyrus.64 Previous cross-diagnostic studies have also implicated the rostral ACC, OFC, inferior frontal gyrus, and temporal lobes.65 Given the close connections among the anterior temporal lobe, OFC, and amygdala,66 our findings suggest that WMHs disrupting this network underlie disinhibition.

Delusions were negatively associated with WMHV/TIV in the bilateral frontal, parietal, and occipital regions in patients with aMCI. Previous neuroimaging studies of delusions in ADD have yielded inconsistent findings. Structural MRI and DTI studies have produced mixed results, with some implicating abnormalities in the frontal, temporal, parietal, or cerebellar regions, whereas others have reported no clear differences between patients with and without delusions.67 Findings regarding WMHs have also been inconsistent, with some studies reporting negative associations—suggesting that intact white matter is necessary for the emergence of psychotic phenomena68—whereas others have reported positive associations, implying that WMHs may contribute to delusional symptoms.141516

Functional imaging studies have provided more consistent evidence of hypoperfusion or hypometabolism in the right frontal and temporal cortices, often accompanied by relative hypermetabolism in the temporal region.676970 However, it remains unclear whether this apparent right-sided deficit reflects an absolute reduction in activity or a relative increase in contralateral perfusion and metabolism.70

These discrepancies may reflect not only methodological variability but also differing theoretical perspectives. On the one hand, delusions are often conceptualized as abnormal mental states requiring underlying neural dysfunction; on the other hand, they are complex cognitive phenomena that presuppose a certain degree of preserved neural integrity, enabling patients to construct internally coherent beliefs and communicate them. The available literature14151667686970 suggests that delusions in ADD do not arise from a single focal lesion but rather emerge from interactions between damaged and relatively preserved neural systems. Within this framework, the negative association between WMHs and delusions observed in our study may represent one facet of this interplay, suggesting that intact or even hyperactive circuits are necessary for the formation of delusions. Supporting this interpretation, gray matter integrity was also associated with delusions in our ADD group.

Overall, these findings suggest that WMHs influence NPS in patients with aMCI in a region-specific rather than global manner, with distinct NPS mapping onto specific WMH distributions.

In the ADD group, no NPS were significantly associated with WMHV/TIV. Although some studies have reported associations between WMHs and NPS,14182021 these studies often involved small sample sizes, and their findings have rarely been replicated. Among the four relatively large studies that reported associations,15161719 three involved patients with more advanced stages of dementia, in contrast to our cohort. Furthermore, two of these studies used the Fazekas scale rather than volumetric quantification,1619 and none adjusted for GMV. All four also involved smaller sample sizes than our study. By contrast, several other studies have found no significant associations,2930313233 which is consistent with our findings. One of these studies involved a relatively large sample of patients with mild dementia, similar to our cohort,32 although it also relied on visual assessments. Taken together, the data suggest that in mild ADD, WMHs—when quantified volumetrically and adjusted for GMV—do not contribute meaningfully to NPS.

In the DLB group, no significant associations between WMHs and NPS were observed, diverging from a large-scale study of 449 patients that reported higher levels of delusions, elation, apathy, and sleep behavior disorders in individuals with greater WMH burden based on the Fazekas rating.23 Differences in WMH quantification, sample size, and adjustment for GMV may account for these discrepancies. In particular, the present study found that hallucinations were significantly negatively associated with GMV/TIV in patients with DLB, consistent with previous findings.7172 The influence of GMV may therefore be an important factor to consider when examining the associations between WMHs and NPS in neurodegenerative diseases.

In the patients with aMCI, WMHV/TIV was significantly associated with multiple NPS, whereas no such associations were observed in those with ADD or DLB. WMHs were significantly associated with cognitive decline in patients with ADD at Braak stages I–II, but not at stages III–IV or V–VI.73 Similarly, WMHs were significantly associated with lower verbal fluency in patients with aMCI but not in those with ADD.74 Furthermore, a meta-analysis75 found that the negative association between WMHs and MMSE scores was significantly stronger in patients with MCI than in those with ADD. Given the significant positive associations of amyloid and Lewy body pathologies with certain NPS,767778 when these pathologies are severe, the association between WMHs and NPS may be overshadowed by those between NPS and neurodegenerative pathology.26 Because MCI is a prodromal stage of dementia in which neurodegeneration is less advanced, the impact of white matter impairment on NPS may be greater than in patients who have already developed dementia. WMHs have been associated with multiple NPS in older adults without cognitive impairment, but only with anxiety in patients with MCI—presumably due to greater neurodegenerative pathology in the latter group.26 This relationship between NPS and neurodegenerative pathology may have influenced the observed associations between many NPS and the CDR–SB score in patients with ADD in the present study.

WMHs are often interpreted as markers of small vessel disease and vascular pathology, but they may also reflect other underlying mechanisms such as age-related myelin changes, inflammation, and neurodegenerative processes.79 Thus, although we identified associations between WMHs and specific NPS in aMCI, the underlying etiology of WMHs cannot be determined from the present data.

The strengths of this study include its relatively large sample size and the adjustment for various potential confounders, including GMV/TIV. In addition, WMHs were quantified using computerized volumetric methods, which are generally considered more reliable than the visual rating approaches used in many previous studies.17808182 We used the BAAD software for quantification, incorporating the LST algorithm for automatic WMH detection, which has demonstrated good accuracy and reproducibility in multicenter settings.83

However, this study also had some limitations. First, although all participants in the MCI group met the criteria for aMCI, we could not differentiate the underlying etiologies of these cases. This heterogeneity—reflecting the possibility that the aMCI group included prodromal stages of various dementias—represents an important limitation and may have influenced the observed associations. Second, detailed profiles of vascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and smoking) were not available, preventing us from determining whether the observed associations between WMHs and NPS were specifically driven by vascular risk or by other mechanisms contributing to WMH formation. Third, the residuals did not meet normality assumptions in the multiple regression analyses, except for apathy in ADD; delusions, hallucinations, depression, apathy, sleep behavior disorders, and appetite changes in DLB; and apathy in aMCI. To account for violations of the normality assumptions, we performed sensitivity analyses using Poisson regression with overdispersion correction. These analyses supported the overall conclusion that WMHs are not associated with NPS in ADD or DLB but are associated with delusions and depression in aMCI. Although the associations with apathy and disinhibition in aMCI were slightly attenuated, the directions of the effects remained consistent with those in the main analyses. Fourth, the number of patients with DLB was considerably smaller than that of patients with aMCI or ADD. Fifth, the study cohort was drawn primarily from multispecialty hospitals in Japan, which may limit the external validity and generalizability of the findings. Finally, the retrospective cross-sectional design precluded causal inferences. Future research addressing these limitations will be important for confirming and extending the present findings.

In conclusion, this multicenter study demonstrated a stage-dependent pattern of disease effects: greater WMH burden in aMCI was associated with higher levels of depression, disinhibition, and apathy, but with lower levels of delusions. No such associations were evident in ADD or DLB. These findings suggest that WMHs contribute to NPS primarily during the prodromal stage, whereas their influence may diminish in more advanced stages of dementia, when neurodegenerative pathology becomes predominant. Elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying WMHs is critical for establishing their potential as targets for interventions aimed at modifying the progression of NPS.
