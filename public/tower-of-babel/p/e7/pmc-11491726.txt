Regulation of cardiovascular diseases by histone deacetylases and NADPH oxidases
Authors: Hui Yan, Yidan Yin, Yichen Zhou, Zhanghang Li, Yuxing Li, Lingxuan Ren, Jiazheng Wen, Weirong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistone deacetylases (HDACs) play critical roles in cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). In addition, reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced by NADPH oxidases (NOXs) exert damaging effects due to oxidative stress on heart and blood vessels. Although NOX-dependent ROS production is implicated in pathogenesis, the relationship between HDACs and NOXs in CVDs remains to be elucidated. Here, we present an overview of the regulatory effects and interconnected signaling pathways of HDACs and NOXs in CVDs. Improved insights into these relationships will facilitate the discovery of novel therapeutic agents that target HDACs, oxidase stress pathways, and the interactions between these systems which may be highly effective in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular disorders.Keywords: Histone deacetylases, NADPH oxidases, Reactive oxygen species, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) encompasses a wide array of disorders, including in cardiac muscle and in the vascular system that supplies blood to the heart, brain, and other vital organs. CVDs account for more than two-fifths of all deaths globally that are caused by non-communicable diseases [1]. The potential roles of oxidative stress in CVDs have been under investigation for many years. Risk factors for CVDs, including diabetes mellitus, aging, and hypercholesterolemia, increase the possibility of the production of damaging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [2]. Each layer of the vascular wall may produce ROS under pathological conditions [3]. ROS generated by oxidative stress directly oxidizes biomacromolecules, including membrane lipids and enzymes, which leads to cellular dysfunction and potentially to cell death. In addition, ROS interfere with cell signaling pathways and induce abnormal signaling transduction. ROS may be generated in the arterial vascular wall through both mitochondrial and non-mitochondrial systems. The principal enzymes responsible for ROS production in the non-mitochondrial system are NADPH oxidases (NOXs), xanthine oxidase, and uncoupled endothelial nitric oxide synthase [4].

NOXs are a class of complex enzymes that are expressed widely in vascular endothelial cells (VECs), vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), cardiomyocytes, and cell membranes of other cell types. NOXs may be activated by diverse factors, including cytokines and high glucose concentrations. All NOX isoforms display structural homology that includes six transmembrane domains and a single cytoplasmic domain that binds NADPH and flavin adenine dinucleotide. Seven NOX isoforms have been identified in human cells, among which NOX1, NOX2, NOX4, and NOX5 are expressed in vascular cells [5]. NOX1, NOX2, and NOX5 are main sources of intracellular superoxide. However, NOX4 has unique characteristics, and is a constitutively active enzyme that primarily produces hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [6]. The principal function of NOXs is to generate ROS which regulate multiple physiological and pathological processes. NOXs are expressed at basal levels under physiological conditions during which the enzymes catalyze the generation of low ROS concentrations which act as key signaling molecules in regulating vascular function and structure. However, when exposed to transforming growth factors, high glucose, high blood cholesterol, or other conditions, NOXs significantly enhance ROS production. Excessive ROS lead to an imbalance in the oxidative and antioxidant defense systems and ultimately trigger oxidative stress reactions [7]. NOX-dependent ROS generation plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of CVDs, including inflammation, oxidative stress, autophagy, and lipid deposition [8]. These damaging effects of NOX-mediated oxidative stress on heart and blood vessels have been the subject of increased recent attention.

Epigenetics studies alterations in expression levels and functions of genes lead to heritable phenotypes. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are enzymes involved in epigenetic regulation which is closely associated with chromatin structure and transcriptional control. Eighteen mammalian HDACs have been identified to date and are characterized into four classes: class I (HDAC1, 2, 3, and 8), class II (HDAC4, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10), class III (sirtuin family SIRT1-SIRT7), and class IV (HDAC11). HDACs are located predominantly in the nucleus, but also are detected in the cytoplasm and mitochondria. Moreover, certain HDACs translocate between the cytoplasm and nucleus [9] (Fig. 1). HDACs are intimately linked to the progression of CVDs, and inhibition of these enzymes alters transcription of NOXs and consequent superoxide and H2O2 production [10,11]. In turn, the overproduction of ROS leads to oxidative stress, which modulates HDACs activity and subsequent epigenetic processes [12,13]. However, the relationship between HDACs and NOXs in CVDs has not yet been elucidated fully. This review discusses the role of HDACs in CVDs, and the relationship between these enzymes and NOXs in the pathogenesis of CVDs.

The relationship of class I HDACs and NOXs in CVDs

Recent studies demonstrated that HDAC1, 2, 3 and NOXs were associated with CVDs and other diseases, including cerebral trauma, spinal cord injury, and intermittent hypoxia [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Moreover, a clear connection was identified between the activity of HDAC8 and ROS-generating NOXs in neuroblastoma tumor cells [18]. In the following section, we will introduce class I HDACs and the potential pathways that involve NOXs which play important roles in CVDs (Fig. 2).

The relationship of class I HDACs and NOXs in CVDs. HDAC1 and HDAC2 up-regulate NOX1/4/5 promoter activities, affect NOX expression and the production of superoxide and H2O2 partly through JAK/STAT signaling pathway. HDAC3 inhibits Nrf2 activation by promoting the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction which promotes NOX4-associated H2O2 production.

HDAC1, HDAC2, NOXs and CVDs

HDAC1 and HDAC2 are localized to the nucleus, and are involved in the regulation of chromatin structure and gene expression [19]. HDAC1 participates in the control of endothelial cell physiological function, including in angiogenesis, the inflammatory response, oxidation-reduction (redox) homeostasis, and nitric oxide (NO) production [20,21]. In contrast, HDAC2 is a key target of clinical HDAC inhibitors in cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure [22].

Levels of HDAC1 and HDAC2, along with those of NOX1, NOX2, and NOX4, were increased significantly in the aortas of diabetic mice compared with control mice [20]. This study also showed that treatment of diabetic mice with a HDAC inhibitor, suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA), down-regulated the expression of NOX1, NOX2, and NOX4, and diminished ROS production in the aortas of these mice [20]. Furthermore, SAHA significantly reduced high glucose-induced expression of NOX1, NOX4, and NOX5, superoxide and H2O2 production, and the formation of malondialdehyde-protein adducts in VSMCs. These effects may be related to the interaction of HDAC1, and HDAC2 with promoters during active transcription of the NOX1/4/5 genes [20]. In addition, SAHA decreased NOX4 transcription in human endothelial cells by preventing the binding of transcription factors and RNA polymerase to the NOX4 promoter [23]. Class I HDAC inhibitors also contribute to the differential regulation of superoxide dismutase and NOX4 genes. Class I HDAC inhibitors induced the expression of superoxide dismutase up to 10-fold, whereas decreased the expression of the prooxidant gene NOX4 by more than 95 % in human pulmonary artery endothelial cells [24]. Furthermore, overexpression of HDAC2 also enhanced NOX1/4/5 promoter activities in VSMCs [20].

Knockdown of HDAC1 and HDAC2 markedly reduced IFNγ-driven gene activation, whereas overexpression of these histone deacetylases enhanced STAT1-dependent transcriptional activity [25]. JAK/STAT is a key regulator of NOX1 and NOX4 in human VSMCs [26]. Therefore, class I HDACs may affect NOX expression and H2O2 production partly through the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Numerous studies also showed that NOXs affect CVDs by mediating inflammatory cell adhesion, platelet activation, degenerative structural changes and calcification of arterial walls, and endothelial cell proliferation [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. These combined observations suggest that HDAC1/HDAC2-induced modulation of NOX levels are a key event in CVDs and that these HDACs are potential targets for effective CVD therapy.

HDAC3, NOXs, and CVDs

HDAC3 may exert dual roles in CVDs by regulating diverse target genes and pathways. Direct evidence showed that HDAC3 is a key regulator of endothelial cell viability that functions, and that global HDAC3 knockout in mice is lethal at the embryonic stage [31,32]. Recent studies also revealed that HDAC3 inhibition improved myocardial ischemia, fibrosis, remodeling, and atherosclerosis. In addition, growing evidence implicates HDAC3 in regulating redox systems, including modification of antioxidant genes, and ROS sensitivity [[33], [34], [35]].

There are the following pathways that may be involved in HDAC3-mediated regulation of NOXs to affect the production of H2O2. Inhibition of HDAC3 activated nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) by decreasing the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction which suppressed downstream NOX4 redox signaling in the vascular endothelium [36]. In addition, inhibition of HDAC3 reduced superoxide production, and alleviated endothelial injury caused by high glucose [34]. Furthermore, knockdown of HDAC3 suppressed the interaction of transcription factors with the NOX4 promoter, thereby inhibiting the production of NOX4-associated H2O2 in human endothelial cells [36]. Finally, inhibition of HDAC3 in mice with spinal cord injury decreased the production of NOX2 and NOX4 proteins and weakened H2O2 production [16]. These observations highlight the interconnections between HDAC3 and NOXs that potentially impact superoxide and H2O2 production during CVDs.

The relationship of class II HDACs and NOXs in CVDs

Class II HDACs are divided into IIa (HDAC4, 5, 7 and 9) and IIb (HDAC6 and 10) subclasses [37]. Subclass IIa proteins possess a long amino-terminal adaptor domain and a catalytic domain, whereas subclass IIb enzymes comprise two catalytic domains [38]. Class II HDAC proteins demonstrate complex molecular mechanisms and may prove of therapeutic value in CVDs. For example, class II HDACs are considered widely to serve as corepressors of myocyte enhancer factor 2 (MEF2) transcriptional activity in cardiomyocytes which inhibits hypertrophy [39,40]. In contrast, overexpression of HDAC5 diminished the expression of MEF2 which led to pathological hypertrophy in response to certain stimuli [41]. Therefore, the signaling pathways that involve class II HDACs and the potential roles of HDAC inhibition in CVDs, as well as the effects of specific HDACs on signaling pathways, remain under intense investigation [42] (Fig. 3).

The relationship of class II HDACs and NOXs in CVDs. NOX4 increases H2O2 generation and the phosphorylation and cysteine oxidation of HDAC4, which modulated the nuclear output of HDAC4 and promoted the transcription of hypertrophic genes by MEF-2 and NFAT. NOX4-derived H2O2 regulates the location of HDAC5 and HDAC7 through phosphorylation. HDAC6 is implicated in superoxide generation by NOX2.

HDAC4, NOXs, and CVDs

The HDAC4 deacetylase fulfils a diverse set of functions. A clinical survey revealed that HDAC4 was decreased in patients with coronary heart disease compared with healthy volunteers. In addition, elevated circulating HDAC4 level was associated with a lower lipid profile, good vascular status, and less narrowing of blood vessels [43]. Moreover, HDAC4 is weakly expressed in aortic tissues of atherosclerosis mouse model and in oxidized-low density lipoprotein (ox-LDL)-induced macrophages [44,45]. Overexpression of Krüppel-like factor 7 (KLF7) attenuated atherosclerotic lesions in atherosclerosis mouse model, and reduced glucose intake in ox-LDL-induced macrophages. Interestingly, KLF7 bound to the HDAC4 promoter to activate this gene. HDAC4 downregulation abolished the protective role of KLF7 overexpression in atherosclerosis mouse model and ox-LDL-induced macrophages [45]. In addition, overexpression of miR-200 b-3p promoted apoptosis of endothelial cells induced by oxidative stress by targeting HDAC4 in atherosclerosis [44].

A liability threshold model that predicted the risk of CVDs in patients with type 2 diabetes indicated that HDAC4 and NOX4 were important for the proliferation and migration of VSMCs that occur after vascular damage [46]. As HDAC4 shuttles between cytoplasm and nucleus, it is possible that HDAC4 passes an area similar to a micro domain with high H2O2 concentration made by NOX4. So HDAC4 can be specifically oxidized by NOX4-derived H2O2, which is a prerequisite for HDAC4 to exit the nucleus. And the oxidation of HDAC4 also increased phosphorylation of residue Ser 632 in HDAC4 and reversed the inhibitory effect on angiogenesis [13,47]. Furthermore, the cysteine residues of HDAC4 are oxidized by hypertrophic stimuli, which in turn regulates the nucleocytoplasmic localization of HDAC4 and promotes the transcription of hypertrophic genes by MEF-2 and nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) which lead to cardiac hypertrophy [48]. In addition, the rapid upregulation of NOX4 by hypertrophic stimuli increased H2O2 generation and induced cysteine oxidation of HDAC4 which in turn promoted nuclear exit of HDAC4 [49]. Therefore, the phosphorylation and oxidation status of HDAC4 is an essential feature of the NOX4-HDAC4 axis in CVDs. These results suggest that increased HDAC4 phosphorylation and cysteine oxidation by NOX4 are key events in the occurrence of CVDs and that this axis may be an important target for treatment of CVDs.

HDAC5, NOXs, and CVDs

HDAC5, the most abundant HDAC transcript in the adult human heart [9], is a class II histone deacetylation protein. The enzyme plays a significant role in the pathological cardiac hypertrophy via diverse transcription factors [39,40]. When HDAC5 is phosphorylated, it binds the 14-3-3 regulatory protein, dissociates from the MEF2 transcription factor, and is exported from the nucleus. Upon release of HDACs, MEF2 is free to associate with histone acetyltransferases and lead to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [40].

Increased HIF-1-dependent NOX4 transcription occurred in the adrenal medullae of rats treated for 10 days with trichostatin A, an inhibitor of HDACs, leading to repression of HDAC5 and elevation of plasma catecholamines and blood pressure [50]. The NOX4 protein also manipulates nuclear output of HDAC5 which potentially promotes pathological cardiac hypertrophy or other CVDs [41,[51], [52], [53]]. Diphenyleneiodonium chloride (DPI), a potent inhibitor of flavoenzymes including NADPH oxidases, markedly decreased HDAC5 phosphorylation in VSMCs in response to IGF-1. In addition, siRNA-mediated reduction of NOX4 level caused the suppression of HDAC5 phosphorylation in VSMCs induced by IGF-1. Furthermore, IGF-1 also significantly reduced the nuclear level of HDAC5 which was restored in DPI-treated VSMCs [54]. NOX2-dependent generation of superoxide caused nuclear efflux of HDAC5 and contributed to nuclear efflux of HDAC4 during intense skeletal muscle activity [55]. Similarly, ROS also increased nuclear exit of HDAC5 in ischemic stroke [56]. These studies suggest that NOX4-derived H2O2 regulates the nuclear efflux of HDAC5 through phosphorylation and may play an essential function in CVDs. It has been reported that the sumoylation and ubiquitination modifications of HDAC4/5 are altered in CVDs. However, whether the sumoylation and ubiquitination of HDAC4/5 play a role in the pathogenesis of CVDs deserve further investigation.

HDAC6, NOXs, and CVDs

HDAC6, located in the cytoplasm, acts by deacetylating target molecules [57,58]. For example, the protein induced histone H3K9 deacetylation, aggravated vascular endothelial injury, and promoted atherosclerosis [59]. The HDAC6 protein also inhibited the expression of Ras association domain family protein 1 isoform A (RASSF1A) which promotes cardiac fibrosis [60]. Correspondingly, inhibition of the HDAC6 enzyme activity may attenuate cardiac fibrosis in hypertensive rats [61].

Telomere shortening in a mouse model of heart failure coincided with increased superoxide production, NOX2 expression, and HDAC6 activity. Moreover, treatment with the HDAC6 inhibitor tubastatin A or NOX inhibitor apocynin ameliorated heart failure and telomere shortening [62]. NOX2 is an enzymatic source of superoxide generation in diverse cells [63]. HDAC6 is implicated in HIV-1 Tat-induced superoxide generation by NOX2 in astrocytes [64]. In addition, HDAC6 overexpression significantly induced superoxide production via upregulation of NOX activity in macrophages. Finally, an inhibitor of HDAC6 enzymatic activity significantly inhibited superoxide production and expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages [65]. These observations point to a strong link between HDAC6 function and NOX activity.

HDAC7, NOXs, and CVDs

Differently from class II HDAC4, 5, and 9, HDAC7 plays a protective role in vascular integrity. HDAC7 is expressed specifically in the vascular endothelium during early embryogenesis where it maintains vascular integrity. Deletion of the HDAC7 gene in mice resulted in embryonic lethality due to a failure in endothelial cell-cell adhesion and consequent rupture of blood vessels [66]. In general, HDAC7 exerts its functions in the nucleus, and cytoplasmic HDAC7 is shuttled through the proteasome-mediated protein degradation pathway. However, HDAC7 may be exported from the nucleus and undergo cytoplasmic stabilization after phosphorylation [67,68]. When cardiac hypertrophy progresses, HDAC7 is phosphorylated and is localized constitutively to the cardiomyocyte cytoplasm which induces the activation of the c-Myc proto-oncogene thereby leading to cardiomyocyte stress responses [68]. C1q/tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related protein 9 (CTRP9) induces HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation via AMPK in VECs which promotes angiogenesis by increased MEF2 activity [67]. The relationship between NOXs and HDAC7 has not been described. However, based on previous studies, we speculate that NOX-ROS mediated-phosphorylation of HDAC7 may exert damage in cardiomyocytes, whereas a protective effect may be elicited in vascular endothelium.

The relationship of class III HDACs and NOXs in CVDs

Class III HDACs consist of the Sirtuins 1 to 7 which are most similar to the Sir2 protein in yeast. Sirtuins are involved in numerous physiological and pathological process in cells, including stress resistance, apoptosis, inflammation, and biorhythms. Sirtuins are NAD+-dependent proteases that possess a zinc finger structure and a Rossmann fold domain that bind nucleotides. The SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 proteins are distributed in mitochondria, SIRT1, SIRT6, and SIRT7 are located mainly in the nucleus, and SIRT2 is found primarily in the cytoplasm [9]. Class III HDACs play important roles in the occurrence and development of CVDs. It has been reported that SIRT1, SIRT2 and SIRT6 interact with NOXs to affect the production of superoxide in CVDs, whereas the regulatory effects of SIRT3, SIRT4, SIRT5, and SIRT7 on NOXs in CVDs deserve further investigation in the future (Fig. 4).

The relationship of class III HDACs and NOXs in CVDs. SIRT1 inhibits the activity of NOX2 and subsequent superoxide production. SIRT1 also down-regulates the acetylation of H3K9 and results in the transcriptional inhibition of NOX4. CASC2 induced the expression of NOX2 thereby inhibiting the expression of miR-18a. Moreover, miR-18a suppresses the expression of SIRT2 thereby inhibiting superoxide-induced oxidative stress. SIRT6 deficiency also increases the transcription of NOX2.

SIRT1, NOXs, and CVDs

SIRT1 is a NAD+-dependent deacetylase that is most similar to the Sir2 family of yeast. Clinical investigations have demonstrated that resveratrol, a natural polyphenol found in various plants, has a beneficial effect on CVDs. Resveratrol can activate SIRT1 and exerts protective effects against atherosclerosis and heart failure in low CVD risk individuals [[69], [70], [71]]. SIRT1 upregulation alleviates high glucose-induced endothelial dysfunction and vitamin D reverses oxidative stress-induced endothelial damage through SIRT1 activation [72,73]. In addition, SIRT1 is involved in a broad range of cellular functions for endothelial protection against vascular diseases. The phosphorylation of SIRT1 at Ser 47 induced by atypical cyclin-dependent kinase 5 activates the protein and exerts antisenescence effects on vascular ageing [74]. Moreover, mice that lack SIRT1 have abnormally developed hearts and a severely shortened lifespan which suggests that SIRT1 plays is key to maintaining cardiac protective function.

SIRT1 is important in oxidative stress resistance by diminishing NOX production [75]. Inhibition of SIRT1 promotes the upregulation of NOX2, eventually leading to endothelial dysfunction due to the production of superoxide [76]. Maternal exercise during pregnancy increased SIRT1 expression of blood vessel which lead to a low acetylation level of H3K9 and resulted in the transcriptional inhibition of NOX4 in mesenteric arteries of hypertensive fetuses [77]. These findings indicate that maternal exercise relieves oxidative stress and endothelium-dependent vasodilation injury through SIRT1-mediated NOX4 deacetylation which may contribute to improved vascular function in hypertensive offspring. Meanwhile, quercetin-induced upregulation of SIRT1 enhanced AMPK activity and decreased NADPH production, thereby suppressing the hyperglycemia-induced oxidant damage in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Therefore, the SIRT1/AMPK/NADPH pathway participates in antioxidant effects of quercetin [78]. These studies revealed that SIRT1 and NOX4 are involved in vascular endothelial injury, myocardial hypertrophy, and the inflammatory response in CVDs.

SIRT2, NOXs, and CVDs

SIRT2 is primarily located in the cytoplasm but can also translocate to the nucleus under certain conditions and performs equivalent functions in both compartments [79]. Deletion of SIRT2 gene in the hearts of mice displayed improved cardiac function which suggests that SIRT2 exerts maladaptive effects in the heart in response to stress [80]. However, treatment of mouse heart with a specific SIRT2 inhibitor reduced cardiac size and attenuated cardiac hypertrophy induced by pressure overload [80]. These data indicate that SIRT2 exerts detrimental effects in the heart and participates in the progression of cardiac hypertrophy which makes it a unique member of the SIRT family. Mechanistic studies suggested that SIRT2 modulated cellular levels and activity of NRF2 which resulted in reduced expression of antioxidant proteins [80]. The Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) reduced the expression and activity of SIRT2 and increased acetylation of p53, thereby enhancing superoxide levels in cardiomyocytes and promoting cellular apoptosis [81].

A mouse model was developed of acute myocardial infarction with reduced expression of the lncRNA cancer susceptibility candidate 2 (CASC2) gene in heart tissue. lncRNA CASC2 induced NOX2 expression thereby inhibiting the expression of miR-18a. Moreover, inhibition of miR-18a promoted the expression of SIRT2 thereby inhibiting superoxide-induced oxidative stress. These observations indicate that lncRNA CASC2 downregulates the SIRT2/superoxide pathway and further confirm that the SIRT2/NOX2 signaling pathway is implicated in acute myocardial infarction [82].

SIRT6, NOXs, and CVDs

SIRT6 is one of the few sirtuins that is located predominantly in the nucleus. The protein is implicated in aging, metabolism, and CVDs [83]. SIRT6 expression is decreased in the lesions of atherosclerosis from human patients and ApoE−/− mice [84,85]. Furthermore, SIRT6 is critical for maintaining endothelial function and increased SIRT6 activity may be a new therapeutic strategy to combat atherosclerosis [86]. The study also demonstrated that SIRT6 decreased proatherogenic gene tumor necrosis factor superfamily member 4 (TNFSF4) expression by binding to and deacetylating H3K9 at the TNFSF4 promoter [86]. In addition, SIRT6 binds to promoters of NKG2D ligand genes and regulates the acetylation levels of H3K9 and H3K56 to stabilize atherosclerotic plaques and limit the progression of atherosclerosis [87]. SIRT6 also acts as a negative regulator of cardiac hypertrophy: SIRT6-deficient mice developed cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure, whereas SIRT6 transgenic mice were protected from hypertrophic stimuli. Furthermore, a new link has been described between SIRT6 and IGF-Akt signaling in the progression of cardiac hypertrophy and failure [88].

The SIRT6 protein also is implicated in oxidative stress pathways. Reduced SIRT6 expression led to the production of superoxide in VECs, enhanced oxidative stress, and impaired endothelial function [89]. Correspondingly, SIRT6 alleviated oxidative stress through diverse signaling pathways in CVDs [83]. Furthermore, SIRT6 deficiency resulted in progressive renal inflammation and fibrosis and increased transcription of NOX2 and CXCL10 in mice [90]. Thus, SIRT6 participates in numerous molecular pathways, including the oxidative stress response.

Class IV HDACs, NOXs, and CVDs

HDAC11 is the only class IV HDAC. In addition to the common catalytic activity shared by the HDAC family, HDAC11 exhibits versatile enzymatic functions as a potent long-chain fatty acid deacylase [91]. HDAC11 is expressed highly in the brain, heart, kidney, and is present in mitochondrial, nuclear, and cytoplasmic compartments depending on the tissue and cell type [92]. Global HDAC11 knockout mice suggest that the protein has dispensable features for tissue development and survival, but also influences a range of pathological and physiological processes, including tumor growth, DNA replication, immune regulation, and the neural function of cocaine [93].

The link between HDAC11 and NOXs in CVDs is ambiguous, but HDAC11 may be involved in the progression of CVDs. Animal experiments showed that lncRNA HDAC11-AS1 levels in the vascular tissue of ApoE−/− mice were suppressed, and HDAC11-AS1 enhanced adropin expression by silencing HDAC11, stimulated lipoprotein lipase expression, and inhibited atherosclerosis development in ApoE−/− mice [94]. In addition, HDAC11 potentially is involved in VEC pyroptosis. HDAC11 promoted VEC pyroptosis via ERG in HUVECs through GSDMD and GSDME activation [95]. Further investigation is required to probe the link between HDAC11 and oxidative stress in CVDs.

Conclusions

In general, the deacetylation of target proteins is associated with expression inhibition of certain genes which is likely to play a positive protective role against CVDs. HDACs are important players in the occurrence and development of CVDs. There is growing evidence that class I-III HDACs interact with NOX1/2/4/5 and inhibit the production of ROS. These HDACs may reduce CVD damage such as myocardial hypertrophy, coronary heart disease, and atherosclerosis, and therefore provide a new clinical target for the treatment of CVDs. In contrast, although class IV HDACs tentatively is linked with CVDs, the pathways that connect these HDACS and NOXs lack robust evidence and require further study.
