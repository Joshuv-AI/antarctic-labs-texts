Tebentafusp (IMCgp100), a first in class immune-mobilizing monoclonal T-cell receptors against cancer (ImmTAC) for HLA-A*02:01 positive uveal melanoma: Product review
Authors: Jonathan E Cohen, Inderjit Mehmi, Mordechai Avner, Justin Moyers, Omid Hamid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTebentafusp (IMCgp100, Kimmtrak) is a first-in-class Immune mobilizing monoclonal T-cell receptors against cancer (ImmTAC). ImmTAC are fusion proteins comprising an affinity-enhanced soluble T-cell receptor (TCR) against a specific peptide-HLA complex and an anti-CD3 single-chain variable fragment (scFv). Tebentafusp binds gp100(280–288) peptide from the melanocyte lineage-specific protein Glycoprotein 100 presented by HLA-A*02:01; concurrently, it engages CD3 on polyclonal T-cells leading to T-cell activation, and melanoma cell lysis. In the phase III IMCgp100-202 trial, tebentafusp significantly improved overall-survival versus investigator’s choice, establishing it as a first-line treatment for HLA-A*02:01-positive advanced uveal melanoma. The safety profile is characterized by predictable on-target effects, mainly cytokine release syndrome and skin reactions, both diminishing over time. Tebentafusp validated ImmTAC as a novel class of TCR-based biologics targeting intracellular antigens. Ongoing research explores tebentafusp in other settings, including cutaneous melanoma. Other ImmTACs, e.g. brenetefusp is being developed, underscoring the potential of this modality in cancer immunotherapy.KEYWORDS: Uveal melanoma, cancer immunotherapy, Immune-mobilizing monoclonal T-cell receptors against cancer (ImmTAC), T-cell receptor (TCR), human leukocyte antigen (HLA)

Uveal melanoma

Melanoma is a malignant tumor arising from the melanocyte pigment cells. These cells are present in the human body primarily in the skin but also in mucous membranes, such as the oral cavity, rectum, and vulva and conjunctiva as well as in the ocular compartment called the uvea comprising the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. Melanoma arising from the uvea is termed uveal melanoma (UM).

UM is a rare disease with an incidence of ~5 per million.1 The majority of cases are identified as local diseases and treated with brachytherapy, external beam radiation, or surgery. Despite optimal local therapy, up to 50% of the cases develop distant metastases.2 The most common site for metastasis is the liver, which is involved in 91% cases, with additional sites including lung (16%), bone (9%), brain (6%), and other rare sites.3 In the metastatic setting, prognosis is poor with a median overall survival of 10.2 months prior to the introduction of novel therapies.4

There are significant distinctions between uveal and other sites of melanoma despite their shared origin. UM harbors largely unique mutations, the most common being in GNA11\GNAQ appearing in ~90% of the cases; additional mutations include, for example, SFB31, BAP1, and EIF1AX. Chromosomal aberrations are also common, including Chr. 3 monosomy and Chr. 8 trisomy.5 Tumor mutational burden, often associated with response to cancer immunotherapy and typically high in cutaneous melanoma, is rarely elevated in UM.6 These fundamental biological differences explain, at least partially, why UM does not respond well to therapies that have revolutionized care in melanoma: immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI): PD-1, CTLA4 and LAG-3 blockade, and targeted therapies aimed at the BRAF and MEK proteins.

ICI have been employed in UM with modest efficacy. In cutaneous melanoma, PD-1 blockade leads to a ~40% tumor objective response rate (ORR), while the combination of CTLA-4 and PD-1 blockade has a 58% ORR7; this response to combination therapy is accompanied by over 50% melanoma-specific survival at 10 y.8 A recent meta-analysis conducted on a pooled population of 1414 UM patients found an only 3.4% vs. 12.4% ORR for anti-PD1 monotherapy vs. CTLA4/PD-1 blockade, with a median survival of 9.8 vs. 16.3 months.9 This evidence indicates a very modest efficacy. While several targeted therapies for UM are under investigation, until recently there was no phase III established systemic treatment for UM, creating an immense unmet need.

Melanocyte differentiation antigens as targets

Melanocytes have several distinct features, largely retained in their malignant progeny and shared by both cutaneous and UM. These include proteins involved in differentiation, such as the melanocyte inducing transcription factor (MITF), melanin biosynthesis, such as tyrosinase and melanosome formation. e.g. MELAN-A. Differentiations associated with antigens derived from such proteins have the advantage as immunotherapy targets robust expression across melanoma patients, on the one hand (as opposed to patient-specific neo-antigens) and are specific to melanocyte lineage cells, limiting potential cross-reactivity with other tissue types on the other. Glycoprotein 100 (gp100) is a transmembrane protein encoded by the premelanosome protein (PMEL) gene and is also involved in melanosome formation. A recent study found high expression levels of gp100 in UM cell lines and in both primary and metastatic cutaneous melanoma, with stronger expression in samples derived from metastases. Significantly, some level of expression was observed almost universally.10 Previous studies have established a strong and universal gp100 expression for UM.11 Indeed, an antibody targeting gp100, HMB-45 is employed for histological confirmation of a melanoma diagnosis. A peptide derived from gp100, YLEPGPVTA from positions 280–288 is presented on HLA*A 02:01. Previous studies have shown that tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) leading to melanoma regression are often reactive against this peptide. Early attempts were made to utilize this for dendritic cell-based vaccination with immunological responses12 but unconvincing clinical results.13 Nevertheless, gp100 remained an attractive target for melanoma-specific immunotherapy.

Origin and research basis for the design of the product

Traditional cancer-immunotherapy, such as ICI, relies on native autologous cancer-identifying immune effector cells. A more novel approach that has potential efficacy in patients in whom such cells are not available or are insufficient, circumvents the antigen recognition machinery of the effector cells, instead binding and activating these cells directly, on the one hand, while binding and bridging tumor cells on the other. The proximity of activated effector cells to tumor cells leads to direct cytotoxicity while also stimulating a broader inflammatory response. This type of treatment leads to polyclonal effector-cell activation, irrespective of their receptor repertoire. The classical instance of this class of drugs is bispecific t-cell engagers (BiTE). Tarlatamab is one of the first BiTE to be approved in solid tumors; it binds CD8+ cells through its CD3 engaging arm and DLL3 (delta-like ligand 3), which is overexpressed by small cell lunger cancer cells (SCLC) through the other; this therapy is HLA independent. The phase III DeLLphi-304 study compared durvalumab (PD-L1 blockade) alone to the combination of tarlatamab in the second line advanced SCLC. It demonstrated a significant survival advantage to the combination, median OS 13.6 vs 8.3 months, HR 0.6.14 A similar approach is that of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) engineered T-cells. In CAR-T, an antibody-based recognition element is introduced ex-vivo to autologous lymphocytes, then the product is infused back to the patient. This form of therapy is highly toxic and costly. A limitation of BiTE and CAR-T, inherent to their antibody structure, is their requirement for cell-surface expressed cancer-specific antigens. This is a major obstacle, as the majority of cancer-associated antigens are intra-cellular.

T-cell receptors (TCR) are the naturally occurring antigen recognition elements of lymphocytes. They bond peptides from degraded proteins, expressed in human leukocyte antigen (MHC) molecules, which can be derived from both the intra- and extracellular compartments. TCR’s recognize a specific peptide only in the context of a specific HLA; as HLA haplotypes vary considerably in the population, TCR’s aimed at cancer-associated peptides will bind only to those with the relevant HLA. Because TCR’s are highly specific and require few HLA-peptide complexes in order to activate their T-cell,15 TCR-based therapeutics have the advantage of high specificity and coverage of the entire proteome but the disadvantage of being HLA restricted. A noteworthy limitation of targeting HLA-bound antigens is the phenomena of tumor HLA downregulation as a means of immune-evasion.16 This has been described in cutaneous melanoma (e.g.,17), and variable expression of HLA class I and II has also been described in UM.18,19

Preclinical studies and toxicology

ImmTAC were first described in a peer-reviewed publication by Liddy et al.20 in 2012 (though data were released earlier in abstract form, e.g., Hassan et al., AACR 201021). Four ImmTAC were developed targeting peptides derived from two melanoma differentiation proteins: gp100280–288 (YLEPGPVTA) and MELAN-A/MART-126–35 (EAAGIGILTV) and two cancer-testis antigens: MAGE-A3168–176 (EVDPIGHLY) and Ny-ESO-1157–165 (SLLMWITQC) all in the context of HLA-A*02:01.20 The initial TCR sequences had been previously identified from isolated peptide-MHC reactive patient-derived lymphocytes. Through induced mutagenesis, the soluble high-affinity progeny of these TCRs was generated, selected22 and fused with CD3 directed short-chain variable fragments (scFv, Figure 1A).23 In co-culture experiments of CD8+ cells with antigen expressing, but not antigen non-expressing, melanoma cell lines or T2 antigen-loaded cells, the ImmTAC induced interferon-gamma secretion. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) incubated with ImmTAC and antigen expressing melanoma cells showed activation of not only the CD8+ subset but also CD4+ cells.20,21 Crucially, ImmTAC were able to induce melanoma lysis by Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) specific monoclonal CD8+ T-cells, which are normally non-reactive with melanoma. In xenograft mouse models (NOD-SCID and Beige-SCID), ImmTAC (including to gp100) inhibited tumor growth in the presence of PBMCs in a dose-dependent manner.

Acquired resistance to cancer immunotherapies may develop in multiple mechanisms.24 An important consideration in monoclonal single-antigen targeting is the downregulation or eliminated expression of the target antigen. This might be circumvented by eliciting an inflammatory response that can overcome a prohibitive tumor-environment and unleash an autologous cancer-specific immune response. Bossi et al.25 showed that following the incubation of viral-specific CD8+ cells with IMCgp100 and HLAA 02:01, gp100+ melanoma cells, dendritic cells displayed melanoma-specific antigens. This presentation increased activation by IMCgp100, but more importantly also increased interferon-gamma secretion from MELAN-A specific T-cells, attesting to the broader potential to recruit an inflammatory response. Boudousquie et al.26 explored the effect of various CD8+ phenotypes on IMCgp100 killing efficacy. They found that terminally differentiated effector T-cells (Temra) and effector memory cells (Tem) had the highest efficacy. Furthermore, CD4+ cells were also cytotoxic in the presence of IMCgp100. Importantly, both CD8+ and CD4+ cells secreted an array of pro-inflammatory cytokines, again suggestive of the potential of IMCgp100 to induce a broader pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment.

Safety evaluation of T-cell based therapies in the pre-clinical setting is challenging as these TCR’s are human-specific. With IMCgp100, the anti-CD3 moiety specifically identifies human lymphocytes and the TCR moiety recognizes the human HLA-peptide complex; animal models are not likely to represent the true safety profile of the drug. In order to gain genuine safety information in the pre-clinical setting, an in-vitro set of tests was developed by the manufacturer and is described by Harper et al.27; testing was performed on several ImmTAC’s, of which only IMCgp100 was advanced to clinical trials. To test for on-target toxicity, healthy melanocytes were employed from HLA A * 02 donors and incubated with PBMCs and IMCgp100. INFgamma secretion was observed in a dose-dependent manner. However, as melanocytes are the sole cells with gp100 expression, expected toxicity was considered acceptable. To rule out a nonspecific activation of immune cells by ImmTAC, whole blood from HLA concordant donors was incubated with the target ImmTAC and cytokine secretion was evaluated. As compared with anti-CD3 alone or in combination with anti-CD28 activation, cytokine secretions were negligible. Similarly, HLA dis-concordant cell lines showed no activation by the ImmTAC. While TCR’s are known to be exquisitely specific, cross-reactivity with non-tumor specific peptides is a concern. Two methods were employed to address this. In an alanine-scan, systematically, each amino-acid in the target-peptide is replaced with an alanine. Non-essential amino-acids are thus mapped, and the sequence of essential amino-acids can be searched for sequence homology with human proteins. In the second approach, each amino-acid was replaced by each of the remaining amino-acids, and the resultant peptides were tested for reactivity with the ImmTAC.

Production

Kimmtrak is a soluble, affinity-enhanced TCR fused into a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) of an anti-CD3 antibody with a molecular weight of ~77KD. It is composed of two chains alpha and beta, joined by a disulfide-bridge. The two chains are manufactured separately. Each chain is expressed via recombinant DNA in Escherichia coli cell cultures. The separate chains are then joined, purified, and filtered for sterility.28 Details of the production are proprietary and undisclosed.

Clinical studies

Safety and efficacy

Early phase

The first-in human study with tebentafusp was initiated in 2010, NCT01211262. This study enrolled 84 patients with advanced line stage IV or unresectable melanoma, cutaneous (n = 61), uveal (n = 19), or other (n = 4), who were HLA A * 02+. Two dosing regimens were explored: a weekly dose from 5 to 900 ng/Kg (n = 31) and a daily dose from 10 to 50 µg given for four consecutive days once every 21 d (n = 18). At the 900 ng/Kg dose, two patients experienced DLTs of hypotension. The MTD was determined to be 600 ng/Kg. Thirty-five additional patients were enrolled in an expansion phase and received either 600 ng/Kg or 50 µg weekly. Ninety-nine percent of patients had any grade treatment related adverse effects (TRAE), only two leading to drug discontinuation. Common grade 3 and above AE’s included rash and lymphopenia. Any grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) appeared in 60% patients, none reaching grade 3.29

One-year OS was 65% and was similar for cutaneous and UM cases. ORR was 8.7% representing six patients (three each from cutaneous and UM). Tebentafusp treatment was associated with an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines: CXCL10, CXCL11, IL2, IL6, and IL10. Notably, cytokine elevations were transient and decreased with repeated dosing, suggesting the development of tachyphylaxis. An increase in CXCL10 was negatively correlated with peripheral blood CXCR3+CD8+ cells, which may reflect trafficking of these cells to the tumor. Indeed, pre- and on-treatment biopsies showed an increase in T-cell infiltration following tebentafusp treatment in most samples.

IMCgp100-102 was a phase I/II study of IMCgp100, (NCT02570308) focusing solely on the UM population, conducted between March 2016 and December 2017. Major inclusion criteria included: metastatic UM and HLA A * 02+ or HLA A * 02:01+. The initial dose-escalation phase had a 3 + 3 design to define MTD and recommended phase two dose (RP2D). The drug was administered weekly, with an intra-patient dose-escalation. The “step-up” dose design was intended to mitigate toxicity while allowing tachyphylaxis to develop. The first dose comprised 20 µg, followed by 30 µg and then the designated dose, starting with 54 µgat week 3. Nineteen patients were treated in the phase I portion, additional tested doses included: 64 µg, 73 µg, and 68 µg. Three patients developed DLT’s of increased liver functions, one at 64 µg and two at 73 µg, all resolved spontaneously. This led to a step-down to the 68 mcg dose with no observed DLTs and this was determined to be the RP2D (Figure 1B). The expansion portion of the trial enrolled additional 23 patients.

In the dose escalation portion, 68% had received prior immunotherapy and 63% received liver-directed therapy, and 89% had hepatic involvement. In the dose expansion, all had received prior immunotherapy, 70% liver directed therapy, and all had hepatic involvement. All patients experienced treatment for emergent adverse effects, 71% of whom had a grade 3 or higher event. Common AE’s included pyrexia, nausea, chills, pruritus, and fatigue. Common grade 3 and above AE’s included abdominal pain, hypotension, and hypophosphatemia. Two patients developed AEs that led to treatment discontinuation due to CRS and abdominal pain. Ninety percent of patients developed any grade CRS, all but one were low grade reversing with either fluids or short course corticosteroids; only one case was grade IV. None of the patients required anti-interleukin-6 to reverse the CRS. Skin toxicity was common. Importantly, the severity and incidence decreased with treatment cycles mirroring the cytokine measurements over time as described above. This suggests long-term treatment tolerance with low predicted discontinuation due to toxicity.

With regard to efficacy, 5 of 42 patients achieved an objective response (11.9%) all at the RP2D of 68 mgc; median duration of response was 8.5 months. Median PFS was 4.6 months, median OS was 25.5 months, and the one-year survival was 67%. Patients who developed skin toxicity had superior outcomes. Terminal half-life of the drug was 6–8 hr. A small subset of patients (6) had paired pre- and on-treatment biopsies, these demonstrated in all but one an on-treatment increase in CD4+ and CD8 lymphocytes as well as inflammatory monocytes.30

This trial continued enrollment to the phase II portion beyond the initial publication with ORR as the primary endpoint. A total of 127 patients were enrolled, 96% had hepatic disease, 58% elevated LDH, and all patients had received at least one prior line of therapy, 72% of these ICI’s. ORR was 5%, disease control at 24 weeks was 23%. Estimated OS at 1-y was 62% and 37% at 2-y. Common toxicities included rash and CRS, however 3.1% had grade III and 0.8% grade IV CRS. Two patients developed severe CRS, both at cycle 1, which led to drug discontinuation, one of which required tocilizumab (anti-IL6). Both rash and CRS showed a clear decrease in incidence and severity with repeated administration.31 A later analysis reported results for gp100 IHC staining in this population. Gp100 H-score was not associated with survival, however of the six objective responses seen, five had an above median gp100 H-score.32 In a transcriptomic analysis of patient tumor biopsies, division based on gp100 expression with the threshold at the 1st quartile, showed an on-treatment more substantial increase in lymphocyte infiltration in the high expressors yet no difference in survival.33 Thus, gp100 IHC does not lend itself as a predictive biomarker for patient selection for tebentafusp treatment. Additional findings from this transcriptomic analysis highlight the prognostic value of INGgamma response genes at baseline and the on-treatment increase in “stemness” associated genes such as IL-7R.

Registrational study

These initial encouraging results together with a manageable safety profile led to the initiation of the IMCgp100-202 phase III randomized, open-label study in patients with untreated advanced UM (NCT03070392). HLA A * 02:01+ patients were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to tebentafusp 69 µg weekly (after two initial doses of 20 and 30 mgc) or investigator’s choice from: pembrolizumab (2 mg/Kg or a flat dose of 200 mg Q21D), ipilimumab (3 mg/Kg Q21D) or dacarbazine (1000 mg/m2 Q21D). Treatment beyond progression was allowed if deemed beneficial. Cross-over was initially prohibited, but following a positive interim analysis was subsequently allowed. Patients treated with tebentafusp were observed overnight as in-patients for the 1st three weeks. The primary endpoint was OS.

A total of 378 patients were randomized to tebentafusp (n = 252) or control (n = 126), 120 (82%) received pembrolizumab, 16 (13%) ipilimumab, and 7 (6%) dacarbazine. LDH was similarly increased in 36–37% of the patients, hepatic only involvement was seen in 52% vs. 47%, extrahepatic only in 4% vs. 8%, and the remainder had both in the tebentafusp vs. Control groups, respectively.

In the initial publication in 2021,34 the estimated 1-y OS was 73% (CI 66–79) vs. 59% (CI 48–67) for tebentafusp vs. Control. The estimated median OS was 21.7 vs. 16.0 months, hazard ratio (HR) 0.51, p < .001. In a subsequent report from 2023,35 with a minimum follow up of 36 months, the median OS was similar 21.6 vs. 16.9 months, HR 0.68 (CI 0.54–0.87). The 3-y survival was 27% vs. 18%. It should be noted that 16 patients from the control group had crossed over to receive tebentafusp; however, removing them from the analysis had a negligible effect on the hazard ratio. In the tebentafusp arm, 202 patients had pre- and on-treatment (week 9) serum samples for ctDNA analysis. In 123 (61%), there was a tumor-associated mutation identifiable in the serum. Patients with undetectable ctDNA had a better OS. More interestingly, patients with ctDNA clearance had a superior OS compared with non-clearance 29.6 vs. 10.2 months. Similarly, a 50% cutoff for ctDNA decrease was prognostic with an HR for death of 0.41.

In the initial 2021 publication, a sub-analysis was performed looking at patients with progressive disease as their best response. Surprisingly, tebentafusp remained superior with a median OS of 15.3 vs. 6.5 months, HR 0.43 (CI 0.27–0.68) in this population and in fact seems to have explained a large part of the benefit for tebentafusp. A further analysis stratifying patients with progressive disease based on continued- or stopped-tebentafusp beyond progression found a non-significant trend toward improved survival in those treated beyond progression after controlling for prognostic factors at baseline and at time of progression.36 This has led to a paradigm by which treatment with tebentafusp may be continued beyond initial progression.

ORR was 11% vs. 5% in tebentafusp vs. control with one complete response (<1%) in the tebentafusp arm. Disease control at 12 weeks (i.e. no progressive disease) was 46% for tebentafusp vs. 27% for control. Median PFS was 3.4 vs. 2.9 months, HR 0.76 (CI 0.60–0.97).

Real-world data

Following the regulatory approval for tebentafusp and its uptake in standard practice, several real-world data reports were published. A French multicenter study identified 23 patients treated with tebentafusp. Of these, 48% had received prior systemic therapy. The 1-y OS was 66%, median PFS was 5.7 months. There was one CR (4%), an additional 18% PR and 41% SD.37 A multicenter study from Germany and Switzerland included 78 patients. Of these, 35.9% had previously received liver-directed therapy and 51.3% had received systemic therapy, largely ICI. PR was seen in 8.7%, SD in 27.5%; median OS was 22 months. In this cohort, one case of tumor lysis syndrome was noted. A non-significant trend was seen in survival favoring tebentafusp administration following ICI as opposed to those given in the 1st line setting.38

A report from four European centers on their experience in the framework of an early access program includes 175 patients treated with tebentafusp. About 50% had received prior treatment, over 20% with ICI. The ORR was 5%, and clinical benefit rate was 52%. Importantly, 43% patients continued tebentafusp beyond progression, with 40% becoming stable disease and two converted to responders, one a CR. The median OS was 20 months, and 1-y survival was 63.6%. When comparing those who received tebentafusp as 1st line vs. Subsequent lines, PFS and OS were comparable. With regard to toxicity, 18.3% patients experienced a grade III or IV AE.39

An additional French study was a prospective analysis of ctDNA using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) as a prognostic and predictive biomarker in UM patients treated with tebentafusp. Sixty-nine patients were included. Sequencing of the tumor was performed to identify somatic mutation, in a few cases where tissue was not available sequencing was performed on blood samples. Ninety-seven percent had mutations identified GNAQ (n = 37), GNA11 (n = 29) or SF3B1 (n = 1). Patients with an identified mutation were followed by serial ddPCR targeting this mutation at baseline, 3 and 12 weeks and upon progression. The median OS for the entire cohort was 21.8 months. CtDNA was detectable in 61% of patients, 67% of these had reduced ctDNA at 3 weeks, 22% had been cleared completely. Baseline positive ctDNA was a poor prognostic marker with a striking HR of 10.1. However, ctDNA clearance at 12 weeks indicated a considerably improved outcome, including for those with progressive disease by RECIST.40

These real-world series are in-line with the phase III data in a selected patient population, affirming the efficacy of tebentafusp in daily practice (see Table 1 for summary of outcomes in both prospective and retrospective trials).

Tebentafusp vs. ipilimumab/nivolumab as 1st line and preferred sequence

It is noteworthy that while the benefit in retrospective studies from combination CTLA4/PD-1 blockade is modest as described above, it is superior to monotherapy and remains the standard of care for patients with advanced UM who do not carry the HLA A 02:01 allele. Thus, the choice of the control arm options in IMCgp100-202 (largely pembrolizumab) may have favored the study drug. Several retrospective studies have attempted to address this by indirectly comparing tebentafusp with the ipilimumab/nivolumab (ipi/nivo) combination with the inherent limitations of such an approach.

A meta-analysis based on published data including 2,682 patients concluded that tebentafusp was superior to ipi/nivo with regard to survival.41 Piulats et al.42 extracted individual patient data from the IMCgp100-202 study and compared this with data for ipilimumab\nivolumab from the single-arm GEM1402 study. The latter was a Spanish study including 52 patients with advanced UM with the outcome of a median OS of 12.7 months and a 1-y survival rate of 51.9%.43 To facilitate the comparison, propensity score matching was performed, controlling for several variables including among others LDH, disease location (i.e. hepatic only, extra-hepatic only or both) and performance status. The primary outcome was OS. To rule out that HLA A 02:01 positivity in itself is prognostic and could bias the comparison, they analyzed an additional cohort of 40 patients, none of whom received tebentafusp. They found no association of HLA status with OS. In the propensity-score analysis, tebentafusp was superior to ipi/nivo with a median OS of 21.7 vs. 12.6 months, 1-y survival of 73% vs. 50% and an HR of 0.52.

A related question regards the optimal sequence of tebentafusp and ipi/nivo. A report of six patients treated evenly with either sequence suggested a possible advantage to the tebentafusp first regimen.44 A large retrospective study included 51 patients treated with tebentafusp first and 80 treated with ICI first. The groups were not entirely balanced with the tebentafusp first cohort having more bi-lobal liver disease and a higher rate of elevated LDH due to poor prognostic features. Median OS favored the ICI first with 33.6 vs. 22.4 moths, p = .004. After controlling for possible confounders using a cox proportional hazards regression analysis OS remained superior for the ICI first cohort.45 Still the national comprehensive cancer network (NCCN) guidelines for the treatment of advanced melanoma defers ICI to failure following tebentafusp therapy.46

Use in cutaneous melanoma

As mentioned earlier, the FIH study showed activity also in cutaneous melanoma, and the biological rationale for its use in this indication is solid. A phase Ib study explored the administration of tebentafusp with the PD-L1 blocker durvalumab (durva) and/or the CTLA4 blocker tremelimumab (trem) in advanced line melanoma. Eighty-five patients were enrolled in receiving durva (n = 43), trem (n = 13), or both (n = 29). In 72 patients available for efficacy analysis, there was a 14% ORR and 76% 1-y survival. In a sub-set of 58 patients who had progressed on prior PD(L)-1 blockade, there was a 10% ORR, 75% 1-y and 32% 2-y OS.47 A randomized open-label phase III study (NCT05549297) is currently enrolling; investigating the administration of (1) tebentafusp, (2) tebentafusp with pembrolizumab, or (3) investigators choice of therapy in advanced line non-ocular melanoma.

On-going trials

Ongoing trials are investigating tebentafusp as adjuvant therapy in high-risk UM (ATOM trial, NCT06246149) and neoadjuvant therapy (NEO-TB, NCT07057596, and NCT06414590). Combination strategies are also being initially explored, including local therapy with percutaneous hepatic perfusion (PHP) with melphalan (NCT07276386) or liver directed radioembolization (NCT06627244) and systemic combinations including roginolisib (a PI3Kδ inhibitor, NCT07203391) and Interleukin-2 (NCT07063875).

An ImmTAC targeting PRAME (Brenetafusp, IMC-F06C), a cancer testis antigen expressed in multiple tumor types, is under investigation, and initial data has been presented.48 As mechanistically predictable, this drug elicited pyrexia and CRS, similar to tebentafusp, but did not show substantial dermatological toxicity as PRAME in contrast to gp100 is not expressed in normal skin. Responses were reported in cutaneous and UM as well as ovarian cancer. Brenetafusp is being investigated currently in the phase III randomized PRISM-MEL-301 trial in combination with nivolumab for the treatment of naive advanced melanoma (NCT06112314).

Safety

The safety profile of tebentafusp is characterized by predictable, on-target toxicities that are generally manageable and decreases in incidence and severity over time. Safety data from the three Immunocore sponsored trials were pooled and analyzed jointly, including 410 patients in total.49 Sixty-one percent of patients were treatment naive, yet there was no apparent difference in AE incidence between pre-treated and naive patients. Any grade AE was observed almost uniformly (99.5%), most commonly pyrexia (77%), pruritus (71%), and chills (53%). Gr III AE’s appeared in 46% of patients, including rash and pruritus, increased LFTs and hypotension. Six percent had Gr IV AE’s consisting of either lymphopenia or hypotension. There were no deaths attributable to tebentafusp. To note, in the randomized trial, the control arm consisting largely of pembrolizumab monotherapy, had a GR III and an above rate of 17%.34 However, the combination of ipilimumab and nivolumab in the checkmate 067 trials had a ≥GR III rate of 59%.7 Most tebentafusp-related AE-s appear early on treatment with incidence and prevalence decreasing with repeated cycles and becoming rare after the 1st three months. For example, median time to onset and resolution were the following (in days): CRS, 1, 2; acute skin toxicities, 1, 31; LFT increases 13.5, 22. Vitiligo and other pigmentation changes appeared later on up to 12 months but were rare thereafter.

CRS was reported in 88%, 19% Gr I, and 67% Gr II. Pyrexia was reported in nearly all cases and was an early sign of CRS, appearing 2–4 hr post-infusion. Most cases of CRS were resolved within a day from development. No pre-medication was provided in the clinical trials to prevent CRS. In treating CRS, the drugs employed included anti-pyretics in 88%, intravenous fluids in 45%, systemic corticosteroids in 25%, and tocilizumab in 1%. A post-hoc analysis found no effect of corticosteroids on survival.

Skin toxicity appeared in 92% patients, 21% being Gr III, and these varied and included pruritus, rash, erythema, skin exfoliation, and periorbital edema. Histological analysis showed lymphocyte infiltration adjacent to melanocytes within biopsies from skin-toxicity as well as a decrease in melanocyte number likely representing their immune-elimination. Skin toxicities have been associated in some reports with improved outcomes for tebentafusp (e.g.50,51). This was also true in the initial analysis of the phase III trial.34 However, a later analysis found that rash is associated with good prognostic factors and when taking this into account in a multi-variate analysis found that rash was not associated with improved OS.52 Skin toxicities were managed with antihistamines in 76% of cases, topical steroids in 59%. See supplementary Table 1 for detailed safety data across studies.

Advanced age does not appear to significantly impact the safety or tolerability of Tebentafusp based on currently available evidence. In the pivotal phase III trial and supporting regulatory datasets, approximately 47% patients were aged ≥65 y and ~9% were ≥75 y, with no overall differences in safety or efficacy observed between older and younger subgroups.53 Accordingly, no dose adjustments are required in the geriatric population.54 In the EUROCAN RWE report, ~30% of 175 patients were ≥70; gr III or IV AEs were similar in below, 19.5% and above 70, 15.4% patients39 supporting safety in a non-selected elderly clinical population. Cardiovascular comorbidity has not formally been investigated in the context of tebentafusp toxicity; however, this could potentially put patients at risk in the case of CRS. Most tebentafusp- induces CRS cases are mild, yet it would be prudent to monitor such patients more closely during the initial cycles of therapy in which CRS is most common.

Mitigating toxicity

Strategies for mitigating toxicity have been devised. This has included pre-hydration with intravenous fluids and holding anti-hypertensive therapy. For secondary prophylaxis following CRS, premedication with corticosteroids at a dose of 4 mg dexamethasone or equivalent has been suggested; and for patients with adrenal insufficiency, stress doses of steroids such as primary prophylaxis. Antipyretics could be considered, however, as the often-earliest sign of CRS, these could mask symptoms and delay intervention.55

Regulatory issues

The results of IMCgp100-202 led to an FDA approval in January 2022 of tebentafusp for HLA-A*02:01-positive adult patients with unresectable or metastatic UM. It was similarly approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in April 2022. In fact, the regulatory review of tebentafusp was carried out under project Orbis, which facilitates concurrent review by various international agencies. In the case of tebentafusp, the Canadian, Australian, and UK regulatory agencies took part.

Public-health implications

The Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH) carried out a pharmaco-economic analysis of tebentafusp utility. They estimated the cost per patient for tebentafusp treatment to be 1,062,137 Canadian dollars (CAD), with a quality-adjusted life year (QALY) of 2.4 as compared to a cost of 147,736 CAD and QALY of 1.14 for the comparator. This yielded an Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) of 728,513 CAD per QALY. For reference, a QALY of 50,000 CAD is often considered cost-effective.56 Similar analyses were conducted by the United Kingdom’s NICE yielding an ICER of over 250,000 British pounds.57

It is noteworthy that the prevalence of the HLA A 02:01 haplotype in the global population is variable. The prevalence in European descent populations is highest 27.1%, is ~19% in Hispanic and native American, 17.4% in Middle Eastern and North African, 11.9% in African and African American, and 6.5% in Asian and Pacific Islan populations.58 This has substantial implication regarding the relevance of tebentafusp in various regions. This has been flagged as an issue of disparity in health care attention to the development of drugs for HLA haplotypes frequent in diverse populations.59 HLA-A*02:01 may be more prevalent in the UM population than in the base population, in part due to its higher prevalence in patients of North European descent. In the registrational IMCgp100-202 trial, 1,072 patients were screened for HLA, of which 551 (51%) were HLA-A*02:01 positive.34 This should be interpreted with caution due to potential biases, such as countries in which the study was conducted.

Advantages and disadvantages relative to other products for UM

Therapeutic options in advanced UM are limited. The modest contribution of ICI has already been discussed.

The majority of advanced UM patients are diagnosed with liver metastases. In the context of oligometastatic or hepatic-only disease, several methods for local therapy have been employed including surgery, ablation, and radiotherapy. A recent publication retrospectively explored the outcome of adding local therapy to tebentafusp after initial progression of this drug. The series included 30 patients treated with radiation for soft tissue, liver-directed therapy, or both. While the median PFS for tebentafusp alone was 5.8 months, the median PFS2, defined as PFS after the addition of local therapy, was 8.1 months, suggesting a possible role for combination therapy.60

The FDA has approved isolated liver perfusion with melphalan using the Hepzato kit, which was evaluated in the FOCUS study. This trial enrolled 102 patients, 92 of whom were treated; among those there was a 36.3% ORR and a medial OS of 20.5 months.61 A recent publication details results from the phase II randomized CHOPIN trial of percutaneous hepatic perfusion alone or with ipilimumab\nivolumab in treatment naïve patients with liver only or predominant UM (38 patients per arm). Combination improved 1-y PFS from 15.8% to 54.7%, HR 0.34, this was associated with increased high-grade toxicity from 41% to 82%.62,63 This combination is further supported by an earlier single-arm SCANDIUM II trial, with a similar regimen showing response in 4 of 7 patients receiving the ICI therapy following hepatic perfusion (57%) and 2 of 9 (22%) in a cohort receiving ICI before and after perfusion.64

Darovasertibis is a PKC-gamma inhibitor capitalizing on the common GNA11/GNAQ mutations that activate downstream PKC and other pathways. In the phase I-II OptimUM-01 trial expansion cohort of darovasertib with crizotinib, ORR was 34% (14 of 41) with a median response of 9 months; disease control rate was 90%, OS was 21 months.65 In the neoadjuvant setting, initial data for darovasertib showed 19 of 32 patients with tumor shrinkage converting from planned enucleation to eligibility for vision preservation.66 A phase III clinical trial (OptimUM-02, NCT05987332) completed accrual investigating the combination darovasertib with crizotinib vs. investigator’s choice (pembrolizumab, ipilimumab + nivolumab, or dacarbazine) in treatment naïve metastatic UM that are HLA-A*02:01 negative.

The newest promising data in metastatic UM comes from the presentation of the IMA203 study by Patel67 at the ESMO 2025 conference. IMA203 (Anzutresgene Autoleucel) is a TCR-T targeting PRAME restricted to HLA A 02:01. In a pre-treated population, in 15 evaluable patients, an ORR or 67% was reported with 5 of 10 responses ongoing at a median follow up of 13.4 months.

Conclusion

Tebentafusp was validated as a first in class ImmTAC, being the first drug to show a survival advantage in advanced UM. It has been adopted as first-line therapy for advanced UM HLA A 02:01 carrier and is being investigated for cutaneous melanoma as well. The drug has a predictable, on-target toxicity profile, which is manageable and largely transient. Despite the significant results for this novel modality, the absolute gain in survival is modest, and synergistic strategies might be required to maximize the clinical impact.
