A Multi-Regional Single-nucleus Atlas of the Huntington’s Disease Brain
Authors: Jiawei Li, Qin Zhou, Chunxiang Shi, Yingqi Lin, Bofeng Han, Jiale Gao, Jiacheng Li, Caijuan Li, Yizhi Chen, Chunhui Huang, Jianhao Wu, Wei Wang, Junzhu Song, Yuanpei Zhang, Qingqing He, Wentai Wang, Zhuchi Tu, Shihua Li, Xiao-Jiang Li, Bo Ning, Sen Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuntington’s disease (HD) is a neurodegenerative disorder caused by an abnormal expansion of the CAG trinucleotide repeat sequence in the HTT gene. Research on HD, a rare condition, relies heavily on donations of post-mortem brain tissue, which are extremely valuable yet difficult to obtain. While numerous studies have published single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from the brain tissues of HD patients, such datasets remain scattered across various public repositories. A lack of standardized metadata and analytical workflows has hindered the integration and reutilization of these resources. To address this challenge, we constructed a curated, multi-brain-region, single-nucleus atlas of controls/HD patients by integrating 104 tissue samples from 39 HD patients and 34 controls, spanning six brain regions: the striatum (caudate and putamen), frontal cortex, cingulate cortex, nucleus accumbens, hippocampus, and cerebellum. This atlas comprises 330,892 cells that have been subjected to standardized quality control, batch effect correction and cell type annotation. Here, we present the first large-scale, multi-region single-nucleus reference atlas for HD, providing a comprehensive resource for the research community. It will serve as a critical data foundation for deepening the investigation of cell-type-specific molecular mechanisms underlying HD, facilitating cross-brain-region experimental validation, and enable the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

Background & Summary

Huntington’s disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by an abnormal expansion (>36 repeats) of the CAG trinucleotide repeat sequence in exon 1 of the HTT gene. Its core clinical symptoms include choreiform movements, cognitive decline, and psychiatric abnormalities, which ultimately progress to death1,2. At the neuropathological level, HD is characterized by severe striatal atrophy and lateral ventricular enlargement, accompanied by the selective loss of medium spiny neurons in the striatum3–5. The cerebral cortex is also extensively affected, exhibiting cortical thinning and a reduction in pyramidal neurons6–8. Notably, motor cortex atrophy correlates with the severity of motor symptoms, whereas neuronal loss in the cingulate cortex is particularly prominent in HD patients with severe cognitive and psychiatric deficits7,9. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this selective neuronal loss remain unclear. Currently, mechanistic insights into HD pathogenesis in the field are largely derived from mouse models, which exhibit incomplete phenotypic recapitulation of the human disease10–12. Substantial interspecies differences exist between mouse and human brains, including disparities in brain volume, the absence of cortical sulci, and the inability to distinguish the putamen and caudate nucleus from the striatum13. These differences severely limit the elucidation of HD patient-specific pathogenic mechanisms and the identification of effective therapeutic targets.

With the rapid advancement of snRNA-seq technology, multiple studies have performed single-nucleus transcriptome sequencing on brain tissues from HD patients. This technology provides a unique perspective for systematically dissecting cell-type-specific alterations, intercellular interactions, and mechanisms of neuronal degeneration in HD at single-nucleus resolution14. However, these valuable datasets lack unified curation and standardized processing, which limits their potential for comprehensive integration and reutilization15,16. In addition, the massive scale of single-nucleus data and the complexity of analytical workflows pose substantial challenges for researchers without expertise in bioinformatics. Thus, the systematic curation, standardized processing, and open sharing of HD patient single-nucleus datasets scattered across different platforms have become an urgent necessity to support in-depth research in this field17–19. Against this background, we conducted systematic data cleaning, quality control, standardized preprocessing, and analytical workflow integration for three publicly available HD patient single-nucleus datasets17–19. Ultimately, we successfully constructed a large-scale, multi-brain-region integrated single-nucleus atlas of HD patients. This atlas comprises 330,892 cells derived from 104 tissue samples, taken from 39 HD patients and 34 healthy controls, spanning six key brain regions: the striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen), frontal cortex, cingulate cortex, nucleus accumbens, hippocampus and cerebellum.

In conclusion, our study presents the comprehensive single-nucleus data resource for HD patients, laying a solid foundation for an in-depth exploration of disease-specific mechanisms and the identification of potential therapeutic targets at the single-nucleus level.

Methods

Data sources

Through a literature search on Google Scholar using the keywords “Huntington’s disease”, “snRNA-seq” and “scRNA-seq”, we identified candidate single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) or single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies. To be included, a study had to (i) cover multiple HD-relevant brain regions, and (ii) provide complete gene expression matrices with sample-level metadata (donor identifiers, region, sex, age, grade) in a public repository. These criteria ensured that all cells could be traced to a specific donor and brain region, enabling rigorous donor-aware integration and descriptive analyses. Ultimately, three datasets that met all the criteria were selected. These datasets are publicly available on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with the following accession numbers: GSE152058, GSE180928 and GSE28106917–19.

Specifically, GSE152058 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE152058)17 includes caudate nucleus and putamen tissue from 14 HD patients and 14 healthy controls; GSE180928 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE180928)18 covers the caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens, and cingulate cortex from 19 HD patients and 13 controls; and GSE281069 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE281069)19 contributed samples from 6 HD patients and 7 controls, encompassing hippocampal and cerebellar tissues that have been less studied in HD, in addition to the frontal cortex, caudate nucleus and putamen. In total, 104 tissue samples from 39 HD patients and 34 healthy controls were integrated, covering the majority of brain regions known to be affected in HD (Fig. 1a). Data curation and standardization

To ensure uniformity across the datasets, we systematically curated all cells based on the sample information provided in each original study, standardizing metadata fields accordingly. This process aims to associate a complete set of standardized metadata with each cell, including but not limited to: disease status (Condition: HD patient or healthy control), tissue origin (Tissue: specific brain region), pathological grade (Grade), and donor age (Age), etc. All standardized metadata are uniformly stored in the “meta.data” of the integrated data object to ensure the consistency of research terms and the reliability of subsequent integrated analysis. Subsequently, we adopted a unified data processing procedure and threshold criteria to conduct a systematic quality control, batch effect correction, data standardization preprocessing, and cell type identification on the integrated dataset. Finally, we constructed a comprehensive single-nucleus atlas of multiple brain regions in HD patients (Fig. 1b–f).

Quality control

To ensure the reliability of the subsequent analysis, we applied standardized quality control to all single-nucleus datasets using Seurat (v4.4.0)20. The specific process is as follows: Firstly, a preliminary filtering was conducted to remove genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells to reduce technical noise21; at the same time, cells with fewer than 200 expressed genes were excluded to eliminate empty droplets or low-quality cells (Fig. 2a)22. Subsequently, we used the PercentageFeatureSet function to evaluate the mitochondrial gene expression ratio of each cell and filtered out cells with a ratio higher than 20% as dying or damaged cells18. Doublets and ambient RNA contamination had been identified and removed by the original source studies. Therefore, our pipeline did not apply any additional doublet or ambient RNA contamination filtering. After the standardized quality control process, a total of 330,892 cells were obtained for subsequent integration and analysis (Table 1).

Batch effect correction and clustering

Technical variation introduced by different laboratories, library preparation, and sequencing batches (i.e., batch effects) represents one of the primary challenges encountered when integrating multi-source single-nucleus datasets23. To evaluate the impact of batch effects, we initially merged the three datasets without correction. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) visualized a distinct separation of cells according to their study of origin (Fig. 2b, left), indicating substantial batch effects. To eliminate these technical variations and enable robust data integration, we applied batch correction using the Harmony package24. Harmony is designed to integrate large, multi-donor, multi-region single-nucleus datasets while modelling sample- and donor-level covariates. This preserves biologically meaningful variation and effectively removes technical batch effects25. Importantly, the batch correction and integration effects of Harmony have been validated and applied to large-scale single-nucleus brain atlases26,27. We used the sample identifier (104 unique tissue samples) as the batch covariate in Harmony, as each individual post-mortem brain sample underwent its own tissue processing, library preparation, and sequencing run, making sample identity the most direct source of technical variation. No additional covariates (e.g., donor age, or sex) were included in the model to avoid over-correction and to preserve biological signals that may correlate with these factors. After correction by Harmony, the batch effects present in the different datasets were mitigated, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent analysis (Fig. 2b right).

Next, we performed unsupervised clustering (Louvain algorithm28) on the corrected data. First, we utilized the clustree package to evaluate cluster stability at different resolutions and ultimately selected a resolution parameter of 0.9 for clustering29. The clustering results were visualized using t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), resulting in a total of 46 clusters (Fig. 2c)30. To further illustrate the data characteristics, we colored and visualized the cells based on their source (Fig. 2d), brain regions (Fig. 2e), and disease groups (Fig. 2f).

Identification of major cell types and construction of a single-nucleus atlas

To harmonize cell identities across datasets, we first transferred the original cell-type labels provided by each original study onto our integrated object by matching the original barcodes. In parallel, we performed unsupervised clustering on the Harmony-integrated embedding (resolution 0.9, 46 clusters). For each cluster, we identified marker genes using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Seurat, FindAllMarkers, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). These two steps are the foundation for the subsequent process of manual annotation (Supplementary Table 2).

By analyzing the cell type-specific marker genes, we successfully identified 13 major cell types in the six brain regions covered (striatum, frontal cortex, cingulate, nucleus accumbens, hippocampus and cerebellum) (Fig. 3a)31. The neuronal population comprises primarily medium spiny neurons (MSNs) located in the striatum, which represent the most vulnerable cell type in HD. Also known as spiny projection neurons (SPNs), these cells are further classified based on dopamine receptor expression into DRD1-expressing dSPNs (direct pathway) and DRD2-expressing iSPNs (indirect pathway)32,33. Additionally, excitatory neurons (SATB2+, LDB2+), inhibitory neurons (GAD1+, GAD2+), and specific inhibitory interneuron subpopulations, including PVALB-positive, VIP-positive, and SST/NPY-positive interneurons, were identified throughout the cortex34,35. Furthermore, granule cells enriched in the cerebellum (RELN+, RBFOX3+) were characterized36. Glial cell populations comprise astrocytes (GFAP+, SLC1A2+), microglia (P2RY12+, CSF1R+), oligodendrocytes (MOG+, MBP+), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (PDGFRA+, CSPG4+), and newly generated oligodendrocytes (FYN+)37. Vascular-associated cells include vascular endothelial cells (FLT1+) and vascular wall cells (PDGFRB+), which are key components of the blood-brain barrier38,39.

Based on the above characterization, we successfully constructed and analyzed a large-scale integrated single-nucleus atlas of multiple brain regions in Control/HD patients (Fig. 3b). To illustrate the regional composition of the atlas, we divided it into six sub-atlases, each corresponding to an individual brain region (Fig. 3c–h). The comparative view of cell-type proportions across these regions is consistent with the established cellular anatomy of the human brain (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Table 3). For example, iSPN and dSPN are predominantly found in the striatum, while cerebellar granule cells are almost exclusively present in the cerebellum. Together, these patterns confirm that the integrated atlas faithfully captures the cell type identities of known brain regions.

Data Records

The integrated dataset generated in this study is available in the Zenodo archive (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20301898)40. It comprises RDS and H5AD files for the multi-brain-region integration, as well as for each individual brain region, including the striatum, frontal cortex, cingulate cortex, nucleus accumbens, hippocampus, and cerebellum. Moreover, we offer a data list table that includes file names, brain regions, cell counts and MD5 values. The main dataset file (Integrated_data_of_brain_from_HD_patients.rds) contains expression matrices for 330,892 annotated single nucleus along with complete metadata. After loading the RDS object or H5AD object, users can access detailed information for each nucleus via its “meta.data” data frame, including standardized fields such as disease status (control/HD group), brain region, pathological grade, donor age, and cell type. Complete metadata information can also be accessed in Supplementary Table 5. A donor-level table listing information for each sample from different datasets can be accessed via Supplementary Table 1. This includes donor identifiers, disease status, sample ID, Vonsattel grade, brain region, sex, age, source dataset, post-QC cell counts and references. Notably, Vonsattel Grade information is available for HD samples from GSE152058 and GSE281069, but it was not provided by the original GSE180928 dataset (marked as “NA” in Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, any grade-stratified analyses should be limited to the two datasets with complete grade metadata or be regarded as exploratory.

To facilitate in-depth studies of specific brain regions, we also extracted subset data for six key brain areas (striatum, frontal cortex, cingulate cortex, nucleus accumbens, hippocampus, and cerebellum) from the main dataset. These region-specific data files are similarly stored in RDS format and H5AD format under the same Zenodo record, allowing researchers to download and utilize them independently as needed.

The present atlas is built from three publicly available snRNA-seq datasets that met our inclusion criteria. Several recent large-scale HD studies could not be incorporated at this stage owing to incomplete sample metadata, restricted data access, or lack of a formal peer-reviewed release. Therefore, the current resource does not cover all HD-relevant brain regions or capture the full spectrum of disease stages. Nevertheless, this resource is designed to be expandable, and we plan to incorporate new, high-quality datasets in future releases to enhance its completeness progressively.

Technical Validation

Data integration and batch effect correction assessment

To objectively measure batch mixing and biological conservation, we calculated the Local Inverse Simpson’s Index (LISI) on both the uncorrected PCA space (first 20 PCs) and the Harmony-integrated embedding24. For integration LISI (iLISI), we used the full set of 104 tissue sample identifiers as labels (104 samples across all donors and datasets). The iLISI score measures the mixing effect of different batches of cells, with a higher score indicating better integration. Before batch correction, the median iLISI was 4.032 (Fig. 5a). After batch correction by Harmony, the iLISI increased to 6.307 (Fig. 5b), indicating an improvement in sample mixing.

Quantitative assessment of batch effect correction. (a,b) The density map shows the iLISI scores before (a) and after (b) Harmony batch correction integration. (c) Density plot of cLISI. (d) The violin plot shows the cLISI for each dataset. (e) t-SNE plot showing cell distribution across datasets before batch effect correction. (f) t-SNE plot showing cell distribution after batch effect correction.

The cell-type LISI (cLISI) score indicates the degree to which a specific cellular cluster is separated from others, with a value closer to 1 representing ideal separation. The median cLISI was close to 1 after integration (Fig. 5c,d), suggesting that the transcriptomic boundaries between cell types were preserved.

Additionally, we compared the cell distribution both before and after batch correction. In the uncorrected t-SNE plot, cells primarily segregated according to their source datasets (Fig. 5e). After being corrected by the Harmony algorithm, the cells from different datasets achieved better mixing in the low-dimensional space (Fig. 5f)24. Notably, tissue-specific populations such as striatal dSPN/iSPN and cerebellum-enriched granule cells remained clearly distinguishable post-correction, indicating that their intrinsic biological characteristics were not obscured (Fig. 3).

Determination of the optimal number of principal components for clustering

To ensure the reliability of the clustering analysis, we systematically evaluated and determined the number of principal components (PCs) used to construct the cell neighborhood map. First, we visualized key genes driving the initial PCs (such as the first 16) using the DimHeatmap function to confirm that the selected PCs primarily reflect biologically meaningful gene expression patterns rather than technical noise (Fig. 6a). Concurrently, we plotted an ElbowPlot to illustrate the contribution explained by each PC20. We observed a distinct inflection point in the variation captured by PCs after approximately the first 20 components, with marginal contributions diminishing for subsequent PCs (Fig. 6b). Integrating the inflection point from the elbow plot with the gene features revealed by the heatmap, we ultimately selected the first 20 PCs for subsequent cell clustering and dimensionality reduction. This choice maximizes the retention of biological variation while effectively mitigating interference introduced by technical noise.

Identification of principal components of clustering. (a) The heatmap displays gene expression patterns driving the top 12 principal components. (b) The elbow plot shows the variance explained by each principal component.

Determination of the optimal clustering resolution

To determine the optimal resolution parameter in unsupervised clustering to ensure the biological rationality and stability of cell grouping, we conducted a systematic evaluation using the Clustree package29. This tool provides a visual representation of the derivation and merging relationships of clusters at different resolutions, intuitively demonstrating the stability of the grouping structure. We evaluated the clustering results at various resolutions ranging from 0.1 to 1.9 (in 0.2 increments). By analyzing the cluster dendrogram, we observed that, within the resolution range of approximately 0.7 to 1.1, the cell clusters were divided without excessive subdivision (Fig. 7a). This prevented the inappropriate merging of biologically significant subgroups, indicating that the grouping structure was most stable within this range. After a comprehensive evaluation, we ultimately chose 0.9 as the best resolution for all subsequent clustering analyses to balance the sensitivity and specificity for cell type identification. Each cluster is supported by specific marker genes, and clusters that consist of the same cell type have similar marker genes (Supplementary Table 2).
