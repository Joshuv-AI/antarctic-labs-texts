Glucose-dependent dynamics of glucagon, cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone before and after gastric bypass
Authors: Vasileios Chronopoulos, Maria C Kjellsson, Martin H Lundqvist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextRoux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) improves glucose homeostasis beyond weight loss, but the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Counter-regulatory hormones such as glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol are key components of glucose regulation.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to characterize alterations in glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol regulation before and after RYGB using nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) population modeling, and to explore potential mediators of these changes.DesignPost-hoc analysis of one cross-sectional and two prospective cohort studies.SettingClinical research unit.Patients or Other ParticipantsIn total, 62 individuals without diabetes from three previous clamp studies were included: 39 from a cross-sectional cohort covering a wide range of BMI and insulin sensitivity (cohort 1, utilized for model development only), and 23 participants examined both before and approximately four months after RYGB (cohort 2).InterventionsHyperinsulinemic-euglycemic-hypoglycemic (both cohorts) and hyperglycemic (cohort 1 only) clamps.Main Outcome MeasuresGlucose-dependent secretion dynamics of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol quantified through NLME modeling and the impact of RYGB and metabolic characteristics on corresponding model parameters.ResultsAccording to models developed using presurgical data from both cohorts, glucagon and ACTH turnover rates and baseline concentrations decreased post-RYGB in cohort 2. Insulin-mediated glucagon suppression and the glucose deficit required for ACTH stimulation increased, while cortisol dynamics remained stable. Higher insulin sensitivity was associated with lower baseline glucagon levels.ConclusionRYGB induces reductions in glucagon and ACTH responsiveness to hypoglycemia, partially indicative of central nervous system-mediated resetting of glucose regulation. These adaptations may contribute to improved metabolic control following bariatric surgery.Keywords: bariatric surgery, glucagon, ACTH, cortisol, hypoglycemia

The global increase in obesity has led to a rise in associated metabolic disorders, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), underlining the need for interventions that address metabolic dysregulation [1]. Traditionally, the link between obesity, insulin resistance, and T2D development has been proposed to be mediated by peripheral processes, such as ectopic fat accumulation and inflammatory pathways [1, 2] Recently, however, a potential role of the brain has been increasingly emphasized [3, 4]. When systemic glucose levels decline, glucose-sensing neurons in the brain, most prevalent in the hypothalamus, activate neurocircuits that stimulate downstream secretion of so-called counter-regulatory hormones such as glucagon (released by pancreatic alpha cells), ACTH (released from the pituitary gland), and cortisol (released from the adrenal cortex upon ACTH stimulation) [5]. While the primary role of this hormonal response is to prevent hypoglycemia, it has been proposed that dysregulation of these hormones, alongside insufficient insulin secretion, can contribute to hyperglycemia and the development of T2D [3]. In support of this theory, chronically elevated secretion as well as altered glucose-dependent dynamics of these hormones have been linked to obesity, insulin resistance, and T2D [6-12].

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) surgery has become a key treatment for obesity, demonstrating significant benefits beyond weight loss, including improved insulin sensitivity and, in many cases, remission of T2D. Notably, some metabolic benefits occur rapidly after surgery, before substantial weight loss, suggesting weight-independent mechanisms [13]. While bariatric surgery favorably affects gastrointestinal hormone secretion and nutrient processing, the underlying mechanisms behind these rapid and sustained metabolic effects remain poorly understood [14]. Interestingly, previous studies, using modified euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic-hypoglycemic clamp protocols, have shown that the secretion of several counter-regulatory hormones during hypoglycemia is attenuated after RYGB [15, 16], which may contribute to the metabolic effects of the procedure. However, it is unclear which specific components in the complex regulation of these hormonal systems are impacted by RYGB.

To shed further light on the nature of post-RYGB hormonal adaptations, non-linear mixed effects (NLME) population modeling provides a powerful approach, allowing the integration of existing findings into a comprehensive model that quantifies hormonal changes across variable glucose concentrations. With such modelling strategies, heterogeneity in study populations and experimental protocols constitutes a strength rather than a limitation in terms of validity and performance of the models. Building on previous models in individuals who have not undergone bariatric surgery [8, 17], the present study aimed to investigate RYGB-mediated adaptive responses of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol in variable glucose concentrations and to explore possible mediators of the bariatric surgery effect, focusing on clinical parameters. To this end, we have implemented NLME population modeling on integrated data from three previously performed clamp studies [7, 10, 15, 16].

Materials and methods

Overview of the study population and experimental conditions

This modeling study used data from three clinical glucose clamp studies conducted at Uppsala University Hospital [7, 10, 15, 16], all using standardized protocols to assess the hormonal responses to variable glucose concentrations before [7, 10, 15, 16] and after [15, 16] bariatric surgery. Individuals with type 2 diabetes who had been enrolled in Lundqvist et al [7]. were excluded from the present study. The models for hormone secretion were first developed based on pre-surgical data from all clamp studies, including a substantial proportion of lean and metabolically healthy individuals. The resulting models were then applied to investigate differences post- vs pre-surgery in Abrahamsson et al [15] and Almby et al [16]. In the study by Lundqvist et al [7, 10], individuals with a wide range of BMI and insulin sensitivity underwent one hypoglycemic clamp and one hyperglycemic clamp on separate occasions. In the studies by Abrahamsson et al and Almby et al [15, 16], individuals with severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2) underwent paired hypoglycemic clamps before and approximately four months after RYGB. The clamp designs and assessments are summarized in Fig. 1. During the normoglycemic phase of the hypoglycemic clamps, insulin was infused at a rate of 56 (Lundqvist) or 80 (Abrahamsson, Almby) mU/m2/min, and glucose was infused at a variable rate to achieve a target glucose of 5.0 mmol/L). This phase lasted for 60, 80, and approximately 110 minutes in Abrahamsson, Lundqvist, and Almby, respectively. In the hypoglycemic phase, glucose was then lowered to 2.7 mmol/L, either in one step (Almby) or gradually (Lundqvist, Abrahamsson). This phase lasted 90-105 minutes after which insulin infusions were discontinued, and glucose levels were restored to the normal range. Potassium was infused continuously in all clamp protocols (8 mmol/h). In the hyperglycemic clamp in Lundqvist et al, glucose was infused to achieve plateaus of +3, +6 and +9 mmol/L above fasting levels for 45 minutes each. The glucose infusion was then terminated to allow glucose levels to fall to the normal range.

Biochemical analyses and other clamp assessments

In all experiments, blood was drawn from arterialized venous lines at multiple timepoints as specified in Fig. 1. Glucose was measured every 5 minutes using a Contour XT glucose meter (Bayer Healthcare, Leverkusen, Germany). Glucagon was analyzed from blood collected in p800 tubes (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lake, USA) with ELISA (#10-1271-01, RRID: AB_2737304, Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden) at the Clinical Diabetology and Metabolism laboratory at Uppsala University. According to the manufacturer, the inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) for glucagon is 7.0% to 16%, and the intra-assay CV is 2.1% to 14%. ACTH (Immulite 2000XPi, Siemens Healthcare Global), cortisol, and insulin (both CobasE, Roche) were analyzed at the Clinical Biochemistry laboratory at Uppsala University Hospital. For ACTH, according to the laboratory, the inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) is 5.91% and 5.09% at 6.6 pmol/L and 89.5 pmol/L, respectively; the intra-assay CV is 5.70% and 1.46% at 5.03 pmol/L and 71.06 pmol/L, respectively. For cortisol, the inter-assay CV is 4.09% and 4.17% at 282 nmol/L and 610 nmol/L, respectively; the intra-assay CV is 1.20% and 1.33% at 239 nmol/L and 555 nmol/L, respectively. Due to the variable duration of the euglycemic phases of the original studies, insulin sensitivity was consistently assessed using the mean glucose infusion rate per kg lean body mass during the 40 to 60 minutes of the normoglycemic phase (GIR40-60). For further details, we refer to the original publications [7, 10, 15, 16].

Software and estimation method

Model development was performed in R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://www.r-project.org), using NONMEM (v7.6; Icon Development Solutions, https://www.iconplc.com/solutions/technologies/nonmem, Ellicott City, MD) with the ADVAN13 subroutine and the FOCE-I estimation method. The Pearl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN) toolkit (v5.3.1; Department of Pharmaceutical Biosciences, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden, https://uupharmacometrics.github.io/PsN/) was used to manage the model execution and diagnostic workflows. Parameter uncertainty was assessed using sampling importance resampling (SIR), carried out in three stages with increasing sample sizes (1000, 2000, 2000) and corresponding resample sizes (500, 1000, 1000). Diagnostic plots from the SIR procedure were used to assess the convergence of the parameter uncertainty estimates.

Model reliability was assessed using standard NLME validation procedures. Goodness-of-fit plots (observed vs predicted values and residual diagnostics) and visual predictive checks (VPC) based on 1000 simulations were generated to evaluate bias and predictive performance.

Structural model development

The pre-RYGB NLME model was developed using pre-RYGB data to characterize the secretion dynamics of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol in response to variable glucose concentrations. Hormone secretion was modeled as a deviation from the individual baseline concentrations, with constant zero-order production and first-order elimination for each hormone. Glucose and insulin were incorporated as time-varying drivers of secretion, modeled through their observed slopes. For each individual, the rate of change in glucose and insulin levels between adjacent time points was calculated using linear regression and entered into the model as external inputs, governing the dynamics of their respective compartments. To account for physiological regulation, the secretion rate of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol was dynamically regulated by a time-varying function such that the effective secretion rate was given by Kin⋅f(t), where f(t) represents the regulatory input. All estimated parameters are directly interpretable in physiological terms. For each hormone, the model outputs the individual baseline concentration, the turnover rate (Kout), and regulatory parameters that quantify the maximal response, the relevant stimulus threshold, and the steepness of regulation. A schematic representation of the structural model is provided in Fig. 2.

Assessment of pre- and post-RYGB differences in hormonal regulation

To evaluate the effects of RYGB surgery on hormonal regulation, the model developed using pre-RYGB data was applied to the post-RYGB dataset, and all parameters were re-estimated. To determine the statistical significance of the observed parameter changes, each parameter was fixed individually to its pre-RYGB value, while all other parameters were freely estimated. The model fit was then re-evaluated under this constraint, and the change in the objective function value (OFV) was recorded. A deterioration in fit exceeding 3.84 units was considered statistically significant, corresponding to a likelihood ratio test with 1 degree of freedom at the .05 significance level. In the next step, each parameter that showed a significant difference was tested using the full dataset to assess whether a single shared estimate across both surgical states could be assumed. This was done by re-estimating the model with one parameter at a time, constrained to be the same pre- and post-RYGB, while all other parameters were estimated separately. If this constraint increased the OFV by more than 3.84 units, the parameter was modeled with separate estimates for the two surgical states. Otherwise, it was modeled as a shared estimate. This procedure was followed by a covariate investigation to define the full NLME model structure.

Interindividual variability, residual error handling, and covariate investigation

Interindividual variability (IIV) was modeled exponentially on baseline hormone concentrations and some regulatory parameters. Inter-occasion variability was also evaluated to account for potential differences between clamp studies.

Residual unexplained variability (RUV) was handled using a log-transformed both sides approach, with the additive error specified on the log-transformed scale. Separate residual error terms were estimated for glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol. For observations below the limit of quantification (BLQ), the residual error was inflated with a multiplicative factor.

In the pre-RYGB model, the relationship between clinical variables and NLME model parameters was assessed using linear regression. These clinical variables included age, sex, BMI, Waist-Hip ratio, body fat percentage, HbA1c, fasting glucose, HOMA-IR, and GIR40-60. Covariate relationships were evaluated statistically, with P-values (P) derived from the t-test on the regression coefficient and a significance threshold of P < .05. Based on these results, covariate effects were introduced into the NLME model one at a time and retained only if the decrease in ΔOFV exceeded 3.84 units, corresponding to a likelihood ratio test with 1 degree of freedom at the .05 significance level. In the full NLME model, including both pre- and post-RYGB data, these clinical variables were tested as covariates for all parameters that had shown significant differences between the surgical states. These covariates were retained only if they led to a statistically significant improvement in the model fit based on the same ΔOFV criterion.

The glucagon dynamics exhibited substantial post-RYGB changes, consisting of decreases of the elimination rate, the baseline glucagon concentration, and hence the production rate, as well as an increase of the insulin-mediated glucagon suppression (ΔOFV >3.84 for all corresponding model parameters, Table 2). By contrast, the glucose-driven stimulation parameters did not significantly differ between the surgical states. As illustrated in Fig. 3A and 3B, the net effect of these alterations was a reduction of glucagon secretion across the hypoglycemic range in post- vs pre-RYGB.

Likewise, the ACTH dynamics changed post-RYGB. The elimination rate and the baseline ACTH concentration (both ΔOFV > 3.84, Table 2), and hence the production rate, decreased. In contrast, the maximal stimulatory effect and the EC50ACTH increased while the γACTH (the steepness of the ACTH response) decreased (both ΔOFV > 3.84, Table 2). The estimated t1/2 for the glucose effect delay remained similar between the surgical states. As illustrated in Fig. 3C and 3D, these changes overall resulted in lower ACTH secretion across the hypoglycemic range in post- vs pre-RYGB.

The cortisol dynamics remained largely stable. The elimination rate slightly decreased (ΔOFV > 3.84). However, the maximal stimulatory effect, the baseline cortisol concentration, and the EC50CORT remained comparable across the surgical states. Notwithstanding this limited effect on ACTH-responsiveness of cortisol secretion, the lower ACTH secretion following RYGB led to a model-estimated reduction in cortisol secretion throughout the hypoglycemic range (Fig. 3E and 3F).

Interindividual variability and covariate investigation

Interindividual variability (IIV) was included on baseline concentrations of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol, as well as on EGL, IC50GLH, EC50ACTH, t1/2 of glucose effect delay, and INSeffect. The inclusion was guided by improvements in the OFV and VPC. The inclusion of inter-occasion variability did not improve the model fit. IIV differed significantly for the baseline ACTH and the EGL parameter between the pre- and post-RYGB states; therefore, separate IIV parameters were included in the NLME model.

Among the covariate relationships tested in the pre-RYGB model, only the baseline glucagon and the EC50ACTH showed a statistically significant relationship with the pre-RYGB GIR40-60 in the linear regression analysis. None of the other clinical variables (ie, sex, BMI, Waist-Hip ratio, HbA1c) exhibited significant associations with the model parameters in these analyses. The inclusion of the Gn0–GIR40-60 relationship significantly improved the model fit and was incorporated into the pre-RYGB NLME model. In contrast, the EC50ACTH–GIR40-60 relationship did not result in a statistically significant improvement in the NLME model (ΔOFV ≤ 3.84) and was not retained.

In the full NLME model covariate investigation (including both pre- and post-RYGB data), only the relationship between GIR40-60 and baseline glucagon allowed the parameter to be modeled as shared across the pre- and post-RYGB populations without a significant deterioration in model fit (ΔOFV 0.67). The covariate effect was modeled exponentially, indicating that higher insulin sensitivity was associated with lower baseline glucagon concentrations. Additionally, for the ACTH baseline, KoutA, and the γACTH, incorporating the GIR40-60 as a covariate provided modest improvements in model fit, although not sufficient to justify sharing the parameters across surgical states (ΔOFV > 3.84). For all other parameters tested, GIR40-60 inclusion did not improve the model performance. A summary of the ΔOFVs when the parameters were tested as GIR40-60-dependent shared estimates is provided in Table 2, and visual predictive checks of the full NLME covariate models are shown in Supplement S4 [18].

Discussion

In this study, an NLME model was developed to describe the dynamics of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol in response to variable glucose concentrations, with the aim of characterizing physiological adaptations after RYGB. Thus, post-RYGB changes were observed in glucagon and ACTH regulation, while cortisol dynamics remained largely stable. Covariate analyses showed that higher insulin sensitivity was associated with lower baseline glucagon levels, possibly suggesting that reversal of hyperglucagonemia may partially account for improvements in insulin sensitivity after surgery. The results reinforce previous evidence of RYGB-effects on the regulation of counter-regulatory hormones and, as a novelty, provide detailed insights regarding the precise nature as well as potential mediators of these effects.

Adaptive changes in glucagon and ACTH regulation following RYGB

We found that glucagon regulation post-RYGB was characterized by lower fasting concentrations and reduced overall turnover. These findings are consistent with previous research showing reduced fasting glucagon concentrations after RYGB, particularly at later post-RYGB stages [19, 20].

Similar to glucagon, ACTH turnover was reduced following RYGB, and, in addition, the responsiveness of ACTH secretion to relative glucose deficit was attenuated. This is consistent with one of the original studies in the current material (Almby et al), where a non-significant tendency toward an attenuation of the ACTH response was observed post-surgery. Furthermore, newly available pre- and post-RYGB ACTH data from the Abrahamsson et al study, which were absent from the original publication, were incorporated and supported the same pattern of reduced ACTH sensitivity to hypoglycemia after surgery [15]. As currently shown and as a natural consequence of the reported preservation of adrenal ACTH-sensitivity across surgical states, the secretion of cortisol was also attenuated during hypoglycemia post-RYGB. There are a few studies that have investigated the impact of bariatric surgery on other measurements of HPA-axis activity (eg, fasting cortisol levels, total daily urine output), but results are widely inconsistent [21, 22].

Potential underlying mechanism behind RYGB-dependent hormonal adaptations

The fact that BMI did not emerge as a significant determinant of glucagon or ACTH secretion in models applied to pre- and post-RYGB data supports mechanisms other than weight loss per se—and possibly unique to the surgical procedure—behind the observed hormonal adaptation.

In relation to the reduced glucagon turnover, the current results support alleviation of alpha cell insulin resistance—postulated to drive hyperglucagonemia in the development of type 2 diabetes [23]—as the principal mechanism, which is novel to the best of our knowledge. Notably, however, we have not evaluated the potential role of incretins in this regard. Postprandial GLP-1 levels are known to increase multifold after RYGB [24] and GLP-1 has been shown to suppress glucagon secretion indirectly via paracrine mechanisms [25]. Intriguingly, both GLP-1 and GIP have also been shown to increase during hypoglycemia and more markedly post-RYGB [15]. The implications of this finding remain unclear, however, in particular because GIP exerts an opposite, stimulatory effect on glucagon, most likely via a direct effect on alpha cells [25]. Thus, alterations in post-prandial incretin dynamics may underlie many favorable metabolic effects of bariatric surgery, but their importance in the current experimental setting is questionable.

As for the observed post-RYGB changes in the regulation of the HPA-axis, the results point to a CNS-mediated lowering of the glycemic threshold required to activate the HPA axis after RYGB. This has not been demonstrated before to our knowledge, but is congruent with other CNS-adaptions following bariatric surgery, relating not only to hypoglycemia, but also, for instance, satiety and cognition [16, 26, 27]. The exact pathways that link post-RYGB alterations in the gut—and potentially other organs such as the adipose tissue—to the brain remain to be explored.

Metabolic consequences of the observed hormonal adaptations—role in post-RYGB improvement of insulin sensitivity?

While there is ample evidence of early improvements in HOMA-IR and other indirect measures of insulin resistance following bariatric surgery [28, 29], reductions in clamp-derived measures of insulin resistance have predominantly been observed later in the postoperative period [19, 30, 31], perhaps due to increased lipolysis and other metabolic consequences of rapid weight loss before establishment of weight stability. This pattern was reflected in the current material, where we observed marked improvements in HOMA-IR but not the GIR40-60 as a marker of insulin sensitivity. While reduction in glucagon and ACTH turnover, as well as improvements in gold standard measures of insulin resistance, could both be delayed effects of weight loss per se, it is also possible that amelioration of insulin resistance after RYGB partially occurs via reversal of hyperglucagonemia and hypercortisolism. In fact, the association between GIR40-60 and baseline glucagon and, to a lesser extent, ACTH levels in the current study is compatible with the latter causal relationship. Previous work has highlighted hyperglucagonemia as a hallmark of both obesity and T2D, and a principal role in the development of T2D has been postulated [32, 33]. There is also some evidence of systemic and tissue-specific hypercortisolism in obesity, as reflected by increased cortisol concentrations in hair, a flatter circadian curve, increased reactivity to various stress stimuli, and increased tissue expression of 11-βHSD1, leading to increased conversion of biologically inactive cortisone to cortisol [10, 12, 34, 35]. Moreover, recent evidence indicates a very high prevalence (24%) of subclinical hypercortisolism in patients with difficult-to-treat T2D [11].

All considered, we find it highly plausible that the currently reported RYGB-induced adaptations in the regulation of glucagon and the HPA-axis may mediate some of the well-established metabolic benefits of bariatric surgery [36]. However, the current study design does not permit firm conclusions regarding the direction of causality, and we recognize that all possible mechanisms and consequences of RYGB-induced adaptations in the investigated hormonal systems have not been exhaustively explored.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to apply population modeling to jointly characterize the regulation of glucagon, ACTH, and cortisol before and after RYGB. A major strength lies in the integration of the peripheral and central hormonal axes into a unified NLME model, enabling translation of clamp data into distinct physiological components. These include baseline hormone concentration, turnover rate, maximal response, and the EC50 or IC50 values that describe the stimulus level required for half-maximal activation or inhibition. By incorporating all time points and individuals, the NLME model can reveal whether RYGB changes a hormone's baseline, its stimulus sensitivity, or the magnitude of its response. These aspects cannot be separated using summary measures such as the area under the curve. In contrast to previous models [8, 17], regulatory inputs were expressed relative to individual baselines, improving physiological interpretability and allowing for subject-specific characterization of the hormonal responses to glycemic changes. For ACTH and cortisol, modeling them jointly increases statistical power by leveraging their physiological relationship, as ACTH directly stimulates cortisol secretion. In the case of glucagon, two independent drivers, insulin and glucose, regulate a single dependent variable, allowing for a mechanistic understanding of how glucagon responds to both insulin-mediated suppression and glucose availability. This modeling approach, applied here for the first time in the context of metabolic surgery, offers a novel and physiologically grounded framework for understanding endocrine adaptations to RYGB. Moreover, the current integration of existing data from different cohorts provides a resource-effective route toward generating novel insights regarding biological processes, for further evaluation and validation in future studies.

Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. First, this study has investigated glucose-dependent hormonal responses before and four months after RYGB in a predominantly female cohort, limiting its generalizability to other post-RYGB stages and populations. This is relevant since many effects of bariatric surgery exhibit distinct patterns of evolution over time; some manifesting early, others later after the surgical procedure [21, 37]. Second, the post-RYGB dataset was relatively small, resulting in limited statistical power, and was available only under hypoglycemic conditions, restricting the ability to assess hormonal dynamics across the full glycemic range. Missing data for certain clinical variables may have further reduced the statistical power to detect significant associations with model parameters. Third, the experimental design of the glucose clamp studies did not allow for consideration of pulsatile, ultradian, and circadian secretory patterns of the investigated hormones [38, 39] that may have influenced the results. However, the pulsatile secretion of glucagon seems to be dependent on intra-islet communication between alpha-, beta-, and delta cells and is probably of minor importance in the experimental setting of hypoglycemia achieved by a continuous infusion of insulin [38]. Moreover, all experiments were performed in the morning, and there is no previous evidence of RYGB-related changes in diurnal patterns of HPA-axis activity to our knowledge. Fourth, previously reported post-bariatric reductions in corticosteroid-binding globulin [40] and 11β-HSD1 expression [41] were not accounted for in this study. Lower CBG concentrations may increase the free cortisol fraction, potentially suppressing ACTH secretion through enhanced negative feedback. While this mechanism could partly explain the lower fasting ACTH levels observed in the post-bariatric state, it is unlikely to account for the altered acute response to hypoglycemia. In contrast, reduced 11β-HSD1 expression after bariatric surgery may limit peripheral cortisol regeneration, rendering the net biological effect of cortisol more complex and difficult to predict. Fifth, blood samples were obtained at discrete time points during the clamp experiments and analyzed using methods with non-negligible coefficients of variation. This sampling approach may not have fully capture rapid hormonal fluctuations induced by glucose changes per se, thereby limiting the resolution of short-term regulatory dynamics and potentially affecting the precision of the modeling analyses. This limitation is particularly relevant for insulin-mediated glucagon suppression, which had to be represented as an all-or-nothing effect in the model, an approach driven by the temporal resolution of the sampling rather than the underlying biology. Furthermore, it would have been ideal to use glucose measurements by standard laboratory methods rather than by a bedside glucometer as was currently done. However, we prioritized the higher temporal resolution provided by the latter which enabled a better evaluation of time lags between systemic glucose levels and hormone secretion. The resulting reduction of accuracy may have limited the model performance somewhat, but is unlikely to have introduced a bias in relation to the primary research questions. Finally, the original studies were not primarily designed to evaluate insulin sensitivity and the duration of the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic phase was therefore not standardized to the conventional 120 minutes [42, 43]. The consequential use of GIR40-60 may not have been an ideal proxy for insulin sensitivity and results may have been different if gold standard methods had been implemented to quantify this variable.

Conclusions

In summary, we have shown that RYGB reduces glucagon turnover via potentiation of insulin inhibition and reduces turnover as well as the hypoglycemic responsiveness of ACTH and, consequently, cortisol. These adaptations, partly CNS-mediated, of glucose-raising hormones may partially explain long-term improvements in glucose metabolism, particularly insulin resistance, that have been previously and presently associated with bariatric surgery. In the future, the currently implemented NLME model could be expanded to capture hormonal dynamics under more physiologic conditions, such as a mixed-meal test, and to evaluate the interplay between counter-regulatory hormones, gastrointestinal hormones, and other nutrients such as free fatty acids.
