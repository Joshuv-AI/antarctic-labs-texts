Testicular cancer: a bibliometric review of the Middle East and North African region
Authors: Oussama G. Nasrallah, Eliane Alhalabi, Yasser Fadlallah, Bilal Alameddine, Moustafa A. Al Hattab, Jana H. Mahdi, Adnan El Achkar, Jose M. El Asmar, Bassel G. Bachir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Testicular cancer is the most common solid malignancy diagnosed in young men. The incidence of testicular cancer has been on the rise over the last decades including in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Our objective is to perform a review on research publications from countries in the MENA region about Testicular cancer and map these results across the cancer care pathway.Main body:A search was conducted on PubMed, Medline, and Scopus about testicular cancer and the MENA region between 2000 and 2020. Research output, publication trends, study designs, research questions, funding, collaborations, authors affiliation, and author gender disparities were assessed across the MENA region. Our search yielded a total of 1026 publications of which 462 articles were selected. There has been a steady rise in publications over these 20 years. It was notable that Turkey had the largest proportion (56.7%) accounting for 262 publications. Testicular cancer publications across the cancer care pathway showed that most studies focused on diagnosis (48.9%), whereas the most common study type was case reports (48.3%). Female authorship was mostly present in basic science publications 82% whereas female presence was only evident in 57.1% of publications.Conclusion:Several limitations hinder testicular cancer research in the MENA region. Analysis of the collected data revealed significant gaps related to a low number of impactful original articles, randomized controlled trials, and basic science research. Potential areas for improvement include creation of standardized oncology registries, increasing research related funding, and expanding collaborations with healthcare institutions abroad.

Introduction

Testicular cancer is the most commonly occurring solid malignancy in young men between 15 and 44 years of age[1]. Incidence of testicular cancer has been on the rise over the past 10–20 years, a trend largely attributed to an improved access to diagnostic testing and awareness[2,3]. Moreover, a positive correlation was reported between a country’s Human Development Index (HDI) and the incidence of testicular cancer, yet, the correlation is negative when it comes to mortality rates of testicular cancer patients: the higher a country’s HDI, the lower the cause-specific mortality rates[4]. In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, the incidence of testicular cancer remains relatively low compared to the incidence in the United States. This may be attributed to the lower HDI of MENA countries implicating a lack of screening modalities and awareness campaigns that are often seen in the Western world[4]. The MENA region is spread across two continents: Asia and Africa, encompassing a group of 21 countries, 14 of which are located in the Middle East region which lies in Asia while the remaining 7 countries are located in the northern part of Africa. Each of these countries varies in terms of population demographics, economic status, stability, access to healthcare, and overall development[5,6]. Consequentially, these countries widely vary in terms of research output related to testicular cancer[7]. We sought to characterize discrepancies in research productivity amongst the MENA countries and map knowledge gaps related to the disease by performing a bibliometric analysis of publications across the MENA countries. A quantification of research output was made whilst emphasizing Western collaboration, sexual diversity, and funding. Materials and methods

A search was conducted on PubMed, Medline, and Scopus for all testicular cancer publications in the MENA region between 2000 and 2020. The collected publications were then screened and included according to the following criteria: article should be discussing testicular cancer in its content, at least one author affiliated to an academic institution in the 21 countries of MENA listed below. Duplicates and editorial replies were removed. For each article included, the publication year, country of the 1st author, collaboration, funding status, presence of a female author, type of publication, study design, and research question involved were all collected. Gender-neutral names were web-searched for accuracy.

MENA region countries included Algeria, Bahrain, Djibouti, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Malta, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Yemen. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS®; SPSS Inc., IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) statistical tools were both used for analysis of the data collected. Numerical data were reported as frequency and percentage. Graphical data were presented by tables, bar graphs, and a heat map.

Results

A total of 1025 articles were retrieved from the databases searched, of which 462 articles met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study. A total of 563 articles were excluded. All the included articles were in English, no language barrier was encountered. Research output was identified in 14 out of 21 countries in the MENA region which included: 262 publications in Turkey (56.6%), 54 publications in Iran (11.7%), 30 publications in Saudi Arabia (6.5%), 19 publications in Lebanon and Tunisia each (4.1%), 14 publications in Egypt (3.0%), and 10 publications in Morocco (2.2%). The published articles covered various cancer care pathway areas based on the type of study design and research question type that was handled. Most of the articles published were case reports which accounted for 223 (48.3%) out of 462 articles, descriptive studies and cohort studies accounted for 87 (18.8%) and 67 (14.5%) articles respectively, while case-control studies, basic science, and cross-sectional studies altogether amounted to 76 (16.4%). However, there was only 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in the MENA region which was done in Turkey by Akar et al[8].

In terms of research question types, 226 articles (48.9%) covered diagnosis, 121 articles (26.2%) covered treatment and therapy, 89 articles (19.3%) covered prognosis, and 26 articles (5.6%) covered harm.

Testicular cancer research funding in the MENA region had an increasing trend over the years. Although zero funding was noted for all MENA research projects published between 2000 and 2005, funding started trending up in subsequent years, with 45 publications out of 462 (9.7%) being funded over the next 15 years, as shown in Fig. 2. These publications received either public or private sector funding (6.1% and 3.6%, respectively). In terms of female contribution in authorship, at least one female author was present in 57.1% of all published articles, indicating nonetheless that a major number of published articles (42.9%) had zero female authorship contribution. However, a rising trend in female contribution is noted between 2000 and 2020, as demonstrated by Fig. 3. This is a direct reflection of the continuous marginalization of women’s role in the Urological research field taking place in the MENA world (more of a discussion point). In regards to types of studies published, female authorship was mostly present in basic science publications 82% (32/39) whereas female presence was only evident in 55% (232/423) of clinical publications. Discussion

Risk factors for testicular cancer include cryptorchidism, personal or family history of testicular cancer, ethnicity, age, and previous HIV infections, among others[9,10]. Awareness of symptoms, such as painless lumps or swelling in the testes, and early detection through self-examinations, is critical for prompt diagnosis and treatment[9].

If a solid intratesticular mass is discovered, orchidectomy is both diagnostic and therapeutic[10]. Treatment options following orchidectomy include active surveillance, chemotherapy, retroperitoneal lymph node dissection, and radiation therapy, depending on the clinical stage and histological subtype of the tumor[9,10].

The aforementioned treatments can lead to temporary or permanent infertility or impotence[9,10]. The American Urological Association and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine recommend discussing fertility preservation with patients, such as sperm banking, early in the course of treatment, prior to initiating gonadotoxic therapies[11,12]. Given the significant impact of testicular cancer treatments on fertility and quality of life, ongoing research and funding are essential for early diagnosis and the development of improved treatment modalities[9,10].

During the last 20 years, the MENA region underwent constant remodeling of its economic, political, technological, healthcare strategies thereby endlessly affecting its populations. In addition, these vision changes directly impacted access to scientific information and interest in research contributions. However, many MENA countries still lag behind in terms of providing standardized medical care to their general population. Many factors contribute to the aforementioned such as rural-urban disparities in access to healthcare, financial burden of the private sector, prolonged waiting times for ambulatory visits thereby favoring emergency visits, and lack of electronic services that empower physician-patient communication[5,6]. Despite the many hurdles, a recent study conducted by Mula-Hussain et al[7] revealed that survival outcomes for male genitourinary cancers including testicular cancer significantly improved in 2020 accompanied by a similar decrease in mortality to incidence ratio for that year. This reflects the cancer care advancements in terms of diagnosis, treatment, and long-term follow-up in the MENA region.

Research output and medical publications play a crucial role in a country’s overall health sector development which translates into a direct improvement in the quality of care and standard of living for the general population. Governments around the Western world heavily invest in medical research to limit knowledge gaps and expand treatment options for various medical conditions. In the MENA region, investments are mostly limited leading to lower publication rates varying from one country to another; nevertheless, medical research productivity has improved significantly from 2007 to 2016 in parallel with improved access to medical services and overall development of MENA countries[13]. Our results yielded a total of 462 testicular cancer-related articles published in the MENA region from the year 2000 till 2020 with varying distribution across the different countries, as illustrated in Fig. 1. A bibliographic study by Abdul-Khalek et al[14], revolving around breast cancer-related publications in the MENA region attributed the significant lack of research productivity in many MENA region countries, such as Syria, Libya, and Yemen to the economic and political turmoil over the last decades. Similarly for testicular cancer research, our study showed no research productivity since the year 2000 from Libya and Yemen, whereas a sole case report manuscript was published from Syria.

In congruency with the rise in testicular cancer research productivity over the last two decades, there has been a shift in study types toward higher level of evidence publications, where oncology-related clinical trials in the MENA region steadily increased over the years[15]. In fact, clinical trials are favorably conducted in MENA countries due to the large patient pool, the high demand for medications, and the lower clinical trial operational costs compared to the western countries[15]. In contrast, our study revealed an almost nonexistent publication record for randomized clinical trials related to testicular cancer where over the last 20 years only one randomized clinical trial was published in the year 2014. The overall oncology productivity shift toward clinical trials in the MENA region did not translate to testicular cancer mainly due to: decreased availability of testicular cancer registries, decreased research funding, and limited collaborations with healthcare industries abroad.[15,16] The latter being evident by the results of our study showing an American or European collaboration rate of 12.3%.

Funding of research projects plays a fundamental role in improving rates of publications and the quality of research output from a countries’ healthcare institutions. This remains a major hindering factor for countries in the MENA region, where the number of funded institutions is a minority compared to Western institutions that dedicate annual funds to different departments ensuring continuous productivity and long-lasting scientific progress[17]. This was clearly reflected upon our study results, whereby only 45 publications out of 462 (9.7%) were funded by public or private sectors (6.1% and 3.6%, respectively). To note, Iran and Turkey were the two most funding-oriented countries in the region.

Lastly, the contribution of female authors in research revolving around testicular cancer in the MENA region remains largely low, this is in line with prior studies revealing a significant female authorship omission in Arab countries as compared to research and female representation worldwide[18]. As a matter of fact, our data showed that female author contribution was absent in 42.9% of all testicular cancer output over the last two decades. Factors that highly contribute to these observations include gender disparities present in the MENA region, especially the Arab world, when it comes to female representation in the healthcare sector. Globally, women’s contribution to the healthcare field exceeds 70%, yet they only fill up to 25% of senior roles[19]. These numbers are further accentuated in the MENA region as discrimination is more pronounced and heavily mirrors the gender disparities in research productivity as evidenced by our study results.

Conclusion

The incidence of testicular cancer in the MENA region is on the rise leading to a significant increase in cancer-related mortality rates. Risk factors for testicular cancer in young men include undescended testis, family history, HIV infections among others. Treatment modalities can include orchidectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy which can ultimately lead to temporary or permanent infertility or impotency. Therefore, the advancement of related research and funding is required for early diagnosis and improved treatment modalities. Across the MENA region, increasing the research output of individual countries is essential for accentuating health policies and facilitating the delivery of care for testicular cancer patients. Despite the increase in research output related to testicular cancer over the last two decades, several limitations still impede significant progress. The authors suggest three important changes that include the creation of standardized oncology registries, increasing research-related funding, and expanding collaborations with healthcare institutions abroad. Female contribution in research has also increased yet higher contribution rates are expected to increase research output in terms of quantity and potentially raising the quality of research studies performed such as RCTs which remain lacking in the MENA region.
