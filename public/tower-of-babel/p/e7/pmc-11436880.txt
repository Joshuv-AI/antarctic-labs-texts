Enhanced removal of tetracycline by vitamin C-modified cow manure biochar in water
Authors: Haoran Ma, Baiting Zhang, Shiyao Wang, Chunrui Liu, Liya Zhu, Zitong Zhao, Wei Li, Ziyi Shao, Xiao Liu, Yingjie Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitamin C (VC), due to its chemical properties, can provide more oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl groups for biochar (BC), which promotes the adsorption of tetracycline on biochar. Therefore, in this study, cow dung biochar (CDBC) was modified with VC and VC-modified CDBC (CDBC-VC) was synthesized. The modified biochar was characterized and related factors, adsorption kinetics, isotherms and adsorption mechanisms were investigated. Adsorption kinetics indicate a fast rate of adsorption. The adsorption isotherms showed that the maximum adsorption capacity was 31.72 mg/g (CDBC) and 50.90 mg/g (CDBC-VC), respectively, and the adsorption process was inhomogeneous with multiple molecular layers and the adsorbent has a higher affinity. Mechanistic studies showed that hydrogen bonding interactions, π-π electron donor-acceptor interactions, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions were the key to the adsorption process. The analysis of adsorbent regeneration showed that CDBC-VC had good adsorption performance. CDBC and CDBC-VC showed the best performance in simulated industrial wastewater with removal rates of 78.81% and 93.69%. The adsorption mechanism was comprehensively analyzed using six machine learning models. The extreme gradient boosting model gave the best fit. Analysis of the weights of the input variables for predicting adsorption efficiency showed that the ratio of initial TC concentration to BC dosage (29.8%), specific surface area (23%), isoelectric point (8.8%), and ash content (7.7%) had a significant effect on the predicted results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73210-4.Keywords: Adsorption, Biochar, Machine learning, Tetracycline, Wastewater applicationsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Antibiotics are natural or synthetic compounds with antimicrobial properties, and they have extensive applications in treating human diseases, as well as in animal husbandry, agriculture, and aquaculture1,2. The global usage of antibiotics was 93 Mt. in 20173, and forecasted to be over 236 Mt. in 20304. In particular, tetracycline (TC) is a commonly used antibiotic in agricultural production due to its good antimicrobial efficacy, and it is the second most widely utilized antibiotic worldwide5. However, the abuse of TC leads to severe environmental pollution. Reports indicate that TC levels can reach 5.4–8.1 ng/L in surface water, 86.2–198.7 µg/kg in soil, and up to 1000 ng/L in municipal sewage6,7. The presence of TC hinders the growth and development of aquatic species, and its over-accumulation in the food chain can affect human health and lead to diseases such as cancer, endocrine disruption, and central nervous system defects8. Furthermore, the prolonged presence of TC antibiotics can potentially induce the emergence of antibiotic resistance genes, thereby making it difficult to eliminate pathogenic bacteria9. Consequently, removing residual TC from water environments is crucial for ecological safety and human health.

The commonly applied methods employed for removing TC from aqueous solutions include biodegradation, photocatalysis, electroflocculation, and adsorption10–13. In particular, adsorption is a straightforward, facile, and highly efficient approach14. In contrast to several other methods, adsorption is characterized by its versatile applicability, low cost, and lack of by-products15. Biochar (BC) is a carbonaceous material generated through pyrolysis under oxygen-limited conditions. Due to its large specific surface area (SBET) and high porosity, BC is highly effective at adsorbing TC as well as being economical and environmentally friendly, thereby explaining its widespread use16. In a previous study, BC was synthesized via the pyrolysis of arrowroot leaves at 650 °C, and the resulting BC had a high maximum TC adsorption capacity (qm) of 122.65 mg/g17. In another study, BC was prepared from oak and 0.05 g of the BC was added at an initial concentration of 25 mg/L to 50 mL of TC solution at 20 °C, and the qm for TC was determined as 96.10 mg/g18. However, some adsorbents are relatively complex and expensive to produce, so there is a need to find more suitable adsorbent materials. Cow dung biochar (CDBC) is favored because of its abundant supply, mild preparation conditions, simple synthesis technique, and excellent adsorption properties19.

Due to the limited SBET and pore structure of the pristine biochar material, TC adsorption could easily reach saturation20. It was shown that the adsorption capacity of biochar can be improved by chemical modification in addition to increasing SBET21. For example, modification of BC with humic acid to remove methylene blue from water showed that the modified hydroxyl functional group effectively improved the chemisorption capacity22. Vitamin C (VC) is rich in oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl groups. Modification with VC is effective for altering the surface properties of BC by eliminating metals and impurities, increasing the number of active sites and chemical bonds in the BC’s structure, enhancing the mesoporous structure and SBET for efficient channel transportation, and improving the synergy of hydrogen bonding and π–π electron donor–acceptor (EDA) interactions, thereby increasing the adsorption capacity and complexation stability23–27. VC modification makes the BC surface rough, cracks appear on the surface, and the particles are spherical and broken23. The increased number of hydroxyl groups contributes to an increase in the negative charge on the BC’s surface to strengthen its electronegativity and indirectly enhance its stability27. The ability to absorb various pollutants through ion-exchange, ligand, van der Waals force, and hydrogen bonding interactions is also increased5. Furthermore, modification with VC widens the pH range operating for the BC28. In particular, the use of weak acidic VC for BC modification rather than strong acid–base modification effectively prevents potential pore collapse29. A previous study showed that BC prepared from grapefruit peel rich in VC had a more abundant array of surface functional groups and excellent TC adsorption performance30. Therefore, this new modification method has the possibility and importance for biochar adsorption of TC. However, the possibility of VC-modified CDBC (CDBC-VC) for TC removal has not been investigated. We will aim to synthesize CDBC-VC to investigate its adsorption TC effect.

Previous investigations mainly elucidated the effects of individual variables on the adsorption of TC by BC based on characterization experiments and batch adsorption experiments. However, these studies often overlooked the roles of individual variables in the adsorption process, thereby limiting our understanding of the effects of individual variables on the capacity of BC to adsorb TC31. Furthermore, the limitations of conventional laboratories, which are both labor- and time-intensive, impede progress in enhancing the design of BC by comprehensively studying the surface properties of adsorbents, adsorbent–pollutant interactions, and adsorption systems32. Machine learning is characterized by a diverse array of algorithms and high portability to allow the optimization of process parameters and understand adsorption mechanisms to solve the above problems33. At the present time, machine learning has increasingly been applied in studies of antibiotic adsorption but machine learning models have not been integrated with experiments to investigate the adsorption of TC on VC-modified BC derived from CD. In addition, research in recent years uses only 1–3 machine learning models, and other models not used may have a better fitting effect for the experiment. Thus, in the present study, we focused on the TC adsorption process by VC-modified BC derived from CD by using a combination of experimental data and six machine learning models fitting.

The main objective of this study was to investigate the adsorption effect of CDBC-VC on TC and the prediction data obtained by combining the experimental data with six machine learning models. The prepared BCs were analyzed by comparing their physicochemical properties, such as the surface morphology, SBET, size, and elemental composition. The adsorption behavior of CDBC-VC was investigated based on the adsorption kinetics, related factors, isotherms, adsorption mechanisms, and regeneration properties, as well as applying it to adsorb TC from simulated wastewater. Six models were fitted using machine learning to assess their ability to predict the adsorption of TC by BC, and the relative importance of each input variable was evaluated based on the root mean square error (RMSE) loss function. The effects of the physicochemical properties of the BC and adsorption conditions on the TC adsorption process were also investigated.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

The CD utilized in this study was sourced from Songhua River Dairy Farm in Harbin City, Heilongjiang Province, China. TC (C22H24N2O8; purity > 98%) was procured from Shanghai Een Chemical Technology Co. Other chemicals, including VC (ascorbic acid: C6H8O6), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium chloride (NaCl), orthoboric acid (H3BO3), sucrose (C12H22O11), sodium acetate (CH3COONa), among others, were of analytical purity and obtained from Tianjin Fuchen Chemical Reagent Factory.

Preparation of CDBCs

The pretreatment involved tap water-washed CD, which was subjected to drying in a constant temperature blast drying oven set at 105 °C until reaching a constant weight. Subsequently, under oxygen-limited conditions in a muffle furnace, the CD was placed in a porcelain crucible within a muffle furnace and subjected to pyrolysis at a heating rate of 15 °C/min, reaching a temperature of 700 °C. The pyrolysis process lasted for 120 min, followed by natural cooling to room temperature, extraction, and thorough cleaning of the carbonized products with distilled water. The electrically heated blast dryer was then employed at 105 °C to achieve a constant weight. The resulting material was ground, dried, and passed through a 100-mesh sieve, with the prepared raw BC designated as CDBC. In the modification process, a solution of VC (ascorbic acid: C6H8O6) was utilized. Specifically, 2.0 g of VC was dissolved in a 50 mL solution, mixed with BC, and the impregnation ratios of BC to VC were set at 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 (gBC/gVC). Following this procedure, the specimens underwent removal, and the modified materials were separated through filtration using a filter membrane. Subsequently, they were subjected to drying in an oven at 105 °C for a duration of 3 h, resulting in the preparation of modified BC denoted as CDBC-VC1, CDBC-VC2, and CDBC-VC3.

Physical and chemical characteristics of CDBCs

A Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) was employed for the comprehensive analysis of adsorbent morphology and physical properties. Elemental composition percentages were determined through organic elemental analysis using 3 H-200PM1 (Best Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.). Vacuum degassing and drying procedures were conducted with an appropriate quantity of BC. Isothermal adsorption-resolution was carried out under nitrogen, with non-local density functional theory utilized to calculate pore volume and pore diameter, contributing to the determination of the SBET. For crystal structure analysis, X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Dmax/2200, Rigaku, Japan) was employed. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) (8400 S, Shimadzu Instruments Co., Ltd., Japan) was utilized to analyze surface functional group species and their distribution.

Adsorption experiments

In this study, the influence of various factors, such as reaction time, BC dosage, initial solution concentration, solution pH, and salt ion concentration on the adsorption efficacy of both the CDBC and CDBC-VC were systematically explored, and three replicated experiments were performed to explore each factor. Additionally, the regenerative capabilities of the adsorbent and characterized the kinetics and isotherm of the adsorption process were investigated. The experimental setup involved introducing 50 mL of a 10.00 mg/L TC solution into a 100 mL conical flask. Subsequently, 10.0 mg of BC was added for adsorption. The mouth of the conical flask was sealed with a sealing film after the completion of the reaction, and then the flask was subjected to constant-temperature oscillation under the conditions of rotational speed of 165 rpm and a temperature of 25 °C, and then taken out and set aside after the completion of the oscillation. After filtration through a 0.45 μm water-based filter, the TC residue in the filtrate was determined at 358 nm using an UV spectrophotometer (V- 1800, Shimadzu, Japan). According to the standard curve, the corresponding removal rate of TC as well as the TC concentration of the solution were calculated, and the average of three determinations was the final data. Throughout the experiments, different contact times were set from 0 to 720 min, adsorbent additions were set from 0.04 to 0.8 g/L, the initial TC concentration was set from 5.00 to 100.00 mg/L, the solution pH was adjusted from 3.0 to 11.0 with HCl or NaOH, and NaCl was added to the TC solution, and the salt ion concentration was set from 0 to 0.10 mol/L.

Adsorbent regeneration and simulated wastewater experiments

In this experiment, we used the classical high-efficiency ultrasonic desorption, where the treated BC was allowed to stand, the supernatant was removed, and ultrapure water was added and stirred well, and then shaking at constant temperature for 24 h, speed and temperature as above, drew out and dried at 60 °C in a constant temperature oven, regenerative adsorption experiments were performed by adding 50 mL of TC solution at a concentration of 10.00 mg/L.

Static adsorption experiments were carried out using the CDBC and CDBC-VC, and the artificial water distribution method was used to simulate domestic wastewater, aquaculture wastewater and industrial wastewater, and TC at a concentration of 10.00 mg/L and a certain amount of BC were added to each of the three different simulated wastewaters, the running time was 24 h.

Machine learning fitting

In this experiment, the data related to BC adsorption TC in the past 10 years were collected on the Web of Science search engine. A larger range of data was filtered to make the model more generalizable. The experimental data obtained from the paper were screened to exclude missing and inaccurate data. Twenty relevant papers with complete data were eventually screened and included in the database for analysis along with this paper. Of these, 45 types of BC are from livestock waste, agricultural waste, forestry waste, food waste and other specific solid wastes such as banana peels, coconut shells, bamboo residue and crayfish shells. Results and discussion

Effects of different impregnation ratios on adsorption by CDBC

The TC removal rates by CDBC, CDBC-VC1, CDBC-VC2, and CDBC-VC3 were characterized by an initial rapid increase in adsorption, followed by a slower rate of increase until a steady state was reached. Equilibrium was attained after 6 h by CDBC-VC1, CDBC-VC2, and CDBC-VC3 with removal rates of 90.86%, 97.14%, and 94.00%, respectively. By contrast, CDBC reached equilibrium at 12 h with a removal rate of 82.36%. Therefore, CDBC-VC2 was the most effective at removing TC, so our subsequent TC adsorption experiments applied BC modified with VC at an impregnation ratio of 1:2.

Characterization of the CDBC and CDBC-VC

SEM analysis was conducted for CDBC and CDBC-VC, and the results are presented in Fig. 1a–b. The pristine CDBC had a relatively smooth surface with elevated structures and a high degree of integrity, with only a few sparse pores. CDBC-VC was characterized by a substantially higher degree of raised folding, with a deeply folded structure and fold-like fragmentation.

(a) Scanning electron microscopy of CDBC (b) Scanning electron microscopy of CDBC-VC.

Table 1 presents the results of organic elemental analysis for CDBC and CDBC-VC. The findings revealed a decrease in the oxygen content of CDBC-VC, while carbon, nitrogen and hydrogen content all increased, along with a reduction in ash content. Studies have demonstrated a correlation between organic functional groups on the BC surface and elemental hydrogen and oxygen, and these functional groups affect the performance of BC adsorption of TC1. The H/C ratio serves as an indicator of aromaticity and carbonization, with a smaller ratio signifying a higher degree of aromaticity. The H/C ratio of CDBC increased from 0.126 to 0.138, indicating that VC modification led to a reduction in the aromaticity of BC. The O/C ratio serves as an indicator of alterations in the hydrophilicity of BC. The O/C ratio of CDBC exhibited values of 0.704 before modification and 0.532 after the modification. In this investigation, the (O + N)/C ratios exhibited a decreasing trend, accompanied by diminishing O/C and (O + N)/C ratios, indicating an augmented hydrophobicity of BC and a reduction in surface polar functional groups39.

Adsorption isotherms

The adsorption isotherm describes the correlation between the concentration of the residual pollutant and the adsorbed quantity when the adsorption reaction reaches equilibrium at a specific temperature, and thus studying the adsorption isotherms can help to understand the interactions between the TC and adsorbent, as well as the structural characteristics of the adsorption layer8. The TC adsorption isotherms obtained for CDBC and CDBC-VC are presented in Fig. 3c. The capacities of CDBC and CDBC-VC for adsorbing TC tended to increase as the equilibrium concentration increased, possibly due to the increase in the TC concentration providing more opportunities for TC to make contact with BC, and thus more adsorption sites on the BC’s surface were occupied, and the adsorption capacity increased. The adsorption process was nonlinear according to the gradual stabilization of the adsorption capacity as the equilibrium concentration increased. We employed Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin models for isothermal fitting analysis, where the results are shown in Fig. 3d–f and the isothermal fitting parameters are presented in Table 4. The Langmuir model is usually applied to homogeneous monolayers and short-term adsorption processes where all adsorption sites have equal energies, whereas the Freundlich model is applied to multilayer adsorption where the energy distribution of the active sites is not homogeneous44. Therefore, for both CDBC and CDBC-VC, the Langmuir’s R2 values were lower than 0.9800 and the that of Freundlich’s were greater than 0.9800, and thus the Freundlich model fitted better than the Langmuir model, thereby suggesting that TC was unevenly adsorbed as a multimolecular layer on the surfaces of CDBC and CDBC-VC45. The 1/n values derived from the fitting of CDBC and CDBC-VC were 0.1500 and 0.1352, respectively, and the values lower than 0.5 indicated that the adsorption processes were characterized by rather strong nonlinear properties. Temkin model showed a good fit with R2 values of 0.9817 and 0.9808 for CDBC and CDBC-VC, respectively. KT values were 632.33 and 3856.93 for CDBC and CDBC-VC indicate that at equilibrium, the adsorbate is more likely to be adsorbed onto the surface of the adsorbent, suggesting that the adsorbent has a higher affinity. In addition, the capacities of CDBC and CDBC-VC for adsorbing TC were stronger compared with those reported in the previous studies presented in Table 55,21,46–48.

Adsorbent regeneration analysis

Regeneration tests were performed to assess the reusability of the adsorbents in practical applications. The results presented in Fig. 4a show that the TC removal efficiencies by the initial CDBC and CDBC-VC were 82.36% and 97.14%, respectively. These high removal rates were sustained throughout subsequent cycles but after the third cycle, the adsorption rates dropped to 19.63% with CDBC and 30.11% with CDBC-VC. After repeated adsorption, the decreased adsorption may be explained by the BC adsorbing TC to irreversibly occupy the surface adsorption sites, and thus the adsorption capacity was lost. After three cycles, CDBC reached saturation but CDBC-VC maintained a considerable adsorption capacity, thereby demonstrating its superior performance as an adsorbent.

(a) Effect of regeneration on adsorption of TC (The adsorption conditions were 25 °C, the TC concentration was 10.00 mg/L, V = 50 ml, the adsorbents were 0.4 g/L, and the reaction time was 12 h, pH = 7.0) (b) Adsorption effect of additive on simulated wastewater (The adsorption conditions were 25 °C, the TC concentration was 10.00 mg/L, V = 50mL, the adsorbents were 0.4 g/L, set the adsorption period to 24 h).

To further assess the practical applications of the BCs, we evaluated the TC adsorption capacities of CDBC and CDBC-VC in three simulated wastewater samples under an adsorption cycle of 24 h, as shown in Fig. 4b. The TC removal efficiencies of the adsorbents declined rapidly in domestic wastewater, probably due to the presence of coexisting ions that may have competed for active adsorption sites49. In farm wastewater, the TC removal rates by CDBC and CDBC-VC were 76.53% and 83.62%, respectively, possibly due to the presence of more microorganisms inducing a synergistic effect50. The performance of the adsorbents at treating industrial wastewater agreed with their efficiency in deionized water, which may have been due to the higher concentration of heavy metal ions in industrial wastewater. Studies suggest that heavy metals such as Cu2+ can form complexes with TC to enhance the TC adsorption rate51. Consequently, CDBC and CDBC-VC could possibly be applied to remove TC from industrial wastewater with good results.

Adsorption mechanisms

The functional groups comprising –C6H5, –NH2, and –OH on TC could have formed hydrogen bonds with –OH or –NH2 on the BC’s surface to produce strong, energy-efficient hydrogen bonds that were difficult to break52–54. The FTIR spectra contained characteristic peaks related to O–H or N–H stretching vibrations in both CDBC and CDBC-VC, and they differed after adsorption, thereby confirming that hydrogen bonding interactions were mainly responsible for TC adsorption by CDBC and CDBC-VC. In particular, the size of the peak centered at 3380 cm[–1 decreased after TC adsorption, which denoted the presence of hydrogen bonding interactions55–57. Changes in the characteristic peaks due to C-H bending vibrations or the symmetric stretching vibration peak of the benzene ring at distinct positions were found after adsorption in both adsorbents, thereby demonstrating the importance of π–π EDA interactions in the TC adsorption process by BC. The increases in π–π EDA interactions influenced the intensity of the peaks, possibly by modifying the molecular conformation or affecting the symmetric stretching vibrational activity of the molecules. After VC modification, the peak intensity increased but the subsequent adsorption of TC led to a decrease in size of the peak, which suggests that the interaction may have been enhanced by the modification with VC. TC is a hydrophobic organic compound and it is adsorbed onto BC through hydrophobic interactions58–60. CDBC-VC modified with VC had a reduced O/C ratio, and thus the hydrophobicity of the BC was significantly increased, which facilitated the migration of TC from polar water molecules to the hydrophobic domain on the surface of the modified BC to amplify hydrophobic interactions and increase the removal efficiency61–63. The changed pH significantly affected the surface charge on the BC and the form of TC in solution, thereby modulating the adsorption dynamics between BC and TC through electrostatic interactions64,65. TC is present as TC+ when the pH is < 3.3, as TC0 when 3.3 < pH < 7.7, and as TC−, TC2− when pH > 9.7. The experiment measured the pHpzc of CDBC and CDBC-VC as 6.82 and 7.03. In acidic solutions, where the solution pH is lower than its pHpzc, the BC surface is positively charged, which leads to electrostatic repulsion of the predominantly positively charged TC+. In contrast, in alkaline conditions, where the solution pH is higher than its pHpzc, the BC surface is negatively charged, which leads to the repulsion of the predominantly negatively charged TC− and TC2−. In addition, the higher concentration of -OH in alkaline solutions prevents the formation of hydrogen bonds between BC and TC, which reduces the adsorption efficiency. Therefore, as shown in Fig. S2f, at pH = 3.0–7.0, the removal of CDBC and CDBC-VC increased with the increase of pH. At pH = 7.0, the solution pH is closer to the pHpzc of CDBC and CDBC-VC, ions in the form of TC0 predominate, electrostatic repulsion is weakest, and adsorption efficiency is highest. At the sametime, the maximum adsorption rates were 82.36% and 97.14%, respectively. Thereafter, at pH = 7.0–11.0, the removal rate started decreasing with the increase in pH. This also indicates that electrostatic interactions play an important role in this process66,67. The introduction of salt ions had inhibitory effects on CDBC and CDBC-VC. Previous studies have also suggested the involvement of cations in solution where they may engage in competitive interactions with antibiotics for adsorption sites to decrease the removal rate (see Fig. 5 for the detailed adsorption mechanisms)68.

Statistical analysis of physical and chemical properties of BCs

Considering the amphoteric nature of TC, the equipotential charge point of BCs was particularly significant, and the equipotential charges in the database ranged between 5.02 and 8.10.

Construction and fitting of machine learning models

The fitting results and predictive performance of the six machine learning models are shown in Fig. 6; Table 7. Widely employed metrics comprising R2, mean square error (MSE), and RMSE were utilized to assess the performance of the machine learning models. R2 is used to assess the fit of the model to the target variable and its capacity to explain the variance in the data. An R2 value closer to 1.000 denotes a superior fit between the model and the data. MSE is the mean of the squared errors between the model’s predictions and the actual observations, where a smaller MSE indicates that the difference between the values predicted by the model and the true values are lower, and thus the performance is better70. RMSE is the average difference between the predicted and true values, where a smaller RMSE denotes a better fit between the model and the data. In the present study, the XGBoost model obtained the optimal fit, with an R2 of 0.9992, MSE of 0.3012, and RMSE of 0.5488. The other models were capable of predicting the effect of BC on TC adsorption but they exhibited less stability and higher discreteness71. Consequently, in this study, we analyzed the impact of each input variable on the prediction results obtained by using the XGBoost model72.

Construction and fitting of machine learning models

After identifying the XGBoost algorithm as the most suitable machine learning method, the XGBoost model was employed to treat individual features as split features, determine the optimal segmentation points, calculate the gains from splits, and select features with the highest gains as split features. Subsequently, the model filtered based on the selected frequency for each feature at the time of node splitting was used to obtain the effects of weights for individual input variables. The results are presented in Fig. 7a. We found that η (the ratio of the initial concentration of TC relative to the dose of BC) had the most significant effect on TC adsorption by BC in aqueous solution, followed by SBET (23%), pHpzc (8.8%), ash (7.7%), pHsolute (7.2%), (O + N)/C (6.5%), T (6.1%), C (5.5%), and H/C (5.4%). Figure 7b–e shows the dependence of different variables on the adsorption of TC. Clearly, η had the most significant effect on the adsorption of TC by BC due to the availability of adsorption sites on the BC’s surface. In most cases, η was within a relatively stable interval because the initial concentration in the solution increased the amount adsorbed and increasing the BC dose decreased the amount adsorbed31. Thus, the effect of the ratio of TC relative to BC on the amount adsorbed remained stable within a single interval, which agreed with the results obtained in the experiments. In addition, among the properties of the BC, the SBET value for BC significantly affected the results, where the amount adsorbed increased with the SBET value because a larger SBET value indicated the presence of more adsorption sites73. The equal charge points indicated the positive and negative charges on the BC’s surface, which influenced the electrostatic attraction and repulsion forces between BC and TC, thereby affecting the amount adsorbed. The ash content had a negative correlation with the amount adsorbed, possibly because increasing the ash content decreased the aromaticity of the adsorbent74. In this study, modification with VC reduced the ash content of the BC.

Conclusions

In this study, CDBC and CDBC-VC were prepared to assess their effectiveness at adsorbing TC from aqueous solutions. The qm values of TC on CDBC and CDBC-VC were 31.72 mg/g and 50.90 mg/g, respectively. CDBC-VC adsorbed TC through hydrogen bonding, π–π EDA interactions, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions mechanisms. The extreme gradient boosting model obtained the best fit. Using the extreme gradient boosting model fit to obtain the effect weights for each input variable based on the predicted results showed that the ratio of the initial TC concentration relative to the BC dose, SBET value for the BC, pHpzc and ash content wee most important for the predicted adsorption efficiency, with weights of 29.8%, 23%, 8.8%,7.7%, respectively. Due to the use of abundant, low-cost BC, CDBC-VC is less cost-effective compared to other reported adsorbents, making it a competitive option.In addition, the combination with machine learning models saves a lot of valuable resources for the comprehensive study of adsorption properties and the improvement of adsorbents.
