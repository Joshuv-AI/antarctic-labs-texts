Lumbar puncture–induced negative-pressure hydrocephalus in a child with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt and ventricular obstruction: illustrative case
Authors: Bryn L Hoffman, Arjun Suresh Chandran, Suzanne Laughlin, George M Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDNegative-pressure hydrocephalus is a rare form of symptomatic ventriculomegaly characterized by subatmospheric intracranial pressure (ICP). It can occur following lumbar puncture (LP) in patients with impaired CSF dynamics and is frequently mistaken for ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt malfunction.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe the case of a 9-year-old boy with prior medulloblastoma and a VP shunt who presented with neurological decline following LP. Symptoms transiently improved following a large-volume shunt tap, but ventriculomegaly persisted despite shunt revision. ICP monitoring confirmed negative pressure, consistent with post-LP negative-pressure hydrocephalus. Negative-pressure hydrocephalus, a severe form of low-pressure hydrocephalus, represents a rare but clinically important syndrome characterized by ventriculomegaly with paradoxically low or negative intracranial pressure (ICP). First characterized by Pang and Altschuler in 1994,1 the condition has since been described in both children and adults, often in the context of prior shunt placement, posterior fossa tumors, cranial base surgery, or craniospinal irradiation.2–5 Negative-pressure hydrocephalus poses a diagnostic challenge, as the radiographic appearance of enlarged ventricles is often attributed to shunt malfunction, leading to repeated and often unsuccessful revisions. Lumbar puncture (LP) or lumbar drain insertion has been identified as a precipitating factor in patients with a shunt, particularly those with posterior fossa pathology, where altered viscoelastic properties of the brain and impaired CSF compliance create vulnerability to abrupt pressure shifts.3,6–8 Here, we present the case of a 9-year-old boy with treated medulloblastoma who developed post-LP negative-pressure hydrocephalus.

Illustrative Case

This is the case of a 9-year-old boy with a history of WHO grade 4 medulloblastoma who underwent posterior fossa craniotomy for tumor resection followed by ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt placement. His initial postoperative course was complicated by shunt infection requiring shunt externalization, 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics, and eventual reinsertion of a medium-pressure shunt valve. He subsequently underwent craniospinal irradiation followed by 9 cycles of multiagent chemotherapy.

He presented to care at our institution 4 years following the initial resection and underwent LP for disease monitoring. Three days later, he presented to the emergency department with severe headache, vomiting, and somnolence. CT of the head demonstrated marked ventriculomegaly (Fig. 1). Shunt tap yielded 20 mL of low-pressure CSF with transient clinical improvement. He was taken to the operating room for shunt exploration. CSF flow from the burr hole reservoir was sluggish, and the decision was made to revise the entire shunt system with insertion of a new medium-pressure valve. However, postoperative imaging demonstrated persistent ventriculomegaly, and his symptoms did not improve.

He underwent reexploration, which demonstrated sunken dura, adequate flow, and functioning of both the proximal and distal shunt catheters. The valve was changed to a programmable Codman Certas valve (Integra LifeSciences), initially set to 3, and a left frontal external ventricular drain (EVD) for ICP monitoring was placed. Postoperative ICP monitoring demonstrated persistently negative pressures. Initially, a neck wrap to increase venous pressure was trialed with no effect. Although no epidural collection was found on spinal MRI, the working diagnosis was post-LP negative-pressure hydrocephalus, secondary to an occult spinal CSF leak from the initial LP.

An epidural blood patch was performed, and within hours, ICP rose and symptoms began to improve, although the patient’s clinical status fluctuated over several days. The EVD was removed after 1 week, and follow-up imaging demonstrated persistent slight ventricular enlargement. The Certas valve was adjusted from setting 3 to 1, and the patient experienced a gradual improvement in symptoms, returned to his pre-LP baseline, and was discharged home in stable condition with the neck wrap. Neck wrap use was gradually weaned over 2 weeks. Follow-up imaging showed resolution of the ventriculomegaly (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Observations

This case highlights several diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of negative-pressure hydrocephalus. The defining paradox of this condition is symptomatic ventriculomegaly despite low or negative ICP, a presentation that often mimics shunt failure and may lead to repeated, ineffective shunt revisions. In our patient, multiple shunt revisions failed to restore ventricular size or clinical improvement.

The current understanding of negative-pressure pathophysiology centers on a “transmantle” pressure gradient between the ventricular system and the subarachnoid space.6,9 A reduction in spinal subarachnoid pressure—most often from a CSF leak—impedes ventricular wall compliance and prevents normalization of ventricular size even under low- or negative-ICP conditions. In the context of presumed obstructive hydrocephalus, CSF may accumulate within the ventricular system, causing symptomatic ventriculomegaly despite negative pressure. Several authors have emphasized that spinal CSF loss can precipitate this, leading to ventriculomegaly refractory to standard shunt adjustments.4,6,7,10–12

Additionally, changes in brain viscoelasticity and compliance may contribute, with repeated or prolonged ventricular stretching resulting in persistent enlargement despite normal or low ICP.1,13 One proposed hypothesis, described by Vilela et al., suggests that loss of spinal CSF can result in siphoning of cerebral venous blood into the spinal subarachnoid space when normal craniospinal CSF communication is disrupted.4 In this case, there was evidence of membranous fourth ventricular outlet obstruction (Fig. 2). In the setting of foramen magnum or fourth ventricular obstruction, the spinal and intracranial compartments may no longer equilibrate effectively. This venous siphoning reduces cerebral venous volume and brain turgor, leading to decreased ventricular compliance and persistent ventriculomegaly despite low or negative ICP.

Importantly, our patient improved without the need for prolonged subatmospheric external ventricular drainage. Clinical and radiographic recovery occurred following epidural blood patch placement, neck wrapping, and shunt opening pressure adjustment alone, supporting the hypothesis that spinal subarachnoid pressure loss from an occult post-LP leak was the main driver of the negative-pressure state.

Similar pediatric cases have been reported. Dias et al. described post-LP negative-pressure hydrocephalus in children with posterior fossa tumors and shunts, where enforced bed rest alone enabled recovery.7 Smalley et al. reviewed the cases of 29 pediatric patients with low-pressure hydrocephalus, 37% of whom were post-LP. Interventions included bed rest, valve adjustment, epidural blood patch placement, and external CSF drainage.3 The seminal series of Pang and Altschuler demonstrated that ventricular size, rather than ICP, correlated with symptoms, and effective treatment often required prolonged subatmospheric drainage followed by shunt reinsertion.1 More recent reports have reinforced the link between LP or spinal CSF diversion and the onset of negative-pressure hydrocephalus, even in the absence of demonstrable spinal collections.4–6,9

Lessons

Negative-pressure hydrocephalus should be suspected in patients with a shunt who develop ventriculomegaly with low or negative CSF pressure following LP, even in the absence of an epidural CSF collection on imaging. The presence of foramen magnum or fourth ventricular obstruction should further heighten suspicion. Shunt revision alone is rarely curative and may subject patients to unnecessary procedural risk. Effective management requires a stepwise approach: initial conservative measures such as flat bed rest to reduce CSF loss and neck wrapping to increase venous and intraventricular pressure; interventions to restore spinal CSF pressure, most commonly epidural blood patch placement to seal an occult CSF leak; and shunt adjustments, including lowering the valve opening pressure or temporarily externalizing. Importantly, repeated LPs in patients with a history of low- or negative-pressure hydrocephalus should be avoided. Early recognition and adherence to this algorithm may prevent unnecessary shunt revisions, optimize patient outcomes, and guide individualized management strategies for this rare but diagnostically challenging syndrome.
