Effects of metallic debris and environmental factors on negative DC needle–plate air-gap discharge
Authors: Shaotong Pei, Xiaomeng Yang, Yunpeng Liu, Chenlong Hu, Saike Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the effects of metallic debris and environmental factors on the discharge characteristics of a negative DC needle–plate air gap, a high-voltage discharge test platform was established. Discharge spectra in the solar-blind ultraviolet band, ultraviolet images, high-speed images, and breakdown voltages were obtained. The results show that the emission spectra in the 240–280 nm band are mainly composed of the N2 Vegard-Kaplan band system and the NO γ band system, and the spectral profiles remain essentially unchanged under different metallic-debris conditions. Compared with the clean gap, metallic debris significantly reduces the breakdown voltage. At a gap of 2.5 cm, the breakdown voltage decreases from 48.66 kV to 15.50 kV and 18.55 kV after adding 0.6 g of aluminum and copper debris, respectively. At a 1 cm air gap, increasing the temperature from 15 °C to 65 °C reduces the breakdown voltage from 18.44 kV to 10.16 kV, corresponding to a decrease of 44.89%, indicating a reduced insulation margin at high temperature. Increasing pressure raises the breakdown voltage, whereas humidity has a relatively weak effect. These results provide an experimental basis for discharge analysis and condition assessment of air-insulated equipment under particle-contaminated conditions.Keywords: Air-gap discharge, Metallic contaminants, Environmental factors, Spectral analysis, Breakdown characteristicsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Discharge phenomena occurring in non-uniform air gaps are closely related to insulation deterioration, defect evolution, and operational reliability assessment of high-voltage power equipment. Under practical operating conditions, conductive foreign particles, sharp protrusions, and surface irregularities can lead to local electric-field distortion, thereby initiating corona discharge or even premature breakdown, which threatens the safe operation of air-insulated structures1,2. Compared with AC conditions, discharge behavior under DC voltage is generally more difficult to characterize3,4, mainly because it lacks the regular phase-resolved features commonly used in AC partial discharge diagnosis, while its evolution is more susceptible to voltage polarity, defect type, and environmental conditions5. In recent years, studies on DC partial discharge and corona have consistently emphasized the necessity of defect-oriented experimental investigations in gas insulation, particularly for non-uniform electric-field configurations that are closely associated with practical engineering insulation systems6.

Among the many factors affecting air-gap discharge, environmental conditions have long been regarded as critical variables. Humidity can alter electron attachment behavior and thereby influence the inception and development of negative corona in air, whereas temperature and pressure further affect ionization, photoionization, charge transport, and the formation of effective discharge channels. Previous studies have shown that the characteristics of negative corona vary with air humidity7,8; temperature, humidity, and pressure can jointly influence corona development through processes associated with photoionization9; and pressure variation in particular can cause pronounced changes in the corona discharge characteristics of a negative needle-plate electrode configuration10. Studies on short rod-plate air gaps have further indicated that, under non-uniform electric-field conditions, breakdown characteristics are highly sensitive to environmental conditions11. Nevertheless, existing studies have mainly focused on the effects of a single environmental variable on corona inception or breakdown characteristics under clean-gap conditions. Systematic experimental investigations performed on a unified test platform are still lacking with respect to the correspondence among discharge spectral features, image-based characteristics, and electrical breakdown behavior under varying environmental conditions. In other words, although the influence of environmental factors on discharge behavior in non-uniform air gaps has been widely recognized, there is still insufficient comprehensive experimental evidence to clarify how these factors are further reflected in discharge signatures and in the evolution of insulation performance. Therefore, temperature, humidity, and pressure should not be treated merely as auxiliary test settings, but should instead be systematically examined as key factors in the analysis of discharge behavior and insulation performance variation in non-uniform air gaps.

Another issue of considerable engineering significance is the influence of conductive contaminants in air-insulated gaps. Solid particles, metallic debris, and suspended conductors can all significantly distort the local electric-field distribution, thereby altering corona inception and breakdown characteristics12. However, previous studies have shown that the effects of conductive matter are not consistent across different configurations, and the relevant behavior depends strongly on particle type, concentration, size, morphology, spatial location, and electrode geometry13,14. In particular, compared with the broader body of research on solid particles, studies specifically addressing metallic debris distributed on or near the grounded electrode surface in negative DC needle-plate air gaps remain limited, although this form of conductive contamination is highly relevant in practical engineering because of its close association with local field distortion and defect-related discharge phenomena.

Meanwhile, optical methods have been increasingly applied in discharge diagnostics because they are non-contact and are less susceptible to electromagnetic interference15. Ultraviolet imaging has been widely used for corona detection in power systems16,17, while solar-blind ultraviolet technology in the 240–280 nm band offers distinct advantages for weak-discharge observation because it can effectively suppress most background radiation outside the solar-blind region18,19. In addition, optical emission spectroscopy can directly provide radiative characteristic information of the discharge plasma, which is helpful for identifying the dominant emitting species20,21 and interpreting the development process of discharges in air22,23.

Based on the above considerations, this paper experimentally investigates the discharge characteristics of a negative DC needle-plate air gap in air. On the one hand, to examine the influence of metallic debris, solar-blind ultraviolet emission spectra in the 240–280 nm band, discharge images, and breakdown voltage data were obtained for both the clean needle-plate configuration and configurations with different metallic-debris conditions, and the effects of debris material and mass on the discharge spectra and breakdown behavior were analyzed. On the other hand, in the absence of metallic contaminants, the effects of individual environmental factors, including temperature, humidity, and pressure, on the breakdown voltage of the needle-plate air gap were further investigated. By combining spectral measurements, image observation, and electrical breakdown tests on the same experimental platform, this study aims to clarify the influence of conductive contaminants and environmental factors on the discharge behavior of negative DC non-uniform air gaps, and to provide an experimental basis for discharge analysis and condition assessment of air-insulated structures.

Experimental platform for high-voltage discharge tests

A typical needle–plate electrode configuration was used in the experiment. The needle electrode was made of iron, with a length of 510 mm, a shaft diameter of 1.72 mm, and a tip diameter of 0.8 mm. The plate electrode was made of copper, with a diameter of 150 mm and a thickness of 3 mm. The electrode arrangement is shown in Fig. 1. After each breakdown event, the needle tip was visually inspected for erosion, deformation, and surface damage. The needle electrode was replaced after every ten breakdown events, or immediately when visible tip degradation was observed, to minimize the influence of electrode erosion on subsequent breakdown-voltage measurements.

Photograph of the needle-plate electrode setup.

The high-voltage discharge test platform, shown in Fig. 2, mainly consisted of four parts: the power supply, the discharge chamber, the environmental factor control system, and the optical measurement system.

High-voltage discharge test platform.

The power supply was a Dongwen DW-P503-1ACH1 negative DC high-voltage source with a maximum output voltage of − 50 kV. The high-voltage output terminal was connected to the needle electrode through a 10 kΩ protective resistor, which was used to limit the discharge current and improve the safety of the experimental system.

The discharge chamber was a customized enclosed chamber with detachable quartz observation windows installed on both sides, providing optical access for ultraviolet imaging, high-speed imaging, and spectral measurement. BNC interfaces were arranged on the chamber wall to connect the temperature–humidity probe and the electrode-gap adjustment device to the external control unit, enabling real-time monitoring of environmental parameters and precise adjustment of the electrode spacing. A lifting device with an accuracy of 0.01 mm was installed at the bottom of the chamber. The plate electrode was fixed on the lifting stage, and the needle–plate gap was adjusted by moving the plate electrode. In the pressure tests, gas was introduced into the chamber from a gas cylinder, and the internal pressure was controlled by adjusting the inlet valve. The pressure condition was determined according to the gauge-pressure reading. In this study, a gauge pressure of 0 MPa indicates that the pressure inside the chamber was equal to the ambient atmospheric pressure.

The environmental control system was used to regulate the gas temperature and relative humidity inside the discharge chamber. For temperature control, heating plates installed inside the chamber were used to heat the internal gas. Through gas convection and heat conduction, the gas, the needle–plate electrodes, and the other internal components gradually reached thermal equilibrium at the preset temperature. The chamber temperature was monitored in real time by an internal temperature sensor, and the heating process was adjusted by the external control unit through feedback control. For each temperature condition, the breakdown-voltage test was carried out only after the chamber temperature had stabilized. For humidity control, a self-developed humidity regulation device was used to adjust the relative humidity inside the chamber through humidification and dehumidification. The humidity was monitored during the experiments to ensure that the preset humidity condition was maintained before each breakdown-voltage test.

The optical measurement system integrated three functions: spectral measurement, ultraviolet imaging of the discharge, and high-speed imaging of the discharge process. Spectral measurements were carried out using a Spectropro HRS-750 spectrometer (Princeton Instruments) equipped with a PIX1024-BUV-SF-Q-F-A detector, whose quantum efficiency for incident radiation in the solar-blind ultraviolet band ranges from 53% to 68%. Spectral acquisition was controlled using LightField software. Ultraviolet images of the discharge were acquired using a UV camera manufactured by Changzhou Bose Technology Co., Ltd., while high-speed images were captured using an MS-XG51GM-T high-speed camera manufactured by Shenzhen Mingshiwei Technology Co., Ltd., as shown in Fig. 3a. The optical path for spectral measurement is shown in Fig. 3b. A direct focusing scheme with a converging lens was adopted to focus the radiation emitted by the discharge directly onto the entrance slit of the spectrometer, thereby effectively avoiding the attenuation of optical radiation signals associated with fiber transmission.

Spectrum measurement system. (a) Ultraviolet and high-speed imaging acquisition system for discharge, (b) Optical path diagram for spectral measurement.

In each test, the applied voltage was gradually increased until breakdown occurred, and the corresponding voltage was recorded as the breakdown voltage. For each experimental condition, the breakdown-voltage test was repeated five times. The arithmetic mean of the five measurements was used as the representative breakdown voltage for the corresponding condition, and the sample standard deviation was calculated to characterize the dispersion and repeatability of the measured data.

Experiments and analysis

Analysis of discharge emission spectra in the solar-blind ultraviolet band

To reduce the influence of ambient stray light on the spectral measurement, the spectroscopic experiments were conducted at night, and the test platform was shielded with 99% blackout cloth. During the spectral and imaging tests, the gas pressure was maintained at the measured ambient pressure of 1004 hPa, and the temperature was maintained at 15 °C. The spectrometer slit width was set to 250 μm, the grating density was 150 g/mm, and the exposure time was 100 ms. The gain of the ultraviolet camera was set to 4%, and the frame rate of the high-speed camera was set to 1000 fps.

Figure 4 shows typical discharge images under three conditions: 0.6 g copper debris, 0.6 g aluminum debris, and the clean needle–plate gap. The high-speed images mainly record broadband optical emission during the discharge process and can directly reflect the overall morphology, extension direction, and branching characteristics of the discharge channel. In contrast, the ultraviolet images respond to the characteristic radiation in the 240–280 nm band and therefore more clearly reflect the spatial distribution of the characteristic ultraviolet-emission region. The combined use of the two cameras enables mutual verification of the main discharge path and allows the discharge behavior under different debris conditions to be analyzed from two complementary perspectives: visible discharge morphology and characteristic ultraviolet-radiation distribution.

Discharge images captured by the high-speed camera (left) and ultraviolet camera (right). (a) 0.6 g copper debris, (b) 0.6 g aluminum debris, (c) clean needle–plate gap.

As shown in Fig. 4, the main discharge channels obtained from the high-speed and ultraviolet images are generally consistent, indicating that the 240–280 nm solar-blind ultraviolet radiation is mainly generated near the actual discharge channel. Meanwhile, certain differences in discharge morphology can be observed under different conditions. Under the copper- and aluminum-debris conditions, the discharge channels are relatively slender and the local brightness distribution is non-uniform, whereas under the clean needle–plate condition, the discharge region is more concentrated and the main channel is more distinct. This suggests that the introduction of conductive debris changes the local electric-field distribution and the position of discharge energy deposition, thereby affecting the discharge-channel morphology and radiation distribution.

The discharge spectrum of the clean needle–plate gap in the 240–280 nm solar-blind ultraviolet band is shown in Fig. 5. By comparing the measured characteristic peaks with the spectral lines reported in Refs.23–26, the main particle transitions responsible for the solar-blind ultraviolet emission in this band can be identified, as listed in Table 1. Table 1 summarizes the excited species, characteristic wavelengths, electronic transition forms, and vibrational transition information corresponding to the main spectral lines in the 240–280 nm band. As shown in Table 1, the characteristic spectral lines in this band mainly originate from the radiative transitions of N2 and NO molecules. Specifically, the N2 spectral lines are mainly attributed to the N2(A3Σu+ → X1Σg+) transition, namely the Vegard–Kaplan band system, whereas the NO spectral lines are mainly attributed to the NO(A2Σ+ → X2Π) transition, namely the NO γ band system. Here, ν′ and ν″ represent the vibrational energy levels of the upper and lower states involved in the transition, respectively26. These results indicate that the solar-blind ultraviolet radiation in the 240–280 nm band during air discharge mainly originates from the excitation and de-excitation processes of N2 and NO molecules.

Figure 6 compares the discharge spectra obtained under the aluminum-debris and copper-debris conditions in the 240–280 nm band. It can be seen that the spectral profiles under the aluminum-debris, copper-debris, and clean needle–plate conditions are generally similar. This result indicates that the introduction of conductive debris does not significantly change the dominant emitting species in this band, which remain mainly related to the radiative transitions of N2 and NO.

Discharge spectra of aluminum filings (left) and copper filings (right) in the 240–280 nm band.

From the viewpoint of discharge mechanism, the conductive debris placed on the plate electrode can distort the local electric-field distribution because of its irregular edges and protrusions. These local field-enhancement sites affect the inception position, propagation direction, and energy deposition region of the discharge channel, which explains the differences in discharge morphology and local brightness observed in Fig. 4. However, the discharge medium remains air under all tested conditions. Energetic electrons still mainly collide with N2 and O2 molecules, leading to the excitation of N2 and the formation and excitation of NO-related species. As a result, although the introduction of conductive debris changes the discharge-channel morphology and local radiation intensity, it does not fundamentally change the dominant gas-phase excitation processes in the 240–280 nm band. This explains why the ultraviolet spectral profiles remain similar under the aluminum-debris, copper-debris, and clean needle–plate conditions.

Breakdown-voltage characteristics of conductive-debris-contaminated gaps under different electrode spacings

Effect of conductive debris on breakdown voltage under different electrode spacings

To investigate the effect of electrode-gap variation on the breakdown voltage in the presence of conductive debris, comparative experiments were conducted using a clean needle–plate electrode configuration and configurations in which 0.6 g of copper or aluminum debris was placed on the surface of the plate electrode. Representative spatial distributions of the copper and aluminum debris on the plate-electrode surface are shown in Fig. 7. The conductive debris consisted of irregular fragments with non-uniform sizes and morphologies and was randomly spread over the plate-electrode surface after weighing. During the experiment, no fixed spacing, orientation, ordered arrangement, or compaction was imposed on the debris.

Schematic arrangement of copper and aluminum debris with different masses on the surface of the plate electrode. (a) 0.6 g aluminum debris, (b) 0.6 g copper debris.

The aluminum debris mainly exhibited silver-white irregular flake-like, strip-like, and locally curled morphologies, with relatively sharp edges. The projected equivalent length of individual aluminum fragments was approximately 0.5–4.0 mm, with most particles concentrated in the range of 1–2 mm. The copper debris mainly appeared as reddish-brown irregular flakes, granular fragments, and curled particles, with a projected equivalent length of approximately 0.3–2.5 mm, mostly concentrated in the range of 0.8–1.5 mm.

Owing to differences in density, fragment morphology, and accumulation characteristics, the copper and aluminum debris exhibited different spatial distribution features under the same mass condition. The copper debris tended to be more compactly distributed near the central region of the plate electrode, whereas the aluminum debris generally occupied a relatively larger spreading area. The equivalent diameter of the aluminum-debris deposition region was approximately 74 mm, corresponding to a coverage area of about 43 cm2, which accounted for approximately 24% of the plate-electrode surface. In contrast, the copper debris was more concentrated, with an equivalent deposition diameter of approximately 42 mm and a coverage area of about 14 cm2, accounting for approximately 8% of the plate-electrode surface.

In the experiments, the electrode gap was adjusted in a stepwise manner over a range of 1.0–5.0 cm, with an increment of 5 mm. Owing to the upper output-voltage limit of the power supply and the relatively high electric-field strength required for breakdown in the needle-plate configuration, the maximum electrode gap at which stable breakdown could be achieved in the needle-plate tests was 2.5 cm. The electrode gap was precisely adjusted using a precision lifting displacement device. As shown in Fig. 8, the grounded plate electrode was first raised slowly until it just contacted the needle electrode, and this position was taken as the zero reference position. The plate electrode was then moved downward to the preset gap with an adjustment accuracy of 0.1 mm, thereby establishing the experimental conditions for different gap spacings. This method ensured accurate control of the electrode gap and improved the repeatability of the experiments.

Schematic diagram of electrode gap adjustment.

The measured breakdown voltages under different conditions are listed in Table 2, and the corresponding variation trends are shown in Fig. 9. Within the measured gap range for each condition, the breakdown voltage increased with increasing electrode spacing. This is because, as the gap distance increases, the discharge channel must propagate over a longer distance before bridging the two electrodes. During this process, electrons experience more frequent collisions, diffusion, and energy loss, making it more difficult to sustain continuous electron multiplication and streamer propagation throughout the gap. Therefore, a higher applied voltage is required to sustain streamer propagation and form a continuous discharge channel across the gap.

Variation in breakdown voltage with gap distance for the clean needle–plate configuration and configurations containing 0.6 g of aluminum or copper debris.

Compared with the clean needle-plate gap, the gaps containing aluminum or copper debris exhibited significantly lower breakdown voltages. For example, at an electrode spacing of 2.5 cm, the mean breakdown voltage of the clean needle-plate gap was 48.66 kV, whereas the corresponding values for the gaps containing 0.6 g of aluminum and copper debris decreased to 15.50 and 18.55 kV, respectively. This reduction is mainly attributed to the local electric-field enhancement caused by the conductive debris. The irregular edges and sharp protrusions of the debris can be regarded as microscopic conductive extensions of the plate electrode. Owing to their small local radii of curvature, charges tend to accumulate at these positions, leading to local electric-field enhancement and distortion. The enhanced local field increases the energy of seed electrons and promotes impact ionization, electron-avalanche development, and streamer inception. In addition, some protruding fragments may reduce the effective insulation distance that must be bridged by the discharge. As a result, a continuous discharge channel can be formed at a lower applied voltage in the debris-contaminated gaps.

As shown in Fig. 7, the copper debris was more concentrated near the central region of the plate electrode, which is close to the main discharge path in the needle–plate configuration. This concentrated distribution may cause stronger local electric-field distortion near the axial discharge region, thereby affecting streamer inception and breakdown development. In contrast, under the same mass condition, aluminum debris generally has a larger total volume and a wider coverage area, which may introduce more exposed tips and local field-enhancement sites on the plate-electrode surface. Therefore, the difference in breakdown voltage between the aluminum- and copper-debris conditions should be interpreted as the combined result of material density, fragment size, morphology, spatial distribution, and contact state, rather than being explained solely by the central accumulation of copper debris or the intrinsic electrical conductivity of the materials. Because the size, morphology, orientation, and contact state of the debris were random in this study, the breakdown-voltage difference between the aluminum- and copper-debris conditions cannot yet be strictly attributed to a single factor. This mechanism will be further investigated in future work using more controlled debris size, morphology, and arrangement conditions.

Electrostatic-field simulation and corrected streamer-criterion prediction for the needle–plate gap

To further reveal the physical mechanism underlying the variation of breakdown voltage with gap distance in the non-uniform needle–plate air gap, two-dimensional axisymmetric electrostatic field models were established for four clean needle–plate gaps of 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 cm. In the simulation, a negative DC voltage was applied to the needle electrode, while the plate electrode was grounded. The air domain was treated as a homogeneous dielectric medium with a relative permittivity close to unity. After solving the electrostatic field, the electric-field strength E(s) and the reduced electric field E(s)/N were extracted along the central axis from the needle tip to the plate electrode, where s denotes the axial path coordinate and N is the gas number density.

Figure 10 shows the potential distribution and equipotential lines of the needle–plate structure under different gap distances. A pronounced non-uniform electric-field distribution can be observed in all cases, with the strongest potential gradient concentrated near the needle tip. This indicates that the high-field region near the needle tip is the key region for streamer inception. As the gap distance increases from 1.0 to 2.5 cm, the axial region over which the potential decays becomes longer, meaning that the streamer must propagate over a longer distance after inception before bridging the two electrodes. Therefore, for longer non-uniform gaps, a local streamer-inception criterion alone is insufficient to describe the complete breakdown process, and the propagation capability of the streamer across the gap must also be considered.

As shown in Table 3; Fig. 11, the conventional Meek criterion gives values of 15.47, 16.08, 16.71, and 17.15 kV for the 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 cm gaps, respectively. These values vary only slightly with gap distance and are much lower than the experimental breakdown voltages for larger gaps, indicating that the conventional Meek criterion mainly describes streamer inception near the needle tip and tends to underestimate breakdown in longer non-uniform gaps. After introducing the effective ionization propagation-length criterion, the corrected predicted voltages become 18.44, 27.97, 39.71, and 52.67 kV, with relative errors of 0, − 4.11%, − 1.13%, and + 8.25%, respectively. The corrected prediction agrees much better with the experimental results and reproduces the increasing trend of breakdown voltage with gap distance. This indicates that breakdown in the needle–plate gap is governed not only by local ionization and streamer inception near the needle tip, but also by the ability of the streamer to continuously propagate across the gap and finally bridge the two electrodes.

For the 2.5 cm gap, the corrected predicted voltage is slightly higher than the experimental value. This may be because the present model is still based on the pre-breakdown electrostatic-field distribution and does not explicitly include local electric-field enhancement caused by space charge at the streamer head, photoionization-assisted propagation, or the influence of humidity on the effective ionization coefficient. Therefore, the propagation-length criterion may be slightly conservative for longer gaps. Overall, the corrected effective ionization propagation-length model significantly improves the underestimation caused by the conventional Meek criterion and provides useful support for both mechanism interpretation and quantitative breakdown-voltage prediction in non-uniform needle–plate gaps.

As shown in Table 4; Fig. 12, the breakdown voltage decreased with increasing temperature for all electrode gaps. When the temperature increased from 15 °C to 65 °C, the breakdown voltages of the 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 cm gaps decreased from 18.44, 29.17, 40.17, and 48.66 kV to 10.16, 19.77, 28.64, and 35.65 kV, respectively. The corresponding absolute decreases were 8.28, 9.40, 11.53, and 13.01 kV, which increased with gap distance. This is mainly because larger gaps have higher initial breakdown voltages. To compare the temperature effect among different gap distances more reasonably, the relative decrease was used here to evaluate temperature sensitivity. The relative decreases for the 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 cm gaps were 44.89%, 32.22%, 28.70%, and 26.74%, respectively, indicating that smaller gaps exhibited stronger temperature sensitivity in the normalized sense.

From the viewpoint of gas-discharge mechanism, increasing temperature reduces the molecular number density per unit volume and increases the electron mean free path. Under the same applied electric-field condition, electrons can more easily gain sufficient energy between collisions, thereby promoting impact ionization and streamer development and making the air gap more prone to breakdown. Meanwhile, within the temperature range of 15–65 °C, the breakdown voltage at the same temperature consistently followed the order of 2.5 cm > 2.0 cm > 1.5 cm > 1.0 cm. This indicates that increasing temperature reduces the breakdown voltage overall, but does not change the dominant influence of electrode spacing on the breakdown-voltage level.

However, this does not mean that the temperature effect is always weaker than the gap-distance effect. When different gap distances and different temperature conditions are compared together, a large temperature rise may cause a reduction in breakdown voltage that is comparable to, or even greater than, the difference caused by a limited change in gap distance. For example, the breakdown voltage of the 2.5 cm gap at 65 °C was 35.65 kV, which was lower than that of the 2.0 cm gap at 15 °C, 40.17 kV. This indicates that both temperature and gap distance can significantly affect the insulation margin of the air gap.

It should be noted that the behavior at temperatures higher than 65 °C should not be inferred by simple linear extrapolation from the present data. This is because, at higher temperatures, the gas molecular number density, reduced electric field, electron collision and attachment processes, humidity coupling, thermal convection inside the chamber, and electrode-surface conditions may change further, leading to more complex nonlinear breakdown behavior. Therefore, the relative strength of the temperature effect and the gap-distance effect at higher temperatures still requires further experimental verification.

As shown in Table 7; Fig. 15, the influence of humidity on the breakdown voltage of the needle–plate air gap was relatively limited, and only slight fluctuations were observed for all gap distances. Unlike the monotonic decrease caused by increasing temperature, the humidity effect exhibited a weak non-monotonic behavior. For the 1.0 cm and 1.5 cm gaps, the breakdown voltage reached a relatively high level near 80% RH, whereas for the 2.0 cm and 2.5 cm gaps, higher breakdown voltages mainly appeared around 60%–70% RH, followed by a slight decrease under high-humidity conditions. Overall, the effect of humidity was much weaker than that of temperature and gap distance.

Physically, this non-monotonic behavior can be understood as the result of two competing mechanisms. Within the low-to-moderate humidity range, as the ambient humidity increases, water molecules in air, owing to their relatively strong electronegativity, tend to capture free electrons generated during the discharge process, thereby suppressing the initiation and development of electron avalanches and initial streamers. As a result, the breakdown voltage under negative DC conditions generally shows an increasing trend. As the humidity continues to rise, the electron-capturing effect of water molecules gradually approaches saturation, and the rate of increase in breakdown voltage correspondingly slows down. When the relative humidity approaches the saturation region, moisture condensation and water-film formation are likely to occur on the electrode surface, leading to local electric-field distortion and enhanced surface conductivity, which weakens the increasing trend of breakdown voltage and may even cause a certain degree of decline.

Integrated discussion

Cross-factor comparison and unified interpretation

Although metallic debris, temperature, humidity, and pressure all affect avalanche development and streamer propagation, their dominant mechanisms differ. Conductive debris mainly enhances and distorts the local electric field, whereas temperature and pressure alter the bulk gas density, electron mean free path, and reduced electric field. Humidity has a more complex effect involving electron attachment, charge transport, and photoionization.

Within the investigated ranges, conductive debris caused the greatest reduction in insulation performance. At a gap distance of 2.5 cm, the breakdown voltage decreased from 48.66 kV under clean conditions to 15.13 and 16.04 kV with 1.0 g aluminum and copper debris, respectively. By comparison, increasing temperature decreased the breakdown voltage, whereas increasing pressure raised it. Humidity produced only small, non-monotonic variations because several competing processes partially offset one another.

Accordingly, the investigated factors can be classified as local electric-field factors represented by conductive debris, gas-state factors represented by temperature and pressure, and gas-chemical or surface-state factors represented by humidity. However, this comparison is limited to the electrode configuration and parameter ranges considered in this study.

Possible coupled effects and engineering implications

Although the factors were investigated independently, they may coexist in practical equipment and produce coupled effects. For example, elevated temperature or low pressure may further reduce the insulation margin of a gap containing conductive debris by simultaneously promoting discharge inception and streamer propagation. Humidity may also alter particle agglomeration, surface wetting, charge transport, and particle–electrode contact.

Therefore, conductive contamination, elevated temperature, low pressure, and reduced local clearances should receive particular attention in insulation assessment. Particle removal, burr elimination, post-maintenance inspection, ventilation improvement, condensation prevention, and appropriate clearance correction are recommended. In addition, high-speed and solar-blind ultraviolet imaging provide complementary information on discharge morphology and ultraviolet-emission regions, improving defect identification reliability.

Limitations and future work

The proposed mechanisms are qualitative interpretations based mainly on breakdown voltages, discharge images, and ultraviolet spectra. Microscopic parameters such as the local electric field, electron density, space charge, particle-contact resistance, and surface moisture were not directly measured. Moreover, the size, morphology, distribution, and contact state of the debris were not independently controlled, and multi-factor coupling was not experimentally quantified.

Future work will combine controlled particle experiments, electric-field simulation, discharge-current measurement, plasma-chemical modelling, and multi-factor testing to quantitatively evaluate the individual and coupled effects of conductive debris and environmental conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, a high-voltage discharge test platform for a needle-plate gap under negative DC conditions was established. By combining spectroscopic measurement, ultraviolet imaging, and high-speed imaging, the discharge spectral characteristics and breakdown behavior of needle-plate air gaps in the presence of metal debris were systematically investigated. In addition, the effects of environmental factors, including temperature, humidity, and pressure, on the breakdown voltage were examined. The main conclusions are summarized as follows

In the 240–280 nm solar-blind ultraviolet band, the emission spectrum of the needle-plate gap discharge is mainly composed of the Vegard–Kaplan band system of N2 molecules and the γ band system of NO molecules. The spectral profiles obtained under the aluminum-debris, copper-debris, and clean needle-plate conditions are generally consistent, indicating that, under the present experimental conditions, variation in the metal-debris material does not alter the types of dominant radiative species in this band.
