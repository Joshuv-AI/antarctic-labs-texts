Beyond the Fixed Charge: A New On‐Tissue Derivatisation Strategy Employing Photoionisable Chromophores for Mass Spectrometry Imaging of Amino‐Containing Metabolites
Authors: Sofia R. McGowan, Andrew J. Tague, Ashna A. Kumar, Marie Ranson, Christopher J. T. Hyland, Shane R. Ellis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMatrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionisation mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI‐MSI) has been widely adopted for the spatially resolved mapping of chemical composition within biological tissues. However, inherent limitations in ionisation efficiencies limit the scope of analyte detection. Currently, methods to enhance molecular coverage have focused on chemical derivatisation of target analyte classes or post‐ionisation techniques, namely, laser post‐ionisation (MALDI‐2). Previously, we observed that under reduced laser fluence conditions, MALDI‐2 can be utilised to selectively enhance photoionisation of aromatic analytes. Leveraging these observations, we have conducted a proof‐of‐concept study to establish a conceptually novel class of derivatisation reagents, employing a tocopherol active chromophore O‐linked succinimide (TAC/OS) for the selective derivatisation of primary amines. Using TAC/OS, amino metabolites were selectively detected as the derivatised radical cations in the presence of MALDI‐2. Across three tissue types, over 30 metabolites were detected and localised, including 20 amino acids alongside neurotransmitters such as γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) and dopamine. Imaging at 10 µm pixel size revealed minimal delocalisation, allowing for MSI at near single‐cell resolution. Furthermore, we demonstrated the ability of the developed protocol on late‐stage pancreatic cancer xenografts.

Introduction

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) is a rapidly expanding and powerful approach for label–free molecular imaging. It is primarily applied to imaging of biological tissues [1, 2, 3] and, more recently, individual cells [4, 5, 6]. The most widely adopted approach in MSI is matrix–assisted laser desorption/ionisation (MALDI), wherein a pulsed UV laser (λ = 349–355 nm) is used to desorb material from a matrix‐coated sample surface, generating a plume in which photoexcited matrix molecules initiate analyte ionisation, typically forming protonated or deprotonated species through a variety of ionisation processes [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, low ionisation efficiencies present a significant hurdle that limits sensitivity and analyte coverage. It is estimated that ion yields are of the order of 10−3 to 10−9 ions per desorbed molecule, and this varies dramatically for different analyte classes [11, 12, 13]. Moreover, inherent ion suppression effects arise when analysing complex samples such as heterogeneous tissues and further reduce ion yields [14]. Consequentially, the most abundant and readily ionisable analytes account for the majority of detected signals, leaving a plethora of biologically important analytes under‐represented, or entirely undetected [15].

One method to overcome this is on‐tissue chemical derivatisation (OTCD) which involves the chemical modification of analytes within the tissue section (or other sample type) with highly reactive functional group‐targeting tagging reagents that contain a conserved fixed charge, typically a quaternary nitrogen [16, 17]. Targeted analytes are modified in situ to produce covalently bound derived ions of higher m/z that are more efficiently ionised due to the installed fixed charge. To selectively derivatise key analyte classes, an appropriate reactive moiety must be installed [16]. Amine‐targeting reagents often utilise nucleophilic acyl‐substitution reactions, such as O‐succinimidyl carbamates favourable for the derivatisation of amino acids, incorporated in the design of TAHS [18]. Pyrylium salts have also been employed, for example, by the Andren group in the design of the reactive matrix, polyphenylated 2‐fluoro‐1‐methyl pyridinium (FMP‐10) for the rapid derivatisation of amino and phenolic analytes, such as neurotransmitters [19]. Numerous derivatisation strategies have been reported for the detection of short‐chain fatty acids [20], bile acids [21], corticosteroids [22], sterols [23], and pharmaceuticals [24, 25]. To increase the scope of analyte detection beyond a single target class, more complex OTCD protocols have been reported, including combination procedures [26] and the design of dual‐target reagents [27]. Outside of OTCD approaches, the application of novel matrices can also enhance the signals for certain low mass metabolites such as amino acids; however, many analyte classes, such as some neurotransmitters and low mass volatile analytes, remain challenging for matrix‐only approaches [28, 29, 30, 31, 32].

An alternative strategy to enhance ion yields in MSI is the use of post‐ionisation strategies, which involve implementing a secondary ionisation event after the initial ionisation event. This leads to ionisation of analytes not ionised by the initial ionisation event [33]. A wide array of secondary ionisation sources, including VUV‐induced chemical ionisation [34, 35], plasma‐based methods [36, 37, 38], and UV laser‐induced post‐ionisation (MALDI‐2), have been deployed and provided significant breakthroughs in sensitivity for many analytes. First reported in 2015, MALDI‐2 involves the application of a secondary pulsed UV laser with a wavelength below the two‐photon ionisation threshold of the matrix (typically < 280 nm) to intercept the plume of desorbed ions and neutrals. This induces additional charge transfer events, typically leading to enhancements in [M+H]+ or [M‐H]− signals [39, 40]. MALDI‐2 is available commercially and shown to enhance overall detection coverage for many lipids [40], glycans [41], peptides [42], and low mass metabolites [28]. MALDI‐2 has also been shown to be beneficial for increasing detection of small molecules when used in tandem with fixed‐charge OTCD strategies [43]. The increased sensitivity of post‐ionisation strategies has also been central for recent development, pushing MALDI‐MSI pixel sizes down to 1 µm and below, including for single‐cell imaging [44, 45, 46, 47]. In addition to enhancing ionisation via MALDI‐like protonation and deprotonation processes, MALDI‐2 can also induce direct two‐photon ionisation of aromatic analytes via [1+1] resonance enhanced multiphoton ionisation (REMPI) to produce radical cations ([M]●+) for analytes with suitable chromophores [39, 48]. This effect was recently investigated by Sarretto et al. [49], who observed that the formation of radical cations of aromatic antioxidants such as vitamin E and ubiquinols increased as the primary (MALDI) laser fluence was decreased below the threshold required for MALDI‐2, which enabled simultaneous reduction in sampled area and increased signal intensity. Direct photoionisation via REMPI can potentially allow for more predictable ionisation trends, as it circumvents the complex and poorly understood charge transfer processes involved in MALDI/MALDI‐2 that make predicting sensitivity for a given analyte challenging [50, 51]. Photoionisable chromophores could offer an alternate core structure for OTCD reagent design strategy instead of traditional reagents that rely on fixed‐charge reagents. This concept was demonstrated in 2006 by Edirisinghe et al., who incorporated anthracenyl‐ and aminoquinolyl‐chromophores observed to undergo single photon ionisation into the synthetic design of peptide‐targeting reagents [52, 53]. Derivatised peptides were selectively detected as the tagged radical cation [M]●+ in the presence of a post‐ionising molecular fluorine laser (λ = 157 nm), conserving photoactive behaviour of the parent chromophore [53]. However, such approaches have not yet been harnessed for MSI.

Herein, we report for the first time the development of a custom MALDI‐2‐activated derivatisation strategy. It utilises novel OTCD reagents containing chromophores that are selectively photoionised as radical cations by MALDI‐2. In this proof‐of‐concept study, a novel tocopherol active chromophore‐linked O‐linked succinimide (TAC/OS) was designed and applied for the on‐tissue derivatisation of numerous primary amine‐ and thiol‐containing endogenous metabolites across numerous tissue types down to 10 µm pixel size. This work paves the way for a vast expansion of possible OTCD strategies that leverages recent advances in laser post‐ionisation, and unrestricted by synthetic and solubility requirements associated with the use of salts, as is the case with fixed‐charge based species.

Results and Discussion

Rationale, Design, and Synthesis of OTCD Reagent

For this study, a novel reagent was designed to include two functional regions that in combination enable MSI of amine‐containing metabolites: (1) a photoionisable chromophore domain for promotion of radical cation formation through direct two‐photon ionisation using a 266 nm laser; and (2) a reactive functional handle to enable the covalent tagging of low molecular weight containing analytes (Figure 1). The initial design of the chromophore is motivated by recent work demonstrating REMPI of endogenous vitamin E from biological tissues using the same experimental setup [49]. Thus, the aromatic chromanol core of α‐tocopherol was selected as the template for the required photoionisable chromophore domain (purple component, Figure 1). For the reactive handle, we selected an O‐succinimidyl carbamate. Such activated carbamates are a common choice in reagent design for the derivatisation of primary amines and thiols due to high selectivity and reactivity under mild conditions,– which are favourable for on‐tissue protocols, including those compatible with MSI such as the key parent reagent TAHS (Figure 1) [18, 54].

Conventional approach to chemical derivatisation reagent design versus our approach, employing the chromophore‐containing TAC/OS reagent, in combination with the application of a secondary pulsed 266 nm laser (MALDI‐2). Example fixed‐charge containing reagents TAHS, FMP‐10, and Pi‐BBA, originally reported by Shimbo et al. [18], Shariatgorji et al. [19], and Liu et al. [27], respectively.

To synthesise the required derivatisation reagent the chromanol core methyl 6‐hydroxy‐2,5,7,8‐tetramethylchromane‐2‐carboxylate 1 was prepared through a Mannich condensation then [4+2] hetero‐Diels–Alder cyclisation of the corresponding 2,3,5‐trimethylhydroquinone (Figure 2A). A short diamine linker was introduced to obtain N‐(2‐aminoethyl)‐6‐hydroxy‐2,5,7,8‐tetramethylchromane‐2‐carboxamide 2 – this linker was introduced not only as an accessible position for the installation of the reactive handle but also to reduce potential side reactions associated with N‐hydroxysuccinimidyl esters [55]. Finally, a reactive O‐succinimidyl carbamate was installed by coupling 2 with disuccinimidyl carbonate, producing the titled TAC/OS reagent in an overall yield of 24% over three steps.

Reagent Evaluation and In Solution Derivatisation of Standards

First, TAC/OS was evaluated for photoionisation capability using MALDI and laser post‐ionisation (MALDI‐2). As shown by Figure 2, TAC/OS retains key properties of the parent chromophore α‐tocopherol, readily forming the [M]●+ observed at m/z 433.1844 following laser desorption and photoionisation using a 266 nm laser pulse. The full mass spectrum is shown in Figure S1 and confirms the selective formation of the intact ionised reagent with minimal background signals. Notably, very little signal for the reagent [M]●+ was observed in the absence of MALDI‐2, confirming the specificity of the ionisation process for MALDI‐2 (Figure 2B). Upon photoionisation by MALDI‐2, several peaks were observed corresponding to fragmentation along the diamine linker and carbamate moiety of the resulting radical. As shown in Figure S1, analogous fragments are absent from the MALDI‐only spectra, indicating the process is dependent upon the presence of the 266 nm laser.

Having confirmed conservation of photoionisation properties, we next explored the ability of TAC/OS to derivatise biologically relevant amines via in‐solution reactions with synthetic standards (See Figure 2A for general schematic). Reactions for the chemical derivatisation of some biologically relevant amines, namely, γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), serotonin (5‐HT), and dopamine (DA), were conducted. Following incubation with TAC/OS, aliquots (n = 3) of the reaction mixture were spotted onto a microscope slide, coated with a 2,5‐DHA matrix, and analysed using MALDI‐2. For all analytes, peaks corresponding to the expected TAC/OS derivatisation product detected as the radical cation following REMPI were observed (Figure 2C). Additionally, multiply derivatised products can be observed for analytes possessing more than one reactive site, as reported for other OTCD approaches [16]. While both GABA and 5‐HT appeared primarily in the singly derivatised form, in the case of DA the doubly derivatised form was also observed (m/z 789.3959, 1.9 ppm), likely due to secondary derivatisation occurring at the present phenolic hydroxyl groups consistent with the literature (Figure S2A) [19]. Like analysis of the lone reagent, MALDI‐2 acquisition of the derivatised standards revealed several identical radical signals correlating to previously noted fragment ions arising from the reagent. The specific cleavage points are outlined in Figure S2B. Additionally, the base peak across spectra shown in Figure S2A (m/z 610.3367) is attributed to the formation of a reagent dimer that occurs because of hydrolysis and subsequent SN2 attack of the remaining intact TAC/OS reagent.

Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed on the ionised TAC/OS reagent, as well as several derivatised standards, to evaluate and understand TAC/OS‐specific fragments that could help identify derivatised features in more complex samples. Ion trap MS/MS of the mass‐selected isolated reagent at m/z 433.1844 revealed that dominant product ions of [TAC/OS]·+ were due to loss of the complete analyte group (m/z 318.17), as well as fragmentation along the diamine linker (fragment ions at m/z 205.09, 276.09, 292.17) (Figure 2D). Subjecting derivatised analytical standards of GABA, 5‐HT, and DA, alongside additional key amino acids cysteine, glycine, alanine, and glutamine to CID demonstrated matching reagent‐derived product ions, as well as characteristic fragments related to cleavage of the reagent‐analyte covalent bond position (Figure S3; Figure S4). As reported by Shimbo et al., the ureide or thiol ester formed as a product of the nucleophilic acyl substitution reaction between the O‐succinimidyl carbamate handle and the target analyte provides a distinctive cleavage site, allowing for differentiation of derivatised amine and thiol‐containing species [18]. For example, the cysteine [M+TAC/OS]●+ observed at m/z 439.1765 yielded fragment ions that indicated the formation of both the N‐linked ureide and S‐linked thiol ester derivatives that can be distinguished by the presence of product ions at m/z 336.17 and m/z 352.17, respectively (Figure S4).

TAC/OS for MSI of Amine‐Containing Metabolites

Having established the ability of TAC/OS to both derivatise amino metabolites and to selectively enable their ionisation and detection via REMPI using MALDI‐2, we next investigated the ability of TAC/OS to perform as an OTCD reagent for the MSI of endogenously present metabolites in biological tissues. The detailed workflow is provided in the Supporting Information. To summarise, prepared murine brain samples were coated with TAC/OS and allowed to incubate overnight prior to 2,5‐DHB matrix application. Following MALDI‐2 MSI, detected TAC/OS derivatised metabolites were putatively identified through accurate mass matching (≤ 3 ppm mass error), chemical feasibility considerations, comparison with previously reported ion distributions, along with evaluation of data‐dependent acquisition MS/MS data and comparison with MS/MS of derivatised analytical standards when available [56] (Table S1, Figure S5).

Pre‐washing of tissues in a selected solvent system can enhance the detection of low molecular weight metabolites and reduce potential ion suppression effects by removing abundant analytes such as lipids. In particular, pretreatment with cold chloroform has been reported as beneficial for the improved detection of polar metabolites such as amino acids [57]. When applied here, 13 out of 15 definitively identified metabolites showed a significant improvement in detection when subjected to the chloroform wash prior to TAC/OS application (n = 4), in comparison to the unwashed control (n = 2) (Figure S6). For example, ion intensities for [M+TAC/OS]•+ of GABA, serine, glutamate, and spermine increased by ∼threefold for each after washing. A comparison of washed and unwashed images for selected analytes is provided in Figure 3A (note for comparison these ion images are shown without any normalisation), while a full scope is provided in Figure S7. Thus, a chloroform washing step was employed prior to all MSI experiments discussed below. Subsequently, the optimised TAC/OS MALDI‐2 MSI protocol including the implemented prewashing step, was applied to consecutive murine brain sections (n = 3). Following filtering of database annotations to rule out chemically implausible and non‐biological annotations via an in‐house Python script, 77 peaks were indicated as TAC/OS‐derivatised annotations, of which 29 derivatised metabolites were assigned through comparison with MS/MS and/or supporting literature. A selection of ion images of representative metabolites which could be definitively assigned are shown in Figure 3B, while a full scope is provided in Figure S9.

Alongside 12 amino acids, short‐chain peptides, including the antioxidant glutathione (m/z 625.2387, −4.0 ppm), as well as its degradation intermediate cystinylglycine (m/z 496.1973, 2.6 ppm), were observed [58]. Several small alkyl amines were also identified, including ethanolamine (m/z 379.2101, −0.3 ppm), as well as the polyamines spermidine (m/z 463.3143, −0.9 ppm) and spermine (m/z 520.3711, −3.8 ppm). Some peaks matching the theoretical derivatised masses of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) lipids were also observed; however, detection was reduced by the chloroform washing step (see Figure S8). Analyte distributions were consistent with known biological distributions, amino acids alanine (m/z 407.2048, −0.7 ppm), serine (m/z 423.1998, −0.5 ppm), leucine/isoleucine (m/z 449.2512, −1.8 ppm), and glutamate (m/z 465.2093, −1.8) show fairly ubiquitous distribution across the brain with an increased expression within the corpus callosum and interbrain, while the neurotransmitter GABA (m/z 421.2205, −0.5 ppm) could be detected predominantly localised to the hypothalamic, mid brain, and basal forebrain regions, in line with previous reports [19, 59, 60]. Peaks matching the theoretical derivatised mass of the dopaminergic molecule DA (m/z 471.2352, −2.5 ppm) and the degradation metabolite 3‐methoxytyramine (m/z 485.2509, −2.3 ppm) appeared consistently localised to the caudate putamen, in line with the reported biological distribution [19]. Definitive identification of some derivatised features was prevented by the presence of isomeric reagent‐related background features. Although significantly lower than their ester counterparts, O‐succinimidyl carbamate‐derived reagents have been reported to undergo side reactions which appear isomeric to derived amino acids [55]. In particular, the hydroxamate ester side product, which appears as a result of alkaline hydrolysis, is isomeric with an m/z value matching the theoretical mass of derivatised aspartic acid (m/z 451.1941, −1.8 ppm). This is evidenced upon visualisation of the ion image, which reveals a strong and consistent off‐tissue signal (see Figures S9 and S10).

Given the use of solvent‐based deposition and incubation steps, we next explored the capability of the TAC/OS derivatisation protocol for high spatial resolution MSI. Figure 4 demonstrates selected ion images of derivatised metabolites in the fore‐ and hind‐brain regions of a murine brain at 10 µm pixel size, revealing fine anatomical localisations at near single‐cell resolution. Spatial distributions for many derivatised metabolites were observed in agreement with previously noted localisations, such as GABA (m/z 421.2205, −0.5 ppm) (Figure 4A, right), which appeared primarily confined to the hypothalamus in line with Figure 3B. For other features, application of high‐spatial‐resolution MSI revealed anatomical localisations not distinguishable in the previously discussed lower‐spatial‐resolution MSI brain dataset (60 µm pixel size—Figure 3B). In particular, cysteine (m/z 439.1765, −0.2 ppm) (Figure 4A, middle), which was observed to localise to interbrain and cerebellar regions at 60 µm pixel size (Figure 3B), could be distinguished as acutely constricted to the lateral ventricle at 10 µm pixel size. The distinct localisation of cysteine (green, Figure 4B) is further evident when visualised overlayed with [M+TAC/OS]●+ of alkyl amines spermine and spermidine (Figure 4B, right). Interestingly, spermidine (m/z 463.3146, −0.2 ppm) (blue, Figure 4B) appeared primarily localised to the corpus callosum and thalamus, while spermine (m/z 520.3721, −1.9 ppm) (red, Figure 4B) inversely appeared accumulated in the cerebral cortex, as well as the stratum oriens and pyramidal layer of the hippocampus. The distinct anatomical compartmentalisation of these derivatised features, combined with the sharp edge of the ion images across different tissue regions, indicates the derivatisation protocol is suitable for MSI at pixel sizes at least as low as 10 µm. This is further evidenced as very little signal was observed for all derivatised features within off‐tissue regions, which is particularly notable in the lateral ventricle, where cysteine is distinctly confined to the ependymal cells and choroid plexus and absent within the CSF space. In the mid‐hindbrain dataset (Figure 4C), glycine (m/z 393.1894, 0.0 ppm) (Figure 4C, middle) was strongly present in the pons and medulla of the brainstem, matching the localisation of glyceric receptors 1 to 3 [60, 61]. Additionally, the amino acid alanine (m/z 407.2049, −0.5 ppm) (Figure 4C, right), demonstrated some co‐localisation with GABA to the hypothalamus. This is expected, as like GABA, alanine has multiple roles in gabaminergic neurons [62]. Overall, this data demonstrates the suitability of TAC/OS for MSI of amino metabolites at pixel sizes as low as 10 µm.

Having demonstrated the applicability of TAC/OS for MSI in brain tissues, we next applied our protocol to the investigation of mouse kidney. MALDI‐2 acquisition of a murine kidney section (50 µm × 50 µm pixel size) subjected to TAC/OS derivatisation revealed 52 peaks which were indicated as plausible TAC/OS‐derivatised features (Figure S10). Of particular note, in comparison to the derivatised brain dataset, was the increased detection of thiol‐containing metabolites, including the [M+TAC/OS]●+ of methionine (m/z 467.2084, 0.0 ppm), alongside cysteamine (m/z 395.1874, 0.3 ppm), and m/z tentatively assigned as ethanethioic acid (m/z 394.1560, 0.8 ppm). Several metabolites involved in the renal metabolic recycling of glutathione were also observed. Cysteine, alongside derivatised γ‐glutamylcysteine (m/z 568.2194, −0.7 ppm), appeared localised to the renal cortex. Inversely, cystinylglycine appeared localised to the medulla and inner cortex, while glutathione appeared predominantly accumulated within the renal pelvis and outer cortex, matching previous reports by Smith et al. [63].

Application of TAC/OS to Spatial Metabolomics of Cancer Tissue

Encouraged by the applicability of TAC/OS for high‐resolution MSI, we applied TAC/OS to investigate metabolic distribution within tissue sections acquired from a late‐stage tumour explant, prepared as previously reported by Hosen et al. [64]. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive cancer type accounting for close to 90% of pancreatic neoplasms [65]. In particular, changes in amino acid profiles have been noted as strongly characteristic of pancreatic tumour occurrence and malignancy grade, with significant alterations in expression observed during PDAC progression [66]. Subjecting TAC/OS treated PDAC tumour sections to MALDI‐2 MSI (20 µm pixel size) allowed for the detection of 89 peaks suspected as derivatised, including 52 peaks not observed in brain or kidney datasets. Of particular note were the detection of derivatised lysine (m/z 464.2621, −1.7 ppm) and tryptophan (m/z 522.2463, −1.9 ppm), alongside m/z matching the exact mass of derivatised ornithine (m/z 450.2574, 0.4 ppm) and proline (m/z 433.2206, −0.2 ppm).

Despite significant heterogeneity and complexity associated with the study of a late‐stage tumour specimen, analysis of the post‐MSI microscopy of histologically stained tissue (Figure 5A) allowed for the identification of several anatomical regions of relevance, including the presence of a desmoplastic stromal border (SB), as well as viable (VT) and necrotic (NT) tumour regions. Several representative ion images that demonstrate distinct spatial distributions are shown in Figure 5B (See Figure S11 for a more comprehensive list of ion images). Many derivatised features appeared localised in agreement with identified anatomical zones; for example, spermine (Figure 5B, upper left) appeared predominantly confined to regions containing viable tumour tissue. This is further demonstrated by the heatmap shown in Figure 5C, where the differential localisation of selected derivatised metabolites [M+TAC/OS]●+ is displayed as the standard deviation from the RMS normalised global mean across the microscopy‐defined tissue regions shown in Figure 5A, acquired from two consecutive tissue sections (n = 2). Interestingly, only a handful of derivatised features appeared elevated within non‐necrotic regions. In particular, small peptides glutathione and cystinylglycine (Figure 5B) appeared primarily confined to regions of viable tumour (Figure 5C: VT—cyan component). Glutathione levels are often upregulated in progressing carcinomas as a key antioxidative protector for maintaining redox homeostasis [67, 68]. Derivatised polyamines spermidine and spermine (Figure 5B) also appeared elevated, with localisation extending to the surrounding desmoplastic stroma (Figure 5C: SB—pink component). The increased presence of the alkyl amines spermidine and spermine is consistent with the reported literature—polyamine catabolism is strongly promoted in stromal and tumour cells, leading to reduced levels within surrounding tissues [69, 70].

Further investigation to determine the relevance of these features was conducted through a region‐of‐interest (ROI)‐informed principal component analysis of the PDAC dataset. As shown by the score plot displayed in Figure 5D, selected ROIs visible by histology were able to be resolved by their differential molecular ion compositions. As shown by the loadings plot (Figure 5D), distributions of derivatised amino metabolites appeared to drive separation of selected ROIs. Interestingly, 65% of detected derivatised amino acids, including valine (Figure 5B), alongside methionine and serine, appeared distinctly elevated in regions subject to extensive necrosis, possibly associated with known processes of nutrient scavenging. To sustain metabolic demand, PDAC has been shown to induce cell death and protein catabolism in surrounding tissues to promote the release of crucial amino acids for subsequent tumour uptake [71, 72, 73]. The above findings suggest distinct alterations in amino acids and amine metabolites between histologically relevant regions of PDAC in line with the available literature, indicating TAC/OS as highly applicable for the study and identification of potential biomarkers within diseased tissue models.

Conclusion

This work describes the development of a novel OTCD approach enabling MSI of amine‐containing metabolites throughout biological tissues. Unlike previous OTCD approaches that rely on a fixed positive charge, here we have exploited the ability of MALDI‐2 to induce direct REMPI of suitable chromophores to generate radical cations. In this proof‐of‐concept work, we have designed, developed and investigated the applicability of the novel TAC/OS reagent that contains an α‐tocopherol‐derived chromophore as the photoionisable group and an O‐succinimidyl carbamate as the reactive group to derivatise amine‐containing metabolites. As a custom MALDI‐2 specific reagent, TAC/OS has been shown to be selective for MALDI‐2 induced ionisation processes and allow for the MSI of amino acids, small peptides, and neurotransmitters down to 10 µm pixel size. Overall, across brain, kidney, and pancreatic tumour tissues, 152 unique peaks were identified as TAC/OS derivatised, of which 36 metabolites could be assigned.

Alongside the ability to detect MALDI‐2‐specific native (i.e., lipids) and derivatised analytes in tandem, the method offers several other broader advantages. Firstly, as the desorption and ionisation of derivatised analytes are decoupled, each process can be optimised independently of the other. For example, finding the optimal laser energy for desorption without compromising ionisation. This is in contrast with the fixed‐charge reagents widely used for MALDI, where desorption and ionisation are intimately coupled and generated via the same laser pulse, and compromises often need to be found between soft and efficient desorption and ionisation. Second, as the method does not require fixed‐charge reagents to enhance ionisation, more potential and practical synthetic routes are now accessible. Fixed charge‐containing molecules can often face synthetic challenges due to reagent incompatibility, unwanted side reactions and low solubility in organic solvents during their preparation [74]. This can extend the repertoire of OTCD approaches to study the spatial distribution of an increasing array of analytes that are not amenable to conventional MSI analysis. Future work will explore the use of alternative reactive groups to target different functional groups, as well as modifications of the core chromophore to explore how efficiency for REMPI can be improved for increased sensitivity.
