Designer bright and fast CsPbBr3 perovskite nanocrystal scintillators for high-speed X-ray imaging
Authors: Zhi Yang, Jisong Yao, Leimeng Xu, Wenxuan Fan, Jizhong Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bright and fast scintillators are highly crucial for high-speed X-ray imaging in the medical diagnostic radiology including angiography and cardiac computed tomography. The CsPbBr3 nanocrystal scintillator featuring a nanosecond radioluminescence decay time is a promising candidate. However, it suffers from a substantial photon self-absorption limiting the light output, and being bright and fast simultaneously is difficult. Here we design and in-situ synthesize multi-site ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 heterostructures to modulate the bright and fast features of scintillators. We find external energy from ZnS(Ag) can effectively transfer to CsPbBr3 based on the non-radiative Förster resonance energy transfer, resulting in a light yield of 40,000 photons MeV−1. By combing a radioluminescence decay time of 36 ns and a spatial resolution of 30 lp mm−1, the scintillator enables high-speed X-ray imaging at 200 frames per second. This study showcases the structure design is significant for obtaining bright and fast perovskite scintillators for the real-time X-ray imaging.

Introduction

Recently, metal halide perovskites have revived worldwide interest as next-generation scintillators due to their high atomic number (Z), efficient luminescence, tunable luminescent color and lifetime, high radiation hardness and low-temperature synthesis. Since the first report of CsPbX3 (X = Cl, Br, I) nanocrystal (NC) scintillators in 2018, significant progresses have been made in the development of halide perovskites including 3D, 2D, 1D and 0D1–10. Compared with traditional semiconductor (CdSe, InP and PbS) NCs, CsPbBr3 NCs exhibit excellent carrier dynamical characteristics at the several nanosecond levels (~20 times faster)11. The unique nanosecond radioluminescence (RL) of CsPbBr3 NCs is highly attractive for the ultrafast X-ray imaging in the medical diagnostic radiology such as computed tomography (CT), time of flight positron emission tomography (TOF-PET), and TOF X-ray12–15. For example, the high-speed angiography (HSA), one kind of CT, can obtain the blood-flow pattern and velocity information used for the diagnosis of certain pathologies such as tumors and vessel blockage16.

Ultrafast X-ray imaging requires scintillators not only to have a short RL decay time, but also to have high light yield and light output determining the brightness threshold at a low X-ray dose in the high-speed imaging mode. Unfortunately, CsPbBr3 NCs suffer from an inevitable photon self-absorption problem due to a small Stokes shift, and the currently reported light yield17 is limited to 21,000 photons MeV−1, which is far below than that of commercial CsI (Tl) (54,000 photons MeV−1). To improve the light yield of CsPbBr3 NC scintillators, charge transfer (guest/host structures, such as CsPbBr3@Cs4PbBr6 and CsPbBr3@CsPb2Br5) and energy transfer (dopants, such as Eu3+ and Mn2+) strategies have been developed6,18–22. However, the light yield has not been improved to a desirable level, because the increased light emission is still not enough to compensate for the self-absorption light loss.

In contrast, introducing an external energy source is another strategy to brighten CsPbBr3 NC scintillator, because CsPbBr3 NCs can be further excited by a bright external source. For example, UV or blue emitters such as CsI, ZnO(Ga), 2,5-diphenyloxazole, and thermally activated delayed fluorescence molecules have been reported to increase the light output of CsPbBr3 NCs23–27. Considering that bright donors are usually inorganic microcrystals (MCs) with the low solution dispersibility, physical mixing method is commonly used25. Unfortunately, the enhancement of light yield is still inadequate due to the limited energy transfer efficiency in the hybrid scintillators. Based on the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) theory, a short donor-acceptor distance (typically of the order of 5 nm) is usually required for an efficient FRET28,29. However, it is still a great challenge to achieve such a short distance because the physical mixture of donor and acceptor in the solid state produces the significant NC aggregation, meaning that it is highly difficult to disperse the CsPbBr3 NC acceptors on the donor surface30,31. Thus, how to design and synthesize perovskite nanostructures with an efficient FRET is highly feasible for constructing bright and fast CsPbBr3 NC scintillators.

In this work, we design a bright and fast ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 heterostructured scintillator for the application of the high-speed X-ray imaging. Multi-site ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 heterostructures are in-situ synthesized by a solid-phase method, enabling the uniform growth of CsPbBr3 NCs on the surface of ZnS(Ag). In-situ synthesized heterostructure ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 (IH-type CsPbBr3) can produce a higher energy transfer efficiency of 95% due to a shorter donor-acceptor distance, as compared with that of 63% for physically mixed ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 (PM-type CsPbBr3). The built-in FRET is revealed by steady-state RL spectra, transient absorption (TA) and time resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), which is conductive for simultaneously achieving bright RL emission and fast RL decay. Benefiting from an efficient FRET process, the IH-type CsPbBr3 scintillator presents a light yield of 40,000 photons MeV−1, which is much higher than that of 30,000 photons MeV−1 for PM-type CsPbBr3 scintillators. Meanwhile, the heterostructured scintillator presents a short RL decay time of 36 ns and a high spatial resolution of 30 lp mm−1. These traits enable the heterostructured scintillator displaying an excellent dynamic X-ray imaging capability, as evidenced by a clear solution flow with a linear velocity of 11 cm s−1 recorded at 200 frames per second (fps). The as-obtained scintillator presents advanced performances in terms of RL brightness, response speed and spatial resolution, suggesting that the structural configuration is crucial for designing high-performance hybrid scintillators.

Results and discussion

Design of the heterostructured scintillator

Figure 1 presents the design concept for an IH-type CsPbBr3 scintillator. As well-known, ZnS(Ag) microcrystal (MC) scintillator has a high light yield of 50,000 photons MeV−1, a moderate RL decay time of 200 ns and a wide-band 450 nm blue emission32,33. ZnS(Ag) MCs are considered as an ideal external energy for boosting the emission of CsPbBr3 NCs, so a ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 scintillator is designed to improve the light yield of CsPbBr3 NCs without scarifying the fast response speed. We further used fluorescence microscopy and steady-state PL and RL spectra to demonstrate the design concept of energy transfer process in the IH-type and PM-type scintillators. We found a strong green emission and no blue emission for the IH-type CsPbBr3 (Fig. 1d, fluorescence microscopy images), and the achieved high FRET efficiency is a result of a short donor-acceptor distance and multiple donor-acceptor sites (almost all of ZnS(Ag) MCs are covered by CsPbBr3 NCs). In contrast, we can observe a relatively weaker green emission and a much stronger blue emission for the PM-type CsPbBr3 (Fig. 1e, fluorescence microscopy images). The weak CsPbBr3 emission indicates a low FRET efficiency due to a relatively long donor-acceptor distance. The obvious ZnS(Ag) emission results from the insufficient coverage of ZnS(Ag) MCs due to the CsPbBr3 NC aggregates. This comparison is further quantitatively studied by the steady-state PL spectra (Fig. 1d, e). The IH-type CsPbBr3 exhibits a strong PL peak at 530 nm, which is about two times higher than that of the PM-type CsPbBr3. Meanwhile, the IH-type CsPbBr3 has a much weaker blue emission of ZnS(Ag) than the PM-type CsPbBr3, further confirming a highly efficient energy transfer in the IH-type CsPbBr3. Moreover, RL spectra presents the consistent tendency with PL spectra, suggesting the similar non-radiative FRET process (Supplementary Fig. 4) under X-ray and UV excitations. Thus, combining the efficient energy transfer and the intrinsic fast time response of CsPbBr3 NCs, the IH-type scintillator is expected to realize bright and fast features simultaneously.

Synthesis of heterostructure powders

The ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 heterostructure was in-situ synthesized via a solid-phase method, as shown in Fig. 2a. Under an ultrafast grinding speed of >15,000 rpm, ZnS(Ag) and Cs, Pb and Br precursors are mixed rapidly and uniformly with the assistance of collision, friction and shear forces34,35. With increasing grinding time, CsPbBr3 NCs are grown on the surface of ZnS(Ag) MCs with decreased sizes (Supplementary Fig. 5). The IH-type CsPbBr3 was characterized by high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF), elemental mapping and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). As seen from the TEM image in Fig. 2c, the ZnS(Ag) crystal is covered by a large number of non-aggregated CsPbBr3 NCs to create the multi-site heterostructure. The distinct lattice plane fringes in the HRTEM image (Fig. 2d) are assigned to the (110) crystal plane of CsPbBr3 and the (111) crystal plane of ZnS(Ag), unambiguously confirming the formation of heterostructure. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Fig. 2e exhibit the monoclinic CsPbBr3 phase (PDF#18-0346) and sphalerite ZnS phase (PDF#65-1691). In addition, a small amount of CsPb2Br5 (PDF#54-0753) appears due to the excessive addition of PbBr2. Scintillation performances

In order to fabricate free-standing screen and bulk scintillators, we introduced polyethylene polymer as a matrix to disperse and bond the inorganic powders. Microstructure analyses of hybrid powders (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10) reconfirm the uniform coverage of CsPbBr3 NCs on the ZnS(Ag) MCs in the IH-type CsPbBr3 as characterized in the TEM analysis. Through the hot-pressing technique (Supplementary Fig. 11), a flexible screen with a large-area of 14 × 14 cm2 and a thickness of 0.2 mm was fabricated, as shown in Fig. 3a. This free-standing screen exhibits a homogeneous configuration at the micrometer scale, as characterized by the optical microscopic image (Fig. 3b) and SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 12), which is favorable for high-resolution X-ray imaging. Notably, ZnS(Ag)-CsPbBr3 heterostructured particles can be uniformly dispersed in the polymer matrices, because the surface-rich polymer provides the sufficient reaction environment34. Moreover, a series of bulk scintillators were also successfully fabricated via a commercial 3D printing technique (Fig. 3c), further demonstrating the benign processability of solid-phase synthesized perovskite NCs. After fabrication, the scintillation performance was optimized to achieve a tradeoff between RL intensity and screen uniformity (Supplementary Notes 3, 4, and Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). The slight changes in RL intensities of different scintillators further demonstrate the repeatability of the all-solid-phase fabrication technique (Supplementary Fig. 15). By comparing with BGO, the IH-type CsPbBr3 reaches a light yield of 40,000 photons MeV−1, which is 1.3-fold higher than that of 30,000 photons MeV−1 of the PM-type CsPbBr3 (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 16). This higher light yield demonstrates that the multi-site heterostructure has a higher energy transfer efficiency than the physically mixed hybrid. Moreover, the IH-type was observed to have a higher spatial resolution than the PM-type (Fig. 3g), which was confirmed by the less fluctuating peaks and valleys of gray profiles across the five lines (Supplementary Fig. 21c). The slanted-edge method also indicates the IH-type has a higher spatial resolution of 30 lp mm−1 than the PM-type with that of 14 lp mm−1 (Fig. 3h), approaching the highest resolution of non-array thin film scintillators42,43. Förster resonance energy transfer mechanism

To probe the mechanism of improved energy transfer efficiency and light yield in the IH-type scintillator, steady-state and ultrafast time-resolved dynamic experiments were conducted to demonstrate the FRET process, as shown in Fig. 4. With increasing fraction of CsPbBr3, a progressive dropped RL intensity of ZnS (Ag) and a growing RL intensity of CsPbBr3 are observed in the IH-type CsPbBr3 (Fig. 4a). Relatively, the PM-type CsPbBr3 exhibits a smaller decrease of ZnS (Ag) and a smaller increase of CsPbBr3 (Fig. 4b). These RL spectral evolution confirms the energy transfer from ZnS (Ag) to CsPbBr3. The energy transfer efficiency was calculated from the quenching of ZnS (Ag) as shown in Fig. 4c. We find the IH-type CsPbBr3 has a higher energy transfer efficiency of 95% compared with that of 63% for the PM-type CsPbBr3. A higher energy transfer efficiency in the IH-type CsPbBr3 is attributed to a shorter donor-acceptor distance of about 2.6 nm compared with that of 4.2 nm for PM-type CsPbBr3 (Supplementary Note 1, comparable to that of CsPbBr3-dye pairs44,45) according to the FRET theory44–47. Moreover, TA spectroscopy (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Fig. 22) was performed to investigate the difference in the energy transfer dynamics of IH-type and PM-type CsPbBr3. Two distinct ground-state bleach peaks at 450 and 520 nm are observed, which are assigned to ZnS(Ag) and CsPbBr3, respectively. As compared with PM-type CsPbBr3, the absorption signal of ZnS(Ag) in the IH-type CsPbBr3 is weakened, but the absorption signal of CsPbBr3 is markedly enhanced, indicating a more efficient energy transfer from the ZnS(Ag) donor to the CsPbBr3 acceptor. Timescale analysis further indicates that ZnS(Ag) is first excited and then transferred to CsPbBr3. Importantly, for the same ZnS(Ag) quenching ratio of 55%, the IH-type has a shorter delay time of 10 ps than that of 50 ps for the PM-type, indicating a faster FRET process. Moreover, the IH-type has a longer PL lifetime of the CsPbBr3 acceptor (Fig. 4f), further demonstrating a higher energy transfer efficiency from the aspect of the acceptor. It should be noted that the PL lifetime tracking of the donor is difficult in experiments because the afterglow of ZnS(Ag) can affect the accuracy of PL lifetime32. The combination of steady-state RL spectral evolution and ultrafast time-resolved TA and PL decay can confirm the efficient FRET process in the IH-type scintillator.

High-speed X-ray imaging

Figure 5a presents the static X-ray imaging of the IH-type and PM-type scintillators. The IH-type can clearly resolve a bright copper mesh with a linewidth of 0.02 mm, while the PM-type produces a blurry and dark image. In particular, we can find a damaged hole at the outside copper ring from the IH-type scintillator, but the PM-type cannot provide any details. The bright and high-resolution features of the IH-type scintillator can be further demonstrated by the additional images of a micro SD card in Supplementary Fig. 23. Figure 5b shows that the IH-type can also resolve dynamic details. Then, a dynamic X-ray imaging system including a liquid circulator and a dynamic imaging apparatus was designed to simulate the medical HSA X-ray imaging (Fig. 5c). In Fig. 5d, a clear position of the iohexol solution edge can be distinguished when the X-ray imaging was acquired at the 200 fps. To the best of our knowledge, this is the fastest dynamic X-ray imaging using perovskite scintillators until now (Supplementary Table 3), which is enough to meet the requirements of HSA X-ray imaging for visualizing blood-flow patterns and calculating flow velocity48,49. Brightness gap between static and dynamic imaging

The light yield plays a vital role in determining the brightness of X-ray imaging, especially in the dynamic imaging. In Fig. 6a, we describe the relationship among light yield, brightness and X-ray imaging mode. The static X-ray imaging can be collected with a long exposure time, and a low dose rate can still guarantee the sufficient exposure dose. In contrast, the dynamic X-ray imaging in a high-speed mode has a much shorter exposure time, and a much higher dose rate is necessary to meet the requirement of essential dose exposure. Thus, scintillators working in the dynamic mode have to tackle the difficulty that how to reach a sufficient brightness when each frame is collected under a low X-ray dose. We evaluated the brightness of two scintillators under different dose rates (Supplementary Fig. 24). Taking 5% of relative brightness as a threshold, Fig. 6b shows that the IH-type requires a much lower dose rate of 0.03 mGyair s−1 than that of 0.1 mGyair s−1 for the PM-type operating at the static X-ray imaging. Relatively, the dynamic imaging requires a much higher dose rate than the static imaging. A high dose rate of 10 mGyair s−1 is required for the IH-type (40,000 photons MeV−1), but the PM-type (30,000 photons MeV−1) requires a much higher dose rate of 30 mGyair s−1. In summary, we have designed an IH-type CsPbBr3 scintillator to manipulate the external energy focusing in CsPbBr3 NCs through the efficient energy transfer from ZnS(Ag). IH-type CsPbBr3 scintillators were fabricated by an all-solid-phase technique combining in-situ synthesis and thermoforming, and various shaped scintillators could meet the requirements of high-energy ray imaging and detection. Benefiting from an efficient FRET, the IH-type CsPbBr3 achieved a high light yield of 40,000 photons MeV−1, and FRET-induced emission increase can compensate for the self-absorption light loss of the thick-film CsPbBr3 scintillator. Meanwhile, the IH-type scintillator achieved a high spatial resolution of 30 lp mm−1, because the improved light yield and brightness can produce less noise dots, and the homogenous dispersion of less-agglomerate emitters in the polymer matrix can decrease the light scattering. Importantly, the IH-type CsPbBr3 scintillator simultaneously exhibited a high brightness, a flash response, and a high spatial resolution, enabling the high-speed X-ray imaging applications. We envision that these findings will provide a perspective for designing and fabricating high-performance scintillators for the real-time X-ray imaging in the practical medical diagnostic radiology.

Methods

Chemicals

Cesium stearate (≥98%) and lead stearate (98%) were purchased from D&B. Pyridinium tribromide (90%) was purchased from Macklin. ZnS(Ag) powder was purchased from Shanghai Keyan Phosphor Technology Co. Polyethylene was purchased from Dongguan Huachuang Plastic Raw Material Firm. All materials were used as received without any further purification.

Solid-phase synthesis of perovskite hybrid powders

CsPbBr3 NC-polyethylene hybrid powders were synthesized according to our developed solid-phase method34. In a typical synthesis of the IH-type CsPbBr3, 5 g of commercial ZnS(Ag) powder, 2.4 g of cesium stearate, 7.6 g of lead stearate and 80 g polyethylene were firstly ground for 2 min in an automatic grinder machine. Then, 7.4 g of pyridinium tribromide was added, and the mixture was continually ground for 3 min. In contrast, the PM-type CsPbBr3 was prepared by directly physical mixing commercial ZnS(Ag) powder and solid-phase synthesized CsPbBr3 NC powder.

Scintillator fabrication

A free-standing scintillator screen was fabricated via a hot-pressing technique. Perovskite-polyethylene powder was loaded in a smooth steel mold (Ra = 50 nm) under a pressure of 10 MPa at 120 °C for 10 min. After cooling, the polymer composite film was removed from the mold. Finally, a smooth flexible film with the thickness of 0.2 mm was obtained after a slight polishing. Bulk scintillators were fabricated via a fused deposition modelling (FDM) 3D printing technique. Perovskite-polyethylene powder was firstly extruded to prepare a standard filament with a diameter of 1.75 mm, and then a filament was inserted into a commercial 3D printing machine.

Characterization

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) was conducted on a JEM-F200 instrument with an Ultim Max 80 detector (element mapping). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM), high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF), elemental mapping and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) were performed. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) was performed on a ZEISS Gemini 300. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was taken on an ESCALAB 250xi instrument equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV). X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out with a Bruker D8 Advance using Cu Kα radiation. The optical absorption spectra were collected with a Hitachi UV-Visible spectrophotometer UH5700. The PL spectra and time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra were acquired with a FLS980 spectrometer. Fluorescence microscopy images were collected on a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X. Due to the difficulty of observing X-ray-excited luminescence under an optical microscope, a 365 nm UV light excitation was used due to the similar spectral shapes for the PL and RL spectra in Fig. 1d, e. TA spectra were measured by an ultrafast TA spectrometer (HARPIA, Light Conversion), and samples were excited by a fs-pulse laser at 365 nm.

Radioluminescence measurement

The RL measurement was conducted in an optical system designed by the Dowell photonics. The X-ray source was an Amptek Mini-X2 instrument operated at various voltages (20-50 kV) and currents (10–200 μA). X-ray dose rate was measured with an XR detector (MagicMaX Universal). The X-ray excited steady-state RL spectra were recorded by a fiber optical spectrophotometer (B&W TEK Exemplar Plus LS). The relationship between the RL intensity and the X-ray dose rate was obtained by adjusting the dose rate ranging from 70 nGyair s−1 to 24 mGyair s−1. The detection limit was derived from the slope of the fitting line. The RL decay was collected by a photomultiplier tube connected to an oscilloscope, and scintillators were excited by a pulsed X-ray generator (Golden, XRS-4, pulse width: 15 ns).

Static X-ray imaging measurements

An industrial X-ray source with a large emission window area of 20 cm2 was used in X-ray imaging, and dose rate was adjusted by changing voltage (60-100 kV) and current (0.1-1.2 mA). Static X-ray imaging was captured by reflecting scintillator images to a CMOS camera (Tucsen FL-20) equipped with different lenses. For the X-ray excited scintillators with no magnified images (Figs. 3c, and 4a, b), a prime lens (AZURE-5022ML12M) with a fixed focal length of 50 mm was used. The dose rate was 0.1 mGyair s−1 and the exposure time was 1 s. For the X-ray excited scintillators with magnified images (Figs. 3g, h, and 5a), a high-resolution continuous zoom lens (Coosway, CSW-0650) with a magnification factor of 3X was used. The dose rate was 0.4 mGyair s−1 and the exposure time was 1 s. The spatial resolution of scintillators was evaluated by a standard line-pair card (0.03 mm Pb, CN89727), which was further confirmed by the modulation transfer function (MTF) based on the slanted-edge method. The gray values of X-ray imaging photographs were analyzed by ImageJ software.

Dynamic X-ray imaging measurements

Dynamic X-ray imaging was recorded by an industrial CMOS camera (Mindvision, frame rate, 1594 fps), equipped with a prime lens (AZURE-5022ML12M). The dose rate was 10 mGyair s−1 and the exposure time was 5 ms. To simulate the HSA scene, a series of real conditions including vascular diameter, blood velocity flow, X-ray photon energy, X-ray exposure time, dose rate and iodine contrast agent were considered to design a dynamic X-ray imaging system. The speed of blood flow in the human inferior vena cava with an inside diameter of 20 mm is ~ 10–45 cm s−1. It was simulated with an iohexol water solution (300 mgI mL−1) in a soft tube with an inside diameter of 3 mm, which was driven by a microemulsion circulating pump to achieve a linear velocity of 11 cm s−1. The flow velocity could be calculated by measuring the movement distance of the solution edge within a short time interval.

The relative brightness of perovskite scintillators was acquired by integrating RL spectra compared with a commercial 0.3 mm-thick ZnS(Ag) powder screen (Shanghai Keyan Phosphor Technology Co.), which was fabricated by blade-coating ZnS(Ag)/glue (50 wt.%) on a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrate.
