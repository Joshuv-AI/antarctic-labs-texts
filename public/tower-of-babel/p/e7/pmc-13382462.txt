Home-based telework and worker health and well-being across service occupations: A critical interpretive synthesis
Authors: Juyeon Lee, Yasmeen Almomani, Abbey Davis, Michaella Miller, Peining Li, Daniel Cockayne, Stephanie Premji, Ellen MacEachen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of home-based telework since COVID-19 has diversified the remote workforce to include service occupations centered on real-time interaction with patients, clients, and customers. Yet the literature on telework and health remains largely oriented toward the experiences of knowledge workers, obscuring how home-based telework shapes the health and well-being of interaction-intensive service workers. We conducted a Critical Interpretive Synthesis of 69 peer-reviewed studies (2000 to 2025) to develop a theoretical account of how home-based telework is experienced across service occupations. The evidence base was profoundly skewed: 64 studies examined healthcare and social work professionals, while only three focused on scripted, routinized, and monitored service roles such as call and contact center work. The synthesis identified two divergent narratives, each reflecting a distinct labor process through which home-based telework shapes worker health and well-being. For healthcare and social work professionals, home-based telework eroded the institutional boundaries that had contained clinical distress within the workplace, transforming the home into a “contaminated sanctuary” as the emotional and psychological demands of care work increasingly spilled into private life. For call and contact center workers, while home-based telework initially appeared to reduce direct physical surveillance, control was reconstituted through digital technologies, producing a “digital panopticon” marked by algorithmic monitoring, enforced immobility, and the collapse of work-home boundaries. These findings suggest that the health consequences of home-based telework are shaped as much by the organization and demands of the work being performed as by the domestic setting.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of telework, precipitated by the COVID-19 pandemic, has significantly transformed the demographic composition of the remote workforce (Eurofound, 2020; ILO, 2020; López-Igual & Rodríguez-Modroño, 2020). Historically, the most visible teleworkers were predominantly highly educated, privileged professionals in creative or knowledge-intensive sectors. However, recent evidence reveals a substantial shift as telework expands to include clerical support staff and individuals in precarious, lower-paid roles previously anchored to fixed office desks (López-Igual & Rodríguez-Modroño, 2020). This transition is supported by the development of a contemporary virtual office enabled by mobile information and communication technologies (ICTs) that allows work to be performed anywhere and anytime (Messenger, 2019).

As telework establishes itself as the new normal for an increasingly diverse workforce, investigating the health and well-being outcomes of telework has become a critical subject of inquiry. Crucially, as Boell et al. (2016) argue, telework outcomes are not universally positive or negative; instead, they are fundamentally determined by the nature of work activities. Consequently, the literature often characterizes these outcomes through inherent paradoxes rather than linear effects (Allen et al., 2015; Bailey & Kurland, 2002; Boell et al., 2016). For instance, studies highlight a work-life balance paradox, where improved work-life coordination is undermined by an intensified pressure to remain constantly engaged with work demands, and a productivity paradox, where the elimination of physical office distractions is countered by a surge in ICT-mediated interruptions (Leonardi et al., 2010; Thulin & Vilhelmson, 2022; Wray, 2024). Similarly, a social isolation paradox persists, in which gains in autonomy and job satisfaction are frequently negated by the psychological costs of professional and social disconnection (Boell et al., 2016).

However, most telework discussions have focused almost exclusively on knowledge workers who enjoy high levels of autonomy and control over their workflows (Allen et al., 2015; Bailey & Kurland, 2002; Boell et al., 2016). Even when routine service roles are examined—most notably in the Bloom et al. (2015) large-scale randomized controlled trial of call center employees—the primary analytical lens has prioritized organizational metrics, such as productivity and retention, over the complex lived experience of the worker. Given the recent demographic expansion of the remote workforce, it is now necessary to move beyond this traditional focus to comprehensively understand the experiences of service workers as the emerging cohort of teleworkers. Because service-oriented roles involve real-time interaction with service recipients, they entail a labor process structurally distinct from that of knowledge-intensive roles, with corresponding implications for worker health and well-being. Furthermore, these implications may vary internally within the service workforce, shaped by the emotional intensity of the interaction, the worker's degree of professional discretion, and the level of digital monitoring. Analyzing these nuances is therefore crucial for a comprehensive and stratified understanding of the evolving relationship between telework and worker health and well-being.

This review focuses specifically on home-based telework as a distinct arrangement within the broader landscape of remote labor. This focus is driven by an inquiry into how the domestic environment is structurally reconfigured for productivity, often at the direct expense of the mental and spatial boundaries between work and personal life. Crucially, the migration of low-autonomy service roles into the home is facilitated by advanced digital surveillance technology that provides management with “prodigious powers of measurement, monitoring, and control” (Head, 2003, p. 69) effectively easing traditional organizational concerns regarding trust and physical oversight (Brown et al., 2010). Moreover, home-based telework differs from other telework arrangements in the composition of its workforce. Whereas occasional and highly mobile teleworkers remain predominantly male and skewed toward managers, professionals, and technicians, home-based telework departs from these patterns: clerical support workers form a substantial share of this group, male predominance no longer holds, and correlations with permanent contracts and higher pay are weaker (López-Igual & Rodríguez-Modroño, 2020). This suggests that home-based telework has brought into its fold occupational groups, particularly clerical and customer-service workers, whose experiences of telework have received comparatively little attention in the existing literature. By isolating home-based telework, this review can examine the unique health and well-being outcomes that arise when client-facing service workers perform their labor within the domestic sphere.

In light of this complex backdrop of digital surveillance and home-based labor, a new synthesis of the literature is urgently needed. A recent comprehensive systematic review by Lunde et al. (2022), focusing specifically on employed office workers teleworking from home, concluded that high-quality evidence regarding telework and health remains scarce. However, their review was restricted to quantitative studies, and with a search cut-off of February 2021, it excluded the surge of peer-reviewed literature analyzing the pandemic telework boom, much of which was published subsequently, as well as recent studies regarding the stabilization of the new normal.

To address these limitations, this study moves beyond the traditional focus on professional knowledge workers to center on the workforce most responsible for this recent expansion: home-based labor where the primary job responsibility involves direct, real-time online interaction with service recipients, including customers, patients, or clients. This workforce encompasses workers across multiple service sectors, including healthcare, social work, and call/contact centre roles, with the labor process differing across these contexts in its emotional demands, the discretion afforded to workers, and the intensity of digital oversight. Accordingly, our review is guided by the following research question: How does home-based telework shape the health and well-being of this interaction-intensive service workforce?

To answer this question, we employ a Critical Interpretive Synthesis (CIS) design (Dixon-Woods, 2006; Dixon-Woods et al., 2006). Unlike traditional aggregative reviews, which often treat teleworkers as a homogeneous group to test specific hypotheses, CIS aims to produce a theoretical account that integrates diverse qualitative and quantitative findings. This approach is uniquely suited to capturing the profound heterogeneity of interaction-intensive service labor, specifically the nuances that are often flattened or overlooked by standard quantitative metrics, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of the evolving relationship between telework and worker health and well-being.

Method

Study design

Originally developed by Dixon-Woods et al. (2006), CIS has become a popular method for addressing complex health policy questions (Perlman et al., 2026). It is explicitly designed to produce a middle-range theoretical account, situated between grand theory and purely descriptive synthesis, by integrating diverse forms of evidence. We selected CIS over conventional systematic review methods because the latter are primarily aggregative, requiring pre-specified concepts to test hypotheses (e.g., measuring the impact of defined interventions on fixed health outcomes). In contrast, our objective is interpretive, enabling the conceptual framework and its categories to be developed and refined iteratively throughout the synthesis process. Consequently, CIS prioritizes developing a synthesizing argument—a new conceptual structure that integrates diverse findings—while taking a critical stance toward the existing literature. This approach is well-suited to fields with large, amorphous, and complex literatures because it allows for the questioning of taken-for-granted assumptions and the ways in which problematics have been constructed (Dixon-Woods, 2006; Dixon-Woods et al., 2006).

Iterative scope development

In line with CIS principles, our research question served as a compass rather than a fixed anchor (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006), allowing the scope of the review to evolve through iterative engagement with the literature. Our inquiry began with the health and well-being of client-facing service workers who perform real-time interactive work from home. Because this arrangement had expanded rapidly among call center and other customer-service workers during the pandemic, we expected research on this segment of the workforce to be relatively well developed and to feature prominently in the review. Preliminary searches, however, revealed the opposite: research on home-based telework and worker health was overwhelmingly concentrated in healthcare and social work occupations, while studies of call center, contact center, and comparable customer-service work were scarce.

We retained both bodies of evidence and treated their imbalance as analytically meaningful in its own right. Because the two literatures describe forms of labor that are organized very differently, reading them alongside one another promised more insight than either could offer in isolation. Our inquiry was thereby specified, moving from a general interest in client-facing service work toward a more precise question: how home-based telework shapes worker health and well-being across an interaction-intensive service workforce, and, in particular, whether and how these experiences diverge according to the labor process that characterizes different occupations.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of four major electronic databases (Scopus, CINAHL, MEDLINE, and ABI/INFORM) on March 2, 2025. The search strategy employed a Boolean logic combining three conceptual blocks to capture the intersection of (a) work arrangement, (b) occupational sector, and (c) health and well-being outcomes. Specifically, the search combined terms for remote and hybrid work arrangements (e.g., “telework,” “home-based work,” “hybrid work,” “digital work”) with terms for service and care occupations, including both broad service sector terms (e.g., “service work,” “service sector,” “customer,” “client”) and specific occupational terms (e.g., “call center,” “social work,” “nurse,” “counsellor”) and terms for multidimensional health and well-being outcomes (e.g., “burnout,” “sedentary,” “musculoskeletal pain,” “work-life conflict”). To ensure the capture of all relevant literature, the reference lists of the included articles were manually searched to identify additional citations. We did not apply search filters for study design, allowing for the inclusion of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research (see Supplementary Material #1 for the complete search strings used in each database).

Study selection

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To ensure transparency, we established specific eligibility criteria based on the PCC framework (Population, Concept, Context), supplemented by specific study characteristics. We included all forms of peer-reviewed empirical research (qualitative, quantitative, mixed methods, and reviews) and relevant reviews published from January 1, 2000, to March 2, 2025, to capture the modern era of home-based service work.

Regarding the population, we focused strictly on client-facing home-based service workers, defined as individuals whose primary job responsibility involves direct, real-time online interaction with service recipients (e.g., customers, patients, or clients). This definition encompasses a broad spectrum of interactive service work, including roles in healthcare and social work (e.g., physicians, nurses, social workers, therapists, and crisis line staff) and customer-service and sales roles (e.g., call center agents, contact center operators, customer support agents). We excluded back-office administrative roles that lack direct client interaction, as well as educational roles where the client is a student (e.g., teachers), and non-standard employment, such as volunteer work or student jobs.

In terms of concept and context, the review was limited to home-based telework arrangements where the home serves as the primary worksite. We excluded digital nomad arrangements and remote work performed in satellite offices or co-working spaces because these lack the domestic boundary dynamics that are central to our inquiry. This distinction enables us to thoroughly examine the varied experiences that arise when the private domestic sphere is repurposed as a professional workplace. We selected studies that analyzed the direct consequences of home-based telework on worker health and well-being. We specifically excluded research that attributed these outcomes solely to the transient stressors of the COVID-19 pandemic, such as lockdown isolation or viral anxiety. This allowed us to capture multidimensional outcomes ranging from physical health (e.g., sedentary behavior, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain) to mental health (e.g., burnout, technostress, and isolation) and the work-life interface (e.g., work-family conflict and boundary management). Consequently, our review maintains a strict focus on the health and well-being of the worker; we excluded research focusing purely on organizational outcomes, such as productivity and turnover, or the mechanics of technology implementation, as these dimensions fall outside the scope of our inquiry.

Screening and sampling

We conducted the screening process using Covidence, a systematic review management software. Prior to formal screening, inter-rater reliability was assessed to ensure consistency in applying the eligibility criteria. A pilot screen yielded a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 75.5%, indicating substantial agreement (McHugh, 2012). Following this calibration, the review proceeded in two stages. First, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and classified them as “Yes,” “No,” or “Maybe.” Discrepancies were resolved through direct discussion to reach a consensus. Persistent disagreements were flagged and adjudicated collaboratively during research team meetings. Second, the full text of all “maybe” and “yes” entries was reviewed to determine final eligibility. For every excluded study, the reviewers documented the specific reason based on a hierarchical decision list: (1) not in English, (2) not an empirical study, (3) the study population did not meet the definition of client-facing service workers, (4) the work arrangement was not home-based telework, (5) no specific health outcome resulted from the work arrangement, or (6) the analysis lacked a focus on worker health and well-being. This process resulted in a final sample of 69 papers included in the review (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram of the literature search and study selection process.

Although CIS typically employs theoretical sampling, which is the purposive selection of a subset of papers to refine emerging conceptual categories (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006), this review adopted a comprehensive sampling strategy. Retaining the full body of identified literature was a deliberate methodological choice necessary to map the full range of occupational contexts represented in the evidence. This inclusive approach enabled granular sub-analyses of gender dynamics and distinct clinical contexts (e.g., psychiatric versus general medical practice) that a narrower theoretical sample might have obscured. Furthermore, this approach ensured that fragmented evidence on call center, contact center, and comparable customer-service work was utilized to interrogate the structural silence of this sector in existing literature rather than discarded.

Quality appraisal

In line with the critical and interpretive nature of CIS (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006), our quality appraisal prioritized the relevance of the findings to theory generation over strict methodological rigor. Accordingly, we did not employ a formal quality checklist to exclude studies, instead, all articles meeting the eligibility criteria were retained. With this approach, quality assessment was intrinsic to the synthesis process. Rather than using technical scores as a threshold for inclusion, we treated the literature as an object of scrutiny, questioning the assumptions and epistemological foundations of the evidence as part of the analysis and theory generation.

Data extraction and analysis

Consistent with the CIS methodology, data extraction and analysis were not treated as distinct, linear stages but as an iterative, recursive process of theory generation (Dixon-Woods, 2006; Dixon-Woods et al., 2006). We adopted a flexible approach that moved from a systematic management of the large dataset to a deep interpretive critique.

While CIS suggests that rigid data extraction can limit the interpretive scope of a review (Dixon-Woods et al., 2006), the large volume of literature in this review required an initial systematic organization. We utilized a standardized extraction template in Covidence to capture the descriptive characteristics of the included studies. Each included article underwent a primary extraction by one reviewer, followed by a verification step where a second independent reviewer cross-checked the entries. The extraction protocol captured four key domains: (1) study characteristics, including bibliographic details, research aims, study design, and population demographics; (2) occupational context, specifically the nature of the service work and the intensity of the remote arrangement; (3) well-being outcomes, categorized into physical and psychological health impacts; and (4) key findings, capturing qualitative and quantitative evidence regarding the risks, benefits, and general environmental conditions of home-based telework. This phase provided the evidentiary foundation for the subsequent synthesis.

The core analysis followed the CIS principles of constant comparison and critique, treating the literature not merely as a neutral source of data but as an object of scrutiny. Rather than taking the experiences reported in the dominant healthcare and social work literature at face value, we asked which features of these occupations’ labor process made such experiences possible. Reading those experiences as products of a particular way of organizing work, rather than as intrinsic to home-based telework itself, opened the possibility that differently organized labor processes, such as those characterizing call center, contact center, and comparable customer-service roles, might produce distinct health and well-being effects. This recursive, reflexive critique directly informed the generation of theory.

The analysis culminated in a synthesizing argument about how home-based telework shapes worker health and well-being, which we develop across the Results in two parts. The first draws on the large healthcare and social work literature, where workers appear to bear a psychological burden of autonomy (the struggle to self-regulate in a boundaryless space), with effects that vary by clinical context and social location. The second reads the few, ambivalent studies of call center, contact center, and comparable customer-service work against established accounts of these occupations’ labor process, surfacing a possibility that current research has left unexamined: that a physical burden of control (entrapment by rigid surveillance) produces a different profile of health and well-being effects.

The numerical disparity between these occupational cohorts, whereby 96% of the evidence is derived from healthcare and social work contexts, masks a deeper epistemological bias. Synthesizing this body of work, we find that home-based telework distress comes into view primarily as a psychological crisis of boundary management, in which the home is characterized as a “contaminated sanctuary” (Section 3.2.). The smaller body of evidence on call center and contact center work points to a more ambivalent picture, surfacing a concern that this dominant understanding has tended to leave unexamined: that home-based arrangements may intensify rather than relax managerial control over the labor process. We develop this in Section 3.3 as a “digital panopticon”: a workspace in which algorithmic metrics and electronic monitoring constrain workers both bodily and psychologically. The following sections take up each narrative in turn, attending to the variations and limits within each.

The dominant narrative: the contaminated sanctuary

Quantitative assessments of pandemic telework often suggest a broadly protective effect for workers in healthcare and social services. For instance, Boulet and Parent-Lamarche (2022) identified an overall positive association between teleworking and well-being during the first pandemic lockdown, specifically within the health and social services network. Yet, our critical interpretive synthesis of the quantitative and qualitative literature suggests that such overarching statistical measures, which likely reflect the immediate relief from acute on-site infection risks, do not fully convey the complexities of these workers’ daily realities. When examining their lived experiences, a different narrative emerges, one in which the transition to the home eroded the spatial and psychological boundaries that had previously contained therapeutic labor. Without the physical separation of a traditional workplace, the home was rendered permeable to the emotional demands of the work, transforming what had been a private refuge into what we term a contaminated sanctuary.

Pre-pandemic foundations: home as sanctuary and site of repair

The scarcity of pre-pandemic literature meeting inclusion criteria (n = 2) underscores a defining feature of historical telework: before COVID-19, home-based telework was largely confined to non-client-facing knowledge work. Real-time, interactive service provision remained institutionally anchored to enable oversight and access to infrastructure. Consequently, the health impacts of home-based telework were rarely studied in these occupations. In the few instances where home-based telework did occur, it was typically elective and oriented toward protecting workers’ health and well-being.

van den Broek (2017) shows that the health effects of telework are not intrinsic to home as a setting but are shaped by the physical and emotional strain of the prior workplace. For veteran tele-nurses, the shift to the domestic sphere was often driven by an acute need for self-preservation. The study highlights that early telework arrangements offered “regenerative possibilities” (van den Broek, 2017, p. 911), providing an opportunity to repair injured bodies and heal from the cumulative physical wear-and-tear and emotional exhaustion sustained in traditional, ward-based care environments. By physically removing themselves from demanding on-site labor, these workers actively utilized the home as a sanctuary to extend their working lives.

Although this transition was sometimes characterized as a compromise regarding career mobility (van den Broek, 2017), it yielded measurable well-being and organizational benefits. St George et al. (2009) found that, compared with colleagues in centralized health call centers, home-based tele-nurses demonstrated higher productivity, fewer sick days, and lower attrition, while maintaining clinical safety. They also reported exceptionally high satisfaction, citing flexible scheduling and the elimination of the daily commute as distinct advantages.

Taken together, these studies establish a critical baseline for interpreting post-pandemic telework: in the pre-COVID period, home-based arrangements in interaction-intensive healthcare and social work were rare and typically elective, often mobilized as a protective exit from physically and emotionally demanding settings. The well-being gains reported in this literature therefore reflect not the inherent qualities of home, but the conditions under which telework was implemented, particularly worker choice and spatial control. The COVID-era shift is consequential here because it transformed telework from an episodic option into a normalized mode of real-time service provision, fundamentally altering the conditions under which home-based work is experienced. Accordingly, the post-pandemic literature examined below offers insight into the health and well-being implications of home-based telework when interaction-intensive work becomes structurally embedded in everyday home life.

The intrusion of occupational distress into the domestic sphere

In contrast to the pre-pandemic framing of the home as a restorative sanctuary, recent literature highlights how the boundaries that once kept work-related distress confined to the office have collapsed. Historically, the physical office functioned as a symbolic and material container for clinical distress; however, the transition to widespread home-based telework has rendered the domestic sphere permeable to the heaviness of professional obligations (Schwarz & Welch, 2024). This phenomenon was characterized by an “evaporating emotional distance” between work and home (Maclean et al., 2024, p. 1267), where the psychological detachment required for the repair identified in earlier studies became nearly impossible to sustain. Consequently, rather than providing an exit from “the sights and the smells of nursing” (van den Broek, 2017, p. 913), the home environment becomes a primary site for processing them.

Boundary erosion was also temporal. Mishna et al. (2021, p. 490) found that as the “9 to 5” schedule became blurry, practitioners increasingly received work-related communications on weekends and evenings. This heightened contact-ability left workers feeling compelled to check their emails and respond to work demands during off-hours, making it difficult to maintain the necessary temporal separation between their professional and private lives.

Performing high-stress, emotionally charged tasks in a domestic setting was thus often experienced as a visceral transgression of personal boundaries. Practitioners reported that the presence of aggressive or abusive service-user voices within their private rooms felt like a violation of their living space (Ross et al., 2021; Stempkowski & Grafl, 2021). Some explicitly refused to engage with hostile clients from home because they did not permit such disturbance in their personal sanctuary (Kingstone et al., 2022; Sen et al., 2024). This sense of violation is best captured by the realization that “the violence is in my house” (Baffsky et al., 2022, p. 12), a sentiment expressed by workers who felt that traumatic client narratives remained physically present at their dining tables long after the laptop was closed.

This constant spilling of professional distress into the home could trigger a significant identity struggle, as practitioners found it difficult to sustain a professional persona within a domestic setting (Croxford et al., 2024, p. 26; James et al., 2022). Such permeation was particularly acute where workers lacked resources for a separate workspace. In the domestic and family violence (DFV) sector, for instance, practitioners often worked from improvised spaces such as kitchens, bedrooms, or bathrooms (Baffsky et al., 2022; Butler et al., 2023). Forced to process complex narratives of abuse from such spaces, these workers remained physically tethered to traumatic triggers. Without physical workplace cues to trigger psychological detachment, these narratives were described as lingering in private areas, leaving staff overwhelmed, anxious, and tearful during their personal time (Baffsky et al., 2022; Burd et al., 2023; McKeown et al., 2024; Pulman & Fenge, 2024; Saraniemi et al., 2023; Sims et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024). This was characterized as “contaminating” the home environment (Pulman & Fenge, 2024, p. 3762). This dynamic was further exacerbated by the absence of a physical commute that previously allowed workers to decompress (Ross et al., 2021).

Finally, the intrusion of clinical distress necessitated a complex negotiation of domestic space to shield others. Counsellors and clinicians faced the particular burden of ensuring privacy not only for client confidentiality but to prevent family members from being exposed to clinical content involving violence, abuse, or other distressing material (Croxford et al., 2024; O'Neil et al., 2024). Practitioners described the stress of shifting instantly from discussions of extreme violence and trauma to interacting with children who were nearby, waiting for their attention (Baffsky et al., 2022). This proximity forced workers to pre-emptively brief family members to prevent interruptions, further eroding the emotional distance between professional duties and private life (Maclean et al., 2024; O'Neil et al., 2024). Ultimately, the core struggle was defined by relentless boundary-maintenance labor: workers were tasked with policing a separation that the domestic setting was structurally ill-equipped to sustain.

Intensified emotional demands and the erosion of peer support

The literature characterizes the office not merely as a physical workspace, but as a critical infrastructure for both client intervention and professional support. For workers in healthcare and social work, home-based telework reshaped the labor process by introducing technological and relational frictions that amplified emotional demands and strained well-being. Across diverse clinical contexts, reduced face-to-face proximity can undermine trust-building with domestic violence survivors (Ferreira et al., 2025), while general practice nurses reported that remote consultations may reduce therapeutic care to a fragmented transaction focused merely on completing a checklist (Anderson et al., 2024). Dakin et al. (2025, p. e215) conceptualize these pressures as technosuffering among primary care staff, and counsellors similarly reported that remote work requires intensified deep acting to project empathy through a screen (O'Neil et al., 2024). The cumulative effort of compensating for these digital barriers was linked to heightened stress, fatigue, and emotional exhaustion (Ferreira et al., 2025; O'Neil et al., 2024).

A central structural failure was that emotional and relational demands increased just as the support systems that sustained workers were weakened. In office settings, spontaneous peer interaction often functions as an immediate shock absorber after distressing clinical encounters (Burd et al., 2023; Griffith et al., 2023; Schiff et al., 2024). Colleagues may overhear a difficult call, recognize when someone is struggling, and offer immediate informal relief, such as preparing a drink, exchanging brief words in a hallway, or simply being present, that helps the worker decompress (Celentano et al., 2022; Griffith et al., 2023; Liberati et al., 2021; Maclean et al., 2024; McKeown et al., 2024). Under remote arrangements, this safety net was lost. Support became available only through scheduled meetings, effectively raising the threshold for reaching out to a colleague and removing the small encounters that sustained high-functioning teams (Daffern et al., 2022; Jeyasingham & Devlin, 2024; Saraniemi et al., 2023). This erosion was echoed in the “red dot on Teams” phenomenon, where workers reported that managers became difficult to reach, leaving them to compete for their limited time (Pulman & Fenge, 2024, p. 3764).

Consistent with this shift, the transition to home-based telework is associated with increased social isolation, which the literature identifies as a major driver of work-related distress (Ayyala et al., 2021; Bentham et al., 2021; Faija et al., 2020; Gostic et al., 2024; Langran et al., 2022; Liddiard, 2022; van Gelder et al., 2021). Without a physically available colleague after a difficult case, the reflexive process of processing and recalibrating could stall, leaving domestic abuse helpline staff to carry trauma alone (Maclean et al., 2024). Community mental health nurses described this loss of collegial buffering as a clinical safety risk, particularly when managing high-risk cases without the immediate buffering typically available in offices (McKeown et al., 2024). For homelessness case managers, the loss of customary peer support was reflected in significantly higher traumatic stress scores compared with their on-site peers (Schiff et al., 2024).

These support losses were also culturally patterned. For Indigenous practitioners, the shift to remote work could produce a sense of disconnection from both their clients and the wider community, where collective presence and shared protocols are integral to well-being. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Werkmeister et al. (2023) argue that the individualistic, Westernized structure of home-based telework could conflict with Indigenous understandings of mental health rooted in collective practice, severing Tikanga Māori (customary practices) and the communal conditions required for Māori cultural safety during high-stakes work.

To mitigate these risks, organizations need to actively recreate support as formal infrastructure rather than an optional social add-on. Studies advocate institutionalizing more frequent supportive debriefing and dialogical spaces to compensate for the structural deficit of remote work (Griffith et al., 2023; Maclean et al., 2024; Saraniemi et al., 2023). The consequences of failing to do so extend beyond immediate psychological distress to clinical safety and workforce retention: remote work can constrain supervision and pedagogical oversight essential for safe practice (Weir et al., 2022), and social workers cited “too much working from home” as a primary reason for intending to leave the profession (Pulman & Fenge, 2024).

Physical strain and improvised environments

Physical strain within the healthcare and social service sectors was reported as a direct consequence of the cognitive intensity required for remote clinical care, compounded by the use of unsuitable, improvised domestic workstations. The literature suggests that the physical toll of home-based telework is deeply rooted in an increased cognitive load, which is the compensatory effort required to monitor micro-cues, eye contact, and facial movements through a screen (Shklarski et al., 2021).

Cognitive exhaustion and virtual fatigue

Maintaining remote therapeutic relationships required intense focus and visual concentration, creating a cognition-driven fatigue that was significantly more exhausting than in-person work (Cantone et al., 2021; Ingham et al., 2024; O'Neil et al., 2024; Pericleous-Smith et al., 2023; van Gelder et al., 2021). This exhaustion was exacerbated by the lack of natural stimulation found in a physical presence and the requirement to maintain a constant performative visual presence on camera (Hughes et al., 2022; Werkmeister et al., 2023). Sturm et al. (2021) observed this paradox among probation officers: while the remote modality provided a protective physical barrier against potential client aggression, the extra energy required to sustain online supervisions was also more draining and mentally taxing than traditional in-person contact.

O'Neil et al. (2024) further demonstrated that this depletion was intensified by the structural realities of atypical employment. For counselors in atypical or contract-to-contract arrangements, the removal of institutional scheduling boundaries often created a financial-emotional bind, where the ease of online access compelled them to increase client volume to ensure economic viability. Consequently, the lack of traditional buffer time between appointments led to relentless back-to-back virtual sessions, which practitioners reported lasted 10 to 12 h daily, resulting in profound somatic depletion (O'Neil et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024). The physiological toll of this continuous online performance frequently manifested as chronic migraines, acute visual strain, and severe sleep disturbances (Singh et al., 2024), alongside musculoskeletal injuries requiring physical therapy (Lamb et al., 2022).

Improvised workstations and ergonomic deficits

Beyond the cognitive burden, the transition to home-based telework revealed a stark reality of care work conducted in highly restrictive and unconventional domestic spaces. During the initial emergency shift, practitioners resorted to working from “cupboard” workstations, garden sheds, and even pitched tents to secure clinical confidentiality or stable internet connections (Singh et al., 2024; Werkmeister et al., 2023). In shared or overcrowded homes, staff were forced to deliver sensitive services from bathrooms and toilets to prevent family members from overhearing traumatic content (Baffsky et al., 2022). Others worked from their home sauna, which in Finland often represented the only lockable, private space available for sensitive client work, utilizing laundry baskets on benches as makeshift laptop stands (Saraniemi et al., 2023).

While these extreme improvisations were born of crisis-driven necessity, the widespread lack of dedicated office infrastructure translated into an enduring risk of physical degradation. Allied Health Professionals (AHPs) and nurses reported debilitating neck, back, and shoulder problems attributed to working from settees, dining tables, or beds for extended periods (Burton et al., 2022; Lamb et al., 2022). Rather than a temporary inconvenience, these improvised environments represented a structural downloading of occupational health risks onto the worker. Because the physical footprint of the workplace was dissolved, the structural and financial burden of maintaining clinical ergonomics became an out-of-pocket expense for employees, leaving many to endure chronic musculoskeletal strain while awaiting employer-provided solutions like standing desks or supportive seating (McChesney et al., 2023).

Furthermore, as hybrid arrangements became normalized, the physical persistence of makeshift workspaces, such as a laptop permanently stationed on a dining table, ensured that the stress of the workday remained visually and spatially anchored in the home. This spatial anchoring effectively prevented physiological recovery even after the shift ended, as the domestic environment was no longer perceived as a site of escape from the labor process (Daley, 2023; Maclean et al., 2024; Mirbahaeddin & Chreim, 2023).

Stratified experiences of home-based telework

The literature shows that the impacts of home-based telework varied across intersecting axes of social inequality. The extent to which remote arrangements functioned as a protective mechanism for health and well-being was shaped by workers’ career stage, gender roles within the home, racialization in the workplace, socioeconomic position, and the nature of the clinical task.

Occupational tenure and spatial autonomy

The capacity to use spatial flexibility as a protective mechanism was closely tied to occupational experience and learned boundary management. The literature highlights a divergence in how telework was experienced by seasoned practitioners compared with those newly entering the field (Jeyasingham & Devlin, 2024; Sen et al., 2024). For experienced workers, telework often afforded the autonomy to work efficiently and creatively. Jeyasingham and Devlin (2024, p. 2177) found that senior staff used digital platforms to maintain reflective relationships with colleagues and to reject “empty work,” preserving their energy for meaningful client engagement. The benefits of this tenure-based autonomy were further reflected in Singh et al. (2024), who, studying an exclusively seasoned cohort, found these experienced workers leveraged telework for self-care, with participants reporting that they had largely managed their work-life balance.

Conversely, newly qualified practitioners struggled with the domestic location of high-stress tasks. As Sen et al. (2024) demonstrated by comparing new cohorts to those with five years of experience, junior staff had not yet developed the practice-conscious work routines necessary to safely navigate a home-based work environment. This vulnerability was compounded by professional isolation. Jeyasingham and Devlin (2024) emphasized that less experienced practitioners faced weaker support systems at home, missing out on the incidental learning that traditional office spaces provided. Furthermore, while senior staff thrived on digital autonomy, the reliance on text-based communication often reduced the confidence of newly qualified workers, leaving them siloed rather than supported (Jeyasingham & Devlin, 2024).

Structural stratification: the intersections of gender, class, and race

While telework was often framed as a universal tool for flexibility, its benefits were complicated by the gendered division of care work. For women in these occupations, the home functioned as a site of role collision, characterized by a double burden: the simultaneous requirement to manage the emotional and physical needs of family members and clients (e.g., trauma survivors) within the same physical space (He et al., 2023; Maclean et al., 2024; Marshall et al., 2023). The continuous interweaving of familial commitments and professional obligations, such as parenting or managing household logistics between clinical calls, eroded mental well-being and made psychological detachment difficult to achieve (Rahimipour Anaraki et al., 2022).

This gendered burden was further compounded by inequalities across the occupational positions within the healthcare sector. Sims et al. (2022) found that nurses and social workers in their sample mitigated telework pressures through resources such as reduced work hours, domestic assistance or grocery delivery services. By contrast, community health workers and medical assistants reported fewer such resources, and the spatial constraints of their home environments were more pronounced. Workers from these latter groups described, for instance, paying for work-related utilities such as personal phones used for client calls out of pocket, and the psychological toll of being confined to a single room for both work and sleep. Sims et al. (2022) linked these patterns to the lower-paid position of community health workers and medical assistants within the healthcare workforce, a workforce predominantly composed of low-income ethnic minority women, and argued that these structural conditions shaped how home-based telework during the pandemic was experienced.

For racialized staff, by contrast, the digital interface could function as a protective resource rather than only a site of intrusion. He et al. (2023) found that caseworkers of color were more likely than their White colleagues to face client-based barriers, including family refusal of face-to-face visits during the COVID-19 pandemic. Staff of color in that study expressed a significantly stronger desire to retain virtual visits and remote access post-pandemic. By providing a mechanism to reduce the stress of direct client-initiated friction, digital tools functioned as a buffer against racialized workplace hostility. This pattern highlighted how the protective benefits of virtual care were distributed differently along racial lines, offering workers of color a spatial shield from discriminatory hostility encountered in face-to-face settings.

Institutional mediation and the nature of the clinical task

The well-being effects of telework also depended on how home-based arrangements were institutionally sanctioned. Leung et al. (2023) found that physicians who could have teleworked but were not authorized to do so reported significantly higher burnout than those whose telework was formally recognized, suggesting that institutional legitimation of the arrangement is itself a determinant of well-being outcomes. In children's hospice settings, Bedendo et al. (2024) found that burnout levels were independent of working arrangements during the pandemic; what predicted lower burnout was access to training and development and employee well-being programmes, underscoring that organizational support structures, rather than the location of work itself, mediate well-being outcomes.

Well-being outcomes also depended on the nature of the clinical task and whether it permitted clear segmentation between work and personal life. Unlike mental health clinicians, who processed continuous emotionally laden narratives that often seeped into private domestic spaces, workers in primary care and virtual outpatient roles described more positive well-being trajectories, reporting improved work-life balance and protection against burnout (Breton et al., 2021; Cannedy et al., 2023; Kranke et al., 2024; Kujansivu et al., 2024). Because primary care and outpatient roles often involved routine follow-ups or structured shift work, the labor was more transactional and temporally contained. This structural containment allowed practitioners to maintain routine daily activities and engage in recovery activities, such as sharing meals with family, between virtual sessions, allowing them to frame telework as a vital organizational resource rather than a hazard (DePuccio et al., 2022; Kranke et al., 2024).

This capacity for segmentation extended to emergency contexts, such as UK 999 telephone clinicians, where home-based triage functioned as a form of stress attenuation. In this context, the home provided a controlled bubble that replaced the high-intensity environment of an on-site operations center. This arrangement preserved well-being because the shift-based structure of the work allowed clinical responsibility to end at log-off, and participants actively developed separation routines, such as putting equipment away and going for a walk, to create a psychological demarcation between work and home life (Harry & Brady, 2024). For these workers, the home functioned as a site of recovery precisely because the temporal containment of the clinical task supported their ability to enact boundary preferences.

The emergent narrative: the ambivalence of home-based service work

While the dominant narrative centers on the psychological boundary struggles of workers in healthcare and social work, the smaller body of evidence on call centre, contact centre, and B2B sales work points to a more ambivalent picture. Workers in these sectors reported immediate relief from physical managerial scrutiny and reclaimed time and bodily capacity from the daily commute. At the same time, the same evidence raises a concern that the dominant narrative has tended to leave unexamined: that home-based arrangements may intensify rather than relax managerial control over the labor process, while also producing new forms of spatial confinement, the collapse of work-home boundaries, and the loss of informal peer support.

Shifting frontiers of control: physical relief vs. digital surveillance

For call center workers, the transition to home-based telework was often experienced as a reprieve from the constant face-to-face scrutiny and physical monitoring of the traditional call center floor. Experienced workers, in particular, reported feeling more at ease and better able to perform when freed from direct managerial oversight (Santos & Pereira, 2023). Rangarajan et al. (2022) identified a similar dynamic in B2B sales, noting that while the profession historically relied on in-person encounters to build relationships, sales leaders resisted telework because it was viewed as reducing managerial visibility and control.

However, this subjective sense of relief should be interpreted with caution. While workers reported a reduction in interpersonal pressure, the removal of the physical gaze was accompanied by the introduction of technological systems capable of measuring and monitoring work in detail. For salespeople, this manifested as a reliance on newer IT tools for monitoring sales activities, transforming home-based work into an experience of being “completely glued to [one's] laptop 24/7” (Rangarajan et al., 2022, p. 2364). Contact center workers similarly reported digital workflows and constant availability pressures following them into their private lives, as the physical presence of the manager gave way to technological connectivity (Manner et al., 2024). Consistent with what Hodder (2020, p. 262) characterizes as a “shifting frontier of control” and Brown et al. (2010) describe as digital Taylorism, surveillance did not disappear with the move to home-based work but reconstituted itself through the digital infrastructure of the work itself.

This shift toward internalized self-regulation represented a precarious change in the labor process. When stripped of the environmental cues and immediate managerial assistance of the physical office, the autonomy of telework could paradoxically increase worker anxiety rather than reduce it (Rangarajan et al., 2022). Without the structural buffer of on-site supervision, remote arrangements could also undermine performance; this was particularly evident among younger workers, who reported difficulty maintaining focus when working independently at home (Santos & Pereira, 2023). Workers were thus left to perform the additional, often invisible labor of self-regulation within a domestic setting that offered little structural support for maintaining focus.

In this emergent narrative, the home did not function simply as a site of liberation but could take on features of a digital panopticon, where the overt control of the manager was supplemented by less visible pressure from digital monitoring and internalized surveillance.

The work-life trade-off: resource reclamation vs. spatial collapse

Similar to workers in healthcare and social work, call center and B2B sales workers experienced a double burden in which domestic and professional obligations collided within the same territory. For this group, however, the conflict was shaped less by the defense of an emotional sanctuary and more by a strategy of resource reclamation. The primary value of home-based work lay in the optimization of time and economic resources (Morán et al., 2022). Eliminating the daily commute released time that allowed workers to secure essential sleep and to integrate household maintenance into their structured work breaks (Manner et al., 2024; Santos & Pereira, 2023).

Yet, these gains came at the cost of spatial and psychological collapse. Because call center workers often performed high-volume, psychologically exhausting labor in bedrooms or shared communal spaces, they lacked the physical and environmental cues necessary to separate from their duties. The absence of spatial boundaries offered little retreat from the mental weight of the work, leaving the domestic sphere a site of structural exhaustion rather than a place of rest (Santos & Pereira, 2023).

Where the dominant narrative emphasizes emotional leakage of work into the home, the experience of workers in this sector was characterized more by high-density multitasking. This involved managing rigid, algorithmically paced call-handling metrics while remaining constantly tied to the phone, alongside active family care within a confined space (Manner et al., 2024; Morán et al., 2022).

Physical health: structural immobility vs. compensatory activity

Physical well-being presented a paradox: the immobility produced by the digital workflow stood alongside compensatory health behaviors enabled by time reclaimed from the daily commute. On one hand, the domestic setting could exacerbate sedentary behavior. Because the path to basic amenities was shorter at home, workers lost the incidental movement, such as walking to a vending machine or a colleague's desk, that came with navigating a large office complex (Manner et al., 2024). This structural immobility was often reinforced by a perceived need to remain at the desk and contactable to demonstrate engagement, replacing previously mobile face-to-face queries with sedentary digital alternatives such as Skype or Teams (Manner et al., 2024). On the other hand, the home environment could also facilitate lifestyle adjustments through the reallocation of commute time toward physical maintenance. Contact center workers used this gain to secure essential sleep, manage household chores, or engage in physical and restorative activities such as sunbathing or exercise (Morán et al., 2022; Santos & Pereira, 2023).

Crucially, the reallocation of commute time toward physical maintenance may not be best understood as a passive benefit of telework but as a proactive survival strategy: workers appear to have utilized this spatial autonomy as a compensatory mechanism to mitigate the bodily wear and tear imposed by the home's sedentary nature (Manner et al., 2024).

The loss of collective defense and professional atomization

The transition to home-based work weakened the informal social rituals and collective support systems that had traditionally buffered the high-stress nature of call center work. Initially, this shift created a sense of relief. Workers noted that the relative quiet of the home environment eliminated the constant overlapping voices characteristic of the call center floor, allowing for better task concentration and temporary reprieve from sensory overload (Santos & Pereira, 2023).

However, this physical isolation quickly transformed from a benefit into a source of vulnerability. While digital platforms like Skype or Teams replaced formal communication channels, they failed to replicate the spontaneous workplace conversations that allowed workers to share the emotional burden of their roles (Manner et al., 2024). Without the physical presence of peers, workers described an acute perception of abandonment, feeling unsupported when navigating hostility or aggression from clients (Santos & Pereira, 2023). The transition to the home removed the immediate, informal relationships that allowed workers to decompress, vent, or laugh with a colleague immediately following a difficult interaction. Because formal digital channels were perceived as colder and less personal, workers were left to process emotional friction largely on their own (Santos & Pereira, 2023). The silence of the home did not function as a sanctuary but as a condition of structural isolation, leaving workers without the informal peer support and collective coping practices that had been routinely available in shared workplaces.

Discussion

This critical interpretive synthesis examines how home-based telework impacts the health and well-being of client-facing service workers, a workforce whose labor is defined by real-time, online interaction with patients, clients, or customers. This workforce has expanded rapidly into home-based arrangements since the COVID-19 pandemic, yet remains largely peripheral to telework scholarship, which has been built predominantly around knowledge workers with high levels of task autonomy. By centering this interaction-intensive workforce and treating worker health and well-being as the primary outcome of interest, this synthesis develops a theoretical account of how the nature of the labor process shapes the health consequences of home-based telework.

Principal findings: A tale of two teleworks

This synthesis reveals that home-based telework is not a monolithic experience but is shaped by the distinct labor processes that characterize different service occupations. A striking finding of this review is the profound occupational skew in the existing evidence base: of the 69 studies analyzed, 64 were concentrated within healthcare and social work, while research examining the health and well-being consequences of home-based telework for call center, contact center, and comparable customer-service roles remains scarce. We treat this numerical disparity not as a limitation but as an analytical finding in its own right, one that reveals how the academic preoccupation with professional boundary struggles has inadvertently left undertheorized the distinct harms faced by service workers whose labor is scripted, routinized, and subjected to rigorous digital surveillance.

The dominant narrative on telework and health has been constructed through the lens of workers in healthcare and social work whose interactions with service recipients involve significant professional discretion and exposure to distress, trauma, and vulnerability, framing telework-related harm as a psychological struggle over boundary management and the maintenance of a contaminated sanctuary. By surfacing the few, ambivalent studies of scripted and monitored service roles, this review exposes an underdeveloped counter-narrative: for workers in call and contact centers, the primary health burden lies not in boundaryless autonomy but in physical and psychological entrapment within a digital panopticon.

Theoretical implications

This synthesis proposes that home-based telework shapes health and well-being through two distinct mechanisms, each rooted in the labor process that organizes a given occupation. For workers in healthcare and social work, the central mechanism is a burden of autonomy: the freedom to work from home dismantled the physical and institutional boundaries that had previously contained the distressing content of clinical and social care labor within the workplace. Where the office had functioned as a structural container, its removal transferred the entire burden of boundary management to the individual worker. This dynamic bears surface resemblance to the autonomy paradox identified by Mazmanian et al. (2013) among knowledge professionals, in which flexibility to work anywhere, anytime paradoxically erodes the very autonomy it promises. However, the consequences are qualitatively distinct. For knowledge professionals, what penetrates the home when boundaries erode is work demand and connectivity pressure. For healthcare and social work professionals, what penetrates is the content of the work itself, specifically encounters with trauma, crisis, and vulnerability that do not dissipate when the working day ends. Contaminated sanctuary names this distinction: not only the burden of being always available, but the deeper burden of having one's private space inhabited by the psychological weight of others' suffering.

For workers in call and contact centers, the available evidence, though limited to three studies and ambivalent in places, points toward a distinct mechanism: a burden of control. A synthesis of these findings suggests that any initial relief from physical surveillance is often partial: rather than dissolving managerial oversight, home-based telework appears to reconstitute it through digital means, as performance tracking and constant availability pressures follow workers into the domestic space. This interpretive pattern is consistent with what occupational scholarship has long established about call center labor. Fernie and Metcalf (1998) documented that the physical call center constitutes an electronic panopticon, a near-total surveillance regime in which computer telephony enables real-time monitoring of every call, idle time, and deviation from performance targets. Brown et al. (2010) further showed that this control is not contingent on physical co-presence: by translating labor processes into standardized software packages, templates, and automated metrics, digital Taylorism embeds managerial control into the digital infrastructure itself, making it operable regardless of location. Altenried (2022) extended this argument spatially, asserting that digital technology does not dissolve the factory but reconstitutes its logic across distributed spaces, turning the home itself into a digital factory where labor is algorithmically organized and continuously monitored. Collectively, these developments converge on what this review terms a digital panopticon: the extension of a digital control regime into private and domestic spaces never originally designed to contain industrial discipline.

Two recent empirical studies outside this review's inclusion criteria lend further plausibility to this reading. Fana et al. (2022) found that home working intensified rather than relaxed technological surveillance for workers in lower-level positions, as personal, bureaucratic, and technical forms of control hybridized under remote arrangements. Beaufils and Chung (2026) found that the mental health benefits of home-based work observed among women in routine occupations before the pandemic disappeared in the post-pandemic period, a pattern the authors attribute in part to the intensification of control as remote work expanded into these roles. Together, these converging lines of evidence support the argument that for scripted, monitored service workers, home-based telework does not attenuate the control structures of the labor process but relocates and, in some cases, intensifies them, with attendant consequences for worker health and well-being.

Despite operating through distinct mechanisms, both narratives point to a common structural consequence: the transition to home-based telework constituted not merely a spatial relocation but a privatization of occupational risk. For healthcare and social work professionals, the workplace had provided peer support and informal debriefing that buffered the psychological demands of high-stakes interactions; its removal placed that burden on the individual worker. For call and contact center workers, the shared physical floor had sustained collective solidarity that partially offset the pressure of monitored, scripted labor; its removal intensified isolation. In both cases, the protective functions previously embedded in the collective work environment were transferred to the individual worker, without the institutional resources or social infrastructure to sustain them.

Finally, the health and well-being consequences documented in this review do not fall evenly across the workforce. The evidence already shows that outcomes vary by gender, caregiving status, occupational position, and career stage, yet these axes of inequality remain undertheorized. Chung (2022) demonstrates that flexible and remote work is experienced differently across gender lines, with women disproportionately absorbing its costs through the expansion of unpaid domestic and caregiving labor rather than gaining genuine autonomy. Altenried (2022) adds a further dimension: the digital factory strategically incorporates structurally vulnerable workers, including migrants, racialized workers, those whose credentials go unrecognized, precisely because their precarious status makes them simultaneously accessible and disposable to algorithmically managed labor regimes. The workers most likely to occupy digitally surveilled, home-based service roles are often those already navigating overlapping structural vulnerabilities. An intersectional analytic that attends to gender, race, class, and migration status is therefore essential to understanding who works from home, under what conditions, and with what consequences for health and well-being.

Conclusion

The evidence synthesized indicates that home-based telework is not a monolithic experience, but is profoundly shaped by the distinct labor processes that organize service work. Without targeted interventions, the home environment may become either a contaminated sanctuary for healthcare and social work professionals, where the emotional strain of uncontained clinical distress erodes recovery, or a digital panopticon for call and contact center workers, where algorithmic monitoring reconstructs and intensifies managerial control. These divergent experiences underscore that the health consequences of telework are not intrinsic to the domestic setting, but are derivative of the specific organizational demands and digital infrastructures governing the labor process. Given that these labor processes are differentially structured by gender, race, and class, designing policy around a singular, knowledge-centric occupational model remains insufficient and potentially exclusionary. Future research should therefore prioritize three directions: extending empirical attention to scripted, routinized occupations subjected to continuous electronic monitoring; examining how intersectional axes of gender, race, and class interweave with the labor process to shape telework outcomes; and investigating contexts beyond the Global North, where the interplay of housing, digital connectivity, and labor regulation produces distinct configurations of risk and well-being.
