Burden and forecast of severe periodontitis in BRICS-Plus nations: trends from 1990 to 2040
Authors: Li-Sha Luo, Jiao Huang, Hang-Hang Luan, Lan Wu, Wen-Zhong Xie, Xian-Tao Zeng, Xia Zhou, Lan Yang, Yuan-Yuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere periodontitis is a global public health challenge, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries. BRICS-Plus nations (35 emerging economies representing > 50% world population) face unique pressures from socioeconomic inequalities and aging populations, yet their epidemiological trends remain understudied. This study aims to quantify the burden of severe periodontitis and project future trends in these nations.MethodsUsing data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021), we analyzed historical trends (1990–2021) of incidence and years lived with disability (YLDs) via the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was used to generate future projections (2022–2040) that comprehensively account for age, period, and cohort effects.ResultsIn 2021, BRICS-Plus contributed 62.52% of global incident cases (89.61 million) and 64.31% of YLDs (6.90 million). Regional disparities were pronounced: Mercosur and SAARC showed the highest age-standardized burdens (incidence: 1132.20/100,000; YLD: 95.52/100,000), while SACU had the lowest (incidence: 600.50/100,000; YLD: 36.78/100,000). Laos exhibited the sharpest historical rises (incidence EAPC = 1.33, 95%CI: 1.02 to 1.65; YLD EAPC = 1.62, 95%CI: 1.18 to 2.06). BAPC projections indicate sustained growth in Laos, with 40–60-year-olds remaining as the high-risk group, while over half of BRICS-Plus nations may experience downward trends by 2040.ConclusionsSevere periodontitis exhibits divergent trends across BRICS-Plus, with Laos facing escalating burden while most nations show projected declines. Implementing targeted interventions—including tailored dental healthcare strengthening in high-prevalence regions and evidence-based preventive strategies targeting vulnerable middle-aged demographics—is essential for effectively addressing these divergent trends and reducing the overall disease burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02660-7.

Introduction

BRICS-Plus, an extension of BRICS bloc (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa), comprises 35 emerging economies across Mercosur (core members as well as acceding members), South African Customs Union (SACU), SAARC (SAFTA members), China-ASEAN FTA and Eurasian Economic Union (EEU) [1, 2]. This collective, representing a demographic majority exceeding 50% of the global population, displays profound socioeconomic diversity that ranges from upper-middle-income economies to low-resource settings, while concurrently experiencing significant demographic shifts, marked by accelerated urbanization trends and progressive population aging [3, 4]. The BRICS-Plus framework provides a compelling platform for disease research due to member states’ shared socioeconomic development pathways and healthcare histories [5]. These nations collectively face growing non-communicable disease burdens, including periodontitis, and the cooperative model offers opportunities for developing targeted interventions [6].

Severe periodontitis is a multifaceted, long-standing chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal ligaments, alveolar bone, and eventual tooth loss [7]. Its etiology involves intricate interactions between pathogenic microbial biofilms (notably Porphyromonas gingivalis), dysregulated host immune responses, and genetic susceptibility factors such as the interleukin-1α −899 C→T polymorphism [8, 9]. Importantly, recent researches have established significant associations between severe periodontitis and systemic health consequences. Yuan et al. proposed it as a novel risk factor for genitourinary cancers, while Wang et al. demonstrated its association with increased risk of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) [9, 10]. Given the multisystemic impacts and significant health burden associated with this condition, a comprehensive understanding of its disease burden carries substantial clinical and public health importance.

Recent findings from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) reveal severe periodontitis as a growing global health crisis, with approximately 1.2 billion cases reported worldwide in 2021 [11]. Notably, the burden disproportionately impacts low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where age-standardized incidence rates increased significantly between 1990 and 2021 (e.g., Laos: +1.33%/year; Vietnam: +1.17%/year) [11]. Demographic aging and persistent oral health disparities in resource-constrained regions are projected to drive a potential 24% rise in global cases by 2050 [12].

While BRICS-Plus nations collectively represent substantial economic and demographic influence, a critical knowledge gap persists regarding comprehensive burden quantification and global contribution assessments of severe periodontitis within these nations. Therefore, this study leverages GBD 2021 data to: (1) quantify the historical burden (incidence and years lived with disability [YLD]) of severe periodontitis across BRICS-Plus nations from 1990 to 2021; (2) analyze temporal trends using estimated annual percentage change (EAPC); and (3) project future burden (2022–2040) using a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model. These analyses aim to provide unprecedented evidence crucial for targeted resource allocation and the development of effective prevention strategies within and across BRICS-Plus nations.

Methods

Data source

Data used in this study were obtained from GBD 2021, a comprehensive global research initiative that estimated key health metrics and assessed the impact of various factors on health across global, regional, and national levels. Specifically, GBD 2021 provided updated estimates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, years of life lost (YLLs), YLDs, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) for 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 risk factors, spanning 204 countries and 811 subnational locations from 1990 to 2021 [13]. These estimates enabled synchronized resource allocation and facilitated cooperative initiatives to address interrelated health challenges.

For severe periodontitis prevalence estimation, GBD 2021 employed DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool, with stratification by age, sex, year, and country [13–15]. Mortality attributed to severe periodontitis was fixed at zero, as this condition is not typically fatal [13, 15]. Disability weight (DW), quantifying health impairment severity, were used to calculate YLDs through multiplication of total cases, duration until recovery or death, and DW [13]. The DW for severe periodontitis in GBD 2021 was 0.007 (95% uncertainty interval: 0.003–0.014). The modeling framework incorporated data from an updated systematic review of observational studies on severe periodontitis epidemiology and health survey [13, 16].

In this study, we retrieved the incidence and YLD data for severe periodontitis across all ages, 15 age-specific groups (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85+), and age-standardized groups in BRICS-Plus countries from 1990 to 2021 for trend analysis.

Case definition and study population

In GBD 2021, disability associated with severe periodontitis was defined as “bad breath, a bad taste in the mouth, and gums that bleed a little from time to time, but which does not interfere with daily activities”, corresponding to ICD-10 codes K05.0–K05.6 and ICD-9 codes 523.0–523.9 [12, 17]. The case definition utilized a hierarchical approach prioritizing: Community Periodontal Index of Treatment Needs (CPITN) = 4, Clinical Attachment Loss (CAL) > 6 mm, and Gingival Pocket Depth (PD) > 5 mm [15]. These parameters were considered equivalently as reference definitions without additional cross-walking [12]. Data using alternative thresholds, (e.g., CPITN = 3, CAL > 5 mm, CAL > 4 mm) were cross-walked to the reference definitions using the Meta-regression tool (MR-BRT) to maximize data inclusion [12, 18]. Prevalence estimates were adjusted for edentulism to correct systematic overestimation bias arising from excluding edentulous populations in modeling [12].

Our study population were restricted to BRICS-plus nations, categorized into five different regions: Mercosur, SACU, SAARC, China-ASEAN FTA and EEU and an independent subdivision for 35 nations [1]. These subdivisions included Mercosur (Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, Bolivia, Venezuela), SAARC (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka), China-ASEAN FTA (China, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Brunei, Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, Cambodia), SACU (Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia, South Africa, Swaziland), and EEU (Kyrgyzstan, Armenia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Russia) [3].

Statistical analysis

In our study, we presented all estimates as counts, age-specific rates and age-standardized rates (ASR) per 100,000 population, and the ASR was calculated using the direct method with age-specific weights from the GBD standard population [19]. The EAPC and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed to assess temporal trends in severe periodontitis burden using a linear regression model. In this model, the natural logarithm of the ASR (Y) was expressed as α+βX+ε, where X represented the calendar year and ε denoted the error term. The EAPC was then calculated as 100×[exp(β) − 1] [20]. A positive EAPC indicated increasing incidence or YLD rates over the 31-year period, while a negative value implied a decreasing trend. Additionally, the BAPC was employed to project severe periodontitis burden from 2022 to 2040, as this approach has shown superior performance and more reasonable predictions compared to other models [21]. The BAPC model, based on the age-period-cohort analysis, utilized a second-order random walk to smooth prior rates relating age, period and cohort effect, with the integrated nested Laplace approximations (INLA) package estimating marginal posterior distribution [22]. A major advantage of the BAPC model was its ability to address convergence issues commonly observed in traditional Bayesian approaches using Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling, thereby improving prediction reliability [22]. The BAPC and INLA packages within the R 4.3.2 software were used to develop a BAPC model, and p < 0.05 was regarded statistically significant.

Results

Severe periodontitis burden in BRICS-Plus and globally in 2021

In 2021, the global burden of severe periodontitis comprised approximately 89.61 million incident cases (ASR: 1,069.44 per 100,000) and 6.90 million YLDs (ASR: 80.89 per 100,000). BRICS-Plus nations bore a disproportionate share of this burden, contributing 62.52% of global incident cases and 64.31% of YLDs (Fig. 1). Temporal trends in ASR of incidence and YLD for severe periodontitis over 30 years in BRICS-Plus and globally

Between 1990 to 2021, 17 out of 35 BRICS-Plus countries exhibited increasing ASRs of incidence for severe periodontitis. Notably, Laos and Vietnam showed the most pronounced upward trajectories, with EAPCs of 1.33% (95% CI: 1.02–1.65) and 1.17% (95% CI: 0.74–1.60), respectively (Table 1, Fig. 3). Parallel trends in ASRs of YLD showed EAPCs of 1.62% (95% CI: 1.18–2.06) for Laos and 1.00% (95% CI: 0.68–1.33) for Vietnam. Conversely, the Philippines and Sri Lanka demonstrated the most rapid declines in incidence ASR, with EAPCs of −3.60% (95% CI: −3.88 to −3.32) and −2.43% (95% CI: −2.93 to −1.93), respectively. Their YLD ASR reductions were even more pronounced, with EAPCs of −4.21% (95% CI: −4.56 to −3.86) and −3.91% (95% CI: −4.72 to −3.10), respectively (Table 1, Fig. 3). Age-specific rates of incidence and YLD for severe periodontitis across BRICS-Plus and globally

Figures S1 and S2 depicted age-group trends in severe periodontitis incidence and YLD rates. Globally and within the SACU region, incidence rates increased monotonically with age from 1990 to 2021, with older demographics exhibiting the highest risk. Conversely, EEU, Mercosur, SAARC, and China-ASEAN FTA regions demonstrated steep increases among younger age groups followed by plateaus or declines after age 40. Figure S2 showed age-specific YLD rates followed an inverted U-shape pattern across most BRICS-Plus nations, reaching peak levels between 40 and 60 years of age.

Figures S3 and S4 illustrate cohort-related trends in the incidence and YLD rates of severe periodontitis across age groups. The findings demonstrate that globally and in most of BRICS-Plus countries, the incidence risk of severe periodontitis declines progressively with each successive birth cohort, with earlier birth cohorts facing notably higher risk. For YLD rates, both global and most BRICS-Plus country trends exhibit a near “inverted U” pattern, where YLD rates peak in intermediate cohorts.

Projected burden of severe periodontitis across BRICS-Plus countries through 2040

From 2022 to 2040, 23 of 35 BRICS-Plus countries are projected to experience declining incidence ASR, with India (−20.3%) and Nepal (−18.5%) showing steep declines (Fig. 4). Conversely, Laos is projected to have a 29.4% increase in incidence ASR (from 591.55 to 765.23 per 100,000). Discussion

Severe periodontitis imposes a substantial global health burden in aging populations, with profound implications for healthcare systems worldwide [23]. The BRICS-Plus framework provides a critical lens to analyze this burden across emerging economies 3]. Utilizing GBD 2021 data, this study reveals that BRICS-Plus countries collectively accounted for over 50% of global severe periodontitis burden, with striking regional disparities: Mercosur and SAARC exhibited the highest ASRs, whereas SACU showed the lowest. Notably, although most BRICS-Plus nations have experienced declining ASRs in incidence and YLDs, Laos and Vietnam showed concerning upward trends, with Laos projected to experience continued increases through 2040.

The large proportion of incidence cases and YLDs of severe periodontitis among BRICS-Plus countries was primarily due to population growth and shift in age-structure [25]. It’s estimated that these 35 countries encompass over 50% of the global population, with China and India alone constituting 36% of worldwide population [26, 27]. Aging was also a recognized risk factor for severe periodontitis, and our findings revealed that population aged 40–60 years groups suffered higher risk for severe periodontitis incidence and disability among most of BRICS-Plus countries and globally, which was consistent with the previous studies [16, 18, 24]. This vulnerability reflects a convergence of natural and accelerated aging processes, including immune-senescence, chronic inflammation, impaired tissue repair, and cumulative exposure to modifiable risks (e.g., tobacco, poor diet) [23, 28]. Although individuals over 60 years often prioritize non-communicable diseases like cancer and cardiovascular disorders, chronic periodontitis acts as a silent risk factor for these conditions, underscoring the need to integrate oral health into elderly care [16, 29]. The characteristic inverted U-shaped cohort effect across many BRICS-Plus nations reflects some interlinked pathological mechanisms, including lifetime risk accumulation, aging-induced biological decline and inflammation synergy. Cumulative exposure to risk factors like tobacco over decades in mid-life cohorts has led to a significant buildup of damage in the oral cavity. For instance, long-term smokers in this age group often have a more diverse and pathogenic oral microbiota, as the toxins in tobacco disrupt the normal balance of oral bacteria [30]. Moreover, the synergy between systemic inflammation and periodontal disease in this age group cannot be overstated. Conditions like diabetes, which are more prevalent in mid-life, can exacerbate periodontal inflammation, and in turn, the inflammation from severe periodontitis can contribute to poor glycemic control, creating a vicious cycle [31].

Our study revealed that Mercosur and SAARC regions exhibited a higher disease burden of severe periodontitis, while SACU had a lower burden. This disparity may be attributed to several contributing factors, including healthcare system limitations, socioeconomic inequalities, lifestyle and dietary habits, and public health-related policies [32]. The elevated burden in Mercosur appears predominantly associated with socioeconomic inequalities, inconsistent implementation and monitoring of public health policies, variable prioritization of oral healthcare within health systems, and dietary factors like high sugar consumption [33]. SAARC’s higher burden primarily stems from widespread poverty, constrained healthcare accessibility, inadequate oral health awareness, and common behavioral risk factors including substantial tobacco consumption (both smoked and smokeless varieties) and carbohydrate-rich diet [34, 35]. In contrast, SACU has lower ASRs attributed to relatively higher economics, better socioeconomic indicators, and robust healthcare infrastructure with higher dentist-to-population ratios and public health initiatives, along with lower tobacco use rates, better dietary habits and oral health education. Tackling these regional disparities requires tailored, bottom-up interventions: SAARC nations would benefit from community-based prevention (e.g., school fluoride programs, tobacco cessation), task-shifting to mid-level providers, and subsidized basic care; Mercosur needs stronger policy enforcement (sugar taxes, compliance for WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control), reducing healthcare inequalities via Universal Health Coverage integration, and sustained public awareness campaigns [25]. Notably, sparse epidemiological data, limited surveillance infrastructure and absence of standardized periodontal registries in several countries, particularly remote/rural areas (like Nepal and Pakistan), may lead to underreporting of severe periodontitis cases, potentially masking subnational inequalities. Future burden assessments should prioritize establishing sentinel surveillance sites and implementing standardized diagnostic criteria to generate locally validated data.

Projections indicate approximately 70% of BRICS-Plus countries will maintain a downward trajectory in severe periodontitis burden through 2040, largely attributed to effective prevention strategies and WHO policies implementations including Universal oral health coverage, WHO Global Oral Health Strategy in 2022, Global Oral Health Action Plan (2023–2030) and WHO’s Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) [36]. These preferential policies would help us to change the current situation of oral health neglect among limited-resourced countries and improve the state of global oral health. Notwithstanding overall progress, some of BRICS-Plus countries exhibited rising ASRs for both severe periodontitis incidence and YLDs during 1990–2021, with Laos projected to experience the most significant increase through 2040. This divergence underscored uneven implementation of preventive measures and deeper structural vulnerabilities [6]. In countries like Laos, delayed implementation of tobacco controls, limited preventive care access, and rapid population aging were highly associated with increasing burden of severe periodontitis. Without interventions targeting early-life exposures, particularly tobacco cessation, sugar reduction policies, and integrated management of comorbidities, this upward burden will perpetuate rising national trends [36, 37].

This study had several limitations. First, our results were contingent upon the accuracy and quality of the GBD 2021 data. Given that most of BRICS-Plus countries were low- and middle-income countries, data scarcity and quality in some countries may have constrained the precision of GBD estimates and consequently affected our results. Second, the hierarchical definition used in GBD study may affect the sensitivity and specificity of the severe periodontitis diagnosis. Furthermore, lack of transparency about case definition in each country could introduce regional interpretation bias. Future GBD updates would benefit from incorporating the 2018 World New Classification of Periodontal Disease and Conditions to enhance diagnostic validity [18, 25]. Third, although we conducted a comprehensive perspective of severe periodontitis burden by location, year, age and sex in BRICS-Plus, we were unable to evaluate other potentially influential factors due to data unavailability in the GBD repository.

Conclusion

BRICS-Plus nations bear over half the global severe periodontitis burden, with Mercosur and SAARC exhibiting the highest burden while SACU reports the lowest. Projections reveal Laos will experience substantial rise in incidence and YLD by 2040, underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions. Given the disproportionately high disease burden observed in the 40–60-year age group, targeted preventive measures for this demographic represent a critical public health priority. Strategic implementation of customized approaches, particularly intensive prevention initiatives in Laos and age-specific intervention programs, is imperative for effectively mitigating the periodontitis burden throughout these nations.
