Responsive neurostimulation in children, adolescents, and young Adults—Longitudinal effectiveness and safety
Authors: Steven T Lee, Shital Patel, Cemal Karakas, Kimberly Houck, Cristina Trandafir, Matthew J McGinley, Deepankar Mohanty, James J Riviello, Howard L Weiner, Daniel Curry, Irfan Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) remains challenging to treat, and seizure control often fluctuates over time. Responsive neurostimulation (RNS) is increasingly used in pediatric and young adult populations, yet long-term effectiveness and safety data are limited. We evaluated longitudinal seizure control trajectories and safety of RNS in children, adolescents, and young adults treated at our center. Seizure frequency was assessed at approximately 6-month intervals from serial follow-up visits through the most recent follow-up, with each sampled visit categorized by ≥ 50 % (all-responder, AR) or ≥90 % (super-responder, SR) seizure reduction. We examined the persistence and durability of seizure control and used recurrent-event regression to identify clinical modifiers of sustained response. Responder rates improved over time, with 57 % and 75 % of patients achieving AR status at 24 and 47 mean months post-implantation, respectively; corresponding SR rates were 22 % and 33 %. Once achieved, seizure control was typically maintained: the probability of remaining an AR for 24 months was 70 %, and the median proportion of follow-up with sustained response exceeded 85 % across age groups. Concurrent epilepsy surgery favored maintenance of AR status (HR 1.83, p = 0.02), while syndrome-related epilepsies reduced the likelihood of sustained SR status (HR 0.30, p = 0.03). Age at implantation and responder status at 24 months both associated with long-term seizure control. Serious (0.032 events/person-year) and non-serious (0.038 events/person-year) adverse effects were infrequent. RNS provided durable benefit for most, though not all, patients, reflecting the dynamic nature of seizure control. The favorable effectiveness and safety profile support use of RNS in young patients with DRE.Keywords: Pediatric RNS, Responsive neurostimulation, Pediatric neuromodulation, Pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy

Introduction

Epilepsy affects one in 26 people [1] and is characterized by variable seizure frequency trajectories [2]. One third of patients will develop drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) after failing two appropriately chosen anti-seizure medications (ASMs) [[3], [4], [5]]. Uncontrolled epilepsy profoundly impacts cognition, mood, quality of life, and mortality risk [[6], [7], [8]]. Epilepsy surgery can be highly effective [9], and early intervention in pediatric patients is particularly critical to protect neurodevelopment [10,11]. However, many patients are not ideal candidates because the seizure onset zone (SOZ) may involve eloquent cortex, bilateral or dominant mesial temporal structures, or the contralateral region may have been previously ablated or resected. In other cases, patient preference precludes resection. For these patients, neuromodulation or dietary therapies may offer a more favorable risk–benefit profile [[12], [13], [14]].

Neuromodulation approaches include vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), and brain-responsive neurostimulation (RNS® System, NeuroPace, Inc.) [15]. The RNS System is a closed-loop device with detection-driven targeted stimulation and is Food and Drug Administration-approved for use in adults with focal DRE involving one or two epileptogenic foci [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Off-label use in pediatric patients is increasing, but the retrospective evidence is predominantly focused on last-visit outcomes, which fail to capture longitudinal variability and are temporally scattered. Existing reports with regular sampling are limited by sample size and follow-up duration [20], or combine pediatric and adult cohorts without age-specific analyses [21]. Given that epilepsy is a dynamic disease, sustained seizure control – not just a single timepoint – is clinically important. The lack of pediatric and young adult longitudinal effectiveness data represents a major knowledge gap with direct implications for patient counseling and clinical decision-making.

In this study, we aimed to characterize the long-term effectiveness and variability of RNS therapy in children, adolescents, and young adults with DRE. Specifically, we: i) report seizure frequency changes in regular intervals up to seven years; ii) examine ‘persistence’ and ‘durability’ of seizure control; iii) model sustained outcomes using recurrent event analysis; iv) assess the influence of age at implantation and seizure control status at 24-months post-implantation on long-term effectiveness; and v) summarize safety findings.

Materials and Methods

We performed an institutional review board–approved retrospective review of patients with RNS implantation between July 2016 and December 2022, managed at Texas Children's Hospital through September 2023. Exclusion criteria were: i) less than 9 months of post-implantation follow-up; ii) RNS used only for diagnostic monitoring and subsequently explanted; and iii) inability to quantify seizure frequency. Patients were grouped by age at implantation: children (<12 years), adolescents (12–17 years), and young adults (≥18 years; hereafter referred to as adults). Previously reported patients were included to strengthen longitudinal analyses [22,23].

All patients underwent comprehensive presurgical evaluation and multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery conference review prior to RNS recommendation. Active leads targeted either two cortical foci, one cortical and one thalamic focus, or two thalamic nuclei. RNS with concurrent epilepsy surgery was considered when the seizure onset zone (SOZ) involved both eloquent and non-eloquent cortex or there were greater than two SOZs. Follow-up visits occurred initially within 4–6 weeks, then every 2–12 weeks. Additional medical management was provided at the discretion of the treating epileptologist.

We analyzed demographics, patient- or caregiver-reported seizure frequency, and adverse events. Maximal stimulation parameters (charge density, current level, frequency, burst duration, and stimulations per day) were sampled at the most recent follow-up (MRFU) and reported separately for cortical and thalamic leads. To quantify stimulation titration, charge density was collected at initiation, then at 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, and then every 6 months subsequently. Longitudinal stimulation current densities between age groups were compared using linear mixed effects modeling [24]. Categorical variables were compared with the Fisher-Freeman-Halton (FFH) test [25]; continuous variables were compared using one-way conventional ANOVA [26] with Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test [27].

Seizure frequency change

Seizure frequency after RNS implantation was categorized as ‘increased’, ‘no change’, or ‘decreased’. Seizure reductions were further stratified into six categories: 1–24 %, 25–49 %, 50–74 %, 75–89 %, 90–99 %, and 100 %. For longitudinal analysis, one data point was sampled from each 6-month interval (e.g., 3–8.9, 9–14.9, …, 81–86.9 months) post-implantation. When seizure frequencies were indeterminate, estimates were first based on expert consensus by at least two epileptologists (SL, IA, KH, CT). If a seizure frequency consensus was not reached, linear interpolation was implemented. For patients who transferred into our care, historical records were used to estimate pre-transfer seizure frequencies. At each visit, patients achieving ≥50 % reduction in seizure were classified as all-responders (AR) and those achieving <50 % reduction were non-responders. For separate analyses, those achieving ≥90 % reduction were classified as super-responders (SR) (Table S1).

Longitudinal effectiveness

Cohort-level response patterns were described by reporting AR, SR, and seizure-free rates across mean follow-up times for each 6-month interval. Linear trends were assessed using the Cochran–Armitage test [28]. In separate time-to-event analyses, we compared time to first AR and time to first SR across age groups.

Seizure control maintenance

We examined two dimensions of seizure control: persistence and durability. Persistence was defined as the number of consecutive sampled visits maintaining similar or better seizure reduction (≥50 %, ≥90 %, or 100 %). We evaluated sequences of one, two, and four consecutive samples; only patients with sufficient follow-up were included in each analysis. Time-to-loss of initial AR or SR status was analyzed using separate time-to-event models. Durability was defined as the proportion of time spent with the same or better seizure control after achieving a significant reduction (AR or SR status). This was calculated from first attainment through MRFU. Patients with <12 months follow-up after first AR or SR attainment were excluded. To assess imputation bias, we conducted sensitivity analysis for durability and persistence by excluding imputed seizure frequency data.

Recurrent event regression analysis

We applied the Andersen-Gill (AG) model [[29], [30], [31]], an extension of the Cox proportional hazards framework, to model longitudinal maintenance of seizure control. For separate models, recurrent events were defined as any visit meeting AR (≥50 % seizure reduction) or SR (≥90 % seizure reduction) criteria. Analyses included data up to 48 months post-implantation; patients followed at or beyond 48 months were censored due to small sample size. Hazard ratios (HRs) > 1 indicate improved maintenance of seizure control capturing both persistent and durable responders. Based on sample size considerations, our primary aim to assess age effects, and findings from a recent meta-analysis [20], we included covariates of age group (children, adolescents, young adults), concurrent epilepsy surgery (yes, no), epilepsy syndrome diagnosis (yes, no), and VNS presence (yes, no). Expected event counts per patient were visualized using covariate-stratified models; HRs were derived from non-stratified models [31].

Conditional recurrent event analysis from 24 months

To explore age-related and responder status-related benefits in long-term therapy, we performed a conditional recurrent event analysis (also known as ‘Landmark Analysis’ in survival analysis) beginning at 24 months and continuing up to 48 months. Based on the sample sizes, we only included two covariates: age group and seizure control status at 24 months (non-responder: <50 %; intermediate responder [IR]: 50–89 %; SR: ≥90 % reduction). Expected event counts per patient were visualized using age- and status-stratified models; HRs were derived from non-stratified models.

Results

Patient cohort

Of 66 patients with RNS, 56 (85 %) met inclusion criteria. Of the 10 patients excluded, seven had less than nine months of follow-up at TCH; one underwent diagnostic RNS with subsequent explantation; and two patients (ages 3 and 17 years) had clinical improvement, but seizure frequency changes could not be quantified on review of documentation. Three of 56 patients received RNS implantation at outside institutions.

Demographics and clinical characteristic

Age of epilepsy onset was significantly earlier in children (mean 3.9 years, p < 0.01) and adolescents (mean 5.2 years, p < 0.01) than adults (mean 8.6 years). Children also had a shorter duration of epilepsy (4.6 years) prior to RNS implantation compared to adolescents (10.3 years, p < 0.001) and adults (10.8 years, p < 0.001). Structural and structural-genetic etiologies were most common (46/56, 82 %) with no age group differences (p = 0.18). No significant differences between age groups were observed for other patient characteristics, including the proportion of patients undergoing concurrent surgery with RNS (Table 1) and RNS lead combinations (Table 2).

Longitudinal response patterns

Responder rates increased over time (Fig. 1a and b). At a mean of 24 months post-implantation (range 21–26 months), 57 % (21/37) of patients were ARs, 22 % (8/37) were SRs, and 14 % (5/37) were seizure free. At a mean of 47 months (range 45–50 months), AR, SR and seizure freedom rates were 75 % (9/12), 33 % (4/12), and 33 % (4/12), respectively (Table 3). Kaplan–Meier analyses showed no significant difference in time to first AR status (p = 0.80, log-rank test; Fig. 1c) or SR status (p = 0.26, log-rank test; Fig. 1d) among age groups.

Persistence of seizure control

Persistence of AR status was observed in 73 % (41/56), 62 % (35/56), and 54 % (20/37) of patients for at least one, two, and four consecutive visits, respectively. For SR status, corresponding values were 46 % (26/56), 33 % (19/56), and 14 % (5/37), respectively. For seizure freedom, corresponding values were 23 % (13/56), 20 % (11/56), and 8 % (3/37), respectively. For the three levels of seizure control, no differences were observed for persistence across children, adolescents, and adults (all p ≥ 0.25, Fig. 2a). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no difference by age group in loss of initial AR (p = 0.63, log-rank test) or SR (p = 0.32, log-rank test) status (Fig. 2c–d). The probability of maintaining ≥50 % reduction at 12 and 24 months after first achieving it was 0.80 (95 % CI: 0.76–1.00) and 0.70 (95 % CI: 0.54–0.91), respectively. For SR, these probabilities were 0.67 (95 % CI: 0.48–0.92) and 0.31 (95 % CI: 0.14–0.67), respectively. Sensitivity analyses excluding imputed data yielded consistent results (Table S4).

Durability of seizure control

After an initial AR status (n = 30), median durability of seizure control was 100 % (IQR: 100–100 %) in children (n = 11), 87 % (IQR: 55–100 %) in adolescents (n = 13), and 91 % (IQR: 41–100 %) in adults (n = 6), with no significant group difference (p = 0.28, Kruskal-Wallis test; Fig. 2b). Median follow-up duration was 28 months (IQR: 20–38) and was positively correlated with age (Pearson's r = 0.33, p = 0.04).

After an initial SR status (n = 20), median durability was 100 % (IQR: 100–100 %) in children (n = 7), 43 % (IQR: 35–100 %) in adolescents (n = 6), and 67 % (IQR: 33–91 %) in adults (n = 4), with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.10, Kruskal-Wallis test; Fig. 2b). Median follow-up duration was 30.5 months (IQR: 20–46) and positively correlated with age (Pearson's r = 0.52, p = 0.02). Sensitivity analyses excluding imputed data yielded consistent results (Tables S5 and S6).

Recurrent event regression analysis

In the AR Andersen–Gill model (n = 286 visits, 141 AR events), age group was not associated with sustained ≥50 % reduction. Compared with adults, adolescents (HR = 0.85, 95 % CI: 0.50–1.42, p = 0.53) and children (HR = 1.10, 95 % CI: 0.62–1.95, p = 0.75) showed no significant difference; children and adolescents were also similar (HR = 1.30, 95 % CI: 0.87–1.95, p = 0.98; Fig. 3a). Concurrent epilepsy surgery was associated with improved AR maintenance (HR = 1.83, 95 % CI: 1.26–2.65, p = 0.02; Fig. 3b). There was no association of seizure control with VNS (HR = 0.68, 95 % CI: 0.44–1.06, p = 0.09) or a syndromic diagnosis (HR = 1.07, 95 % CI: 0.61–1.90, p = 0.80). Model fit: concordance = 0.61; Wald test χ2 = 16.07, degrees of freedom [df] = 5, p = 0.007; robust score χ2 = 11.99, df = 5, p = 0.03.

In the SR model (n = 286 visits, 60 SR events), age group was associated with sustained ≥90 % reduction (Fig. 3c). Compared with adolescents, children (HR = 3.03, 95 % CI: 1.46–6.30, p = 0.003) and adults (HR = 2.93, 95 % CI: 1.32–6.49, p = 0.008) had higher likelihood of SR maintenance. Children and adults were similar (HR = 1.04, 95 % CI: 0.43–2.47, p = 0.94). Concurrent surgery showed a near-significant benefit (HR = 1.98, 95 % CI: 0.96–4.06, p = 0.06). Syndromic diagnosis was associated with worse seizure control (HR = 0.30, 95 % CI: 0.11–0.88, p = 0.03, Fig. 3d). The presence of a VNS had no association with seizure control (HR = 0.94, 95 % CI: 0.52–1.70, p = 0.83). Model fit: concordance = 0.72; Wald test χ2 = 21.23, df = 5, p < 0.001; robust score χ2 = 13.64, df = 5, p = 0.02. Cross-sectional outcomes stratified by concurrent surgical status are provided in Table S7.

There were 316 clinic visits across 56 patients, of which 22 (7 %) seizure response scores were imputed across 10 (18 %) patients without difference across age groups (p = 0.75). Imputed seizure frequencies across visits were 5 % (1/22) with seizures increased, 27 % (6/22) with no change, 27 % (6/22) with 1–24 % seizure reduction, 22 % (5/22) with 25–49 % seizure reduction, and 18 % (4/22) with 50–74 % seizure reduction. Right censoring increased with follow up duration was primarily due to study duration at our institution (53/56, 95 %) rather than treatment-related factors (3/56, 4 %) and without difference across age groups (p = 0.70, Table S3).

Conditional analysis from 24 Months post-implantation

From 24 months onwards in the AR model (n = 100 visits, 54 AR events), children had a higher likelihood of maintaining ≥50 % reduction compared with adults (HR = 2.41, 95 % CI: 1.13–5.13, p = 0.02; Fig. 4a). Adolescents did not significantly differ from adults (HR = 1.96, 95 % CI: 0.96–4.00, p = 0.07). There was no difference between children and adolescents (HR = 1.23, 95 % CI: 0.81–1.87, p = 0.32). Response status at 24 months was strongly associated with sustained control: IR (HR = 2.39, 95 % CI: 1.37–4.16, p = 0.002) and SR (HR = 2.75, 95 % CI: 1.43–5.25, p = 0.002; Fig. 4b) both outperformed non-responders. Model fit: concordance = 0.67; Wald test χ2 = 24.92, df = 4, p < 0.001; robust score χ2 = 12.8, df = 4, p = 0.01.

Safety

Adverse events (AE) for all patients were reported through their MRFU. For serious adverse effects, across all age groups, two patients (4 %) experienced 3 cumulative intracranial hemorrhages (0.019 events/person-year), one patient had worsening of seizures (2 %, 0.006 events/person-year), and one patient (2 %) had a lead infection (0.006 events/person-year) all requiring hospitalization. For non-serious adverse effects, five patients (9 %) had stimulation-related symptoms of paresthesia, apraxia, or increased seizures (0.038 events/person-year). Given the low number of AEs, we did not compare age groups (Table 4).

Of the two adults experiencing intracranial hemorrhages, one had two events which occurred at 32- and 82-months post-RNS. The first event was likely fall-related while the second had no clear cause. Episodes were associated with emesis and severe headaches. Brain imaging confirmed bleeds near the implant and resolved with conservative management (steroids and observation). Computed tomography angiograms before RNS implantation for stereotactic planning and after each hemorrhage were negative. The other patient experienced fall-related intracranial hemorrhage at 18 months and required emergent RNS removal.

One child experienced infection of leads placed over a residual dysplasia after a partial resection. The leads were intended for a planned RNS implant later. This required a washout with explant. New leads with the RNS device were implanted 20-months after. This patient also experienced non-serious stimulation-related paresthesias and is included below.

One child experienced a serious stimulation-related adverse effect. Since RNS implantation at an outside hospital, the patient had significant worsening of myoclonic and tonic seizures with new semiology requiring repeat stereoelectroencephalography evaluation. The RNS stimulation was turned off, and the patient underwent resection of a peri-tuber seizure onset zone with subsequent seizure freedom.

Among the five patients with non-serious stimulation-related symptoms, two were children and three were adolescents. Three patients experienced either focal distal hypothermia, intermittent numbness, or tingling (motor hand lead); increased auditory auras (left superior temporal gyrus leads); or increased contralateral weakness with increased seizures (left parietal and midline leads), all at charge densities ≤1.3 μC/cm2. Despite attempted re-titration, the patients turned off stimulation of implicated lead(s) as adverse effects were intolerable. The other two patients had tolerable non-serious stimulation-related symptoms. The first had a battery replacement after 2.4 years and resulting pain with stimulation around the new implant. The second had a left inferior occipital lead. Initially there were tolerable visual scotomas but intolerable migraines. Stimulation was turned off and later successfully titrated slowly to 1.5 μC/cm2 with improved seizure control and tolerable daily visual symptoms.

RNS data

RNS data at MRFU are shown in Table S2. Four patients had lead changes between 23 and 70 months. Mean cortical charge density was lower in children (1.5 μC/cm2, p < 0.001) and adults (2.0 μC/cm2, p = 0.02) versus adolescents (2.9 μC/cm2). Mean stimulation current was significantly lower for children versus adolescents (1.1 vs 4.1 mA, p = 0.03). For thalamic leads, there was no difference in stimulation parameters. Early battery replacement occurred in four patients at 2.4–3.6 years. These RNS implants occurred in 2016 or early 2017.

Cortical lead charge density increased significantly over time in a quadratic pattern (linear: β = 0.087 μA/cm2 per month, 95 % CI 0.069–0.104, p < 0.001; quadratic: β = −0.0014, p < 0.001), indicating initial escalation followed by plateau. The rate of increase differed significantly by age group, with adolescents (interaction β = 0.064 per month, 95 % CI 0.041–0.087, p < 0.001) and adults (β = 0.043 per month, 95 % CI 0.017–0.070, p = 0.001) demonstrating steeper escalation compared to children. Baseline charge density did not differ significantly between age groups.

During the plateau phase (≥24 months follow-up, Fig. S1), adolescents exhibited the highest charge density (3.08 μA/cm2), followed by adults (2.43 μA/cm2) and children (1.61 μA/cm2), with both adolescents (β = 1.47, 95 % CI 0.91–2.04, p < 0.001) and adults (β = 0.81, 95 % CI 0.13–1.50, p = 0.02) having significantly higher plateau charge densities than children.

Discussion

This study evaluated the longitudinal effectiveness and safety of RNS in children, adolescents, and young adults at a large pediatric epilepsy center. By characterizing both persistence and durability of seizure control and implementing recurrent event analysis to capture modifiers of seizure trajectories, we provide new insights into the temporal dynamics of RNS effectiveness and potential age-related effects.

Treatment with the RNS System was associated with progressive improvement in AR, SR, and seizure-free rates over 85 months aligning with prior adult [17,19] and mixed pediatric-adult studies [21] with follow-up beyond 24 months. Given the data structure of this work, comparison of effectiveness to most prior retrospective pediatric and young adult studies is challenging as last follow-up reports have wide temporal variance. Nevertheless, similar to our findings, a meta-analysis of the pediatric RNS studies through 2021 found an AR rate of 80 % (n = 44) at a median of 22 months follow-up [32], and a more recent multi-center study found an AR rate of 65 % (n = 51) with a mean of 11.7 (range 2–42) months follow-up [33]. Most similar to our approach, a single-center study of pediatric and young adult patients corroborated our findings, demonstrating responder rates that increase with time, reaching 71 % (4/7) for ARs and 43 % (2/7) for SRs at 24 months [20]. A limitation of available retrospective RNS studies is the absence of non-RNS control groups, leaving the comparative effectiveness of RNS versus other neuromodulation, surgical, or non-surgical treatments uncertain. Collectively, as in adults, these findings support the proposed mechanism of chronic remodeling of epileptiform networks to decrease epileptic propensity [34].

A key contribution of this study is the characterization of longitudinal variability in seizure control. DRE is inherently dynamic, and how pediatric and young adult patients respond to RNS over time has not been well characterized. In our cohort, while most patients achieved meaningful seizure frequency reductions, fluctuations were common as evidenced by the declining persistence probability with follow-up time. Nonetheless, durability for ARs was generally high with the median proportion of time spent with significant seizure control being at least 87 % across age groups. Notably, a minority of patients experienced a durability of less than 50 % suggesting only transient improvements in seizure control. Unsurprisingly, transient rather than sustained periods of seizure freedom were more common and have been previously described in adults [17,19]. While patient- or caregiver-reported seizures remain the gold standard for assessing seizure burden in clinical trials, their well-documented inaccuracies [35,36] may partially explain some of the variability observed in seizure frequency trajectories. Overall our characterization of longitudinal variability of seizure burden underscores the dynamic nature of epilepsy [2] even under chronic neuromodulation, and while meaningful significant seizure reduction is typically durable, sustained seizure freedom is unlikely.

To capture the complexity of seizure control trajectories up to 48 months following RNS implantation, we implemented a recurrent-event regression framework—an approach widely used in cardiovascular trials to more fully characterize the burden of chronic diseases [37,38]. This framework allows each patient to contribute repeated observations until censoring, thereby appropriately handling variable follow-up durations while accounting for within-subject correlation. The model treats clinic visits as approximate event occurrence times, recognizing that state transitions (e.g., achieving a responder status) may occur between scheduled assessments. A key limitation of this dataset is the degree of right censoring, which increases with follow-up time. Consequently, estimates at later time points are informed by fewer remaining patients, and long-term effects should be interpreted cautiously and validated prospectively. Importantly, most censoring was administrative in nature, reflecting the duration of available follow-up rather than treatment failure or adverse events, which supports the assumption of independent censoring underlying the model [31]. Multistate models [39] may be of future use, but to be robust, such models would require much larger data sets.

With recurrent event analysis, we identified concurrent epilepsy surgery as a positive modifier of sustained effectiveness at the AR level and near significance at the SR level, pointing to the known efficacy of epilepsy surgery [9]. Syndrome-related epilepsies were a negative modifier at the SR level, and we hypothesize these findings point to the ‘intrinsic severity’ of the most severe cases of DRE and is supported by a recent meta-analysis of pediatric RNS studies [40]. Surprisingly, adolescents also experienced worse seizure control than both children and adults at the SR level that was not detected by our time-to-first event or MRFU analysis. Importantly, there were no differences in stimulation targets between age groups. We suspect that our adolescent cohort exhibited a higher proportion of severe DRE not fully captured by our demographic data. Our adolescents had more rapid titration and higher current densities than children and adults, which were likely reflective of ongoing challenges with seizure control as stimulation parameters were typically titrated based on reported seizure frequency and tolerability. However, we acknowledge that rapid titration itself may contribute to suboptimal outcomes; in the Real-World Outcome study [18] versus Pivotal trial [41], the cohort with lower and more gradual charge density titration exhibited improved seizure reduction. Thus, the worse outcomes in adolescents may reflect both intrinsic disease severity and potentially less favorable programming trajectories.

Long-term age-related effects and the prognostic value of responder status at 24 months were evaluated using conditional recurrent-event models. Pediatric patients demonstrated improved maintenance of ≥50 % seizure reduction compared to adults, and the SR-level model indicated a strong association between pediatric status and sustained seizure control. Importantly, responder status at 24 months was strongly associated with future outcomes: patients who achieved ≥50 % or ≥90 % seizure reduction by 24 months were substantially more likely to maintain long-term control than non-responders or intermediate responders at the AR and SR levels, respectively. In the SR model, 24-month intermediate responders were not significantly different from non-responders, indicating that reaching and sustaining SR level after 24 months was unlikely. Large effect sizes and a significant robust Wald test suggest clinical relevance for age and seizure control at 24 months; however, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and number of events, which is reflected by the (more appropriately conservative) global robust score test (p = 0.06) [31] and wide covariate confidence intervals. The age-related findings raise the hypothesis that earlier neuromodulation may confer greater benefit through neuroplasticity to improve long-term outcomes [34,42,43] and mechanisms underlying neuromodulation should be further investigated [34,44]. Consistent with principles established in epilepsy surgery, these findings support the importance of early candidate identification with consideration for neuromodulation [45,46] and should be validated prospectively.

The RNS System demonstrated a favorable safety profile. Adverse event rates were comparable to prior reports, with hemorrhage, infection, and stimulation-related symptoms occurring at similar frequencies [7,16,17,19,21,33]. Notably, while stimulation-related side effects were observed in a minority of patients, at the conclusion of our study, optimization success after re-titration of parameters was lower than in published adult cohorts, where most were able to continue therapy [47]. While these findings support safety in the pediatric population, a comprehensive safety assessment should include neuropsychological outcomes, which were not included in this study. Continued longitudinal follow-up and prospective studies will be critical to fully define the risk–benefit profile of RNS in younger patients.

In summary, RNS was effective and generally well tolerated in children, adolescents, and young adults with DRE, with progressive improvement in responder rates over time. By characterizing both the persistence and durability of seizure control and applying recurrent event modeling, this study highlights the dynamic nature of epilepsy under chronic neuromodulation and identifies potential age-related advantages and the prognostic value of response at 24 months. These findings suggest that RNS is longitudinally effective, and cautiously that children may derive greater long-term benefit. A minority of patients achieve seizure freedom, although this may be transient. Further, patients with the most severe DREs may be less likely to achieve a sustained super responder status. Continued research into modulators of RNS effectiveness and mechanisms of neuromodulation-driven plasticity are needed. These findings support safety and effectiveness in pediatric RNS and will aid in the design of multi-center prospective studies to validate RNS safety and efficacy in children and adolescents.
