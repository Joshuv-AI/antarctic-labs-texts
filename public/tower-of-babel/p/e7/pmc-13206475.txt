Paternal use of metformin and risk of major congenital malformations: A meta‐analysis of 4 studies
Authors: Per Damkier, Marleen M. H. J. van Gelder, Krista F. Huybrechts, Lin‐Chieh Meng, Sonia Hernandez‐Diaz, Jan Nielsen, Bente M. Nørgård, Hedvig Nordeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetformin is the first‐line drug for the treatment of Type 2 diabetes. In 2022, a Danish registry‐based study reported a 40% increased risk of major congenital malformations following paternal exposure during spermatogenesis. This raised widespread concern about the use of metformin in males of reproductive age. Three subsequent studies were unable to replicate this finding. We performed a meta‐analysis of four observational studies with data from five countries covering more than 11 000 live‐born children whose fathers used metformin during spermatogenesis. The pooled risk ratio from an inverse variance‐weighted random effects model was 1.03 (95% CI 0.88–1.21). We conclude that paternal exposure to metformin does not confer a meaningfully increased risk of major congenital malformations.

INTRODUCTION

Metformin is the first‐line pharmacological treatment for Type 2 diabetes (T2D) worldwide. 1 In 2022, a Danish population‐based registry study reported a 40% increased risk of major congenital malformations (MCM) in offspring following paternal use of metformin during spermatogenesis (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 1.40; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.08–1.82). 2 This finding was reported without noting that it represented one of two positive associations identified in a study that screened paternal exposure to 973 medications, with statistically significant associations observed for only metformin and balzalaside. 3 This finding gained considerable media attention and raised concerns about the use of metformin in reproductive‐age males. An independent hypothesis‐driven study (i.e., not designed as a confirmatory or replication study) of the almost identical (two additional years of data added) Danish population data yielded a similar adjusted OR of 1.40 (95% CI 1.11–1.76) while controlling for a more elaborate set of confounders. 4 Subsequent studies including data from Norway, Taiwan, Israel and the United States have not replicated the Danish findings. 5 , 6 , 7

To synthesize the available evidence for clinical decision support, we performed a meta‐analysis of the most recent Danish study and the three additional studies covering five distinct countries to assess the potential association between paternal metformin exposure during spermatogenesis and the risk of MCMs in offspring.

METHODS

We performed a meta‐analysis following Cochrane guidelines (Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions version 6.5, chapter 10), 8 extracting adjusted risk estimates, that is, ORs or relative risks (RR) and 95% CIs from primary studies. We prioritized data from fully adjusted models using nonexposure as a comparator when possible. ORs were converted to RRs for meta‐analysis using RR = OR/(1 − P0 + [P0*OR]), where P0 is the incidence of the outcome in the unexposed group. 9 We applied a random effects model using inverse variance weighting. We performed a sensitivity analysis with the exclusion of Danish data to address replication in an independent data source. Analyses were performed using R. This study was not preregistered.

RESULTS

Two studies covered an almost identical Danish dataset; we included the risk estimate from the most recently published study, which covered 2 years more than the original dataset 2 (2017 and 2018) and had a more comprehensive confounder adjustment. 4 One study covered two populations (Norway and Taiwan)—the data were analysed separately for each population. 7 Study characteristics are listed in Table 1, and the meta‐analysis forest plot is displayed in Figure 1. A total of 11 508 metformin‐exposed men fathered 528 children with an MCM (4.6%). The studies differed in comparison groups; the number and type of confounders adjusted for in their analyses (Table 1). The pooled adjusted RR was 1.03 (95% CI 0.88–1.21), with I 2 = 64% (95% CI 6%–84%). Sensitivity analysis with exclusion of the Danish data (Figure 2) resulted in a pooled adjusted RR of 0.93 (95% CI 0.83–1.04) and I 2 of 0.0%. This demonstrates that the heterogeneity in the primary analysis is solely driven by the Danish data.

DISCUSSION

This meta‐analysis of more than 11 000 children born to fathers exposed to metformin during spermatogenesis yielded a pooled adjusted RR of 1.03 (CI 0.88–1.21). Although the upper bound of the CI is compatible with a small increased risk, our finding does not—with a high degree of precision—suggest an increased risk of MCM following paternal use of metformin.

Although two initial Danish studies suggested a 40% (with slight differences in the precision of this estimate) increased risk of MCM, subsequent studies using data from Norway, Taiwan, Israel and the United States did not replicate this finding. For causation, consistency across independent studies is crucial, and the lack of replication of the original finding across four different countries substantially weakens the case for metformin as a teratogen through paternal exposure. Some heterogeneity in the analysis was found. This appears driven by the Danish data, which are the only data with a positive association, as evidenced from the analysis excluding the Danish data (Figure 2). The strength of a potential association reported from the Danish population was weak, leaving room for residual confounding, such as underlying paternal cardiometabolic risk, to partly explain the initial findings. It may also be that the safety signal in the Danish data was a spurious finding in the context of multiple comparisons and arising from a chance distribution of MCM among children born to fathers receiving prescription drugs during spermatogenesis. We could find no animal and other preclinical studies to provide supporting evidence. A previous meta‐analysis, 10 yielding an OR 1.05 (CI 0.95–1.17), pooled data from the two Danish datasets, which have at least a 90% overlap. This is inappropriate and raises concerns about the non‐independence of the pooled estimate. 2 , 4 It did not include the large US dataset, which was not published at the time of submission. 6

In summary, cumulative evidence demonstrates that paternal exposure to metformin during spermatogenesis does not confer an increased risk for MCMs in offspring. This finding provides reassurance for continued use of metformin in reproductive‐age males with T2D.
