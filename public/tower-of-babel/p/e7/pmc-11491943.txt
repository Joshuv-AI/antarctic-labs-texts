Protocol for quantification of intercellular connection transmission in ebolavirus infections using ImageJ
Authors: Rodrigo I Santos, Alexander Bukreyev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrevious work demonstrates that ebolaviruses can spread to neighboring cells through intercellular connections. Here, we present a protocol to quantify the intercellular spread of ebolaviruses via immunofluorescence. We describe steps for cell plating, Bundibugyo virus infection, and adding a neutralizing antibody. We detail procedures for quantitative microscopy assay using ebolavirus immunodetection. Strong virus accumulation around the plasma membrane leads to high fluorescence signal preventing quantification of viral spread based on signal intensity. This protocol minimizes the impact of this bias.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Santos et al.1Subject areas: Cell culture, Health Sciences, Immunology, Microbiology, Microscopy

Before you begin

Spread of the virus by intercellular connections is an important feature of diseases caused by viruses and is widely used by different viruses, including, as a minimum, human immunodeficiency virus,1 hepatitis C virus, African swine fever, and vaccinia virus.2,3,4 Recently, Miao et al. and our laboratory demonstrated cell-to-cell spread of Ebola virus (EBOV).1,5,6 Understanding viral spread through intercellular connections is important for the development of therapeutic interventions that target viral spread.

This protocol delineates specific steps of quantitation of viral spread through intercellular connections. It includes infecting Huh7 cells with ebolaviruses, staining infected cells for subsequent immunofluorescence imaging, and quantification of the viral spread through intercellular connections through ImageJ. Notably, this protocol is adaptable for quantification of spread of various viruses by intercellular connections in a variety of cell types.

Step-by-step method details

Ebolavirus infection, performed in the BSL-4 laboratory

Timing: 5 days

For the study of spread of viruses through intercellular, virus transmission through the supernatant must be blocked. The use of semi-solid media used in plaque assays would not completely block free virus infection and is not suitable for these studies. The most effective way to block the spread of the virus through the medium is the use of highly potent virus-neutralizing antibodies (Figure 1).

1.Ebolavirus infection.a.Utilizing a low Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) (MOI of 0.01 PFU/cell or lower is recommended).i.Calculate the virus amount needed.ii.For a seeding density of 50,000 cells per well, use 500 PFU of ebolavirus per well.b.Prepare the working stock by diluting the calculated amount from item “a” to 500 PFU/100 μL.c.Add 100 μL of the working stock to each well.d.Incubate for 4 h at 37°C.e.Wash all wells 3 times with DPBS.

Virus quantification by fluorescent microscopy analysis

Experimental procedures. Huh7 cells were infected with BDBV for foci-quantification analysis by fluorescence microscopy. Following a 4-h adsorption, the cell monolayers were washed with PBS, and mAbs were added at a concentration of 50 μg/mL. Cells were fixed, immunostained and analyzed by fluorescence microscopy.

Some antibodies may inhibit viral spread through intercellular connections while others do not. Therefore, employing a panel of neutralizing antibodies similar to the one described in our recent manuscript1 is ideal for selection of individual antibodies with various abilities to inhibit spread of viruses through intercellular connections.

Fixation and staining, performed in the BSL-4 laboratory

Timing: 4 h

After adding neutralizing antibodies to the supernatants of infected monolayers, viral infection may occur only through intercellular connections. Quantification of virus spread relies on immunofluorescence staining. Here we describe how to fix infected cells, stain the fluorescent foci, and remove samples from high biocontainment facilities.

Expected outcomes

In our recent publication,1 we investigated the impact of different mAbs on viral spread through intercellular connections. To investigate cell-to-cell spread in ebolavirus infections, we developed a method employing immunofluorescence and image analysis through ImageJ software. Our observations revealed that impairment of viral spread through intercellular connections by some mAbs leads to a substantial accumulation of virus around the membrane, resulting in a heightened fluorescence signal. This accumulation posed challenges for quantifying viral spread based solely on signal intensity. To address this bias, we developed a quantitative microscopy assay utilizing ebolavirus immunodetection (Figures 1 and 2).

Virus quantification by fluorescent microscopy analysis

Huh7 cells were infected with BDBV for foci-quantification analysis by fluorescence microscopy. We selected two regions of interest (ROI): first, the entire focus, defined as all fluorescence quantified in a cluster of infected cells (ROItotal) and second, the initially infected cell, defined as the fluorescence quantified in the central cell in a cluster of infected cells (ROIinitial) (detailed in the analysis session; Figure 2). With a very strong fluorescence in a single cell, the value of (ROItotal)/(ROIinitial) would be equal to 1. The method alleviates the possibility that a fluorescence present in a single cell surpasses the value of multi-cell foci with a lower fluorescence.

In our experimental setup, we utilized a panel of mAbs described in the literature.6,7 Notably, glycan cap targeting antibodies demonstrated inefficiency in inhibiting viral spread through intercellular connections, resulting in the production of large virus foci with high quantification values. Conversely, antibodies targeting membrane proximal external region (MPER) exhibited efficient inhibition of viral spread through intercellular connection, generating smaller foci (Figure 3).

Limitations

The described methodology has some limitations.

The reliance on neutralizing antibodies poses a potential constraint. The feasibility of the assay is contingent upon the availability of suitable antibodies for testing. In instances when commercially available antibodies are lacking, the execution of the assay may be unfeasible.

Moreover, the protocol’s scope is confined to in vitro settings. Consequently, mechanisms of viral spread through intercellular connection in vitro may not fully reflect the mechanisms in vivo. The controlled environment of in vitro studies may not fully capture the dynamic and multifaceted aspects of viral interactions within a living organism.

Additionally, the effectiveness and impact of neutralizing antibodies on viral spread through intercellular connections may exhibit variability between viruses. Generalization of findings may be restricted to specific viruses and conditions investigated.

Acknowledging these limitations is vital for contextualizing the outcomes and understanding the boundaries of applicability for this methodology.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The use of glass surfaces is considered optimal for acquiring high-quality microscopy images. However, certain cells, such as 293T cells, may exhibit poor attachment to glass surfaces, leading to the complete detachment of the cell monolayer before the experiment’s conclusion (see step 1, seeding).

Potential solution

•To address this challenge, we achieved successful outcomes by seeding 293T cells onto pre-coated Poly-D-Lysine coverslips. Sterile coverslips were placed into 24-well plates at a seeding density of 100,000 cells per well. The experiment closely followed the described protocol for Huh7, except that the fixation occurred at 3 days post-infection.
