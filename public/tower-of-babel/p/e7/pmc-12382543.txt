Assessing healthcare organisations’ readiness to implement a learning health system: protocol for questionnaire validation using a Delphi method
Authors: Catherine M Giroux, Hervé Tchala Vignon Zomahoun, Sophie Boies, Paula Bush, Mohammed Alkhaldi, Pascaline Kengne Talla, Marie-Eve Poitras, Yves Couturier, Sara Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the health sciences, it can take up to 17 years for 14% of research findings to be adopted in clinical practice. Adopting a learning health system (LHS) approach may help accelerate the transition of medicoadministrative and clinical data to knowledge, knowledge to performance and performance to data. However, little is currently known about whether healthcare organisations are both willing and able to adopt such an innovation. Therefore, the aim of this study is to generate validity evidence in support of a measure assessing healthcare organisations’ readiness to implement an LHS approach.Methods and analysisA three-round Delphi method will be used to establish consensus on the relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness of the LHS readiness questionnaire’s domains, subdomains and items. The questionnaire has been developed based on a review of the literature. Participants with expertise in LHS across Canada and internationally will be purposively recruited using a modified Dillman approach, with opportunities for additional snowball sampling. Descriptive statistics will be calculated from all closed-ended Delphi survey responses. A conventional content analysis will be conducted on all open-ended responses.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained from the McGill University Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (AO3-E23-24B). The findings of this study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed publications, academic conferences, knowledge mobilisation workshops and through policy briefs and position papers.

Background and rationale

In the health sciences, there is a gap between evidence and practice. Indeed, it takes up to 17 years for 14% of empirical research findings to be adopted into practice for the benefit of patients.13 In addition, knowledge, user experiences, policy and the contextual environment are constantly evolving, requiring an adaptation of practices to deliver integrated, high-quality and evidence-informed care. Moreover, these processes of adapting or integrating practices may help to maintain or ultimately improve the well-being of patients and professionals and mitigate any costs associated with care provision.4 5 The COVID-19 pandemic challenged all healthcare and social services systems in terms of knowledge, resources and workforce,2 6 7 illustrating a pressing need to bridge the knowledge-practice gap between policymakers, health and social services system managers and professionals, researchers, patients and their families. One solution to bridge this gap is by adopting a learning health system (LHS) approach. The LHS approach allows for cyclical transitions from medicoadministrative and clinical data—alongside findings from empirical research and quality improvement activities—to knowledge, from knowledge to clinician and manager performance, and from performance back to medicoadministrative and clinical data.4 8 This cycle highlights the importance and value of regular data collection to optimise knowledge translation activities within and beyond health systems or organisations. Thus, the benefit of an LHS approach is improved care that focuses on patient needs, population health, patient and health professional experience, health equity and health-related costs.9 10

Despite the benefits of an LHS approach, there are implementation challenges and few specific examples of successful LHS implementation in practice.11 Specifically, little is known about health organisations’ readiness to identify, measure, resolve and monitor a given clinical practice or health condition. The literature on LHS discusses two levels of organisations: individual organisations (eg, primary care clinics, family medicine groups, hospital departments, etc) and networks (eg, multicentre hospitals, provincial or federal health networks, etc).12 However, before learning health networks can be established to drive learning and improve practice across large multisite centres,13 individual organisations must first assess their readiness to implement these innovations and their relevance to addressing organisational needs.14 Weiner15 defines organisational readiness as the collective determination of organisation members to implement a change (change commitment) and their shared belief in their ability to successfully carry it out (change efficacy).15 Since an organisation’s readiness for change can significantly impact whether an intervention is successfully adopted,16 17 it is an important factor to evaluate.

Our research team has engaged in a programme of work focused on the development, validation and implementation of an LHS readiness questionnaire for healthcare and social services settings (eg, primary care clinics or family medicine groups, hospitals, etc). The development of the initial questionnaire has been informed by a previously conducted scoping review (in review)18 that aimed to map the breadth and depth of the literature on LHS, with the goal of identifying relevant domains (ie, sets of interrelated attributes, such as behaviours, attitudes or values) and items (ie, specific questions) for inclusion.19 Overall, our scoping review found that over half of the included articles defined LHS as continuously generating data, creating new evidence and applying evidence to practice using learning cycles with the ultimate goal of driving improvement in healthcare. Moreover, 50 of the included articles presented 21 unique LHS frameworks, with many drawing specifically on the Friedman learning cycle. Accordingly, the domains identified in our review described the data to knowledge (D2K), knowledge to performance (K2P) and performance back to data (P2D) cycle as being key LHS domains. Several subdomains related to this D2K-K2P-P2D cycle were also identified, notably actions like assembling data, analysing data, integrating external evidence, interpreting results, designing an intervention, acting, forming a learning community and capturing data about practice. Key barriers to implementing an LHS included limited human and financial resources alongside challenges related to research and data collection. Key facilitators to implementing an LHS included creating a culture of learning and focusing on partnerships or processes that could enhance the uptake of an LHS. Proposed implementation strategies focused on the data and technical components, the human components and the design components of an LHS. Our review also found that while examples of LHS maturity assessments were included, no specific LHS readiness assessments were identified. Importantly, 14 articles stressed the importance of including patient and public engagement in LHS implementation, but only nine provided concrete examples of what this engagement could look like in practice.18

We subsequently refined our initial questionnaire by iteratively collecting and integrating feedback from interested parties. Specifically, we consulted six researchers with expertise in LHS, knowledge transfer, implementation science, health measurement and evaluation tool development or primary care; four primary care professionals (ie, family physicians and nurses); two health and social service system managers; and three patient partners. These interested parties were asked to read the questionnaire, check the clarity of items, suggest rewording if needed and indicate potentially irrelevant or new items. Finally, we translated the initial questionnaire from English into French. The aim of the present study is to generate evidence in support of the content validity of our questionnaire that aims to assess healthcare organisations’ level of readiness to implement an LHS approach.

Methods

Study design

We will conduct a three-round Delphi to establish consensus on the relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness of our LHS readiness questionnaire’s domains and subdomains. The Delphi method is a consensus group method where participants iteratively rank items using anonymous surveys until consensus is reached.2022 Consensus methods are useful when empirical evidence is lacking, limited or contradictory; they are predicated on the idea that an accurate and reliable assessment can best be achieved by consulting a panel of experts and accepting the group consensus.22 23 Our research questions are:

Instrument development

This study will consist of a Delphi survey about our LHS readiness questionnaire. The LHS readiness questionnaire includes four domains, specifically: (1) performance to data, (2) data to knowledge, (3) knowledge to performance and (4) LHS core values. Online supplemental appendix 1 depicts the blueprint for our LHS readiness questionnaire, which illustrates the alignment between our domains, subdomains and item numbers. Online supplemental appendix 2 contains the pre-Delphi version of our LHS readiness questionnaire.

The Delphi survey will address key validation questions related to the four domains of the LHS readiness questionnaire. Participants will undergo a preparation phase, where they will consent to participate and complete a short demographic survey. During round 1, participants’ main task will be to rate each of the LHS readiness questionnaire items on three aspects:

Participants will also be asked to identify any areas not captured by the existing items or other items that should be added. Several weeks after completing round 1, we will email participants the results of this round together with the round 2 questionnaire. They will be asked to review the round 1 results and complete the round 2 survey. This survey will be shorter since it will only contain items on which the Delphi participants disagreed at round 1. If consensus is not reached for all items in round 2, we will proceed with a third round in the same manner as the second. The Delphi survey will be administered via Lime Surveys, which ensures that data are entered into a secure, web database that complies with Provincial and Federal regulatory requirements through any browser. Lime Surveys also offers several affordances, including dashboards, reporting and data extraction functionalities.

Delphi steering committee

We will assemble a steering committee to review and interpret the results of each Delphi round. This committee will consist of academics, clinicians and educators from occupational therapy, physical therapy, nursing, social work, implementation science and health professions education. We will choose them based on their recognised expertise in digital health, measurement, evaluation, health policy research and LHSs. The committee will be responsible for meeting in between Delphi rounds to discuss the findings and make decisions about which items needed to be reworded for clarity and retested in subsequent rounds.

Participant recruitment

Participant recruitment will occur in Spring of 2024. A purposive sampling strategy24 will be used to recruit a panel of participants with expertise or interests in LHS who are involved in healthcare settings across Canada and internationally. We intend to broadly recruit participants who represent diverse settings (eg, academia, healthcare services, advocacy, policy) and roles (eg, researchers, clinicians, managers, decision-makers and patients and/or caregivers) within healthcare. Notably, we have identified approximately 100 people with LHS expertise or experience based on their recent publications, research activities and academic or professional affiliations. Specifically, participants were identified through key informant recommendations, environmental/website scans of organisations working with LHS, searching Scopus for recent publications and using existing provincial and national research and policy networks. We will contact potential participants using personalised emails from the study principal investigator (SA), with the goal of recruiting approximately 40 participants from local, national and international organisations to participate in this Delphi study. While the recommendations for specific sample sizes are unclear in the context of Delphi studies, a key objective is to ensure the stability of responses over the three rounds.25 We derived this sample size from the literature that suggests an ideal Delphi sample consists of between 20 and 40 people26 and opted to try to include more people than recommended to help mitigate any participant attrition over the three rounds. Decision-makers in clinical settings will be invited to share the invitation to participate with members of their organisation whom they believe to have LHS-specific knowledge or experience for additional snowball sampling.24 No participant incentives will be offered during this study.

Data collection

Data collection for the Delphi will take place between Spring and Summer of 2024. We will contact participants and provide them with an information letter and informed consent form. Data collection will occur in three rounds. We will follow a modified version of Dillman’s Tailored Design Method (depicted in table 1) to help ensure the stability of responses, which will include multiple follow-ups to the Delphi participants. Specifically, we will follow-up with participants on the 7th and 14th day of the data collection period for each Delphi round.27 28 This approach is known to achieve high response rates27 and will, therefore, be used to help mitigate potential participant attrition. Completion of the survey will signify consent to participate in this Delphi study. The survey will take participants approximately 60 min to complete in round 1 and 30 min in rounds 2 and 3; all questions will be optional, and participants may choose to skip any questions they do not wish to answer. Participants who do not respond to the preparation phase and round 1 surveys will be removed from the Delphi. All data collection tools, documents and communications will be in both English and French.

Modified version of Dillman’s tailored design method

Round 1

In the first round, a link to the survey—comprising the readiness questionnaire, open-response questions and space for additional comments—will be sent to all participants. The survey will be open for 14 days. The purpose of this round is to reach consensus on the domains, subdomains and items included in the LHS readiness questionnaire. Participants will be asked to assess the relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness of the content of the LHS readiness questionnaire on a 3-point scale as well as its overall organisation and structure. Participants will also be asked to suggest changes (eg, merging items) or recommend additional concepts that could be captured by the tool. The survey will close after 2 weeks, at which point the research team and Delphi expert committee will perform a preliminary analysis of the aggregated survey results to identify which items achieved consensus (~70% agreement, in line with other Delphi studies20 21 29). The research team will aggregate all findings and make decisions about what items meet the criteria regarding clarity, comprehensiveness and relevance and can, therefore, be included in the final LHS readiness questionnaire and removed from future Delphi rounds. Items that require further review—and new items added in round 1—will be rated using the round 2 Delphi survey.

Round 2

In the second round, participants will receive a summary of the round 1 findings, including the descriptive statistics (ie, mean, median, SD, IQR) for each domain, subdomain and item for which there was no consensus. They will also receive a table of substantive changes made to the LHS questionnaire based on round 1 results and a link to the round 2 survey—comprising the items that did not reach consensus in round 1, open-response questions and space for additional comments. The survey will be open for 14 days. This second round will be conducted to determine agreement on the items revised based on the round 1 results and determine agreement on the new or modified items generated in round 1. We also aim to solicit any additional comments and feedback from participants. As with round 1, the survey will close after 2 weeks, and the research team will conduct an analysis of the aggregated survey results to identify which items achieved consensus. Items that have achieved consensus will be identified for inclusion in the final questionnaire; items that did not achieve consensus will be integrated into the survey presented to participants in the final round of this Delphi study.

Round 3

In the third round, participants will be provided with a summary of round 2 findings and a link to the survey—comprising the items that did not reach consensus in round 2, open-response questions and space for additional comments. The survey will be open for 14 days. The purpose of this round is to aim to achieve 70% consensus on all remaining domains, subdomains and items of the LHS readiness questionnaire. Similar to the previous two rounds, round 3 will close after 2 weeks, and the results will be analysed and discussed by the research team before being shared with the Delphi participants.

Data analysis

The questionnaires for each round of the Delphi will consist of both closed-ended and open-ended responses, which will be analysed separately after which the quantitative and qualitative findings will be integrated.30 To analyse the quantitative data, we will use Microsoft Excel to calculate descriptive statistics on all closed-ended responses; we will specifically calculate mean, median, SD, IQR and per cent agreement for each item in each of the three Delphi rounds. These analyses will help provide an overview of the extent to which participants agree or disagree about each item in the questionnaire. To analyse the qualitative data, we will also conduct a conventional content analysis on all open-ended responses using MAXQDA (V.24.1.0).31 In a conventional content analysis, researchers avoid using preconceived categories, coding inductively from the data instead.31 We will engage in multiple cycles of inductive coding and analysis, drawing on Miles et al’s32 approach to qualitative data analysis, wherein we will summarise sections of data using descriptive, process and in vivo coding, write memos of our initial codes and impressions and group codes into categories or constructs using matrices or displays. Doing so will help us to identify potential relationships or themes in the data. All survey responses will remain confidential; participants will not know who else is participating in the Delphi. Participants will be assigned a unique identifier during the preparation phase and this unique identifier will be maintained throughout all Delphi rounds. Only two postdoctoral fellows and a research associate—and not the principal investigators—will have access to the information linking participant identities to their unique identifiers. Being able to connect participant responses to their demographics will enable us to answer all three research questions. We will report all study components using the ACCORD framework for consensus studies.

Trustworthiness

We will ensure that our qualitative analyses are credible, dependable, confirmable and transferable.3335 We will use overlapping methods, facilitating the triangulation of findings. Moreover, we will use peer debriefing, wherein we meet in analytic teams and discuss our findings, interpretations and impressions throughout the research process. We will also engage in memoing throughout the Delphi process and analysis, thereby creating an audit trail. Finally, we will consider all rival explanations in our analyses.35

Ethics and dissemination

This study has received Research Ethics Board approval from McGill University’s Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (AO3-E23-24B). Regarding data security, participant demographic information provided in the preparation phase will be stored separately from their survey data in a password-protected electronic file on the McGill server, using the principal investigator’s secured Microsoft Teams platform. Only the principal investigator, two postdoctoral fellows, and a research associate will have access to this platform and the data stored therein. Results of this study will be disseminated using peer-reviewed publications, presentations at institutional research rounds and through presentations at national or international conferences. We will also draft a position paper to make the findings of this study publicly available to interested parties (eg, health system partners, patients and the public).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and caregivers provided feedback on early versions of our LHS readiness assessment but were not involved in the development of this Delphi protocol.

Anticipated outcomes

This study will directly contribute to health services and policy research by generating evidence in support of the content validity of a questionnaire that can help healthcare organisations determine their level of readiness to implement an LHS. Moreover, the findings of this study have implications for the broader health systems, since the main goal of an LHS is to improve organisational learning across a health centre’s clinical, operational and research activities to achieve high value and quality healthcare.14 36 By helping organisations better assess their level of readiness to implement an LHS, we can help position them to achieve the quintuple aim, which means to provide care that focuses on patient needs, population health, patient and health professional care experience, health equity, and health-related costs.9 10

This Delphi is one step in a series of validation assessments. As next steps, we will engage in cognitive interviewing and focus groups with intended users to ensure applicability to their specific contexts (e.g., within a Canadian family medicine context, within specific rehabilitation hospitals in the province of Quebec, Canada, etc). We will also undertake implementation studies across a wide variety of settings and work to validate and refine our LHS readiness tool in a variety of contexts.
