A composite approach for Tamai zone I fingertip replantation in the absence of suitable veins: a case report
Authors: Dung Van Chu, Hieu Trung Nguyen, Vien Cao Nguyen, Hung Phuoc Do
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFingertip amputation at Tamai Zone I remains technically challenging due to extremely small vessel size and the frequent absence of suitable veins for venous anastomosis. Venous congestion is a major cause of failure, and various strategies have been proposed to address limited venous outflow. We report a 28-year-old man with a complete Tamai Zone I amputation of the left index fingertip following a motorcycle chain injury. Replantation was completed approximately 8 h after injury. As no suitable vein for conventional venous anastomosis, a composite microsurgical strategy was implemented, integrating multiple coordinated components: arterial repair, direct arteriovenous (AV) anastomosis, controlled ligation of the distal palmar arterial arch, nail plate removal, and short-term postoperative anticoagulation. At one-year follow-up, the replanted fingertip survived completely with satisfactory aesthetic and functional outcomes. The nail regrew with minimal deformity, the distal interphalangeal joint regained full range of motion, and sensory recovery was satisfactory with a two-point discrimination of 7 mm. This case demonstrates the feasibility of a composite microsurgical approach for Tamai Zone I fingertip replantation in the absence of suitable veins. However, given the combined nature of the interventions, the relative contribution of each component cannot be determined, and further studies are required to validate this strategy.

Introduction

Fingertip amputation is one of the most common hand injuries encountered in emergency care and hand surgery. Treatment options range from conservative management and local skin flaps to revision amputation and microsurgical replantation, depending on the severity of the injury, tissue viability, ischemia time, and patient-related factors.1–3 Among these options, fingertip replantation offers the theoretical advantages of preserving finger length, nail structure, sensation, and aesthetic appearance. Therefore, it has the potential to recover superior functional outcomes and patient-reported results when successful.3

According to Tamai’s classification, Zone I amputations occur distal to the base of the nail or the lunula and represent the most distal level of finger amputation.4 Replantation at this level requires high technical proficiency because the vessel diameters are extremely small, veins are often absent or severely damaged, and the risk of postoperative venous congestion is very high.5 Consequently, venous insufficiency remains the most common cause of failure in Tamai Zone I fingertip replantation.6–10 To address the challenge of venous flow, several strategies have been described, including artery-only replantation with external bleeding, delayed venous anastomosis, medical leech therapy, and the creation of AV shunts.8,11,12 Smith and colleagues were the first to propose AV shunting as a solution for the lack of venous drainage, suggesting that early AV patency and subsequent neovascularization could maintain the replanted digit in cases where direct venous repair is not possible.6

Subsequent studies have expanded on this concept. Hsu et al. introduced the arterial branching and Y-graft technique to establish both inflow and outflow through arterial channels in Tamai Zone I replantation, reporting reliable survival rates and acceptable functional results.13 More recently, Wu et al. reported a large series of 45 Tamai Zone I fingertip reconstructions using AV anastomosis for venous drainage, achieving a survival rate of approximately 91%, even in cases of crush and avulsion injuries.14

Despite these advances, the indications for reconstruction in cases of complete Tamai Zone I amputation remain controversial. Many surgeons still prioritize revision amputation or local flap reconstruction due to concerns regarding technical complexity, operative time, and unpredictable outcomes.15 Therefore, detailed case reports remain valuable for illustrating the decision-making process, microsurgical strategies, and clinical outcomes in such challenging scenarios. We present a case of successful replantation of a complete amputation of the left index fingertip in Tamai Zone I, highlighting the surgical rationale, vascular strategy, and postoperative results within the context of the existing literature.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old right-hand dominant male (born in 1996) presented to the emergency department in 2024 following a domestic accident involving a motorcycle chain, which caused a crush-cut injury to his left hand. The time from injury to admission was approximately four hours, and the amputated fingertip was properly preserved in cold storage immediately after the accident.

Initial examination

Left index finger: Complete fingertip amputation at Tamai zone I, accompanied by a distal phalanx fracture. Left middle finger: A crush-cut injury with an open distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint dislocation and extensor tendon rupture. However, the deep flexor tendon remained intact and digital perfusion was stable (Figure 1). X-rays confirmed the distal phalanx fracture of the left index finger and the open DIP joint dislocation of the left middle finger.

Complete fingertip amputation at Tamai zone I of the left index finger, and open distal interphalangeal joint dislocation and extensor tendon rupture of the middle finger.

Surgical management

Assessment of the amputated part of the left index finger showed moderate soft tissue crushing but remained viable for preservation. During microsurgical debridement and exploration, the distal palmar arterial arch with two collateral arteries and three terminal banches (marked as 1, 2, 3 from ulnar to radial side, respectively) were identified (Figure 2A and 2B). However, no suitable veins were found for anastomosis.

The radial collateral artery of the distal palmar arterial arch was directly anastomosed to the proximal radial palmar digital vein to establish venous outflow (AV anastomosis).

(C) Completion of the arterial repair at the ulnar side of the left index finger.

(D) Final intraoperative appearance following completion of arterial repair, AV anastomosis, distal palmar arterial arch ligation, and nail plate removal. The fingertip appears pink and no clinical signs of venous congestion.

Surgical management was performed under general anesthesia with tourniquet control. After considering various treatment options—including revision amputation, local flap coverage, and microsurgical replantation—the surgical team decided to proceed with microsurgical replantation of the index fingertip. This decision was based on the patient’s young age, the well-preserved amputated part, the short ischemia time, and the desire to preserve finger length, nail aesthetics, and function. The middle finger was managed during the same operative session, stabilized with a 1.2 mm Kirschner wire across the DIP joint, followed by extensor tendon repair and skin closure.

The index finger: After debridement of crushed tissue, a mid-lateral longitudinal incision was made along both the radial and ulnar aspects of the index finger, extending proximally from the level of the amputation margin to allow adequate exposure of the neurovascular bundles. Dissection was carried out under magnification using a combination of sharp and blunt techniques. The ulnar and radial palmar digital arteries and accompanying veins were identified within the neurovascular bundle and carefully mobilized over a sufficient length to allow tension-free anastomosis. Perivascular tissue was gently dissected, and the adventitia was trimmed under the microscope to optimize vessel preparation while avoiding spasm or intimal injury. The amputated fingertip was replanted and stabilized using two 18-gauge needles inserted through the surrounding soft tissue. The positioning was guided by the lateral nail folds on both sides to ensure proper alignment and prevent rotational deformity. Direct fixation through the distal phalanx was avoided due to its very small size and fragility, which could increase the risk of bone damage. The proximal ulnar palmar digital artery was anastomosed to the ulnar collateral artery of the distal palmar arterial arch using 10-0 nylon sutures under a × 20 operating microscope (Figure 2C). After arterial repair, strong pulsatile flow with active bleeding was observed at the opposite end of the arterial arch. We concerned that preferential flow through this pathway could compromise distal perfusion. Therefore, complete ligation of the distal palmar arterial arch was performed at the level between its terminal branch 2 and 3 (Figure 2B) to interrupt direct shunting while preserving capillary perfusion via the branching network. After ligation, bleeding from the radial collateral artery of the distal palmar arterial arch persisted but at a reduced, non-pulsatile rate, suggesting redistribution of flow through the capillary bed. As no suitable vein was identified, the radial collateral artery of the distal palmar arterial arch was directly anastomosed to the proximal radial palmar digital vein to establish venous outflow (AV anastomosis). The nail plate was removed to allow external bleeding in case of venous outflow insufficiency, and the risk of postoperative congestion. The procedure was completed approximately 8 h after injury. The fingertip remained pink with good capillary refill and no signs of venous congestion (Figure 2D).

Postoperative monitoring and results

Postoperative perfusion was closely monitored for the first 72 h, with no signs of ischemia or venous congestion (Figure 3A). A radiograph obtained on postoperative day 2 demonstrated that the fracture was well reduced and adequately stabilized (Figure 3B). The patient received postoperative anticoagulation consisting of aspirin (81 mg/day) and continuous intravenous heparin infusion (15,000 IU/24 h) for one day. Coagulation facilitated external bleeding from the nail bed following nail plate removal, thereby contributing to temporary venous decompression during the initial postoperative period.4 Moreover, it may reduce the risk of microvascular thrombosis, particularly in the setting of small-caliber vessels and crush–cut injury.5 External bleeding at the nail bed was observed during the first 24 h, contributing to venous decompression as part of the composite strategy. The total length of hospital stay was 7 days. Radiographic evaluation at 6 weeks postoperatively demonstrated bone union of the distal phalanx of the index finger (Figure 3C).

(B) Radiograph on postoperative day 2 demonstrating stable alignment of the distal phalanx.

(D) One year postoperative outcome demonstrating complete survival of the replanted fingertip with satisfactory aesthetic and functional results.

At one year follow-up, the fingertip demonstrated complete survival with satisfactory aesthetic and functional outcomes. The nail regrew with acceptable deformity, resulting in a satisfactory cosmetic appearance. The patient achieved full range of motion of the distal interphalangeal joint (Figure 3D). Sensory examination revealed preserved light touch and temperature perception, with a two-point discrimination of 7 mm, representing functionally acceptable recovery. Cold intolerance was assessed using the Cold Intolerance Symptom Severity (CISS) questionnaire, with a total score of 0, indicating no cold intolerance.

Discussion

Microsurgical fingertip replantation at Tamai zone I remains technically challenging due to the extremely small vessel caliber and, in particular, the difficulty in establishing adequate venous outflow.6–9 Venous congestion is widely recognized as the leading cause of failure in distal fingertip replantation.16

In the present case, no suitable vein was identified for conventional venous anastomosis, a situation frequently encountered in Tamai zone I injuries and one that often leads to revision amputation or local reconstruction. However, these alternatives may result in loss of finger length, nail deformity, and reduced patient satisfaction, particularly in young patients requiring fine motor function.4,17

Compared with local reconstruction, microsurgical replantation has been associated with superior aesthetic outcomes and higher patient satisfaction, while functional outcomes may be comparable.3 This supports consideration of replantation even in distal injuries when technically feasible.

The core issue in these cases remains venous drainage. Several strategies have been described, including artery-only replantation with external bleeding, leech therapy, or delayed venous repair. Although these approaches can achieve acceptable survival rates, they often require intensive postoperative monitoring, carry a risk of infection, and place a significant burden on patient care.1,8

In this case, successful replantation was achieved using a composite microsurgical strategy, including arterial inflow repair, direct AV anastomosis, controlled arterial arch ligation, nail plate removal, and short-term postoperative anticoagulation. Importantly, the favorable outcome should be interpreted as the result of this combined approach rather than the isolated effect of any single component. In situations where no suitable vein is available for conventional repair, direct AV anastomosis has been proposed as an alternative method to facilitate venous outflow. It has been reported in distal fingertip replantation as one component of strategies aimed at maintaining circulation when venous anastomosis is not feasible.6,10,14 The creation of an AV shunt raises theoretical concerns regarding excessive shunting and potential ‘steal’ phenomena. Specifically, direct artery-to-vein flow may bypass the nutrient capillary network, and preferential low-resistance flow through the shunt could compromise tissue perfusion.6,7 Based on intraoperative observations following arterial repair, controlled ligation of the distal palmar arterial arch was performed with the intention of limiting excessive shunting. However, this decision was based on subjective assessment, and no direct hemodynamic measurements of tissue perfusion were available. Therefore, the proposed effect remains hypothetical and requires further investigation.

As part of the composite strategy, external bleeding induced by nail plate removal combined with anticoagulation has contributed to early venous decompression. In distal fingertip replantation without a suitable vein, external bleeding has been described as a standardized salvage strategy. Partial removal of the nail plate exposes the vascular nail bed, creating a temporary outlet for venous drainage through its rich capillary network, while systemic and/or topical anticoagulation helps maintain continuous bleeding by preventing clot formation.16,18 This mechanism can support transient venous decompression during the early postoperative period until neovascularization develops, typically within 5–7 days.19

Additionally, in this case, the distal phalanx fragment was very small, and only 1.2 mm Kirschner wires were available, making conventional fixation technically challenging and potentially harmful to the bone. Therefore, two 18 G needles were used for temporary soft-tissue-based stabilization to maintain alignment and prevent rotation. Although unconventional, this method provided sufficient stability, as evidenced by satisfactory bone union at 6 weeks postoperatively (Figure 3C). Similar technical challenges in distal phalanx fixation have been reported in fingertip replantation, where alternative stabilization methods may be required.8

Clinically, the patient achieved complete fingertip survival with full distal interphalangeal range of motion, satisfactory nail regrowth. Sensory recovery was functionally acceptable, with a two-point discrimination of 7 mm. Although this value is slightly inferior to normal fingertip sensibility (typically 2–5 mm), it falls within the range reported in successful fingertip replantation and is considered clinically meaningful.4

This report has several limitations. First, this is a single case, and the observed outcome may not be generalizable. Second, the contribution of each individual component of the combined strategy—including AV anastomosis, arterial arch ligation, nail plate removal, and systemic anticoagulation—cannot be independently determined. Third, quantitative intraoperative vessel measurements and objective hemodynamic data were not available. Finally, standardized functional outcome measures were limited.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that successful replantation of a complete Tamai Zone I fingertip amputation is feasible even in the absence of suitable veins for conventional venous anastomosis. In this patient, a composite microsurgical approach—including direct AV anastomosis, controlled arterial arch ligation, nail plate removal, and postoperative anticoagulation—was associated with stable microcirculation and complete survival of the replanted fingertip. At one year, satisfactory functional and aesthetic outcomes were achieved, including full distal interphalangeal joint motion, acceptable nail regrowth, and functionally meaningful sensory recovery. However, given the combined nature of the interventions and the single-case design, the relative contribution of each component cannot be determined. This report therefore supports the feasibility of this approach rather than its definitive efficacy, and further studies are required to validate its role in Tamai Zone I fingertip replantation.
