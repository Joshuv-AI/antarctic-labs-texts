Systematic screening of archaeal MazF homologs reveals Tth-MazF1, a versatile, sequence-specific ribonuclease from Thermococcus thioreducens
Authors: Benjamin F Jepson, Eric J Wolf, Anthony R Dawson, Jingmin Jin, Vladimir Potapov, Nan Dai, Ivan R Corrêa Jr., Erbay Yigit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNucleotide- and sequence-specific ribonucleases are essential tools for dissecting RNA structure, modification, and composition, yet the enzymatic repertoire available for high-resolution RNA analysis remains limited. Here, we systematically screened 186 MazF homologs, including 164 from archaeal organisms, and identified 132 active endoribonucleases with diverse sequence preferences. Using a semi-high-throughput expression and purification workflow combined with an Illumina-based RNA-seq assay, we defined recognition motifs for 90 enzymes, uncovering a striking diversity of UG-rich cleavage specificities across the archaeal domain. Among these, Tth-MazF1, a thermostable endoribonuclease from the hyperthermophilic archaeon Thermococcus thioreducens, displayed robust and specific cleavage at U↓GN motifs across a broad range of temperatures (4°C–85°C). Tth-MazF1 enabled high sequence coverage of 0.8–8.9 kb messenger RNA (mRNA) transcripts by LC-MS/MS and direct detection of key structural features, including the 5′ cap, poly(A) tail, and mass-altering epitranscriptomic modifications in ribosomal RNA. Tolerance of Tth-MazF1 to high temperatures, salt, and chemical modifications highlights it as a powerful enzymatic tool for analyzing chemically modified and structured RNAs. These findings position archaeal MazF proteins as a rich source of customizable RNases for biotechnology applications, such as mass spectrometry-based analysis of RNA-based vaccines and therapeutics as well as cellular RNAs.

Introduction

Toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems are prevalent amongst bacteria and archaea, where many organisms encode multiple TA loci [1]. Amongst the most well-studied of the TA systems is MazEF, composed of a MazF toxin and a cognate MazE inhibitor antitoxin. The MazF toxin in each of these systems typically encodes a sequence-specific endoribonuclease that recognizes and cleaves a defined RNA motif, the length of which generally ranges from three to seven bases [2]. Without any stressors, these MazF toxins are inhibited by their tightly bound protein antitoxins, MazE. However, under cellular stress conditions, such as starvation or during phage infection, the less stable MazE is readily degraded by ClpAP serine protease [3] and releases the MazF toxin to cleave RNA molecules containing the recognition sequence of interest. The first MazF was discovered in Escherichia coli, where it primarily cleaves N↓ACA sequences in RNA (↓ indicates cleavage site) [2]. Bacterial MazEF systems have been shown to play roles in a broad range of biological processes, including microbial pathogenesis, antibiotic drug resistance, production of biofilms, plasmid maintenance, resistance to phage, and cellular responses to stress conditions [4].

Multiple applications have exploited the sequence-specific cleavage activity of E. coli MazF (Eco-MazF). Notably, two independent studies harnessed the sensitivity of Eco-MazF to N6-methyladenosine (m6A) to profile m6A modification landscapes transcriptome-wide without reliance on antibody-based enrichment strategies [5, 6]. In a separate approach, a Single-Protein Production system was developed, in which ACA-less messenger RNA (mRNA) and inducible expression of MazF in E. coli enabled selective translation of the protein of interest [7]. Additional in vivo uses of MazF include protease-triggered activation of MazF from inactive MazE–MazF complex to cleave specific mRNA [8], as well as the inhibition of viral replication through conditional expression of MazF in immune cells [9].

Eco-MazF has recently emerged as a valuable tool for bottom-up analysis of RNA by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) [10], which has been widely applied to the precise mapping of internal chemical modifications in transfer RNA (tRNA) [11, 12], ribosomal RNA (rRNA) [13, 14], and the characterization of RNA-based therapeutics and vaccines [10, 15–18]. These analyses typically rely upon a toolkit of nucleotide or dinucleotide-specific RNases to produce a population of oligonucleotide fragments compatible with mass spectrometry-based detection and quantification. Recent efforts have significantly expanded this enzymatic toolkit through the application of RNases with distinct nucleotide or sequence preferences—such as human RNase 4 (U↓R), MC1 (H↓U), and Eco-MazF (N↓ACA) [10, 15, 19]—as well as through the use of immobilized RNase variants for a controlled and tunable digestion, including RNase T1 (G↓N) and RNase A (Y↓N) [16, 20].

However, two major challenges remain incompletely addressed in bottom-up RNA LC-MS/MS workflows. First, many RNases used in these analyses are highly sensitive to RNA secondary and tertiary structures, making it difficult to achieve complete digestion of structurally complex RNA species such as rRNA and tRNA. Second, the sequence selectivity of existing RNases—often limited to nucleotide or dinucleotide motifs—hinders comprehensive sequence coverage of long RNA molecules. In addition to these structural and sequence-related constraints, RNA modifications—both natural and synthetic—can further impede RNase cleavage, either by directly altering recognition sites or by influencing local RNA folding. As a result, bottom-up characterization of long RNA substrates, including viral genomes, circular RNA, or self-amplifying RNA therapeutics remains challenging. We envision that an RNase capable of overcoming these limitations would ideally be thermostable or stable enough to resist a chemical that denatures RNA, such as formamide or urea, allowing for digestion under denaturing conditions to minimize structural interference, and possess a defined cleavage preference spanning 2–3 bases to generate analyzable fragments conducive to robust sequencing. While bacterial MazF proteins have been extensively studied, archaeal MazF homologs offer a largely untapped source of functional and mechanistic diversity. Archaea often inhabit extreme environments, and their enzymes, including MazF toxins, likely adapted to function in extreme conditions, such as high temperature and salinity, providing potential advantages for applications requiring robust performance under harsh conditions [21]. Moreover, a couple of known MazF toxins from Archaea, Mev–MazF (Y↓UGGU) [22] and Hwa–MazF (UU↓ACUCA) [23], suggests that these enzymes may recognize distinct RNA motifs compared to their bacterial counterparts.

To this end, we computationally identified and developed a high-throughput sequencing-based experimental screening approach to examine the cleavage selectivity of a diverse group of archaeal MazF proteins. From this initial screen, we identified and performed detailed biochemical characterization of a novel U↓GN-cleaving thermostable MazF protein from the hyperthermophilic archaeon Thermococcus thioreducens (Tth-MazF1) with an optimal growth temperature of 83°C–85°C [24]. We next investigated the use of Tth-MazF1 for bottom-up LC-MS/MS characterization of mRNAs ranging from 0.8 to 8.9 kb, and furthermore, mapped epitranscriptomic modifications in E. coli rRNA extracts. Collectively, we demonstrated that Tth-MazF1 is a useful and versatile tool for the characterization of diverse RNA substrates by LC-MS/MS, helping to overcome previously noted challenges in RNA analysis and epitranscriptomic modification mapping.

Materials and methods

BLAST search and clustering of archaeal MazF homologs

To identify homologs of archaeal MazF proteins, BLAST searches were performed using the amino acid sequences of 11 MazF family proteins described in [25] as queries. Searches were conducted against the NCBI non-redundant protein sequence (nr) database, restricted to the archaeal taxon (NCBI Taxonomy ID: 2157). The BLASTP algorithm was used with default parameters, except that the maximum number of target sequences was increased to 5000 to capture a broader range of potential homologs. Search results were downloaded, manually curated, and further analyzed to assess sequence conservation and diversity across archaeal lineages. The resulting protein sequences were aggregated and subjected to empirical clustering based on pairwise sequence identity. Sequences sharing a minimum of 25% global identity were grouped into the same cluster, resulting in 91 distinct clusters. This clustering approach was used to capture the diversity of MazF homologs across archaeal lineages while ensuring meaningful sequence similarity within clusters. Representative proteins from selected clusters were expressed, purified, and used for further comparative and functional analyses.

Low-throughput expression and purification of MBP-MazF-6×His fusion proteins

Candidate genes were cloned into expression vectors as described in the section “semi-high-throughput expression and purification of maltose-binding protein (MBP)-MazF-6×His proteins.” T7 Express competent E. coli cells (NEB, C1013I) were transformed with 10 ng of each plasmid and plated on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/ml). Plates were incubated overnight at 30°C. Single colonies were then used to inoculate 5 ml LB medium containing 50 µg/ml kanamycin in culture tubes and grown overnight at 30°C.

The following day, 20 ml to 1 l of rich LB medium was inoculated at a 1:100 dilution with the subculture and grown at 30°C until reaching an OD600 of ∼0.3, then temperature was set to 16°C and cells were grown more until OD600 reached 0.4–0.8. Protein expression was induced by adding IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) to a final concentration of 10 µM, followed by incubation at 16°C for 16–20 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Pellets were washed with 50 ml of ice-cold 1 × phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in three volumes (v/w) of column buffer (CB: 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl) supplemented with protease inhibitors (1 mM PMSF (Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), 0.5 nM leupeptin, 2.75 mM benzamidine, 2 nM pepstatin). Cell suspensions were stored at –20°C until purification.

For lysis, thawed cell suspensions were sonicated on ice and centrifuged at 21 000 × g for 45 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected and filtered using a 0.8 µm syringe filter (Cytiva, 4618). Amylose resin (NEB, E8021) was packed into a 10 ml disposable gravity column (Bio-Rad) and equilibrated with 15 ml of column binding buffer (CB) containing protease inhibitors. Filtered lysates were applied to the column, and MBP-tagged proteins were allowed to bind the resin via gravity flow. The column was washed with 20 ml of wash buffer 1 [WB1: 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)], followed by an additional wash with 15 ml of CB. Bound proteins were eluted with 15 ml of elution buffer 1 (EB1: 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl, 10 mM maltose), and eluates were stored at 4°C until further purification.

For secondary purification, a His GraviTrap TALON column (Cytiva) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and equilibrated with 10 ml of CB. The eluate from the previous purification was loaded directly onto the column. The column was washed with 10 ml of WB2 (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl, 5 mM imidazole), and proteins were eluted with 3 ml of elution buffer 2 (EB2: 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl, 150 mM imidazole). The eluate was dialyzed at 4°C in two successive steps against 1 l of storage buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50% glycerol, pH 7.4) using 3 500 MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer cassettes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 66333). Protein purity was assessed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and staining with SimplyBlue SafeStain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, LC6065).

Semi-high-throughput expression and purification of MBP-MazF-6×His proteins

Candidate genes were commercially synthesized (Twist Bioscience) and cloned into a custom-designed periplasmic expression plasmid under the control of an T7 promoter. The plasmid encodes an N-terminal MBP with its native signal sequence, followed by a thrombin cleavage site, the gene of interest, and a C-terminal 6×His tag (Supplementary Fig. S2A). NiCo21(DE3) E. coli cells (NEB, C2529H) were transformed with 10 ng of each expression plasmid and plated on LB agar containing kanamycin (50 µg/ml) and incubated overnight at 30°C. The following day, single colonies were used to inoculate 5 ml of Dynamite medium supplemented with 40 mM IPTG and 80 µg/ml kanamycin in 14 ml culture tubes. Cultures were grown at 30°C for 40 h with shaking at 300 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 15 min), washed once with 1 × PBS, transferred to 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes, and pelleted at 16 000 × g for 2 min. Pellets were either processed immediately or stored at –20°C. For lysis, each pellet was resuspended in 0.8 ml lysis buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5) supplemented with protease inhibitors (1 mM PMSF, 0.5 nM leupeptin, 275 µM benzamidine, 2 nM pepstatin) and lysed on ice using a multi-tip sonicator. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (22 000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), and the soluble fraction was transferred to deep-well 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher). For affinity purification, 20 μl of pre-washed magnetic amylose beads (NEB, E8035S) were added to each well, and plates were incubated at 4°C for 1 h with shaking at 1000 rpm. Beads were then processed on a KingFisher Flex purification system (Thermo Fisher). Magnetic beads were washed twice with wash buffer (1 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5) and once with lysis buffer, followed by elution in 100 μl elution buffer (10 mM maltose, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5). Purity of the eluted proteins was assessed by SDS–PAGE and Coomassie staining. Samples were stored at 4°C for short-term use or mixed with 50% glycerol and stored at –20°C for long-term preservation. Optional second purification using the 6×His tag: Following amylose purification, recombinant proteins can be further purified using Ni-NTA magnetic beads (NEB, S1423). This step can be used for removing endogenous MBP or truncated protein carrying MBP from the protein preparations. However, as no functional differences were observed between enzymes purified with or without this additional step, we omitted the second purification in our high-throughput expression and purification experiments.

Large-scale expression and purification of 6×His-tagged MazF proteins

Codon-optimized MazF genes were synthesized (Twist Bioscience) and cloned into a custom-designed periplasmic expression plasmid under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter. The plasmid encodes an N-terminal DsbA signal peptide, followed by a linker, a 6×His affinity tag, and the gene of interest (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Competent E. coli cells (NEB, C2523) were transformed with the expression constructs and plated on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/ml). Single colonies were used to inoculate 25 ml starter cultures in 125 ml flasks containing LB medium and kanamycin (50 µg/ml), which were grown overnight at 37°C with shaking. The following day, 5 ml of starter culture was used to inoculate 200 ml of Dynamite medium (12 g tryptone, 24 g yeast extract, 6.3 ml glycerol, 3.8 g KH₂PO₄, 12.5 g K2HPO4, 5 g glucose, 0.195 g anhydrous MgSO4 per liter) in a 1 l flask, supplemented with kanamycin at 50 µg/ml (final concentration) and 0.2% (final concentration) arabinose (Thermo Scientific, 365180250). Cultures were incubated at 26°C for 40 h with shaking at 250 rpm.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, washed once in 50 ml of ice-cold 1 × PBS, and pelleted again under the same conditions. Pellets were resuspended in 10 ml of lysis/binding buffer [NEB, S1423: 21 ml 2 × IMAC buffer, 0.21 ml 2 M imidazole, 20.86 ml nuclease-free water, and 420 μl protease inhibitors (1 mM PMSF, 0.5 nM leupeptin, 275 mM benzamidine, 2 nM pepstatin)]. The resuspension was then transferred into a metal sonication beaker. An additional 5 ml of lysis buffer was used to collect the remaining liquid. Cell lysis was performed via sonication, and lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 21 000 × g for 50 min at 4°C. The supernatant for thermostable MazF was heat-treated at 75°C for 30 min in a water bath, followed by another clarification spin at 21 000 × g for 40 min at 4°C.

One hundred fifty microliters of Ni-NTA bead slurry (NEB, S1423) was pre-equilibrated by washing three times with 1 ml of lysis/binding buffer (NEB, S1423: 21 ml of 2 × IMAC Buffer + 0.21 ml of 2M Imidazole + 20.86 ml of nuclease free water + 420 μl of 100 × protease inhibitors [1 mM PMSF, 0.5 nM leupeptin, 275 mM benzamidine, 2 nM pepstatin]). Subsequently, the beads were incubated with the supernatant for 1 h at room temperature with gentle rotation. The beads were washed three times with 5 ml of wash buffer (NEB, S1423: 30 ml 2 × IMAC buffer, 0.6 ml 2 M imidazole, 29.4 ml nuclease-free water). The bound protein was eluted in 300 μl of elution buffer (0.5 ml 2 × IMAC buffer, 0.25 ml 2 M imidazole, 0.25 ml nuclease-free water).

Eluted protein was dialyzed overnight against 2 l storage buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50% glycerol, pH 7.4) using 3500 MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer cassettes (0.1–0.5 ml capacity; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 66333). Protein purity was confirmed by SDS–PAGE followed by staining with SimplyBlue SafeStain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, LC6065).

Assessment of MazF enzyme activity

To generate an RNA substrate that includes all 1024 possible 5-mer sequences, the curlcake software [26] was used with the following parameters: java -jar curlcake.jar 5 40 1 100 incomplete.txt 5mers.txt./RNAshapes 0. This command generated a library of unstructured 40-nt RNA fragments in which each 5-mer was represented at least once. A single RNA template for in vitro transcription (IVT) was created by randomly concatenating these 40-nt sequences, resulting in a 1.57 kb RNA. The corresponding DNA template was chemically synthesized (Twist Bioscience) and cloned into a pET28 vector using BamHI and HindIII restriction sites. This construct was designated “curlcake RNA-43.” The DNA template for the IVT reaction was generated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using forward and reverse T7 primers using Q5 PCR master mix (NEB, M0492) and purified (NEB, T1130S). The template DNA was in vitro transcribed using T7 RNA polymerase (NEB, E2040) following manufacturer’s recommendation at 37°C overnight, and template DNA was removed by DNase I (NEB, E2040S). The quality and purity of the resulting 1.57 kb RNA was assessed by gel electrophoresis, purified, and quantified by both Nanodrop and Qubit devices to ensure the absence of contaminating NTPs.

Approximately 0.7 pmol (400 ng) of curlcake RNA-43 was digested with 1 μl of each MazF in the presence of 10 Units of murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, M0314S) for 30 min at 37°C in 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0) in a total volume of 10 μl. Due to the low concentration of purified MazF proteins, 1 μl was used per reaction. Cleavage activity and frequency were qualitatively assessed using 6% Novex TBE-Urea gels (Thermo Fisher, EC68655).

Identifying recognition motifs of MazF proteins by Illumina sequencing

To identify recognition motifs of putative MazF enzymes, a chemically synthesized chimeric RNA library (IDT) containing all 16 384 possible 7-mer sequences, flanked by short linkers and Illumina adapter sequences, was used as a substrate (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S3: R347). A total of 0.4 pmol of the 7-mer RNA library (R347) was digested in 1× MazF cleavage buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5) using 2 μl of purified MazF enzyme at 37°C for 30 min in a 20 μl reaction volume. The exact amount of purified enzymes used could not be quantified due to its low concentration. Reactions were terminated by adding seven volumes of DNA Cleanup Binding Buffer, and the digested R347 library was purified using the Monarch PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T1030) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified library molecules were eluted in 10 μl of nuclease-free water and stored at −20°C until further use. All digestion reactions were performed at 37°C, except for enzymes originating from thermophilic or hyperthermophilic organisms, which were incubated at their respective optimal growth temperatures.

(B) Enrichment scores and WebLogo of the Eco-MazF recognition motif, ACA. The five most enriched 7-mer sequences containing ACA motifs are labeled. (C) Phylogenetic tree of predicted MazF homologs based on protein sequences, annotated with their associated RNA cleavage motifs. Highly conserved core motifs are shown. Branch colors indicate the taxonomic and ecological origin of the proteins: blue branches represent bacterial homologs, red branches indicate homologs from hyperthermophilic organisms, and black branches correspond to archaeal homologs from non-hyperthermophilic environments.

The digested library products were subsequently converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) as follows: 1.5 μl of 0.8 µM library substrate was combined with 0.75 μl of 0.8 µM D575 PCR primer, 1 μl of 10 ×Isothermal Amplification Buffer II (200 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM (NH4)2SO4, 1500 mM KCl, 20 mM MgSO4, 1% Tween 20, pH 8.8 at 25°C), an additional 0.6 μl 100 mM MgSO4, 1.4 μl 10 mM dNTP solution (NEB, N0447L), and 2.75 μl of water in a total volume of 8 μl. Samples were heated at 65°C for 3 min, cooled at room temperature for 10 min, and then supplemented with 0.4 μl of Bst3.0 polymerase. The reaction was incubated at 50°C for 30 min followed by inactivation at 80°C treatment for 5 min. The resulting cDNA served as the template for a subsequent PCR reaction. For indexing PCR, the reaction was prepared with 0.75 μl of 10 µM SR primer, 0.75 μl of 10 µM indexing primer, 5 μl of cDNA, 6 μl of water, 12.5 μl of 2× NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (NEB, M0544L) in total volume of 25 μl. The PCR reaction was run for eight cycles. PCR products were purified using a PCR & DNA purification kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions (NEB, T1030). Illumina libraries were validated using a TapeStation system and sequenced on a NextSeq instrument using a 2 × 15 cycle protocol.

Bioinformatic identification of MazF recognition motifs

Within each Illumina read, the 7-mer RNA insert, flanked by CAA and TAA DNA sequences (CAA–rNrNrNrNrNrNrN–TAA), was extracted. The frequency of each unique insert was calculated and normalized against a control library (untreated with RNase), which was verified to contain all 16 384 possible 7-mer permutations. For each insert, an enrichment score (E) was computed as: E = log10(Fcontrol/ Fsample), where Fcontrol and Fsample represent the relative frequency of a given insert in the control and MazF-treated samples, respectively. Positive enrichment scores indicate depletion (i.e. underrepresentation) of an insert in the treated sample, while negative values indicate enrichment (i.e. overrepresentation). The magnitude of E reflects the extent of depletion or enrichment.

To identify significantly depleted sequences, we determined the maximum enrichment score expected from random sampling of 7-mer sequences in our NGS assay. Sequences exceeding this threshold were selected for further analysis. These depleted sequences were compiled into a synthetic FASTA file, proportionally represented by enrichment score, and analyzed with the MEME suite to uncover shared motifs and generate sequence logos [27].

Characterization of Tth-MazF1 cleavage activity across a range of temperatures

A 5′ FAM-labeled RNA oligonucleotide containing a centrally located Tth-MazF1 cleavage motif was synthesized to monitor temperature-dependent cleavage activity (Supplementary Table S3: R508). Cleavage reactions were assembled by combining 1 μl of 10 µM RNA substrate (IDT), 1 μl of Tth-MazF1 at either 1 µM or 0.05 µM, 1 μl of 10× Tris buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5), 1 μl of murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, M0314), and 6 μl of nuclease-free water to a total volume of 10 μl. Reactions were incubated for 15 min at 4°C, 25°C, 37°C, 60°C, or 85°C. Following digestion, 1 μl of Proteinase K (NEB, P8107S) was added to each sample and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to inactivate the enzyme. Next, 10 μl of 2× RNA loading dye (NEB, B0363S) was added, and samples were heated at 70°C for 5 min. A total of 18 μl from each reaction was loaded onto a pre-cast 15% TBE-Urea polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed at 180 V for 60 min. Gels were imaged using the Cy2 setting on the Amersham Typhoon scanner. Cleaved RNA products migrated faster than the full-length substrate, enabling quantitative assessment of cleavage efficiency.

Characterization of Tth-MazF1 cleavage across a range of salt concentrations

A 5′ FAM-labeled RNA oligonucleotide containing a centrally located Tth-MazF1 cleavage site was synthesized to evaluate the effect of salt concentration on enzymatic activity (Supplementary Table S3: R508). Cleavage reactions were assembled by combining 1 μl of 10 µM RNA substrate (IDT), 1 μl of Tth-MazF1 at either 1, 0.1, or 0.05 µM, 1 μl of 10 × Tris buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5), and 1 μl of murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, M0314). Varying volumes of 5 M or 500 mM NaCl were added to achieve final salt concentrations ranging from 0 to 500 mM. Reactions were brought to a final volume of 10 μl with nuclease-free water and incubated at 85°C for 15 min. Following digestion, 10 μl of 2× RNA loading dye (NEB, B0363S) was added to each sample, which was then heated at 70°C for 5 min. A total of 18 μl from each reaction was loaded onto a pre-cast 15% TBE-Urea polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed at 180 V for 60 min. Gels were imaged using the Cy2 setting on the Amersham Typhoon scanner. Cleaved products migrated faster than the full-length RNA substrate, allowing for quantitative assessment of cleavage efficiency across the tested salt concentrations.

Characterization of Tth-MazF1 specificity by capillary electrophoresis

5′ FAM-labeled chimeric oligonucleotides were designed to contain varying lengths of 5′ DNA sequence upstream of RNA segments harboring different expected Tth-MazF1 cleavage motifs (Supplementary Table S3: R535–R539). These oligos were pooled to enable multiplex analysis, with each substrate distinguishable by its unique DNA length.

Specifically, 1.5 μl of 10 µM pooled oligonucleotides (IDT) were incubated with 1.5 μl of Tth-MazF1 at either 2 or 5 µM, 1.5 μl of murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, M0314), 1.5 μl of 200 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), and varying volumes of 5 M or 500 mM NaCl to achieve final NaCl concentrations ranging from 0 to 300 mM. Reactions were brought to a final volume of 15 μl with nuclease-free water and incubated at 85°C for 15 min. Following digestion, reactions were diluted 1:100 in nuclease-free water, and each sample was loaded in triplicate into a 96-well plate for analysis by capillary electrophoresis using POP-6 polymer and the LIZ 1.0 size standard.

Kinetic analysis of Tth-MazF1 specificity

To monitor cleavage kinetics, 15-nucleotide RNA substrates were synthesized with a 5′ FAM fluorophore and a 3′ BHQ1 quencher (Supplementary Table S3: R523–R527 and R685). Each of the six substrates contained a distinct Tth-MazF1 recognition motif centrally positioned within the sequence. In the intact substrate, the proximity of the fluorophore and quencher suppresses fluorescence. Upon cleavage by Tth-MazF1, the separation of the fluorophore and quencher results in increased fluorescence, enabling real-time monitoring of enzymatic activity.

Reaction mixtures were prepared by combining 3 μl of 25 μM synthetic RNA oligo from IDT (Integrated DNA Technologies) with 3 μl of Murine RNase Inhibitor (NEB M0314), 3 μl of 200 mM Tris (pH 7.5), and 6 μl of nuclease-free water (a total reaction volume of 15 μl). This mixture was then incubated in a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR machine (Bio-Rad) at 4°C for 20 min, capturing fluorescence output in the SYBR channel every 10 s. After 20 min, 15 μl of reaction mixture was added to each well containing 3 μl of various concentrations of ribonuclease (2.4, 2.2, 2, 1.8, 1.6, 1.4, 1.2, 1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, and 0.025 μM) and 12 μl of nuclease-free water. No-enzyme control wells were mixed with 15 μl of reaction mixture containing 3 μl of diluent H, no BSA and 12 μl of nuclease-free water. All wells were then incubated for another 30 min at 4°C capturing fluorescence output in the SYBR channel every 10 s.

A calibration curve was also performed on each plate. Synthetic RNA oligos from IDT (Integrated DNA Technologies) were designed for each of the cleavage products of the oligos used for kinetic analysis (Supplementary Table S3:R597, R598, and R600–R604). The two oligos corresponding to the cleavage products being tested on each plate were mixed in equimolar amounts (3, 1.5, 0.6, 0.3, 0.15, and 0.06 μM), and 5 μl of each mixture was incubated with 3 μl of Murine RNase Inhibitor (NEB M0314), 3 μl of 200 mM Tris (pH 7.5), and 4 μl of nuclease-free water (a total reaction volume of 15 μl). This mixture was then incubated in a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR machine (Bio-Rad) at 4°C for 20 min, capturing fluorescence output in the SYBR channel every 10 s. After 20 min, 15 μl of reaction mixture was added to each well containing 3 μl of diluent H (no BSA) and 12 μl of nuclease-free water. All wells were then incubated for another 30 min at 4°C, capturing fluorescence output in the SYBR channel every 10 s. The data from each standard curve was then plotted as RFU values over substrate concentration, and a liner regression was fit to each graph. The equation of the linear regression was then used to convert the RFU values of the kinetic experiments to nM of cleaved oligo for each respective plate.

To test the assumption that Tth-MazF1’s rate of cleavage is not impacted when substrate concentration is altered, three concentrations of each oligo (250, 100, and 50 nM) were cleaved with 5 nM Tth-MazF1, and initial velocities were compared. There was no significant difference in calculated rates between the three substrate concentrations from any of the six oligos tested. Specifically, 3 μl of 25 μM, 10 μM of 5 μM synthetic RNA oligo from IDT (Integrated DNA Technologies) was combined with 3 μl of Murine RNase Inhibitor (NEB M0314), 3 μl of 200 mM Tris (pH 7.5), and 6 μl of nuclease-free water (a total reaction volume of 15 μl). This mixture was then incubated in a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR machine (Bio-Rad) at 4°C for 20 min, capturing fluorescence output in the SYBR channel every 10 s. After 20 min, 15 μl of reaction mixture was added to each well containing 3 μl of 0.05 μM of ribonuclease and 12 μl of nuclease-free water. No enzyme control wells were mixed with 15 μl of reaction mixture containing 3 μl of diluent H (no BSA) and 12 μl of nuclease-free water. All wells were then incubated for another 30 min at 4°C, capturing fluorescence output in the SYBR channel every 10 s.

Kinetic data analysis

For each data set, the first 20 min were excluded from the analysis (the time before the enzyme or diluent mixture was added). The subsequent RFU values were then converted to nM of cleaved oligo using the linear regression from the standard curve on each plate. Then, the no-enzyme values at each time point were subtracted from the values for each enzyme concentration in each replicate. These values were then plotted over time, and a linear regression was fit to each graph. To obtain the initial linear velocity, these linear regressions were only fit to the first 252 s of data. The slopes of these linear regressions represent the initial velocity at each enzyme concentration. These initial velocities were then plotted against enzyme concentration, and linear regressions were then fit to these graphs. The slopes of these linear regressions represent the kcat of the enzyme on each oligo under these reaction conditions.

Characterization of Tth-MazF1 and Tpr-MazF sensitivity to RNA modifications

FAM-labeled RNA oligonucleotides were designed with a centrally located cleavage site, allowing ribonuclease-mediated cleavage into two distinct fragments. In each oligonucleotide, the uridine in the recognition sequence was modified with various chemical groups to assess sensitivity to base modifications (Supplementary Table S3: R508, R510–R511, R540–R546).

Cleavage reactions were assembled by combining 1 μl of 10 µM synthetic RNA oligonucleotides (IDT and Horizon), 1 μl of Tth-MazF1 or Tpr-MazF at concentrations of 1, 0.2, 0.1, or 0.05 µM, 1 μl of 200 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 1 μl of murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, M0314), and 6 μl of nuclease-free water, for a total reaction volume of 10 μl. Reactions were incubated at 85°C for 15 min.

Following digestion, 10 μl of 2× RNA loading dye (NEB, B0363S) was added to each sample, which were then heated at 70°C for 5 min. A total of 18 μl from each reaction was loaded onto a pre-cast 15% TBE-Urea polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed at 180 V for 60 min. Gels were imaged on the Amersham Typhoon scanner using the Cy2 setting. Cleaved products migrated faster than the full-length substrates, allowing quantitative assessment of modification sensitivity.

Characterization of thermostable MazF selectivity by LC-MS/MS using a multiplexed RNA oligonucleotide pool

A 10-fold serial dilution of each candidate thermostable MazF enzyme was prepared in 40 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). For each dilution, a 20 μl reaction mixture was assembled containing 1 μl of the enzyme dilution in 40 mM sodium phosphate buffer. In parallel, a pool of eight synthetic 35-nt RNA oligonucleotides (Supplementary Table S3: OligoPool-1 to OligoPool-8) was prepared at a final concentration of 1 µM for each oligo in 40 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). Both the MazF enzyme mixtures and the RNA substrate pool were independently preheated at 80°C for 15 min. Following pre-incubation, the enzyme and substrate mixtures were combined and incubated together at 80°C for an additional 30 min. Reactions were then spin-filtered using Ultrafree-MC centrifugal filters (Millipore Sigma, UFC30GV0S), and 25 μl of the resulting filtrate was subjected to UHPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Preparation of mRNA by in vitro transcription and purification of E. coli rRNA

mRNA synthesis was performed as described in [15] following standard protocols of the HiScribe® T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, E2040L). Briefly, 1 µg of each DNA template was mixed with 10 mM of each NTP, 1 μl of T7 RNA polymerase mix, and 2 μl of 10× T7 reaction buffer to a total volume of 20 μl in the nuclease-free water. The resultant mixture was incubated at 37°C for 2 h in a thermocycler. For the synthesis of m1Ψ and mo5U-modified FLuc mRNA transcripts, UTP was replaced with either m1ΨTP (Trilink, N-1081) or mo5UTP (Trilink, N-1093) in the reaction mixture, respectively. Following each IVT reaction, the products were diluted in 1× DNase I reaction buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.6) to a volume of 100 μl, and template DNA was digested with 2 μl of DNase I (NEB, M0303). Finally, mRNA was purified using Monarch Spin RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T2050).

Cap-1 modified FLuc mRNA was generated using Faustovirus Capping Enzyme (FCE; NEB, M2081S). Briefly, 50 µg of FLuc mRNA was diluted to a final volume of 50 μl in FCE capping buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, pH 8.0) containing 200 µM S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), 500 µM GTP, 50 units of FCE, and 200 units of mRNA Cap 2′-O-methyltransferase (NEB, M0366). The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following incubation, Cap1-modified mRNA was purified using the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (500 µg capacity; NEB, T2050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Escherichia coli rRNA was purified from E. coli 70S ribosomes (NEB, P0763S) using the Monarch® RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T2050) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Digestion of mRNA and rRNA with Tth-MazF1

Digestion reactions were assembled by combining 0.4 µM Tth-MazF1 with 5 µg of each RNA substrate—EPO, FLuc, 5 kb-BglII, 9 kb-BglII, GFP poly(A), E. coli 5S, 16S, and 23S rRNAs (Supplementary Table S3)—in a total volume of 10 μl containing 125 mM NaCl and 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). Reactions were incubated at 80°C for 15 min in a thermocycler, rapidly cooled to 12°C, and subsequently ethanol precipitated following the protocol described by [28].

Briefly, each reaction was diluted to 45 μl with nuclease-free water, and the salt concentration was adjusted by adding 5 μl of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.5). Three volumes of ice-cold 100% ethanol were added, and the mixtures were incubated at −20°C for 1.5 h. Precipitated RNA was collected by centrifugation at 13 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was washed with 500 μl of 70% ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 20 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. After carefully removing the supernatant, pellets were air-dried at room temperature for 15 min. Each RNA pellet was resuspended in 35 μl of nuclease-free water and incubated at 65°C with shaking (300 rpm) for 5 min. Resuspended RNA samples were then spin-filtered using Ultrafree-MC centrifugal filters (Millipore Sigma, UFC30GV0S), and 25 μl of the filtrate was subjected to Ultra High-Performance Liquid Chromatography Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) analysis.

RNA oligonucleotide analysis by UHPLC-MS/MS

UHPLC separation of RNA oligonucleotides was performed on a Thermo Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system equipped with a Waters Acquity Premier Oligonucleotide BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, 130 Å) at 70 °C. A 20-min gradient mobile phase [solvent A: 1% hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), 0.1% diisopropylethylamine (DIEA), 1 µM EDTA in water; solvent B: 90% methanol, 10% water, 0.075% HFIP, 0.0375% DIEA, 1 µM EDTA] at a flow rate of 300 μl/min was applied.

MS/MS data were acquired using an Orbitrap Eclipse Fusion mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Oligonucleotides were ionized in negative mode using a spray voltage of –2700 V, with a sheath gas setting of 35 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas at eight arbitrary units, an ion transfer tube temperature of 320°C, and a vaporizer temperature of 300°C. Intact oligonucleotide masses were measured at a resolution of 120 000 in the Orbitrap, with the RF lens set to 50. MS/MS (MS2) data were acquired in data-dependent (ddMS2) mode. Precursors in the m/z range of 600–1500 and charge states from 2 to 50 were selected for fragmentation using a 3 Da isolation window. Fragmentation was performed using stepped higher-energy collisional dissociation at normalized collision energies of 22%, 24%, and 26%. MS2 spectra were acquired at a resolution of 60 000 using automatic AGC target and maximum injection time settings.

Computational interpretation of UHPLC-MS/MS data from RNA oligonucleotides

RNA oligonucleotide ddMS2 data were analyzed using the Oligonucleotide Mode in BiopharmaFinder 5.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Candidate Tth-MazF1 cleavage products were identified from mRNA and rRNA sequences (Supplementary Table S3: EPO, Fluc, 5kB-BGII, 9kB-BGII, E. coli 5S, 16S, and 23S rRNAs) based on expected cleavage at U↓G, U↓AG, and C↓GG motifs with 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate termini at the 3′ end. For E. coli rRNAs, 25 canonical mass-altering modifications were included at known positions, and each site was modeled with and without the modification to estimate stoichiometry, along with variable methylation at unmodified positions. High-confidence oligonucleotides were defined by a minimum confidence score of 90 and a maximum delta mass of 5 ppm relative to the predicted Tth-MazF1 cleavage product. Downstream data summarization was performed using custom R scripts.

Results

Discovery and characterization of archaeal MazF proteins

To explore the diversity of recognition motifs among archaeal MazF proteins, we first performed a bioinformatic search to identify candidate homologs. Subsequently, we established a semi-high-throughput workflow encompassing protein expression, purification, and an Illumina-based sequencing approach to characterize the recognition motifs of these putative enzymes (Fig. 1A). To this end, we performed a BLAST search using 11 previously described MazF homologs (Supplementary Table S1) [25] as queries against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database, restricted to archaeal sequences. The initial dataset comprised 705 protein hits. To investigate the activity of candidate archaeal MazF proteins, we clustered our set of sequences into 91 empirically defined groups based on sequence similarity, applying a minimum pairwise identity threshold of 25% to define each cluster (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S2). Following clustering, we reanalyzed the resulting BLAST hits and excluded proteins or clusters that lacked a predicted PemK-like/MazF-like domain, a hallmark of the MazF family of proteins [25]. The final curated list comprised 696 MazF-like proteins grouped into 83 clusters (Supplementary Table S2). This set contained six MazF proteins from thermophilic and 19 from hyperthermophilic organisms, using temperature cutoffs of 45°C–79°C for thermophiles and ≥80°C for hyperthermophiles, as defined by public databases [29, 30] (Supplementary Fig. S1A and Supplementary Table S2). The number of proteins in each cluster ranged from 1 to 116 proteins, with 22 clusters containing only a single MazF protein. Eco-MazF was the only bacterial protein included in clustering, which fell into cluster number 51, comprising nine proteins.

To capture a broad range of potential recognition motifs, we selected 164 representative archaeal MazF proteins from 75 distinct clusters for further analysis. These proteins were selected based on their sequence diversity, the ecological diversity of their source organisms, and their representation across distinct protein clusters, to ensure comprehensive sampling of potentially diverse functional properties. Before evaluating the archaeal proteins, we optimized our protein expression and purification workflow by testing 22 bacterial MazF homologs (Supplementary Table S2) that exhibit varying degrees of sequence identity to Eco-MazF. Bacterial MazF proteins were expressed using culture volumes ranging from 1000 ml down to 5 ml and manually purified using amylose and nickel gravity columns. Since 5 ml culture was the smallest volume that consistently produced reproducible results, we adopted this volume for our high-throughput screening strategy, which we subsequently applied to 164 archaeal MazF proteins. In total, this brought the number of MazF proteins tested to 186.

Selected archaeal MazF proteins were expressed and purified in a semi-high-throughput manner using a periplasmic expression system in E. coli, enabling compartmentalization of toxic MazF endoribonucleases and mitigating their deleterious effects on the host [31] (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). To our knowledge, this is the first study to express MazF-like proteins without their neutralizing cognate antitoxins, enabling high-throughput expression and purification. To assess the endoribonuclease activity of each purified MazF protein, we designed a compact 1.57 kb synthetic RNA substrate encompassing all 1024 possible 5-mer sequences, generated using the curlcake algorithm [26] (Supplementary Table S3). This design was informed by previous findings that MazF proteins generally recognize sequence motifs between 3 and 7 bases in length. Each MazF protein was incubated with the 1.57 kb curlcake RNA substrate for 15 min, and cleavage products were analyzed by denaturing gel electrophoresis. Proteins exhibiting endoribonuclease activity produced distinct banding patterns indicative of sequence-specific cleavage (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S2C). Of the 186 MazF proteins tested, 132 exhibited detectable cleavage activity on the 1.57 kb substrate. The remaining 54 proteins were classified as inactive, although the underlying reasons remain unclear. We hypothesize that inactivity may be due to poor expression or solubility, the requirement for an unknown cofactor, or the absence of a cognate recognition motif within the substrate.

To define the recognition motifs of active MazF enzymes, we developed an Illumina-based RNA-seq assay. We designed a DNA–RNA(7mer)–DNA chimeric substrate containing a central 7 nt degenerate RNA region—representing all 16 384 possible 7-mer sequences—flanked by constant 3 nt DNA sequences to facilitate data processing. Illumina adapter sequences from the NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep Kit were used to enable paired-end sequencing (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S2D). The RNA insert length in the library was limited to 7 nt, as longer randomized RNA sequences would require substantially deeper sequencing to achieve the coverage necessary for robust statistical analysis, thereby increasing both the cost and computational demands.

Each active MazF enzyme was incubated with the chimeric 7-mer library, where cleavage at specific recognition motifs was expected to deplete corresponding 7-mers from the pool. The undigested RNA population was then converted to Illumina RNA-seq libraries. Sequence reads from MazF treated libraries were compared to enzyme-untreated control libraries to calculate an enrichment score (E) for each 7-mer RNA sequence. Positive E scores reflected depletion of a motif in the enzyme-treated sample, with higher values indicating stronger and more specific cleavage preferences. Sequence motifs corresponding to cleavage specificity were derived using the MEME suite [32] for each MazF enzyme. (Fig. 1A and B and Supplementary Table S4). Sequencing of the chimeric 7-mer input library confirmed that all 16 384 7-mer sequences were present.

To validate our Illumina-based assay, we used a commercially available Eco-MazF enzyme at three different concentrations as a benchmark. Comparison of the chimeric 7-mer library sequences in the enzyme-untreated control with those detected in Eco-MazF-treated samples revealed strong enrichment of 7-mer sequences containing one or more ACA motifs (Fig. 1B). Motif discovery using the MEME suite further confirmed that these enriched sequences corresponded to the known ACA recognition motif of Eco-MazF (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table S4), demonstrating that our assay reliably recapitulates the established cleavage specificity of Eco-MazF enzyme. As an additional control in the high-throughput workflow, we included MazF6 from Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The recognition motif identified from our dataset, UCCU (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table S4), closely matched the previously reported motif UUCCU for this enzyme [33], further validating the accuracy of our assay in capturing MazF cleavage specificity. It is worth noting that our assay was not designed to pinpoint the exact cleavage position of MazF enzymes within each recognition motif, as the workflow prioritized rapid identification of the recognition motifs.

To illustrate the relationship among MazF homologs and the origins of their associated recognition motifs, we created a phylogenetic tree of 84 protein sequences whose recognition motifs demonstrated sequence specificity (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table S4). Most of the predicted motifs were 3 to 4 nucleotides in length, with the majority ranging from 2 to 5 nucleotides. Consistent with a link between cleavage specificity and protein sequence similarity, clusters of homologous proteins generally shared similar motifs. Notably, motifs generated from bacterial MazF homologs resembled the known Eco-MazF cleavage sequence ACA, whereas many archaeal MazF proteins exhibited UG-rich motifs. Despite this broader trend, we identified a subset of archaeal MazF proteins with bacterial-like motifs such as UACA and UAC, as well as others displaying UC-rich cleavage preferences. Collectively, we determined the recognition motifs for 90 of the 132 active MazF enzymes from archaea and bacteria. Compared to their bacterial counterparts, the archaeal MazF proteins predominantly recognized UG-rich sequences.

Functional characterization and substrate specificity of thermostable MazF homologs

To investigate the potential of thermostable MazF enzymes for controlled RNA cleavage in mass spectrometry analysis, we focused our efforts on MazF homologs derived from hyperthermophilic organisms. We reasoned that higher reaction temperatures reduce the influence of RNA secondary structures, which often hinder enzyme access and cleavage efficiency at temperatures in the mesophilic range (20°C–45°C). To further explore the relationship among these MazF enzymes, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using only MazF proteins derived from hyperthermophilic archaea for which we had determined recognition motifs (Fig. 2A).

We observed that all the proteins recognized a UG dinucleotide within their recognition motifs. Moreover, most of the motifs begin with a U. However, the biological significance of this pattern remains unclear (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, some organisms encode multiple MazF homologs with overlapping sequence specificity. For example, Tth-MazF1, Tth-MazF2, and Tth-MazF3 all originate from Thermococcus thioreducens, yet Tth-MazF2 clusters separately from the other two proteins in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2A). Closer inspection of the sequences revealed that Tth-MazF1 and Tth-MazF3 are nearly identical, differing by only two amino acids at the N-terminus. This raises the possibility that these two proteins are derived from the same gene and are misannotated in the database.

The cleavage activity of seven out of 16 thermostable MazF proteins (Fig. 2A) was verified using a 5′ FAM-labeled synthetic RNA oligonucleotide containing a single UGG cleavage site (Supplementary Table S3). These MazF proteins exhibited robust cleavage activity, though with varying patterns (Fig. 2A and B). For example, Tma-MazF and Tth-MazF3 generated multiple bands on denaturing urea gels, indicative of cleavage at multiple sites. In contrast, enzymes such as Tpr-MazF and Tth-MazF1 produced single, distinct cleavage products, although the sizes of these products differed (Fig. 2B). These differences suggest functional divergence among closely related MazF homologs, potentially enabling nuanced control of RNA cleavage under varying physiological conditions. Alternatively, the variation in cleavage patterns may reflect differences in enzyme concentrations across reactions, as the purified proteins were below the detection limit, and enzyme-to-substrate ratios could not be standardized.

To define the substrate selectivity of thermostable MazF proteins better and identify the exact cleavage site within each recognition motif, eight different 35-nt RNA oligonucleotides were synthesized, containing all possible trinucleotide combinations repeated at least three times. An equimolar mixture of these oligonucleotides created a synthetic 3-mer RNA library for enzyme evaluation by mass spectrometry (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table S3). Enzymes were evaluated by titrating the enzyme concentration while maintaining a constant substrate concentration. Relative fold-dilutions were used instead of actual molarity because the protein concentration was too low to be reliably detected. At 100-fold dilution, six of the seven MazF proteins cleaved exclusively at UGG sequences, while Tpr-MazF exhibited no detectable cleavage at this sequence (Fig. 2D). At the highest enzyme concentration (undiluted enzyme), several of the MazF proteins began to exhibit cleavage activity at sequences other than UGG, suggesting that enzymes may exhibit relaxed sequence specificity or recognize degenerate motifs at higher enzyme-to-substrate ratios. For example, Tma-MazF cleaved RNA at 11 different 3-mer sequences (Fig. 2D). A MazF protein from Thermococcus thioreducens, Tth-MazF1, preferentially cleaved four different 3-mer motifs, all beginning with a UG dinucleotide, suggesting that Tth-MazF1 may have a UGN cleavage preference, acting like a two-base cutter. This is consistent with the gel-based assay shown in Fig. 2B, where the substrate oligonucleotide contained only one UG site and cleavage by Tth-MazF1 yielded a single prominent product band. Interestingly, while Eco-MazF cleaves immediately 5′ of the first adenine in its recognition motif (↓ACA), the archaeal MazF homologs characterized here cleaved within their respective trinucleotide motifs, specifically at the 3′ side of the uridine in U↓GG (Supplementary Fig. S3A–C).

One of the key features of mass spectrometry-compatible ribonucleases is their ability to generate RNA fragments in the ∼4 to 60 nucleotide range. To predict whether any of the enzymes in our study would yield high sequence coverage in mass spectrometry-based RNA analysis, we performed an in silico digestion of 1000 randomly selected human transcripts, each ranging from 5 to 20 kb in length. All possible 2-mer and 3-mer cleavage motifs were evaluated, assuming cleavage occurred at the 3′ end of each recognition sequence. The results of this computational analysis are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. The highest sequence coverage was predicted with dinucleotide-specific motifs, particularly CA, CU, and UG, with UG-cleaving enzymes yielding the greatest coverage at 78%. In contrast, cleavage by a trinucleotide-specific enzyme (UGG), a motif frequently observed in our data set among archaeal proteins, resulted in 33% coverage. Based on these predictions and supporting experimental data, we selected the UGN-cleaving Tth-MazF1 for further characterization. To facilitate downstream studies, we redesigned the expression construct to produce Tth-MazF1 without the MBP tag, replacing it with smaller 6× His tag. Cleavage assays using the 6× His-tagged Tth-MazF1 on a multiplexed RNA oligonucleotide pool confirmed a consistent preference for UGN motifs across all tested enzyme concentrations, with UGG emerging as the most favored cleavage site (Supplementary Fig. S3D).

Biochemical characterization of Tth-MazF1

Thermococcus thioreducens is a hyperthermophilic archaeon isolated from deep-sea hydrothermal vents, with an optimal growth temperature of 83°C–85°C [24]. Based on its origin, we hypothesized that Tth-MazF1 may exhibit activity across a broad range of temperatures. To test this, we assessed cleavage efficiency at five temperatures (4, 25, 37, 60, and 85°C) using two concentrations of Tth-MazF1 (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S5A). At the higher enzyme concentration (0.1 µM), Tth-MazF1 cleaved a short synthetic RNA substrate (Supplementary Table S3) with >96% efficiency at all tested temperatures, including 4°C. At the lower concentration (0.005 µM), activity was markedly reduced at low temperatures, cleaving only 16% of the substrate at 4°C, but remained robust at higher temperatures, achieving 90% cleavage at 85°C. The ability of a hyperthermophile-derived MazF to retain measurable activity at 4°C is highly unusual, as such enzymes are typically inactive at low temperatures due to their structural rigidity [34, 35]. This property may indicate an atypical degree of conformational flexibility in Tth-MazF1 and may prove valuable for temperature-sensitive RNA manipulation.

Following temperature optimization, we next evaluated the tolerance of Tth-MazF1 to variation in reaction buffer composition, focusing on salt concentration. Similar to its performance across temperatures, Tth-MazF1 maintained robust cleavage activity at higher enzyme concentrations (0.1 and 0.01 µM) across a broad range of NaCl concentrations (0–500 mM) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S5B). At the lower enzyme concentration (0.005 µM), cleavage efficiency declined with increasing salt, dropping to 43% at 500 mM NaCl, while remaining high (92%) under low-salt conditions (0 mM NaCl). Taken together, these results demonstrate that Tth-MazF1 is a versatile and salt-tolerant endoribonuclease, capable of sequence-specific RNA cleavage under a wide range of biochemical conditions.

To determine whether salt concentration influences the sequence specificity of Tth-MazF1, we assessed its activity on defined RNA substrates representing both canonical and degenerate motifs. We synthesized five RNA oligonucleotides, each containing a distinct trinucleotide motif identified in our LC-MS/MS assays: UGG, UGA, UGC, UAG, and CGG (Supplementary Table S3). Cleavage assays were performed across a range of NaCl concentrations to evaluate sequence preferences under different salt conditions (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S5C). Cleavage of the UGN-containing substrates (UGG, UGA, and UGC) remained largely unaffected by salt concentration. In contrast, Tth-MazF1 also cleaved the non-UGN substrates (UAG and CGG), but with reduced efficiency, and cleavage of these off-target motifs declined progressively with increasing salt (Fig. 3C). These results suggest that Tth-MazF1 becomes more sequence-selective under high-salt conditions, preferentially cleaving canonical UGN motifs. The enhanced sequence specificity of Tth-MazF1 under high-salt conditions may result from electrostatic shielding, which weakens non-specific RNA interactions and favors cleavage at high-affinity canonical sites.

To obtain a more quantitative assessment of the sequence preferences of Tth-MazF1, we conducted real-time kinetic analyses using profluorescent RNA substrates containing distinct trinucleotide motifs. Each RNA probe was labeled with a 5′ FAM fluorophore and a 3′ BHQ1 quencher. Upon cleavage by Tth-MazF1, the fluorophore and quencher become spatially separated, resulting in increased fluorescence intensity, which serves as a real-time readout of enzymatic activity [36]. Using this approach, we generated kinetic cleavage curves for six RNA substrates (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. S5D and E, and Supplementary Table S5). All reactions were performed at 4°C to slow the reaction rate and enable more accurate measurement of cleavage at early time points. Tth-MazF1 exhibited a strong preference for substrates containing UGG, UGC, UGU, and UGA motifs. In contrast, substrates containing UAG and CGG were cleaved with lower efficiency, with turnover rates at least two orders of magnitude lower. These results indicate that, in vitro, Tth-MazF1 essentially cleaves UGN-containing motifs, effectively functioning as a dinucleotide-specific ribonuclease with preference for the UG motif. This preference is consistent with the mass spectrometry analysis of RNA substrates (Fig. 2D).

Epitranscriptomic modifications are critical regulators of RNA function and have been extensively harnessed in the development of mRNA therapeutics and vaccines. Among cellular RNAs, tRNAs, and rRNAs are particularly enriched in such modifications, which can impede RNase cleavage. To evaluate the sensitivity of Tth-MazF1 to natural and synthetic RNA modifications, we synthesized RNA oligoribonucleotides containing various modified uridine nucleosides at the first position of the UGG recognition motif (Supplementary Table S3). Cleavage activity was assessed across four enzyme concentrations to determine the impact of each modification on substrate processing (Fig. 3E and F). At a fixed substrate concentration of 1 µM, Tth-MazF1 cleaved nearly all modified and unmodified substrates to >98% completion at the highest enzyme concentration (0.1 µM), with the exception of the 4-thiouridine–containing substrate, which reached only 60% completion. At the lowest enzyme concentration (0.005 µM), cleavage efficiency decreased markedly for substrates containing 4-thiouridine, N3-methyluridine, 1-methylpseudouridine, and pseudouridine, with cleavage efficiencies of 2%, 3%, 10%, and 12%, respectively. In contrast, Tth-MazF1 retained robust activity (>50% cleavage) on the remaining modified and unmodified substrates. Interestingly, the modification sensitivity profile of a related hyperthermophilic homolog, Tpr-MazF from Thermococcus profundus, differed substantially (Supplementary Fig. S6A and B), with greater sensitivity to 1-methylpseudouridine and dihydrouridine compared to Tth-MazF1. Together, these results demonstrate that Tth-MazF1 is active across a broad range of buffer and reaction conditions and is tolerant to many uridine modifications. They also underscore the influence of the buffer composition on modulating the enzyme’s sequence specificity.

Bottom-up mRNA characterization by LC-MS/MS with Tth-MazF1

Given its strong preference for UGN-containing motifs, broad temperature activity range (4°C–85°C), and tolerance to diverse chemical modifications, Tth-MazF1 could serve as an effective enzymatic tool for LC-MS/MS characterization of various RNA biotypes, including mRNA and highly modified, structured RNA.

Recent studies have employed UHPLC-MS/MS to verify the internal sequences and detect modifications of mRNA therapeutics and vaccines using a range of enzymatic tools [10, 15–18]. To assess the utility of Tth-MazF1 for this application, we developed a streamlined digestion workflow for LC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 4A). Briefly, RNA samples were incubated with Tth-MazF1 in a high-salt buffer at 80°C for 15 min, followed by ethanol precipitation to isolate a purified pool of cleavage products suitable for UHPLC-MS/MS (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S7). To optimize enzyme concentration, we used firefly luciferase (FLuc) mRNA at 0.88 µM as a model substrate and tested a range of Tth-MazF1concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B). The Tth-MazF1 cleavage yields an OH at the 5′-end and a 2′,3'-cyclic phosphate at the 3′ end. Increasing enzyme concentrations resulted in a greater abundance of shorter RNA fragments (Supplementary Fig. S8C) and more complete digestion (Supplementary Fig. S8D). Based on these results, the highest concentration tested (0.4 µM) was selected for all subsequent experiments.

We next applied the Tth-MazF1 digestion workflow to a panel of mRNA substrates ranging from 0.8 to 8.9 kb in length (Fig. 4B). Incubation with Tth-MazF1 resulted in efficient digestion of all four mRNA substrates, as evidenced by robust cleavage profiles and high-resolution chromatograms (Supplementary Fig. S9A). The number of high-confidence oligonucleotide fragments identified by LC-MS/MS increased proportionally with transcript length (Fig. 4C). Tth-MazF1-generated cleavage products ranged in length from 4 to 64 nucleotides, with median lengths between 15 and 18 nt (Fig. 4D). The majority of these fragments were uniquely mappable to specific positions within each mRNA sequence (Supplementary Table S6).

This distribution of relatively long and uniquely assignable oligonucleotides resulted in high experimental sequence coverage across all mRNA substrates, ranging from 73% to 99%: 99% for EPO, 95% for FLuc, 87% for BGII (4.9 kb), and 73% for BGII (8.9 kb) (Fig. 4E and F). Finally, consistent with the U↓GN selectivity of Tth-MazF1, the most abundant identified fragments originated from U↓GN cleavage sites, with a smaller fraction attributable to cleavage at U↓AG motifs (Supplementary Fig. S9B and Supplementary Table S6). There are some regions of low or missing coverage in the tested mRNAs, for instance the 3′-end of FLuc mRNA. The sequences in these unidentified regions are primarily stretches (>50 nt) of mRNA sequence devoid of UG sequences (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Current mRNA therapeutics and vaccines frequently incorporate nucleobase modifications, particularly N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ), to enhance translation efficiency and reduce immunogenicity [37]. Given Tth-MazF1’s sensitivity to m1Ψ (Fig. 3E) and the therapeutic relevance of uridine modifications, we further evaluated its utility for characterization of FLuc mRNA substrates fully substituted with either m1Ψ or 5-methoxyuridine (mo5U) (Supplementary Fig. S11). Both modified mRNAs were efficiently digested into shorter oligonucleotide fragments at the optimized Tth-MazF1 concentration (0.4 µM), as shown by chromatography (Supplementary Fig. S11A). The mo5U-substituted FLuc mRNA yielded a sequence coverage comparable to the unmodified uridine control (mo5U: 94%, U: 95%). In contrast, the m1Ψ-modified substrate exhibited a substantially lower coverage of 79% (Supplementary Fig. S11B and C).

This reduction is likely due to impaired cleavage at m1Ψ↓G sites by Tth-MazF1 (Fig. 3E). Supporting this interpretation, increasing the enzyme concentration threefold improved the overall sequence coverage of m1Ψ-modified FLuc mRNA from 79% to 87% (Supplementary Fig. S11D and E). Together, these findings suggest that Tth-MazF1 is a promising tool for mRNA characterization, capable of achieving high sequence coverage by UHPLC-MS/MS across a range of chemically modified and unmodified mRNAs.

Direct analysis of 5′ cap and poly(A) tail with Tth-MazF1

A major advantage of LC-MS/MS-based RNA analysis is its ability to directly detect RNA modifications and structural features, such as 5′ cap incorporation and poly(A) tail length distributions. To support such analyses, several specialized methods have been developed for the enzymatic cleavage, enrichment, and LC-MS/MS detection of capped and polyadenylated RNA fragments [38–43]. Additionally, two recent studies have demonstrated simultaneous detection of both capping and polyadenylation in mRNA digests using RNase T1. However, these approaches have limitations, including artifactual RNA degradation during analysis of long poly(A) tails and difficulty in assessing the proportion of successfully capped mRNA (capping efficiency) due to the short 5′ end fragments generated by RNase T1 [17, 18].

To expand the analytical utility of Tth-MazF1, we investigated whether cap identity and capping efficiency could be directly evaluated from mRNA digests produced by Tth-MazF1 (Fig. 5A). Specifically, FLuc mRNA bearing an m7GpppGm cap structure (Cap1) was prepared via sequential incubation with FCE and an mRNA 2′-O-methyltransferase. Cleavage by Tth-MazF1 was expected to occur at the first 5′-terminal UG site, generating an 18 nt 5′-end fragment (Fig. 5B). In uncapped FLuc mRNA control samples, this 18 nt fragment displayed a mixture of diphosphorylated and triphosphorylated 5′ ends (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S12A), whereas the corresponding fragment derived from capped FLuc mRNA contained the expected Cap1 modification (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S12B).

Next, we evaluated whether poly(A) tail length could be directly measured in Tth-MazF1-digested mRNA samples (Fig. 5A). To this end, GFP mRNA bearing a 63-nt poly(A) tail was synthesized by IVT. Tth-MazF1 was expected to cleave at the UGN site closest to the 3′ end, generating a poly(A)-containing fragment with an additional 9-nt overhang (Fig. 5C). Chromatographic analysis of the digestion product (Supplementary Fig. S12C) revealed a distribution of monoisotopic masses, which were assignable (within 10 ppm) to poly(A) tails carrying the expected 9-nt overhang (Fig. 5C and D). The observed oligonucleotides ranged from 69 to 84 nt, corresponding to poly(A) tails of 60 to 75 nt in length, with the most abundant species measuring 63 nt (Fig. 5D). Together, these results demonstrate that Tth-MazF1 enables simultaneous characterization of 5′ cap and poly(A) tail structures in mRNA digests using a LC-MS/MS approach.

Detection of E. coli rRNA modifications via LC-MS/MS following Tth-MazF1 digestion

In combination with RNA sequencing and structural biology approaches, RNA mass spectrometry offers an orthogonal approach for mapping and quantifying RNA modifications in rRNA [13, 14]. Previous studies have employed multiple ribonucleases or selective purification of individual rRNAs to achieve reliable maps of RNA modifications by LC-MS/MS [13, 14].

We next investigated whether Tth-MazF1 alone could be used to generate a reliable map of rRNA modifications from a mixed population of E. coli 16S, 23S, and 5S rRNAs. To this end, mature rRNA was purified from E. coli 70S ribosomes and digested with Tth-MazF1 (Fig. 6A; see “Materials and methods” section for details). Consistent with our results for mRNA, Tth-MazF1 yielded high sequence coverage across the 16S (85%), 23S (81%), and 5S (100%) rRNAs (Fig. 6B). Notably, high-confidence Tth-MazF1 cleavage products captured 84% (21/25) of known mass-altering modifications in E. coli rRNAs (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Table S7). Additionally, monoisotopic masses corresponding to two additional cleavage products were detected by MS1, containing the m7G modification at position 527 of the 16S rRNA and m3Ψ at position 1915 of the 23S rRNA (Supplementary Table S6). We also assessed the stoichiometry of modified sites and observed partial modification for m5C at position 1962 and for ho5C at position 2447 in the 23S rRNA (Supplementary Fig. S13). These results are consistent with previous reports showing that m5C1962 is substoichiometric [44], and that ho5C2447 varies dynamically with growth phase [45]. Together, these findings demonstrate that Tth-MazF1 enables rapid and informative profiling of epitranscriptomic modifications in E. coli rRNA mixtures without requiring prior isolation of individual rRNA species.

Discussion

Nucleotide- and sequence-specific RNases have become indispensable tools for controlled RNA cleavage, enabling a wide range of applications in research and biotechnology, including RNA sequence characterization by mass spectrometry and mapping of epitranscriptomic modifications through high-throughput sequencing. However, the set of RNases with defined and useful cleavage preferences remains limited. In this study, we addressed this need by performing a systematic analysis of archaeal MazF homologs. The MazF family of endoribonucleases, known for their sequence-specific activity, presents a promising source of novel enzymes with potential utility in these applications. Using a semi-high-throughput expression, purification, and motif discovery pipeline, we examined 164 archaeal MazF proteins spanning 83 sequence-divergent clusters along with 22 bacterial homologs. Remarkably, 133 of the 186 of proteins tested exhibited robust activity, a success rate likely attributable to our expression and purification strategy, which eliminates the need for antitoxin co-expression and allows native purification without protein denaturation, unlike previous approaches that required guanidine HCl or urea-based denaturation step for the separation of TA complexes [2, 36]. Furthermore, through an Illumina-based RNA-seq assay and complementary validation approaches, we successfully defined the recognition motifs of 90 MazF enzymes, revealing an unexpectedly broad spectrum of UG-rich sequence preferences among archaeal MazF homologs.

Our data suggest that enzymes clustering together based on sequence homology tend to recognize similar RNA motifs. This observation implies that recognition specificity is at least partially encoded in the primary sequence of MazF proteins, potentially providing a framework for predicting substrate preference from amino acid sequence alone. Such predictive capability could accelerate the discovery of novel sequence-specific ribonucleases for applications that require controlled or targeted RNA cleavage. However, the extent to which a tertiary structure of MazF protein can predict recognition motif remains to be determined, which could be tested using AlphaFold protein structure, further advances in computational approaches for protein structure prediction will significantly aid this task.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic approach for identifying novel recognition motifs of MazF proteins, with a particular focus on the underexplored archaeal domain. Our RNA-seq-based cleavage assay was designed to be rapid, ligation-free, cost-effective, and optimized for streamlined library preparation and sequence analysis. While our approach offers advantages in throughput and convenience, it does not allow precise determination of the cleavage sites within the recognition motif. However, the exact cleavage sites can be determined using complementary methods such as oligonucleotide digestion followed by gel electrophoresis, mass spectrometry, or alternative sequencing strategies.

The archaeal MazF homologs tested here displayed a striking enrichment for UG-rich recognition sequences. The biological importance of such bias is unclear. The predominance of short (2–4 nt) cleavage motifs among these enzymes makes them particularly well-suited for biotechnology applications requiring controlled and targeted RNA fragmentation. This is especially advantageous in mass spectrometry-based RNA analysis, where predictable and high-coverage fragmentation is essential for accurate identification and quantification of RNA.

Most used ribonucleases, such as RNase A, RNase T1, and human RNase 4, are unable to efficiently cleave double-stranded RNA. This poses a significant challenge in RNA analysis, as RNA molecules naturally fold into complex secondary structures in solution, and many functional RNAs, such as tRNA and rRNA, are highly structured. These structural elements can obstruct enzymatic access, resulting in unpredictable or biased cleavage patterns.

Among the thermostable homologs, Tth-MazF1 emerged as a promising candidate for further development due to its dinucleotide cleavage preference, U↓GN, which yielded the highest theoretical transcriptome coverage in our in silico digestion simulations of human transcripts. Tth-MazF1 remains active across a broad temperature range (4°C–85°C), offering a valuable advantage. At elevated temperatures, RNA secondary structures are destabilized, enabling the enzyme to cleave RNA in a more linear, unstructured form. Notably, Tth-MazF1 functions effectively under a variety of buffer conditions and does not require divalent metal ions, which can otherwise promote nonspecific RNA hydrolysis. This temperature-dependent unfolding facilitates more uniform and predictable fragmentation. In addition to structural complexity, epitranscriptomic modifications in RNA can also hinder enzymatic cleavage. Although Tth-MazF1 is sensitive to certain modifications, such as 4-thiouridine, it retains the ability to cleave modified RNAs with little or no loss of efficiency, enhancing its utility for robust and sequence-specific RNA fragmentation in analytical workflows.

Surprisingly, we observed distinct sensitivities to epitranscriptomic modifications between closely related proteins, such as Tth-MazF1 and Tpr-MazF. Despite their high structural similarity based on AlphaFold predictions (RMSD = 0.801 over 460 atoms), the two enzymes exhibited opposing responses to specific uridine modifications. Tpr-MazF activity was blocked by N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ), whereas Tth-MazF1 readily cleaved RNA substrates containing this modification. In contrast, Tth-MazF1 was blocked by 4-thiouridine, while Tpr-MazF maintained activity on substrates containing this modification. These differential responses suggest that even subtle structural variations can have a pronounced impact on modification recognition. This observation lays the groundwork for engineering MazF homologs with tailored substrate specificities, enabling their development as precision tools for the selective detection, mapping, and functional analysis of epitranscriptomic modifications.

Our systematic screen of archaeal MazF proteins identified Tth-MazF1, a sequence-specific endoribonuclease from the hyperthermophilic archaeon Thermococcus thioreducens, as a versatile tool for bottom-up analysis of diverse RNA classes by LC-MS/MS. We demonstrated that Tth-MazF1 enables high sequence coverage (73%–99%) using a panel of mRNA substrates ranging from 0.8 to 8.9 kb in length. In addition, we showed that Tth-MazF1 can be used to assess key mRNA features, including 5′-cap and poly(A) tail, and to detect site-specific mass-altering modifications present in the E. coli rRNA. Collectively, these findings support the utility of Tth-MazF1 as a robust and broadly applicable tool for the characterization of therapeutic and vaccine mRNAs, as well as for mapping RNA modifications in complex cellular RNA populations by LC-MS/MS.

In addition to Tth-MazF1, we discovered and characterized many archaeal MazF proteins with diverse putative RNA cleavage preferences. Having multiple enzymes that cleave at a variety of sequence motifs allows for greater flexibility when it comes to substrate design. Drawing inspiration from the DNA restriction enzyme field, we envision that the use of these endoribonucleases will facilitate the manipulation and assembly of point-modified or partially modified RNA transcripts. The diversity in modification sensitivity displayed by different MazF proteins also opens the door for new detection methods for epitranscriptomic modifications, similarly to previously described m6A mapping methods [5, 6]. One current limitation related to the application of many bacterial and archaeal MazF proteins as tools for biotechnology is their degeneracy of cleavage. Fig. 2D displays the range of off-target cleavage events that MazF enzymes can exhibit at high concentrations. As the length of the target transcript increases, the likelihood of an off-target cleavage site also increases. Future work aimed at improving our understanding of the degenerate or off-target cleavage of each enzyme and protein engineering efforts to improve selectivity will pave the way for broader biotechnological utility of these proteins.
