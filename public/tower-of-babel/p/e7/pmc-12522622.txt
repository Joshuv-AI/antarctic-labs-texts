Use of two virtual dissection tables in a postgraduate oral anatomy course: feedback from the students
Authors: Davide Gerardi, Diana Torge, Sara Bernardi, Serena Bianchi, Guido Macchiarelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvancements in medical education technologies transformed the teaching and learning of several disciplines, including human anatomy, which is considered the core of health sciences education. Recent technological innovations, including virtual dissection tables like the Anatomage® and SECTRA® table, provided interactive 3D models and clinical imaging that address these limitations. This study aims to evaluate the rating of two different VDTs in teaching clinical anatomy to post-graduate dental doctors, emphasizing their perceived advantages for clinical practice.MethodsA population (N = 24) of post-graduate students in oral surgery (N = 7) and orthodontics (N = 17) were enrolled in this study. The design of the lecture included practical sessions on the Anatomage® and SECTRA® table, including cases evaluations on anatomy structures. Anonymous surveys were used to evaluate the grade of perception on the use of both VDTs. The software used for statistical calculations was GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0 (621) for Windows.ResultsThe post-graduate students significantly positively considered the use of VDTs as very useful and user-friendly learning instruments, offering very satisfying quality of the digital images. The level of clinical self-confidence and satisfaction of the proposed teaching method lecture was considered statistically positive.ConclusionsThe integration and the diversification of the anatomy teaching strategies, using innovation technologies, increase the learning processes and improve the clinical self-confidence in post-graduate dental students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07840-1.Keywords: Education, Anatomy, Digital anatomy education, Anatomy learning tools, Virtual dissection, Morphology, Clinical oral anatomy education

Introduction

Innovative technology in medical education has revolutionized teaching and learning in all medical specialties, starting with the curricula of human anatomy, which can be considered the core of biomedical education [1]. Anatomy is the science that studies macro and microscopically, the constitution and development of the human body [2]. Human anatomy is an essential basic discipline in any undergraduate health course and is one of the pillars common to the various health courses necessary to understand subsequent disciplines [2–5]. Historically, the teaching of human anatomy focused on theoretical, expository-dialogued classes, with the exploration of the cadaver through dissection [6, 7]. This traditional methodological approach proposed in the Italian universities included two important elements: the teacher in an active, leading role and the students acting as passive observers. At the same time, the donation of bodies for teaching anatomy was an important setback over the years, which has posed serious concerns regarding the difficulty of obtaining and preserving cadavers, according to strict legal and ethical regulations present in Italy [2, 8]. With the onset and development of technology, it was necessary to provide new resources for teaching anatomy, which would be beneficial for improving educational processes. These devices worked in consonance with theory and practice. They provide students with opportunities to develop autonomy, teamwork, critical thinking, creativity, and logical reasoning skills in various educational contexts [2, 7]. Over the centuries, human anatomy has been taught using different strategies, such as lectures, digital presentations, textbooks, images, videos, and atlas used during theoretical sessions [9].

Furthermore, the recent coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, a global health threat that determined the forefront of challenges worldwide [10], significantly impacted university teaching. Medical schools were affected by strict lockdown regulations and were forced to adapt teaching and training methods, utilizing alternative virtual platforms. Among them is the application of virtual dissection tools, 3D visualization technologies, and online collaborative platforms to ensure continuity and accessibility of learning in a socially distanced environment [11].

In addition, the anatomy core is placed in the preclinical years and not integrated vertically in Italy, but also in most dental curricula in Europe, the United Kingdom, and the United States of America [12].

Teaching and learning in human anatomy have experienced the most revolutionary change in the last decade, thanks to the rise of the virtual dissection table (VDT), which lets students explore a real human body through digital devices that offer interactivity with digital cadavers during education and training simulations [13].

Anatomage® table incorporates advanced imaging technologies such as CT scan, X-ray, ultrasound, and MRI, perfectly integrated into a user-friendly touchscreen interface [14–16]. SECTRA® table, instead, is an anatomical table in the IDS7® database of medical imaging cases, 3D cadaveric dissections, including histology, embryology and radiology, based on real cases of clinical interest [17].

Given the potentiality of the offered digital technology, the integration of VDT in anatomy teaching in undergraduate and post-graduate dental curricula appears to be an advantage in improving the quality of anatomy teaching.

This study aims to evaluate the students feedback of two different VDTs in enhancing the quality of clinical anatomy lectures in post-graduate courses.

Materials and methods

Population

The VDTs were investigated on a population of (N = 24) post-graduate students in Oral Surgery and Orthodontics, who represented the entire cohort available, thus all first-year residents in those programs. The informed consent was obtained by all participants.

The sample size was derived from the total of the enrolled at first-grade of post-graduate students in Oral Surgery and Orthodontics. Even though students in Oral Surgery and Orthodontics specialize differently, they often share courses in many dental schools (anatomy, treatment planning seminars), and they also frequently use 3D planning and VDTs; for these reasons, they were enrolled in the study. In addition, the number of the subjects was allowed a better interaction with the tables, than in larger group of students.

Teaching method

The teaching approach used during anatomy lectures in the post-graduate programs included practical hands-on Anatomage® and SECTRA® tables sessions; the total duration of the teaching sessions was four hours for each post-graduate program (Oral surgery, Orthodontics and post-graduate who followed the Orthodontics-Shangai Chili Education technology, a post-graduate educational program); the practical session was divided in two lessons of 2 h each: 2 h were dedicated on Anatomage® table session, the last 2 h on SECTRA® table. Each lesson consisted of general training on Anatomage® and SECTRA® table, respectively, whose aim was to let students know the main functions of the software and hardware investigated, which took place in the first hour of each lesson; the last hour was dedicated to the clinical application of the table directly conducted by the students which could explore the topical anatomical structures using gross anatomy libraries and importing radiological exams as Cone beam Computed Tomography (CBCT). The tables’ sessions were kept one per week, for a total six weeks.

Anatomage® and SECTRA® dissection tables

The Anatomage® Table (Medical Convertible, EDU 8.0) and the SECTRA® table both use advanced imaging technology (Visible Human Project® data) to let users explore digital cadavers via a high-resolution interactive display. The reconstructions and developed software, combined with touch screen hardware, enable users to interact with anatomical models in real-time, allowing for dissection, rotation, and zooming, thereby providing an in-depth understanding of the human body. Additionally, the software includes a library of histological samples of human tissues and animated renderings that illustrate the functions of various systems, such as joint movements and heart rates.

Evaluation questionnaire on the use of the VDT

The survey on VDT usage was distributed. The evaluation questionnaire was created using “Google Online for Creator” on “Google Workspace” and consisted of twelve questions in various formats, including five multiple-choice questions and open-ended questions (Fig. 1. Suppl. 1). The Google Form was distributed online, kept anonymous, and shared with the post-graduate students through a link generated by the Google Forms platform.

The submitted evaluation survey (english translation)

The model used to develop the evaluation survey was those used in our earlier studies [17, 18], and the questions were set to cover the topics of our investigation.

Students were asked whether they had any previous experience with similar technology. The survey then assessed their satisfaction with the VDT sessions, the user-friendliness of the interface, and the overall usefulness of the software. We also invited recommendations for improving the software or the structure of the VDT lessons. Finally, students rated their interest in the virtual dissection method and their self-confidence in clinical practice after the course. The evaluation survey was delivered two weeks after the end of the anatomy course.

Statistical analysis

Due to their categorical nature, the data were analysed using the Freeman-Halton extension to Fisher’s exact test, comparing the results of the two VDTs, the satisfaction of the diversified lecture, and the level of clinical self-confidence. The null hypothesis was that there was no difference between the VDTs, the level of satisfaction with the lecture, and the level of clinical self-confidence. The null hypothesis was considered rejected if the p-value would result ≤ 0.05. The software used for statistical calculations was GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0 (621) for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.graphpad.com.

The sampled population consisted of 24 post-graduate dental students of oral surgery (N = 7) and orthodontics (N = 17). Gender and age were not considered categories, so this data is not reported in the present study.

Discussion

Teaching anatomy in post-graduate dental school and the challenge to meet the theory with the clinical application

The horizontal teaching of anatomy in the undergraduate dental curricula has the concentration of notions as a limit, because they are rarely recalled in the clinical years (3rd – 6th in Italy) or appropriately recalled integrating the clinical need with the anatomy study [12], leading neophyte graduated professionals to attend Cadaver-Lab courses to revisit and apply clinical anatomy.

The new anatomy teaching strategies rely on innovative technologies and the availability of digital platforms and online content, as well as prosections and plastinated 3D models. Among the different methods of evaluation of student’s perception on using VDT, the administration of a questionnaire has been often preferred, as this study applied it as method of investigation. Reymus et al. [19] introduced virtual reality (VR) for studying root canal anatomy and asked forty-two third-year undergraduate dental students in preclinical training to fill a questionnaire, reporting their capacity of detecting all anatomic features using CBCT scan, 3D printed models or rendering obtained through virtual reality (VR) software program. The students welcomed the introduction of VR in the learning process, thus their performance in detecting root canal configuration was significantly better [19].

In this study, an evaluation questionnaire on the use of Anatomage® and SECTRA® table was administered to explore two main data, thus if students have already experienced a training on VDTs and their level of satisfaction about the quality of images and features offered by both tables. Furthermore, two open questions were proposed to highlight the advantages and above all the limits of the VDTs, according to student’s perceptions, which represents crucial information to improve the teaching method and the characteristics of VDTs. In particular, the needs of increasing the hours on the table, the online remote access, improving the interface of the SECTRA® table, and implemented the clinical cases dedicated to the Maxillofacial and oral regions, have emerged.

Our findings are in line with the current evidence that diversifying teaching strategies is welcomed by students and can enhance the learning process, e.g. complementing lectures with interactive 3D tools clinically or orienting the approach to anatomy courses including example clinical cases and discussing peer groups, as demonstrated by da Silveira et al. [20].

Better preparation for better self–confidence in clinical practice

The guidelines issued by the Association for Dental Education in Europe (ADEE) emphasize the need for dentists to be well-prepared to autonomously recognize clinical issues, through accurate diagnosis, that arise in their professional routine and to provide the most suitable treatment for the patient [21]. However, as dentistry is a surgical medical discipline, it is crucial to make practical simulators available. In Italy, the need for a proper practical component, starting from cadaver dissection in human anatomy courses, could affect the quality of student learning: practical dissection sessions on cadavers have proven effective for students, who could consolidate their theoretical knowledge and develop skills in isolating and identifying anatomical structures on different planes of dissection of the anatomical specimen [22].

Over the years, numerous institutes in Italy have dismissed the use of cadavers’ dissections, and replaced them with the use of plastic models, leading to a decline in the quality of student learning. A specific meeting point for orthodontics and oral surgery post-postgraduate students, is that anatomy education, specifically in dentistry programs, was challenging, due to cadaver shortages, compared to medical faculties [23].

Currently, thanks to advancements in technology, the availability of VDTs represents an advantage, thanks to their ability to offer practical exercises during anatomy classes, helping the students explore the human body and understand clinical anatomy structures.

Unlike dissection rooms, which are often challenging to set up due to bureaucratic and legislative constraints, investigative software and hardware utilizing VDT, such as Anatomage® and SECTRA®, can be used in simple multimedia laboratories, creating a safe and comfortable environment for instructors and students. Regarding this study, the presented results significantly support the hypothesis that VDTs can impact anatomy education: the data survey showed that both VDTs were found to be very useful and user-friendly, thanks to the very satisfying quality of the digital images of both VDTs. Regarding SECTRA®, the students found the application challenging, but they also reported the necessity to improve the SECTRA® platform by enriching the library of cases from maxillofacial and oral regions: probably, this might have positive learning outcomes for dental education. A virtual table like “Sectra” can motivate dental postgraduate students, but to provide them a more interactive, realistic, and visually appealing learning experience, it should improve as much as possible the access to additional cases and resources from maxillofacial and oral regions [24].

Different recent evidence demonstrated how new digital and 3D technologies have gained an important place in the anatomy teaching curriculum, complementing the use of traditional resources: for example, the groundbreaking success of Karolinska Institutet was due to the integration of anatomical resources into both undergraduate and post-graduate, with to emphasize the ‘student perspective’ in the use of 3D technologies and enhance the perceived relevance of studying human anatomy [18].

Furthermore, cadaver dissection requires prior skills in incision and tissue separation; trainees are often influenced by the risk of making mistakes and irreversibly damaging the anatomical specimen. Digital simulators using VDT, on the other hand, allow students to practice specific procedures by simulating them with the possibility of making mistakes, recognizing them, and quickly reverting virtually to repeat the practical activity. The advantage consists of the student’s awareness that they can make errors without consequences—an essential concept for effective practical training and a precursor to clinicians’ treatments [25]. Furthermore, students might integrate the VDTs application into their teaching and clinical practice, also performing hands-on training and exercises on cadavers: this application is frequently used by odontologists and physicians, to improve the process of teaching and training. Specifically, the forensic odontology education requested the application of hands-on training and exercises on cadavers, for head dissections, dental autopsies, and radiographs [26]. For these reasons, the Anatomage Table marks a turning point in the learning process of dental and radiological data collection and analysis, by using 3D reconstructions of the head, the contrast-enhanced CT scans and the radiographs on the selected planes [26].

Our results align with recent evidence that immersive technologies improve anatomy education. The systematic review conducted by Bevizova et al. in 2024 found that virtual reality training significantly enhanced dental anatomy learning outcomes and student engagement. This suggests that whether via VR or virtual dissection tables, digital tools can effectively supplement traditional anatomy teaching [27].

The level of clinical self-confidence and the satisfaction on using VDTs, investigated through the perception questionnaire, were considered significantly positive. The majority of our students reported increased confidence after training with VDT: while we did not measure their skills directly, this perceived boost in confidence may reflect the benefit of being able to practice anatomy in an interactive, low-risk environment. Indeed, as demonstrated in the study of Bianchi et al. [28] VDTs represent a crucial aid to reduce anxiety, often triggered by the fear of not being able to manage a particular procedure due to insufficient preparation or inadequate practical training, increasing the risk of errors or failure to handle complications [28].

Limitations of the study

Despite the positive and encouraging preliminary results from our study, the current investigation present limitations in the study design and the method used for the evaluation of the VDTs’ rating. In particular, the number of hours spent during the table sessions is low (4 h) as well as the sample size, and a negative control group is missing.

In addition, the self-perception of own clinical skills after the anatomy course is not complemented with a more objective evaluation, such as a clinical skill assessment by a supervisor. However, the presented results are expected to encourage new studies with more performing evaluation tools, with a larger sample size, and performing more objective assessment method to evaluate the effectiveness of VDT, including pre-tests and post-tests.

Conclusions

Teaching anatomy in healthcare curricula is crucial for the core background of future healthcare professionals, including medical and dental doctors. The high importance of having a good anatomical background implies the necessity of high-quality anatomy lectures and the development of high-quality learning strategies, that should consider also the students’ point of views.

Within the limits of the study, the integration of VDTs into postgraduate dental anatomy teaching was welcomed, according to the students’ feedback, as regards the learning experience and the professional self-confidence.
