Protein coacervation-driven active forces power protocell dynamics
Authors: Haiyang Jia, Huan Sun, Weijie Zhang, Xiao Ning, Stephen Mann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein coacervates formed by liquid-liquid phase separation are emerging as active force generators, independent of ATP-driven motors. Nevertheless, the coordination and force scaling of protein coacervates remain largely unexplored. Here, we engineer a temperature-responsive elastin-based protocell model displaying temperature-modulated contractility and attendant force harnessing. By leveraging the phase separation properties, we modulate the protocell dynamics associated with volume contraction and membrane budding. Crosslinking of the elastin-based membrane influences the contraction dynamics such that the accumulation of mechanical forces in the protocells results in the spontaneous expulsion of internally trapped protein liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) complexes. We use a simple mathematically model to show how protein coacervation can amplify small piconewton-scale forces to perform large-scale mechanical work, highlighting the mechanical potential of protein coacervation dynamics. Taken together, our results provide a model framework for harnessing protein coacervates-driven forces and offer a step to future applications in synthetic biology, biomaterials and next-generation soft robotics.

Introduction

Cells are extraordinary engines of force, driving the fundamental processes that sustain life, including muscle contraction1, cell migration2, cytokinesis3, exocytosis4, and tissue morphogenesis5. Central to this remarkable capability are the active forces generated by a range of mechanisms: cytoskeletal dynamics, cell-matrix adhesion, osmotic pressure, membrane tension, and protein phase separation1,6–9. These forces, often operating in the piconewton range, scale up, and orchestrate the cellular activities. Among these mechanisms, protein liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), including protein condensation and coacervation, have recently emerged as a surprising source of substantial interfacial forces within living cells, functioning independently of ATP-fueled molecular motors9. Such LLPS-based forces, arising from processes like coalescence10,11, Ostwald ripening12, and interface wetting13, underscore the liquid-like nature of protein condensates, enabling them to flow, fuse, and dynamically assemble or disassemble in response to environmental cues. This capacity allows cells to harness these forces for the dynamic compartmentalization of intracellular structures, influencing critical processes such as cell signaling, endocytosis, and autophagy. Unraveling the molecular origins and chemical principles underlying large-scale protein LLPS dynamics should provide insights into how non-motor protein harness forces within living cells, and potentially revolutionize our understanding of the structure, organization, and function of living cells. Furthermore, employing and scaling-up such forces could be important for the biomimetic engineering of soft robots14,15 and synthetic cells16,17, and offer new frameworks for the utilization of protein LLPS-based forces in fields such as biomaterials and drug delivery18,19.

The ability of protein coacervates to rapidly transition in response to local cytoplasmic conditions facilitates dynamical adaptation to changes in environmental conditions and regulation of various cellular processes. However, the chemical and biological complexities of how these protein coacervation-induced forces are coordinated and scaled to perform complex mechanical tasks remain largely elusive and challenging to unravel. As a step towards addressing these challenges, we present a simple protocell model based on the stabilization of water-in-oil microemulsion droplets with a self-coacervating protein (elastin)20,21 membrane capable of exhibiting temperature-modulated contractability and attendant force harnessing within controllable confined spaces. The protocell membrane consists of a closely packed array of amphiphilic elastin-polymer nanoconjugates that spontaneously assemble at the water/oil droplet interface. If required, the elastin-based membrane can be further stabilized by covalent crosslinking to modify the contractile behavior. By leveraging the phase separation properties of the membrane building blocks, we engineer the protocell dynamics associated with contraction, membrane budding and shape remodeling, and model the collective accumulation of force during large-scale phase fusion. Finally, we show how continuous protocell contraction results in the transient production of multiphase droplets and their subsequent force-induced expulsion into the surrounding environment.

Results

Temperature-driven contraction dynamics of elastin-based protocells

Model protocells capable of temperature-dependent contractability were constructed using elastin-stabilized water-in-oil microemulsion droplets. Covalent coupling of amino-functionalized elastin to mercaptothiazoline-terminated poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm20, Mn 8800 g/mol) was used to prepare surface-active amphiphilic protein-polymer nanoconjugates. In this system, PNIPAAm primarily contributes amphiphilicity that promotes interfacial enrichment and stabilizes a water-oil interface (Fig. 1a). Although both elastin and PNIPAAm exhibit lower critical solution temperature (LCST)-type thermal transitions21,22, their functional contributions are distinct. Elastin alone forms simple coacervates (Supplementary Fig. 1a), whereas elastin–PNIPAAm conjugates display enhanced temperature responsiveness (Supplementary Fig. 1), producing smaller particle-like domains that assemble into an interconnected interfacial network. In contrast, BSA–PNIPAAm conjugates form only network-like aggregates without evident LLPS (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c), indicating that PNIPAAm alone is insufficient to drive phase separation. Addition of the nanoconjugates to a dextran-containing aqueous/2-ethyl-1-hexanol mixture at a range of temperatures produced microemulsion suspensions comprising elastin-stabilized polysaccharide-containing water-in-oil droplets with a mean diameter of 14.32 ± 4.76 μm (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). The emulsion droplets were stable when stored at 4 °C but exhibited a temperature-dependent decrease in mean diameter above 20 °C (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2c). This diameter reduction was consistently observed by both confocal and wide-field imaging (Supplementary Fig. 2d), and is consistent with the thermos-responsive behavior of elastin and its nanoconjugates (Supplementary Fig. 1). For example, the mean volume of the droplets decreased by 25% and 74% when incubated at 25 °C or 33 °C for 20 min. In contrast, water-in-oil droplets stabilized by a surface-active shell of bovine serum albumin (BSA)-PNIPAAm nanoconjugates showed only a weak response below 35 °C23,24. This indicated that the droplet contractability was driven by LLPS of the elastin-based conjugates due to the onset of enhanced hydrophobic interactions between the elastin molecules as the temperature was raised above 20 °C. In general, the rate of volume contraction increased as the temperature was increased to 37 °C.

Fluorescence microscopy was used to track the time-dependent localization of elastin and dextran during progressive contraction of the elastin-polymer-coated water droplets (Fig. 1c–f and Supplementary Movie 1). Over time, elastin, which was initially distributed both in the droplet interior and membrane, became enriched at the water/oil interface as the protocell diameter decreased (Fig. 1g). During this coacervation process, the inner elastin–Atto647 fluorescence decreased between 5 and 20 min due to the migration and accumulation of elastin molecules at the interface, resulting in reduced internal signal and a corresponding increase in interfacial fluorescence (Supplementary Movie 1). In contrast, dextran remained homogeneously distributed in the droplet interior where it became increasingly concentrated as the volume of the droplet decreased (Fig. 1h). Contraction of the protocell resulted in an increase in the concentration of the entrapped soluble elastin-polymer nanoconjugates such that LLPS occurred within the interior of the shrinking droplets to produce an encapsulated dispersion of elastin coacervates with diameters around 2.03 ± 0.31 μm (n = 10) (Fig. 1i, j). The size of the phase-separated coacervates within the confined environment was consistent with previous reports21. The entrapped coacervates became localized under the elastin-based membrane as the droplets continued to contract (Supplementary Movie 1). Similar results were observed with elastin-based protocells containing aqueous solutions of BSA (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Movie 2). By around 25 min, the interfacial elastin layer had dissipated, and elastin appeared as dispersed small coacervates in the surrounding phase rather than remaining concentrated at the interface (Supplementary Movie 1).

Quantitative analysis of temperature-dependent changes in protocell diameter and the fluorescence intensity of entrapped FITC-dextran was performed during gradual heating from room temperature to 37 °C. The contracting droplets became transiently enlarged and irregular in surface morphology after ca. 30 min due to the onset of solvent molecule exchange across the elastin-polymer interface (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Movie 3). To gain insight into the dynamics and force accumulation in the elastin-based protocell system, we developed a biophysical model incorporating principles of thermodynamics26, pressure dynamics27, and liquid-liquid phase separation28. The model postulated that elastin phase separation at the droplet interface induces a pressure differential, which drives contraction forces within the protocell (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Model parameters were obtained by global fitting to experimentally measured diameter–time shrinkage profiles across multiple temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). Using these parameters, the model predicted a pressure differential across the elastin-based interface (Supplementary Fig. 5d), corresponding to an integrated contractile force on the order of 10²–10³ pN at 37 °C for protocells with a diameter of 25 µm. This magnitude of force is sufficient to initiate the inward contraction of the protocell (Supplementary Fig. 5e). As LLPS typically generates forces on an order of 10−1 pN9, the model suggests that these small forces are amplified by the microcompartmentalized structure by several hundred-fold. Moreover, the model simulated an increase in capillarity pressure as a function of time and decrease in droplet radius, eventually reaching a maximum pressure of ca. 0.1 Pa at 37 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Overall, the model captures the dynamics of elastin-driven phase separation and allows an estimation of the collective forces that influence the mechanical properties of the protocell membrane during contraction.

Crosslinked-enhanced membrane dynamics in elastin-based protocells

We employed three chemical crosslinkers with different chain lengths – glutaraldehyde, DTSSP and PEG-bis(N-succinimidyl succinate) (Mn 2000 g/mol)—to elucidate the influence of membrane dynamics on the temperature-dependent contractibility of elastin-stabilized water-in-oil emulsion droplets (Fig. 3a). In each case, time-dependent fluorescence microscopy was used to track the structural changes accompanying droplet contraction at 37 °C. Crosslinking the elastin-PNIPAAm nanoconjugates at the water droplet/oil interface with glutaraldehyde gave rise to significant morphological changes during contraction, transitioning the protocells from a relatively smooth and spherical form to a more compacted and irregular shape (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Movie 5). Time-resolved imaging confirms that, upon glutaraldehyde crosslinking, contour irregularities nucleate locally early during contraction and then subsequently amplify (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Such behavior likely arises from the formation of short, rigid covalent crosslinks, which restrict the elastin membrane’s ability to uniformly redistribute strain and accommodate rapid surface-area changes during contraction. In contrast, the protocells remained spherical under contraction when crosslinked with the longer molecular connectors of DTSSP or PEG (Fig. 3c, d), indicating that the membrane dynamics were adaptive to the changes in volume. Moreover, compared with non-crosslinked droplets, DTSSP-crosslinked elastin membranes became progressively thicker and more condensed as the droplets decreased in size, and exhibited approximately 2.7-fold slower contraction rates to reach 50% of their original volume demonstrated in Fig. 1b (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Movie 6).Fig. 3Crosslinking enhanced dynamics of elastin-protocell.a Protein crosslinkers with different lengths. b Fluorescence imaging of elastin protocell crosslinked with glutaraldehyde. Notably, no process of transient volume enlargement was observed for the glutaraldehyde- or DTSSP-crosslinked protocells, indicating that unlike the non-crosslinked droplets there was minimal transfer of oil, water, and elastin across the water droplet-oil interface during contraction. In contrast, transient enlargement was observed when the protocells were crosslinked with PEG (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Movie 7), indicating that the crosslinked membrane remained sufficiently flexible to support the uptake of oil molecules. Consequently, localized microphase separation of elastin in the PEG-crosslinked membrane was still apparent during contraction and gave rise to the formation of coacervated domains at the water droplet/oil interface (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Movie 8). Notably, although PEG-crosslinked elastin membranes retain some flexibility, they exhibit limited fluidity (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c), in contrast to lipid membranes.

Increasing the degree of PEG crosslinking resulted in a progressive increase in the size and fluorescence density of the microphase-separated regions as the droplets contracted such that distinct irregular patches of condensed elastin-PNIPAAm nanoconjugates became embedded in the protocell membrane (Fig. 3g; Supplementary Fig. 6d and Supplementary Movie 9). Surprisingly, the elastin coacervate particles were radially ejected from the protocell surface along linear trajectories under high levels of contraction (Fig. 3h, i and Supplementary Movie 10). The rapid ejection of particles without filament or bead-on-a-string morphologies indicates that this process is probably driven by the sudden release of elastic stress stored in the contracting elastin-rich membrane29,30. The propelled particles traveled linearly for 71.74 ± 16.69 µm within 110 s with a median speed of 0.82 ± 0.16 µm/s (Fig. 3j, k), which was approximately 2.65 times higher than the speed of free-diffusing particles (0.31 ± 0.13 µm/s) under similar environmental conditions. Analyses of single ejected particles revealed a rapid initial acceleration, with initial peak velocities of ca. 0.93 µm/s, followed by gradual deceleration (Fig. 3l, m). Using Stokes’ law together with the measured median velocity, the viscous drag force was calculated to be Fdrag ≈ 0.31 ± 0.06 pN. Under low–Reynolds-number conditions, this drag balances the active launching force, such that Flaunch≃Fdrag.

Taken together, the above observations indicate that crosslinking of the elastin-based membrane not only influences the dynamics associated with protocell contraction but also generates mechanical forces capable of launching and rapidly distributing the internal contents of the protocell into the environment, providing insights into the nature of force generation during phase separation at water droplet/oil interfaces.

Force-mediated release of nested coacervates

Given the above observations, we designed a thermally mediated self-driving protocell system for the contraction-induced release of encapsulated LLPS complexes (Fig. 4a). The latter were endogenously produced by encapsulating encapsulated dextran and PEG3300 within the elastin-based protocells and inducing protocell contraction at 37 °C. As the dextran and PEG levels were initially below the threshold for segregative LLPS, thermally induced elastin-mediated contraction initiated coacervation within the protocells by volume-dependent increases in concentration. Undertaking the above procedure in non-crosslinked elastin-stabilized water-in-oil droplets gave rise to partial fusion of the trapped elastin/dextran coacervate microdroplets as the contraction increased but without expulsion from the protocells due to the inherent flexibility in the absence of membrane crosslinks (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast, replacing PEG3300 with a PEG crosslinker gave rise to contractive protocells capable of a two-stage process involving the induction and ejection of the phase-separated elastin/dextran droplets at 37 °C (Fig. 4e). In the first stage, the protocells underwent enlargement-contraction and corresponding increases in internal concentration to initiate dextran/PEG demixing and elastin/dextran phase separation (Supplementary Movie 12). This was followed by an influx of oil droplets, movement and partial coalescence of the LLPS particles at the membrane to generate a densely packed shell of discrete elastin/dextran satellite particles that were spontaneously ejected (Fig. 4f–h and Supplementary Movie 13). During the second stage, the satellite particles on the interface fused with each other, gradually formed larger coacervates with diameters increasing from 0.73 ± 0.06 µm to 4.96 ± 0.71 µm over 16 min (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 7b). Notably, protocell contraction drove particle release during the initial 50 s, with a measured ejection frequency of 0.91 ± 0.14 s−1 on a single confocal plane (Fig. 4g, and Supplementary Fig. 7c–e).

Discussion

In summary, our work presents a contractile elastin-based protocell model that is driven by the dynamics of protein LLPS, offering insights into the ability of condensed phases to generate and harness mechanical forces. Our findings demonstrate that phase separation-driven forces, although small at the piconewton scale, can be amplified by their implementation within micro-compartmental spaces such as model elastin-based protocells. In the present study, these forces are investigated and estimated through a combination of experiments and a simplified physical model. While our results support a dominant role of elastin LLPS in driving contractility, a more stringent mechanistic decoupling—such as conjugating elastin to a non-thermoresponsive polymer to fully isolate LCST contributions—was beyond the scope of this work and remains an important direction for future investigation. Further development of quantitative mechanical characterization approaches will be required to more fully resolve the non-homogeneous elasticity and spontaneous force generation in future work. Building on this force-generation framework, the ability to control such forces enables the engineering of temperature-induced contraction, nested coacervate formation and cargo release, mirroring the adaptable mechanisms found in biological systems. This opens possibilities for designing stimuli-responsive systems, such as synthetic cells16 soft robotics14, and advanced drug delivery platforms19, where controlled release and dynamic behavior are key criteria. Moreover, scaling up the forces generated by these systems and integrating them into more complex architectures—for example by 3D printing—could facilitate the design of adaptive, self-regulating biomaterials18.

Methods

Amine-modified elastin (elastin-NH2) was synthesized according to a previously described method24. Briefly, 1,6-hexanediamine (HMDA, sigma, H11696) was covalently conjugated to aspartic and glutamic acid residues on the surface of elastin via carbodiimide activation. Typically, an aqueous solution of HDMA (500 mg, 4.3 mmol, 5 mL) was adjusted to pH 6.5 using 5 M HCl and added dropwise to a 5 mL stirred solution of elastin from bovine neck ligament (100 mg, 20 mg/ml, sigma, E6527). The pH was readjusted if necessary to 6.5 with 0.1 M HCl. The coupling reaction was initiated by adding 50 mg N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, sigma, E7750) to the stirred solution. The pH value was maintained at 6.5 by adding aliquots of 0.1 M HCl, and another 50 mg of EDC were added after 4 h. The solution was then stirred overnight. The elastin-NH2 was then dialyzed (Medicell, dialyzing tubing, MWCO 12–14 kDa) for 2 days against Milli-Q water, then filtered (Sartorius Ministart NML syringe filters, 45 μm) to remove any precipitate and the supernatant was freeze-dried.

The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 5 h, then dialyzed (Medicell, dialyzing tubing, MWCO 12–14 kDa) for 2 days against Milli-Q water and dialyzed for 1 days against PBS buffer (pH 8.0). Then protein solution was centrifuged to remove any precipitate and the supernatant was concentrated to 2 mg/ml. Based on UV–vis spectroscopy measurements, there were on average 1–2 dye molecules per protein.

(2) Alexa Fluor 647 labeling: 50 μL Alexa Fluor 647 Succinimidyl Esters (NHS esters, thermos fisher, A20006) solution in DMSO (2 mg/mL) were added dropwise to 1 mL elastin-NH2 solution (2 mg/mL in 0.1 M carbonate buffer with pH 9.0). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h, then dialyzed (Medicell, dialyzing tubing, MWCO 12–14 kDa) for 2 days against Milli-Q water and dialyzed for 1 days against PBS buffer (pH 8.0) at 4 °C. Then protein solution was centrifuged to remove any precipitate and the supernatant was concentrated to 2 mg/mL.

PNIPAAm-conjugated elastin building block. PNIPAAm polymer (Mn = 8800 g/mol) was synthesized according to published approach24. Briefly, 4,4′-azobis(4-cyanovaleric acid) (ACVA) (4.01 g, sigma, 1159) and 2-mercaptothiazoline (4.50 g, Sigma, M6204) were dissolved in 1,4-dioxane (200 mL, sigma, 360481) and degassed with nitrogen for 30 min. N,N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (6.82 g, sigma, D80002) and 4-(dimethylamino)pyridine (0.10 g, sigma, 107700) in 1,4-dioxane (100 mL, sigma, 36048) were added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 20 h. After filtration and concentration, the product was precipitated in cold diethyl ether to yield the mercaptothiazoline activated 4,4’-azobis(4-cyanovaleric acid) (ACVA-ACPM). Then 3 g ACVA-ACPM was then reacted with bis(propylsulfanylthiocarbonyl) disulfide (1.37 g, sigma, 723126) in ethyl acetate (50 mL, sigma, 319902) under reflux for 18 h to generate the mercaptothiazoline-activated RAFT chain-transfer agent, which was purified by silica gel chromatography (ethyl acetate/hexane, 1:1 v/v). For polymerization, the RAFT agent (18.9 mg), 2,2′-azobisisobutyronitrile (1.7 mg, sigma, 441090), and N-isopropylacrylamide (7.5 mmol, sigma, 731129) were dissolved in acetonitrile (8 mL, sigma, 34851) in a sealed round-bottom flask. The solution was degassed by four freeze–pump–thaw cycles and polymerized at 60 °C for 8 h. The polymer was isolated by three precipitation cycles in diethyl ether/hexane (2:1 v/v) and dried under vacuum. Elastin-PNIPAAm conjugates was synthesized based on reaction of the PNIPAAm and amino group of proteins. Briefly, a 500 μL aqueous solution of the polymer (2 mg/mL) was added dropwise to 500 μL fluorescence-labeled elastin-NH2 (2 mg/mL in PBS, pH8.0). The mixed solution was stirred for 12 h at 4 °C, then purified by using a centrifugal tube (Millipore, Amicon Ultra, MWCO 100 kDa) to remove any unreacted PNIPAAm and salts. The solution was further concentrated to 5 mg/mL. The conjugates can be shortly stored at 4 °C.

Elastin conjugation level quantification

Primary amine content was quantified using a TNBSA (2,4,6-Trinitrobenzene Sulfonic Acid) assay31. Briefly, samples (elastin, elastin-NH2, elastin-NH₂-FITC and elastin-NH₂-FITC-PNIPAAm) were reacted with TNBSA in bicarbonate buffer (alkaline pH) at 37 °C for a fixed incubation time. The reaction product was quantified by absorbance at 335 nm and compared to a glycine calibration curve. Primary amine content was reported as µmol NH2 per mg protein. Unmodified elastin exhibited a primary amine content of 0.293 ± 0.001 µmol NH₂ per mg (n = 3). After amination (elastin–NH₂), the primary amine content was 1.007 ± 0.005 µmol/mg (n = 3). Following FITC labeling (elastin–NH₂–FITC), the accessible primary amine content decreased to 0.666 ± 0.001 µmol/mg (n = 3). After PNIPAAm conjugation (elastin–NH₂–FITC–PNIPAAm), the primary amine content further decreased to 0.528 ± 0.002 µmol/mg (n = 3). Because PNIPAAm conjugation proceeds via reaction with elastin primary amines, the decrease in TNBSA-accessible amines during this final step provides a quantitative proxy for PNIPAAm conjugation extent. Unless otherwise specified, elastin–NH₂–FITC–PNIPAAm samples used throughout this study correspond to a PNIPAAm conjugation level of approximately 20.7%, calculated from the fractional decrease in primary amines relative to elastin–NH₂–FITC immediately prior to PNIPAAm coupling.

Thermo-response of elastin protein and elastin-PNIPAAm conjugates

Elastin-NH₂-FITC and elastin-NH₂-FITC-PNIPAAm were each prepared at 0.8 mg/mL and stored on ice. Phase separation was triggered by adding 15 µL of 5 M NaCl to 50 µL of protein solution, yielding a final concentration of 0.6 mg/mL protein and 1.15 M NaCl. Samples were incubated for 15 min at 4, 15, 20, 25, 30, 33, or 37 °C using a metal bath. Phase separation was subsequently monitored by confocal microscopy (Leica SP8).

Preparation of elastin protocells

To test the dynamics of elastin protocells, the interfacial elastin proteins were either non-crosslinked or crosslinked.

(1) Non-crosslinked: The inner aqueous solution of protocell was mixed with 5 μL of a 4.0 mg/mL elastin-PNIPAAm conjugate solution (no labeling, labeled with FITC or Alex Fluor 647) with 1 μL 0.1 M NaHCO3 buffer. To encapsulate other contents, 1 μL NaHCO3 buffer can be replaced with equal volume of Dextran-FITC or BSA-FITC. Unless otherwise noted the concentration of BSA-FITC and Dextran-FITC (70 kDa, sigma, 90718) was 2 mg/mL. Elastin-PNIPAAm stabilized w/o droplets were prepared by 6 μL elastin-PNIPAAm aqueous solution with 0.1 mL of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol (sigma, 538051) (aqueous/oil volume fraction, 0.06). The mixture was gently hand-shaken for 30 s to produce a protein-polymer stabilized water-in-oil emulsion. The water-in-oil emulsion should then be stored on ice or 4 °C before the microscopy imaging.

(2) Crosslinked: Unless otherwise noted elastin protocells were crosslinked with PEG crosslinker (O,O’-Bis[2-(N-Succinimidyl-succinylamino) ethyl]polyethylene glycol), average Mn 2000, sigma, 713783. Briefly, 1 μL 0.2 mg/μL PEG cross-linker (in 50 mM carbonate buffer, pH 8.5) was mixed with 5 μL of a 4.0 mg/mL elastin-PNIPAAm conjugate solution (no labeling, labeled with FITC or Alex Fluor 647), immediately followed by the addition of 0.1 mL of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol (aqueous/oil volume fraction, 0.06). The mixture was gently hand-shaken for 30 s to produce a protein-polymer stabilized water-in-oil emulsion. The water-in-oil emulsion should then be crosslinked on ice or 4 °C for at least 5 h before the microscopy imaging. Contents such as Dextran-FITC (70 kDa) can be encapsulated by following the above non-crosslinked protocol. Different types of crosslinkers were used as well, such as glutaraldehyde (sigma, 340855) and 3,3′-dithiobis[sulfosuccinimidylpropionate](DTSSP, sigma, 803200). For glutaraldehyde crosslinker, a 23% solution was prepared first. In the step of w/o droplet preparation, 1 μL 23% glutaraldehyde was used. The reaction between glutaraldehyde with protein was fast. Thus, the crosslinking was performed at 37 °C for 2–3 min and stored on ice or 4 °C before the microscopy imaging. Regarding the DTSSP crosslinker, a stock solution of DTSSP with 50 mM concentration was prepared in DMSO. In the step of w/o droplet preparation, 1 μL 50 mM DSP was added. The water-in-oil emulsion should then be crosslinked on ice or 4 °C for at least 2 h before the microscopy imaging.

Thermal dynamics of elastin protocells

All samples were imaged in silicone isolator chambers (9 mm diameter, 1 mm depth; Thermo Fisher Scientific) adhered to glass coverslips (24 × 24 mm, thickness #1.5; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The coverslips were pretreated one day prior with trimethoxy-[3-(2-methoxyethoxy)propyl]silane (sigma, 551635) (0.05% v/v in toluene) to enhance surface compatibility. Dynamic measurements of elastin protocells were conducted using a confocal microscope equipped with a heating chamber. Unless otherwise specified, the chamber was pre-set to a target temperature of 37 °C before imaging. Due to the large, open design of the chamber, stabilization of the temperature required additional time. During this period, a calibration coverslip of the same thickness as the sample was used to pre-focus the microscope at the correct z-position, minimizing delays in finding the focus during actual measurements. Once the target temperature was achieved, the sample chamber was mounted on the microscope, and imaging commenced immediately. Temperature-dependent dynamics shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 were measured sequentially in the heating chamber. Measurements began at 20 °C (room temperature) and progressed incrementally to 25 °C, 33 °C, 35 °C, and 37 °C. A fresh sample was used for each temperature to ensure reproducibility and avoid thermal history effects.

Microscopy and imaging analysis

All images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a 63× HC PL APO CS2 objective. Fluorescence excitation was performed with a 65 mW Ar laser for FITC and a 10 mW Red He/Ne (633 nm) laser for Alexa Fluor 647. Images were captured with a pinhole size of 1 Airy unit for both channels and a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. Representative micrographs and intensity profiles were generated from at least four independently repeated experiments, as detailed in the respective figure captions. Image processing and analysis were conducted using Fiji software (version v1.54 f). Adjustments to brightness and contrast were applied homogeneously across all displayed images to ensure consistent presentation. For line intensity plots, Gaussian smoothing with a pixel width of 2 was applied in Fiji to reduce noise.
