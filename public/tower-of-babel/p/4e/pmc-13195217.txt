Pancreatic Tail Leiomyosarcoma With Hepatic Metastases: Diagnostic and Therapeutic Challenges: A Case Report
Authors: Somaya Al Kiswani, Nid’a Mubarak, Renad Abu Ruman, Muna Shahwan, Rahaf Zorba, Shaimaa Abu Laban, Omar Sawafta, Abdullah Nofal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary pancreatic leiomyosarcoma (PLMS) is a rare malignant tumor accounting for less than 0.1% of all pancreatic neoplasms and often presents at an advanced stage with distant metastases or local invasion. Clinical symptoms are nonspecific, including gastrointestinal discomfort, abdominal pain, or distension, which frequently leads to delayed diagnosis. We report a 53-year-old woman with persistent abdominal distension, bloating, and central discomfort, in whom imaging revealed a 7.5 cm mass in the pancreatic tail with multiple liver metastases, encasement of the splenic arteries, and no direct invasion of the spleen or adjacent adrenal gland; her family history was notable for hematological, breast, and pancreatic cancers. Due to its rarity, aggressive metastatic behavior, and lack of standardized therapy, PLMS presents significant diagnostic and treatment challenges. This case highlights the importance of recognizing pancreatic leiomyosarcoma as a distinct clinical entity and contributes to the limited literature guiding its management.Keywords: pancreatic leiomyosarcoma, pancreatic tail tumor, liver metastases, rare pancreatic neoplasm, smooth muscle sarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma, case report

Introduction

Leiomyosarcoma of the pancreas is an extremely rare malignant neoplasm derived from smooth muscle cells, accounting for less than 0.1% of primary pancreatic malignancies.1,2 Since its first description in 1951, approximately 100 cases have been reported in the literature.2,3 Patients typically present in the fifth to sixth decades of life with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, or weight loss, often resulting in delayed diagnosis.

Pancreatic leiomyosarcoma is characterized by aggressive behavior with a high propensity for local invasion and hematogenous spread, most commonly to the liver. 2 Imaging findings are frequently nonspecific and may mimic pancreatic adenocarcinoma or other mesenchymal tumors, making histopathology and immunohistochemistry essential for definitive diagnosis. Tumors typically express smooth muscle markers such as SMA, desmin, and H-caldesmon while lacking epithelial markers.3,4

Treatment remains challenging due to the absence of standardized protocols. Surgical resection is preferred in localized disease, whereas advanced cases are managed according to soft tissue sarcoma guidelines. 5 This report highlights a metastatic tail-origin PLMS managed with sequential systemic therapies and emphasizes the importance of excluding metastatic uterine leiomyosarcoma in female patients.

Case Presentation

A 53-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, gout, and dyslipidemia, presented with intermittent abdominal pain in November 2023 which progressed to abdominal distention and flatulence. She said that in the weeks prior to the pain, she was in good health and there was no associated weight loss, fever, or gastrointestinal bleeding at presentations. She denied the use of alcohol, tobacco, or illegal drugs. Family history was positive for malignancy: her mother passed away from pancreatic cancer at age 55, her sister passed away from breast cancer at age 53, her maternal aunt was a breast cancer survivor at age 45, and her paternal uncle was undergoing treatment for leukemia in his 60s. This oncologic history raised suspicion for a familial predisposition to malignancy.

Physical examination revealed the patient to be alert and oriented, with stable vital signs (blood pressure 118/76, heart rate 76/min, respiratory rate 17/min, temperature 36.8°C). A general examination revealed no pallor, jaundice, cyanosis, lymphadenopathy, or peripheral edema. An abdominal examination revealed mild distention and fullness in the left upper quadrant. On deep palpation of the epigastrium and left hypochondrium, there was mild tenderness and guarding but no palpable mass. There was no clinical evidence of hepatosplenomegaly or ascites. There were no abnormalities in the bowel sounds. In addition, the rest of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

The first abdominal ultrasound revealed multiple liver lesions of concern. This was followed by CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast, which revealed a 7 cm × 4 cm × 4.5 cm poorly delineated mass arising from the posterior margin of the tail of the pancreas (shown in Figure 1). The mass was noted to partially encase the splenic artery and vein. There were also several focal liver lesions, the largest in the left lobe, measuring approximately 2.5 cm, with bull’s-eye type enhancement highly suggestive of metastatic disease.

The images show a large soft tissue mass lesion involving the body and tail of the pancreas, measuring approximately 7.5 cm in maximum dimension, consistent with the known primary pancreatic leiomyosarcoma. The mass extends posteriorly into the retro pancreatic fat and toward the splenic hilum without definite splenic parenchymal invasion. There is loss of the normal fat plane between the tumor and the left adrenal gland, though no definite direct invasion is seen. The lesion invades the splenic vein and encases the splenic artery. There is also loss of a clear cleavage line between the tumor and the adjacent gastric wall. No pancreatic ductal dilatation is identified. Multiple hepatic lesions (at least 18) are present in both hepatic lobes, measuring up to 2.5 cm, consistent with known hepatic metastases.

Tru-cut biopsy of a hepatic lesion demonstrated spindle-cell malignant neoplasm arranged in intersecting fascicles with marked cytologic atypia, consistent with leiomyosarcoma. Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse positivity for smooth muscle actin (SMA) and H-caldesmon, focal desmin positivity, and negativity for pan cytokeratin, ERG, CD34, and S100, supporting smooth muscle differentiation and excluding epithelial, vascular, neural, and stromal tumors.

Given that uterine leiomyosarcoma represents the most common source of metastatic leiomyosarcoma in women, additional evaluation was performed to exclude a gynecologic primary tumor. The patient underwent detailed gynecologic history assessment and pelvic imaging, which demonstrated no uterine mass or suspicious adnexal lesion. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining for estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) was performed and both were negative. These findings supported a primary pancreatic origin rather than metastatic uterine leiomyosarcoma.

Further staging with MRI and CT revealed a 7.5 cm mass in the tail and body of the pancreas (shown in Figure 2). The tumor extended into retro pancreatic fat, splenic hilum, and involved the splenic artery, which it surrounded, and invaded the splenic vein but no clear indication of direct adrenal or splenic invasion. At least 18 bilobar liver lesions were identified, consistent with widespread metastatic disease.

The images demonstrate a large, heterogeneous soft tissue mass involving the body and tail of the pancreas. The lesion extends posteriorly into the retro pancreatic fat and toward the splenic hilum, without definite splenic parenchymal invasion. There is effacement of the fat plane between the mass and the left adrenal gland as well as the adjacent gastric wall, but no definite signs of direct infiltration are seen. The splenic vein appears invaded and the splenic artery partially encased. No pancreatic ductal dilatation or biliary dilatation is identified. Multiple variable-density lesions are noted in both hepatic lobes, compatible with hepatic metastases.

Diagnosis was challenging due to the tumor’s nonspecific imaging features, which mimicked pancreatic adenocarcinoma and delayed definitive identification. She was reviewed with the sarcoma multidisciplinary team and initiated first-line doxorubicin chemotherapy. After 2 cycles, repeat imaging showed stable pancreatic disease but deteriorating liver metastases, so she was changed to second-line gemcitabine and docetaxel. This was complicated by the onset of cough, tachycardia, hypoxia, and diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities on CT chest. The differential diagnosis was atypical infection versus chemotherapy-induced pneumonitis. She was hospitalized, given intravenous antibiotics and corticosteroids, and subsequently had steroid-induced hyperglycemia, which was managed with insulin and later oral metformin. Disease progression was observed despite therapy, and chemotherapy was discontinued.

She was changed to third-line therapy in July 2024 with the tyrosine kinase inhibitor pazopanib. This was tolerated with only mild musculoskeletal pain and transient diarrhea. During the next year, she had stable disease on serial imaging, with good appetite, weight, and performance status (ECOG 1). Repeat CT scans in August 2025 showed evidence of disease progression, such as increased size of the pancreatic tumor with a new satellite nodule and enlargement of hepatic metastases. Pazopanib was withheld, and following much multidisciplinary deliberation, the patient and family chose to approach palliative chemotherapy.

In September 2025, a 15% dose reduction was made and Ifosfamide was administered. On her follow-up, she was stable and had a 1 ECOG performance status. She had mild epigastric pain, which was managed with pain clinic analgesics and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (Creon). The laboratory findings were within normal limits and no new sites of metastases outside of the pancreas and liver (shown in Figure 3).

The images show a heterogeneous soft tissue mass involving the distal pancreatic body and tail, extending toward the splenic hilum and partially encasing the splenic vessels, similar to the pre-treatment appearance. She continues to be followed by the sarcoma multidisciplinary team with supportive treatment, maintaining quality of life, and symptom control. Timeline of Key Events in the Patient’s Clinical Course (Table 1).

Discussion

PLMS is an exceptionally rare malignant neoplasm, representing only about 0.1% of pancreatic tumors, with tail-origin cases being particularly uncommon. 1 This case, presenting with pancreatic tail involvement, vascular encasement, and multiple liver metastases illustrates the diagnostic challenges of PLMS and highlights the importance of individualized systemic therapy. Documenting such cases remains essential to guide clinical decision-making, refine management strategies, and expand the limited literature. Although approximately 100 cases have been described in the literature, metastatic tail-origin PLMS with extensive bilobar hepatic involvement at initial presentation remains uncommon, and detailed documentation of sequential multi-line systemic therapy with prolonged clinical stability is rarely reported.

PLMS often presents with large tumors, with literature showing sizes ranging from 1 to 30 cm in greatest dimension (mean ~ 11.4 ± 7.1 cm). 6 In contrast, the tumor in our patient had a maximum diameter of 7 cm, which is smaller than the reported mean but still within the published range. Most published cases involve tumors in the head of the pancreas, though a substantial number are located in the body or tail.1,7 Epidemiological data indicate nearly equal incidence between males and females, or slight female predominance in metastatic series,6,8 with diagnosis most often in the fifth to sixth decades of life (mean age ~ 53.9 years). 6 Our patient, a 53 year old female with a tail-origin tumor, aligns closely with these characteristics., emphasizing the need to include PLMS in the differential diagnosis of pancreatic tail masses.

Diagnosis of pancreatic leiomyosarcoma (PLMS) is challenging due to non-specific imaging findings that can resemble other pancreatic lesions, including pseudocysts, cystic tumors, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, GIST, neuroendocrine tumors, or localized pancreatitis. 2 On imaging, these tumors usually appear as large, heterogeneous masses with areas of necrosis or hemorrhage, and they generally show contrast enhancement during both the arterial and venous phases. 2 In this case, poorly defined margins and encasement of nearby vessels further complicated preoperative evaluation. Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology and immunohistochemistry with characteristic positivity for smooth muscle markers such as SMA, desmin, and h-caldesmon, and negative staining for S100 and often CD34.2,9 In our case, the tumor showed positivity for SMA and h-caldesmon, with focal desmin staining, and was negative for CD34, ERG, pan cytokeratin, and S100. Biopsy from a metastatic site, combined with multidisciplinary radiologic–pathologic assessment, was essential in confirming the diagnosis and guiding treatment decisions.

Historically, management of PLMS has evolved from primarily surgical resection in localized disease to sarcoma-based systemic therapy for advanced cases. Earlier reports emphasized radical surgery when feasible, while more recent literature supports the use of anthracycline-based chemotherapy, gemcitabine combinations, and targeted therapies such as pazopanib for metastatic disease. 10 However, evidence remains limited to case reports and small series, underscoring the need for detailed longitudinal documentation of treatment response and outcomes. 11

Management of metastatic PLMS is challenging. Surgery is generally reserved for localized disease, while liver or widespread metastases usually receive palliative interventions. 12 Systemic therapies, including Doxorubicin, gemcitabine-based regimens, ifosfamide, and Pazopanib, remain the mainstay, though responses are variable.12,13 In this patient, sequential systemic therapy was administered without surgery due to vascular encasement and metastases; despite radiological progression, clinical stability was maintained, underscoring the role of individualized therapy and supportive care.

This case of pancreatic leiomyosarcoma highlights several key clinical lessons: clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for rare pancreatic tumors, particularly in the tail, due to non-specific imaging findings; definitive diagnosis requires histopathology and immunohistochemistry, often from metastatic sites, with characteristic smooth muscle marker positivity; management of metastatic disease relies on individualized sequential systemic therapy and early supportive care, which can maintain clinical stability despite radiological progression; and detailed reporting of such rare cases is essential to expand the literature, refine management strategies, and guide future clinical decision-making. A limitation of this report is the lack of histopathologic confirmation from the primary lesion due to the patient’s advanced disease and inoperability.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rarity and clinical complexity of primary pancreatic leiomyosarcoma presenting with hepatic metastases. The nonspecific symptoms, vascular involvement, and absence of standardized treatment protocols underscore the need for multidisciplinary and individualized management. Reporting such cases contributes to greater clinical awareness, aids in differential diagnosis of pancreatic masses, and supports the development of evidence-based therapeutic strategies for this uncommon malignancy.
