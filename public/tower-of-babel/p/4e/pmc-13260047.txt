Health Economic Evaluations of Breast Cancer Screening Strategies: A Systematic Review Focusing on Methodology and Quality Assessment
Authors: Thi Hao Pham, Susan de Braak, Sjoukje van der Werf, Sudewi Mukaromah Khoirunnisa, Xuanqi Pan, Andre Pfob, Abrham Wondimu, Jurjen van der Schans, Maarten J. Postma, Antoinette D. I. van Asselt, Lisa A. de Jong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer screening is vital for early detection and improved health outcomes but requires robust health economic evaluations to guide implementation. This systematic review examines the methodology, quality, and results of health economic evaluations of screening strategies to support decision making and future research.MethodsA literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, EconLit, and the HTA database. Health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening strategies using imaging modalities were included and screened independently by two reviewers. Data on study design, screening strategies, and outcomes were extracted and synthesized. Quality was assessed using the ISPOR checklist for model-based studies and the Consensus on Health Economics criteria (CHEC-extended) checklist for empirical data–based studies. All results were made publicly accessible via an interactive platform and the Open Science Framework, providing an open resource that facilitates transparency, reuse, and future updates.ResultsThe review included 128 studies, comprising 96 model-based studies, 14 empirical data-based studies, 15 studies combining empirical data with extrapolation using a modeling approach, and 3 studies with unclear methods. Microsimulation and cohort simulation were used in 47 and 53 studies, respectively. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios varied widely across studies depending on the screening modality, risk factors, age range of screening, and screening interval. Most studies found mammography to be cost effective compared with no screening, while some studies showed it as being not cost effective, especially for women at average risk of breast cancer, in young screening ages (40–49 years), or with an annual interval. Ultrasound-based screening programs were generally cost effective compared with no screening in the women with average risk of breast cancer. Supplementing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with mammography was generally cost effective in women with dense breasts and a family history of breast or ovarian cancer but not cost effective in women with previous treatment using radiation therapy. The median quality score was 55% for model-based studies, with microsimulations having higher quality than cohort simulations. Empirical data–based studies with and without extrapolation had a similar median quality score of 65%.ConclusionsMammography was generally reported as cost effective compared with no screening. Supplementing mammography with ultrasound and/or MRI could be cost effective depending on comparator, risk factors, age range, and screening interval. Suboptimal quality was commonly observed across published health economic evaluations. Future studies should prioritize enhancing overall quality, particularly in data, validation, and reporting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01049-z.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Mammography screening is the most evaluated strategy and generally reported as cost effective compared with no screening, while evidence for ultrasound- and MRI-based screening remains inconsistent.Evidence on cost effectiveness is highly context-dependent, varying by screening modality, interval, risk profile, and target age group, while overall study quality is moderate to suboptimal, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation in policy decisions.An openly accessible database and interactive platform accompany this review, enabling policymakers to efficiently access, compare, and reuse evidence to support context-specific decision making and promote more transparent and coordinated breast cancer screening strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, with 2.3 million new cases and 670,000 deaths reported in 2022 [1, 2]. Breast cancer screening aims to enable earlier detection and thereby potentially reduce breast cancer mortality. Evidence from randomized trials and subsequent evidence syntheses suggests that mammography, the most widely used screening modality, may reduce breast cancer mortality, but estimated effects vary across systematic reviews and methodological assessments [3]. Beyond mammography, emerging technologies and various combinations of modalities have demonstrated effectiveness in breast cancer detection, such as digital breast tomosynthesis [4], thermography [5], or mammography combined with ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6, 7].

While these approaches offer potential clinical benefit, the introduction of such strategies into large-scale programs requires careful consideration of both their health impacts (effect and harm) and economic implications. For example, incorporating MRI alongside standard mammography improves detection rates but raises concerns about overdiagnosis and overtreatment due to higher false-positive rates [7]. Additionally, MRI is substantially more expensive than mammography, further complicating the comparison across strategies. In such new screening scenarios, health economic evaluations play a critical role by synthesizing clinical outcomes and cost parameters into a single metric. These analyses facilitate the identification of the most appropriate screening approach for the target population, ultimately guiding evidence-based decision making in healthcare.

To achieve credible and relevant results, health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening must employ suitable methodological approaches and demonstrate high quality. Broadly, evaluations can be classified as empirical data–based or model-based or a combination of both types, based on data availability and the decision question. Empirical data–based analyses use clinical trials or observational studies to estimate short-term outcomes while the model-based evaluations are often necessary to assess the long-term cost and health benefits of breast cancer screening by synthesizing information from multiple sources [8]. Within the model-based approach, the choice of model type can significantly impact the outcomes of the analysis, with state-transition models and discrete-event simulations commonly used for cancer screening due to their dynamic representation of disease progression [9]. Similarly, the choice of analytical approach, such as microsimulation or cohort simulation, can influence the results [10]. Ensuring high quality and transparency of health economic evaluations is critical.

Numerous systematic reviews have examined health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening over the past decade [11–23], offering valuable, focused insights tailored to specific populations, time periods, or settings. However, these restrictive inclusion criteria limit the ability to reuse evidence for broader research questions. Some reviews only included studies from a specific group of countries [13, 21, 23], while others focused solely on economic models of mammography [12, 19, 22] or specific risk groups [14, 15, 17, 18]. Additionally, certain reviews limit their inclusion criteria to modeling studies [11, 16, 20], overlooking the potential insights from empirical data–based analyses. Moreover, many reviews extract health economic results without providing detailed information on methodological approaches or quality assessments [13, 14, 17]. Therefore, a more comprehensive review is needed to synthesize evidence across diverse contexts and methodologies, providing a holistic view of the health economic value of breast cancer screening strategies and serving as a valuable resource for future research and decision making.

This study aims to systematically review and assess the methodological approach, quality, and results of health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening strategies. There is no restriction on language, country, population, screening modality, and outcomes. All intermediate outputs and results are made publicly accessible via an interactive platform and a repository on the Open Science Framework, providing an open resource that facilitates transparency, reuse, and future updates.

The systematic review was conducted following a protocol registered on PROSPERO under ID CRD42023423840 [24], and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 checklist [25], with the completed checklist provided in Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) 1—section III.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A systematic search was performed in MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Elsevier), Web of Science, EconLit (EBSCO), and the international HTA database. As the pre-2013 literature has already been synthesized in earlier systematic reviews [19, 22], our search was limited to articles published in or after 2013 to ensure the findings remained relevant to the current and future research. No language restriction was applied. The search strategies were conducted on 25 July 2023 and updated on 17 September 2025.

The search strategy was based on three concepts: (1) breast cancer, (2) screening, and (3) health economic evaluation. For each concept, a comprehensive search block was developed including indexing terms such as medical subject headings (MeSH) and text words searched in titles and abstracts. Full search strategies in all databases are presented in ESM 1—section I.

In addition to the database searches, reference lists of eligible articles were checked to further identify potentially relevant studies.

Data Management and Selection Process

The process from searching to data extraction was conducted using the Pitts web application software [26], which facilitated study management, deduplication, and screening, complemented by the Zotero reference management tool. Duplicates were first automatically identified then manually verified by one author (THP). Titles and abstracts were assessed independently by two reviewers from a team of five (THP, LdJ, XP, AW, and SMK) based on the eligibility criteria. Subsequently, full-text screening was performed independently by two reviewers from the same group, following the eligibility criteria. Systematic reviews which met the eligibility criteria (population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, and study design) were included in the title and abstract screening but excluded in full-text screening. For exclusion, the platform requires both reviewers to list identical exclusion criteria, with disagreement resolved through discussion or involvement of a third independent reviewer if consensus could not be reached. Non-English articles were translated to English using DeepL, an artificial intelligence–based translation platform [27]. Native speakers were consulted to verify the accuracy of the translations and to support the study selection and data extraction processes in case of ambiguity.

Data Collection Process

A standardized data extraction form was developed and tested on 10% of the included articles by two reviewers (THP and LdJ). Based on discussions following the testing phase, the form underwent multiple revisions until it was suitable for use by a single reviewer. Any uncertainties regarding the extracted data were discussed to ensure accuracy and consistency. The data extraction form included study details (title, authors, publication year), economic evaluation details (study design, perspective, time horizon, setting, discount rates), input data, outcomes, and uncertainty assessment. Particularly, we extracted information on model structure, model type, included health states, and cycle length of model-based analyses.

Evidence Synthesis

Extracted data from all selected studies was synthesized. Quantitative summaries were generated to describe population characteristics, screening modalities, methodological aspects, and quality assessments. Quality assessment results were stratified by model type and analytical approach, as differences may arise across modeling frameworks. More complex approaches (e.g., discrete-event simulation or microsimulation) can better capture individual heterogeneity (e.g., genetic factors, breast density) and disease trajectories, but typically require more extensive data and structural assumptions, which may introduce additional sources of uncertainty and potential bias. Additionally, we pinpointed distinctive features influencing the quality of the models. Due to differences in assessment tools used for evaluating model-based and empirical data–based studies, quality scores between these two types of models were not directly compared.

Moreover, a summary of the health economic outcomes reported in the included studies was provided. To ensure consistency in economic comparisons, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and incremental cost utility ratio (ICUR) values were inflated to 2026 using the consumer price index (CPI) obtained from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) [28], then converted to US dollars using foreign exchange rates from the US Federal Reserve [29]. If the price year, country, or currency were unknown, the study was excluded from the economic comparison. Cost-effectiveness conclusions were based on the interpretation reported in the original studies, which typically applied their own willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds. For studies that did not specify a WTP threshold, no cost-effectiveness conclusion was assigned. Additionally, the results were stratified by risk groups to better understand the variability in health economic outcomes. The analysis also examined the impact of uptake and adherence to screening programs on the reported health economic results. Although these terms are sometimes used interchangeably in the literature, adherence is often considered a broader concept. Uptake refers to the proportion of the target population that participates in the screening program for the first time after becoming eligible or following the introduction of an intervention. Adherence, on the other hand, reflects the degree to which individuals comply with recommended schedules and guidelines for ongoing or subsequent screenings [30].

Quality Assessment

For model-based analyses, a checklist developed by the International Society of Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) taskforce [31] was utilized to assess the methodological quality and reporting bias. To tailor it to our topic, the ISPOR checklist was modified by refining the helper questions to align with the context of breast cancer screening and restricting the response options to “Yes” or “No”. A detailed explanation of the rationale and adaptations process is provided in ESM 1—section II. To assess the quality of studies that utilized empirical data solely or in combination with extrapolation using a modeling approach and those with unspecified methodology, we employed the consensus on health economics criteria checklist (CHEC)–extended checklist [32, 33]. Quality assessment was conducted by two reviewers from a team of three (SdB, AW, and THP), with one reviewer conducting the assessment and another independently verifying the results. Disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers, with the third reviewer (LdJ, JvdS, or ADIvA) available to adjudicate if required.

Straightforward scoring systems were applied to both the ISPOR and CHEC checklists (e.g., “yes” = 1, “No” = 0) to enable quantitative summarization of the results. Scores were then expressed as percentages by dividing by the total number of applicable criteria and multiplying by 100. Details are provided in ESM 1, which also presents a temporal analysis of model-based quality by publication year.

Open Access Platform and Data Sharing

An openly accessible platform was developed using R shiny and R flexdashboard packages to provides an interface for users to view and download data in various formats, including Excel, CSV, and PDF based on user-defined criteria. These criteria include publication period, language, type of economic evaluation, study design, model type, and analytical approach. For the subset of studies meeting the selected criteria, the platform presents detailed information on health economic outcomes and study quality in tables and graphs. In addition, the platform lists previously published systematic reviews that meet our selection criteria, enabling researchers and decision makers to integrate evidence beyond the scope of the present review.

The web application is available at: https://hao-pham.shinyapps.io/OpenSLR_dashboard/.

Additionally, all relevant documents related to this work are openly accessible through the Open Science Framework, with the project identifier 10.17605/OSF.IO/V6W9A [34].

Ultimately, 128 articles met the eligibility criteria and were included in this review [35–162]. Population

Health economic evaluations were conducted in 36 countries, with most studies originating from the United States, the Netherlands, and China. The majority of studies (n = 84) focused on women at average risk of breast cancer, while others examined women with dense breasts (n = 17), women with a family history of breast or ovarian cancer (n = 6), genetic mutations (n = 5), and other risk factors (n = 39). The ‘other’ category encompasses a diverse range of risk groups defined using various risk assessment tools, such as Nottingham Prognostic Index, lifetime risk score or Harvard Cancer Index. Due to this heterogeneity, further stratified analysis regarding risk factor was not conducted.

Screening was typically initiated between ages 40–49 years (n = 64) or 50–59 years (n = 62) and ended between 70 and 79 years (n = 67), though early cessation (60–69 years, n = 54) was also commonly assessed.

Screening Modalities

Mammography, either alone or combined with other modalities, was the most studied screening modality, evaluated in 109 studies as an intervention and in 76 studies as a comparator. No screening was also a common comparator in the health economic evaluations, featured in 75 studies. Other screening modalities, such as MRI, ultrasound, and tomosynthesis, were evaluated less frequently.

Study Designs

Among included studies, 96 employed model-based health economic evaluations, 15 relied on empirical data, 14 combined both approaches, while three studies did not specify their approaches. Cost-utility analysis (n = 62) and cost-effectiveness analysis (n = 42) were predominant, with 18 studies using both methods. Additionally, cost-consequence analysis was utilized in four studies, presenting the costs and consequences of alternative interventions without aggregating them into a single metric such as an ICER.

Model Types and Analytical Approaches

State-transition modeling was the most frequently used model type (35 studies), followed by discrete-event simulation (n = 14) and natural history model (n = 13) as a standalone model type. Moreover, a combination between one of these types and a decision-tree or other model type were also commonly applied. A substantial proportion of studies did not specify their model types (n = 19). Most studies used a single model for the health economic analysis, though six studies utilized multiple models (up to five). Cohort-simulation (n = 53) and microsimulation (n = 47) were the main analytical approaches.

Other Aspects

Most model-based studies (61/96) and those combining empirical data with extrapolation using a modeling approach (10/14) employed a lifelong time horizon, while solely empirical studies used shorter time horizons (1–5 years). The health care sector perspective was most adopted, followed by the payer, societal, and patient perspectives.

Results Stratified by Modality

Mammography

Mammography, either alone or combined with other screening modalities, is reported to be cost effective compared with no screening across all populations, based on the conclusions of the individual studies. Among women with average risk, mammography alone is shown to be cost effective in 33 studies while not cost effective in 20 studies, especially in the younger age range (40–49 years). Comparisons of mammography strategies with alternative modalities are less common, and the cost-effectiveness results are mixed depending on risk factors, starting age of screening, and screening interval.

Ultrasound

Among the 19 studies examining ultrasound as either a standalone modality or as a part of breast cancer screening strategies, cost-effectiveness results were mixed. In women with an average risk of breast cancer, four studies [78, 88, 150, 161] found that ultrasound-based screening was cost effective compared with no screening. In women with dense breasts, one study found that one round of screening with automated breast ultrasound alone or combined with mammography is cost effective compared with mammography [141]. However, another study found that recurrent screening with ultrasound combined with mammography (with/without MRI) is not cost effective compared with no screening or mammography alone [103].

MRI

MRI was only applied among women at high risk of breast cancer or at the second screen in people at average risk among the included economic papers. The cost-effectiveness results varied, mostly depending on risk factors. For instance, compared with mammography, MRI either alone or in combination with other modalities was generally cost effective for women with dense breasts [72, 76, 99, 101] and for those with a family history of breast or ovarian cancer [66]. However, screening with MRI starting from a young age (25–30 years) was not cost effective compared with no screening or mammography alone in women with previous treatment using radiation therapy [42, 87, 109]. When examining different MRI strategies, MRI alone every 4 years was found to be cost effective compared with MRI every 5 years, however, further shortening of the screening interval did not result in more favorable cost-effectiveness outcomes [72].

Impact of Uptake and Adherence on Cost Effectiveness

The influence of uptake on the cost effectiveness of screening strategies was investigated in seven studies [37, 46, 51, 147, 152, 162, 163]. All studies showed that as uptake increased, the ICER rose slightly but did not change the overall cost-effectiveness conclusion. However, it could significantly increase health gains. Similarly, the effect of adherence on cost effectiveness was examined in four studies [95, 109, 119, 163]. These studies consistently showed that as the adherence increased, health gain improved, costs rose, and the ICER remained stable or slightly decreased. Wong et al. (2023) specifically demonstrated that decreasing adherence from full to partial (65% of women fully adherent, 25% partially adherent, and 10% not undergoing screening) reduced the LY and QALY by 13–20%, decreased costs by 0.6–2%, and increased the ICER by 3–11%, while maintaining consistent cost-effectiveness results [109]. Additionally, Arnold and Quante (2018) evaluated the impact of varying adherence levels on the evaluation and selection of personalized screening strategies. They found that even small changes in adherence levels significantly affected the performance of screening strategies, suggesting that personalized screening may be a viable alternative to routine screening [119].

The Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Screening Programs

Four studies evaluated the economic implications of integrating AI into population-based breast cancer screening programs in Norway [153], the United Kingdom [143, 159], and Sweden [151]. AI was incorporated in different ways—as a decision-support tool for radiologists, as a second reader replacing one human reader, and within risk-stratified screening strategies that tailor screening intensity based on AI-based risk prediction. The evaluations focused on routine screening populations in organized mammography programs (generally women aged 40–74 years).

Overall, these modeling studies suggested that AI-assisted screening could be cost effective or cost saving, mainly by reducing reading workload or enabling more efficient screening strategies. However, results were sensitive to assumptions about AI performance, costs, and implementation models. Empirical evidence is needed to reduce the uncertainty.

Empirical Data–Based Studies

Empirical data–based studies, either with or without extrapolation, achieved the highest quality, with a median score of 65%. The studies with unspecified health economic evaluation methods exhibited the lower quality score, with a median of 30% (Fig. 4B).

Key Methodological Features Influencing Model Quality

Among the 48 model-based studies that scored above the median for quality, state-transition models were the most frequently used model type, either as a standalone model type (18/48) or in combination with other models (6/48). Microsimulation was the most common analytical approach (27/48). These studies predominantly employed a lifelong time horizon (32/48), applied a discount rate (45/48), and conducted both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (27/48).

One study achieved a perfect quality score (100%). This study was a microsimulation state-transition model that incorporated all the aforementioned features, conducted comprehensive validation, and provided detailed reporting of inputs, results, and supporting data [130].

In contrast, among the 48 studies scoring below 55%, more than half employed a cohort simulation (25/48). Only 16 studies (33%) used a lifelong time horizon and just 13 studies performed both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (22%). Common issues included incomplete validation, limitations in data suitability for the decision problem, and insufficient reporting. Many of these studies lacked documentation on face validity, internal and external validation, and essential technical details for replicating.

Discussion

Summary and Interpretation of the Results in the Context of Existing Evidence

Study Characteristics

Our systematic review included 128 health economic evaluations of BC screening strategies conducted globally. Mammography emerged as the most evaluated modality. The age range for screening varied across studies, with 40–79 years as the most frequent range. Notably, earlier screening initiation was frequently considered in women with increased risk. These observations are consistent with other systematic reviews on this topic [18, 21], and they align with the current clinical recommendations in both the US and Europe [164, 165].

Health Economic Results

The majority of studies indicated that mammography was cost effective compared with no screening for women at both average and elevated risk of breast cancer. This underscores why mammography remains the gold standard for breast cancer screening worldwide, despite advancements in technologies [166–168]. However, the cost-effectiveness results should be interpreted with full awareness of uncertainty surrounding assumptions and input parameters, especially related to clinical effectiveness of mammography [3, 169]. Cost-effectiveness findings for ultrasound and MRI-based screening strategies were more spread. Ultrasound-based screening was generally cost effective compared with no screening, suggesting its potential use in low-resource settings where mammography is unavailable [78, 88, 150, 161]. Although clinical evidence supported MRI plus mammography in women with dense breasts [7, 170], this combination has not yet been adopted into national screening programs [171]. Given its favorable cost effectiveness, along with strong clinical evidence, decision makers may consider incorporating it into screening strategies for women with dense breasts.

Methodological Insights

In terms of model types, our review highlights a shift in the methods used for assessing the cost effectiveness of breast cancer screening. Historically, discrete event simulation (DES) models were predominantly employed, particularly in literature published before 2013 [18]. However, our findings indicate that state-transition models have become the preferred approach in recent years. This shift may be attributed to the simplicity and transparency of state-transition models, although inherently memoryless and less flexible than DES in handling time-dependent transition probabilities. State-transition models are often sufficient for many applications to represent long-term disease progression, while omitting individual-level interactions and event-level complexity that DES models explicitly include [172].

Regarding the analytical approaches, microsimulation models previously dominated the literature [20], with the MISCAN model (the ‘micro-simulation analytic model’) being particularly prominent [16]. However, cohort simulation is often used in more recent evaluations. This shift may be explained by the fact that cohort simulations are less complex to develop. Although cohort simulations performed slightly better in the domain of data analysis, microsimulations exceled in the domains of model validation, design, and interpretation. This highlights the trade-off between simplicity and comprehensiveness—cohort simulations offer simplicity, which can be beneficial in certain contexts, while microsimulation provides a more sophisticated and comprehensive framework capable of addressing a wider range of policy questions [173]. To address some of the limitations associated with microsimulation, Shafik et al. proposed the flexible stage distribution model—a novel framework designed to evaluate the cost effectiveness of breast cancer screening in long-standing programs with incomplete historical screening data [174]. Although this model represents a promising advancement, it is important to note that it has yet to undergo rigorous validation. Additionally, it could be good practice to use multiple models for the analysis to allow examination of results across models, which has been done in some of the included studies [38–40, 42, 116, 126]. This can help address structural uncertainties and strengthen the credibility of study conclusions.

Among solely empirical data–based evaluations, a key limitation observed was the misalignment between the reported time horizon and the outcomes measured. Most of these evaluations employed relatively short time horizons but included long-term outcomes, such as LYs or QALYs gained. This approach contradicts the CHEC-extended checklist recommendation that trial-based economic evaluations are appropriate only when the intervention effects do not extend beyond the trial duration and intermediate outcomes have no significant impact on long-term clinical endpoints [32].

Strengths and Limitations of the Current Review

Our review has several key strengths. First, our review applied the FAIR principles (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability) in research. This approach ensures that future researchers can easily locate, access, and build upon our work. It also facilitates the generation of further evidence through more focused literature reviews. Additionally, we developed an interactive platform that allows decision makers to tailor their selection criteria and instantly view synthesized results. This platform and the associated data repository also support the updating and extension of the review as new evidence emerges. Through collaboration via the Open Science Framework, future research teams can contribute to and build upon the existing dataset, representing an important step toward more dynamic and updatable evidence synthesis in the field. Second, we developed a comprehensive literature search strategy that balanced precision and breadth, ensuring that all relevant studies meeting the eligibility criteria were identified. Consequently, our review included more articles than any previous systematic reviews on this topic. Moreover, our platform also reported all other published systematic reviews on this topic. Previous systematic reviews covered broad and varying study periods, with the earliest reviews including studies from as early as 1974 and search end dates ranging from 2010 to 2021. If needed, findings from these earlier reviews could be integrated with those of the present review to provide a more comprehensive synthesis of the overall evidence base. Third, we employed a thorough synthesis of all included studies, providing a detailed analysis of their characteristics and methodological approaches. This comprehensive synthesis enabled us to critically appraise the quality of the existing evidence and summarize the health economic outcomes. Our findings pinpointed distinctive features associated with the models’ quality that support future health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening.

Despite its strengths, a limitation of our review is that data extraction and quality assessment were conducted by a single reviewer for each study rather than independently by two reviewers. To mitigate potential bias and ensure the quality and reliability of our findings, we implemented a multi-step data extraction process. This included testing and refining the data extraction form collaboratively with two reviewers and performing a final quality check of the extracted data and quality assessment results.

Implications, Generalizability of the Results for Policy, and Recommendations for Future Research

Our systematic review highlights global evidence on health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening, offering valuable insights for decision makers considering new screening programs or improving existing ones.

The transparency and openness of our review pave the way for future evidence synthesis in various forms, including replication, extension, and updates to our current work. New evidence can be derived from our data extraction table by applying specific filters, such as focusing on health economic evaluations of breast cancer screening in high-income or low- and middle-income countries. Additionally, researchers can investigate the impacts of methodological approaches, sponsorship, screening aspects, and population characteristics on the quality and outcomes of health economic evaluations.

From a methodological perspective, our description of distinctive features associated with the models’ quality offers recommendations for future health economic model development in the context of breast cancer screening. First, the choice between model-based and empirical data–based evaluations should be guided not only by the availability of empirical data but also by the relevance of the outcomes that researchers aim to estimate. For short-term outcomes (e.g., the number of cancer cases detected, false negatives, and unnecessary biopsies due to false positives), empirical data–based evaluations are appropriate. However, if long-term outcomes such as LYs or QALYs are desired, extrapolation using a modeling approach should be incorporated. Such extrapolation requires careful consideration of potential uncertainty, as long-term projections may overestimate or, in some cases, underestimate the true effect depending on assumptions about the persistence of effects over time. Second, we recommend considering state-transition microsimulation models as a primary modeling approach due to their capacity to handle complex scenarios over extended time horizons. However, researchers could also benefit from combining different model types or using cohort simulations to balance the strengths and limitations of individual models, ensure robustness, and avoid bias caused by insufficient data or overcomplication. Whichever methodological approach is chosen, emphasis should be placed on a systematic approach for data collection, transparency, and thorough validation to ensure that models are reliable and their results credible for policymakers. There are several tools available to guide these processes, for example, the AdViSHE checklist is recommended for model validation [175] and the CHEERS checklist is advised for reporting health economic evaluations [176]. Moreover, authors should use tools such as the CHEC-extended checklist or ISPOR checklist to guide quality of the evaluation. Although these checklists are well suited to assess the quality of original health economic evaluations, they lack scoring systems or thresholds that could be applied in the context of systematic reviews. Therefore, a modified version of these tools, which we have developed and made available in the ESM, can be used for systematic reviews on breast cancer screening. These modified tools will serve as a temporary solution until more refined versions or new generic tools become available.

Conclusions

Our systematic review indicates that mammography is the most extensively studied screening modality and often found to be cost effective compared with no screening. Supplementing mammography with ultrasound and/or MRI could be cost effective depending on comparator, risk factors, age range, and screening interval. The transition from discrete event simulation to state-transition models, along with the increasing use of various methodological approaches, signifies advancement in the field. However, the overall suboptimal quality of studies highlights the necessity for greater transparency, thorough validation, and comprehensive reporting in future health economic evaluations. The interactive platform we have developed enables users to access and analyze the included studies effectively. This tool supports informed decision making and encourages further research by offering detailed insights into the evidence base.
