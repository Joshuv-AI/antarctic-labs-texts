The rheumatry registry: design and data collection methods

Introduction

Disease registries systematically collect standardized, longitudinal data on patients with defined conditions and serve multiple critical roles in modern healthcare. They support clinical care by tracking treatment quality and patient outcomes, inform public health policy, and enable real-world research on treatment effectiveness and safety, for example, through evaluation of biologic agents in routine practice outside clinical trials. In rheumatology, registries such as the U.S. CORRONA [1] (Consortium of Rheumatology Researchers of North America), the U.K.’s BSRBR [2] (British Society for Rheumatology Biologics Register), and the EUSTAR [3] (European Scleroderma Trials and Research) registry for systemic sclerosis have been instrumental in yielding insights exceeding those gleaned from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) alone. These registries frequently include assessments of disease activity, medication use, health-related quality of life, adverse events, and comorbid conditions, collected during routine care and over long periods, thereby filling essential gaps left by RCTs.

In chronic and autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), registries monitor long-term disease course, real-world use of therapies, including high-cost biologics and the burden of comorbidity. The ability to capture diverse patient populations over time enables comparative effectiveness research, including safety surveillance of serious infections and malignancies due to anti-tumor necrosis factor agents. European and North American registries have been leveraged for large-scale epidemiological and health economics studies, reflecting registry use in policy guidance, such as optimizing biologic therapy choices based on cost-effectiveness and safety data.

Despite growing global awareness of their value, robust rheumatic disease registries remain limited in low- and middle-income regions, including the Middle East [4]. Although biologic agents have become more available, many countries in the region lack registries to ensure post-market pharmaco-surveillance and generate region-specific real-world evidence [5]. A notable review highlighted that only a few countries in Africa and the Middle East, such as Morocco and South Africa, had functioning biologics registries by 2012, underscoring significant gaps in infrastructure [6]. Significantly, Middle Eastern region bears a rising burden of musculoskeletal disease; yet, systematic registries are largely absent [4, 7].

Iran exemplifies this registry deficit even though population studies report rheumatoid arthritis prevalence estimates in the country in the range of approximately 0.33–0.37% [8]. In addition, recent epidemiological analyses [7, 9] show that musculoskeletal disorders are major drivers of disability in the Middle East and North Africa, and that their burden has increased over recent decades. In particular, Iran is among the countries in the region with a high burden of musculoskeletal disease, which increases the need for locally relevant, longitudinal clinical data to inform health policy and clinical care. These population-level findings support the establishment of a national registry like Rheumatry to collect real-world evidence on disease patterns, treatment use, and long-term outcomes in Iranian patients [7].

Recognizing this gap, rheumatologists at Tehran University of Medical Sciences established Rheumatry, the country’s inaugural national registry for rheumatic diseases, in 2016. Supported by Iran’s Ministry of Health and led by the Rheumatology Research Center, Rheumatry aims to foster collaboration among patients, clinicians, and researchers to promote research and new discovery in the field of rheumatic diseases including RA, SLE, SSc, and Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) via standardized data collection on a national scale.

This study outlines the design, governance, infrastructure, and data collection instruments of Rheumatry. We describe the electronic case-report forms (CRFs) used at patient enrollment and follow-up visits, highlight disease-specific domains (including classification criteria, disease activity indices, treatment histories, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs)), and compare these tools with internationally established registries, such as CORRONA [1], BSRBR [2], EUSTAR [3], and Portugal’s Reuma.pt [10], to demonstrate alignment with best practices and identify points of innovation. In doing so, our goal is to document Rheumatry’s structure for an international audience, enable local investigators to contextualize their research variables, and provide a framework that may guide similar registry efforts in other low- and middle-income settings.

Registry overview

Rheumatry is a national, multi-center rheumatology registry launched in 2016 under the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education. It is governed by the Rheumatology Research Center (RRC) at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS). An overview of patient enrollment, data flow, governance, and research access within Rheumatry is shown in Fig. 1. Rheumatry enrolls patients with confirmed rheumatic diseases from 26 participating hospitals and clinics across Iran (currently > 11,900 patients and 26,100 visits in total). Membership is open to rheumatologists at academic or hospital centers; after institution-level approval and signing a data-sharing agreement, individual clinicians receive secure logins to the web-based portal (designed by BioArc). Access is role-based: central administrators view all data, center coordinators see only their center’s patients, and treating physicians see only their own patients’ records. The platform is hosted on a protected server with HTTPS encryption and maintains audit trails for all data entries. Routine backups are performed and data are stored in a relational database. Patient records are de-identified (unique registry IDs) to protect privacy, and data exports (e.g. CSV) can be generated for approved research.

Ethics and consent

The Rheumatry protocol was approved by the TUMS institutional review board. All participants provide written informed consent. No personal identifiers (name, contact, national code, etc.) are exported for research. Data collection complies with national data protection regulations.

Participant consent is designed to be broad enough to permit future approved research and controlled linkage to national registries (for example, mortality, cancer, and tuberculosis registries) while preserving confidentiality. Linkage to external data sources will be performed using secure, approved protocols (e.g. trusted third party or privacy-preserving linkage) so that direct personal identifiers are never exposed to analysts. An independent data access committee reviews proposals for external data use, ensuring scientific merit, ethical oversight, and compliance with national privacy rules. These measures balance research usefulness with participant privacy and are consistent with current good practice in registry governance [11].

Governance, data access and oversight

To ensure transparent stewardship and fair access to Rheumatry data, governance is provided through a multi-member Steering Committee and an independent Data Access Committee (DAC). The Steering Committee (clinical leads, registry data managers, and representatives from participating sites) oversees strategic priorities, budget, and quality assurance. The DAC receives external data requests and evaluates them for scientific rigor, ethical compliance, feasibility, and potential conflicts of interest. Investigators wishing to access de-identified registry data must submit a brief proposal (research question, variables requested, planned analyses, and ethical approval letter) to the DAC. Approved projects require a Data Use Agreement (DUA) that specifies permitted uses, authorship expectations, and obligations to return derived datasets and aggregated results to the registry. The DAC will prioritize projects that advance public health, foster capacity building among participating centers, and protect participant confidentiality. Membership, conflict-of-interest policy, and a redacted DAC application form are available on request from the corresponding author.

Long-term sustainability and governance

To support long-term sustainability and good governance, the registry was designed following common lessons from modern registry initiatives. In planning stage we engaged diverse stakeholders (clinical leads, site coordinators, health-informatics staff, and policy representatives) and set up formal roles and responsibilities for data custodianship, access review, and quality oversight. This approach follows recommended best practices for establishing sustainable disease registries, including involvement of patients and stakeholders, a defined governance body, and procedures to secure ongoing funding and stakeholder buy-in. These steps help to reduce duplication, improve transparency in data use, and facilitate multi-center collaboration [11].

Participants and data collection

Eligible patients are adults (and pediatric patients where relevant) with a confirmed diagnosis of one of the registry diseases, ascertained by standard classification criteria. Inclusion is disease-specific: for example, rheumatoid arthritis (RA) cases fulfill the 2010 ACR/EULAR [12] (or 1987 ACR [13]) criteria; systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) cases meet ACR 1997 [14] or SLICC 2012 [15] criteria; systemic sclerosis (SSc) cases meet 2013 ACR/EULAR criteria [16]; ankylosing spondylitis (AS) cases meet the modified New York criteria; Takayasu’s arteritis (TAK) cases meet the 1990 ACR [17] criteria. An osteoarthritis (OA) module is also listed in the system (under development). Patients unable or unwilling to consent, or unable to attend follow-up (e.g. terminal illness), are excluded.

We prioritized conditions based on prevalence, burden of disease, and the availability of validated classification criteria. Other CTDs and vasculitides were excluded from the initial rollout primarily due to heterogeneity in presentation and the need for specialized CRF development; we explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation and propose modular expansion.

To balance completeness and feasibility, Rheumatry uses a two-tiered data-collection strategy in practice. A compact core dataset (demographics, key diagnoses, date of onset, principal disease activity score, current major therapies, and serious adverse events) is required for all enrolled patients to ensure good coverage and minimal missingness. More detailed modules (full laboratory panels, imaging scores, extended family pedigrees, and biospecimen logging) are optional or collected at research sub-centres with capacity. This staged approach reduces burden for busy clinics and maximizes data completeness for primary variables while preserving ability for deep phenotyping at specialized sites [11].

Classification criteria and re-classification flexibility

At registry inception we recorded the classification system(s) used for each enrolled patient (for example, 1987 ACR and/or 2010 ACR/EULAR for RA; ACR 1997 and SLICC 2012 for SLE; 2013 ACR/EULAR for SSc; Modified New York for AS; and 1990 ACR for Takayasu arteritis). Importantly, Rheumatry stores the individual constituent elements of these criteria (clinical signs, laboratory results, imaging findings) rather than only a binary “meets criteria” flag. This design enables retrospective re-classification of cases under updated criteria (e.g., newer ACR/EULAR definitions) or sensitivity analyses using alternate diagnostic rules, thereby preserving longitudinal comparability as classification standards evolve.

Data collection forms

For each disease module, Rheumatry uses structured electronic case report forms (eCRFs) at baseline (intake) and at each follow-up visit. In total there are 10 forms (5 intake and 5 follow-up). Intake forms capture comprehensive baseline data, while follow-up forms update interval outcomes and treatments. All forms share core sections on demographics, social history, comorbidities, and treatment history, plus disease-specific sections. Form contents were adapted from international registry instruments and are customized to local use.

Demographics and social history

At intake, all forms record standard demographics (age, sex, residential region (city/province), a contact method to follow-up (telephone and/or email), occupation, education). Lifestyle factors are queried, including smoking (amount and duration), hookah use, alcohol intake (type, volume, frequency), and recreational drug use. Women are asked about pregnancy history, menopause status, and contraception. For example, the RA intake form collects smoking status and exposures (e.g., “Smoking (1 Pipe = 2.5 Cigarettes): Non-Smoker/Smoker, Date of Start, Quit Date, Cigarettes/Day”). All forms also capture comorbid conditions in categories (metabolic, cardiac, respiratory, gastrointestinal (GI), ocular, central nervous system (CNS), dermatologic, bone/muscle, hematologic, etc.). In each category, checkboxes indicate presence (Yes/No) and date of onset of common conditions (e.g. diabetes, hypertension, tuberculosis, asthma, thyroid disease, cataract, depression, osteoporosis, anemia, malignancy). Family history of autoimmune and other chronic diseases is documented in all forms, typically via a pedigree chart with relatives (parents, siblings, children, grandparents, aunts/uncles, etc.) and a list of conditions (e.g. RA, SLE, AS, scleroderma, Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), diabetes, atherosclerosis, hypertension, etc.). Thus, each registry record includes extensive background information on genetics and risk factors.

Disease history and classification

Each intake form records the date of initial symptoms and date of formal diagnosis. Patients are asked about the nature of their first manifestations (e.g. for SLE: mucocutaneous rash, serositis, renal disease, neurological symptoms, etc.; for AS: chronic back pain with morning stiffness; for SSc: Raynaud’s phenomenon onset; for TAK: ischemic or constitutional onset symptoms). Overlap syndromes (presence of another rheumatic disease) are queried in a checklist (e.g. “Overlap Syndrome: Antiphospholipid syndrome; Scleroderma; Rheumatoid Arthritis; Sjögren’s; Behçet’s; etc.”). All intake forms include the formal classification criteria for the disease, allowing verification of diagnosis. For example, the RA form lists both the 1987 ACR and 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria with scoring tables. The SSc form includes the 1980 ACR criteria (including CREST features) and the 2013 ACR/EULAR point system. The AS form includes the Modified New York criteria and notes related diagnoses (psoriasis, IBD). The registry thus explicitly records which classification items a patient meets, facilitating cohort validation.

Clinical features and physical examination

The intake forms systematically record organ- and system-specific symptoms and exam findings. For instance, the SLE form has extensive checklists for all organ systems: neuropsychiatric (e.g. seizures, psychosis, neuropathies), constitutional (fever, fatigue, weight loss), ocular (uveitis, retinopathy), respiratory (Interstitial lung disease (ILD), effusions), musculoskeletal (arthralgias, deformities, avascular necrosis), renal manifestations (edema, hypertension, nephritic/nephrotic syndrome, biopsy class), mucocutaneous lesions (malar rash, discoid lesions, alopecia, ulcers, etc.), vascular events (venous/arterial thromboses, Raynaud’s phenomenon), and pregnancy outcomes (preeclampsia, fetal loss, infant complications). Similarly, the SSc form includes detailed skin and vascular findings: Raynaud’s (onset, triggers), telangiectasia, digital ulcers/gangrene or scars, calcinosis, sclerodactyly, and pigment changes. It also incorporates the Modified Rodnan Skin Score, grading skin thickness in 17 body areas. The SSc form queries GI dysmotility (reflux, dysphagia, diarrhea, malabsorption), pulmonary symptoms (dyspnea, cough, 6-minute walk test), cardiac symptoms (chest pain, syncope, history of myocardial infarction or hypertension), renal crisis or proteinuria, and musculoskeletal symptoms (stiffness, arthritis, tendon friction rubs, muscle weakness). The AS form focuses on axial and peripheral features: chronic inflammatory back pain (duration, severity, improvement with exercise), peripheral arthritis or enthesitis (presence and locations), and extra-articular features such as uveitis, psoriasis, IBD, and dactylitis (not shown). The TAK form emphasizes vascular symptoms: limb claudication, blood pressure discrepancies, bruits, and ischemic pain, though details are similar in format to the other forms (the specific vascular exam findings and imaging results are collected on the follow-up form). In all intake forms, a standard review of systems is included (fever, weight loss, fatigue, cough, rash, etc.), and vital signs (BP, pulse, height, weight, BMI) are recorded.

Laboratory and imaging data

A key feature of each intake form is the comprehensive capture of laboratory tests and imaging studies relevant to the diagnosis and monitoring of the disease. Routine labs (complete blood count, inflammatory markers, chemistry panel, liver tests, lipid profile, muscle enzymes) are listed, usually as blank tables where values and ranges can be entered. Autoantibody and serology results are recorded (e.g. ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, anti-Ro/La for SLE; anti-CCP, RF for RA; anti-Scl-70 and anticentromere for SSc; HLA-B27 for AS). For example, the SLE intake form has sections for “Laboratory Name/City/Date” and entries for ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, anti-RNP, antiphospholipid antibodies, complement levels, etc. It also includes viral serologies and urinalysis. Imaging studies are documented: chest X-ray or CT findings (interstitial lung disease, pleural effusions, nodules), musculoskeletal radiographs (joint erosions, sacroiliitis), echocardiography (pericardial effusion, pulmonary hypertension), bone density scans, and others such as endoscopy or sonography if performed (the SLE form even includes colonoscopy and endoscopy results). These entries allow the registry to track objective disease assessments and organ damage.

Patient-reported outcomes and disease activity

Several instruments measure patient function and disease activity. The Stanford Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) is included in the RA form (Persian version) to quantify disability in dressing, walking, hygiene, etc. The RA forms also include visual analog scales for pain and global health (0–100 mm VAS), the Early Morning Stiffness duration, and the DAS28 calculation (with ESR and CRP). Physician assessments (joint counts for swelling and tenderness, grip strength) and treatment plans are noted. Other forms similarly capture PROs: for example, the AS form records patient pain and global assessment (not shown in excerpt), and likely includes BASDAI/BASFI or similar indices (common in AS trials). In SLE and SSc, specific PROs are less standardized, but symptom severity (e.g. pain, fatigue) can be inferred from VAS fields or question rows. Overall, the registry gathers both subjective and objective markers of disease activity.

Treatment history

Each form records detailed medication history. Intake forms have tables for past medications (generic name, dose, start/stop dates, reason for discontinuation) and current medications (names, dose, frequency, start date). RA and SLE forms list specific DMARDs, biologics, and supportive drugs (e.g. methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, cyclophosphamide, biologics). Comorbidity-related medications (antihypertensives, anticoagulants, etc.) are also noted. Surgical history (joint replacements, synovectomies, vascular interventions) and corticosteroid injections are documented on intake. Follow-up forms update any medication changes, new surgeries, hospitalizations, or complications since the last visit (e.g. flares or infections).

Follow-up forms

After intake, follow-up forms are completed at each clinic visit (typically every 3–12 months). These are streamlined versions of the intake: they repeat updated demographics and history, interval events, and key disease assessments. Follow-ups include fields for any changes in smoking or alcohol, new pregnancies, new comorbidities or infections, new surgeries or hospitalizations since last visit. The physician notes any new symptoms or organ involvement. Physical exam findings (joint counts, skin score, etc.) and laboratory test results are updated. Crucially, disease activity measures and PROs are re-assessed: for example, the RA follow-up form records tender/swollen joint counts, ESR/CRP, DAS28, HAQ and patient global/pain VAS to determine response. New treatments (drug additions or discontinuations, dose changes) are entered along with reasons (inefficacy, adverse event, remission). Adverse events (infections, malignancies) are coded, often using MedDRA terms. The RA follow-up questionnaire also specifically tracks medication adherence (poor compliance: Yes/No) and pregnancy outcomes since last visit. Each visit form has fields for visit date and next planned visit. Optional biospecimen collection (e.g. blood for DNA, serum) is logged in the system.

Data management and quality control

Rheumatry is implemented as a secure electronic data capture (EDC) system. The web portal enforces data validation through controlled lists and logic checks: e.g., drop-down menus for medications and diagnoses, date pickers to ensure valid dates, and numeric range checks to prevent impossible lab values. Free-text entry is minimized. All edits and user actions are audited. The central data management team regularly reviews incoming data for completeness and consistency, issuing queries to sites as needed. Periodic training workshops are conducted to standardize data entry.

BioArc stores its data in tables, which are structured and linked to each other. Every registry is a set of related tables that represent a database model. There are some data tables that are common for all registries, such as patient demographics and clinical visit information, and there are others that depend on the clinical focus of the registries or the data collection protocol. BioArc is equipped with a safe method to produce data outputs without any compromise on patient confidentiality. These outputs may be formed in different formats and under various query conditions. CSV, Excel, and XML are the standard export formats of the system. Moreover, the platform permits users to associate projects with particular datasets so that they can get the data outputs for only that specific project. Besides, BioArc allows partial data exports, whereby users can select only the specified records for download. This method has the advantage of greatly diminishing data volume and thus, the speed of data retrieval increases. The export is done through the use of a streaming method, which is an excellent way to manage server memory and reduce computational overhead. The design guarantees that the system performs fast and efficiently even with large-scale registry data. In addition, despite using online forms, data are ultimately stored in a structured SQL database. Users can export de-identified data for analysis in CSV format or statistical software (SPSS, R, Stata) through built-in functions. Aggregate statistics (numbers of patients by disease, center, etc.) are updated in real time on the portal’s “Statistics” page, which currently shows 26 centers, 80 users, 11,940 patients and 26,098 visits in Rheumatry.

To promote interoperability and reusability of registry data, we are implementing mapping of registry variables to international terminologies and exchange standards. For example, key elements include mapping diagnoses and clinical terms to ICD-10 codes. Using these standards will make future linkage to electronic health records, national surveillance datasets, and international research consortia easier, and it supports the FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) for research data [18]. In addition to terminology mapping, we apply pragmatic data-entry practices to improve quality: automated logic checks, required fields for the core dataset, date pickers to avoid impossible dates, and standardized drop-down lists to limit free-text. Central data managers run periodic completeness and consistency reports and send queries to sites. Where possible, the registry will explore pre-population of fields from local laboratory or radiology systems to reduce manual entry. These combined strategies aim to limit missingness and enhance data quality while minimizing extra workload for clinicians [11].

To maximise interoperability and reuse, we also map core diagnoses and procedures to ICD-10 codes and code adverse events using MedDRA terms. Where appropriate, discrete data fields capture component items that permit re-derivation of common scores (e.g., DAS28 components, SLEDAI items, mRSS). We have adopted a pragmatic approach to exchange standards: the registry’s database schema supports future export in HL7 FHIR profiles or CSV/JSON structures, and variable dictionaries (with units and value sets) are maintained centrally. A full variable dictionary (comprehensive field list) is supplied in Supplementary Table S1 to assist external investigators in preparing DAC proposals.

In summary, the Rheumatry registry employs standardized intake and follow-up CRFs for each major rheumatic disease, capturing demographic, historical, clinical, laboratory, imaging, disease activity, and treatment data in detail. The forms are based on internationally recognized data elements and classification criteria, adapted for use in Iran. Supplementary Tables 1 and the above description illustrate the comprehensive scope of data collected. This robust EDC infrastructure, with governance and quality controls, enables longitudinal study of rheumatic disease phenotypes and outcomes in this cohort.

Discussion

Rheumatry represents an important innovation for rheumatology in Iran and the region. By systematically collecting comprehensive clinical data on RA, SLE, SSc, AS, and TAK, it creates a foundation for future research and quality improvement. Strengths and innovations of Rheumatry include its national scope and multi-disease coverage. Unlike many registries that focus on a single condition or treatment (e.g. biologic registries for RA), Rheumatry’s design anticipates a broad epidemiologic use. Its platform integrates the experiences of patients and physicians (e.g. enabling point-of-care data entry during clinic visits) and standardizes data across centers, which can improve comparability of care. The use of international indices and coding systems (ACR/EULAR criteria, disease activity scores, MedDRA) ensures compatibility with other registries. Moreover, by including diseases like TAK (often neglected in registries) and by planning modules for additional conditions (Behçet’s disease, vasculitis, etc.), Rheumatry fills a regional gap. Its existence may encourage collaborations with global initiatives (for example, Iranian centers could contribute to EUSTAR [3] SSc projects or join international Takayasu studies) and can help benchmark Iranian patients against global cohorts.

Rheumatry aligns in structure and purpose with major international registries (for example, CorEvitas/CORRONA [1], Reuma.pt [10], BSRBR [2] and EUSTAR [3]) in its use of web-based electronic case report forms, standard disease activity indices, PRO measures, and MedDRA coding for adverse events. Key differences reflect context and scope: Rheumatry is a national, multi-disease registry built in a resource-constrained setting and intentionally includes rare vasculitides (for example, Takayasu arteritis) alongside common inflammatory arthritides. Unlike registries principally focused on biologic safety (e.g., BSRBR-RA), Rheumatry’s multi-module design supports both broad pharmaco-epidemiology across drug classes and disease-specific translational substudies. As with established efforts (for example, CARRA and EUSTAR), Rheumatry aims to maintain interoperability and transparent governance to enable multi-center and international collaboration. (A more detailed item-by-item comparison is provided in Supplementary Table S2)

Looking forward, Rheumatry has potential to support pharmaco-surveillance and comparative-effectiveness studies similar to those produced by long-standing RA registries. International experience shows registries can identify long-term safety signals (for infections and malignancy), describe real-world drug utilization patterns, and support guideline-relevant outcomes research. By harmonizing key variables with international registries and by applying robust data-quality processes, Rheumatry can contribute Iranian data to multinational analyses and can answer regionally important questions about treatment safety and effectiveness. This is also aligned with recent syntheses that emphasise registries’ evolving role in real-world evidence and big-data approaches for rheumatology [19].

In summary, Rheumatry aligns well with international registry standards in its structure and scope. It differs primarily in context: it is pioneering for Iran and the region, where large-scale data systems have been rare. Its multi-disease design is somewhat unique, since many registries start with one index condition. This could facilitate comparative research (e.g. differences in comorbidity profiles across diseases) within the same patient population. Moreover, being locally managed, Rheumatry can adapt forms to Iranian clinical practice. For example, local investigators might include questions on consanguinity (relevant in some genetic diseases), or on endemic issues (e.g. screening for strongyloides or TB before immunosuppression), topics outside most Western registries.

Challenges and limitations

Building and maintaining the Rheumatry registry entails several operational and scientific challenges. Ensuring consistent data entry across centers requires training and motivation of busy clinicians and staff; we mitigate this through standardized case report forms, regular training, teleconferences and feedback reports, but missing data remains a risk, particularly for optional modules and biospecimen collection that are not uniformly available across sites. Enrollment is not population based and patients are enrolled at varying disease stages (left-censoring), which limits generalizability and constrains incidence estimates and time-to-event analyses without careful design. Measurement variability across centers (for example differences in laboratory platforms or imaging scoring) introduces heterogeneity despite harmonization efforts and periodic quality control; residual variability likely remains. Funding sustainability and technical maintenance, including software updates and server security, are additional concerns that could affect long-term data continuity. Finally, while we map registry data to international terminologies such as ICD-10 and MedDRA to improve interoperability and have governance that includes an independent Data Access Committee (similar to CORRONA and EUSTAR procedures), data sharing policies must carefully balance openness with patient privacy and differences in coding and local practice may limit direct comparability with some external registries.

Comparison of rheumatry with major international rheumatology registries

Rheumatry shares many structural and functional features with leading international rheumatology registries. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes key differences. Similar to CORRONA [1] (now CorEvitas) in the United States and Reuma.pt [10] in Portugal, Rheumatry emphasizes the systematic collection of “real-world” clinical practice data, including demographics, disease activity indices, PROs, laboratory parameters, and treatment exposures. These elements are captured consistently at baseline and follow-up visits through structured electronic forms, enhancing the registry’s utility for longitudinal observational research. However, unlike CORRONA and Reuma.pt, which operate in high-resource, well-funded healthcare environments, Rheumatry functions within a resource-constrained setting. This underscores the technical and logistical achievements of establishing and maintaining a multi-disease, web-based national registry in such a context.

Compared to the British Society for Rheumatology Biologics Register for RA (BSRBR-RA) [2], which has more than two decades of longitudinal safety data on RA patients receiving biologic therapies, Rheumatry is relatively young and currently lacks comparable historical depth. Nevertheless, it surpasses BSRBR in terms of breadth, encompassing a wider spectrum of rheumatic diseases beyond RA, including SLE, SSc, AS, and notably Takayasu arteritis (TAK). While BSRBR’s core strength lies in pharmacovigilance for RA biologics, Rheumatry integrates medication tracking and adverse event monitoring across all modules, enabling broader pharmacosurveillance in diverse autoimmune conditions.

EUSTAR [3], the European Scleroderma Trials and Research group, serves as an exemplary model of multinational collaboration, data harmonization, and standardized clinical assessment in systemic sclerosis. With over 23,000 patients enrolled from 200 + centers, EUSTAR sets a benchmark for data volume and consistency. Rheumatry’s SSc module mirrors EUSTAR’s in its use of ACR/EULAR classification criteria, Medsger severity scores, and comprehensive organ-specific assessments. While Rheumatry currently contributes only national data, its alignment with EUSTAR data elements and definitions positions it well for potential future collaboration or data pooling with international SSc consortia.

A particularly novel and distinctive feature of Rheumatry is its dedicated inclusion of Takayasu arteritis (TAK). To our knowledge, very few national rheumatology registries, whether in high- or low-resource settings, have systematically enrolled patients with TAK using validated disease activity and angiographic classification criteria. Most TAK data globally arise from small single-center cohorts, retrospective chart reviews, or administrative claims databases. By embedding standardized TAK-specific indices (e.g., NIH activity index [20], Numano angiographic types [21], ACR 1990 classification criteria [17]) into its core data collection, Rheumatry fills a significant gap in vasculitis research infrastructure. This offers an opportunity for Iran to contribute meaningfully to global TAK studies, providing longitudinal, standardized, real-world data from a Middle Eastern population that has been historically underrepresented in vasculitis research.

While Rheumatry may currently lag behind older registries in terms of duration and total enrollment, it demonstrates a remarkably forward-thinking, comprehensive design. It integrates best practices from international registries, while also addressing region-specific needs, such as locally relevant exposures, and Persian-language tools. Rheumatry’s multi-disease scope, combined with its capacity for real-time, web-based data entry and its commitment to international standards, positions it not only as a national resource for Iran but also as a potential contributor to the global rheumatology research ecosystem.

We modelled our governance and access framework on established registries (e.g., CARRA, EUSTAR) that employ independent data access committees and transparent data-use agreements to balance scientific access with participant protection.

Future directions

Going forward, Rheumatry aims to expand enrollment (adding more centers, including pediatric rheumatology clinics), enhance data modules (e.g. including radiographic scoring fields), and establish linkages (e.g. with national mortality or tuberculosis registries). It also plans to incorporate biospecimen collection in a subset of patients, enabling translational research. As data accrues, Rheumatry will facilitate studies on Iranian disease phenotypes, treatment patterns, and long-term outcomes, insights that can complement findings from Western registries.

Long-term sustainability is a main priority. We plan to pursue mixed funding routes including governmental support, competitive research grants, collaborations with academic networks, and possible fee-for-service analyses under strict governance. Engagement of patient organisations and demonstrating registry value by producing regular feedback reports to participating sites usually helps to secure ongoing commitment. These sustainability measures are consistent with lessons learned from recent qualitative studies of registry sustainability, which show that diverse stakeholder engagement, clear demonstration of value, and planning for flexible future data needs are essential for survival beyond initial funding windows [11].

Planned expansion of disease modules

Although the initial rollout prioritized a set of core diseases to ensure immediate feasibility and data quality, Rheumatry intends to expand modularly. We will follow a staged roadmap: (1) scoping and stakeholder engagement (clinical working groups and patient organisations) to define minimal data elements for each new module; (2) pilot implementation at select tertiary centers to refine CRFs and workflows; (3) phased national roll-out contingent on site training and funding. Candidate high-priority modules include Behçet’s disease, ANCA-associated vasculitides, and additional connective tissue diseases that are locally epidemiologically relevant. Final prioritization and timing will depend on consensus from clinical leads, available funding, and data-security review.”

Conclusions

Rheumatry’s national-scale infrastructure provides detailed longitudinal data on diverse rheumatic diseases in Iran. By facilitating research, surveillance, and quality improvement, it bridges a critical information gap in the Middle Eastern context.
