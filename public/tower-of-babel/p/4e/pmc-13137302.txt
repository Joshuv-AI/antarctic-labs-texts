Backbone double‐mutant cycle analysis quantifies hydrogen‐bond energies in proteins
Authors: Haoliang Zheng, Robert W. Newberry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein structure is stabilized by a variety of noncovalent interactions, but many remain incompletely understood. For example, several potentially ubiquitous interactions involving the protein backbone have been identified, but challenges in determining reliable experimental energies have prevented their integration into structural models. To address this challenge, we adapted a popular protein engineering approach, double‐mutant cycle analysis, for the quantification of backbone interactions. By combining this analytical paradigm with chemical peptide synthesis, we selectively probe backbone interactions while avoiding many confounding factors that have complicated previous efforts. We first validate this approach by quantifying the energy of canonical, cross‐strand hydrogen bonds in model β‐sheet proteins and find excellent agreement with previous results. We then extend this approach to quantify weak, intra‐strand hydrogen bonds that have recently been implicated in protein folding and misfolding. Our results provide the first experimental quantification of these interactions, corroborating previous computational predictions that individual intra‐strand hydrogen bonds contribute approx. 0.2 kcal/mol each, which is significantly given the frequency of these interactions. More broadly, our results illustrate a useful approach to probing backbone interactions in proteins, which can be readily applied to a variety of other systems.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding and controlling the structure of proteins and their derivatives remains a central goal of biochemistry (Kuhlman & Bradley, 2019). The diverse three‐dimensional conformations of amino‐acid polymers are stabilized largely by noncovalent interactions within the polypeptide chain, predominantly electrostatics, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, and the hydrophobic effect (Adhav & Saikrishnan, 2023; Newberry & Raines, 2019; Riley & Hobza, 2011; Schneider, 2022). Hydrogen bonds in particular play essential roles in organizing key protein structures, including α‐helices and β‐sheets (Pauling & Niemann, 1939). Together, the dominant forces in protein folding have received great attention (Dill et al., 2008), and yet, prediction of protein structure based solely on the chemical interactions between constituent functional groups remains elusive (Kryshtafovych et al., 2019), highlighting gaps in understanding of the underlying protein chemistry.

These limitations have motivated the search for additional contributions to protein folding, resulting in the identification of a broad suite of secondary interactions that contribute to protein structure and stability (Newberry & Raines, 2019). These interactions include C–H⸱⸱⸱O hydrogen bonds (Horowitz & Trievel, 2012; Scheiner, 2005; Scheiner et al., 2001), n → π* interactions (Bartlett et al., 2010; Choudhary et al., 2014; Newberry & Raines, 2017), chalcogen bonds (Iwaoka, 2015; Iwaoka et al., 2002; Mitchell, 2017), and interactions with aromatic rings (i.e., cation–π, X–H⸱⸱⸱π, π–π, anion–π, and sulfur–arene) (Burley & Petsko, 1985; Chakravarty et al., 2018; Dougherty, 2013; Reid et al., 1985; Steiner & Koellner, 2001). These interactions are generally either weaker (e.g., C–H⸱⸱⸱O hydrogen bonds) and/or less common (e.g., anion–π) than canonical interactions (e.g., electrostatics) (Newberry & Raines, 2019). Nevertheless, computational prediction (Schreiner et al., 2004), structural meta‐analysis (Derewenda, 2004), and a selection of experimental studies (Riemen & Waters, 2010; Tatko & Waters, 2002, 2003; Tatko & Waters, 2004a; Travis et al., 2024; Tsou et al., 2002) have shown that these interactions can contribute meaningfully to the stability, organization, and folding of polypeptides.

Despite these exciting observations, the integration of these forces into current structural models of proteins (e.g., molecular force fields) faces a number of barriers. In particular, accurate experimental quantification of interaction energetics remains challenging. Selective perturbation of individual interactions requires precise chemical substitution to avoid confounding influences. For example, eliminating a functional group that participates in one interaction of interest often disrupts a variety of others, complicating analysis. To overcome some of these challenges, reductionist approaches have sought to mimic protein structures with small molecules at the expense of probing native connectivity, topology, and/or chemical environment.

Quantifying chemical interactions within the protein backbone poses still further challenges. Without robust biological approaches to modification or substitution of the protein backbone, studies of backbone interactions require chemical synthesis. Moreover, most secondary interactions in the backbone are expected to be weak. For example, C–H⸱⸱⸱O hydrogen bonds are generally believed to contribute less than 1 kcal/mol each (Yohannan et al., 2004), and individual n → π* interactions likely contribute only approx. 0.25 kcal/mol each (Newberry et al., 2013). However, given their high expected frequency in proteins, which can be as high as 33% of all residues (Bartlett et al., 2010), the total contribution of secondary interactions to protein folding can be prodigious, motivating precise quantification of their energetics.

As a result of these intersecting challenges, the experimental quantification of secondary interaction energetics in protein models remains limited. A prime example is the so‐called C5 hydrogen bond (Figure 1a), which operates within the backbone of individual residues in β‐strands (Crisma et al., 2018; Gallagher‐Jones et al., 2018; Newberry & Raines, 2016). Experimental studies (Benedetti et al., 1988; Newberry & Raines, 2016; Toniolo et al., 1988) have shown that these interactions share the observable hallmarks of hydrogen bonding (e.g., red‐shifted IR frequencies, downfield NMR chemical shift), and bioinformatic analysis shows that they might be ubiquitous in folded proteins, affecting nearly 5% of residues. The frequency might be even higher in misfolded proteins, which often become enriched in β‐sheet secondary structure (Gallagher‐Jones et al., 2018; Murray et al., 2022; Newberry & Raines, 2016). Given the ongoing interest in understanding and controlling β‐sheet structures (Ardejani et al., 2017; Cochran et al., 2001; Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004; Deechongkit, Nguyen, et al., 2004; Fisk & Gellman, 2001; Jäger et al., 2009; Maynard et al., 2014; Nelson et al., 2005; Sawyer & Arora, 2018), particularly in misfolded proteins (Bieschke et al., 2008; Cheng et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2011; Tillett & Del Valle, 2020; Truex et al., 2016), it is important to understand the contributions of these ubiquitous interactions.

However, the total contribution of these interactions to protein structure remains unclear, due in part to limitations in their experimental quantification. Computations based on density functional theory (DFT) combined with natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis predict that individual interactions can contribute upwards of ~0.25 kcal/mol each, but previous experimental perturbation of C5 hydrogen bonds was only qualitative (Newberry & Raines, 2016). Although the experimental attenuation or enhancement of a single C5 hydrogen bond causes a predictable decrease or increase, respectively, in the stability of a model β‐hairpin, the approaches used to perturb backbone hydrogen bonds necessarily create confounding effects that preclude rigorous quantification.

Quantifying the energetic contributions of hydrogen bonds in general is complicated by their modest energy and dynamic/transient nature. As a result, a variety of complementary strategies have been employed to study hydrogen‐bond energetics (Baldwin, 2003). Computations based on both quantum mechanics and continuum electrostatics predict interaction energies of approx. 1 kcal/mol per hydrogen bond, which becomes significant since ~70% of peptide groups are hydrogen‐bonded in folded proteins (Avbelj et al., 2000; Avbelj & Baldwin, 2002; Honig & Yang, 1995). Computational fragmentation approaches have also been developed to estimate hydrogen‐bond energetics in peptide systems. In particular, the molecular tailoring approach (MTA) enables direct evaluation of interaction energies by isolating interacting fragments and has been applied to a variety of canonical hydrogen bonds. Reported MTA‐based calculations yield N–H···O=C hydrogen‐bond energies of ~4–6 kcal/mol in various tetrapeptide system (Ahirwar et al., 2021; Deshmukh et al., 2006, 2011; Deshmukh & Gadre, 2009, 2021). Those predictions have found experimental support from spectroscopic and calorimetric measurements, with thermodynamic analysis of helix–coil transitions in model peptides playing a central role (Lopez et al., 2002; Scholtz et al., 1991; Siedlecka et al., 1999).

One particularly effective strategy to quantify backbone hydrogen bond energies is to employ amide‐to‐ester substitutions, which both eliminate the hydrogen‐bond donor and attenuate the hydrogen‐bond acceptor (because esters are less basic than amides), and both of these effects have been widely exploited to probe hydrogen‐bond energetics (Choudhary & Raines, 2011; Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004; Deechongkit, Nguyen, et al., 2004; Gao & Kelly, 2008; Koh et al., 1997). For example, amide‐to‐ester substitutions in both α‐helical (Koh et al., 1997) and β‐sheet (Fu et al., 2006) structures point to hydrogen‐bond energies of approx. 0.8 kcal/mol (Fu et al., 2006). This approach has also shown that backbone hydrogen bonds are generally stronger when buried in hydrophobic environments, consistent with theoretical predictions (Deechongkit, Nguyen, et al., 2004). Furthermore, amide‐to‐ester substitutions have been exploited to study the contributions of hydrogen bonding to both folding kinetics (Eildal et al., 2013) and enzymatic catalysis (Desai et al., 2014).

However, though amide‐to‐ester substitutions are highly conservative relative to alternative perturbations, they also introduce a variety of potentially confounding issues. First, amide‐to‐ester substitutions necessarily perturb both a hydrogen‐bond donor and acceptor simultaneously, which can lead to conflation of their contributions (Choudhary & Raines, 2011). For example, inserting a single ester into the backbone of an α‐helix can perturb hydrogen bonds to the turn above as well as the turn below (Koh et al., 1997). Though careful choice/design of the model system can limit these effects (e.g., probing positions that function only as hydrogen‐bond donors but not acceptors), doing so dramatically restricts the interactions that can be probed. Moreover, even if a backbone amide is not involved in a hydrogen bond to other groups in the polypeptide, it is likely engaged in hydrogen bonds to solvent, so confounding effects of ester substitution remain difficult to avoid (Sereikaitė et al., 2018). Second, esters are generally less well‐solvated by water, owing in part to differences in hydrogen‐bonding capacity. Amide‐to‐ester substitutions therefore alter the overall hydrophobicity of the protein, which is likely to contribute to changes in folding and/or stability (Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004; Gao & Kelly, 2008). Previous applications of amide‐to‐ester substitutions have addressed this challenge by invoking the transfer free energies of model amides and esters to correct experimentally derived hydrogen‐bond energies, but these approaches are only approximate (Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004; Gao & Kelly, 2008). Third, esters exert greater inductive effects on adjacent chemical groups due to the higher electronegativity of oxygen relative to nitrogen. As a result, incorporating an ester is likely to perturb not only the position of interest but also nearby hydrogen‐bonded groups. For example, incorporating an ester at the i position of the protein will decrease the basicity and therefore the capacity to accept a hydrogen bond at the i + 1 amide (Sereikaitė et al., 2018). Fourth, esters have less double‐bond character in the C′–O bond than do amides in the C′–N bond, which can change the flexibility of the backbone (Choudhary & Raines, 2011). Fifth, replacement of an amide N–H with an ester oxygen creates an unnatural O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion with the carbonyl of the hydrogen‐bond acceptor (Fu et al., 2006). As a result, incorporating an ester as the hydrogen‐bond donor generally overestimates the strength of a hydrogen bond, which must be corrected by orthogonal experiments. For example, careful studies incorporating alkene isosteres of the peptide bond have estimated the O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion at approx. 0.3 kcal/mol (Fu et al., 2006). Sixth, incorporating an ester as the hydrogen‐bond acceptor conversely underestimates the hydrogen‐bond energy, since esters are still capable of accepting a hydrogen bond (Choudhary & Raines, 2011). As a result, even the ultra‐conservative substitution of a backbone amide with an ester poses a number of challenges for the experimental quantification of hydrogen‐bond energies. These challenges extend to the analysis of more recently discovered secondary interactions (e.g., C–H⸱⸱⸱O or C5 hydrogen bonds), further complicating their accurate quantification.

To address these challenges and enable the rigorous quantification of backbone interactions, we turned to a popular protein engineering approach for quantifying chemical interactions: double‐mutant cycle analysis (Cockroft & Hunter, 2007; Fersht et al., 1992; Horovitz & Fersht, 1990; Serrano et al., 1992). To quantify the energy of interaction between two chemical groups, each interaction partner is separately perturbed, and the ensuing changes in the energy of folding are compared to the change imparted by perturbing both groups simultaneously (Figure 1b). By subtracting the thermodynamic impact of each individual perturbation, this approach allows for rigorous quantification of the coupling between the perturbed groups while minimizing confounding variables (Maxwell et al., 2005; Pagano et al., 2021). For example, whereas the removal of a positive charge to probe a single electrostatic interaction might simultaneously change the solubility of the protein, the confounding change in solubility caused by this perturbation is largely the same in both the single and double mutants, so subtracting the change in energy of the single mutant from that of the double mutant effectively removes the confounding contributions.

We reasoned that double‐mutant cycle analysis would offer an opportunity to address many of the issues confounding the use of amide‐to‐ester mutations in probing hydrogen‐bond energetics, which might therefore enable us to probe heretofore neglected interactions like C5 hydrogen bonds (Figure 2). Specifically, double‐mutant cycle analysis stands to dramatically reduce confounding influences from coincident interactions, differences in functional‐group solvation, inductive effects on nearby groups, and changes in double‐bond character, since each would be expected to occur in both the double mutant and the relevant single mutants. Moreover, double‐mutant cycle analysis also offers an opportunity to reduce the confounding effects of the artifactual O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion that results from replacing an amide hydrogen‐bond donor with an ester, which is likely weaker in the presence of an ester acceptor than an amide, owing to the difference in partial negative charge on the carbonyl oxygen of the acceptor. The only major limitation of amide‐to‐ester modifications not addressed by double‐mutant cycle analysis is likely the propensity of this approach to underestimate interaction energies due to incomplete deletion of the hydrogen‐bond acceptor. However, amide‐to‐ester substitution does substantially reduce the hydrogen‐bonding capacity of the acceptor by about 50% (Eildal et al., 2013), resulting in a useful lower bound on the energy of the interaction.

This approach has been widely applied to study the thermodynamics of interactions between protein side chains, including noncanonical interactions such as C−H···π (Shi et al., 2002; Tatko & Waters, 2003, 2004b) and aromatic–aromatic interactions (Serrano et al., 1991), as well as the effects of different microenvironments on hydrogen bonding (Gao et al., 2009), among others (Carver et al., 2001; Cho et al., 2014; Horovitz & Fersht, 1990; Liu et al., 2019; Schreiber & Fersht, 1995; Searle et al., 1999; Sharman & Searle, 1998). Previous studies have also used this approach to study interactions between amino‐acid side chains and the backbone, revealing, for example, that side chains can significantly affect the strength of backbone hydrogen bonds by altering the polarity of the local microenvironment (Gao et al., 2009). Recent computational studies also predict that double‐mutation cycles are a promising strategy to quantify backbone hydrogen‐bond energetics using amide‐to‐ester mutations (Markthaler et al., 2018). However, to our knowledge, this is the first experimental application of double‐mutant cycle analysis to the quantification of interactions within the protein backbone. After first validating this approach by comparison to established methods, we apply this strategy to provide the first experimental estimates of the energy of a single backbone C5 hydrogen bond.

RESULTS

We first sought to validate our backbone double‐mutant cycle analysis on simple, well‐characterized models of ubiquitous protein secondary structures. As an initial target, we selected the two‐stranded β‐hairpin, specifically focusing on derivatives of the tryptophan zipper (TrpZip) peptide (Andersen et al., 2006), which demonstrate robust, cooperative folding despite their small size. Studies by many labs have shown that this scaffold is robust to a wide variety of modifications (Karnes et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2021; Liu & Gellman, 2021; Newberry & Raines, 2016; Riemen & Waters, 2009a, 2009b, 2010; Spekowius et al., 2021), giving us confidence that we could perturb backbone hydrogen bonds without compromising the overall fold. We identified an unremarkable cross‐strand hydrogen bond within the center of the hairpin, whose geometry is typical of β‐sheet hydrogen bonds (Figure 3a). To probe the energy of this representative interaction, we synthesized four variants with different combinations of amide‐to‐ester substitutions of the hydrogen‐bonded groups. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of each variant were qualitatively similar (Appendix S1), with excellent agreement in the position of minima and maxima, indicating retention of the overall fold upon substitution, as we hypothesized. Furthermore, thermal denaturation of all four variants exhibited similarly cooperative unfolding. Ellipticity data as a function of temperature fit well to a two‐state unfolding model (Greenfield, 2006; Richaud et al., 2021), allowing us to extract high‐confidence estimates of thermodynamic parameters including ΔH of unfolding (Seelig & Seelig, 2023), which has been essential for the calculation of accurate interaction energies in previous double‐mutant cycle analyses (Cho et al., 2014).

Upon substitution of the hydrogen‐bond donor amide with an ester (Figure 3b; HP‐WT vs. HP‐I‐D), we observed a 2.2 kcal/mol decrease in the enthalpy of folding, consistent with destabilization of the structure (Figure 3c,d). The change in enthalpy caused by replacing the donor with an ester is likely an overestimate of the contribution of the hydrogen bond, since (1) esters are less well‐solvated in the unfolded state than are amides, and (2) the ester creates an unnatural O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion that further destabilizes the fold, in addition to the effects of donor deletion. After replacing the donor with an ester, additional substitution of the acceptor with an ester (HP‐I‐D vs. HP‐I‐DA) caused a slight increase in folding enthalpy (0.1 kcal/mol), which likely results from reduction in the destabilizing, artifactual O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion, since esters have less negative charge on the carbonyl oxygen than do amides. This is likely a lower bound on the O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion, since replacing the acceptor with an ester does not eliminate the repulsion (Figure 3c,d).

Upon substitution of the acceptor amide with an ester in the presence of an amide donor (HP‐WT vs. HP‐I‐A), we observed a 1.2 kcal/mol decrease in folding enthalpy (Figure 3c,d), which is likely a lower bound on the actual contribution of this hydrogen bond, since esters are still capable of accepting hydrogen bonds (weakening 1.8 kcal/mol) (Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004). Indeed, upon completing the thermodynamic cycle, we calculated an enthalpic coupling of 1.3 kcal/mol for the interaction between these two peptide bonds (Figure 3c,d), which agrees closely with the energy of cross‐strand hydrogen bonds explored in previous studies (Eildal et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2006; Gao & Kelly, 2008; Wang et al., 2006).

We next sought to apply this method to examine the context dependence of hydrogen‐bond energetics. Like other isolated β‐hairpins, TrpZip hairpins fray near the termini, and as a result, average hydrogen‐bond lengths are generally longer than toward the center of the hairpin (Figure 4a), resulting in weaker interactions. For example, multiple spectroscopic and computational studies have shown that the terminal hydrogen bonds of TrpZip β‐hairpins are longer and therefore weaker than those near the center of the strands (Hauser et al., 2008; Huang et al., 2009; Jones et al., 2013). We therefore applied backbone double‐mutant cycle analysis to a terminal, cross‐strand hydrogen bond (Figure 4b), and in agreement with our hypothesis, we determined an interaction energy of 0.9 kcal/mol (Figure 4c,d), approx. 30% lower than a similar interaction in the middle of the hairpin (see above). This thermodynamic‐cycle approach is therefore sensitive to the known environmental sensitivity of hydrogen bonds.

Having applied this method to quantifying hydrogen bond energies in a simple β‐sheet model, we next sought to validate our approach on other well‐studied hydrogen bonds in canonical β‐sheet domains. Specifically, the three‐stranded Pin1 WW domain has received great attention as a model of β‐sheet folding and stability (De et al., 2012; Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004; Fu et al., 2006; Gao & Kelly, 2008; Hanazono et al., 2016; Jäger et al., 2006, 2009; Kaul et al., 2001; Mortenson et al., 2013; Mu et al., 2006; Sharpe et al., 2007; Stewart et al., 2011; Tang et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2007). Moreover, extensive and careful amide‐to‐ester mutagenesis in this system has produced valuable estimates of typical backbone hydrogen bonds in a variety of structural contexts. Whereas those studies required a variety of complementary analyses (e.g., solvation estimates, comparisons to olefin isosteres that reduce O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion) (Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004; Fu et al., 2006; Gao & Kelly, 2008) to correct the interaction energies obtained from amide‐to‐ester mutagenesis, we expected that double‐mutant cycle analysis could overcome those challenges within a single experimental paradigm. We therefore applied backbone double‐mutant cycle analysis to an unremarkable, prototypical cross‐strand hydrogen bond in the Pin1 WW domain that had been probed previously (Figure 5a) (Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004). To simplify synthesis, we substituted R9K and M10L, which were well‐tolerated in previous studies (Deechongkit & Kelly, 2002), and shown not to alter the Pin1 WW β‐sheet secondary structure (Dave et al., 2016).

As in the TrpZip context, substituting the hydrogen‐bond donor amide with an ester (Figure 5b–d) caused a larger change in folding enthalpy (3.2 kcal/mol; WW‐BS‐WT vs. WW‐BS‐D) than did substitution of the hydrogen‐bond acceptor (2.4 kcal/mol; WW‐BS‐WT vs. WW‐BS‐A). Replacing the acceptor likely underestimates the true hydrogen‐bond energy because esters maintain capacity to acceptor hydrogen bonds, while replacing the donor likely overestimates the true hydrogen‐bond energy by introducing an artifactual O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion. Interestingly, in the presence of an ester at the donor position, replacing the amide acceptor with an ester (Figure 5b), which reduces the artifactual O⸱⸱⸱O repulsion, increased the folding enthalpy by 0.3 kcal/mol (Figure 5c,d), which is in remarkable agreement with orthogonal estimates based on comparison to olefin isosteres of the amide bond (Fu et al., 2006). The complete double‐mutant cycle analysis resulted in a final estimate of hydrogen‐bond enthalpy of 2.8 kcal/mol (Figure 5c,d), which is in excellent agreement with the hydrogen‐bond energy at this position estimated from previous studies (2.8 kcal/mol) (Deechongkit, Dawson, & Kelly, 2004). Using this approach, we obtain lower‐bound estimates of hydrogen‐bond energies that are smaller than the values derived from calculations (Ahirwar et al., 2021; Deshmukh et al., 2006, 2011; Deshmukh & Gadre, 2009, 2021), likely reflecting the effects of amide‐to‐ester modification as well as variations in residue identity and local microenvironment.

Having validated that this approach provides well‐validated estimates of hydrogen‐bond energetics, we next sought to apply this strategy to quantify the previously uncharacterized C5 hydrogen bond within individual β‐strands. Previous spectroscopic and mutagenic studies provide qualitative evidence for meaningful hydrogen bonding in this geometry, but quantitative estimates of individual C5 hydrogen‐bond energies are lacking, preventing accurate simulation and modeling (Benedetti et al., 1988; Newberry & Raines, 2016; Toniolo et al., 1988). We therefore identified a prototypical C5 hydrogen bond in the center of the Pin1 WW domain β‐sheet and designed a backbone double‐mutant cycle analysis to probe its energy via amide‐to‐ester substitutions. The conformation of this residue is typical of C5 hydrogen bonds identified previously (Figure 6a), suggesting that the resulting energetics are a representative estimate. To overcome limitations in the thermal stability of the Pin1 WW domain, we exploited a thermostable variant recently reported by Thomas and coworkers (Lindner et al., 2024). To simplify synthesis, made four side‐chain substitutions (R9K, I10L, Y18F, Y19F); previous mutagenic studies have shown that each of these substitutions is well‐tolerated by the Pin1 WW domain (Lindner et al., 2024).

Upon substituting the hydrogen‐bond donor and/or acceptor with esters (Figure 6b), the resulting thermal denaturation data were well‐described by a two‐state unfolding model, as with our previous peptides (see above), allowing us to extract high‐confidence estimates of the enthalpy of folding for each peptide. We were therefore able to use double‐mutant cycle analysis to estimate the energy of a single C5 hydrogen bond at approx. 0.2 kcal/mol (Figure 6c,d), which is in excellent agreement with computational predictions from DFT calculations combined with NBO analysis (Newberry & Raines, 2016). For example, peptide geometries that support calculated C5 hydrogen‐bond energies above ~0.25 kcal/mol correlated with a variety of classic signatures of hydrogen bonding, including donor bond lengthening, increased hydrogen charge, decreased vibrational stretching frequencies, and decreased chemical shift. Moreover, both the attenuation and enhancement of individual C5 hydrogen bonds at the termini of β‐hairpin models are sufficient to impart predictable changes in thermal stability. The thermodynamic measurements we achieve herein via double‐mutant cycle are therefore highly consistent with complementary assessments of C5 hydrogen bonding in peptides.

As expected from the distorted geometry, C5 hydrogen bonds are substantially weaker than canonical, cross‐strand interactions; however, given that C5 hydrogen bonds likely affect ~5% of all residues in folded proteins, their cumulative contribution to protein stability could be prodigious (Newberry & Raines, 2016). The relatively modest energetic contribution from each individual interaction also suggests opportunities to enhance this interaction to impart conformational stability. For example, we recently demonstrated that thioamides can selectively enhance C5 interactions by increasing acceptor hydrogen‐bond capacity (Zheng & Newberry, 2025).

Based on these results, we have shown that backbone‐double mutant cycle analysis is a valuable approach for quantifying interaction energetics in complex systems like peptides and proteins. Whereas our study focused on hydrogen‐bond energies in protein β‐sheets, this approach can likely be extended to other protein structures like the α‐helix (Shoemaker et al., 1987). Moreover, this strategy might also find application in probing interactions at protein–protein interfaces. For example, interactions involving PDZ domains typically feature the formation of intermolecular β‐sheets, which have previously been probed by amide‐to‐ester mutagenesis (Pedersen et al., 2014). Finally, our success in quantifying C5 hydrogen‐bond energies suggests that this approach could be extended to other under‐characterized interactions, such as C–H⸱⸱⸱O hydrogen bonds (Scheiner, 2005), whose energies have been difficult to quantify within complex protein topologies.
