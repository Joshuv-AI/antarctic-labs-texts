Clinical characteristics and genetic analysis of different Dandy-Walker malformation
Authors: Xingye Wei, Ping Huo, Xuedong Shi, Pingping Zhang, Ning Zhang, Yanmei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDandy-Walker malformation (DWM) is a rare congenital malformation of the central nervous system (CNS). It is characterized by variable hypoplasia of the cerebellar vermis, cystic dilatation of the fourth ventricle, with or without an enlarged cisterna magna. Given that large-sample genetic analysis of DWM has not been conducted to date, this study aims to provide more comprehensive genetic counseling for prenatal diagnosis by summarizing the clinical features and genetic findings of DWM.MethodsBetween January 2010 and May 2025, among 22,048 pregnant women, there were 487 cases of fetal DWM that underwent prenatal diagnosis. Karyotyping, copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq), single-nucleotide polymorphism array (SNP-array) or whole-exome sequencing (WES) were employed for prenatal genetic diagnosis. The results of ultrasonography, genetic analyses, and prognoses were tabulated.ResultsAmong the 487 cases, 35 (7.19%) were diagnosed with classic Dandy-Walker malformation (cDWM), 59 (12.11%) with vermian hypoplasia (VH), and 393 (80.70%) with mega cisterna magna (MCM) according to ultrasonography. A total of 245 cases (50.31%) presented with additional ultrasound abnormalities; central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities were the most prevalent (21.36%), among which ventriculomegaly constituted the primary finding. Prenatal genetic abnormalities were detected in 50 cases (10.27%). Karyotyping identified chromosomal abnormalities in 42 cases (8.62%), including numerical abnormalities, structural abnormalities, and chromosomal mosaicism, with trisomy-18 (2.26%) being the most commonly detected. There were 8 cases with normal karyotypes, but among them, 6 cases were detected with chromosomal abnormalities by CNV-seq/SNP-array, and 2 cases were detected with gene mutations by WES. Advanced maternal age was associated with an increased risk of chromosomal abnormalities, a proportion of which may present with DWM. Among the 453 cases that underwent follow-up, the cDWM and VH groups exhibited a significantly higher incidence of pregnancy terminations compared to the MCM group. Subsequent follow-up of cases with continued pregnancies revealed 1 case of neonatal death in the VH group and 5 cases in the MCM group. Additionally, there was 1 case of abnormal postnatal development in the cDWM group and 3 cases in the MCM group.ConclusionsPregnant women carrying DWM fetuses should undergo prenatal diagnosis including karyotyping, CNV-seq/SNP-array and WES if necessary, especially for fetuses with cDWM or VH, as the fetuses with cDWM or VH showed higher rates of genetic abnormalities. The pregnancy outcomes of most fetuses with MCM are favorable, while fetuses with cDWM or VH are prone to adverse gestational outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09656-3.Keywords: Dandy-Walker malformation, karyotype analysis, CNV-seq, SNP-array, WES

Background

Dandy-Walker malformation (DWM) is a rare congenital malformation of the CNS, characterized by varying degrees of cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, cystic dilatation of the fourth ventricle, with or without an enlarged cisterna magna. It belongs to the category of congenital posterior fossa malformations, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 25,000 to 35,000. DWM can be associated with CNS malformations or non-CNS malformations, and CNS malformations are more prevalent, followed by cardiovascular, urinary system, and digestive system malformations, etc [1–3]. Although DWM has been recognized for an extended period, its precise etiology and pathogenic mechanism remain unclear. Alcohol consumption, maternal diabetes, rubella and cytomegalovirus infections may contribute to the occurrence of DWM. A significant proportion of DWM patients exhibit genetic abnormalities, including chromosomal aneuploidies (e.g., trisomy 13, 18, and 21), chromosomal microdeletions (e.g., in regions 1q25.3-q32.1 and 3q22-q24), chromosome 9p duplication, and mutations in genes ZIC1, ZIC4, FOXC1, etc [1, 4, 5]. The diagnosis of fetal DWM relies primarily on ultrasound examination and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Advances in prenatal examination technology and increased awareness of prenatal screening have improved the detection rate of these abnormalities. Based on the degree of cerebellar vermis development, DWM is classified into classic Dandy-Walker malformation (cDWM), vermian hypoplasia (VH, also referred to as Dandy-Walker variant), and mega cisterna magna (MCM) [6]. In our previous study [1], we focused on cDWM and VH fetuses, detailing their clinical and genetic characteristics, but did not include MCM cases. To provide a more comprehensive understanding, this study incorporates MCM fetuses into the cohort to systematically describe the clinical features and genetic profiles of these three subtypes. To further elucidate the pathogenesis and prenatal diagnosis of DWM, the clinical data and genetic test results of 487 fetuses with DWM were summarized in this study.

Methods

From January 2010 to May 2025, a total of 22,048 pregnant women underwent prenatal diagnosis, and 487 pregnant women whose fetuses presented with DWM underwent amniocentesis or cordocentesis under ultrasound guidance for genetic testing. Based on the degree of cerebellar vermis development, DWM is classified into cDWM, VH, and MCM. cDWM is a relatively rare condition characterized by the complete or partial absence of the cerebellar vermis, an enlarged cisterna magna that communicates with the dilated fourth ventricle; VH or Dandy-Walker variant is characterized by cerebellar vermian hypoplasia, cystic dilatation of the fourth ventricle, with a normal posterior fossa. MCM presents as a simple enlarged cisterna magna, without underdevelopment or dysplasia of cerebellar vermis, and with normal fourth ventricle and supratentorial structures of the cerebellum [6, 7]. The results of intracranial and extracranial ultrasound abnormalities, as well as genetic analysis results of the fetuses were tabulated. Fetal ultrasonography was conducted by experienced sonographers using a 5 MHz transducer (IU22, Philips; VOLUSON E8, GE). Karyotyping was performed for all 487 cases as the first-line prenatal diagnostic test following invasive sampling. CNV‑seq or SNP-array was performed in selected cases including fetuses with structural anomalies (one or multiple), sonographic soft markers highly associated with pathogenic copy number variations, or inconclusive karyotyping results, etc [8]. WES is indicated for fetuses with abnormal prenatal imaging findings (e.g., multiple congenital anomalies, skeletal abnormalities, cardiovascular malformations, central nervous system malformations) after conventional karyotyping and CNV testing have revealed no abnormalities, or for cases with suspected rare genetic syndromes, etc [9]. The choice of genetic testing was determined by the severity and complexity of sonographic findings, clinical indications, and parental preferences. All samples were subjected to rapid prenatal karyotyping, whereas only 104 and 5 cases underwent CNV-seq/SNP-array or WES, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Version 27.0 (SPSS, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The chi-square test was employed for comparisons between groups (P = 0.05). To adjust for multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni method was applied, and the corrected significance threshold was set at 0.0167 (0.05/[3(3 − 1)/2]). Two-sided tests were performed, and P < 0.0167 were considered statistically significant. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to adjust for gestational age and sampling method, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Among 22,048 pregnant women, 487 cases with fetal DWM underwent invasive sampling procedures, including 65 amniocenteses and 422 cordocenteses. The median maternal ages were 28.00 (25.00,32.00) years, 28.00 (24.00,31.00) years, and 29.00 (25.50,32.00) years respectively. The maternal age data of the three groups did not follow a normal distribution (P < 0.05), and the differences were not statistically significant as evaluated by the Kruskal-Wallis H test (P > 0.05). The median gestational ages were 27.00 (20.00,31.00) weeks, 24.00 (20.00,29.00) weeks, and 31.70 (29.40,33.30) weeks respectively. The gestational age data of the three groups did not follow a normal distribution (P < 0.05), and the differences were statistically significant as evaluated by the Kruskal-Wallis H test (P < 0.05). The gestational weeks of the MCM group were significantly higher than those of the other two groups. (Table 1).

Among the 487 cases, 50 (10.27%) presented prenatal genetic abnormalities, including 13 (37.14%) in the cDWM group, 17 (28.81%) in the VH group, and 20 (5.09%) in the MCM group (Table 3). To account for the significant differences in gestational age across subgroups (Table 1), logistic regression was performed with adjustment for gestational age and sampling method. After adjustment, both the cDWM group (OR = 7.39, 95% CI:2.98–18.35, P < 0.001) and the VH group (OR = 4.48, 95% CI:1.81–11.06, P = 0.001) showed significantly higher genetic abnormality rates compared with the MCM group. No significant difference was observed between the cDWM and VH groups (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 0.27–10.14, P = 0.287). Gestational age (P = 0.102) and sampling method (P = 0.855) were not statistically significant in the model, indicating that neither variable independently influenced the genetic abnormality rate after adjustment. To account for potential confounding by gestational age, a subgroup analysis restricted to cases diagnosed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation after gestational age matching was performed; the results are provided in the supplementary materials (Table S1).

Among the 487 fetuses who underwent karyotype examination, 42 (8.62%) exhibited an abnormal karyotype. These included 5 cases (1.03%) of chromosomal mosaicism, 14 cases (2.87%) of chromosomal structural abnormalities, and 23 cases (4.72%) of chromosomal numerical abnormalities. The numerical abnormalities consisted of 11 cases of trisomy 18, 8 cases of trisomy 21, 3 cases of trisomy 13, and 1 case of triploidy (69, XXX), with trisomy 18 being the most commonly occurring. Structural abnormalities included chromosomal translocations, inversions, deletions, derivative chromosomes, marker chromosomes, etc. A more detailed list of abnormal karyotypes with chromosomal structures can be found in supplementary materials (Table S2). Among the 42 cases with chromosomal abnormalities, 12 originated from the cDWM group (n = 35, 34.29%), 16 were from the VH group (n = 59, 27.12%), and 14 were from the MCM group (n = 393, 3.56%), as shown in Table 4. Logistic regression was also performed with adjustment for gestational age and sampling method. After adjustment, both the cDWM group (OR = 9.22, 95% CI: 3.50–24.30, P < 0.001) and the VH group (OR = 5.98, 95% CI: 2.28–15.69, P < 0.001) showed significantly higher karyotype abnormality rates compared with the MCM group. No significant difference was observed between the cDWM and VH groups (OR = 0.648, 95% CI: 0.253–1.660, P = 0.366). Gestational age was independently associated with karyotype abnormalities (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.80–0.99, P = 0.039), while sampling method was not significantly associated with karyotype abnormalities (P = 0.462).

Discussion

Fetal ultrasound and MRI are efficacious approaches for detecting DWM. During prenatal screening, DWM is frequently associated with other CNS malformations or non-CNS malformations, including ventriculomegaly, corpus callosum dysplasia, meningocele, congenital cardiovascular malformations, renal system malformations, limb abnormalities, etc. The other ultrasound anomalies were predominantly observed among CNS malformations, with ventriculomegaly being the most prevalent. Subsequently, cardiovascular system malformations and renal system abnormalities were also commonly detected [1, 5]. This study also yielded consistent results, and polyhydramnios was also frequently observed among the accompanying abnormalities. Further in-depth investigation into the relationship between these systems’ abnormalities and DWM can provide more references for early screening and intervention. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of these systems during prenatal examinations is essential to improve the early detection of DWM, especially in cases involving the CNS, cardiovascular system, or polyhydramnios.

Genetic factors play an important role in the etiology of DWM. In the present study, the abnormality rate of prenatal genetic analysis was 10.27%, with the rates in the three groups being 37.14% (n = 13), 28.81% (n = 17), and 5.09% (n = 20), respectively. The abnormality rates in both the cDWM group and the VH group were significantly higher than that in the MCM group. This finding implies that the incidence of genetic abnormalities in cases featuring a simple enlarged cisterna magna without cerebellar vermis dysplasia is notably lower. When fetal cerebellar vermis dysplasia is present, the risk of genetic abnormalities increases, particularly chromosomal aneuploidy, which is consistent with the results of other studies [10–12]. Although the abnormal rate in the cDWM group was higher than that in the VH group, statistical significance was not observed. Given that not all cases in this study underwent karyotype analysis, CNV-seq/SNP-array and WES, the actual rate of genetic abnormalities is likely higher than the rate reported herein. Furthermore, advanced maternal age is associated with an increased risk of chromosomal abnormalities, a proportion of which may present with DWM.

The genetic abnormalities identified in this study included aneuploidy, chromosomal structural abnormalities, pathogenic/likely pathogenic CNVs, chromosomal mosaicism, and gene mutations, which were consistent with previous research [1, 4, 13]. Trisomy 18 was the most prevalent chromosomal abnormality in this study. Karyotype analysis did not always exhibit consistency with CNV-seq/SNP-array and WES, particularly in the detection of chromosomal structural abnormalities and gene mutations, where discrepancies were observed. Karyotype analysis is only capable of detecting chromosomal fragments larger than 5 Mb and is ineffective against submicroscopic chromosomal aberrations. CNV-seq/SNP-array can compensate for this deficiency and detect pathogenic/likely pathogenic CNVs [14–17].

Previous research has indicated that DWM fetuses may be linked to mutations in genes such as ZIC2, ZIC4, FOXC1, SIL1, and EXOC3L2 [4, 13]. In the present study, WES identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in 2 cases, involving the KDM6A, GAA, RAF1 gene and no chromosomal aberrations were found. WES has emerged as a potent tool for prenatal diagnosis in cases with abnormal ultrasound findings or congenital malformations, playing a significant role in identifying pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations associated with clinical phenotypes. WES has improved the genetic diagnostic rates of fetuses with ultrasound abnormalities, particularly in cases involving kidney abnormalities, amniotic fluid abnormalities, and nervous system anomalies, etc [18, 19]. Therefore, karyotyping, CNV-seq/SNP-array, and WES are recommended for fetuses with DWM.

Maternal primary cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection exerts a significant impact on the development and prognosis of the fetus, with manifestations including ventriculomegaly, fetal growth restriction, and enlarged cisterna magna, etc. Congenital infection may result in sensorineural hearing loss and central nervous system involvement [20–22]. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct comprehensive CMV testing during early pregnancy.

The clinical manifestations of DWM include mental and motor developmental delay, hydrocephalus, increased intracranial pressure, spastic paralysis, and hearing impairment [5, 23]. The prognosis of DWM is associated with multiple factors, such as the presence of chromosomal abnormalities or genetic mutations, other ultrasound abnormalities, the degree of cerebellar vermis absence, and the severity of hydrocephalus [5]. It is extremely challenging to accurately assess the prognosis solely based on ultrasound findings. Even if no abnormalities are detected in the fetal genetic testing, some families may still choose to terminate the pregnancy due to concerns about potential adverse outcomes [1, 10, 12], especially in the cDWM group and the VH group. Although most fetuses with isolated MCM have favorable outcomes, adverse postnatal events (including neonatal death and neurodevelopmental abnormalities) can still occur in a small proportion of cases, sometimes due to non-genetic causes such as congenital cytomegalovirus infection. Therefore, postnatal follow-up remains necessary even when prenatal genetic testing is negative.

This study further analyzed the genetic and clinical characteristics of DWM to enhance the comprehension of this condition. When an enlarged cisterna magna is detected via ultrasound, it is imperative to conduct a further examination of the integrity of the cerebellar vermis. Fetal MRI can serve as a complementary tool to the ultrasound examination for achieving a definitive diagnosis. Transvaginal neurosonography represents a targeted high-resolution ultrasound technique that substantially improves the assessment of the fetal posterior fossa, particularly the cerebellar vermis, enabling detailed qualitative and quantitative evaluation of this structure. When used in addition to standard ultrasound and fetal MRI, transvaginal neurosonography serves as a valuable complementary tool, offering high-resolution anatomical detail that optimizes prenatal evaluation of posterior fossa anomalies [24, 25]. Upon the identification of DWM, genetic analysis should be carried out on the affected fetuses. Karyotype analysis in combination with CNV-seq/SNP-array can screen out the majority of chromosomal aberrations, and WES can detect potential pathogenic gene mutations. In this study, several gene mutations potentially associated with DWM were detected, which broadens the genetic spectrum of this disorder.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. This is a retrospective study, the definition of ‘genetic abnormality’ in this study was a composite outcome encompassing karyotypic abnormalities, CNVs, and gene mutations. However, the coverage of testing methods varies across subgroups, all 487 cases underwent karyotyping, but only 104 cases were tested by CNV-seq/SNP-array, and only 5 by WES. Consequently, the reported rates of genetic abnormalities in the DWM fetuses may underestimate the true prevalence, particularly for submicroscopic CNV abnormalities and gene mutations that are not detectable by karyotyping alone. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to systematically collect cases that undergo karyotyping, CNV testing, and WES, in order to more accurately estimate the true genetic abnormality rates in DWM fetuses. Additionally, subtype classification relies solely on prenatal ultrasound, lacking MRI validation or diagnostic consistency assessment. While ultrasound is the primary screening modality for fetal posterior fossa anomalies, it has inherent limitations in assessing cerebellar vermis development and fourth ventricular morphology, particularly when distinguishing mild vermis hypoplasia from MCM. Fetal MRI can serve as an essential auxiliary tool to ultrasound for achieving a more definitive diagnosis and refining subtype classification. The absence of MRI may have introduced classification bias in this study. Future studies should incorporate systematic MRI evaluation and ultrasound-MRI concordance analysis to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusion

Genetic factors contribute to a subset of fetal DWM cases, particularly cDWM and VH. Fetal DWM are frequently accompanied by other ultrasound abnormalities, with the CNS abnormalities are the most prevalent. Fetal isolated MCM carries a low risk of chromosomal abnormalities and is generally associated with favorable outcomes; however, adverse postnatal events can still occur in a small proportion of cases, sometimes due to non-genetic causes. Therefore, postnatal follow-up remains necessary even when prenatal genetic testing is negative. However, for the cDWM or VH, the incidence of chromosomal aberrations increases significantly and tending towards a poor prognosis. Consequently, prenatal detection of DWM need detailed evaluation of fetal systemic structural abnormalities, with particular emphasis on the central nervous and cardiovascular systems. It is necessary to carry out further prenatal genetic testing including karyotype analysis, copy number variation sequencing and WES.

Data Availability Statement

The variation data from the Whole-Exome Sequencing analysis reported in this study have been deposited in the Genome Variation Map (GVM) [26] in National Genomics Data Center, Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences and China National Center for Bioinformation [27], under accession number GVM001333. The data deposited in GVM are currently in a confidential state to protect patient privacy. Access to the data is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.
