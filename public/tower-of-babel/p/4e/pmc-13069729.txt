The application of process mining for clinical pathways: a systematic literature review protocol

Background

Hospital Information Systems (HIS) in contemporary healthcare environments routinely collect and store extensive datasets [1], which can be broadly classified into three primary categories: clinical, administrative, and process data. Clinical data encompasses information pertaining to a patient’s health status, medical history, diagnoses, treatments, medications, laboratory results, and other healthcare-relevant details. This data is predominantly generated and utilized by healthcare professionals during patient care, serving a pivotal role in informed clinical decision-making, outcome assessment, and health condition monitoring [2]. While clinical data centers on the medical dimensions of care, administrative data relates to the operational and financial management of healthcare services, and process data captures the flow and efficiency of healthcare procedures. Collectively, these data types are indispensable for ensuring effective healthcare delivery and management. In recent years, international scholarship has increasingly focused on the utilization of process data in healthcare, particularly through emerging methodologies such as process mining [3].

Although both process mining and process mapping aim to deepen understanding of healthcare workflows, they diverge significantly in methodology and data utilization. Process mapping typically involves stakeholder-driven, qualitative depictions of workflows—constructed via interviews, workshops, or direct observation. These representations are inherently subjective, relying on participants’ recollections and perceptions. Conversely, process mining leverages event log data extracted from information systems (e.g., EHRs, HIS, RTLS) to automatically generate process models, thereby enabling objective, data-driven insights into actual process executions. This methodological distinction is consequential: process mapping is well-suited for exploring undocumented workflows or fostering team consensus, whereas process mining excels in validating performance, identifying deviations from clinical guidelines, and quantifying efficiency metrics. For a comprehensive overview of process mapping methodologies in healthcare, refer to Antonacci et al. [4].

This systematic review protocol offers a novel contribution to the literature by concentrating specifically on hospital-based clinical pathways—an area characterized by complex care processes and rich process data, which present unique challenges and opportunities for process mining. In contrast to prior reviews that broadly examine process mining applications across diverse healthcare domains [5–8], our review employs PICOTS-based eligibility criteria to identify studies reporting measurable outcomes such as care efficiency, patient flow, and adherence to clinical guidelines. Methodologically, we adopt a rigorous systematic review framework aligned with Cochrane and PRISMA-P guidelines [9, 10], including PROSPERO registration (CRD42024584540) and quality appraisal via the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Additionally, we introduce a structured framework to evaluate the alignment between the stated objectives of process mining applications and their reported outcomes—an implementation science perspective not previously addressed in the literature. While Antonacci et al. [4] provide a quality framework for process mapping in healthcare, they do not address process mining or its technical implementations. By encompassing publications from 1999 onward [11] and including studies in English, French, and Italian, our review also offers a broader temporal and linguistic scope. Supplementary materials—including the search strategy, data extraction form, and PRISMA checklist—are provided to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Process mining is a specialized analytical technique designed to examine and improve business processes by extracting insights from event logs [12]. It facilitates the use of event data to discover, monitor, and optimize real-world processes by visualizing workflows and identifying bottlenecks, ultimately aiming to streamline operations. Originating at the intersection of data science and process management, process mining was pioneered in the late 1990 s and early 2000 s by Wil van der Aalst [11, 13]. The technique emerged in response to the growing need for tools capable of analyzing and optimizing business processes using the vast event data generated by information systems. Process mining tools automatically generate process models from event logs, enabling visualization of current workflows and facilitating comparisons between discovered and predefined models to detect deviations and compliance issues [14]. Moreover, these tools enhance process transparency and comprehension, aiding in the identification of inefficiencies, bottlenecks, and compliance violations [15]. Among the various data types available, process data has gained prominence due to its capacity to uncover inefficiencies and optimize care delivery through process mining techniques.

Process mining holds considerable promise in healthcare, with international literature demonstrating its utility in analyzing and improving healthcare processes [5]. Nonetheless, its adoption remains limited, primarily due to the requirement for highly granular and accurate process data [6]. Despite this constraint, interest in process mining within healthcare has grown markedly in recent years [3]. Although hospitals generate substantial volumes of data, the systematic application of process mining has largely been restricted to isolated case studies within research contexts, as noted by Gama et al. [6]. Accordingly, as Mans et al. [16] observe, the field remains relatively underexplored, particularly in the context of clinical pathway detection.

The expanding use of process mining in healthcare has produced a growing body of evidence that warrants critical appraisal to inform its effective implementation in clinical pathways. While scoping reviews offer a broad overview of existing literature, they do not assess study quality or synthesize findings to support practical applications and policy development. This study adopts a systematic review methodology to rigorously evaluate and synthesize evidence on the application of process mining to clinical pathways. By systematically appraising study quality, extracting detailed outcomes, and synthesizing findings, this approach enables robust conclusions regarding the efficacy and limitations of process mining in enhancing care efficiency, patient flow, and guideline adherence.

In this systematic review, we examine how process mining has been applied to clinical pathways to improve care efficiency, patient flow, and adherence to clinical guidelines within hospital settings. Clinical pathways—also referred to as care pathways—are defined by Gartner et al. [17] as “a long-term and complex managerial intervention adopting a systemic approach, for a well-defined group of patients who journey across the entire continuum of care, from prevention and screening to recovery or palliative care.” By applying process mining to these pathways, we aim to trace the actual progression of patients through various stages of care, model their clinical trajectories, and identify deviations from intended care processes.

Modeling patient clinical pathways is essential for several reasons [18]. First, it provides a clear visualization of actual care processes, enabling healthcare providers to understand patient movement through the system in real time. This insight is critical for identifying variations in care, which may indicate deviations from standards or reveal inefficiencies. Furthermore, modeling these pathways allows providers to ensure that patients receive care aligned with best practices and clinical guidelines [19].

By identifying inefficiencies and deviations within clinical pathways, healthcare organizations can streamline operations, eliminate redundant steps, and optimize resource allocation [20]. These improvements contribute to faster patient throughput and potentially shorter hospital stays. When clinical pathways are refined using techniques such as process mining, care delivery becomes more consistent and effective. This consistency enhances patient outcomes by reducing variability, minimizing errors, and ensuring adherence to evidence-based treatment protocols [21].

Methods

This protocol has been developed in accordance with the guidelines of the Cochrane Methods Group [22], the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines [9], and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Abstracts [10]. In alignment with these standards, a PRISMA-P checklist is provided (Supplementary material 1). Any significant amendments to the protocol will be documented and published alongside the results of the systematic review. The study protocol has been registered with the PROSPERO systematic review registry (CRD42024584540) [23].

A systematic review was selected over a scoping review due to the study’s objective to critically evaluate the quality and outcomes of existing research, thereby offering actionable insights for healthcare professionals, researchers, and policymakers. The systematic review framework presents several advantages, including the assessment of methodological rigor through established appraisal tools, comprehensive data extraction, and synthesis—facilitating the identification of patterns, trends, and research gaps.

By contrast, a scoping review, although valuable for mapping the breadth of existing literature, typically does not evaluate study quality or synthesize findings to inform evidence-based practice. Given the imperative for high-quality evidence to support the integration of process mining into clinical pathways, a systematic review represents a more appropriate and methodologically robust approach.

Objectives

With the increasing volume of data generated by hospital facilities, process mining has emerged as a powerful analytical tool for examining clinical pathways, offering significant potential to enhance patient care and optimize healthcare operations. Nevertheless, the application of process mining within clinical pathways remains underexplored, and a comprehensive synthesis of the existing body of research is currently lacking. The objective of this systematic literature review is to evaluate the current state of research on the use of process mining techniques in clinical pathways since 1999, the year in which the technique first emerged [11].

In light of the growing complexity of healthcare systems and the imperative for data-driven decision-making, this review seeks to provide a detailed analysis from multiple dimensions. These include assessing the role of process mining in research—whether it occupies a central or peripheral position—the primary purposes it serves, such as detection, modeling, analysis, or visualization, its integration with other tools or methodologies, and the challenges and successes encountered in its application. The findings are anticipated to yield valuable insights for researchers, healthcare professionals, and policymakers, thereby informing future applications and guiding continued research in this evolving domain.

Research question

We have adopted the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Timing, and Settings (PICOTS) mnemonic framework [24, 25] to inform the development of our review objective and research questions. The aim of this systematic review is to evaluate the evidence linking the application of process mining in clinical settings (I, P) to both management and clinical outcomes (O) within healthcare environments (S). To guide the review, we have formulated the following research questions:

What are the benefits of adopting process mining in defining and analyzing clinical pathways in healthcare settings?

To ensure methodological rigor, we defined clear eligibility criteria for study inclusion.

Eligibility criteria

Population

Studies will be included if they specifically focus on clinical pathways. Research addressing general process mining in healthcare workflows—without a direct link to clinical pathways—will be excluded, as such applications are often complicated by the variability of data sources and the frequently questionable quality of the data [26]. Where available, we will extract information on study population characteristics, including age, gender, clinical condition, and care setting, to support contextual interpretation and facilitate future replication.

Intervention

Studies will be included if they employ process mining techniques for objectives such as process discovery, process analysis, conformance checking, or predictive process analysis [5]. Additionally, studies that integrate process mining with other methodologies to support these objectives will also be considered. However, the use of process mining must constitute a mandatory component of the study, even if it is not the sole technique employed. Studies that do not incorporate process mining will be excluded.

Comparison

No specific comparator is required for this review, and therefore, no exclusion criteria apply to the comparison component.

Outcomes

Studies reporting measures related to clinical pathways, such as efficiency, patient flow, compliance, or reduced delays in clinical pathways, will be included [27]. Conversely, studies focusing on outcomes unrelated to clinical pathways will be excluded.

Timing

The review will include studies published from 1999 onwards. Any studies published prior to this year will be excluded since process mining can trace its origins to 1999, with one of the earliest references being a research proposal by Wil van der Aalst and A.J.M.M. Weijters [11].

Study design/characteristics

Target studies will include meta-analyses, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, cohort studies (prospective observational studies), case–control studies, cross-sectional studies, case reports, case series, and conference papers that demonstrate the benefits and limitations of applying process mining to clinical pathways. The inclusion of case reports and conference papers is justified by their potential to capture early-stage innovations. However, we acknowledge that these sources may vary in methodological rigor. Accordingly, they will be incorporated into the main analysis but clearly labelled as “non-peer-reviewed” or “grey literature,” synthesized with appropriate caution, and their influence on the overall findings will be evaluated through sensitivity analyses.

In this review, meta-analyses and systematic literature reviews will serve two primary functions: first, to identify additional relevant studies through backward citation tracking, thereby enabling a comprehensive retrieval of pertinent articles; and second, to validate our analysis and/or findings by examining whether the reported benefits and limitations of process mining in clinical pathways have evolved over the past decade. This approach ensures that our conclusions are situated within the broader context of existing research and helps to illuminate trends or shifts in the field over time.

We will also consider summary papers, government and public health reports, and other analytical documents to identify relevant primary studies. Study protocols, doctoral or master’s theses, and letters to the editor will not be included in this systematic literature review.

Information sources

A comprehensive search of academic databases—including Google Scholar, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Embase, and ABI/Inform—will be conducted by a specialized librarian (FB) with expertise in healthcare reviews and systematic review methodology. The search will identify potentially relevant articles using predefined strategies. In addition, a manual search of reference lists from selected studies will be performed to identify further relevant literature. This manual search will follow the backward snowballing technique, which involves examining the reference lists of included articles to uncover additional sources. To ensure saturation of the literature, hand searches will also be conducted for selected key journals. Studies will be excluded if they do not employ a process-oriented approach to analyzing clinical pathways.

Search strategy

The search strategy (Supplementary material 3) will be reviewed by the first (LM) and second (PL) reviewers, under the supervision of the third reviewer (JBG). It has been structured according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Timing, and Study Design (PICOTS) framework [24, 25] (Table 1), with filters applied to limit the search to studies published from 1999 onward and written in English, French, or Italian. The 1999 threshold was selected to align with the emergence of process mining as a recognized technique. Language restrictions are applied to ensure precision in data extraction and synthesis.

Screening and data collection

The abstracts and full-text articles retrieved through the search strategy will be managed using Covidence® (www.covidence.org.) [28], an online systematic review platform recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration. Duplicate records will be removed. Two review authors (LM and PL) will independently screen titles and abstracts, excluding studies that do not meet the eligibility criteria. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (AC or JBG).

To ensure consistency in the application of eligibility criteria, a calibration exercise will be conducted prior to the formal study selection process. The two reviewers (LM and PL) will independently assess a random sample of ten studies. Discrepancies will be discussed and resolved to refine the screening protocol before proceeding with full-scale selection. This step will support the refinement of operational definitions and promote a shared understanding of the inclusion criteria. Inter-rater reliability will be evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, with a threshold of 0.70 considered indicative of sufficient agreement. Once this benchmark is achieved, the review team will proceed with the full screening process, thereby minimizing selection bias and reinforcing methodological rigor.

Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (LM and PL) will assess the methodological quality of eligible studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). This instrument is designed to evaluate the quality of studies across various research designs—including qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods—ensuring a standardized and rigorous appraisal process. Each study will be scored according to the specific criteria outlined in the MMAT guidelines (Supplementary material 4), which encompass domains such as study design, data collection methods, reliability, validity, and generalizability. Any disagreements between reviewers regarding the assigned scores will be resolved by a third reviewer (AC or JBG).

To ensure consistency in the application of the MMAT, a calibration exercise will be conducted using a small subset of studies prior to the formal quality assessment [29]. The results of this exercise will help refine the assessment approach and promote a shared understanding of the appraisal criteria. Findings from the quality assessment will inform the narrative synthesis by enabling differentiation between high- and low-quality studies. A summary table presenting the quality scores for all included studies will be provided in the final review.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction will be conducted using a standardized and piloted extraction form (Table 2 and Supplementary material 5). Two reviewers (LM and PL) will independently extract data from each included study. The extraction form will be piloted on a sample of ten studies to ensure clarity, consistency, and completeness; any necessary modifications will be documented and implemented before full extraction begins.

Predefined fields for data extraction

For each study type, the following categories of data will be collected: study characteristics (authors, year, country, design), clinical pathway description, setting, data sources, process mining techniques, event log characteristics, pathway indicators, health-related and operational outcomes, and cost-related descriptive information. Additional fields may be added if new relevant variables emerge during the review.

To ensure accuracy, the two independently extracted datasets will be compared line-by-line. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion; if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer (AC or JBG) will adjudicate. When information is missing or unclear, study authors will be contacted for clarification. All extraction steps, decisions, and modifications to the extraction form will be recorded in an audit trail within Covidence to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Depending on the nature of the data extracted, both qualitative and quantitative synthesis methods will be employed to address the research questions. Data synthesis will follow a narrative approach, integrating qualitative and quantitative findings to provide a comprehensive overview of the studies that meet the inclusion criteria. This synthesis will identify patterns, trends, and gaps in the application of process mining to clinical pathways. Summary tables will be used to present the extracted data in a structured format. Where sufficient quantitative data are available, basic descriptive analyses (e.g., frequency counts, percentages) may be conducted to complement the narrative synthesis.

If the number and nature of included studies permit, subgroup analyses will be considered to explore potential variations in outcomes. These subgroups may be based on factors such as clinical domain, geographical setting, or the specific process mining techniques employed. Additionally, if enough studies are identified, potential publication bias will be assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and/or the application of appropriate statistical tests. The decision to conduct these analyses will depend on the quantity, quality, and heterogeneity of the available data.

To evaluate the alignment between stated objectives and reported outcomes, the data extraction process will capture both the intended aims of process mining (e.g., process discovery, conformance analysis) and the actual results presented. During synthesis, a structured mapping framework will be applied to assess the degree of correspondence between intentions and outcomes (e.g., fully aligned, partially aligned, misaligned). This analysis will be complemented by thematic coding of qualitative data to identify recurring themes related to benefits, limitations, and implementation contexts. Together, these approaches will enable a systematic evaluation of how process mining delivers on its stated goals across diverse settings and use cases.

Discussion

This systematic review protocol presents a structured approach to evaluating the role of process mining in the analysis of clinical pathways. By synthesizing existing research, the review seeks to bridge the gap between advanced data-driven analytical methods and practical clinical applications, offering insights that may significantly enhance patient care and optimize healthcare processes.

Building on prior exploratory studies, this review aims to systematically consolidate evidence on the use of process mining within clinical pathways, critically assessing the quality and relevance of existing studies to inform practical implementation and identify areas for future investigation. The methodological rigor inherent in systematic reviews ensures that the findings are both comprehensive and grounded in robust evidence, thereby supporting data-driven improvements in healthcare delivery.

The anticipated outcomes will provide a detailed understanding of the current research landscape, illustrating how process mining has been applied to model, analyze, and visualize clinical pathways. Moreover, the review will identify key areas where process mining remains underutilized, highlighting opportunities for further exploration. In doing so, it aims to guide researchers and healthcare practitioners in leveraging process mining to improve clinical outcomes and operational efficiency.

To our knowledge, this will be the first systematic review to comprehensively synthesize available evidence on the adoption of process mining within clinical pathways. The findings will offer valuable insights for stakeholders—including Health Information Systems developers and healthcare managers—who are responsible for designing and implementing analytical tools that enhance clinical pathway performance. Additionally, the review will support the identification of potential bottlenecks within the complex workflows of healthcare organizations, ensuring that process mining techniques are applied to their fullest potential.

By examining study population characteristics—such as patient age, gender, clinical condition, and care setting—the review will provide contextual insights into the applicability of process mining across diverse healthcare environments. This understanding will facilitate future replication and inform ongoing research and innovation in the field. Ultimately, the review has the potential to shape the future trajectory of process mining in healthcare, offering a roadmap for its continued adoption and refinement in response to the evolving needs of the sector. By focusing specifically on hospital-based clinical pathways and evaluating the alignment between process mining objectives and real-world outcomes, this review introduces a novel perspective within the existing literature and aims to inform both methodological advancements and practical implementations.

Limitations

Despite our efforts to ensure methodological rigor and comprehensive coverage, this protocol has several limitations. First, the inclusion criteria are restricted to studies published in English, French, and Italian, which may result in the exclusion of relevant research available in other languages. Second, although we employ a structured narrative synthesis and utilize validated tools such as the MMAT for quality appraisal, the interpretation of findings may still be influenced by the disciplinary backgrounds and perspectives of the review team. While this is an inherent feature of qualitative synthesis, it introduces a degree of subjectivity.

Finally, although we will conduct searches across multiple major databases (e.g., Google Scholar, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Embase, PubMed, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and ABI/Inform), some gray literature or studies indexed outside these sources may remain unidentified. These limitations will be carefully considered during the interpretation and reporting of the review findings.
