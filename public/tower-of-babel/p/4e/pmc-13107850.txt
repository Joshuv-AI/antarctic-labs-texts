Biomarkers of stroke recovery using EEG-based resting-state functional connectivity: a systematic review
Authors: Ettore Napoli, Fabio Masina, Rachele Pezzetta, Federico Vanni, Giulia Oliva, Maria Silvia Saccani, Daniele Marinazzo, Marco Zorzi, Dante Mantini, Giorgio Arcara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEEG-based resting-state functional connectivity (FC) has been widely explored as a prognostic tool in stroke recovery, offering a cost-effective alternative to fMRI. However, it remains unclear whether FC measures are reliable biomarkers for predicting stroke recovery. This systematic review provides a comprehensive overview of existing EEG-based FC measures and their clinical relevance in stroke recovery. Specifically, this study aims to identify the most reliable and predictive FC measures of recovery by examining their relationship with longitudinal changes, clinical outcomes, and neurorehabilitation protocols. Results show that while some studies report associations between FC and recovery, no consistent patterns emerge. Significant methodological heterogeneity, such as differences in sensor- vs. source-level analysis, study design, connectivity measures, and reliance on correlational rather than predictive approach, limits the interpretability and comparability of results. Overall, these inconsistencies raise concerns about the reliability of EEG-based FC measures as biomarkers of stroke recovery. Moreover, while most studies focused on motor recovery, emerging evidence suggests FC may also help predict cognitive recovery. Future research should prioritize predictive models tailored to clinical needs, explore multidimensional recovery domains, and establish standardized protocols to enhance methodological consistency. By addressing these challenges and harnessing advanced computational techniques, EEG-based FC holds the potential to transform personalized rehabilitation strategies and optimize outcomes for stroke patients. Given the wide range of analytical scenarios and the absence of a superior method, we propose that a “multiverse” analytical approach could offer valuable insights into the most promising pathways for establishing connectivity as a potential biomarker for stroke recovery.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01928-5.

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability world-wide, often requiring extensive rehabilitation efforts [44]. Indeed, despite the aging population, advances in prevention and acute stroke care have shifted the burden of stroke from mortality to disability [100]. Post-stroke cognitive and motor impairments are common, with most improvements occurring within the first three months after onset [49, 77]. These deficits span a wide range of neuropsychological impairments, including amnesia, agnosia, aphasia, apraxia, executive dysfunction, and mood disorders, alongside motor impairments such as paresis, spasticity, and mobility disorders [17, 53, 76].

Traditionally, the localizationist perspective has attributed stroke symptoms primarily to the loss of function in neural tissue within the affected brain region [39]. While localized damage does result in typical cognitive, motor, and behavioral patterns of impairments, it is now well-established that these impairments can also depend on functional changes in brain regions far from the lesion site [10, 11, 86]. This evidence underscores the importance of understanding the brain’s large-scale organization, which is governed by the complementary principles of functional segregation and integration [31, 32].

Functional segregation refers to the specialization of distinct neural systems for processes such as sensory input, motor control, and cognition [24, 31, 94]. In contrast, functional integration describes the dynamic interactions across these systems at multiple spatial and temporal scales, enabling coordinated and adaptive behaviors [24, 88, 94]. Given these principles, the neurological and neuropsychological outcomes of stroke and their recovery trajectories should be examined in the context of the brain’s large-scale network organization, often assessed with connectivity measures [22]. Indeed, for some cognitive domains (e.g., memory), the severity of deficit and its long-term recovery are better predicted by connectivity measures than lesion topography [83, 86]. To investigate these intrinsic organizational principles, in the absence of task-related activity, resting-state functional connectivity (RS-FC) methods have emerged as a critical investigative tool [4, 61]. Functional connectivity (FC), defined as the temporal correlation of signals within spatially distributed neural networks [31, 32, 88], provides the methodological framework to assess such integration.

Historically, FC has been predominantly studied using functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), which offers high spatial resolution [30, 34]. However, fMRI is limited by its low temporal resolution and reliance on indirect measures of neural activity mediated by the hemodynamic response [56]. In contrast, electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) provide excellent temporal resolution, making them ideal for capturing the time-dependent correspondence of neural signals [82].

A pioneering study by Liu and colleagues [56] demonstrated that large-scale brain networks could be identified from resting-state EEG recordings without requiring a concurrent MRI scan. Other studies have shown that each Resting State Network (RSN) is characterized by a distinct pattern of brain oscillations [61]. Additionally, the FC properties of RSNs can be suitable for predicting stroke behavioral outcomes [43, 79].

Despite growing interest in EEG-based FC as a tool for assessing stroke outcome and recovery, no consensus exists on which FC measures are most reliable and predictive of clinical status and recovery. Current methodologies vary widely, incorporating diverse metrics and techniques across time, frequency, and information-theoretic domains. Approaches also differ in whether they employ pairwise or multivariate analyses and directed or non-directed methods [9, 18]. Additional File 1 provides a summary of the EEG-based functional connectivity measures reported in the reviewed literature. The table includes the name of each FC measure, its mathematical formulation, the primary reference in which it was introduced or used, and the corresponding DOI.

Importantly, all these measures fundamentally assess statistical patterns or system behavior rather than underlying mechanisms or causes. The researcher bears the critical responsibility for drawing appropriate inferences from the results. Given this framework, we do not differentiate between directional and non-directional measures, nor between directed and conditioned measures, as these distinctions primarily reflect different conditioning strategies, whether on lagged values of the time series itself, on contemporaneous values of other variables, or on some combination thereof. The choice of conditioning approach ultimately depends on the researcher’s judgment and confidence in their integrated data-analysis-inference methodology.

To investigate whether EEG-based FC is a suitable tool for assessing stroke outcome and recovery, and taking into account these methodological considerations, this review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of existing EEG-based FC measures and to evaluate their clinical relevance in the context of stroke recovery. Furthermore, it aims to identify the most reliable and predictive metrics through an examination of longitudinal FC changes, correlations with clinical outcomes, and the specific effects of intervention protocols. Ultimately, this review aims to provide a framework for selecting and evaluating EEG-based FC measures as potential biomarkers of stroke outcome and recovery.

Methods

Literature search

This study was conducted in line with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [69]. The search was performed on three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Embase. To ensure the inclusion of all relevant studies, the following search terms were used: (((connect*) OR (coher*) OR (synchron*) OR (brain connect*) OR (neural connect*) OR (“network analysis”) OR (“graph theory”) OR (“network”) OR (“ICA”) OR (“independent component analysis”) AND ((EEG) OR (electroencephalogra*) OR (brain wave) AND ((stroke) OR (cerebrovascular accident) AND ((recovery) OR (rehabilit*) OR (treat*) OR (outcome)). Details of the complete search strategy for each database are provided in Additional File 2.

No restriction on publication date range was applied (last date of search: 7th July 2024), and only studies with an English version and published in peer-reviewed journals were considered.

Finally, during the revision process, a supplementary targeted search was conducted to ensure comprehensive coverage of studies utilizing specific algorithm names and acronyms (e.g., Phase Synchronization Index, PSI, Partial Directed Coherence, PDC) that might be absent from generic connectivity terms. This search used the specific metric names listed in Additional File 1 as keywords and was constrained to the same time window as the original search (up to July 2024) to maintain methodological consistency. This step allowed for the identification and inclusion of two additional relevant studies [107, 108] that were within the scope but not captured by the initial broad strategy.

Risk of bias

To assess the quality of the studies included, we applied the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), a tool developed to evaluate non-randomized studies for systematic reviews [73]. More specifically, a version adapted from Masina and colleagues [62] was used (see Additional File 3 for information on NOS). Two independent reviewers (E.N. and M.S.S.) assessed the quality of the included articles. Discrepancies in the evaluation were resolved by a third author (G.O.).

Screening and data extraction

Three authors (E.N., F.V., M.S.S.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the articles collected from the database search. The authors applied the inclusion and exclusion criteria to select the articles, and any disagreement in study selection was discussed and resolved among all the authors. Conflicts were resolved through pairwise discussions until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction was performed by E.N., F.V., M.S.S., F.M., and RP. Each author was assigned a subset of papers to review. For each study, we highlighted the FC measure used, the frequency bands examined, and, if present, the clinical assessment performed. Moreover, we underlined whether network analyses were conducted, the electrodes or ROIs investigated, and whether the analyses were performed at the source or sensor level. Finally, to assess technical heterogeneity, specific details regarding signal processing (e.g., referencing, filtering, artifact removal), as well as analysis parameters (e.g., window length, overlap), were systematically extracted and are reported in Additional File 4. Data extracted from each subset of papers was subsequently and independently double-checked by the authors who did not perform data extraction on that specific subset. Results

The systematic literature search identified 1,755 articles. After removing duplicates, 947 unique records remained. These records were screened based on their titles and abstracts using the established criteria, leading to the exclusion of 893 articles. Subsequently, the full texts of 54 articles were assessed. Of these, one article could not be retrieved, and 24 articles did not meet the inclusion criteria, resulting in a final selection of 30 articles deemed suitable for inclusion in this review.

It is important to note that measures with different names do not always quantify completely different aspects of brain interactions, and in some cases, they measure the same underlying dynamics. For example, under the Gaussian/linear assumption, Granger causality and Transfer Entropy are equivalent [5]. This similarity extends to frequency domain measures: Partial Coherence, Directed Coherence, and Partial Directed Coherence, for instance, can all be derived by the same multivariate autoregressive (MVAR) framework [18, 28], while several widely used nonlinear phase-phase or amplitude-amplitude coupling metrics reduce to functions of the cross-spectrum for Gaussian-distributed signals [67].

Nevertheless, these measures are often retained as separate entries in methodological taxonomies and empirical studies, reflecting a text-driven or nomenclature-based classification rather than a principled distinction based on underlying mathematical or functional differences. This practice contributes to redundancy in the reported metrics and may obscure the true methodological diversity (or lack thereof) in the field, complicating efforts to meaningfully compare findings across studies. While we adopt a nomenclature-based classification for consistency with the existing literature, we acknowledge that developing a more principled taxonomy is well beyond the scope of the present work.

Cortical vs. subcortical lesion distribution

Information regarding lesion location, specifically focusing on the distinction between cortical and subcortical damage, was extracted to assess the homogeneity of the study populations. Across the reviewed studies, cohorts were predominantly mixed or insufficiently characterized. Regarding the studies assessing the longitudinal changes in FC (Table 1), only one study focused exclusively on subcortical stroke, while the majority (n = 4) included both cortical and subcortical patients, and one did not specify the location.

In studies examining correlations with clinical measures (Table 2), the sample was similarly heterogeneous: 12 studies recruited mixed populations, whereas a substantial portion (n = 11) did not explicitly report lesion location distinctions. Only two studies focused on cortical lesions, and one on subcortical lesions.

Finally, regarding the studies assessing the treatment effects on FC (Table 3), three studies included mixed populations, while two did not provide specific information. Overall, the scarcity of studies stratifying patients by lesion location precluded a systematic subgroup analysis of functional connectivity patterns based on lesion depth.

Longitudinal changes in FC

Six studies investigated the longitudinal changes of FC in stroke patients. Among these, three focused on coherence-based FC measures, while one study examined both Granger causality (GC)-based and information-theoretic measures. Additionally, phase-based and amplitude envelope correlation FC measures were explored in separate studies. Five out of six studies included rehabilitation protocols such as BCI-guided training, robotic-assisted therapy, or structured rehabilitation programs. In these studies, results primarily refer to within-group changes over time (pre- vs. post-intervention) rather than comparisons against a control group. Consequently, the observed changes in FC may reflect a combination of spontaneous recovery and therapy-induced neuroplasticity [13].

Amplitude envelope correlation

Zheng et al. [109] reported changes in FC measured by Amplitude Envelope Correlation (AEC) in chronic stroke patients. Their findings revealed increased FC between frontal and central electrodes within the ipsilesional hemisphere following conventional rehabilitation, while no significant changes were observed in the contralesional hemisphere.

Phase-based FC measures

Wang et al. [101] observed an increase in ipsilesional intra-hemispheric FC at the sensor level, as measured by the Phase Synchronization Index (PSI) in subacute stroke patients. This study also reported graph theoretical measures. Specifically, it showed an increase in the clustering coefficient and a decrease in path length within the delta frequency band, indicating potential improvements in network efficiency.

Coherence-based FC measures

Pirovano et al. [75] assessed changes in FC after conventional motor rehabilitation in subacute and chronic stroke patients, using Directed Coherence (DC) and Generalized Partial Directed Coherence (gPDC) in the alpha and beta frequency bands at the source level. They found increased intra-hemispheric connectivity from the premotor cortex to primary motor areas in the alpha band and from primary motor areas to the premotor cortex in the beta band. However, the DC significance of the results did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons.

Conversely, gPDC revealed increased connectivity from the supplementary motor area to the contralesional primary motor area in the alpha band and a reduction in connectivity from the contralesional pre-motor cortex to the supplementary motor area in both alpha and beta bands.

Similarly, Yuan et al. [106] reported that Brain-Computer Interface training enhanced FC in chronic stroke patients, as measured by Partial Directed Coherence (PDC) at the sensor level. They observed increased connectivity from ipsilesional motor areas to the contralesional premotor and supplementary motor areas in the alpha and beta bands.

In contrast, Casas et al. [12] found no significant changes in FC in chronic stroke patients, as measured by Magnitude Squared Coherence (MSC) at the sensor level in the alpha and beta bands, after home-based therapy.

Granger causality and information-theoretic measures

Pirovano et al. [74] examined changes in FC in subacute stroke patients after conventional motor rehabilitation using conditional Granger Causality (cGC) and O-Information Rate (O-IR) at the source level. They reported a reduction in O-IR values when the Motor Network (MN) acted as a driver toward the Default Mode Network (DMN) and the Executive Control Network (ECN) in the theta and alpha frequency bands. A similar reduction was observed when the MN and ECN jointly drove the DMN in the theta, alpha, and beta bands.

Additionally, they found a decrease in cGC from the DMN to the MN in the alpha and beta bands, as well as from the ECN to the MN in the beta and theta bands. Conversely, an increase in cGC was observed from the MN to the ECN in the theta band.

In summary, AEC, phase-based, and coherence-based measures predominantly highlighted increased intra-hemispheric connectivity within the ipsilesional hemisphere, particularly between motor-related areas. In contrast, GC and information-theoretic approaches revealed dynamic shifts in connectivity between core brain networks, reflecting the complex reorganization processes underlying stroke recovery.

Correlation between FC and clinical measures

Twenty-four studies focused on the relationship between EEG-based connectivity metrics and clinical outcomes such as motor functions, cognitive abilities, and overall recovery. Among these studies, the majority focused on motor recovery, while fewer studies focused on cognition and functional independence. Moreover, 13 studies investigated coherence-based FC measures, 8 studies examined phase-based FC measures, 3 studies explored amplitude or power envelope correlation, and one study evaluated both Granger causality (GC) and information theory-based measures.

To enhance clarity, the study findings were categorized into four domains:

Motor recovery, as measured by the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA, [33]) and the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT, [58])

Amplitude envelope correlation

Amplitude Envelope Correlation (AEC) has shown mixed results, possibly depending on electrode placement and targeted outcomes.

Motor recovery. Zheng et al. [109] found that intrahemispheric FC between ipsilesional electrode pairs positively correlated with upper extremity motor gains following conventional rehabilitation, although no significant correlation was found for lower extremity motor recovery. Conversely, Lin et al. [55] found that interhemispheric FC between electrodes placed over frontal, central, parietal, and occipital regions, as well as intrahemispheric FC within the frontal cortex, positively correlated with balance and lower extremity motor recovery. Furthermore, their Deep Learning model, which utilized clinical and biomechanical data, EEG FC, and EEG Power Spectral Density as inputs, reported high accuracy in classifying patients into proportional and poor recovery groups. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size (N = 15) and the lack of explicit definitions for the ‘proportional’ and ‘poor’ recovery categories, which limit the model’s robustness and replicability.

Cognition and functional independence. Romeo et al. [79] assessed source-level FC using Power Envelope Correlation and reported that FC within the Dorsal Attentional Network positively correlated with attentional, motor, and functional measures in specific frequency bands. In contrast, FC within the Language and Medial Prefrontal Networks showed negative correlations with Functional Independence Measure (FIM) motor and cognitive scores.

Phase-based FC measures

Several studies have demonstrated associations between Phase Synchronization Index (PSI), Phase Lag Index (PLI), and recovery outcomes.

Motor recovery. Kawano et al. [45] observed positive correlations between PSI over central and parietal electrodes and FMA-UE scores at admission, with delta and theta PSI within the contralesional hemisphere correlating with motor improvement. In a recent study, Zhang et al. [108] provided preliminary evidence on the prognostic value of PSI in a small cohort of stroke patients undergoing BCI rehabilitation. Specifically, they reported a significant positive correlation between PSI in the low alpha band and motor improvement as measured by FMA-UE. Moreover, regression analysis based on these metrics predicted recovery outcomes with high accuracy. Ding et al. [27] reported that temporal and parietal clustering coefficients derived from PLI-based networks positively correlated with improvements in both upper limb and wrist and hand motor functions. Hordacre et al. [43] found that beta-band debiased weighted PLI (dwPLI) between sensorimotor cortices was associated with baseline motor status and recovery. Similarly, Rosenich et al. [81] observed that alpha dwPLI between central electrodes and both right frontal and left parietal electrodes was associated with reduced impairment at baseline. They further reported that increased EEG alpha phase synchronization, less severe stroke, smaller lesion volume, and greater cognitive reserve were associated with upper-limb motor recovery. However, Van Kaam et al. [97] found no significant correlation between wPLI and recovery metrics.

Functional independence and stroke severity. Kawano et al. [46] further found that intrahemispheric PSI in the alpha band correlated with FIM scores, while interhemispheric PSI in the alpha band was positively correlated with both FIM cognitive and motor subscores and negatively correlated with NIHSS. Moreover, the authors reported positive associations between intra- and interhemispheric PLI in the alpha band and FIM scores. Conversely, Wu et al. [105] reported that interhemispheric PSI in the alpha band was positively correlated with patients’ recovery as measured by NIHSS. Wang et al. [101] also highlighted that the direction and magnitude of the correlation between interhemispheric PSI and both NIHSS scores and the level of consciousness varied depending on frequency bands and analysis parameters.

Coherence-based FC measures

Motor recovery. Sun et al. [92] reported negative correlations between motor gains one week after intervention and interhemispheric coherence-based FC in the delta, theta, and alpha bands, contralesional FC in the theta, alpha, and beta bands, and ipsilesional supplementary motor area (SMA) FC in the delta band. Additionally, contralesional local connectivity in the alpha band negatively correlated with motor gains at the 6-month follow-up. Pre-training interhemispheric connectivity in delta, theta, and alpha, as well as contralesional connectivity in beta, differentiated participants with good and poor recovery.

Similarly, Cassidy et al. [16] observed a negative correlation between motor gains and delta-band coherence-based FC between electrodes over contralesional and ipsilesional primary motor cortices. However, a subsequent study [15] reported that low coherence values in low-beta and high coherence values in delta were associated with better motor recovery scores. Furthermore, coherence between ipsilesional primary motor cortex and electrodes over both hemispheres predicted 62% of the variance in motor recovery. Yuan et al. [106] reported that Partial Directed Coherence (PDC) between the ipsilesional primary motor area and both the contralesional premotor cortex and the contralesional sensory motor area was associated with motor recovery. Similarly, generalized PDC from the contralesional to the ipsilesional pre-motor cortex correlated with upper limb motor recovery in the alpha band [75]. Finally, Casas et al. [12] found that increases in beta-band Magnitude Squared Coherence (MSC) between contralesional sensorimotor and ipsilesional pre-motor areas correlated with motor gains.

Motor recovery was also linked to imaginary coherence in alpha, theta, and delta bands, or in delta, theta, and mid-beta bands, depending on whether the EEG signal was recorded with eyes open or closed [54].

Regarding network metrics, Guggisberg et al. [38] found that, at admission, patients with poor recovery showed disrupted beta weighted node degree (WND) in the affected hemisphere, while those with proportional motor recovery exhibited high beta WND within frontal regions. However, longitudinal changes in FC did not differ between the two groups. Additionally, a machine learning model using initial FMA, CST asymmetry, and beta-band coherence as predictors accurately differentiated patients with poor and proportional motor recovery.

Functional independence and stroke severity. Assenza et al. [2] found that interhemispheric coherence in the alpha band did not explain recovery as measured by NIHSS. However, Vecchio et al. [98] used MSC values as weights for network analyses and found that theta small-worldness (SW) negatively correlated with NIHSS scores, delta and theta SW positively correlated with BI scores, and high-beta and gamma SW negatively correlated with both BI and ARAT scores. On the other hand, Van Kaam et al. [97] found no significant correlation between MSC and functional recovery as measured by NIHSS and modified Rankin Scale.

Network analyses incorporating Lagged Linear Coherence at the source level highlighted associations with both functional and motor recovery. Higher beta connectivity and SW values corresponded to lower BI scores at T0, while higher alpha FC and SW corresponded to higher BI scores at T0 and lower recovery [29]. Conversely, Vecchio et al. [99] reported that SW in high-beta and gamma bands negatively correlated with NIHSS scores, while it positively correlated with BI and ARAT scores.

Cognition. Lee et al. [51] used imaginary coherence measured at the source level as weights for network analyses and reported that higher MoCA scores in left- and right-stroke patients were associated with higher clustering coefficients in theta and alpha bands, respectively. Moreover, the predictive accuracy of post-stroke cognitive status was better when using network features alone than when incorporating imaginary coherence.

Furthermore, high-beta coherence between ipsilesional primary motor cortex and ipsilesional parietal cortex positively correlated with training-related gains in a visuomotor tracking task [110].

Granger causality and information-theoretic measures

Motor recovery. Pirovano et al. [74] assessed the role of rehabilitation in FC changes measured by cGC and O-IR. The results suggested that motor gains positively correlated with increased cGC from ECN to DMN in the alpha band and from MN to ECN in the theta band. Moreover, they reported negative correlations between O-IR variations from T0 to T1 and FMA gains when ECN acted as a driver toward DMN and MN in theta and alpha, and when DMN and ECN acted as drivers toward MN in theta.

In summary, the majority of studies investigating EEG-based functional connectivity focused on motor recovery, with fewer examining cognition and functional independence. Different FC metrics, including coherence-based, phase-based, amplitude envelope correlation, and information-theoretic measures, showed varying correlations with clinical outcomes.

Motor recovery was primarily associated with coherence-based and phase-based FC measures, often highlighting intra- and interhemispheric connectivity in alpha, beta, and theta bands.

Cognitive functioning showed links to theta and alpha connectivity, particularly in coherence and network-based analyses.

Functional independence (FIM, BI) and stroke severity (NIHSS) demonstrated mixed correlations with coherence-based FC and network measures, sometimes showing both positive and negative associations depending on frequency bands and brain regions. EEG-based FC metrics demonstrated significant, albeit heterogeneous, correlations with clinical outcomes such as motor and cognitive recovery. Coherence- and phase-based measures primarily highlighted associations between intra- and interhemispheric connectivity and motor and cognitive gains, while envelope correlation measures showed links with attentional, motor, and functional measures. Granger causality and information-theoretic approaches revealed complex directional interactions between large-scale networks, often correlating with functional improvements.

Experimental vs. control treatment effects on FC

Five studies focused on comparing the effects of experimental and conventional control treatments on EEG-based FC. Of these, three studies focused on phase-based FC measures, one study on coherence, and one on GC-based measures.

Phase-based FC measures

Waterstone et al. [90] found that alpha Phase Lag Index measured at the source level significantly increased after spinal manipulation treatment in chronic stroke patients, but not after conventional treatment.

Similarly, Ding et al. [27] used PLI values as weights for network analyses and reported that both nodal and global clustering coefficients increased after mirror visual feedback training in chronic stroke patients, but not after conventional training.

Finally, Tang et al. [93] reported that, in chronic stroke patients, alpha and beta weighted PLI in frontal, limbic, and fronto-parietal regions were heightened after robot-assisted training, but not after conventional training.

Coherence-based FC measures

Sun et al. [92] suggested that delta, theta, and alpha interhemispheric coherence, as well as theta, alpha, and beta contralesional coherence in chronic stroke patients, correlated with motor gains one week after BCI training, but not after control training.

Granger causality and information theoretic measures

Zhan et al. [107] investigated the effects of BCI-guided Functional Electrical Stimulation (BCI-FES) applied to the paretic muscles compared to FES alone in chronic patients using Direct Directed Transfer Function (dDTF). They found that global and local graph theory metrics (e.g., efficiency and clustering coefficient) significantly improved in the BCI group, but not in the control group. Specifically, the intervention induced a significant increase in node strength within the sensorimotor cortex of the contralesional hemisphere, suggesting that BCI-FES promotes functional recovery by boosting compensatory activity in the unaffected hemisphere.

In summary, the Phase Lag Index revealed increased alpha and beta connectivity, along with enhanced network properties, following interventions such as spinal manipulation, mirror visual feedback, and robot-assisted training. Coherence-based measures highlighted correlations between interhemispheric and contralesional coherence and motor gains after BCI therapy. In contrast, conventional treatments showed limited or no impact on FC as compared to experimental treatments. Finally, dDTF suggested that the experimental intervention enhanced global network efficiency and promoted compensatory reorganization in the contralesional hemisphere, while the conventional treatment showed no impact on FC metrics.

Discussion

This systematic review examines the potential of EEG-based resting-state FC as a candidate biomarker for stroke recovery, offering a comprehensive overview of longitudinal changes, correlations with clinical measures, and the effects of various experimental treatments on FC. The findings underscore significant heterogeneity in methodologies, connectivity measures, and analytical approaches, which hinders cross-study comparisons and clinical translation.

Sensor- vs. source-based connectivity analysis

EEG-based functional connectivity is typically estimated in the sensor space, based on activity recorded through scalp electrodes [64]. This method has two main drawbacks: first, it does not provide inferences about the anatomical sources of the signals, and second, it can be affected by volume conduction [95]. Volume conduction refers to the transmission of electric fields from cortical sources through brain tissues [47], which can lead to spurious connections [64, 95]. Therefore, results derived from sensor-level EEG FC should be interpreted with caution, especially if FC measures that fail to control for volume conduction are used [50].

To interpret FC data in neuroanatomical terms, researchers must reconstruct the anatomical sources from the recorded data [59], which allows for control over the effects of volume conduction [85]. Several FC measures account for volume conduction [21, 66, 70]. However, despite its theoretical appeal, our review suggests that the source-based approach remains underrepresented, likely due to its greater methodological complexity and computational demands. Nevertheless, sensor-level functional connectivity metrics can be useful for predicting stroke clinical outcomes. For instance, Yuan et al. [106] found that Partial Directed Coherence correlated with motor recovery, and Lin et al. [55] used sensor-level Amplitude Envelope Correlation to build a highly accurate prognostic deep learning model. This raises the question of whether the added complexity of source-level analysis is always necessary.

It is worth noting that source-level analysis requires solving the inverse problem, that is, mapping the activity from electrodes to the underlying cortical sources [96]. There is no single solution to this, and it involves assumptions and constraints that cannot be directly tested [40, 63]. Even when using source-based analysis, the effects of volume conduction cannot be fully eliminated [96]. However, combining source-level analysis with robust connectivity measures such as imaginary coherence, Phase Lag Index, and weighted Phase Lag Index [41] may mitigate these effects.

From a clinical perspective, sensor-level measures offer practical advantages due to their computational efficiency. Future studies should systematically compare sensor- and source-level metrics to determine when and for what purposes source-based analyses provide additional value.

Descriptive vs. predictive approaches

The majority of studies included in this review were descriptive, focusing on identifying patterns of FC changes or correlating these changes with clinical outcomes. For example, Sun et al. [92] and Pirovano et al. [75] described how intra- and inter-hemispheric connectivity changes over time and with treatment, and how these changes correlate with motor recovery. While these descriptive findings provide valuable mechanistic insights into the effects of stroke and the neural processes underlying recovery, they mainly contribute to basic research and do not yet support the use of EEG-based FC as a reliable clinical biomarker. As highlighted by Stinear et al. [91], a clinically useful biomarker must go beyond group-level associations and demonstrate the ability to make accurate predictions at the individual level, especially in the early stages post-stroke. Currently, only a few studies have attempted to move towards predictive frameworks, though they remain preliminary. For example, Lin et al. [55] applied a deep learning model combining FC, clinical, and biomechanical data to classify recovery outcomes. However, the small sample size (N = 15) limits the generalizability of the findings and suggests this should be viewed as a proof-of-concept rather than a validated clinical tool. Similarly, Cassidy et al. [15] showed that specific coherence metrics could predict 62% of the variance in motor recovery outcomes. While this indicates a strong statistical association at the group level, explaining variance is distinct from making accurate predictions for individual patients [91].

To advance from these exploratory studies to robust clinical tools, future research must adopt rigorous predictive modeling standards [7]. Specifically, establishing clinical utility requires: (i) larger sample size to ensure statistical power and avoid the limitation of underpowered studies [7]; (ii) external validation in independent cohort to ensure generalizability [91]; and (iii) prediction of specific functional outcomes rather than generic binary outcomes, ensuring the tool provides information beyond basic clinical fators like age and stroke severity [91]. Only by adhering to these standards can EEG-based FC transition from a research tool to a viable component of personalized rehabilitation.

Main functional domains under investigation

Most studies in this review focused on motor recovery, underscoring the importance of motor function in stroke rehabilitation. Coherence- and phase-based measures were commonly used to explore motor-related FC, with many studies identifying connectivity patterns linked to recovery. For instance, Kawano et al. [45] found correlations between Phase Synchronization Index (PSI) in the delta and theta bands and upper extremity motor function.

Cognitive recovery, though less frequently studied, has shown associations with EEG-based FC. For example, Lee et al. [51] used imaginary coherence to predict cognitive function as measured by MoCA, while Romeo et al. [79] suggested that connectivity within the Dorsal Attentional Network was linked to various cognitive test scores. These findings indicate the broader applicability of FC metrics beyond motor recovery, extending into cognition.

Future research should adopt a multidimensional approach that integrates motor and cognitive recovery, akin to the hospital setting where various medical disciplines converge to optimize patient outcomes [14]. For example, it has been shown that attentional deficits can hinder upper limb motor recovery in stroke patients [26, 84], thereby suggesting that cognitive status needs to be considered for predicting motor rehabilitation outcomes.

Longitudinal changes and the role of healthy controls

Many studies reported longitudinal changes in FC following rehabilitation, often interpreted as recovery-related. For instance, Pirovano et al. [75] observed increased intra-hemispheric connectivity in the ipsilesional hemisphere following conventional rehabilitation, while Yuan et al. [106] noted enhanced connectivity after BCI training. However, the absence of healthy control groups in many studies raises concerns about the interpretation of these changes. Specifically, comparing post-stroke FC trajectories against healthy controls is essential to elucidate the underlying neural mechanisms specific to recovery and not generically related to training. While patients primarily prioritize functional gains regardless of the neural strategy [57], distinguishing between neural restitution, namely the re-convergence towards healthy network patterns, and neural compensation, namely the establishment of alternative functional pathways, is crucial for optimizing clinical decision-making and selecting appropriate interventions [52]. Furthermore, including healthy controls in longitudinal designs allows researchers to quantify test-retest variability and control for training-induced plasticity (e.g., network modulations driven by motor learning and training rather than pathology-specific recovery; [1]). In sum, without such controls, it remains difficult to determine whether observed FC changes reflect genuine post-stroke reorganization or physical plasticity common to any subject undergoing intensive motor training [102]. Finally, the distinction between restitution and compensation bears critical implications for the selection of behavioural outcome measures. Scales targeting body structure and function, such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), are designed to isolate physiological motor recovery and are therefore likely to align more closely with network reorganization [36]. In contrast, measures of functional independence like the Barthel Index evaluate the ability to complete the task, regardless of the strategy used. Since patients can achieve higher BI scores through behavioural compensation, improvements on such scales may occur independently of neuroplastic changes in the affected network. Consequently, relying on functional independence measures may introduce variability that cannot be captured by FC metrics, potentially obscuring significant brain-behaviour correlations.

Heterogeneity in patient characteristics: cortical vs. subcortical involvement

A critical factor likely contributing to the inconsistency of the reported FC findings is the lack of stratification based on lesion depth. As highlighted in our results, the vast majority of studies either combined cortical and subcortical patients or did not report the specific site of damage. For example, among correlational studies, nearly half failed to specify the lesion location, and only three studies focused exclusively on either cortical or subcortical stroke locations. This represents a significant limitation for the field, as cortical and subcortical lesions impact neural networks through distinct mechanisms. Cortical lesions directly damage the gray matter, potentially altering local oscillatory dynamics, while subcortical lesions primarily affect white matter tracts, leading to disconnection and diaschisis in remote cortical regions despite intact cortical structure [10, 86]. By pooling these populations, studies may obscure specific connectivity patterns associated with each lesion type. Consequently, it remains difficult to disentangle whether the mixed results observed in this review stem from methodological differences or distinct pathophysiological mechanisms.

Types of connectivity measures

The review revealed considerable heterogeneity across studies in the connectivity measures used, reflecting the diversity of analytical approaches in the field. FC metrics vary in the neuronal signal characteristics they consider and the level of analysis, highlighting differences in theoretical frameworks, computational methods, and clinical applications. This variability complicates the synthesis of findings and cross-study comparisons. Crucially, heterogeneity is not limited to the final metric choice but permeates the entire analytical pipeline. As detailed in Additional File 4, the included studies exhibited substantial variability in preprocessing strategies, ranging from reference selection to artifact removal and epoch length, factors known to significantly affect functional connectivity estimates [96].

Measures like Amplitude Envelope Correlation (AEC) and Power Envelope Correlation (PEC) focus on temporal correlations in signal amplitude fluctuations [18]. Crucially, standard implementations of these measures are not intrinsically designed to be robust against volume conduction artifacts [42]. Nonetheless, these measures have proven useful in linking FC to stroke recovery [79, 109], but they are less sensitive to directionality and dynamics, limiting their explanatory power [6].

Phase-based measures like Phase Lag Index and Phase Synchronization Index assess phase consistency between signals, providing insights into the temporal coordination of cortical activity [20]. However, contrasting results across studies indicate variability in measurement parameters, frequency bands, and analytical choices.

Coherence and its derivatives quantify spectral similarity between signals [72], but basic coherence measures do not provide information on directionality, limiting their ability to infer causal interactions. More complex measures like Directed Coherence introduce additional challenges, including noise sensitivity and computational requirements [3].

Advanced methods like Granger Causality and information-theoretic approaches offer valuable insights into directional and higher-order connectivity. However, GC assumes linear interactions [37], which limits its application to complex neural dynamics, while information-theoretic measures can capture both linear and nonlinear interactions but are computationally intensive [80].

As briefly mentioned in the result section, it is important to note that measures with different names do not necessarily quantify entirely different phenomena. For example, under Gaussian and linear assumptions, Granger Causality and Transfer Entropy are mathematically equivalent [5]. Similarly, many phase-phase and amplitude-amplitude coupling measures—such as wPLI, PLI, and variants of PLV—can be related mathematically, often reducible to functions of lagged coherence when accounting for volume conduction artifacts [67]. This highlights a fundamental overlap in the information captured by many commonly used FC metrics, especially under the assumption of Gaussian-distributed neural data. To clarify this landscape and reduce the perceived complexity of the field, we provide a taxonomic categorization of the included metrics based on their underlying mathematical principles in Fig. 3.

Taxonomic categorization of FC metrics. The connectivity measures identified in this review are grouped into four families based on their underlying mathematical principles: (1) Phase dynamics; (2) cross-spectral measures; (3) amplitude correlations; (4) autoregressive and information-theoretic measures. Note that partial directed coherence (PDC) and direct directed transfer function (dDTF) are classified in category (4) as they are mathematically derived from multivariate autoregressive (MVAR) models

This visual synthesis illustrates how a wide array of nomenclature often converges onto a few core analytical families. Moreover, empirical investigations of several pairwise interaction measures reveal that the similarity between metrics depends strongly on the dataset [19]. Many measures cluster into modules of redundant or overlapping behavior, suggesting that the effective methodological diversity is lower than nominal counts imply [19]. This finding supports the call for a highly comparative approach to connectivity analysis: rather than manually selecting metrics based on tradition or name, researchers should systematically assess which measures best capture the relevant interactions in their datasets. Such an approach has multiple practical benefits, including improved interpretability and methodological efficiency. For instance, the computational cost of applying a large battery of FC metrics can be reduced by selecting representative, minimally redundant subsets. Furthermore, dimensionality reduction and feature selection techniques can help isolate combinations of complementary connectivity measures tailored to specific research questions, avoiding overfitting and bias. The complexity and variety of neural interactions also argue for methodological transparency and possibly pre-registration of analytic pipelines to improve reproducibility and robustness.

Regarding metric families, a clear trend emerged: phase-based metrics demonstrated remarkable consistency. As shown in Fig. 4, the vast majority of studies employing these measures reported positive associations between increased connectivity and better recovery. In contrast, coherence-based measures showed high variability, with findings ranging from positive to negative or null across similar clinical populations. Regarding the level of analysis (Fig. 4A), transitioning to source-level reconstruction did not automatically resolve inconsistencies: while some source-level studies identified potential biomarkers, others reported mixed or inverse relationships. Regarding clinical domains (Fig. 4B), the highest consistency was observed in the motor domain, where phase-based metrics consistently tracked recovery. Conversely, findings in cognitive and functional domains remained more fragmented. Thus, while phase-based connectivity yielded the most consistent results, the persistence of heterogeneity across other dimensions confirms that methodological choices interact complexly with sample characteristics.

In summary, the diversity of connectivity measures used in EEG-based studies reflects the evolving methodologies of FC analysis. While each measure has its strengths and limitations, the heterogeneity calls for standardized protocols and greater methodological transparency to enable robust cross-study comparisons. Advanced metrics, though overlooked, offer significant potential to uncover deeper insights into post-stroke recovery. Importantly, even if theoretically separable, many metrics can yield similar results and share mathematical relationships; without testing multiple measures empirically, it is difficult to infer whether findings would hold across different analytical choices. This reinforces the necessity for a multiverse approach to systematically test robustness across these analytical degrees of freedom.

Towards a multiverse approach

Results of this review underscore the variability in results due to differences in methodological choices, such as preprocessing parameters, connectivity metrics, and analysis frameworks. Emerging evidence suggests that research findings may be highly dependent on variations in analysis strategies [48], raising concerns about their robustness and replicability [8, 103].

A promising solution to the replication challenge is the adoption of a Multiverse approach [23, 48]. This approach involves testing multiple plausible analytical workflows to identify robust findings that remain consistent across variations, thus increasing transparency [89]. In stroke recovery research, the multiverse framework can help determine which connectivity aspects are consistently predictive of recovery across different methodological pipelines, enhancing the reproducibility, generalizability, and clinical relevance of findings. A multiverse approach can also elucidate the empirical relation between different measures with methodological and theoretical implications. This has important consequences, as it enables researchers to assess the extent to which findings are driven by specific preprocessing steps, connectivity metrics, or statistical techniques, ultimately refining theoretical models and ensuring that conclusions are not unduly influenced by arbitrary choices [25]. Moreover, the multiverse approach allows researchers to handle the degrees of freedom of data analysis while providing a robust framework for deriving inferences about specific analyses [35] and facilitating model selection among different indices to determine their predictive value and generalizability [23].

Implementing a multiverse approach in EEG-based FC stroke research would entail defining a grid of defensible analysis paths rather than selecting a single pipeline. For example, a researcher investigating motor recovery might systematically vary: (i) preprocessing choices, such as the reference scheme or artifact removal thresholds; (ii) frequency bands definitions, comparing fixed bands (e.g., standard alpha 8–12 Hz) against individualized peaks (e.g. individual alpha frequency); and (iii) connectivity metrics, computing results across a spectrum of measures that handle volume conduction differently. By running the statistical analysis across all these combinations, researchers can report the distribution of p-values or effect sizes (e.g., via specification curve analysis; [87]). If a significant correlation persists across the majority of these variations, the result can be considered robust.

By adopting a multiverse perspective, researchers can improve the reliability of EEG-based FC findings, accelerating the identification of robust potential biomarkers for stroke recovery.

Limitations

This systematic review has limitations that should be considered. First, the restriction to English-language peer-reviewed articles listed in major databases may have introduced a selection bias, potentially excluding relevant findings published in other languages or in the grey literature. Second, while our search strategy was comprehensive, the extreme heterogeneity in methodological pipelines, ranging from preprocessing choices to the mathematical definitions of connectivity metrics, limited our synthesis to a qualitative level. This variability prevented the estimation of pooled effect sizes or the identification of a unified gold standard metric, reinforcing the necessity for the standardized or multiverse approaches discussed above. Finally, the conclusions of this review are inherently constrained by the characteristics of the primary evidence base. As highlighted in previous sections, many included studies relied on small sample sizes, lacked stratification by lesion location, and predominantly employed descriptive rather than predictive designs. Consequently, the associations reported here should be interpreted as potential mechanistic insights rather than validated clinical biomarkers ready for immediate translation.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the potential of EEG-based resting-state functional connectivity (FC) as a biomarker for stroke recovery, while identifying key challenges and opportunities for advancing the field. Significant heterogeneity exists in methodologies, connectivity measures, and analytical frameworks, underscoring the need for standardized protocols to enhance comparability and facilitate translation into clinical practice. In particular, the widespread use of diverse and sometimes mathematically overlapping connectivity metrics can lead to redundancy and complicate interpretation, emphasizing the importance of clarifying the relationships between measures and selecting those that offer complementary information.

The review emphasizes the importance of addressing methodological considerations, such as the distinction between sensor- and source-based analyses, the balance between descriptive and predictive approaches, and the integration of advanced connectivity metrics. Additionally, while motor recovery has been the predominant focus, the exploration of cognitive and other domains remains underrepresented but holds promise.

The multiverse perspective emerges as a critical framework to improve the robustness and replicability of findings. Multiple analytical workflows can help disentangle genuine recovery-related phenomena from spurious effects, which arise due to the excessive degrees of freedom in each method that lead to varying conclusions. In an era increasingly focused on clinical prediction and the growing influence of AI-driven healthcare, a multiverse approach can also play a crucial role in optimizing predictive models. By providing a structured way to assess which functional connectivity metrics contribute most meaningfully to prognostic algorithms, it facilitates the development of more reliable and clinically useful predictive tools.

Looking ahead, the field would benefit from collaborative efforts to establish guidelines for methodological consistency, incorporate multidimensional recovery domains, and prioritize predictive approaches aligned with clinical needs. Moreover, a systematic taxonomy that reflects underlying mathematical relationships, rather than nomenclature alone, would reduce redundancy and enhance methodological transparency. By addressing these challenges and leveraging the potential of advanced computational methods, EEG-based FC holds promise as a transformative tool for guiding personalized rehabilitation strategies and optimizing outcomes for stroke patients.
