Adverse events of special interest after vaccination with COVID-19 vaccines: a cohort event monitoring study in Iran
Authors: Roqayeh Aliyari, Mohammad Hassan Emamian, Sepideh Mahdavi, Mostafa Enayatrad, Sajad Sahab-Negah, Sairan Nili, Mohammad Fereidouni, Parvin Mangolian shahrbabaki, Alireza Ansari-Moghadam, Fariba Shahraki-Sanavi, Mansooreh Fateh, Hamidreza Khajeha, Zahra Emamian, Elahe Behmanesh, Hamid Sharifi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the cumulative incidence of adverse events of special interest (AESI) among Iranians who were vaccinated with COVID-19 vaccines, including Sinopharm, Sputnik V, AZD1222, and COVIran Barekat.MethodsUsing World Health Organization guidelines, this cohort event monitoring (CEM) study was conducted to assess the adverse events of COVID-19 vaccines, in seven cities in Iran. A total of 89783 participants (31690 Sinopharm, 20195 Sputnik V, 23780 AZD1222, and 14118 CoVIran Barekat) who were 18 or older were followed up for 17 weeks (25 weeks for AZD1222) after receiving the first dose of vaccine by weekly phone contacts or self-reporting in a web application. Furthermore, all hospitalized cases were investigated for causes of admission by classification committees.ResultsA total of 11 cases (cumulative incidence: 12.3 per 100,000) of AESIs were recorded. All of the AESI cases were observed among 2080 hospitalized cases. The AESIs included five cases of coagulation disorders (three after AZD1222 and two after Sinopharm vaccination), and three cases of generalized seizure (one after Sputnik V, and two cases after AZD1222). In addition, two cases of Guillain–Barre syndrome, and Sub-acute Thyroiditis were detected after receiving Sputnik V, and one individual developed pericarditis after receiving COVIran Barekat.ConclusionWe found around one AESI among every 8,000 vaccinated Iranian people. The incidence of AESIs after COVID-19 vaccines was relatively rare and the studied vaccines can be considered safe. AZD1222 had a higher incidence of AESIs. Coagulation disorders, and generalized seizure were the main and most common AESIs.

Introduction

From the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic to February 2, 2025, over 777.4 million confirmed cases and more than 7 million deaths have been reported worldwide. Still significant, the disease reported over 330,000 new cases and more than 4,500 deaths in the 28 days between 16 September to 13 October 2024 alone [1]. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, global efforts were made to control and prevent the disease, and in a short time, many vaccines were developed to combat the deadly disease. By March 30, 2023, out of 183 vaccines in the clinical phase, 11 vaccines were in the fourth phase of clinical trials and had safety approvals [2]. Most of these vaccines were initially licensed to be used in emergencies, and therefore studies are needed to check their safety status.

Many efforts were made in most of the countries to identify and report adverse events of COVID-19 vaccines. Based on the results of these studies, policies were made to limit the age group of some vaccines or roll out vaccinations to people under 18 years of age. Given that COVID-19 is still an important disease and vaccination plays an essential role in its prevention, it is necessary to examine all vaccines used in the pandemic and have the safest vaccines available to society in the future. Most of the previous studies have been on the identification of adverse events of COVID-19 vaccines in high-income and European-American countries. On the other hand, most studies done in low-income countries do not have enough published results [3]. In other words, more evidence of the implementation of these studies needs to be published.

Despite efforts to investigate post-vaccination adverse events related to COVID-19 vaccines in Iran, studies have often been conducted with small sample sizes, lacking proper follow-up, or without utilizing a standardized method to identify and report adverse events [4–10]. Additionally, most of the previous studies in Iran focused solely on reactogenicities after COVID-19 vaccination. The Cohort Event Monitoring (CEM) study is one of the well-structured and recommended methods by the WHO to investigate the adverse events of post-immunization and safety signal detection [11]. We examined the adverse events of the four COVID-19 vaccines in a large study in 7 cities in Iran [12], and so far, there have been reports on COVID-19 breakthrough infection [13] and vaccines’ reactogenicities [14] from this study. However, one of the most important questions in vaccine safety is the serious adverse events (SAE) and adverse events of special interest (AESI) following vaccination. The main objective of CEM studies is to identify SAE and AESI, and the sample size of the study is calculated based on these types of adverse events [11].

The present study aims to report the SAEs and AESIs, in people 18 years and older after receiving the BBIBP-CorV (Sinopharm), Sputnik V, AZD1222, or COVIran Barekat vaccines in Iran.

Methods

This CEM study evaluated the SAEs and AESIs following COVID-19 vaccination with BBIBP-CorV (Sinopharm), Sputnik V, AZD1222, or COVIran Barekat between April 2021 and July 2022. All methods, forms and questionnaires were based on the WHO protocol [11]. This study was conducted in seven cities of Iran: Shahroud, Rasht, Zahedan, Sanandaj, Birjand, Kerman, and Mashhad. The study protocol has been previously published elsewhere [12]. The study population comprised all eligible individuals who received one of the above COVID-19 vaccines. Enrollment started upon the signing of written informed consent by individuals who had received the first dose of COVID-19 vaccines. Exclusion criteria included prior vaccination with any COVID-19 vaccines before study enrollment and the inability to comply with study procedures. Participants retained the right to withdraw from the study at any time for any reason.

During enrollment, essential information including contact details, demographic characteristics, and medical history related to underlying conditions (such as diabetes, hypertension, immunodeficiency, cancer, chronic heart disease, and respiratory, renal, hepatic, neurological, and psychiatric disorders) was collected. In addition, other details regarding the administered vaccine, including the vaccine brand, vaccination date, and batch number, were meticulously recorded in the designated registration system.

The trained interviewers contacted the participants every week for three months (13 weeks) after each dose of COVIran Barekat, Sinopharm, and Sputnik V. For the AZD1222 vaccine, the follow-up period was 25 weeks after the first dose due to the longer interval between the two doses of the vaccine. Weekly reports were also generated through self-reporting in a mobile phone application on occasion. If these reports indicated a positive event, such as hospitalization, they were promptly followed up. To minimize the loss-to-follow-up rate, participants were contacted by phone up to twice a day. If they could not be reached, their next of kin was contacted [11]. A participant was considered lost to follow-up after two unsuccessful attempts to contact them by phone, followed by one unsuccessful attempt to contact their next of kin. The follow-up index was calculated by dividing the actual investigated follow-up period by the potential follow-up duration [13]. All hospitalized participants underwent thorough investigation by classification committees at each study site for 22 prespecified AESIs [12]. These committees comprised specialists in infectious disease, immunology, epidemiology, internal medicine, cardiology, clinical pharmacology, and other relevant disciplines. Their responsibility was to review medical records and attribute adverse events to the COVID-19 vaccines [12]. Any overnight hospitalization or reported death related to COVID-19 vaccination, as confirmed by classification committees, was recorded as an SAE. The confirmed cases of SAE and AESI were presented by age, gender, vaccine types and doses, the interval between diagnosis and vaccination, and outcomes.

Results

A total of 89783 participants (31690 Sinopharm, 20195 Sputnik V, 23780 AZD1222, and 14118 COVIran Barekat) were included and followed up. The response rate was very high and follow-up index was more than 98%. The mean age of the participants was 45.4 ± 16.9 years, with 47.6% being women. Recipients of the Sinopharm vaccine had the highest average age, and this group also exhibited a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and heart and respiratory diseases. In addition, 28.9% of participants had a self-reported history of COVID-19 infection, which was more prevalent among Sputnik vaccine recipients (Table 1). In total, 2,080 participants were hospitalized during the follow-up. Most of the hospitalized participants were admitted for COVID-19 (642 cases), accidents (118 cases), chronic diseases and malignancies (125 cases), or elective surgeries (397 cases). Among the other suspicious cases examined by the classification committees (798 cases), 11 cases (cumulative incidence: 12.3 per 100,000; 95% Confidence Intervals CI 6.8–21.9 per 100,000) of AESI were recorded. The mean age of women and men experiencing AESI was 66.7 ± 15.0 and 48.8 ± 18.7, respectively (P = 0.111). Figure 1 presents the incidence rates of AESIs and SAEs by vaccine brands. The AESI included five cases of coagulation disorders: three occurred after the AZD1222 vaccination, and two followed the Sinopharm vaccination. All coagulation disorders were thrombotic events. Additionally, there were three cases of generalized seizure (GS) after receiving Sputnik V, and AZD1222 (two cases) vaccines. Furthermore, Guillain–Barre syndrome (GBS), Pericarditis, and Sub-Acute Thyroiditis were detected in three individuals after receiving Sputnik V, COVIran Barekat, and Sputnik V, respectively. The time to onset varied for different AESI. The minimum and maximum time for generalized seizure events were 2 and 23 days after receiving the vaccines. Regarding coagulation disorders, the minimum and maximum time to events were 43 and 76 days after vaccination. There was one case of mortality, while the remaining 10 cases survived (Table 2).

The SAE typically occurred a few days after receiving the vaccines which can be considered as serious adverse events following immunization (AEFI). The common symptoms in SAE cases included nausea, vomiting, increased heart rate, and weakness. In total, 19 cases with SAE were recorded: six cases after receiving the Sinopharm vaccine, three cases after receiving Sputnik V, seven cases after receiving AZD1222, and three cases after receiving the COVIran Barekat vaccine. The main reasons for hospitalization of SAE cases were severe allergies, abdominal pain, chest pain, irregular heartbeat, high fever, restlessness, dizziness, and fainting. All 19 individuals with SAE were discharged from the hospital within a maximum of one week and were in good general condition. Considering that AESIs are also serious and require hospitalization, the incidence rate of SAEs (30 cases) was 33.4/100,000 in total participants. All cases of AESI and SAE were determined to be causally related to COVID-19 vaccination by the classification committees.

Discussion

This study, which actively followed the recipients of COVID-19 vaccines after each vaccine dose, found that the incidence of causally confirmed SAE and AESI following vaccination was very low. We found around one AESI among every 8,000 and one SAE among every 4,700 vaccinated Iranian individuals. The examined vaccines were deemed safe. Among the investigated AESI, coagulation disorders and generalized seizures were the most common. Notably, AZD1222 ranked first, while Sputnik V came in second place in terms of SAE and AESI as compared to the other two vaccines.

In general, the incidence of reported AESI was very low (12.3 per 100,000) in the current study, while other CEM studies reported higher rates (100–200/100,000) [15, 16]. However, Wong et al. reported a lower incidence (60–90/100,000) of AESI than previous study in a retrospective cohort study [17]. The discrepancy between our results and those of other studies can be attributed to factors such as variations in vaccine brands, follow-up duration, the number and definition of AESIs, and differences in sample sizes. Variations in definitions and follow-up durations for reporting AESIs have resulted in reporting rates as high as 13.8% following the administration of AZD1222 in north India [18]. In addition, different background rates of AESIs have been reported in a study with a follow-up of 197 million people from countries in Europe, Asia, North and South America, and Oceania [19]. Even the background rates of AESI have varied across different seasons of the year [20]. Therefore, different geographical locations and populations may exhibit varying rates of AESI.

By examining the 95% confidence intervals, it is evident that there is no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of AESI across vaccine types. However, when comparing the absolute number of AESI, it becomes clear that vaccines based on inactive viruses have fewer side effects than vector-based vaccines. This observation has been documented previously [21]. Apart from vaccine technology, the difference in the mean age of participants receiving the four types of vaccines may have influenced the incidence of AESI.

Recently, a multicounty cohort study analyzed data from over 99 million vaccinated individuals across eight countries [22]. The study investigated the association of 13 types of AESI with COVID-19 vaccines. Overall, the risk of AESI within 42 days after vaccination was comparable to the background risk. However, the researchers identified safety signals for GBS and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in more than 12 million recipients of the AZD1222 vaccine, relatively consistent with the findings of the present study [22]. Other studies have also reported an increased risk of GBS associated with COVID-19 vaccines that utilize the adenovirus vector [23–27]. In addition, a higher risk of thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) and other coagulation disorders after vaccination with AZD1222 have been reported in other reports [26, 28–32].

Generalized seizure was a common AESI in this study that occurred after the vaccines based on the adenovirus vector (Sputnik V and AZD1222). Similarly increased observed-to-expected risk of GS after receiving Sputnik V has been reported previously [22]. In the investigation of background rates of AESI, GS was also the most common [19]. The incidence of GS after receiving the AZD1222 vaccine was 8.4 in 100,000 people in this study. However, another study with a higher sample size reported GS after receiving AZD122 at 1,003 in the 100,000 population [26]. A higher sample size is needed for truly estimating GS after COVID-19 vaccination.

This study has several strengths, including adherence to a robust approved guideline, active surveillance, weekly participant follow-up, very high response rate and minimal loss to follow-up, and thorough investigation and classification of all hospitalized participants. However, in three of studied vaccines, the total sample size was smaller than the WHO-recommended 30,000 people for detecting events with a frequency of 1 per 10,000 individuals. This limitation may lead to an underestimation of the reported rates of AESI in the present study. As another limitation, we reported only serious adverse events that required overnight hospitalization during the three-month follow-up period. Therefore, other adverse events of lower severity or long-term issues, such as menstrual disturbances, cutaneous manifestations, or musculoskeletal discomfort, which have been reported in other studies [18, 33], were not included in this study. The study's limitations may also include not investigating booster doses or mixed vaccine regimens.

Conclusion

The incidence of SAE (1 in 4,700) and AESI (1 in 8,000) following vaccination with the studied COVID-19 vaccines is rare, and these vaccines can be considered safe, given their high benefits in preventing COVID-19 hospitalization and death. It should be noted that the number of participants was not similar between vaccine brands and the comparison of vaccines for the incidence of AESIs should be done with caution. The generalizability of the results may also be limited for populations with varying comorbidities, demographics, or healthcare infrastructures.
