Anti-inflammatory myeloid angiogenic cells (MIL4-MACs) attenuate angiotensin II‒induced heart failure in mice
Authors: Toshie Kanayasu-Toyoda, Takeshi Tanaka, Kenji Yoshihara, Goki Matsumura, Katsuhiko Hayashi, Hanayuki Okura, Akifumi Matsuyama, Eriko Uchida, Teruhide Yamaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
For cell therapy to treat heart failure, therapeutic cells should possess the abilities to promote vascularization, remodel tissues, and reduce inflammation. Previously we clarified that CD14+-derived myeloid angiogenic cells (MACs) possessed an angiogenic ability. In this paper, we induced strong high anti-inflammatory and tissue-remodeling properties in CD14+ cells by adding both M-CSF and interleukin-4 to the endothelial medium. This culture condition produced a higher number of adherent cells than MACs and exhibited greater angiogenic capability; we named these cells ‘MIL4-MACs’. Through analysis of cytokine production, gene expression, and membrane markers, MIL4-MACs were characterized as anti-inflammatory cells, with lower expression of HLA-Class Ⅱ and higher expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 compared to MACs. Mixed lymphocyte reaction analysis showed that T cells did not attack MIL4-MACs. Moreover, MIL4-MACs expressed active-matrix metalloproteinase-9 on their surface and had high collagen-degrading activity. Administration of MIL4-MACs improved angiotensin Ⅱ‒induced severe cardiac failure and attenuated cardiac fibrosis in mice. These data indicate that the anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and remodeling properties of MIL4-MACs may represent a novel candidate for cell therapy in heart failure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18702-7.

Introduction

Since Asahara et al1. proposed the existence of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) that promote angiogenesis in blood mononuclear cells (MNCs), EPCs have garnered great clinical interest for their potential in angiogenic cell therapy for ischemic disease treatment. For example, EPCs, isolated by various markers, have been evaluated for their ability to improve mouse models of hindlimb ischemia. Although CD14+ cells do not differentiate into endothelial cells2,3, many investigators have reported that CD14+ cells serve as a source of EPCs, playing a significant role in the induction of angiogenesis indirectly through cytokine and chemokine production. Conversely, monocytes differentiate into anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic M2 macrophages4 and are known to be polarized by cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-135. Medina et al. and our group previously reported that EPCs derived from CD14+ cells cultured in endothelial cell growth medium (EGM)-2 resembled M2 macrophages, which we named ‘myeloid angiogenic cells’ (MACs)6,7.

Although research on M2 macrophages has focused primarily on angiogenesis, they also play pivotal roles in immunoregulation, anti-inflammation, and tissue remodeling in the heart and other tissues. Shiraishi et al. reported that M2 macrophages improve dysfunction during myocardial infarction by producing IL-48. Moreover, Jung et al9. reported that exogenous treatment with IL-10 in mice after myocardial infarction improved cardiac function by stimulating differentiation into M2 macrophages. However, few studies on cardiac disease have examined the direct administration of M2 macrophages. These findings suggest that developing culture methods for M2 macrophages or MACs is important for cell therapy, as heart failure is a significant health problem with considerable morbidity and mortality10.

We have developed MACs into more advanced functional cells called MIL4-MACs by adding macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and IL-4 (MIL4) to the culture medium of MACs. CD14+ cells were differentiated into MIL4-MACs, resulting in a higher number of adherent cells compared to MACs, along with enhanced angiogenic, remodeling, and anti-inflammatory capabilities. A recent study has identified a mechanism by which macrophage receptor with collagenous structure (MARCO)-positive macrophages in the periportal vein of the liver protect against inflammatory stimuli from commensals11. In this paper, we report that MIL4-MACs also express MARCO at the mRNA level, in contrast to MACs. Moreover, MIL4-MACs attenuated angiotensin (Ang) Ⅱ‒induced decreases in fractional shortening (FS) and inhibited the production of cardiac fibrosis.

We previously reported that angiogenesis induced by either MACs or M2 macrophages was dependent on IL-8. To confirm whether MIL4-MACs produce angiogenic factors, we performed a tube-forming assay using conditioned medium (CM) from these cells on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in Matrigel. Figure S1B shows a representative photomicrograph of tube formation promoted by the CM from MIL4-MACs and M2 macrophages. Figure 1B presents the quantitative analysis of tubular length measured from the photomicrographs in Fig. S1B. The CM of both MIL4-MACs and M2 macrophages enhanced tube formation in HUVECs. The stimulating effect of the CM from MIL4-MACs on tube formation was inhibited not by a control IgG, but partially by an anti‒IL-8 antibody, suggesting that the stimulation of tube formation induced by MIL4-MACs may be partially dependent on IL-8.

Regarding anti-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine production, MIL4-MACs produced IL-10 at a significantly higher level than MACs. IL-8 production by MIL4-MACs was slightly lower than that by MACs. This result was confirmed by gene expression analysis, which showed that IL-8 was particularly abundant in MACs (Supplementary Table S1 online). IL-6 gene expression was lower in MACs, but there was no difference in IL-6 protein levels (Fig. 1C).

In vivo angiogenic ability of MIL4-MACs using SCID mice

To evaluate the local angiogenic ability of MIL4-MACs, they were infiltrated into a gelatin disc (Spongel™) coated with polymerized type Ⅰ collagen gel. Two weeks after transplantation into the dorsal region of SCID mice (Fig. 1Da), blood vessels were observed surrounding the gelatin disc (Fig. 1Db). After the disc was removed, vessels invading the gel were markedly co-stained with Tomato‒Lectin-FITC and DAPI (Fig. 1Ea and 1Eb). In contrast, the disc without cells did not recruit host vessels. Vascular areas on microphotographs were quantified using ImageJ software and are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1C. These data suggest that MIL4-MACs possess significant angiogenic ability. We then performed gene ontology (GO) analysis and selected angiogenesis-related terms for MIL4-MAC characterization. Genes in MIL4-MACs were found to be enriched in various processes of angiogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S2 online).

These results suggest that multiple immunosuppressive proteins are preferentially expressed in MIL4-MACs. While some discrepancies were noted between gene and protein expression, such as MMP-2 and IDO1 (Supplementary Table S1 online), these discrepancies may be explained by GO analysis, which indicated that MIL4-MACs may have the ability to localize proteins to the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. S2 online).

Effect of MIL4-MACs on mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR)

To clarify the anti-inflammatory abilities of MIL4-MACs, they were incubated with cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). A mixed lymphocyte reaction was performed using Calcein-AM‒labeled target cells. After incubation and fixation, the cell mixture was analyzed using flow cytometry. When cells were fixed immediately after mixing CTLs and Calcein-AM‒stained M1 macrophages (effector-to-target [E:T] ratio of 0.5:1.0) without incubation, a peak indicating 93.7% Calcein-AM‒positive cells was observed on the right side of the histogram (Supplementary Fig. S3a-4). When CTL cells were incubated alone, they were observed in the dot blot (Supplementary Fig. S3a-3). Cytotoxic T lymphocytes were not detected in the area indicating 100% on the histogram because they were detected at intensities below 5 × 102 (log fluorescence intensity), as shown in Supplementary Fig. S3a-6. After a 4 h incubation of the cellular mixture, the ratio of Calcein-AM‒positive M1 macrophages decreased significantly (Supplementary Fig. S3a-5), and the extent of reduction was quantified as a relative ratio (Fig. 2Ba). These results indicate that CTLs may have exerted cytotoxic effects on M1 macrophages.

In this MLR system, HUVECs were also targeted by CTLs, leading to a dose-dependent reduction in the ratio of Calcein-AM‒positive cells (Fig. 2Be). M2 macrophages were affected by CTLs at a low level, but the effect was statistically significant (Fig. 2Bb). In contrast, MACs and MIL4-MACs were scarcely attacked by CTLs, resulting in no significant decrease in Calcein-AM‒positive cells (Fig. 2Bc and Bd), indicating that these cells may be functionally anti-inflammatory by evading CTL attack.

Given the known roles MMP-9 and MMP-2 in cellular invasiveness6,16–18, we conducted a VEGF-induced Matrigel-coated transwell assay of MIL4-MACs using an MMP-2/9 inhibitor. While VEGF significantly enhanced the invasion of MIL4-MACs, the MMP-2/9 inhibitor significantly inhibited VEGF-induced invasion of MIL4-MACs (Fig. 3B), indicating that this invasion might depend on MMP-9 and MMP-2.

During tissue remodeling, triple helical collagen molecules are degraded by specific proteases such as MMP-9, MMP-2, and cathepsin K. We compared the collagen degradation capacity of MIL4-MACs and M2 macrophages on fibroblast-derived collagen matrices and detected denatured collagen strands using Collagen Hybridizing Peptide (F-CHP). As shown in Fig. 3Ca, the area of collagen degraded by MIL4-MACs was significantly larger than that degraded by M2 macrophages (Fig. 3Ca right panel), and the degradation by MIL4-MACs was also significantly inhibited by the MMP-2/9 inhibitor (Fig. 3Cb). These data indicate that MIL4-MACs may possess elevated remodeling ability dependent on MMP-2/9 proteins.

MIL4-MACs administration preserved heart function

To investigate the in vivo role of MIL4-MACs in cardiac remodeling, heart failure was induced in mice by infusing Ang Ⅱ using osmotic mini-pumps. MIL4-MACs were administered twice at a one-week interval. The first administration of MIL4-MACs coincided with the start of Ang Ⅱ infusion. Fractional shortening (FS%), a typical indicator of cardiac function, was measured. As shown in Fig. 4A, while FS in all sham mice gradually decreased in response to Ang Ⅱ infusion, concurrent administration of MIL4-MACs significantly ameliorated the FS decrease induced by Ang Ⅱ. In a separate experiment, one week after the start of Ang Ⅱ infusion, the first administration of MIL4-MACs was initiated. While FS in the sham group decreased over 4 weeks, FS improved significantly in the MIL4-MAC group at weeks 3 and 4 (Fig. 4Ba). Echocardiographic analysis at 1 and 4 weeks showed improved wall motion in the MIL4-MAC treatment group compared to the sham group at 4 weeks (Supplementary Fig. S4A).

MIL4-MAC administration to Ang Ⅱ‒treated mice improved fibrosis compared to the sham group, as shown by Sirius red staining (Fig. 4Bb) and Masson’s staining (Supplementary Fig. S4Ba online) in the corresponding region of the heart section. To quantify fibrosis, Sirius Red‒stained sections were analyzed using image analysis software such as NIH Image to determine the area of red-stained collagen fibers, which represents the extent of fibrosis. The 3.62 ± 0.071% of the red-stained area in mice infused with Ang II alone was significantly reduced to 1.99 ± 0.198% by MIL4-MAC administration. This indicates that MIL4-MACs may promote fibrotic tissue remodeling.

Continuous infusion with Ang Ⅱ for 4 weeks caused ST elevation in some mice, indicating the possibility that cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling were induced at 5 weeks (Supplementary Fig. S5A online). After 4 weeks of Ang II infusion, MIL4-MACs were administered during weeks 5 and 6. Measurements were calculated as relative ratios with the 5-week FS fixed at 1 to eliminate individual differences among mice. All mice receiving MIL4-MAC treatment showed attenuation of FS between weeks 6 and 8. However, no significant difference was found, as one sham-1 mouse recovered spontaneously without cell administration. These results indicate that MIL4-MACs may be effective not only as a preventive treatment but also as a therapeutic option, even in late-stage heart failure.

Discussion

It has been reported that cord blood has low immunogenicity. Moreover, the MIL4-MACs differentiated from CD14+ cells from cord blood are reproducible, as shown by FACS. Therefore, allo-cell therapy using MIL4-MACs derived from CD14+ cord blood cells is considered possible in the near future and may also be less burdensome for the patient than autologous cell therapy using adult peripheral blood, which involves blood sampling.

We demonstrate M2 macrophage‒like cells termed MIL4-MACs that were originally developed to possess proangiogenic, remodeling, and anti-inflammatory properties. A consensus statement on nomenclature is urgently needed in the field of EPCs19. In this study, CD14+ cells isolated from MNCs of cord blood were differentiated and polarized into MIL4-MACs by incubation with M-CSF and IL-4 in 5% FBS EGM-2, containing VEGF, FGF2, EGF, and IGF-1 LR3. M-CSF is a hematopoietic growth factor involved in the proliferation, differentiation, and survival of monocytes, macrophages, and bone marrow progenitor cells20. With the addition of M-CSF and IL-4, the number of MIL4-MACs exceeded that of MACs (Fig. 1A). Beyond increased cell yield, MIL4-MACs exhibited enhanced anti-inflammatory properties, characterized by higher production of IL-10 compared to MACs (Fig. 1C). Both IDO121,22 and ARG123,24 which contribute to anti-inflammatory activity, were expressed at high levels in both MIL4-MACs and MACs, as demonstrated by flow cytometry (Fig. 2A). Conversely, MIL4-MACs showed higher protein expression of PD-L1, PD-L2, and HLA-Class Ⅰ (Fig. 2A) alongside lower expression of HLA-Class Ⅱ compared to MACs (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, MIL4-MACs exhibited similarities to MARCO+ macrophages, as both produced high levels of IL-10, IL-1RN, and TGF-β111 (Supplementary Table S1). MIL4-MACs displayed not only anti-inflammatory but also immunosuppressive characteristics, including resistance to CTL attack (Fig. 2B) and expression of immune checkpoint molecules. Notably, MIL4-MACs share several defining features with MARCO+ macrophages. Thus, we expect MIL4-MACs to exert anti-inflammatory effects similar to those of MARCO+ macrophages.

We previously reported that the angiogenic ability of both MACs and M2 macrophages was dependent on IL-86. However, the increased tube-forming activity induced by the CM from MIL4-MACs could not be entirely inhibited by anti‒IL-8 antibody (Fig. 1B), and IL-8 production by MIL4-MACs was lower than that by MACs at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table S1, respectively). This suggests that MIL4-MACs may release additional pro-angiogenic factors beyond IL-8 and may also be classified as anti-inflammatory. GO analysis supported the involvement of multiple angiogenesis-related processes (Supplementary Fig. S2B).

Matrix metalloproteinases play a pivotal role in tissue remodeling under both physiological and pathological conditions across a wide range of tissues25. MMP-2 and MMP-9 have been implicated in vascular remodeling26–28. In liver regeneration, MMP-9 produced by transplanted bone marrow cells has been shown to reduce liver fibrosis29, and in keloid therapy, the recruitment of M2 macrophages and MMP-9 is essential for tissue repair30. Direct administration of M2 macrophages has been reported in cases of aortic aneurysm31, glomerulonephritis32, and stroke33, all of which have resulted in clinical improvement. MIL4-MACs displayed substantially greater surface expression of both MMP-2 and MMP-9 compared to MACs and exhibited superior collagen degradation activity relative to M2 macrophages (Fig. 3C), suggesting that MIL4-MACs are exceptional remodeling agents across multiple tissue types.

The markers of cardiac functions such as either FS or progression of fibrosis induced by long-term administration of Ang-II have been recovered by the administration of MIL4-MACs. On the other hand, other markers such as blood brain natriureic peptide (BNP), a biomarker of heart failure34,35, could not be determined. Moreover, the contribution for the promotion of angiogenesis induced by factors produced by MIL4-MACs and biodistribution of MIL4-MACs after administration are still unresolved. We believe that additional studies are needed to determine whether these factors contribute to the improvement of myocardial function as the mode-of-action of MIL4-MACs, and we would like to explore the contributions of these factors in the near future.

Angiotensin Ⅱ is known to trigger inflammation and fibrosis in the heart within one week36. Therefore, MIL4-MACs are considered to play a more effective role in mitigating heart failure through their combined anti-inflammatory and matrix-degrading capacities (Fig. 3C). In this study, MIL4-MACs demonstrated both prophylactic and therapeutic efficiency, even in late stages of heart failure. Moreover, their intravenous administration offers a minimally invasive option for patients with heart failure.

Conclusion

We successfully developed pro-angiogenic, remodeling, and anti-inflammatory cells termed MIL4-MACs in large quantities. The intravenous administration of MIL4-MACs significantly mitigated Ang Ⅱ‒induced heart failure. These results indicate that MIL4-MACs may be a promising candidate for cellular therapeutics in heart failure treatment in the near future.

Materials and methods

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Preparation of MNCs

Human cord blood was kindly supplied by the Japanese Red Cross Kanto-Koshinetsu Cord Blood Bank (Tokyo, Japan), which collects donations from volunteers with informed consent. Adult whole blood was prepared from donors at The Japanese Red Cross Society (Tokyo, Japan). The ethical review boards of the National Institute of Health Sciences (NIHS, No. 315) and Nihon Pharmaceutical University (NPU, No. 5–19) approved the use of these blood samples for this study. Mononuclear cells were collected as previously reported37.

Cell culture

CD14+ cells were isolated from MNCs of cord blood using a magnetic bead‒conjugated anti-CD14 antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). MACs were obtained by culturing 106/ml cells in 5% FBS EGM-2 (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) for 7 days on a fibronectin-coated dish. M1 macrophages were obtained by polarization using 20 ng/ml of IFN-γ (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) for 24 h after culturing 106 cells/mL for 6 days in 5% FBS RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 25 ng/ml of granulocyte macrophage‒colony stimulating factor. M2 macrophages were obtained by polarization using 20 ng/ml of IL-4 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 24 h following a 6-day culture in 5% FBS RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 25 ng/ml of M-CSF (Pepro Tech, Cranbury, NJ, USA). MIL4-MACs were cultured in 5% FBS EGM-2 in the presence of M-CSF and polarized with IL-4 using a polystyrene-coated dish.

HUVECs (PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany) maintained in 2% FBS EGM-2, were used at passages 1‒5.

Tube-forming assay of HUVECs

CM was collected from the supernatant of 3 × 105 cells/ml cultured with 2% FBS‒endothelial cell basic medium (EBM-2, Lonza) for 24 h. The tube formation assay was performed in 48-multiwell plates. After gelation of the Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) at 37ºC, 5 × 104 HUVECs in 0.5 ml of one-fifth diluted CM in 2% FBS‒EBM-2 medium, with or without anti‒IL-8 antibody or control IgG (10 μg/ml, R&D Systems), were applied and incubated for 24 h. Tube lengths were measured using AxioVision LE software (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Measurement of cytokines by ELISA

Supernatants were collected from cultured cells (3 × 105 cells/ml) after 48 h in 2% FBS‒EBM-2. Interleukin-8, IL-10, and IL-6 were measured by ELISA (eBioscience, Vienna, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Animal maintenance and experimental treatment

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tokyo Women’s Medical University (TWMU, No. AE24). Eight-week-old male SCID mice (C.B-17/Icr-scid/scidJcl, weighing 23‒26 g) were provided by Clea Japan (Tokyo, Japan). In this study, we used only male SCID mice to minimize hormonal variability and because this Ang II‒induced heart failure model has been well characterized in males. Mice were maintained at constant temperature and humidity under a 12-h light‒dark cycle, with free access to standard mouse chow and water. To avoid causing pain or distress, mice were euthanized with 5% isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation according to institutional guidelines for animal experimentation.

Transplantation of gelatin discs containing MIL4-MACs

All animal interventions were conducted under anesthesia using isoflurane. To create gelatin discs containing MIL4-MACs, Spongel (LTL Pharma, Tokyo) was cut to 8 mm diameter and 5 mm thickness, then placed in 48-well plates. After 150 µl of cell suspension (3 × 105 cells/gelatin disc) was absorbed into Spongel, 200 µl of type I collagen (5 mg/ml, BD Biosciences) was added around the Spongel. After polymerization of the collagen mixture at 37 °C, the gelatin disc was surgically inserted subcutaneously. Two to three weeks later, the mice were sacrificed and the gelatin disc was removed and photographed. The disc was immediately washed and incubated with Tomato‒Lectin‒FITC (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) solution containing DAPI. Blood vessels were then observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Ang II‒induced heart failure and administration of MIL4-MACs

Mice were infused for 2‒4 weeks with Ang Ⅱ (1,012 ng/kg/min) using osmotic mini-pumps (Alzet Model 1004, Durect, Cupertino, CA, USA) implanted subcutaneously. MIL4-MACs (1 × 105 cells/mouse) were administered twice a week at the indicated time points via the tail vein. Electrocardiograms (ECG; Polygraph Systems, Clipen Japan Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were used to determine QTc. Echocardiography (Nemio SSA-550A, Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan) was used to determine the left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVDD) as well as the end-systolic dimension (LVDS). Fractional shortening was defined as (LVDD ‒ LVDS)/LVDD × 100. Blood velocity was also measured.

Histological analysis

Cardiac tissue samples were fixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-μm-thick sections. The sections were stained with Sirius red (Scytek Lab., Logan, UT, USA), Masson trichrome, and hematoxylin & eosin (HE) to determine the area of interstitial fibrosis using standard procedures.

Flow cytometric analysis of various cell surface markers

Cells were incubated with monoclonal antibodies: HLA-A, B, C/class Ⅰ (clone: EMR8-5, isotype: mouse IgG1-κ; Hokudo, Sapporo, Japan), anti-HLA-DR/class Ⅱ (clone: TAL 1B5, isotype: mouse IgG1-κ; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-MMP-9 (clone: 56-2A4, isotype: mouse IgG1-κ; Kyowa Pharma Chemical, Toyama, Japan), anti-active MMP-9 (clone: 4A3, isotype: mouse IgG1-κ; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), anti-IDO1 (clone: #47,150,000; Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) or anti-PD-L2 (clone: #176,611, isotype; mouse IgG2B; R&D Systems), and then labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488)‒conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody. Cells were incubated with polyclonal antibodies: anti‒PD-L1 (Abcam), anti-MMP-2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) or anti-ARG1 (Gene Tex, Irvine, CA, USA), and then labeled with allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody. Cells were also stained with phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-CD163 (clone: GH1/61, isotype: mouse IgG1-κ; BD Biosciences) and PE-conjugated anti-CD206 monoclonal antibodies (clone: 19.2, isotype: mouse IgG1-κ; BD Biosciences). Cells were permeabilized before incubation with anti-IDO1 antibody and anti-ARG1 antibody. Stained cells were subjected to flow cytometry (SH800, Sony, Tokyo, Japan), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Becton, Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) ver. 10.

Mixed lymphocyte reaction

T cells were isolated from MNCs of human adult peripheral blood using the Pan T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified T cells were cultured in 5% FBS RPMI-1640 supplemented with 20 ng/ml IL-2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 7 days. Cells were collected and frozen until use as CTLs/effector cells.

Target cells were labeled with 1 μg/ml Calcein-AM in PBS at 37 °C for 30 min. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes at either 5 × 104 cells/50 μl (E:T ratio = 0.5:1) or 1 × 105 cells/50 μl (E:T ratio = 1:1) in Advanced RPMI-1640 and target cells (1 × 105 cells/50 μl) in Advanced RPMI-1640 were mixed in non-adherent round-bottom 96-well plates (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and then incubated for 4 h. After collecting and washing, cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in PBS (-). As a control, the same number of mixtures of target cells and CTLs were immediately fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde in PBS (-) without incubation. Fixed cells were subjected to flow cytometry.

Trans-Matrigel chemotaxis assay

A Matrigel Invasion Chamber (BD Biosciences) was used. Cells (1.5 × 105/well) in 2% FBS-EBM2 were added to the inserts with an 8 μm pore size and allowed to invade for 23 h against 10 ng/ml VEGF (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) in the lower wells. The cells that invaded the lower surface of the membrane were counted. The MMP-2/9 inhibitor V (Calbiochem, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used at a concentration of 500 nM.

Remodeling ability of MIL4-MACs and M2 macrophages

To detect denatured collagen strands degraded by MIL4-MACs and M2 macrophages during remodeling, collagen molecules were produced by fibroblast cultivation. Cells were then removed by osmotic lysis, and MIL4-MACs or M2 macrophages were cultured for 4 h on the remaining collagen produced by fibroblasts in the presence of MMP-2/9 inhibitor V. Denatured collagen strands were detected by F-CHP (3Helix, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and examined using a Zeiss LSM 780 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The fluorescence images (Fig. 3Ca) were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health). Average fluorescence intensities of five points (Fig. 3Cb), chosen automatically, were also measured with Infinite 200 Pro (Tecan, Zurich, Switzerland).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test. The dose‒response relationships of HUVECs in the MLR assay and collagen degradation assay were analyzed by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test. For heart failure recovery, a two-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used, and values of p < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.
