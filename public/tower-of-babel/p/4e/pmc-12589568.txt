Effect of hydrolyzable tannin and organic acids on productive performance, ileal microbiota and histomorphology, and immunocompetence indices of broiler chicken
Authors: Hamed Daneshmand Khorasgani, Amir Hossein Mahdavi, Mohammad Sedghi, Mehdi Shahsavan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to assess the potential interactions of different levels of hydrolyzable tannin (HT) and organic acids (OA) on the selected ileal microbiota and histology, immunological responses, and growth performance of broiler chickens. A total of 1092 one-day-old female Ross 308 broiler chickens were randomly assigned to six experimental treatments with seven replicates of 26 chicks each. Treatments consisted of a 2 × 3 factorial arrangement, consisting of 0 and 1 g HT/kg of diet (0HT, and 1HT) and 0, 1, and 2 g OA/kg of diet (0OA, 1OA, and 2OA). Dietary administration of 1HT + 2OA led to a significant enhancement (P < 0.01) in average daily weight gain (ADWG) during the finisher phase (25–42 days), as well as a notable reduction (P < 0.01) in feed conversion ratio (FCR) during both the finisher phase and the overall trial period. This treatment resulted in a significant reduction (P < 0.01) in the relative weight of abdominal fat, while concurrently enhancing (P < 0.01) the partial weight of the bursa of Fabricius, the thickness of ileal lamina propria lymphoid follicles (LLF), the ratio of ileal villi length to crypt depth, and the apparent villus surface area. The main effects of feeding either 1HT or 2OA resulted in significantly lower pH levels in the ileum and gizzard (P < 0.01), as well as the highest relative weights of the spleen, bursa of Fabricius, and thymus (P < 0.05). Additionally, the dietary inclusion of these concentrations of HT or OA led to a reduction (P < 0.05) in the ileal population of E. coli, a decrease in the relative weights of the pancreas, liver, and abdominal fat, and an enhancement (P < 0.05) in selected hematological parameters, humoral immunity, the thickness of LLF, and FCR during the starter phase (1–10 days). The main effect of 2OA resulted in significantly elevated numbers of either Lactobacillus or LLF in the ileum (P < 0.05). In general, feeding 1HT + 2OA, without an adverse effect on the microbial population, manifested positive effects on the growth performance of broiler chickens via refinement of the ileal histology, the relative weight of bursa of Fabricius, and the ileal absorptive surface area.Keywords: Broiler chicken, Organic acid, Hydrolyzable tannin, Immune response, Ileal microbiota, HistomorphologySubject terms: Animal physiology, Diseases

Introduction

Although the antibiotic growth promoters potentially influence either the mass production of poultry meat or the healthy situation of birds, it is proven that antimicrobial resistance stems from remaining antibiotics in the poultry product, and, this in turn, brings about a health issue for humans who are consuming the final product of poultry fed with antibiotics1. To overcome this difficulty, the use of antibiotics is being prohibited, and numerous countries are investing in research to identify viable alternatives. These alternatives are sought not only for their lack of environmental hazards but also for their potential to enhance avian health and performance indices while retaining antimicrobial properties2.

Acidifiers, derived from organic acids (OA) and their salts, have emerged as a focal point of considerable interest in current research3. Disparate sorts of OA act through different mechanisms4, which depend on their pKa value5. Pearlin et al.5 briefly explained that the utilization of either acidifiers or OA is cost-effective because of improvements in digestion, absorption, immunity, beneficial bacterial population, intestinal health, and growth performance of poultry, and all of this together brings about the reduction in the expenditures of diet and marketing prolongation6. Of course, worries including neutralization stemming from the intestinal bicarbonate secretion, palatability due to a bitter taste, and action location of either acidifiers or OA are critical considerations that should be taken into account during the formulation of these compounds5.

Tannins, which are plant-derived polyphenols categorized into two types-condensed and hydrolyzable-are another compound that can influence the health and performance parameters of birds7. Hydrolyzable tannins (HT) containing a glucose structure as an esterified central polyol core with nine to ten molecules of gallic acid (GA); have a potential capacity for hydrolysis under the effects of basic or acidic pH, heat, and tannase8. One type of HT is tannic acid (TA), possessing properties such as an immune stimulator9. The higher production of GA molecules stems from the hydrolysis of TA under a temperature between 50 °C and 90 °C means a reduced antimicrobial activity of TA, especially against Salmonella species10. Solubility, interactions with either micro or macronutrients, molecular shape, and particle size of tannins play roles to become available for their easier penetration via the gut11. However, the anti-nutritional effects of high levels of tannin, such as necrosis of gut mucosa, damage of intestinal villus12, binder of protein, and cutback of animal growth performance as its cytotoxic symptoms cannot be neglected13.

According to the prior findings, both tannins and OA could potentially change selected hematological indices of animals14, and HT not only has reduced the gut passage rate2,15, but also might provide more time for exerting the positive effects of OA on local or systemic mechanisms; therefore, there is a possibility that if they are utilized concurrently, to some extent the physiological responses stemming from the administration of OA will be enhanced. Based on this, the current study was undertaken to evaluate the synergistic mechanism of HT and acidifiers on growth performance, microbial population, and selected physiological parameters of broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

This experiment was based on the comprehensive animal welfare guide (FASS, 2020)16. All methods and stages of animal care were performed in accordance with FASS, 2020 and ARRIVE guidelines 2.0., and were approved by the Animal Policy and Welfare Committee of Isfahan University of Technology.

Preparation of organic acids, hydrolyzable tannins, and chemical composition of diets

In the current trial, organic acids (OA: Nufocid plus, Nufoer, Ávila, Spain) were utilized. Its compounds include propionic acid, formic acid, sodium butyrate, ammonium propionate, calcium formate, calcium lactate, ammonium formate, and calcium propionate. The hydrolyzable tannin (HT: Silva feed Nutri P, Silvateam, Italy) used contained 37 g/kg Gallic acid (GA = 6.412%), 450 g/kg gallotannins (77.989%), and 90 g/kg ellagitannins (15.597%) for a total of 577 g/kg of HT.

Before the commencement of the formulation of basal diets, samples of corn and soybean were analyzed to calculate the percentages of dry matter, crude protein, crude fiber, ether extract, and ash. Both samples were analyzed with two repetitions according to AOAC17.

Broiler chickens, diets, vaccination, and experimental procedures

A total of 1092 one-day-old female broiler chicks (Ross 308; Zarbal Broiler Production Integration, Mazandaran, Iran) were randomly allotted to six experimental treatments with seven replicates of 26 chicks each. The broiler chickens were randomly housed on 42 clean floor pens with dimensions of 2 × 1.50 m2 per pen. The trial treatments included a 2 × 3 factorial arrangement, consisting of the two levels of HT (0, and 1 g/kg) and three levels of OA (0, 1, and 2 g/kg). Table 1 shows the trial diets formulated to supply all of the nutritional considerations of broiler chicks during the trial periods at base of guidelines reported by Aviagen18. All broilers benefited from free accessibility to either mash feed or water over the experimental periods. The hydrolyzable tannin, organic acids, or other additives at the intended level were mixed with 3 kg of the basal diet weekly. Then the mixture was added to and thoroughly mixed with the required amount of the basal diet to achieve the intended concentrations. The temperature of the rearing hall was 33 ℃ in the first two days and then gradually decreased to 22 ℃ at the age of 39 days. The bird’s light program was considered according to the protocols available in scientific sources; so the light regime was 23 L: 1D for the first three days; then it was 20 L: 4D until the end of the experiment. Broiler chickens were fed ad libitum with the standard diets (Table 1) during the starter (1–10 days), grower (11–24 days), and finisher periods (25–42 days). Furthermore, according to the veterinarian’s recommendation, the vaccination program included the Bronchitis-Newcastle vaccine on the 1st day (spray form), Newcastle on the 8th and 18th days (administered in drinking water), and Gumboro administered in drinking water on the 23rd day of age.

Performance and the relative weights of organs

At the end of each phase (10th, 24th, and 42nd days of age) of post-hatching, birds and feed that remained in each pen were weighed to evaluate average daily weight gain (ADWG), and also feed intake (ADFI). In this case, mortality was counted and weighed daily to calculate the feed conversion ratio (FCR).

Additionally, three birds per pen were randomly opted and humanely euthanized by CO2 for calculation of the partial weights of selected internal organs encompassing spleen, bursa of Fabricius, heart, pancreas, liver, intestine (duodenum + jejunum + ileum + ceca), abdominal fat, and gizzard according to the live body weight (BW) of birds with use of a sensitive digital scale with 0.001 g accuracy (UW620H, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) in d 42nd of age.

Immune system

Anti-Sheep red blood cell antibody assay

For the preparation of the sheep red blood cells (SRBC) suspension, whole blood was collected from a clinically healthy ram maintained at the university farm. Approximately 30 ml of blood was drawn from the jugular vein into a tube containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as an anticoagulant. The red blood cells were then washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After the final wash, the erythrocyte concentration was adjusted to 10% (vol/vol) in PBS. At 23 and 30 days of age, three birds per replicate were injected with 0.5 mL of 10% SRBC suspension intramuscularly (breast muscle). A week after injection each time, the latter birds bled from their jugular vein to separate their serum for measurement of antibody titer through the hemagglutination method19. To do this, tubes containing the serum samples were heat inactivated through incubation for half an hour in the water bath at a temperature of 56 °C and then evaluated for total, 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME)-resistant (MER, presumably IgG), and 2-ME-sensitive (MES, presumably IgM) anti-SRBC titers, using microhemagglutination assay in 96-well microplates (Nunc MaxiSorp C bottom well modules and frames, 445101, Bethyl Laboratories Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA)20. In summary, 50 µL of serum was added to 50 µL of PBS in the first column of a 96-well v-shaped bottom plate, and the solution was incubated for half an hour at 37 °C. A serial dilution was then made (1:2), and 50 µL of 1% SRBC suspension was added to each well. Total antibody titers were then read after half an hour of incubation at 37 °C. The well quickly preceding a well with a distinct SRBC button was taken into account as the endpoint titer for agglutination. For the MES (IgM) response, 50 µL of 0.01 M mercaptoethanol in PBS was utilized rather than PBS alone, followed by the above procedure. The difference between the total and the IgG titers was taken into account to be equal to the IgM antibody titer21,22. Antibody titers were transformed to reciprocal log2 units for statistical analysis.

Differential leukocyte count

Three broilers via their brachial vein were bled from each pen to prepare smears from the collected blood samples on the 42nd day of the trial. Thus, the provided duplicated smears were stained with Wright-Giemsa method for differential counting of lymphocyte, basophil, monocyte, heterophil, and eosinophil among 100 white blood cells under a light microscope (Olympus CX31, Tokyo, Japan) with a magnification of × 1000. Lastly, heterophil to lymphocyte proportion was calculated23,24.

Intestinal histomorphology

Three broilers were randomly chosen from each replicate to be euthanized on the final day of the trial. Subsequently, 1 cm of a segment after the ileal Meckel’s diverticulum was cut, and then it was washed to be immersed in 10% formalin buffer. After every 48 h, the formalin buffer was swapped with the new one three times to find a suitable time for cutting either longitudinal or transverse segments with a thickness of 5 μm by microtome (RM2245, Leica Co., Wetzlar, Germany), staining with hematoxylin-eosin, and lastly assessment of the fixed (three slides per replicate) samples under a light microscope (Olympus CX31, Tokyo, Japan). Then, the numbers of lamina propria lymphoid follicles (LLF), Goblet cells, length of villi (µm), depth of crypt (µm) from 1 until 4 (mild, moderate, strict, and very strict, alternatively), the villi length/crypt depth proportion, LLF thickness (µm), villi width (µm), and apparent villus surface area index were estimated25.

Measurement of ileal and gizzard pHs

Immediately following euthanasia on day 42, the gizzard and ileum were excised intact from three birds per pen. A digital pH meter (HI 99163; HANNA Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, Vietnam) was inserted directly into the digesta within the lumen of both the proximal gizzard and the ileum of the same bird, ensuring that the pH electrode did not come into direct contact with the intestinal wall. The pH was measured and recorded in duplicate for each site. The mean value of the two readings for each location in the gastrointestinal tract was subsequently calculated and documented26.

Ileal microbial population

Three birds per pen were euthanized on day 42nd of the experiment to immediately gather 1 g of their ileal content (1 cm after Meckel’s diverticulum) per bird and place it into 50 mL plastic Falcon tubes. Then, a shaker device (VELP Scientifica ZX4, Usmate Velate MB, Italy) mixed 9 ml of sterile PBS with 1 g of each of the samples to be provided a solution with a dilution of 0.1 ml. In the next step, 10 µL of the newest produced solution serially five times was diluted into 90 µL of sterile PBS to be prepared as a last solution containing 1 × 10− 5 ml dose of digesta. Consequently, the solution with dilutions of 10− 4 and 10− 5 was cultivated on De Man-Rogosa-Sharp (M.R.S) agar (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to count Lactobacillus after incubation at 37 °C for 48 h in an anaerobic chamber; while both 10− 2 and 10− 3 diluted solutions were aerobically cultivated on plate count agar (PCA) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to count total aerobic bacteria for 24 h at 37 °C. Also, the solution with dilutions of 10− 1 and 10− 2 was cultivated on either Eosin-Methylene-Blue (EMB) agar or Salmonella Shigella agar (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to account for both post-incubated E. coli and Salmonella populations at 37 °C for 48 h in an anaerobic chamber, consecutively26,27. Either analysis or transformation of data to base-10 logarithm of CFU/g of ileal digesta was conducted after the enumeration of all duplicated plates of bacteria.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using a completely randomized design with a 2 × 3 factorial arrangement. The SAS28 procedure (SAS/STAT 9.4) was employed to assess interactions and main effects across different levels of HT and OA. To ensure data normality, all traits were initially evaluated using a univariate procedure, and the Levene’s test was conducted to assess the homogeneity of variance. Traits that followed a normal distribution (such as performance traits, the relative weights of organs, gastrointestinal pH, villus length (VL), crypt depth (CD), villi width (VW), apparent villus surface area index (AVSA), and LLF thickness) were subsequently analyzed using a mixed procedure. Additionally, PROC GLIMMIX in SAS28/STAT 9.4 was used to estimate the effects of HT and OA on the hematology parameters, humoral immunity, ileal microbial population, and goblet cell and LLF numbers, assuming a Poisson distribution with a log link function. A probability value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while P-values between 0.05 and 0.10 indicated a trend. The model was defined as follows:

Yijk = µ + Ai + Bj + (ABij) + eijk.

Where Yijk is the observed value for a special character, µ is the average of a defined character, Ai is the effect of the ith of OA, Bj is the effect of the jth of HT, and eijk is the random error related to ijth record.

The broilers nourished with 1 g HT/kg of diet remarkably benefited from the lowest ADFI and the smallest FCR in the starter phase (P < 0.05). HT easily might be hydrolyzed into glucose and GA, and it is well reported that either ellagitannins or gallotannins belong to the subdivision of HT29. Das et al.30 collected information regarding these subjects that GA, ellagic acid (EA), and pyrogallol group (1, 2, 3-trihydroxybenzene) are the last products as a result of hydrolysis of HT under acidic, basic (use of alkali), and enzymatic conditions. In agreement with either the main effect of HT or the interactive effects of HT + OA in our study, feeding EA at a level of 200 mg/kg of diet noticeably could cause a better ADWG, and FCR without a significant effect on the ADFI of yellow-feathered broilers from day 1 to 56 of age31. Also, disparate levels of dietary GA dose-dependently can refine ADWG, and ADFI over the grower phase (9-21d) of broilers32. In our study HT potentially might lost its capabilities to bind with salivary proteins and, this in turn, did not hurt ADFI as Choi et al.33 reported that 5000 mg TA/kg of the diet with its bitter taste or astringent feature can bind to salivary proteins to form a complex, which might reduce ADFI, ADWG of broilers from an early age and, this in turn, might ceaselessly influence the growth performance of birds at next phases of the starter phase, because firstly high dosage of TA can decrease digestion, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal barrier integration and secondly, the broilers from an early age are more sensitive to sustain the overconsumption of TA34. In another study, ADWG and ADFI of broilers treated with 5 (g/kg of diet) TA and GA were refined, alternatively35. The distinguishments among studies may pertain to the disparate factors encompassing tannin amounts, tannin sources, animal species, environmental effects, diets, experimental periods, and potential dependency among these parameters7.

Although dietary administration of 2 OA (g/kg of diet) was associated with a decrease in ADFI of both starter and grower periods (P < 0.05), feeding this level ultimately resulted in the most favorable FCR during the starter phase (P < 0.05). Overall, Pearlin et al.5 explained that the acidifiers contain the carboxyl group, -COOH of the OA, encompassing the amino, and fatty acids. OAs have differences from each other in having mono-carboxylic acids (butyric, acetic, propionic, and formic acids), carboxylic acids containing the hydroxyl group (tartaric, citric, malic, and lactic acids), and short-chain carboxylic acids with double bonds (sorbic and fumaric acids)36. A potential reason for the reduction of ADFI of broilers in our study has been supported by Le Gall et al.37 who reported that the sodium butyrate with its unpleasant taste and odor can adversely affect feed intake, and conveniently can absorb the air moisture, bringing about negative effects on either feed storage or diet palatability. In contrast with the present study, although feeding OA or Bacillus subtilis alone and their blends could not affect FCR and body weight of broiler chickens infected with Salmonella, they could positively influence blood biochemistry and antioxidant situation of broilers challenged with Salmonella38. In agreement with our findings, Islam et al.39 reported that the broilers treated with 200 mg OA/kg of diet (made up of 4.2% ammonium propionate, 17.5% ammonium formate, 12.8% propionic acid, and 20.7% formic acid), benefited from a heavier weekly body weight gain, and a smaller FCR than those groups nourished with control negative (just basal diet), and control positive (the basal diet + 50 mg Enramycin/kg of feed) in the whole of experiment (1–35 d), starter (1–21 d), and finisher phases (22–35 d). Also, it is important to note that the chemical structure (acid, coated or uncoated, and salt), molecular weight, pKa value, consumption level and minimal inhibitory dose of acid, kind of microorganism, animal species, dietary compounds, action site, and buffering feature of diet are several factors affecting the efficacy of acidifier5.

The relative weights of the pancreas, and liver (P < 0.05) of broilers were significantly decreased when the levels of OA in diet were increased; while the simultaneous enhancement in the dietary levels of OA noticeably enhanced the partial weight of their intestine in d 42nd (P < 0.05). Similarly, Ibrahim et al.40 reported that male mulard ducks have been treated with (fermented feed + 0.5% OA), (fermented feed + 1% OA), and (fermented feed + 2% OA). This OA mixture was made up of 150 g formic acid/kg of diet, 30 g propionic acid/kg of diet, and 30 g calcium propionate/kg of diet. The addition of higher levels of these OA to the fermented diet led to a linear enhancement in the partial weight of the liver, either linear or quadratic enhancement in the relative weights of the intestine, gizzard, and heart of mulard ducks40.

In addition, the partial weights of heart (P = 0.071), liver (P < 0.05), gizzard (P = 0.054), pancreas (P = 0.061) of broiler chickens fed with 1 g HT/kg of diet were slighter than the relative weights of the same selected internal organs in the groups fed diets without HT. However, the relative weight of the intestine of broilers fed with 1 g HT/kg of diet was heavier than that of the groups fed diets without HT (P < 0.05). The previous study reported that the nutritional effects on gut development can be manifested by either the length or weight of the intestine41. In agreement with our study, Barszcz et al.42 reported that different levels of TA (0.25% to 2% of diet) enhanced the cecal weight of rats on the 21st d of the experiment. In agreement with the main effect of HT in our study, the previous studies43,44 have reported that milk thistle contains GA, and 232.12 mg tannin/g dry weight of milk thistle byproduct could not noticeably affect the partial weights of pancreas and gizzard of broiler chickens. Ileal histology, Gastrointestinal pH, and ileal microbial population

Ileal histology

Xu et al.57 described that the gut is a vital immune organ that permits the needed nutrients to arrive at the gut lumen, and it is like a barrier that prevents from entering harmful substances, for instance, pathogenic bacterial infections, into the internal environment58. Shehata et al.59 reported that the gut environment can be evaluated by the histomorphological analysis. The VL, CD, and the VL/CD proportion are useful parameters for the evaluation of intestinal morphology60. Enhanced VL/CD ratio means that either digestion or absorption in chickens has augmented61. Also, reduced VL and increased CD might bring about poor absorption of nutrients, and then poorer performance62. However, the enhanced VL: CD proportion stems from deeper CD, indicating detrimental effects; because stem cells excessively have proliferated or differentiated at the bottom of crypts to produce daughter cells, some of them will remain as stem cells, and the other will become gut epithelial cells to reach to the peak of villus, where they will frequently differentiate to convert into mature epithelial cells63,64.

According to the Table 6, dietary administration of 2 OA + 1 HT had striking effects on the enhancement of ileal VL, VW, VL: CD ratio, AVSA, and the reduction of ileal CD of broiler chickens in d 42nd (P < 0.05). One probable reason for this synergistic effect on the ileal morphology of broilers has been supported that when sodium butyrate is metabolized, its major metabolic products, encompassing either ketone bodies or CO2 will be used as an energy source by gut epithelial cells65,66. In consensus with our study, Pekin ducks treated with OA had a higher VL, VL: CD proportion, and lower CD than the groups fed diets without OA on d 356. In our study, the enhancement in the VL of broiler chickens fed with 2 g OA/kg of diet might be attributed to calcium formate and calcium propionate, which were used in the form of OA. This has been supported by Abudabos et al.67 who reported that feeding Bacillus subtilis, Saccharomyces boulardii, oregano, and calcium montmorillonite ameliorated either growth performance or intestinal histology of broiler chickens infected with Salmonella. In another study, broilers treated with 200 mg OA/kg of diet, benefited from the larger ileal VL, the deeper ileal CD, and the bigger VL: CD ratio than those groups nourished with control negative (just basal diet), and control positive (basal diet + 50 mg Enramycin/kg of feed) in days 21 and 35 of age39. Similarly, compared with the groups fed with a fermented diet, ducks treated with dose-dependent levels of (OA + fermented diet) strikingly could benefit from a quadratic reduction in the VW (mucosal growth), either linear or quadratic enhancement in the VL, an unchanged CD of Lieberkühn, and an increase in the absorption surface area, which led to improvements in their FCR, final ADWG, and carcass characteristics40. Taken together, HT presumably reduced the peristalsis at ileum to provide more opportunity for positive effects of OA on the ileum, this in turn, potentially protected the body from pathogens, improved digestion, absorption, and then growth performance. This hypothesis has been supported by Ren et al.15 who explained that after entering TA at a concentration between 0.01 and 1 mg/ml or 250, 500, 750, and 1000 mg TA/kg of diet into the animals’ gut; it plays an astringent role to it decreases the gut peristalsis, reduces secretion of gut fluid, and enhances water reabsorption via either the CFTR-dependent pathway or calcium-mediated signaling pathways to finally it refines upregulation of genes responsible in gut tight junction protein, gut morphology, and gut barrier integrity2. In agreement with our study, improvements in the VL, VL: CD ratio at ileum, were observed among broilers nourished with 200 mg EA/kg of diet31, and GA from 50 to 100 (mg/kg of diet) to be enhanced either absorption or digestion68. Also, broiler chickens orally infected with Salmonella when fed with 0.15 g Sanguinarine/kg could achieve a smaller FCR, and a higher VL than those groups challenged with Salmonella and simultaneously fed with 0.1 g antibiotic/kg or 1 g oregano powder/kg69. In argument with our findings, Gu et al.7 explained that although TA did not have a noticeable effect on the length of different parts of the intestine, its intake, especially at high doses, could reduce the VL: CD proportion and VL of gut morphology of Brandt’s voles. Anyway, how 1 g HT/kg of diet failed to negatively impress either gut barrier integration or FCR in our study, which was supported by the following potential mechanisms that TA probably forms a complex with nutritional ingredients such as proteins and, this in turn, impairs either nutrient use or the development of gut morphology70. Because Guo et al.56 reported that the phenolic hydroxyl group in tannins might act as the hydrogen donor and the carbonyl peptide group in the enzyme may serve as the hydrogen acceptor. Lower enzymatic activity such as lipase among yellow-feather broilers nourished with 300 and 450 mg/kg hydrolyzed gallotannin potentially led to a weaker intestinal barrier function through either decreased antioxidant capacity or deregulation of gut microbial balance and, this in turn, caused a worse FCR compared with broilers fed with 150 mg/kg hydrolyzed gallotannin56.

Cu@TA NPs adhere and localize on the bacterial outer membrane surface, an external thin peptidoglycan layer, and an internal cytoplasmic membrane of gram-negative bacteria such as E.coli to cause disruptions in metabolic or physiological roles of the bacterial external membrane through the creation of irregular holes on the bacterial wall and, this in turn, facilitates the penetration of self-disassembled Cu+ into the bacterial cytoplasm to Cu+ attaches to intracellular ingredients, induces ROS production, disrupts the nutrient and electron transport chain, which lead to bacterial explosion and intracellular substrates secretion, for instance, groundplasm and protein83. Also, Borges et al.85 explained that GA can permanently alter the structural membrane of pathogens, for example, E. coli, to prevent their growth via the linkage of GA to chitosan86. In our study, the unchanged ileal population of Salmonella might be relevant to its following properties as López-Romero et al.87 well reported that enhancement of Salmonella sensitivity to be inactivated under heat conditions depends on a synergistic correlation between temperature and dose-dependant GA, which was utilized by Ground chicken. Similarly, Choi et al.88 proved that TA as a natural substitution instead of antibiotic growth promoter can not be utilized more than 1 g/kg of diet due to its antinutritional effects on non-challenged broilers with Salmonella, whereas dietary inclusion of TA from 1 to 2 g/kg of diet could linearly decrease the partial frequency of the Enterobacteriaceae family, for example, Salmonella spp., E. coli, and also it reduced cecal colonization of Salmonella among challenged broilers with Salmonella in d 18; thereby improving immunity and growth performance34.

In the current study, the interaction effects between disparate levels of HT and OA were not significant in terms of the ileal population of E. coli, Salmonella, Lactobacillus, and total bacteria in broiler chickens. According to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), TA can cause a dose-dependent alteration in the organismal systems, metabolism, and cellular processes of bacteria existing in the colon of Voles7, and such microbes can form a coevolutionary communication with their host89. Modification of autotrophic bacterial composition depends on TA, because TA potentially can change the intestinal microbial functions, including phototrophy, oxygenic-photoautotrophy, and photoautotrophy90, which are related to the energy harvest of autotrophic bacteria91. Hydrolysis of Ellagitannins for EA yield in the gastrointestinal tract occurs to help either Lactobacillus or Bifidobacterium to grow92. Kong et al.93 reported that Lactobacillus, as a gram-positive bacteria, can help the growth of beneficial microorganisms on mucosa to regulate oxidative stress94. Exopolysaccharides of Lactobacillus and Streptococci species, such as Streptococcus gallolyticus protect these bacteria from the antibacterial activity of tannins, for instance, TA95. Tannins can indirectly either inhibit extracellular microbial enzymes or deprive bacteria of the substrates needed for the growth of microbes11.

In the present study, dietary adminestration of 2 g OA/kg of diet led to the lowest number of ileal E. coli (P < 0.05) and Salmonella (P = 0.056); whereas it brought about the highest population of intestinal Lactobacillus (P < 0.05). In our study, OA presumably through either a probable disruption in the performance of E. coli flagella or influencing the variety of intestinal pathogens could decrease the pathogenic population in the ileum of broilers and, this in turn, played a vital role in either the improvement of activity of gram-positive bacteria such as Lactobacillus or the refinement of some physiological parameters of broilers for example immunity. This hypothesis was first supported by Pearlin et al.5 who reported that the activities of acidifiers depend on the presence of nutritional ingredients with the basic pH in the diet, the variety of the intestinal microbiota, and farm hygiene. Secondly, Verstraeten et al.96 proved that the flagella of E. coli help its movement depending on the environment for its adhesion, penetration, and colonization on the intestinal mucus layer97. Thirdly, acidifiers such as formic acid, propionic acid, and sodium formate have activities against pathogens in the alimentary canal of poultry98. Although either gram-positive or gram-negative bacteria ribosomally can synthesize bacteriocin peptides73, OA provides pathogens with non-pH toxicity by influencing the pathogenic metabolism99. Also, OA can dose-dependently be metabolized as a source of either carbon or energy by microorganisms, and they even play an inhibitory role via influencing some internal agents inside microorganisms5. For instance, sodium butyrate with its unpleasant odor can be converted to a short-chain fatty acid such as butyric acid in the gastrointestinal tract of poultry, and this acidifier is utilized as an antibiotic alternative due to its activities against Salmonella76. OA through enhancement of either acetic acid or butyric acid can potentially cause minor changes in the gut bacterial communities100. Finally, gut microbiota as a barrier plays roles in mucosal reinforcement, stimulation of mucus immunity, and cellular renovation; thereby influencing the physiology of the host101. Except for the main effect of 2 g OA/kg of diet on the Salmonella population in our study, which agrees with these studies that broiler chickens treated with 200 mg OA/kg of diet, benefited from the lower numbers of either E.coli or Salmonella, and the higher number of lactobacillus than those groups nourished with the control negative (just basal diet), and the control positive (basal diet + 50 mg Enramycin/kg of feed) in the 21st and 35th days of the experiment39.

Conclusion

Either hydrolyzable tannins or organic acids has not only enhanced the relative weight of intestine, and reduced the relative weights of liver and abdominal fat in broiler chickens, but has also led to a reduction in the ileal E. coli population and an increase in Lactobacillus count and the apparent villus surface area index in the intestine. This, in turn, has enhanced their ileal absorptive area, which has been beneficial for augmenting the ileal lamina propria lymphoid follicles thickness or the capacity of lymphoid organs to accommodate lymphocytes. It has improved lymphocyte subsets, facilitating the generation of immunoglobulins against external factors. Additionally, the current findings indicate that feeding 1HT + 2OA manifested the best synergistic effects on the growth performance of broiler chickens via enhancing mucosal and systemic immune responses, modifying gut microbiota, and improving health indices related to the intestinal absorptive area.
