Spatiotemporal liposome-zwitterionic nano-hydrogel orchestrates immunomodulation and neurogenesis in spinal cord injury repair
Authors: Wencan Zhang, Qiaoying Hu, Wenxiang Li, Xiaolong Zhou, Mingshan Liu, Jincheng Lu, Jiaoyao Wen, Wenzhao Wang, Feilong Wei, Liang Liu, Lin Jin, Lei Ye, Shiqing Feng, Zhijian Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Following spinal cord injury (SCI), the sequential dysregulation of the inflammatory microenvironment coupled with insufficient endogenous neuroregeneration severely impedes functional recovery. Conventional pharmacotherapies targeting singular pathological cascades and systemic drug administration fail to achieve comprehensive neural repair. Herein, an engineered liposome-zwitterionic nano-hydrogel drug delivery system (RA@Lip/Met-HSB) loaded with the novel neuromodulator retinoic acid (RA) and anti-inflammatory agent metformin (Met) was designed. This system enables temporally programmed release at the lesion site, leveraging the hierarchical degradation of the zwitterionic hydrogel and liposome. Mechanistically, this system orchestrates dual-phase therapeutic actions including acute-phase Met release drives microglial M2 polarization and attenuates neuronal apoptosis, while chronic-phase RA release promotes neural stem cells (NSCs) differentiation into functional neurons and oligodendrocytes, enhancing neuronal signal transmission efficiency via axonal remyelination. In murine SCI models, this system significantly restored motor function through: acute immunomodulation by reducing neuroinflammation and reduces apoptosis, thereby preserving endogenous NSCs, whereas chronic neural remodeling by boosting neurogenesis and facilitating remyelination, axonal regrowth and functional synapse formation. Collectively, this temporally programmed delivery strategy demonstrates robust spatiotemporal immunomodulation and neurogenesis, providing a transformative clinical rationale of the paradigm shift in SCI therapeutics for transitioning drug delivery from systemic to localized, and from temporally unordered to programmed drug administration.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a catastrophic neurological trauma characterized by permanent motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction, with its pathological progression exhibiting distinct temporal characteristics: the acute phase is dominated by an imbalanced inflammatory microenvironment and neuronal apoptosis, while the chronic phase is characterized by impaired neural circuit reconstruction due to insufficient endogenous neuroregeneration [1,2]. Conventional therapeutic approaches including high-dose corticosteroid therapy and surgical decompression fail to precisely regulate the dynamically evolving pathological microenvironment post-injury [3,4]. Additionally, monotherapeutic agents targeting isolated pathological processes such as anti-inflammatory agents or neurotrophic factors often exhibit limited efficacy owing to systemic administration [5,6].

Recent advances in spatiotemporal drug delivery systems (DDSs) offer promising strategies for localized therapeutic delivery in SCI repair. Zuo et al. designed ROS-responsive amphiphilic copolymer nanoparticles encapsulating neuronal agonists, which disassemble under acute-phase oxidative stress to release therapeutics that activate inhibitory interneurons and potentiate axonal regrowth [7]. Zhu et al. developed layered double hydroxide nanocarriers for sustained chronic-phase delivery of neurotrophin-3, enhancing neural regeneration [8]. Nevertheless, prevailing DDSs remain constrained by static release kinetics and single-agent payloads, failing to achieve spatiotemporal coordination of immunomodulation and neuroregeneration [9,10]. Current spatiotemporal drug delivery strategies for SCI face trade-offs between spatial precision (light-responsive systems) and temporal tunability (MMP-sensitive hydrogels). To overcome these limitations, next-generation spatiotemporal DDSs designs must address two critical bottlenecks: (i) matching material degradation properties with stage-specific pathological demands, specifically, rapid release of anti-inflammatory agents in the acute phase to suppress secondary injury, and sustained release of pro-regenerative factors in the chronic phase to activate endogenous repair; (ii) orchestrating multi-target synergies to disrupt the SCI “vicious cycle” wherein inflammation suppresses neuroregeneration and neuroregeneration failure exacerbates inflammation [11,12].

Zwitterionic hydrogels, a class of smart hydrogels with polymer networks containing equimolar positive and negative charge groups, offer unique advantages in drug delivery, including high drug-loading capacity and sustained release properties [13]. Liposomes, spherical vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers, are widely used for drug delivery across various diseases. Their cell membrane-like structure enable efficient cellular delivery via membrane fusion but are plagued by premature degradation and burst release [14]. Retinoic acid (RA) exhibits potent neuroregenerative properties across neurodegenerative contexts. RA potently induces neurite outgrowth, enhances axonal regeneration, and promotes neural stem cells (NSCs) differentiation into neurons, thereby facilitating neural circuit reconstruction and functional recovery in spinal cord [15,16]. Metformin (Met) has recently demonstrated positive effects in modulating neuroinflammation. Met attenuates neuroinflammation by polarizing microglia toward anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and rescuing neuropeptide alterations, thereby mitigating neuronal apoptosis and preserving synaptic integrity in models of neurodegenerative disorders [17,18]. Nevertheless, few studies have leveraged their combined advantages to target the pathological microenvironments of SCI during both acute and chronic phases.

Herein, we engineered a liposome-zwitterionic hydrogel composite DDSs (RA@Lip/Met-HSB) through a two-step process: (i) synthesizing RA-loaded liposomes (RA@Lip) using thin-film dispersion with phospholipid/cholesterol; and (ii) fabricating Met-loaded zwitterionic hydrogel (Met-HSB) through atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) using hyaluronic acid bromide (HA-Br) and sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA). Unlike pure HA hydrogels, our system uses HA-PSBMA copolymer hydrogels, where PSBMA's strong hydration layer (via electrostatic interactions with water molecules) creates an ultra-smooth surface that resists protein adsorption and cell adhesion. This is critical for SCI, where reduced protein fouling minimizes activation of pro-inflammatory pathways, thereby suppressing fibrotic encapsulation. Electrostatic interactions between anionic liposomes and residual cationic groups in the HSB hydrogel prolong liposomal retention and form a liposome-zwitterionic hydrogel composite delivery system, enabling sequential release kinetics. Mechanistically, the stepwise degradation of the zwitterionic hydrogel enables rapid Met elution from the HSB hydrogel during acute inflammation, followed by sustained RA release from liposomes in chronic phase, achieving precise spatiotemporal matching between local drug release and repair processes. Functionally, in vitro and in vivo investigations have confirmed the temporal synergism between Met and RA: Met polarizes microglia to the pro-healing M2 phenotype during the acute phase of SCI, attenuating neuronal apoptosis, whereas RA promotes the differentiation of endogenous NSCs into functional neurons and oligodendrocytes, thereby facilitating axonal regeneration (Fig. 1). The spatiotemporally adaptive strategy proposed in this study transcends the inherent limitations of conventional DDSs which primarily target single pathological stages, synergistically reprogramming the immune-regenerative microenvironment. Results

Fabrication and characterization of the RA@Lip/Met-HSB

A multistep synthesis process was performed to produce RA@Lip/Met-HSB DDSs. Firstly, different proportions of HSB copolymers were synthesized via atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) reaction (Fig. S1A–B). The successful synthesis of HA-Br and HA-g-PSBMA was confirmed through 1H NMR and FTIR. As shown in Fig. 2F, the peak at 1.96 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum of HA-Br was attributed to the original methyl groups of HA and methyl protons in the acyl bromide group. Correspondingly, the FTIR spectrum of HA-Br exhibited the successful grafting of bromine groups. HA-Br had a strong absorption band at 1731 cm−1, which was assigned to the stretching vibration of ester group C=O (Fig. 2E). The successful synthesis of HA-g-PSBMA was confirmed by 1H-NMRspectroscopy, where a peak at 2.86 ppm was attributed to methylene protons adjacent to the sulfonate group (Fig. 2F). Additionally, the FTIR spectrum of HA-g-PSBMA (Fig. 2E) displayed characteristic symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of the sulfonyl (S=O) group at 1087 cm−1 and 1337 cm−1, respectively. HA-g-PSBMA polymers were synthesized with different HA-Br substitution degree and varying HA-Br: SBMA feed ratio. Following a formulation screening process, four hydrogels—HA0.41-g-PSBMA50, HA0.41-g-PSBMA75, HA0.46-g-PSBMA25, and HA0.46-g-PSBMA50—were identified as capable of forming stable hydrogels and were thus selected for subsequent experimental investigations. SEM images revealed that the hydrogels possessed porous and interconnected three-dimensional internal structure, which was conducive to drug loading applications. In the equilibrium swelling ratio experiments, it was observed that the swelling ratio of the hydrogels increased with the rise of HA-Br substitution degrees and SBMA: HA-Br feed ratios (Fig. 2H). The degradation profiles of the hydrogels were assessed in both NaCl solution and deionized (DI) water, as depicted in Fig. 2I and J, respectively. The results showed that the degradation rate of HA0.46-g-PSBMA50 was the slowest in NaCl, attributable to its highest crosslinking density among the tested samples. Interestingly, the degradation rate of HSB hydrogels in NaCl was significantly higher than that in DI water. This phenomenon is likely due to the presence of abundant ions in NaCl, which weakens the electrostatic interactions between the zwitterionic segments, thereby destroying the cross-linking points of the hydrogel skeleton and accelerating degradation. Rheological results demonstrated that, for all four hydrogel formulations, the storage modulus (G′) exceeded the loss modulus (G″), indicating the successful formation of hydrogel networks (Fig. 2G). Among these, HA0.46-g-PSBMA50 exhibited the highest G′, signifying superior mechanical strength relative to the other hydrogels.

To achieve sequential drug release, we loaded the neuromodulator RA and the anti-inflammatory drug Met into the hydrogel system as follows, thereby regulating inflammatory microenvironment and promoting nerve regeneration associated with SCI. The liposoluble RA was encapsulated in liposomes to enhance drug loading capacity and prolong release duration. SEM images showed that RA@Lip exhibited a uniform spherical shape, which indicated the successful synthesis of RA@Lip (Fig. 2A). Dynamic light scattering analysis using a Malvern particle sizer determined the average particle size of RA@Lip to be 134.7 nm (Fig. 2B), with a zeta potential of −19 mV (Fig. S1A). The negatively surface charge of RA@Lip enabled electrostatic interaction with the positively charged quaternary ammonium groups present in HSB hydrogel, further delaying drug release from the liposomes. Liposomal stability was assessed by monitoring average particle size and PDI as indicators. As shown in Fig. 2C, the average particle size and PDI of blank liposomes and RA@Lip did not change significantly in 7 days, confirming that RA@Lip did not rupture in a short period of time, preventing sudden release of drugs. Quantitative analysis via UV spectrophotometry indicated RA@lip drug loading was 3.29%, encapsulation efficiency was 93.73%, which effectively increased the dosage of RA administered. In order to confirm the time-sequential release characteristics of RA and Met, in vitro release experiments were performed (Fig. 2K). Met in RA@Lip/Met-HSB was released rapidly and nearly completely at 24 h, achieving a release efficiency of 98.88%. The release rate of RA was 20.98% on the first day, and reached a plateau on the 13th day, reaching 80.68%. The time-dependent release behavior can be explained by the low diffusion resistance of water-soluble Met in hydrophilic hydrogel network, contrasted with the delayed release of liposome-encapsulated, lipophilic RA, which is further hindered by its incompatibility with the aqueous hydrogel environment. Therefore, RA and Met in RA@Lip/Met-HSB demonstrated sequential release characteristics, providing a mechanistic foundation for spatiotemporal immunomodulation and enhanced neural regeneration of SCI.

Biocompatibility and biotoxicity

As a drug delivery system, hydrogels must exhibit excellent biocompatibility, a critical requirement for in vivo applications. Following the synthesis of the zwitterionic hydrogel, in vitro anti-protein adsorption assays were performed. The results demonstrated that the hydrogel exhibited low protein adsorption capacity, indicating strong anti-protein adhesion properties and reduced potential to induce immune responses (Fig. 3A). Additionally, hemolysis assays revealed that the hemolysis rates of the hydrogel at various dilution multiples were all below 5%, meeting the criteria for non-hemolytic materials (Fig. 3B and C). From the above results, HA0.46-g-PSBMA50 had slow degradation rate, enhanced mechanical properties, blood compatibility and a resistance to protein. Therefore, HA0.46-g-PSBMA50 was selected for further experimental studies. Next, to evaluate the hydrogel's effects on NSCs viability and proliferation, NSCs were co-cultured with the hydrogel for 48 h, and assessments were conducted using live/dead staining and CCK8 assays. Both live/dead staining and CCK8 results consistently showed that neither zwitterionic hydrogel nor liposome-zwitterionic hydrogel affected NSCs viability at 24 or 48 h post-co-culture (Fig. 3D–F). Having confirmed the favorable in vitro biocompatibility, we further evaluated the hydrogel's in vivo safety using SCI mice model for 8 week. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed no deposition or toxic accumulation of hydrogel degradation products (Fig. 3G). Concurrently, hematological analysis indicated no significant impairments in liver function (ALT, AST, and ALP) or renal function (CERA, BUN, and UA) following 8 weeks of hydrogel transplantation (Fig. 3H and I). Met-loaded HSB hydrogel regulates polarization of microglia while suppressing ROS overproduction and attenuating neuronal apoptosis

Microglia predominantly differentiate toward the M1 phenotype (pro-inflammatory) post-SCI rather than the M2 phenotype (anti-inflammatory), which is associated with Arg-1 and TGF-β. Concurrently, activated microglia undergo oxidative stress, generating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) that establish an inflammation-suppressive microenvironment detrimental to neuronal survival. To investigate the effects of HSB hydrogel-sustained Met on microglial behavior, we established a transwell co-culture system where BV2 microglia were seeded in the lower chamber and Met-loaded HSB zwitterionic hydrogel was placed in the upper chamber to simulate in vivo drug release kinetics. In order to evaluate the ability of drugs to regulate the phenotype of microglia under the inflammatory storm state after SCI (where there is neither pure M1 nor M2 phenotype), LPS stimulation of BV2 cells was used to better reflect the in vivo situation, which is not a single M1 induction, but simulates a dynamic mixed inflammatory environment. Notably, Met release from the hydrogel effectively suppressed this morphological change. qPCR analysis revealed that Met-HSB treatment significantly downregulated the RNA expression of M1 markers (iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6) while upregulating M2 markers (Arg-1, CD206, and IL-4) (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence staining for Iba1 (microglia marker) and Arg-1 further demonstrated enhanced Arg-1 intensity in the Met-HSB group, indicating augmented M2 polarization (Fig. 4B and D). Conversely, staining for Iba1 and iNOS showed reduced iNOS expression in Met-HSB-treated cells, confirming inhibition of M1 polarization (Fig. 4C and E). Meanwhile, we used the IL-4 model to investigate the direct M2 polarization effect of Met, and the results were consistent (Fig. S3). To assess oxidative stress modulation, we employed DCFH-DA to label ROS. Met-HSB-treated BV2 cells exhibited significantly lower ROS levels compared to LPS-stimulated controls, confirming its role in mitigating oxidative stress (Fig. 4F and G). To evaluate neuroprotective effects, primary neurons were subjected to OGD model to simulate SCI-induced neuronal ischemia. TUNEL staining revealed that Met treatment markedly reduced neuronal apoptosis (Fig. 4H and I). RA@Lip/Met-HSB directs NSCs differentiation toward functionally mature neurons (enhanced axonal branching/electrophysiological activity) and oligodendrocytes

In the injury core of SCI, endogenous NSCs exhibited reduced differentiation into functional neurons and increased commitment to astrocytes under inflammatory microenvironment, impairing the formation of functional neural circuits required for recovery. To investigate the effects of RA@Lip/Met-HSB on NSCs fate, we established a transwell co-culture model as previously described, with mouse primary NSCs in the lower chamber and RA@Lip/Met-HSB in the upper chamber to simulate in vivo drug delivery. The qPCR analysis revealed that RA@Lip/Met-HSB treatment reduced the gene expression of the NSCs marker Nestin, indicating enhanced NSCs differentiation; while upregulated gene expression of immature (TUJ1) and mature (MAP2) neuron markers and suppressing astrocyte marker GFAP (Fig. 5A). The immunofluorescence staining of TUJ1 and GFAP further validated these findings: more NSCs differentiated into TUJ1-positive immature neurons, and fewer adopted a GFAP-positive astrocytic fate in the RA@Lip/Met-HSB treatment group compared to controls (Fig. 4B and C). Similarly, the immunofluorescence staining of MAP2 and GFAP confirmed that the release of RA promoted NSCs differentiation into MAP2-positive mature neurons while inhibiting astrocytic commitment (Fig. 5D and E)—evidence that RA drives neuronal maturation. Oligodendrocyte differentiation was also assessed: immunofluorescence staining for MBP demonstrated that RA significantly increased the proportion of MBP-positive oligodendrocytes derived from NSCs (Fig. 5F and G). To evaluate functional neuronal maturation, we employed MEA analysis to measure discharge activity. The heatmap of neuronal discharges (with colors representing frequency, red indicating the highest) and spike plot amplitudes revealed that RA-treated NSCs-derived neurons exhibited increased discharge activity and larger spike amplitudes, suggesting improved inter-neuronal connectivity (Fig. 5H and I). Consistent with this, the neuronal morphology (immunofluorescence staining of TUJ1) showed that RA-treated neurons had more extensive axonal branching, a key feature of mature, connection-forming neurons (Fig. 5K). RA@Lip/Met-HSB drug delivery system enhances motor function recovery in spinal cord injury mice

Following spinal cord injury (SCI) in mice, the RA@Lip/Met-HSB drug delivery system was applied topically to the injury site to enable spatiotemporal drug release. Behavioral, electrophysiological, and histological assessments were performed at designated time points as outlined in the experimental timeline (Fig. 6A). To evaluate hindlimb motor function recovery, the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) score was assessed via double-blind, observer-blinded evaluation. Immediately post-SCI, all mice exhibited complete hindlimb paralysis with a BMS score of 0. Longitudinal BMS scoring over 8 weeks revealed that the RA@Lip/Met-HSB group consistently outperformed other groups starting from postoperative week 2, indicating superior hindlimb motor recovery (Fig. 6B). By week 8, the RA@Lip/Met-HSB group achieved a mean BMS score of 3.25 ± 0.62, significantly higher than that of all other groups (Fig. 6C). Notably, the HSB hydrogel alone exhibited a promotional effect on SCI repair (with a BMS score higher than the SCI-only group). However, both RA@Lip-HSB and Met-HSB groups showed comparable BMS scores, both exceeding that of the unloaded HSB hydrogel (Fig. 6C). Electrophysiological experiments further validated functional connectivity: the amplitude of motor evoked potential (MEP) signals recorded in the RA@Lip/Met-HSB group was significantly higher than that in other treatment groups (Fig. 6D and E). The reprogramming of the inflammatory microenvironment in vivo promotes the migration of endogenous NSCs and reduces cell apoptosis

SCI triggers an immediate inflammatory cascade that drives massive neuronal death and suppresses the activation, migration, and differentiation of endogenous NSCs. This pathological sequence leaves few viable neurons and endogenous NSCs in the chronic phase, severely impairing motor function recovery. Thus, modulating the post-injury inflammatory microenvironment has emerged as a critical therapeutic strategy for SCI. To investigate the effects of the Met-HSB drug delivery system on this process, we performed immunofluorescence analysis of spinal cord tissues at the acute phase using cell-type-specific markers. Staining of Iba1(a marker for microglia) revealed that HSB hydrogel treatment modestly reduced Iba1 positive microglial infiltration compared to the SCI group. Notably, Met-HSB treatment further enhanced this effect due to Met's anti-inflammatory properties, resulting in a more pronounced suppression of microglial activation (Fig. 7A and B). TUNEL staining was performed to assess the apoptotic cell death. Consistent with improved microglial regulation, Met-HSB-treated mice exhibited significantly fewer TUNEL positive apoptotic cells than both SCI and HSB groups (Fig. 7C and D). This correlation underscores that dampening the inflammatory microenvironment directly mitigates neuronal apoptosis. Nestin staining, a marker for endogenous NSCs, showed minimal Nestin positive cells in uninjured spinal cords. Post-SCI, NSCs were activated but sparse. In Met-HSB-treated mice, however, the number of activated Nestin positive NSCs increased markedly, and more cells migrated toward the injury site—indicating preparation for subsequent neuronal differentiation (Fig. 7E and F). To elucidate the mechanism underlying microenvironment remodeling, we co-stained for M1 (iNOS) and M2 (CD206) microglial markers in the injury core. Met-HSB treatment significantly increased the proportion of CD206 positive M2 microglia while reducing iNOS positive M1 microglia (Fig. 7G–I). This phenotypic shift demonstrates that Met-HSB reshapes the inflammatory microenvironment by skewing microglia toward an anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative state. Inhibiting glial scars and promoting the regeneration of functional neurons and axons in vivo

Functional neuron regeneration is critical for locomotor and sensory recovery after SCI. However, a major barrier to regeneration is the pathological process where NSCs preferentially differentiate into astrocytes, forming dense glial scars that physically block axonal growth. To elucidate how the RA@Lip/Met-HSB drug delivery system modulates astroglial response and promotes functional neuron/axon regeneration in vivo, we performed histological analyses using cell-type-specific markers for astrocytes, neurons, and functional subtypes. GFAP staining was used to quantify astrogliosis at the injury epicenter and adjacent regions. Compared to SCI-only controls, Met-HSB and RA@Lip-HSB treatment alone reduced astrocyte density and mitigated the extent of reactive astrogliosis at both injury ends. Strikingly, the RA@Lip/Met-HSB group exhibited a further significant suppression of astrocyte proliferation; and the size of the cystic cavity formed between the rostral and caudal injury stumps was markedly diminished (Fig. 8A and C). These data indicate that RA@Lip/Met-HSB potently inhibits pathological astrogliosis and glial scar formation.To evaluate neuronal recovery, we stained for Tuj1. In SCI-only mice, the injury core was largely devoid of neurons due to acute cell death. Met-HSB and RA@Lip-HSB treatment increased neuronal density in the lesion area, suggesting a protective effect on surviving neurons. The RA@Lip/Met-HSB group further augmented neuronal numbers, demonstrating dual benefits: preserving injured neurons and promoting NSCs differentiation toward a neuronal lineage (Fig. 8B and D). We next assessed regeneration of functionally relevant neuron subtypes​ using subtype-specific markers. Choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)—a marker for cholinergic neurons, which are critical for motor circuit reconstruction—staining revealed that RA@Lip/Met-HSB-treated mice had a significant increase in ChAT neurons in the ventral horn and lesion vicinity compared to Met-HSB or RA@Lip-HSB (Fig. 8E and F). This finding suggests that RA@Lip/Met-HSB supports the recovery of motor-related neuronal populations, linking neuronal regeneration to improved motor output. Serotonergic (5-HT) axons, originating from the brainstem, play a key role in modulating spinal locomotor circuits. 5-HT immunostaining showed sparse 5-HT axons crossing the injury site in Met-HSB or RA@Lip-HSB groups. In contrast, the RA@Lip/Met-HSB group exhibited robust regeneration of 5-HT axons through the lesion, indicating restoration of descending serotonergic innervation (Fig. 8G and H). Overall, these results demonstrate that the RA@Lip/Met-HSB drug delivery system drives in vivo functional recovery via a dual mechanism: inhibiting glial scar formation by suppressing pathological astrocyte proliferation, and promoting regeneration of functional neurons and axons—including ChAT neurons (for motor circuit reconstruction) and 5-HT axons (for locomotor modulation). RNA Sequencing Unveils Molecular Drivers of Acute Microenvironment Remodeling and Preparatory Signals for Chronic Neurogenesis

To dissect the molecular mechanisms by which RA@Lip/Met-HSB remodels the acute-phase inflammatory microenvironment, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on spinal cord tissues collected at 72 h post-injury, a critical window for microenvironmental reprogramming. Heatmap analysis identified 28 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with inflammatory responses, neuroprotection, and other relevant pathways (Fig. 9A). The volcano plot showcased a dispersed distribution of these DEGs, highlighting that RA@Lip/Met-HSB exerts targeted modulation on specific transcriptional programs rather than inducing global gene dysregulation (Fig. 9B). The Gene Co-expression Network (GCN) is a crucial tool for revealing functional associations between genes. The results indicate that nodes with high connectivity (such as Ntrk1 and Trpv1) may be “hub genes” and key targets in related signaling pathways (Fig. 9C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis elucidated the enrichment profiles across three major categories: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. We constructed a circular enrichment plot based on 45 GO terms linked to immune microenvironment remodeling and neuroregeneration that were modulated by RA@Lip/Met-HSB, which demonstrated predominant enrichment in the biological process and cellular component categories (Fig. 9D and Fig. S8). Concurrently, the bubble plot of the top 20 enriched GO terms revealed that within the biological process category, “inflammatory response” exhibited the highest gene count of 12, while in the cellular component category, “axon” was the most enriched term (13 genes) (Fig. 9E and F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis identified 8 significantly enriched pathways, which were primarily categorized into environmental information processing, organismal systems, and metabolism (Fig. 9G and Fig. S9). Bubble plots illustrated pathway enrichment significance, whereas bar charts depicted the number of genes per pathway. Notable top-enriched pathways included neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (14 genes), cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (9 genes), inflammatory mediator regulation of TRP channels (7 genes), calcium signaling pathway (5 genes), and glutamatergic synapse (4 genes) (Fig. 9H). Discussion

SCI remains a formidable clinical challenge due to its complex spatiotemporal pathological progression and limited endogenous repair capacity [2]. Conventional therapeutic modalities are constrained by their inability to precisely align with the dynamic post-injury microenvironmental evolution, resulting in suboptimal efficacy [3]. Furthermore, single-target or static-release DDSs struggle to achieve synergistic modulation of inflammation and neuroregeneration. To address these limitations, Our study pioneers a novel liposome-zwitterionic hydrogel composite DDSs (RA@Lip/Met-HSB) with a spatiotemporally tailored release strategy. This RA@Lip/Met-HSB system orchestrates dual-phase pharmacological intervention: i) acute phase: achieves rapid release of Met to suppress neuroinflammation, attenuate neuronal apoptosis, and preserve NSCs populations; ii) chronic phase: facilitates sustained release of RA to promote NSCs differentiation into functional neurons, enhance remyelination, and stimulate axonal regeneration. Notably, this strategy transcends the historical limitations of single-stage/single-target drug delivery, establishing a novel paradigm for precision neuroregeneration in SCI and broader CNS pathologies.

The spatiotemporal heterogeneity of SCI repair necessitates temporally matched drug delivery. The pathological progression of SCI exhibits distinct spatiotemporal heterogeneity: acute phase (within 1 week post-injury) is dominated by inflammatory cascade activation; chronic phase (2 weeks to 2 months post-injury) is characterized by suppression of neuroregeneration and glial scar formation, ultimately impeding functional reconstruction [19]. Conventional therapies such as methylprednisolone pulse therapy lack temporal specificity, potentially exacerbating immunosuppression or disrupting endogenous repair mechanisms during the chronic phase [20]. Existing drug delivery systems including bioengineered bacterial outer membrane vesicles (BM-NPs), ROS-responsive nanoparticles, layered double hydroxide (LDH)-based sustained-release materials achieve stage-selective targeting but fail to accomplish synergistic intervention for “acute anti-inflammatory” and “chronic neuroregenerative” phases. Zuo et al. designed ROS-responsive nanoparticles (CLP-257) that release GABA agonists during the acute phase to enhance axonal regeneration but lack sustained chronic-phase intervention [7]. Zhu et al. developed an LDH-NT3 system achieving chronic neurotrophin release but unable to reverse acute-phase neuronal apoptosis [8]. Li et al. developed BM-NPs using a “tortoise-and-hare synergy” strategy leveraging neutrophil/macrophage infiltration kinetics for drug delivery, but this approach focuses solely on acute-phase inflammation modulation without integrating chronic neuroregenerative promotion [21]. The RA@Lip/Met-HSB in this study realize temporally controlled drug release by stepwise degradation kinetics: acute phase rapidly releases drugs (60% Met within 72 h), where released Met suppresses microglial M1 polarization and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) secretion; chronic phase sustains release (RA continuously released over 14 days), where hydrogel network-restricted liposomes slowly release RA to promote eNSCs differentiation into GABAergic neurons and oligodendrocyte differentiation, driving remyelination. This design precisely matched SCI's pathological temporal demands, but further advanced it through composite carriers enabling multi-stage synergistic regulation within a single system, overcoming limitations of single-phase interventions.

The “inflammation-regeneration” vicious cycle following SCI requires multi-target synergistic intervention strategies [22]. The repair impairment in SCI originates from a negative feedback loop between inflammation and regeneration: excessive inflammation during the acute phase damages surviving neurons and axons; impaired neuroregeneration in the chronic phase reduces secretion of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF, NGF at the injury site, further weakening tissue repair capacity [22,23]. Current research primarily focuses on single targets: anti-inflammatory agents can suppress inflammation but may impair eNSCs proliferation; neurotrophic factors promote regeneration yet are prone to degradation or inactivation by the inflammatory microenvironment [24,25]. Even when some studies attempt combination therapy such as anti-inflammatory drugs and neurotrophic factors, systemic administration results in insufficient drug concentration at the target site or non-specific distribution, failing to achieve synergistic effects [26]. This study directly targets this vicious cycle through the functional complementation of Met and RA: Met inhibits microglial M1 polarization and lower inflammatory factor levels, creating a “permissive microenvironment” for neuroregeneration; RA activates retinoic acid receptors to promote differentiation of GABAergic neurons and proliferation/differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), enhancing myelin regeneration capacity and directly driving neural circuit reconstruction. In vivo experiments, the RA@Lip-HSB group confirmed RA's standalone capacity to enhance chronic neuroregeneration (e.g., neuronal differentiation, functional recovery), albeit with lesser efficacy than the combination therapy, highlighting the added value of Met's acute anti-inflammatory priming; the combination group exhibits significantly improved lower limb motor function recovery compared to the single-drug group according to BMS scores and catwalk, indicating a synergistic effect of inflammation inhibition and regeneration promotion. Meanwhile, local delivery avoids systemic administration side effects of vitamin A toxicity from systemic RA application, significantly enhancing therapeutic safety.

The performance of DDSs directly impacts therapeutic efficacy, necessitating their design to balance drug loading capacity, release kinetics, and biocompatibility [27,28]. Liposomes, owing to their structural similarity to cell membranes, can promote drug endocytosis and enhance stability; however, their phospholipid bilayer is susceptible to environmental factors including enzymatic degradation, pH changes, leading to burst drug release [29]. Zwitterionic hydrogels, characterized by balanced positive/negative charge groups, exhibit low immunogenicity and sustained-release properties, yet their drug loading capacity is constrained by network density [30]. This study loaded liposomes into the HSB hydrogel network via electrostatic interactions (binding of the negatively charged liposomal surface to the small number of cationic groups in the hydrogel), leveraging both the sustained-release properties of the hydrogel to delay liposomal burst release and the membrane fusion capability of liposomes to enhance the intracellular delivery efficiency of RA. In vitro release assays demonstrated that the composite system achieved ∼60% Met release within the initial 72 h (matching acute-phase requirements) and sustained RA release over 14 days (covering the chronic phase), outperforming single liposomes (80% Met burst release within 24 h) or single hydrogels (50% RA release within 7 days) in release patterns [19]. Additionally, the RA@Lip/Met-HSB system exhibits excellent biocompatibility and safety. Animal experiments confirmed that the hydrogel's degradation products are non-toxic to organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. Meanwhile the small particle size of liposomes (∼100 nm) enables penetration of the blood-spinal cord barrier, enhancing drug enrichment at the target site [31].

Spatiotemporally adaptive delivery strategies hold universal implications for the treatment of CNS diseases [32,33]. The “acute anti-inflammatory and chronic pro-regenerative” paradigm of this study can be extended to other CNS diseases with temporal pathological characteristics such as stroke, Alzheimer's disease. Take stroke as an example: its pathological progression is similarly divided into three phases—the acute phase (within 6 h, characterized by ischemic penumbra and oxidative stress), the subacute phase (24–72 h, marked by inflammatory cascades and blood-brain barrier disruption), and the chronic phase (>1 week, involving neural remodeling and glial scar formation) [34,35]. Traditional therapies (e.g., the thrombolytic drug tPA) are limited by their time window (applicable only to the acute phase), making it difficult to cover the entire disease course [36]. Therefore, A free drug mixture control was not included, as our study prioritizes evaluating the spatiotemporal synergy of sequential release—an advantage lost in static combinations, which fail to match disease phase dynamics. The “spatiotemporal adaptation” design of this study, which modulates carrier degradation rates and drug binding modes, achieves precise drug release across different stages, providing a generalizable strategy for multi-stage diseases such as stroke. Additionally, neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer's disease), despite their longer disease courses (years to decades), share a similar “amyloid-beta deposition - inflammatory response - neuronal death” vicious cycle with SCI. The synergistic mechanism of Met (inflammation inhibition) and RA (synaptic regeneration promotion) in this study can also be applied to dual-stage interventions targeting amyloid-beta clearance and neuroprotection, offering theoretical references for multi-target, long-term CNS disease treatment.

Although this study has made phased progress, the following limitations remain: Ⅰ) Animal model limitations: Experiments were only validated in a mouse spinal cord contusion injury model, without considering the impact of SCI model heterogeneity (e.g., differences in injury severity, type, and segment) on the performance of the delivery system. Future studies are needed to verify its universality in large animals such as beagles and cynomolgus monkeys. (Ⅱ) Scalable fabrication challenges: Scalable fabrication processes for the composite delivery system have not yet been optimized, and the binding efficiency between liposomes and hydrogels, as well as batch-to-batch consistency, require improvement through process refinement. (Ⅲ) Insufficient mechanistic depth: Mechanistic investigations primarily relied on transcriptomic, histological, and behavioral analyses, with limited detailed data on in vivo drug distribution (e.g., RA concentration in cerebrospinal fluid) and metabolic kinetics (e.g., Met half-life). (Ⅳ) Focused validation of delivery efficacy: This study emphasized the verification of delivery effects, while the molecular mechanisms underlying the individual actions of RA and Met were referenced from existing studies rather than exploring new signaling pathways.

Conclusion

The present study innovatively designed a liposome-zwitterionic hydrogel composite delivery system. On the one hand, temporally sequential release replaces disordered intervention: the stepwise degradation properties of the RA@Lip/Met-HSB enable rapid release of Met (encapsulated within the hydrogel) during the acute phase, while liposomes facilitate slow release of RA in the chronic phase, achieving precise temporal alignment between drug release and injury repair processes. On the other hand, local administration replaces systemic medication to realize the functional complementarity of Met and RA: the former inhibits microglial M1 polarization to reduce neuronal apoptosis, while the latter activates functional neurons and oligodendrocyte differentiation to promote myelin regeneration, with both synergistically reshaping the “immune-regenerative” microenvironment of SCI. This spatiotemporally adaptive delivery strategy transcends the limitations of conventional systems that focus solely on single pathological stages or targets, not only providing a dynamic, multi-dimensional intervention strategy for SCI repair but also establishing a theoretical foundation for localized, precision therapy in other CNS diseases. The design of this composite delivery system embodies a paradigm shift in SCI treatment from systemic suppression to temporal regulation and offers a scientifically rigorous and feasible solution for clinical translation.
