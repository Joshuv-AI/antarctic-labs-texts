The buffering effect of physical activity on adolescent psychological stress: a cross-sectional survey and a longitudinal follow-up of Chinese adolescents
Authors: Yuyan Qian, Hu Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the increasing psychological stress among adolescents and the widespread adverse effects, physical activity provides a potential intervention measure, but research results and evidence are insufficient.MethodsTo clarify the relationship and accurately describe the buffering effect of physical activity on adolescent psychological stress, a questionnaire survey method was used to distribute 5400 questionnaires in 9 cities across the country, and a follow-up survey was conducted 8 weeks later. Results(1) Whether in a cross-sectional survey or a longitudinal follow-up, there was a significant correlation between the total score and various dimensions of physical activity, stress events, and stress symptoms in adolescents. (2) Cross-sectional surveys showed that physical activity could significantly regulate the relationship between stress events and the total score of stress symptoms, and can significantly buffer the dimensions of depression, anger, cross-nerve arousal, and muscle tension. (3) Longitudinal tracking showed that physical activity could significantly buffer the total score of stress symptoms after 8 weeks, but only significantly buffer the dimension of depression symptoms. ConclusionsThe study used a large-scale nationwide survey for the first time to reflect the relationship between physical activity, psychological stress, and stress symptoms in adolescents. The results supported the cross-stressor adaptation hypothesis and provided a reference plan for physical activity to alleviate psychological stress in adolescents.

Introduction

Adolescents are under immense pressure, and their transition from childhood to adolescence is closely related to the rapid changes in their family, school, and social environments [1]. For example, the conflicts with parents, the pressure to adapt to peers, the requirements to learn more skills to have more possibilities of obtaining future employment, and so on, all of which put enormous stress on adolescents [2]. As a result, adolescents are challenged not only by sexual and physical changes but also are affected and stressed by multiple factors [3]. In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic has created intense and unrelenting stress in the form of social isolation and uncertainty about the future for adolescents [4]. And stress is an important factor affecting their development [5]. Persistent stress can harm the individual’s health and increase the risk of disease [6]. For example, studies have shown that stress affects adolescent dietary behaviors [7] and may contribute to unhealthy diets such as poor appetite or overeating [8], and increase the risk of overweight and obesity [9]. For instance, the higher the stress level of adolescents, the poorer the quality of their meals [10], which increases the frequency of intake of fried and soft drinks and decreases the frequency of intake of fruits and vegetables, thus leading to obesity [11]. Besides, stress can lead to a range of mental health problems in adolescents, such as depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem [12]. It can make it difficult for adolescents to adapt to school life, such as poor academic performance, negative attitude towards school, and aggressive behavior [13], and even increasing the incidence of mental illness [14]. However, not all individuals experiencing psychological stress will have the same adverse symptoms that depend on the individual ability to deal with stress. Physical activity has the function of improving physical and mental health and so on and plays an important role in preventing diseases. It is a potential stress relief method [15, 16].

Literature review and hypotheses proposed

From different research perspectives, stress has different concepts, which can refer to either an external stimulus or the subjective threat perceived by the individual, as well as the physiological, behavioral response or psychological state triggered by the stimulus or threat [17]. Stress is often related to negative emotions such as anxiety and depression in teenagers [12]. For example, Lou et al. [15, 16] surveyed 2420 junior high school students to explore the relationship between stress events and stress symptoms in adolescents and showed that the more stress events adolescents experience, the higher the stress symptoms. Carter et al. [18] followed 240 adolescents for four years and showed that stress levels significantly predicted negative mood levels one year later. Jiang et al.’s [19] cross-sectional survey of adolescents also showed a significant association between stress events and life satisfaction. Witzel et al. [20] analyzed data from a sample of 2022 and found that daily stress events were detrimental to well-being regardless of family involvement. Therefore, we hypothesized that stress events can predict the onset of stress symptoms in adolescents.

The role of physical activity in relieving psychological stress-induced diseases was demonstrated early [21]. Then, the explanatory mechanism of the “cross-stressor adaptation hypothesis” was put forward [22], believing that physical activity is essentially a physical stressor, which can cause a series of physiological and psychological adjustments, to improve the ability to respond to other stimuli. In recent years, based on brain imaging and other evidence, the “endogenous reward hypothesis” was proposed [23], suggesting that physical activity can affect the reward and motivation loop of the nervous system, resulting in self-regulation and endogenous psychological stress relief. Physical activity is an effective means to relieve psychological stress, and physical activity has a significant stress buffer effect [24]. Regular physical activity has a positive effect on the central nervous system (CNS) functions, contributes to an improvement in mood and cognitive abilities [25, 26], and is correlated with an increase in the expression of the neurotrophic factors and markers of synaptic plasticity [27], as well as a reduction in the inflammatory factors [28]. Moreover, physical activity can affect the CNS by improving cellular bioenergetics, stimulating the processes responsible for removing damaged organelles and molecules and attenuating inflammation [29]. Thus, physical activity brings an important benefit: increased stress robustness [24]. There is a negative correlation between physical activity and stress [30] and a significant stress-buffering effect of physical activity [31].

Based on existing studies, hypothesis 1 was proposed: Stress events positively affect stress symptoms in Chinese adolescents. Hypothesis 2: Physical activity can regulate the effects of stress events on stress symptoms. Hypothesis 3: Physical activity buffers the intertemporal effects of stress events on stress symptoms.

Research methods

Sampling method and survey subjects

From March to April 2022, in the three economic belts in eastern, western, and central China, one city with a high GDP level, one city with a medium GDP level, and one city with low GPD level were selected from each economic belt (Eastern: Nantong, Jiangsu Province, Lishui, Zhejiang Province, Fuxin, Liaoning Province; Western: Chongqing, Yibin, Sichuan Province, Yili, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region; Central: Taiyuan, Shanxi Province, Puyang, Henan Province, Xiangxi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Hunan Province). Among the 9 cities, 1 junior high school and 1 senior high school were selected, and stratified random cluster sampling was adopted to entrust local teachers to distribute 100 paper questionnaires to each grade, totaling 5,400, 5,216 questionnaires were recovered, 221 invalid questionnaires were excluded, and 4,995 valid questionnaires were obtained, with an effective recovery rate of 95.7%. The subjects included 2,509 boys and 2,486 girls, and no gender imbalance. 826 students in Grade 1 of junior high school, 913 students in Grade 2 of junior high school, 681 students in Grade 3 of junior high school, 876 students in Grade 1 of senior high school, 847 students in Grade 2 of senior high school, 852 students in Grade 3 of senior high school, with no significant distribution difference among different grades (X2 = 9.033, p = 0.108).

After 8 weeks, the same subjects were surveyed again, among which 128 were not contacted and 73 were refused to investigate for some reason. Therefore, 4,794 questionnaires were distributed, 4,613 questionnaires were returned, 287 invalid questionnaires were excluded, and 4,326 valid questionnaires in the end, with an effective recovery rate of 93.8%. Among them, there were 2,158 boys, and 2,168 girls, with a balanced female ratio. 750 students in Grade 1 of junior high school, 870 students in Grade 2 of junior high school, 522 students in Grade 3 of junior high school, 780 students in Grade 1 of senior high school, 758 students in Grade 2 of senior high school, 646 students in Grade 3 of senior high school, with no significant distribution difference among different grades (X2 = 9.253, p = 0.099).

Survey tools

Adolescent Self-rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC)

Adolescent Self-rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) revised by Xin Xiuhong et al. [32] was used to assess the recent stress events in adolescents. There are 26 items on the checklist, including 5 dimensions (4 interpersonal stress items, 4 learning stress items, 7 punishment items, 6 loss items, and 5 adaptation items). The checklist adopts a 6-point scoring method, “0” means “never happened”, “1” means “happened but had little impact”, and “5” means “a great impact”. The higher the score is, the more seriously affected by life events. In this study, the checklist’s Cronbach’α = 0.887 (T1).

Calgary Symptoms of Stress Inventory (C-SOSI)

Calgary Symptoms of Stress Inventory (C-SOSI) compiled by Carlson [33] was used to measure adolescents’ stress symptoms. Two psychology graduate students were invited to translate the questionnaire, and then a native English student was invited to translate the Chinese questionnaire back, afterwards, two psychology professors were invited to proofread and modify the Chinese and English questionnaires to form the Chinese version of the stress symptoms questionnaire. The questionnaire includes 56 items, contains eight dimensions (8 depression items, 7 anger items, 9 cross-nerve arousal items, 6 nerve items, 8 muscle tension items, 6 cardiopulmonary items, 6 cognitive confusion items, 6 upper respiratory tract items), using a 5-point scoring method, “0” indicates “never”, “4” indicates "very frequent", the higher the score, the more serious stress symptoms. In this study, the questionnaire’s Cronbach'α = 0.934 (T1) and 0.963 (T2). A confirmatory factor analysis of the C-SOSI was performed, which showed that the results of the fitting of the model were x2/df = 1.483, CFI = 0.989, TLI = 0.985, GFI = 0.981, IFI = 0.989, RMSEA = 0.058, and SRMR = 0.016, which were in line with the criteria, indicating that the fitting of the model fit was good.

Amount of physical activity

Using the Physical Activity Level Scale [34] to measure adolescents’ physical activity level. The scale includes three items, namely the frequency of weekly physical activity (1–5), the duration of each time physical activity (0–4), usually the intensity of physical activity (1–5), the total score of 0–100, the higher the score, the greater the youth amount of physical activity. The scale’s retest reliability r = 0.82.

Statistical method

SPSS 25.0 was used to test the reliability and validity of the recovered data, Pearson Correlation was used to analyze the relationship between variables, Linear Regression was conducted to analyze the effect of stress events and amount of physical activity on stress symptoms of adolescents, and “Model 1” of Process 4.0 was used to test the moderating effect of amount of physical activity on the relationship between stress events and stress symptoms. AMOS 24.0 was used to perform cross-lagged regression analysis on the data of the two investigations (T1 and T2), and “p < 0.05” was taken as the standard of statistical significance in all the above operations.

Common method bias test

The data were tested for common method bias using the Harman single-factor test. The results show that 11 factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1 explained 45.024% of the total variance, and the first common factor explained the total variance of 24.78%, 40% below the critical value, indicating that there is no serious common method bias problem.

Analysis of the buffer moderating effects of physical activity

A simple slope test was used to further analyze the moderating effect of physical activity on stress events and stress symptoms in adolescents. The results are shown in Fig. 1. The scores of stress symptoms increased significantly with the increasing influence of stress events when the amount of physical activity was low (M-1SD). At the same time, the slope was slower (△k = −0.069) when the amount of physical activity was high (M + 1SD), which means high physical activity could significantly buffer the relationship between stress events and stress symptoms.Fig. 1Modulation of physical activity between stress events and stress symptoms

Discussion

In this study, a large nationwide survey explored the relationship between stress events and stress symptoms in adolescents, and whether physical activity can buffer the negative effects of stress events on stress symptoms in adolescents.

Cross-sectional surveys found that stress events could affect stress symptoms in adolescents, and longitudinal tracking surveys also found that stress events could significantly affect stress symptoms after 8 weeks. This is consistent with the findings of Sewart et al. [35] and Misaskowski et al. [36] on stress events and stress symptoms. A cross-sectional survey of Chinese senior high school students also showed that stress events were significantly associated with adolescent mental health, and the correlation between stressors and different dimensions of mental health varied [37]. Through a questionnaire survey of adolescents, Jiang Xiaowen et al. [38] found that stress events were positively correlated with anxiety and negatively associated with subjective happiness, and all of them were significant. McKnight et al. [39] also found a moderate negative association between stressful life events and life satisfaction. A longitudinal follow-up study conducted by Michl et al. [40] showed that stress events can predict anxiety and depression 7 months later.

Data from both cross-sectional survey and longitudinal tracking support that physical activity can buffer the effects of stress events on stress symptoms in adolescents, which is consistent with the research results of Chen et al. [41] and Sallis et al. [42] on the impact of physical activity on adolescents’ health. It also supports the stress-buffering hypothesis of physical activity [43, 44]. The stress-buffering effect of physical activity is consideredc a potentially effective means of prescription of stress-related symptoms and diseases [45]. Cross-stressor adaptation hypothesis provides a possible biological explanation for the stress-buffering effects of physical activity [46]. Physical activity causes nonspecific adaptation of the ANS and HPA axis of the autonomic nervous system, and habituation of the physiological stress system, and may lead to reduced sensitivity to subsequent homotypic (physical) and heterotypic (psychosocial) stressors [47].

When analyzing the dimensions of stress symptoms separately, the cross-sectional survey found that physical activity could buffer stress symptoms including depression symptoms, anger symptoms, cross-nerve arousal, and muscle tension. However, longitudinal tracking found that physical activity only buffer depression symptoms, and the findings of Meyer et al. [31] and Wang et al. [48] supported our result. This suggests that while physical activity is able to have a stress-buffering effect, it is not significant for most dimensions of stress symptoms after 8 weeks from intertemporal follow-up. This may be because physical activity is relatively more effective in other dimensions in improving mood and relieving depression. A meta-analysis of 62 randomized trials by Lou Hu et al. [49] showed that physical activity had a moderate effect on alleviating depression. Soares et al. [50] suggested that physical activity can promote the rapid release of extracellular vesicles into the circulation, and this release can help alleviate depression. Mechanistically, physical activity has been shown to regulate circulating levels of serotonin and norepinephrine, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, and thereby improving depression [51]. Rimmele et al. [52] showed that untrained participants experience more emotional deterioration under stress than trained participants. A literature review of 49 studies by Campbell et al. [53] also found that a significant correlation between emotional responses and other physiological responses in individuals under stress was reported in less than 30% of the literature. Therefore, the buffering of stress by physical activity may appear different among the various dimensions.

The study has some practical value, i.e., it may enable adolescents to choose to relieve themselves through moderate physical activity when they face great stress, and this may also have an ameliorative effect on their symptoms of depression, anxiety, and other negative emotions. In addition, in terms of clinical applications, some functions of physical activity (e.g., it can promote neuroplasticity, alleviate the abnormal changes in the hippocampus, prefrontal lobe, and other brain regions related to depression, and reduce the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 to improve the body’s immune system, etc.) can be used to predict the depression status of the patients and to make good preventive and therapeutic programs. Besides, this study has some limitations. Firstly, the period of the longitudinal follow-up study was set at 8 weeks due to the impact of facing the problem of missing data from some participants since adolescents entering their third year of junior or senior high school were unwilling or did not have the time to participate in the follow-up survey because of the stress of academic study, or that students in their third year of junior and senior high school would be entering a new school and might be studying in other cities and would not be able to participate in the follow-up survey as well. Therefore, future research can adopt a more comprehensive tracking method to study the physical activity and stress status of adolescents and conduct a longer-term investigation to produce better results. Secondly, in terms of selecting the sample size, the study took into account the influence of GDP on the family environment, education concepts, and education level of the youth, but other factors may have an impact on the stress events and stress symptoms faced by the adolescents, such as gender and whether they are from single-parent families, etc. Thus, future studies can also control the sample with multiple factors to produce more group-specific results and countermeasures.[54–57]

Conclusion

This study focused on the buffering effect of physical activity on adolescents’ stress. Our study suggests that physical activity can buffer the effects of stress events on stress symptoms in adolescents, and this buffering has intertemporal effects, however, current evidence only supports buffers for depression. Therefore, physical activity can be promoted on a large scale to cope with the increasing psychological stress of the adolescent group, especially to effectively alleviate depression.
