Phosphorus Fractionation and Soil Properties across Smallholder Land Uses in the South Ethiopia Regional State
Authors: Mekdes Lulu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSoil fertility decline and land degradation pose significant challenges to agricultural production and food security for smallholder farmers in South Ethiopia. The widespread deficiency and limited plant availability of phosphorus (P), often locked in less available forms despite its presence. This study aimed to examine P fractionation characteristics across different land uses, encompassing home gardens, forage land, grazing land, cropland, and woodlot in Wolaita Zone, South Ethiopia regional state. Soil samples were collected from two depths (0–20 cm and 20–40 cm) and analyzed for physicochemical properties and P fractions using the modified Hedley sequential method. The result indicated that the home gardens exhibited the highest pH, soil organic carbon (SOC), total nitrogen (TN), available phosphorus (AP), exchangeable bases (Ca, K, Na), and cation exchange capacity (CEC) across both soil depths. These higher values are attributed to intensive management, continuous organic amendment application, and diverse plant species. The analysis of soil P fractions revealed that home gardens had the highest labile P, while cropland showed higher NaOH‐Pi and residual P. The Corg:Po ratio indicated the highest values in grazing lands, suggesting potential P immobilization, and the lowest in croplands, potentially favouring mineralization. Total inorganic P (TPi) was highest in home gardens, supporting enhanced mineralization, whereas total organic P (TPo) showed significant variation influenced by management. The soil pH, SOC, TN, AP, Ca+2, and K+ were positively significant with the various P fractions, while exchangeable acidity showed an inverse relationship. Thus, home gardens demonstrated enhanced P availability and better soil fertility. The findings indicate the critical need for site‐specific nutrient management strategies to optimize phosphorus use efficiency, improve soil health, and enhance agricultural sustainability in South Ethiopia.

Introduction

Agricultural production in South Ethiopia, which is primarily sustained by intensive smallholder farming, faces persistent and critical challenges from declining soil fertility and widespread land degradation [1, 2, 3]. This issue is magnified by increasing population pressure, resulting in smaller and fragmented land sizes, and continuous, intensive cultivation without adequate nutrient replenishment. Such practices have significantly altered crucial soil physicochemical properties, leading directly to suboptimal agricultural yields and profoundly undermining the region's food security and economic stability [4].

A central and limiting aspect of this fertility decline is the prevalent deficiency and limited plant availability of phosphorus (P), a macronutrient essential for crop growth. In many highly weathered, acidic tropical soils common to Southern Ethiopia, total P may be present, but a substantial portion is chemically immobilized due to high P sorption capacity by iron (Fe) and aluminium (Al) oxides [5, 6]. Studies focusing on Southern Ethiopia have indicated that readily available and labile P fractions are very low, with most P sequestered in recalcitrant forms, thus severely limiting crop uptake despite the soil's overall P content [6]. This P immobilization is further exacerbated by low soil organic carbon (SOC) and unfavourable soil pH levels. Furthermore, the common practice of using generic fertilizer recommendations often proves ineffective due to the high spatial variability of soil properties and the removal of crop residues for alternative uses like fodder and fuel, further depleting organic matter [1, 7].

To effectively manage P limitations, a detailed understanding of the different P pools and their susceptibility to transformation is necessary. Soil P exists in various pools, categorized as inorganic (Pi) and organic (Po) forms, ranging from highly labile (readily available) to resistant (chemically stable). The distribution of these pools is highly sensitive to soil management and mineral inputs [8]. The modified Hedley sequential P fractionation procedure [9] is the established method for quantifying these pools, as it separates P into biologically meaningful fractions: Labile P (instantaneously available), Moderately Labile P (associated with Fe and Al oxides), and nonlabile/residual P (stable and generally unavailable). A comprehensive analysis of these P fractions across land uses is therefore critical for developing strategies that optimize P use efficiency and enhance agricultural productivity.

Previous research has explored soil P forms across various croplands and land‐use systems globally [10, 11]. However, despite the importance of linking management practices to nutrient status, limited specific research is available in the high‐density Wolaita Zone of Southern Ethiopia that evaluates the impacts of heterogeneous smallholder land‐use systems (such as intensively managed home gardens versus extensive grazing land) on the distribution and availability of soil P fractions [6]. A considerable knowledge deficit persists regarding how varied farmer management practices directly influence the complex P dynamics in this unique, stressed agroecosystem.

Previous regional studies often focused narrowly on croplands or general fertility parameters; this research, in contrast, directly applies the detailed Hedley sequential P fractionation procedure, a technique rarely implemented in this specific Ethiopian context. This methodology was used to delineate the distinct quantitative effects of highly contrasting farmer management practices (from nutrient‐rich home gardens to nutrient‐depleted grazing lands) on the quantitative distribution of labile, moderately labile, and stable P pools. Specifically, by quantifying fractions like Resin‐P, NaOH‐P, and the Corg:Po ratio, this work provides the first explicit link between heterogeneous management strategies and the resulting complex P cycling and immobilization risks within the region's agroecosystems. This study aimed to examine the phosphorus (P) fractionation characteristics and key physicochemical properties across integrated smallholder land‐use systems—encompassing home gardens, forage land, grazing land, cropland, and woodlots—in the ecologically unique and densely populated Wolaita Zone of South Ethiopia. The findings are expected to provide vital empirical evidence and highly specific mechanistic data necessary for formulating localized and sustainable P management recommendations to improve soil health and agricultural productivity.

Materials and Methods

Description of the Study Area

The study was conducted in the Wolaita Zone, which is part of the newly established South Ethiopia Regional State. The Wolaita Zone was selected as a representative area due to its intensive land use, high population density (averaging 357 people per square kilometre), and the dominance of the mixed crop‐livestock farming system under investigation. Wolaita Sodo, the administrative centre of the zone, is situated ∼329 km south of Addis Ababa. The study specifically focused on two kebeles (the smallest administrative units) within the Wolaita Zone: Gununo Hamus and Waja Kero (Figure 1). Gununo Hamus is situated in Damot Sore District, approximately 44 km southwest of Wolaita Sodo town. Its geographical coordinates are 37°39′0′′−37°43′0′′E and 6°55′0′′−7°5′0′′N. Waja Kero is located in Soddo Zuria District, just 5 km west of Wolaita Sodo. Its coordinates are 37°40′0′′−37°45′0′′E and 6°50′0′′−6°55′0′′N. Both study sites share similar physical characteristics, with elevations ranging from 1900 to 2100 meters above sea level. The topography is characterized by gently sloping to level land, with slopes generally less than 16%. The two districts in the Wolaita Zone were selected based on two primary criteria: capturing the geographic and management gradients of smallholder agriculture. Soddo Zuria (Waja Kero Kebele) represents the high‐pressure, peri‐urban farming system with higher access to external inputs and intense land use, while Damot Sore (Gununo Hamus Kebele) represents the more peripheral and traditional system, relying heavily on internal, organic resources. The main factor justifying this choice was the need to empirically quantify the pervasive problem of phosphorus (P) fixation and limited availability across these contrasting systems. Both districts clearly feature the characteristic soil fertility gradient—where highly‐managed, nutrient‐enriched home gardens are immediately adjacent to nutrient‐depleted croplands and grazing areas—allowing the study to isolate and compare the direct impact of different smallholder management practices on P fractionation and overall soil health. This heterogeneity was essential for formulating site‐specific nutrient management strategies.

Soil Sampling Preparation and Analysis

Soil Sampling and Compositing

The sampling sites were selected using a purposive technique based on geographical characteristics (altitude, slope, and topography). Soil samples were collected from two sites (Gununo Hamus and Waja Kero) across five smallholding land uses (home gardens, forage land, grazing land, cropland, and woodlot), at two soil depths (0−20 cm and 20−40 cm), and with three replications for phosphorus (P) fractionation. The experimental design was a randomized complete block design (RCBD). From each of the sampling points, five random subsamples were collected within a designated area, resulting in a total of sixty composite soil samples for P fractionation. All resulting composite samples were immediately transported to the Soil Laboratory of the College of Agriculture, Hawassa University.

Soil Physical and Chemical Analysis

The soil samples were air‐dried, ground, and passed through a 2 mm sieve. For the determination of SOC and total nitrogen (TN), samples were further sieved through a 0.5 mm mesh. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil–water suspension and a 1 M KCl solution [12]. Available phosphorus (AP) was quantified using the Olsen method [13], total nitrogen via the Kjeldahl method [14], and SOC using the Walkley–Black method [15]. Exchangeable basic cations (Ca2⁺, Na⁺, and K⁺) were extracted using a 1N NH4OAc solution [16]. Cation exchange capacity (CEC) was determined using the NH4⁺ saturation method [16]. Exchangeable acidity (Exc.acdy) was determined according to Rowell [17].

Soil Phosphorus (P) Fractionation Analysis

Soil phosphorus (P) fractionation was performed using the sequential extraction method of Hedley et al. [18], modified by Tiessen and Moir [9], to separate P into different pools, as illustrated in Figure 3. Additional routine soil analyses were also performed at the same facility. The soil phosphorus fractionation was conducted using the modified Hedley sequential fractionation procedure. This sequential chemical extraction method was employed to separate the total soil phosphorus into discrete pools based on their chemical bonding strength and lability. The procedure involved placing a 1 g sample of air‐dried, 2 mm‐sieved soil into a centrifuge tube and subjecting it to a series of extractants in increasing order of strength. The key steps included: (1) Labile P extraction using Resin (anion exchange membranes) and 0.5 M NaHCO3 (pH8.5), which separates the easily available inorganic (Pi) and organic (Po) pools; (2) Moderately Labile P extraction using 0.1 M NaOH, which isolates P bound to Fe and Al oxides and highly recalcitrant humic‐bound Po; and (3) Stable P extraction using 1 M HCl to obtain Ca‐bound P (apatite and other primary P minerals). After each extraction step, the inorganic P in the supernatant was immediately determined using the ascorbic acid method [19]. The organic P (Po) was determined indirectly as the difference between total P (measured after digestion of the extract) and the inorganic P (Pi) in the same extract. Finally, the Residual P (P tightly bound within the mineral structure) was quantified after digestion of the remaining soil residue with concentrated acids (H2SO4 and H2O2). The sum of all these extracted fractions constituted the Ptfr (Total Fractionated P), which was used for comparison with P determined by the total digestion (Pt dig) method. This methodical fractionation allowed for the calculation of crucial nutrient indices, including total organic P (TPo) and the Corg:Po ratio, which is critical for evaluating P mineralization and immobilization risks across the different land‐use systems.

Hedley phosphorous sequential fractionation scheme as modified by Tiessen and Moir [9] (for determination of total P and inorganic P).

Statistical Data Analysis

The data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) by SAS (statistical analysis system) version 9.4 software packages [20]. Mean separation for significant differences was conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) test, while correlation analyses estimated the relationships between P fractionation parameters and selected soil physicochemical properties.

Soil total nitrogen (N) was found to be higher in home gardens, grazing lands, and woodlots compared with cropland. This observation can be attributed to the diverse management practices and ecological interactions within these land use types. Home gardens, often characterized by integrated crop–livestock systems and the incorporation of organic amendments like compost and green manure, enhance nutrient cycling and improve soil health [26]. In contrast, cropland, dominated by monoculture practices and reliant on synthetic fertilizers, can experience nitrogen depletion over time due to inadequate replenishment of soil organic matter [27].

The significant variation in AP levels across different land uses, with the strong influence of agricultural practices and land management on soil nutrient dynamics. Home gardens enhance nutrient retention and availability in the soil compared with more extensive agricultural or unmanaged lands, where nutrient depletion can occur through crop removal and leaching [28]. These need nutrient management strategies to optimize P use efficiency across various land use systems, thereby mitigating environmental impacts and improving soil fertility [29].

Soil exchangeable bases (Ca+2, K+, and Na+) were higher in home gardens compared with other land uses. This can be attributed to the organic amendments and sustainable practices employed in home gardens, which enhance nutrient availability and improve soil structure [30]. On the other hand, the higher Exc.acdy observed in croplands may indicate increased nutrient leaching and soil depletion resulting from intensive agricultural practices and the potential overuse of fertilizers, which can lead to soil acidification [31].

CEC is a crucial soil property that reflects its ability to retain and exchange essential plant nutrients. The significant variation in CEC across land uses, with home gardens exhibiting higher values due to the strong influence of management practices on soil health. Moreover, home gardens could be due to intensive cultivation and incorporation of organic amendments such as compost or mulch, which improve soil structure and fertility by increasing the availability of cation‐exchanging sites [32].

Comparison of Land Use on Soil Physicochemical Properties Across Different Soil Depths

The analysis of soil physicochemical properties across two different soil depths (0–20 cm and 20–40 cm) in the study sites reveals significant variations influenced by different land use practices. Constantly, home garden land use has the most favourable soil health indicators, showing the highest values for soil fertility parameters across both superficial and deeper soil horizons. The pH values were highest in home gardens across both depths. This near‐neutral to slightly acidic pH range is optimal for nutrient availability and microbial activity [36]. This can be attributed to the continuous replenishment of organic materials such as compost and animal manure, which buffer soil acidity and enhance soil fertility, a practice commonly observed in traditional home garden systems [37]. Similarly, TN and SOC, crucial indicators of soil organic matter content and soil health, reached their highest concentrations in home gardens at both depths. In the 0–20 cm soil depths, GH home garden is, with WK home garden showing similar high levels. While concentrations naturally decreased with depth, home gardens still maintained higher levels in the 20–40 cm layer. This sustained high organic matter content is a direct consequence of diverse biomass inputs, minimal tillage, and efficient nutrient recycling within these agroforestry systems, which promotes carbon sequestration and nitrogen retention in the soil profile [38].

AP also showed its highest values in home gardens. The 0–20 cm layer of home gardens had the highest AP for both GH and WK, which, though slightly lower, remained highest at 20–40 cm in both sites. This higher availability of phosphorus, a vital macronutrient for plant growth, is likely due to the continuous mineralization of organic matter and potentially less fixation in the near‐neutral pH conditions of home gardens compared with more acidic soils [39]. These high levels indicate a healthy supply of nutrients crucial for plant vigour and are typically associated with fertile soils that receive adequate organic inputs [40].

The CEC, a key indicator of soil's nutrient‐holding capacity, was also generally higher in home gardens across both depths, correlating well with their highest organic matter content. The cropland at Gununo Hamus exhibited exceptionally high CEC values at 0–20 cm, which is given its comparatively lower organic matter and basic cation content. This suggests that in the cropland, specific clay mineralogy might play a more dominant role in contributing to CEC than organic matter, or it could indicate accumulation of certain clay types, though its lower nutrient levels suggest reduced actual fertility compared with home gardens.

Effects of Land Use and Soil Depths on the Total Soil Phosphorus Fractionation

The results presented in Table 5 show the mean values of phosphorus (P) fractions and related soil properties across different land uses and soil depths (0–20 cm and 20–40 cm). TPi contains the sum of resin P, NaHCO3‐Pi, NaOH‐Pi, and H2SO4‐Pi concentrations (Table 5). TPo is the sum of NaHCO3‐Po, NaOH‐Po, and HCl‐Po concentrations (Table 5). The TPi concentrations ranged from 347.22 mg kg−1 in home gardens at a 0–20 cm depth to 144.72 mg kg−1 in cropland at a depth of 20–40 cm, while TPo levels varied from 137.11 mg kg−1 in cropland at 0–20 cm to 69.25 mg kg−1 in home gardens at 20–40 cm (Table 5). The high concentrations of TPi and TPo in the top 0–20 cm layer are indicative of intensive management and high nutrient inputs. This finding aligns with the research of Ahmad et al. [41], who demonstrated that home gardens often accumulate higher levels of P due to the consistent addition of compost, manure, and kitchen waste. This practice effectively enriches the soil's organic matter pool, as reflected by the high total soil organic carbon (TSOC = 27,800 mg⋅kg−1), which in turn enhances P retention and availability. In contrast, the Grazing land use shows the lowest overall P content and the highest Corg:Po ratio (364). This suggests that phosphorus is a limiting factor in this system relative to carbon. As noted by Deng et al. [42], the conversion of natural ecosystems to pastures often leads to a decline in soil P, particularly the labile fractions like Resin Pi (0.63 mg⋅kg−1). Nutrient removal through grazing animals, coupled with soil compaction, can disrupt natural P cycling and reduce nutrient availability.

The TPi consistently showed higher levels in home gardens due to organic matter decomposition, which enhances mineralization. The TPi values were lower than those found in other studies in Ethiopian soils, and this difference may show regional variations in soil management [6, 43].

TPo were significantly influenced by the application of agricultural practices. The study by Zhang [44] highlights the significant role that organic amendments, such as crop residues and organic matter, play in enhancing TPo levels in cropland. By incorporating these materials into the soil, management practices can influence phosphorus dynamics, improving its availability for plant uptake. This review underscores the importance of understanding how different organic amendments affect soil phosphorus availability, which is crucial for optimizing agricultural productivity and sustainability. Effective management of phosphorus through organic amendments can lead to better soil health and increased crop yields. Total P (Pt) concentrations—derived from the summation of P fractions and extracted using perchloric acid (HClO4) digestion—were significantly influenced by land use. Highest total P concentrations were found in home gardens and croplands at a 0–20 cm depth, surpassing those detected in grazing lands and woodlots (Table 5).

The result, as presented in Table 5, shows a decrease in all P fractions and total SOC with increasing soil depth. For all land uses, the 0–20 cm topsoil layer contains significantly higher nutrient levels than the 20–40 cm subsoil. This trend is a fundamental principle of soil science. Gonfa et al. [45] reinforced this observation, finding that key soil properties like organic matter and AP are concentrated in the topsoil where the majority of biological activity, root growth, and decomposition of plant litter occur. The presence of organic matter in the topsoil is crucial for P retention and cycling, as it provides binding sites for P and fuels the microbial activity that mineralizes organic P forms.

Distribution and Dynamics of Soil Phosphorus Fractions

Land use significantly influenced the distribution of phosphorus (P) fractions in this study, as presented in Table 4. The statistical analysis revealed that land use had a significant influence on most soil phosphorus fractions and related properties. Specifically, for Home Garden, Forage Land, Grazing Land, Cropland, and Woodlot, there was a significant effect (p < 0.05) on Resin Pi, NaHCO3‐Pi, NaOH Pi, HCl Pi, H2 SO4Pi, Ptfr, TPo, Pt dig, TPi, and Corg:Po. The NaHCO3‐Po, NaOH‐Po, and HCl‐Po showed a highly significant effect (p < 0.01) across all land uses, highlighting the profound impact of management on the soil's organic P reserves. Both Gununo Hamus and Waja Kero sites showed a significant effect (p < 0.05) on resin Pi, NaOH Pi, HCl Pi, H2SO4Pi, Ptfr, TPo, Pt dig, TPi, and Corg:Po. Additionally, a highly significant effect (p < 0.01) was observed for NaHCO3‐Pi, NaHCO3‐Po, NaOH‐Po, and HCl‐Po across both sites, indicating that local geological and climatic variations also modulate the expression of P fractionation. Soil depth (0−20 cm vs. 20−40 cm) consistently exerted a significant influence on the distribution of phosphorus fractions. For all land uses (home garden, forage land, grazing land, cropland, and woodlot), depth had a significant effect (p < 0.05) on Resin Pi, NaOH Pi, HCl Pi, H2SO4Pi, Ptfr, TPo, Pt dig, TPi, and Corg:Po. A highly significant effect (p < 0.01) of depth was also observed for NaHCO3‐Pi, NaHCO3‐Po, NaOH‐Po, and HCl‐Po across all land uses, confirming that P is strongly stratified and accumulates in the nutrient‐rich surface layer. The consistent and highly significant influence of management and depth highlights that P dynamics are highly controllable through land‐use decisions.

Labile P, often considered plant‐available P, encompasses both inorganic (Pi) and organic (Po) fractions readily utilized by plants. The highest labile P fractions were detected in home gardens at the 0−20 cm soil depth compared with cropland, grazing land, and woodlot (Table 5). Higher NaOH‐Pi and Po concentration was found in cropland at the 0−20 cm depth, differing from levels found in home gardens, grazing lands, and woodlots (Table 4). The highest concentrations of HCl Po were recorded in home gardens at the 0−20 cm depth compared with other land uses (Table 5). Higher residual P concentrations were observed in cropland at the 0−20 cm depth compared with other land uses (Table 5). These contrasting patterns demonstrate a direct trade‐off: intensive organic management (home gardens) maximizes biologically active and labile P pools, whereas intensive cultivation/fertilization (Cropland) favours the accumulation of fixed P pools. This study revealed significant variations in labile P concentrations across different land uses, following the order: home garden>crop land>grazing land>woodlot. The higher labile P levels in home gardens can be attributed to the frequent, diverse application of organic amendments, as evidenced by the survey data, which enhance P availability through microbial decomposition [11]. Conversely, the lower labile P in cropland, despite external inputs, may reflect insufficient P input relative to crop P uptake, potentially due to limited fertilizer applications by farmers. The negligible labile P in woodlots and grazing lands likely stems from the absence of significant mineral and organic fertilizer inputs, relying solely on slow natural recycling. Interestingly, the labile P fraction exhibited a relatively higher proportion of organic P (Po) compared with inorganic P (Pi), particularly in the home gardens, potentially due to continuous applications of organic amendments, enriching the soil with organic P over time, which serves as a long‐term buffer against P deficiency [46]. This is a key indicator of P‐sustainable management.

Moderately labile P (NaOH‐P), associated with P sorbed to Fe and Al oxides, was higher in cropland. This is strongly linked to lower soil pH in cropland (Table 5), creating acidic conditions that favour P fixation by these crystalline and amorphous oxides [47]. This fixation process reduces the efficiency of applied fertilizers and limits plant access to an otherwise medium‐term reserve. Effective crop rotation practices, particularly the inclusion of legumes, can enhance P mobilization in the rhizosphere through organic acid exudation and potentially mitigate P fixation. The interplay between acidic conditions and the presence of crop residues suggests a complex relationship between soil chemistry and P availability, where P is being actively sequestered into less available forms. In contrast, the lower moderately labile P in home gardens may be attributed to higher available P levels and pH, which compete with P for fixation sites or reduce P fixation by Fe and Al oxides [48].

Stable Po (HCl Po, nonlabile P), associated with organic P bound to primary minerals was more pronounced in home gardens, reflecting higher total organic matter content and stability in these long‐term, structurally sound systems (Table 5). Conversely, the lower stable organic P in cropland highlights the potential negative impacts of reduced organic matter inputs and accelerated decomposition under intensive agricultural practices [49]. This observation underscores the critical importance of maintaining high organic inputs for improving soil P dynamics and ensuring long‐term fertility resilience. The stable organic P represents the most resistant P reservoir, acting as the backbone of the soil P capital.

Residual P (H2SO4Pi), a recalcitrant and largely insoluble fraction, was highest in cropland, suggesting a potential build‐up of highly fixed or occluded P over time due to the continued application of mineral fertilizers (Table 5). The transformation of different P pools within the soil system significantly influences P dynamics and availability for plant uptake (Ahmed et al., 2019). The negative correlation observed between soil pH and residual P strongly suggests that lower pH conditions enhance P fixation into these inaccessible mineral forms, effectively wasting a significant portion of the applied fertilizer. Managing soil pH in cropland is therefore a critical step towards unlocking this fixed P and improving fertilizer efficiency.

The Corg:Po ratio varied significantly across land uses and depths in the study sites (Table 5). Grazing lands exhibited the highest ratios in both depths, suggesting potential phosphorus immobilization due to high carbon inputs (slowly decomposing biomass) relative to available organic phosphorus, possibly exacerbated by biomass removal [29, 50]. This high ratio indicates microbial competition for the limited available P, which can lead to P‐limited microbial activity and subsequently reduced nutrient cycling. In contrast, croplands showed the lowest ratios, which may indicate accelerated organic phosphorus mineralization in the topsoil due to intensive cultivation and lower organic matter content [11, 38]. Home gardens and woodlots displayed intermediate ratios, suggesting a more balanced carbon and phosphorus input and cycling in these systems [31, 32]. Understanding these land‐use specific Corg:Po dynamics is vital for effective phosphorus management and sustainable soil fertility in the region, as it dictates whether a system is a net mineralizer or immobilizer of organic P. Thus, the varying Corg:Po ratios across land uses highlight the distinct impacts of different management practices on soil phosphorus dynamics. Grazing lands appear to be more prone to phosphorus limitation for microbial activity, while croplands may experience accelerated organic phosphorus mineralization in the topsoil, with implications for long‐term phosphorus availability [10, 51]. Sustainable P management must therefore focus on strategies that maintain the Corg:Po ratio near the equilibrium point of ∼200 to optimize the organic P cycling.

The correlation analysis reveals significant relationships between various soil properties and phosphorus (P) pools, as presented in Table 6. The SOC, calcium shows strong positive correlations with the more labile and moderately labile inorganic P fractions. Similarly, the soil pH with show strong positive correlations with the Resin Pi (r = 0.94∗∗∗), TN with NaHCO− 3, Po (r = 0.96∗∗∗), SOC with Resin Pi (r = 0.97∗∗∗), and K+ with TPi (r = 0.91∗∗∗). This indicates that as these soil fertility indicators increase, the availability and overall quantity of various P pools also tend to increase, suggesting that these properties play a crucial role in regulating P solubility and retention in the soil [52, 53]. Specifically, the strong positive correlation of pH with many P fractions, particularly HCl Pi (r = 0.95∗∗∗), which often represents P bound to calcium, aligns with the understanding that in circum‐neutral to alkaline soils, P tends to form less soluble calcium phosphates [54]. Similarly, the strong positive correlations of SOC, TN, and AP with multiple P pools highlight the importance of organic matter and nutrient cycling in maintaining a healthy P status in soils [55, 56].

The effect of Exc.acdy on NaOH Pi and NaOH Po, however, was not significant, suggesting different underlying mechanisms for P retention in these pools under varying acidity. Interestingly, Corg:Po, a ratio reflecting the balance between organic carbon and organic phosphorus, shows significant positive correlations with pH, TN, AP, SOC, Ca2+, and K+ (Corg:Po with TN (r = 0.89∗∗∗). This suggests that a higher proportion of organic carbon relative to organic phosphorus is associated with more favourable soil conditions, potentially indicating a greater capacity for P mineralization from organic matter or a higher accumulation of recalcitrant organic P forms in healthier soils [59].

The CEC displayed a distinct correlation pattern. It showed strong positive correlations with NaOH Pi (r = 0.93∗∗∗) and NaOH Po (r = 0.86∗∗), which are typically associated with P bound to amorphous iron and aluminium oxides. However, CEC had largely nonsignificant or weak negative correlations with other P fractions like resin Pi and HCl Pi. This indicates that while CEC might play a role in retaining P through adsorption to active sites on clay minerals and organic matter, its influence on readily available P or calcium‐bound P is less direct compared with its effect on oxide‐bound P fractions [60]. The nonsignificant correlation of CEC with Ptfr and TPi further supports this nuanced relationship, suggesting that its impact is more pronounced on specific P pools rather than the overall P status.

Conclusion

This study successfully assessed the impact of diverse land use practices on soil physicochemical properties and phosphorus (P) fractionation dynamics within smallholder farming systems in South Ethiopia, providing vital empirical evidence for sustainable nutrient management. The findings consistently demonstrate that home garden systems maintain the most favourable soil health profile. These systems exhibited significantly higher levels of SOC, TN, AP, exchangeable bases (Ca2+, K+, Na+), and CEC across both the 0−20 cm and 20−40 cm depths. These superior fertility indicators are a direct result of intensive, integrated management practices, characterized by continuous organic matter inputs, high plant diversity, and efficient on‐farm nutrient recycling.

The phosphorus fractionation analysis elucidated the fate of P under these management regimes: Home Gardens accumulated the highest concentrations of labile P forms (resin and NaHCO3‐P), indicating the highest immediate P availability for plant uptake. On the other hand, Croplands showed distinct P dynamics, with higher concentrations of NaOH‐Pi and residual P, suggesting a greater proportion of P is being fixed into moderately labile or highly recalcitrant forms, likely due to lower pH and intensive cultivation stress. Finally, grazing lands exhibited the highest Corg:Po ratios (up to ∼360), implying a higher risk of P immobilization and microbial P limitation. In summary, the results highlight the Home Garden system as a successful model for enhancing soil fertility and P availability in the region. The strong link between favourable soil properties (e.g., higher pH and SOC) and beneficial P accumulation suggests that management strategies should target improving overall soil health to unlock P reserves. This research provides critical information for developing site‐specific, sustainable land management strategies that can optimize phosphorus use efficiency, mitigate soil degradation, and significantly contribute to improved food security for smallholder farmers in South Ethiopia.
