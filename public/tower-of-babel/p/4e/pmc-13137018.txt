Open repair after failed endovascular treatment of a visceral arterial aneurysm: The Mattox maneuver
Authors: Rocío Garralda Díaz de Lope-Díaz, Esteban Martín Antona, Manuel Hernando Rydings, María Sánchez Cantalejo-Ferrer, Isaac Martínez López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visceral arteries aneurysms are not common, and evidence regarding multilayer flow modulator stents remains limited. We present the case of a 48-year-old man with aneurysm sac enlargement of a visceral arterial aneurysm arising from a common trunk for the celiac trunk and superior mesenteric artery, previously treated in another institution with a multilayer flow modulator stent. Open repair was performed, including stent explantation and anatomical reconstruction with a venous bypass. The Mattox maneuver proved to be an effective approach for open repair of the visceral arteries.

Visceral arterial aneurysms (VAAs) are relatively uncommon, with an estimated prevalence approximately 0.1% to 2.0%.1 They are often diagnosed incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated reasons, yet rupture can be lethal, with mortality rates reported as high as 70%.2

Contemporary guidelines support endovascular treatment as the preferred first-line strategy.3 Several techniques have been described, with the final choice depending on the aneurysm's morphology and vascular anatomy. Overall, open surgery is associated with higher postoperative complication rates and long-term morbidity4; however, it remains necessary in selected cases where endovascular repair is not feasible or has failed.

In the early 2000s, the multilayer flow modulator stent (MFM) was introduced with the main goal of converting the turbulent flow into laminar flow, thereby promoting sac thrombosis. Unlike covered stents, MFMs were designed to maintain perfusion of the collaterals and potentially decrease ischemic complications.5 Originally developed for intracranial wide-necked saccular aneurysms,6,7 their use expanded to peripheral and VAA.8

We present the case of a 48-year-old man with a VAA arising from a common celiac trunk (CT)-superior mesenteric artery (SMA) who presented with sac enlargement after MFM implantation at another center. The objective of this report was to demonstrate the safety of open repair using the Mattox maneuver when endovascular repair has failed.

Case report

A 48-year-old man was referred to our institution for a second opinion after first postoperative imaging in March 2024 demonstrated sac enlargement of a VAA after endovascular treatment performed in January 2024. He had initially been offered ultrasound-guided thrombin injection at the referring center. The interval between the postoperative computed tomography angiography and definitive open surgical repair was related to the interhospital transfer process and the time required for preoperative anesthesiology assessment.

The patient was an active smoker and hypertensive, which were the only relevant cardiovascular risk factors. He initially presented with a 32-mm VAA arising from a rare anatomical variant consisting of a common origin of the CT and SMA. The first surgery was performed by a vascular interventional radiologist using a MFM, deployed from the common trunk to the CT, jailing the SMA. The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary committee, where open repair combined with the Mattox maneuver was considered the most appropriate strategy for a safe dissection, explantation, and anatomical reconstruction.

Surgery was performed in June 2024 under general anesthesia in collaboration with the general surgery team. Through a midline laparotomy, a Mattox maneuver (Fig 2), or left medial visceral rotation, provided exposure of the visceral aorta, with supramesocolic control of the common trunk and inframesocolic control of the CT and SMA (Fig 3). After clamping, the aneurysm sac was opened and the MFM stent was explanted without complications. Anatomical reconstruction of the SMA was performed using a reversed great saphenous vein graft harvested from the left lower limb, with an end-to-end anastomosis. The CT was reimplanted onto the vein graft in an end-to-side anastomosis (Fig 4). Distal pulse and bowel perfusion were satisfactory. Hemostasis and closure proceeded without incident. An infectious etiology was considered owing to the rapid aneurysm growth after stent placement, but it was ruled out based on negative intraoperative cultures. Discussion

The MFM was originally designed for the endovascular treatment of intracranial aneurysms, but later expanded as an alternative to the custom-made fenestrated or branched devices for thoracoabdominal aneurysms owing to its several advantages, including a wide range of sizes, easy deployment, and theoretical preservation of side branch flow.

Their mechanism of action is based in three different phases: the hemodynamic phase, consisting in flow reduction; the thrombus phase, consisting of progressive sac thrombosis; and the endothelization phase, preventing the MFM from migrating.

The literature reports conflicting results regarding MFMs. Ruffino et al9 reported 100% technical success in 54 patients with peripheral and visceral aneurysms, with a mean diameter reduction of 11% at 12 months. Rabuffi et al10 also reported favorable early results in a small cohort of six patients.

However, subsequent studies have reported higher than expected rates of persistent sac perfusion, need for reintervention, and even rupture during follow-up.11,12 Özdemir-van Brunschot et al13 carried out a retrospective study about all patients treated in their center by implantation of MFM between 2013 and 2020. They included 37 patients, with a technical success of 97.8%. During follow-up, they observed that 68.5% of the aneurysms had persistent flow with a risk of rupture of 2.1%; therefore, they emphasized the need for long-term data.

Contemporary guidelines for the VAA continue to recommend endovascular repair as first-line therapy. Nevertheless, treatment selection must be individualized, taking into account the anatomical features of the VAA such as location, morphology, branch involvement, and landing zones to decide the best endovascular treatment available.14,15

In some cases, open repair is preferable, particularly when reintervention is required or anatomy is unsuitable for safe endovascular management.16 Although open surgery is associated with higher morbidity and mortality rates,17 this is largely attributable to emergency presentations.18

The Mattox maneuver, first described by Dr Kenneth L. Mattox for exposure of the left retroperitoneum in the abdominal trauma,19 provides access to the supramesocolic aorta when needed. Centers with experience report favorable results when performing open surgical repair for VAAs not amenable to endovascular repair.20 Bilman et al21 reported 15 visceral artery reconstructions in the mid and distal portions of the renal arteries with 100% technical success and no procedure-related mortality. Only one asymptomatic bypass occlusion occurred during follow-up.

Published data on the use of the Mattox maneuver in elective vascular surgery remain limited. Our institution has developed substantial experience with this technique for the repair of abdominal aneurysms involving visceral or renal arteries, with satisfactory initial results. This maneuver should be considered in cases where supramesocolic access is required and wider exposure and control of the visceral and renal arteries is needed.

Conclusions

Open repair should be considered in cases of complex anatomy or failed endovascular interventions to ensure a durable and effective solution. The Mattox maneuver provides excellent supramesocolic and inframesocolic exposure of the visceral aorta and represents a valuable approach in selected cases.
