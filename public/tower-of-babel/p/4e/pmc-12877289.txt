Protein source associated with risk of metabolic syndrome in adults with low and adequate protein intake: A prospective cohort study of middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Jieun Lyu, Joong-Yeon Lim, Yerim Han, Khuhee Na, Seungyoun Jung, Yoon Jung Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMetabolic syndrome is associated with an increased risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality. Some evidence suggests that the cardiometabolic health benefits of protein intake may vary by the source (animal or plant); however, the evidence is inconsistent. This study aimed to assess the risk of developing metabolic syndrome according to the protein source.ParticipantsAmong a total of 3,310 participants aged 40 years or older in the Ansan and Ansung population based prospective cohort, 1,543 incident cases of metabolic syndrome were identified between 2007 and 2018.MeasurementsDietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) by quintile (Q), adjusting for demographics and health-related lifestyle factors.ResultsHigher intake of animal protein (HRquintile5 (Q5) vs quintile1 (Q1) [95% CIs]: 0.76 [0.59−0.96], P-trend ≤ 0.0307) and a higher relative intake of animal protein (HRQ5 vs.Q1: 0.78 [0.64−0.95], P-trend ≤ 0.0017) were associated with a significantly decreased risk of developing metabolic syndrome. In subgroup analyses, associations between the risk of metabolic syndrome and the relative intake of animal and plant protein differed according to whether the total protein intake was within the recommended nutrient intake (RNI). Specifically, significant associations were observed only among those with a total protein consumption below the RNI (HRQ5vs Q1 [95% CIs]: 0.72 [0.56−0.93] for the relative intake of animal protein), but not among those consuming above the RNI. This association was more significant in women than in men.ConclusionA higher absolute and relative intake of animal protein were associated with a significantly decreased risk of metabolic syndrome, particularly among those who consumed less than the RNI of protein.Keywords: Animal protein, Plant protein, Total protein intake, Recommended nutritional intake, Metabolic syndrome, Cohort study

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome, characterized by a constellation of metabolic abnormalities including abdominal obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance, is a significant public health challenge because of its strong association with an increased risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality [1,2]. Furthermore, the prevalence of metabolic syndrome has increased rapidly worldwide during the preceding decades [3,4]. In Korea, metabolic syndrome affects at least 23% of adults and 50% of older adults aged 65 years and older [5].

Diet is a well-known risk factors for metabolic syndrome [6]. In recent years, attention has focused on the potential benefit of high-protein diets for the prevention and management of metabolic syndrome owing to the involvement of protein in glucose and lipid metabolism [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. However, epidemiologic evidence is inconsistent. Some studies have reported the inverse association between protein intake and risk of metabolic conditions, including weight reduction and maintenance [7], blood sugar levels [8], and lipoprotein profiles [[9], [10], [11]], whereas other prospective studies have shown variable results associated with high protein intake, including increased mortality [12], weight gain [13], and no effect on the risk of cardiovascular disease [14].

Previous studies on the health benefits and harms of a high-protein diet have had varying results. One possible reason could be differences in protein intake from different sources. Amino acid composition of protein varies depending on whether the protein originates from animal or plant sources [15]. Historically, animal proteins were considered more nutritious than plant proteins, which often have imbalanced amino acid profiles [16]. Previous research has shown that animal protein intake has beneficial effects of on some components of metabolic syndrome [17,18]. However, recent studies have increasingly demonstrated the benefits of plant protein [19,20]. For example, some cohort studies have reported that an increased intake of animal protein, but not plant protein, is associated with long-term weight gain [19,20]. Similarly, a comprehensive review of cohort studies and randomized controlled trials on the effects of dietary protein sources on metabolic condition concluded that plant protein, consumption may have a positive effect on the blood lipid profile [21] and overall health outcomes (especially cardiovascular outcomes) [22], compared with that of animal proteins. Notably, the associations often related to specific components of metabolic syndrome and most of the studies reviewed were cross-sectional studies.

To date, the association between protein intake, including plant and animal protein intake, and the risk of metabolic syndrome among Koreans has been examined primarily using cross-sectional studies [[23], [24], [25]]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to prospectively evaluate the potential benefits of protein intake on the risk of metabolic syndrome according to it source (plant or animal) using data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES). Specifically, we examined the association between the relative ratio of the protein source and the risk of metabolic syndrome. Additionally, we investigated whether this association varied based on the adherence to the recommended nutrient intake (RNI) for protein, given the potential effect of total protein intake on the risk of metabolic syndrome [26].

Methods

Study design and study participants

This large-scale prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Ansan and Ansung cohort within the KoGES cohort [27]. Briefly, the Ansan and Ansung community-based cohort study was initiated in 2001 when 10,030 residents were recruited from an industrialized community in Ansan and a rural area in Ansung in Korea. The participants were followed up every 2 years. At baseline and biennial follow-ups, data were collected on sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, family history, and lifestyle behaviors through interviews using structured questionnaires and standardized protocols.

This analysis used Ansan and Ansung cohort data collected during follow-ups to the end 2018. Participants who met the following criteria were excluded: those who did not attend any follow-up visits (n = 2,806), had metabolic syndrome at baseline (n = 1,289), or did not provide data on covariates (n = 118). No participants were excluded owing to an implausible energy intake (<500 or >5000 kcal/day). After the exclusions, a total of 3,310 Korean adults (1,653 men and 1,657 women) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (2018/No 2018-03-05-P-A). All participants provided written informed consent.

Flow diagram of the study participants.

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was assessed by trained staff using a validated 106 food-item, food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). The design and validation of the FFQ has been described in detail elsewhere [28]. The frequency of consumption was classified into nine categories: never or seldom, once a month, 2–3 times a month, 1–2 times a week, 3–4 times a week, 5–6 times a week, once a day, twice a day, and three times or more a day. The portion size of each food item was classified into three categories: small, medium, and large. The average daily intake of each nutrient was estimated by multiplying the reported frequency of each food item by its nutrient content per portion, using a food composition table developed by the Korean Nutrition Society [29], and then summing across all foods. For the analyses of protein sources, protein sources were classified into plant and animal categories. Plant protein sources included grains and grain products, potatoes and starches, legumes, vegetables, fruits, seaweed, nuts, and seeds. Animal protein sources included meat, fish, shellfish, processed and salted fish, eggs, milk, and dairy products (Supplementary Table S1) [25]. Plant and animal protein intakes were estimated by summing the protein intake from each source (the absolute amounts), and the percentage of protein from plant and animal sources relative to total protein intake was calculated by dividing the intake of each by the total protein intake (the relative amounts). The FFQ has been shown to have good validity in assessing protein intake, with a correlation coefficient of 0.43 for the correlation between total protein intake measured using the FFQ and 12-day dietary records [30]. To determine the criteria for low and high protein intake, the protein intake was divided into two groups based on whether it was within or below the Korean RNI specified in the 2020 Korean Dietary Reference Intake [31].

Definition of metabolic syndrome

Metabolic syndrome was ascertained based on the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) criteria [32,33]. Participants were required to satisfy at least three of the following five components to be classified as having metabolic syndrome: (1) a waist circumference of 90 cm or more in men or 80 cm in women; (2) a blood pressure of 130/85 mmHg or more, or taking antihypertensive medicine; (3) a triglyceride (TG) level or 150 mg/dL or more; (4) a high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) level of less than 40 mg/dL in men or 50 mg/dL in women; and (5) or a fasting glucose level of 100 mg/dL or more or taking an antidiabetic drug.

Anthropometric and blood tests were performed by trained staff using standardized protocols. Height and weight were measured using a stadiometer and scale, respectively. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the ribs and iliac crest. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height(m) squared. Blood pressure was measured twice in the arm using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer (Baumanometer, Baum Co., USA) with the participant was seated after having rested for at least 5 min. The average of the two the two systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings were used for the analysis. Blood samples were collected from each participant at baseline and every 2 years of follow-up after an 8-h fast, and serum concentrations of TG, HDL-C, and fasting glucose were analyzed using enzymatic methods (ADVIA 1650, Siemens, USA).

Other covariates

An interviewer administered structured questionnaire was used to obtain information on demographic (age and sex) and lifestyle variables of participants. Based on responses to the questionnaire, smoking status was grouped into “never-smokers” for those who had never smoked cigarettes or smoked less than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, “former-smokers” for those who smoked 100 cigarettes or more in their lifetime but were not current smokers, and “current-smokers” for those who had smoked 100 cigarettes or more in lifetime and were current smokers. Alcohol consumption status was classified as “never-drinker” for those who had never consumed any types of alcoholic beverages in their lifetime, “former-drinker” for those who had consumed alcoholic beverages in their lifetime but were not current drinkers, and “current-drinker” for those who had consumed alcoholic beverages more than once per month during the past year. For physical activity, participants who answered “yes” to the question “Do you exercise regularly enough to sweat? (yes/no)” were asked a follow-up question about average frequency and duration of activity per week.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study participants were presented as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and numbers (percentages, %) for categorical variables. Differences in population characteristics were tested using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent samples t-tests for continuous variables. The number of person-years of follow-up was calculated from the date of the baseline assessment to the date of metabolic syndrome diagnosis or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) by quintile (Q) of protein intake, including absolute and relative intake of plant and animal protein, for risk of metabolic syndrome overall and stratified by sex, using Q1 as the reference category. Unadjusted and adjusted estimates were calculated. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age (years, continuous) and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status (never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker), alcohol consumption (never drinker, former drinker, or current drinker), regular physical activity (yes or no), and total energy intake (kcal/day, continuous). In addition, Model 3 was adjusted for total protein intake (g/day, continuous) in the analyses of plant protein and animal protein intake. Tests for linear trend were conducted by including the median values for each quintile of protein intake as a continuous variable in the model. The Wald test of the product between the protein intake and each stratification factor was used to test for effect modification. All data analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at a P value less than 0.05.

Results

During a mean of 8.6 years of follow-up (28319 person-years), 1,543 (46.6%) participants (709 men [42.9%] and 834 women [50.3%]) developed metabolic syndrome. At baseline, the mean age of study population were 54.3 years and the mean total protein intake was 57.1 g/day, of which 69.7% was from plant protein sources and 31.6% was from animal protein sources (Table 1). Of the participants 62.5% reporting never smoking, 45.0% reported never drinking alcohol and 39.3% reported exercising regularly. Several characteristics, including smoking status and alcohol consumption differed significantly by sex. In addition, men consumed more energy (kcal/d), carbohydrate, protein, plant protein, animal protein, and fat than women (all P < 0.001). Similarly, the relative intake of animal protein was higher in men than in women (P = 0.0054), although plant protein intake did not differ significantly by sex.

In the analyses of absolute protein intake (Table 2), a higher animal protein intake was associated with a significantly lower risk of developing metabolic syndrome. The multivariable-adjusted HR comparing the highest vs. the lowest Q (Q5 vs. Q1) of animal protein intake was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.59–0.96; P for trend = 0.0307). Total protein intake and plant protein intake were not significantly associated with the risk of developing metabolic syndrome. Furthermore, an analysis of the risk of metabolic syndrome based on protein intake per kilogram of body weight revealed that a higher protein intake was associated with a significantly lower risk of developing metabolic syndrome. This association remained consistent in the analyses of plant and animal protein intake per kilogram of body weight (data not shown).

In the analyses of the relative intake of plant and animal protein, expressed as the plant and animal protein intake as a percentage of the total protein intake (Table 3), a higher relative intake of plant protein was associated with a significantly increased risk of developing metabolic syndrome (multivariable-adjusted HR quintile5 (Q5) vs. quintile1 (Q1) [95% CIs]: 1.28 [1.06−1.56]; P for trend = 0.0017), whereas a higher relative intake of animal protein was associated with a significantly decreased risk of developing metabolic syndrome (multivariable-adjusted HR Q5vs.Q1 [95% CIs]: 0.78 [0.64−0.95]; P for trend = 0.0017). These results were consistent among women (multivariable-adjusted HR Q5vs.Q1: 1.31 [1.01−1.71] for the relative intake of plant protein and 0.76 [0.59−0.99] for the relative intake of animal protein; both P for trend = 0.0120), but among not among men. Neither the relative plant protein intake nor the relative animal protein intake were significantly associated with the risk of developing metabolic syndrome among men.

In stratified analyses, different associations were observed according to whether protein intake was below or above the RNI of total protein (Table 4). Among those with below the RNI of protein, a higher relative intake of animal protein was associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome (multivariable-adjusted HR Q5vs.Q1[95% CI]: 0.72 [0.56–0.93]; P for trend = 0.0037). However, a higher relative intake of animal protein was not associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome among those who consumed total protein above the RNI. Similarly, in analyses stratified by the RNI among women, in those with below the RNI of protein intake, a higher relative intake of animal protein was associated with a decreased risk of metabolic syndrome (multivariable-adjusted HR Q5vs.Q1 [95% CIs]: 0.62 [0.42−0.92]; P for trend = 0.0179) were only evident among those consuming below the RNI, but not among those consuming above the RNI. In contrast, in men the relative intake of animal protein was not associated with risk of metabolic syndrome, regardless of the protein intake relative to the RNI. No significant effect modification was detected by other factors including age, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, and regular physical activity (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

In this study, the absolute and relative intake of animal protein were inversely associated with a lower risk of metabolic syndrome, which was more marked in women than in men. Furthermore, in women these associations were stronger among those who consumed less than the RNI of protein.

To date, four cross-sectional studies [17,18,25,34], two cohort studies [35,36] and one case-control study [37] have examined the association between sources of protein and risk of metabolic syndrome in several regions in Asia [17,18,25,34], Australia [35], North America [36], and Europe [37]. Our findings of an inverse association between animal protein intake and risk of metabolic syndrome are consistent with those of three of cross-sectional studies [17,18,34]. For example, a cross-sectional study of 7,585 Japanese adults found that higher animal protein intake, but not higher plant protein intake, was associated with a lower blood pressure [17]. Similarly, in a cross-sectional study of 2,241 Chinese women, a higher protein intake was associated with a significantly lower blood pressure [18]. In contrast, a cross-sectional study of middle-aged Korean men suggested that low animal protein intake might be beneficial in managing metabolic syndrome [25]. Moreover, in studies conducted in Western populations, animal protein intake has been associated with adverse effects on other metabolic syndrome risk factors [[35], [36], [37]]. For example, in a cohort study conducted in Melbourne, Australia, higher plant protein consumption and lower animal protein consumption was associated with lower risk of metabolic syndrome [35]. Similarly, a cohort study with a 16-year follow-up period conducted in the United States found that a higher intake of plant protein was associated with a lower cardiovascular disease mortality [36]. Additionally, a Swedish study found that higher meat consumption was associated with higher cholesterol levels [37].

The reason for the inconsistent associations between animal protein intake and the risk of metabolic syndrome is unclear. However, substantial differences exist in the protein composition and sources of food protein consumed among participants in the Western countries and this study. For example, in the studies conducted in the United States [36] and Australia [35] participants received 60–62% of their protein intake from animal sources, whereas participants in this study received 69.7% of their protein from plant sources and 31.6% from animal sources. Given that the red meat, processed meat, and other major sources of saturated fat, a risk factor for metabolic syndrome [38] contribute more to the animal protein consumption in Western countries than in the Korean population [39], the discrepancy in the results could be due to the effect of other nutrients in different animal protein sources [40], influenced by cultural differences in diet.

Another possible explanation for the observed differences in the results for the association between animal protein intake and the risk of metabolic syndrome, could be the interaction between animal protein intake and total protein intake. A recent study found that the amount of protein, rather than the protein source, had a stronger effect on cardiac metabolic health indicators, including blood glucose, serum insulin, and serum TG [41]. It underscores the adequacy of total protein on metabolic condition and greater contribution of animal protein addressing deficiencies in total protein over the plant protein. Indeed, supporting our speculation, when we conducted analyses stratified by protein intake according to the RNI in the present study, higher animal protein intake was associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome among those who consumed less than the RNI. This finding is consistent with the results of previous studies with comparable total protein. For example, participants in the Chinese study, that found a lower risk of metabolic syndrome with higher animal protein intake, the total protein intake was relatively low, (51.7 g/d in men and 40.1 g/d in women) [18], which is approximately 60.3% of the total protein intake reported in Western countries [35]. In the cross-sectional study conducted in Korean [25] which did not find an in inverse association between protein intake and the risk of metabolic syndrome, the participants had a relatively high average protein intake of 87.6 g in men and 63.3 g in women, compared with was 60.3 g in men and 53.9 g in women in this study. In a study conducted in Iran, in which the participants consumed a diet composed primarily of carbohydrates, a higher frequency of consumption of total protein, animal protein, and vegetable protein was associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome [34].

Plant protein consumption has certain beneficial properties in preventing metabolic stress, including being associated with a diet that is low in saturated fat and rich in fiber and phytochemicals, which have antioxidant properties [42]. However, animal proteins contain all the essential amino acids required by the body and are more efficient in supporting muscle growth and repair especially when total protein intake is insufficient [43,44]. Specifically, branched-chain amino acids, such as leucine, are crucial not only for muscle protein synthesis and maintaining lean body mass [45], but also for metabolic regulation [46], which increases insulin sensitivity and energy expenditure. Branched-chain amino acids are provided mainly by animal protein intake [[47], [48], [49]].

The results of the study showed that the association between the relative ratio of protein type and metabolic syndrome risk was more marked in women, than in men. Although the reason for this sex difference remains unclear, some hormones have sex-specific effects on glucose and lipid metabolism, which are associated with the development of metabolic syndrome [50,51]. Women might be more sensitive than men to the effects of diet on the development of metabolic. For example, a previous study showed that, a higher dietary protein/energy ratio was associated with a lower risk of obesity in older women but not in older men [52]. Alternatively, men have higher rates of drinking and smoking, which are risk factors for the development of metabolic syndrome. Despite our adjustment for smoking and alcohol consumption, some residual confounding may be present owing to these sex-related differences in lifestyle. Further studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms underlying the interaction between protein intake and sex in modifying the risk of metabolic syndrome.

This study has several limitations. First, the analyses relied on reported food item data listed in the FFQ, which might not have fully captured all the plant-based and animal-based foods consumed by the participants. Nonetheless, the FFQ is an established instrument for assessing usual intake of foods and nutrients, and has previously been validated by comparing the estimated protein intake with that estimated based on 12-day dietary records (correlation r: 0.43) [30]. Second, certain foods, such as pizza, hamburgers, jam, honey, butter, and margarine, could not be exclusively classified as plant- or animal-based food, posing challenges in classifying the type of protein consumption. Third, the analysis was based on baseline data, and we did not take into account changes in dietary behavior, food availability, or lifestyle habits during the follow-up period. Fourth, the study participants were recruited from a specific region (Ansan and Ansung) in Korea and were aged 40 years or older, limiting the generalizability of the results to the broader general Korean population. Finally, we cannot rule out the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding.

Nevertheless, this study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first large prospective cohort study to examine the risk of metabolic syndrome in relation to type of protein consumed in Korean adults. Owing to its large sample size, prospective design, and long-term follow-up the data are less susceptible to recall bias. In particular, we focused on the association of the relative intake animal and plant of protein with the risk of metabolic syndrome, rather than the absolute intake. This approach may provide the advantage of examining the effect of the protein source in the overall diet, instead of looking at absolute intake level of each source. Additionally, we were able to finely adjust for potential dietary and lifestyle confounders and performed explored stratified analyses to control for effect modification.

Conclusions

In summary, this study found that animal protein intake may be associated with a lower risk of metabolic syndrome in the Korean population. This association was stronger in participants with an animal protein consumption below the RNI. Additionally, compared with men, in women, a higher relative consumption of plant proteins was associated with a greater increase in the risk of metabolic syndrome, whereas consumption of animal protein was associated with a greater decrease in the risk. Plant and animal protein intake was not associated with a significantly increased risk of metabolic syndrome in men. The results of this study suggest that Korean adults, especially middle-aged and older women who rely heavily on plant protein, may be at risk of metabolic syndrome due to inadequate protein intake from animal sources; therefore, the importance of adequate protein intake from animal sources to reduce the risk of metabolic syndrome should be emphasized among those at risk of nutritional deficiency. Further a large prospective study of a representative sample of the Korean population, covering a wider age range, is required to validate our results.
