Effect of tobacco–radish rotation for different years on bacterial wilt and rhizosphere microbial communities
Authors: Yuhao Dai, Jixiu Li, Zhenzhen Wang, Shaoqi Yang, Qingju Xiao, Zipeng Gao, Fengjing Zhang, Chenran Zhao, Liang Yang, Shaopeng Chen, Wei Ding
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTobacco bacterial wilt is a major limiting factor for tobacco production and development, and it is more likely to occur under perennial single cropping of tobacco. In recent years, the rotation of tobacco-radish has gradually become popular. Therefore, we studied the effects of years of tobacco–radish rotation on tobacco bacterial wilt occurrence and rhizosphere microorganisms. The results indicated that both SY and TY could significantly reduce the risk of tobacco bacterial wilt occurrence, and SY had the lowest disease index. The rotation of radish plants significantly increased the soil pH but decreased the contents of alkali-hydrolysed nitrogen and organic matter in the soil. Alkali-hydrolysed nitrogen and pH are the key factors affecting the composition of the bacterial community. Furthermore, radish rotation changed the composition of the soil microbial community, increased the diversity of the bacterial community, and significantly altered the bacterial community structure. At the genus level, the abundance of Sphingomonas species negatively correlated with Ralstonia increased significantly, while the relative abundance of Rhodanobacter species positively correlated with Ralstonia decreased significantly. Disease index, pH and available phosphorus were the main factors affecting the variation in different bacterial genera. The network analysis results showed that Ralstonia was less connected in the network than in the CK group, and the SY treatment group had a more complex bacterial network structure. Overall, 2 years of tobacco and radish rotation improved the bacterial community structure of the rhizosphere soil and alleviated the harm caused by tobacco bacterial wilt, which is highly important for the stability and health of the rhizosphere soil ecosystem.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-024-01760-x.Keywords: Tobacco–radish rotation, Bacterial wilt disease, Improved soil acidification, Rhizosphere microorganisms

Introduction

Different plants shape specific core rhizosphere microbial communities even when they grow in the same soil. However, long-term net cultivation of a single plant variety often leads to the accumulation of plant pathogens in the soil, which destroys and reduces the complexity of underground microbial communities, leading to reduced crop yield (Huang et al. 2022; Mariotte et al. 2018). Tobacco bacterial wilt is a devastating soil-borne disease caused by Ralstonia solanacearum, and diseased soil and residues are the main causes of tobacco bacterial wilt (Ameen 2021), which can easily occur under long-term continuous cropping (Kawasaki et al. 2007). The common measures used to control soil-borne diseases, including tobacco bacterial wilt, mainly include soil chemical fumigation and physical disinfection (Sarkar and Chaudhuri 2016). Although traditional control methods can quickly kill soil-borne pathogens in a short period of time, they can also reduce the diversity of the soil microbial community and cannot be maintained for long periods (Zhang et al. 2017b). In recent years, facing outbreaks of soil-borne diseases, green control methods such as resistant cultivar cultivation, biocontrol agents and crop rotation have attracted increasing amounts of attention. Moreover, we are looking forward to finding a natural material/product as an alternative disease control strategy (Singh et al. 2020).

The regulation of soil microbial communities is considered to one of the strategies for enhancing soil health and achieving sustainable disease management (Kinkel et al. 2011). For example, crop rotation is used to maintain the rich array of plant species in the ecosystem (Larkin 2015), thereby reducing pathogen stress on plants via improving the diversity and structure of rhizosphere microorganisms (Cook 2006; D’Acunto et al. 2018). Studies have proven that a reasonable crop rotation pattern can coordinate soil nutrients (Jagadamma et al. 2008), optimize soil chemical properties, enhance the circulation and cooperation of soil microorganisms (Ben Abdallah et al. 2023), and reduce the occurrence of soil-borne diseases (Huang et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2023; Niu et al. 2017a; Yang et al. 2018). For example, three-year radish green manure rotation had a cumulative effect on the soil inhibition of tomato-related pathogens, and tilting radish significantly reduced the occurrence of tomato bacterial wilt (Ben Abdallah et al. 2023). Moreover, another study revealed that radish green manure increased the number of beneficial microorganisms in soil and reduced the number of pathogens in continuously cropped soil (Wang et al. 2010). Furthermore, rotating radish green manure reduced the number of soilborne pathogens (Larkin and Griffin 2007).

The mechanism by which overturning green fertilizer inhibits soil-borne pathogens has been proposed. One is the release of isothiocyanate and other compounds that have direct inhibitory effects on pathogenic microorganisms during the decomposition of Brassica plant residues, thus reducing the number of pathogens. However, compared with those in other brassicas (mustard rape and Brassica napus), only very low levels of isothiocyanate volatile gases were detected in the soil treated with turnip green fertilizer (Hansen and Keinath 2013). Therefore, we are more supportive of another hypothesis, which is that overturning after crop rotation increases the abundance of beneficial microorganisms in plants and decreases the relative abundance of soil pathogens, thus lowering the occurrence of disease (Oka et al. 2007; Wei et al. 2019a). Furthermore, The growing body of research has consistently demonstrated that changes in soil microbial community composition, especially rhizosphere microbial community composition (Liu et al. 2016; Roy et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2019), are closely related to the occurrence of bacterial wilt (Ji et al. 2008; She et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2017; Wei et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2017; Yin et al. 2013; Zheng et al. 2019). Previous studies have shown that Pseudomonas, Bacillus megaterium, Enterobacter, Sphingomonas, Bacillus brevis, Bacillus, Actinospica, Catenulispora, Haliangium and Lysobacter have good antagonistic effects on R. solanacearum (Ahmed et al. 2022; Bora and Bora 2008; Hu et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2012; Nguyen and Ranamukhaarachchi 2010; Ramesh and Phadke 2012; Santiago et al. 2015). However, the changes in these pathogens and beneficial bacteria under different planting systems and the relationships between them remain to be studied.

Ecological networks represent interactions between coexisting organisms that can reveal community responses to environmental changes (Liu et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). For example, studies have demonstrated that ecological networks composed of strong interactions are more stable than are ecological networks with weak interactions and that the presence of separations and interactions increases the stability of the network under disturbance (Wang et al. 2021). The use of co-occurrence network analysis can reveal the relationships between microbial communities under different cropping regimes, but our understanding of the symbiosis or potential interactions within intricate soil microbial communities as well as their ability to respond to and recover from disturbances such as pathogens, remains limited.

Therefore, we used 16 S amplicon sequencing technology to carry out a three-year site-point experiment in Qianjiang, Chongqing, and systematically analysed the soil physicochemical properties, bacterial community composition and Ralstonia abundance under different tobacco–radish crop rotation years. The aim of this paper was to identify the effect of tobacco–radish rotation for different years on soil physical and chemical properties, bacterial group characteristics and the relative abundance of Ralstonia to provide theoretical guidance for implementing suitable crop rotation systems.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experiment design

Daikon Radish (Raphanus sativus ‘Spring White Jade’).

Tobacco: Cultivated variety K326.

The experiment started in August 2020, and tobacco continuous cropping plots ( ten years ) were selected. Four fields with similar soil types (Slightly acidic loam and uncultivated croplands), disease severities and agricultural management systems were selected. Each experimental field was drawn as four cell repeating areas.

The experiment involved four treatments and sixteen cell replicates:

T1: Tobacco-Radish rotation for three years - FY;

T2: Tobacco-Radish rotation for two years - TY;

T3: Tobacco-Radish rotation for one year - FY;

T4:Continuous tobacco monoculture for three years - CK;

After picking and roasting in the tobacco field, the radish plants in the fertilized field were planted in the tobacco ridge (4,000–5,000 plants per acre), and according to the proportion of four radish plants around one tobacco plant(Tobacco plants with 0.6 m apart and radish plants are 0.3 m apart), the radish plants were planted in the smoke ridge in the late harvesting period (when the tobacco leaves were in the second shed of flue-cured tobacco). Two or three radish seeds were planted in each hole to ensure that one radish seed survived per hole. The radish and the above-ground tissue of the radish plants that did not meet the commercial selling specifications (approximately 10% of the radish plants) were turned over and pressed into the soil during the tobacco ridging period.

Soil samples were collected in the third year of the experiment.

Disease investigation, Soil sample collection and treatment

The tobacco boom has investigated tobacco disease, according to the national standard “Classification and Investigation Methods of Tobacco Pests and Diseases” (GB/T 23222 − 2008).

Soil physical and chemical property samples were collected during tobacco transplanting period and were dried through 2 mm sieve for reserve use.Soil rhizosphere samples were collected during the tobacco boom. Five tobacco plants were randomly selected from each sample plot. The soil layer of 0–5 dm was removed with a sterilized soil shovel, and the soil of 0–0.2 cm in the tobacco rhizosphere and 1–2 cm in the root circumference were collected. The soil remove the roots, plant and animal residues, stones and other debris were mixed evenly and quickly put into sterile self-sealing bags. It was temporarily stored in the ice box at 4 ℃, and then brought back to the laboratory and stored in the refrigerator at -80 ℃ for soil DNA extraction.

Measurement items and methods

Determination of soil physical and chemical properties

The electrode method determines the pH, the acidified potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7-H2SO4) method determines soil organic matter (OM) content, The alkaline hydrolysis diffusion method determines soil available nitrogen, The Olsen method determines soil available phosphorus content, soil available potassium was extracted with ammonium acetate and then quantified using atomic absorption spectrophotometry.

Extraction of soil microbial DNA

The total DNA of the Soil microbial community was extracted according to the Power Soil® DNA kit(Axygen), purified and detected by agarose gel (1%) electrophoresis. After staining with C21H12BrN3, the DNA was detected by gel imaging system.After DNA extraction, PCR amplification of V4 ~ V5 region of bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was performed using primers 515 F (5 ‘-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG-3’) and 907R (5 ‘-CCGT⁃CAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3’). The amplification steps were as follows: predenaturation at 95 ℃ for 2 min, denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s, extension at 72 ℃ for 30 s, cycling 25 times, and final extension at 72 ℃ for 5 min. Each sample was repeated 3 times, and the PCR mixture of the same sample was detected by agarose gel (2%) electrophoresis, after which the amplicon was recovered. According to the instructions of Axy-Prep DNA Gel Recovery Kit (Axygen), the PCR products were purified, detected, and quantified, and then sent to Shanghai Megi Biological Company for high-throughput sequencing.

According to the barcode sequence corresponding to the sample, the effective sequence of the corresponding sample was obtained. Use FLASH software (v1.2.7, http://ccb.jhu.edu/ftware/FLASH/) splicing sequence. QIIME2 (2018.2, Quantitative Insight Into Microbial Ecology) software was used to invoke DADA2 to check and eliminate the chimera sequence. A table of amplicon sequence variant ASV(amplicon sequence variant) was constructed for the sequence obtained above, and ASV representative sequence was obtained [DADA2 clustering with 100% similarity (de-duplication), and each de-duplication sequence generated after DADA2 quality control was called ASVs]. Will get ASVs SILVA on behalf of the sequence in the database (http://greengenes.secondgenome.com/, Release 138) J91 annotate and to obtain its classification information. All samples in the ASVs abundance matrix were randomly resampling at the lowest sequencing depth level of 95% to correct for diversity differences caused by sequencing depth. QIME 2 software was then used to calculate the diversity index for each sample separately.

Data analysis

One-way ANOVA were performed using SPSS Satistics software (v17.0) for calculated disease variance. The “vegan” package of R software was used in ADONIS analysis to explore the influence of different grouping factors on sample differences, and the displacement test was used to conduct a significant analysis of the statistical significance of the division. The “vegan” package of R software is used to analyze principal component analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance algorithm at the level. redundancy analysis (RDA) and Manteltest correlation heat maps of bacterial communities and physicochemical parameters. Boxplot, stacked bar chart and PCoA chart were all made by GraphPad Prism8.0.2 software, Vane chart was analyzed and drawn by R software “Venn” package, and network attributes were calculated by Rstudio “igraph” and “WGCNA”. The Gephi software was used to visualize the co-occurrence network at the ASV level. In order to reduce the network complexity, the bacteria genera whose relative abundance proportion was ≥ 01% and were present in ≥ 50% of the samples were retained. Spearman’s correlation coefficient r was ≥ 0.6, and the significance P < 0.05. Rstudio “corrplot” was used to carry out the autocorrelation matrix of different bacterial genera.

The effect of tobacco–radish rotation for different years on the occurrence of tobacco bacterial wilt

As shown in Fig. 1a, compared with CK, tobacco–radish rotation in different years reduced the incidence of diseases, but the effect of rotation year on the incidence of tobacco bacterial wilt differed. The incidence and disease index of tobacco bacterial wilt were significantly lower for SY (second year in the winter rotation) and TY (third year in the winter rotation) (Fig. 1). During the tobacco harvesting period, the disease indices of SY and TY were 5.61 and 6.78, respectively, and that of CK was 10.28 (Fig. 1b).

The effect of tobacco–radish rotation for different years on bacterial community composition

Alpha diversity analysis of the microbial communities in the tobacco rhizosphere soil after different years of tobacco–radish rotation treatment (Supplementary Table S1) showed that the Ace and Chao1 indices of the FY and SY treatments were significantly greater than CK treatment, indicating that tobacco–radish rotation in different years improved the bacterial community richness in the tobacco rhizosphere soil. The Shannon and Simpson indices were similar, indicating that there was no significant difference in the biodiversity index between the bacterial communities of the tobacco–radish rotation treatments and the control. The results indicated that the bacterial community richness improved after years of tobacco–radish rotation.

The effect of tobacco–radish rotation for different years on the soil bacterial community structure were further investigated via Bray‒Curtis distance principal coordinate analysis (Fig. 2a). PC1 explained 29.52% of the bacterial components, while PC2 explained 11.32% of the bacterial components, for a total of 40.84%. Compared with the CK, different years of tobacco–radish groups were significantly separated. The distance between the FY sample and CK was smaller, followed by that of SY. However, the distance between the SY and TY treatments and the control sample points was great, and the soil β diversity significantly differed among the different years of tobacco–radish treatment (R = 0.4609, P = 0.001). This suggests that radish–tobacco crop rotation in different years may be an important factor leading to differences in soil bacterial communities.

The effect of tobacco–radish rotation for different years on tobacco rhizosphere bacteria

As shown in Fig. 3a, the relative abundance of Sphingomonas increased to a certain extent after tobacco–radish rotation for different years, and TY increased the most, which was significantly greater than that of CK. Moreover, the relative abundance of Rhodanobacter decreased after different years of tobacco–radish rotation treatment (Fig. 3d). SY and TY were significantly lower than those in the CK. In addition, compared with the control, the SY treatment also significantly increased the relative abundance of norank_f__Gemmatimonadaceae, unclassified_f__Comamonadaceae and other genera (Fig. 3b, c). The relative abundance of Ralstonia after tobacco–radish rotation treatment in different years was measured, and the relative abundance of Ralstonia in the tobacco rhizosphere of the SY treatment was the lowest (0.038%) and that of the control (0.248%) which almostly decreased 84.7%.

The different bacterial genera and the abundance of Lauerella were used in the autocorrelation matrix, and the results showed that the abundances of the differential bacteria Sphingomonas, norank_f__Gemmatimonadaceae, and unclassified_f__Comamonadaceae were negatively correlated with the abundance of Ralstonia, with R value of -0.17, -044, and − 0.18, respectively. Rhodanobacter abundance was positively correlated with Ralstonia abundance, and the correlation coefficient was 0.28. Moreover, the abundances of Sphingomonas, norank_f__Gemmatimonadaceae, and unclassified_f__Comamonadaceae were significantly negatively correlated with the abundance of Rhodanobacter (-0.60) (Fig. 3f). These results indicated that changes in the abundance of bacteria in the tobacco rhizosphere after different years of tobacco–radish rotation may also affect the relative abundance of Ralstonia in the tobacco rhizosphere.

Network topology analysis of the soil microbial community under different years of tobacco–radish rotation

The coexistence network of the bacterial communities significantly differed among the different treatments. The number of nodes and edges of the bacterial communities in the SY treatment was greater than that in the CK treatment. The ratio of positive correlation to negative correlation in the TY soil was the highest, reaching 19.88. The average connectivity of the FY and TY treatments was greater than that of the control. The above results showed that the interaction between soil bacteria under the CK treatment was more complex than that under the CK treatment (Spplementary Table S2). The modularity of each treatment was greater than 0.44, while that of the CK treatment was lower than that of the SY and TY treatments, indicating that all the treatments reached a certain degree of modularity. These results indicated that tobacco–radish rotation treatment improved the soil bacterial community to a certain extent and enhanced the disease resistance of tobacco rhizosphere microorganisms.

According to the co-occurrence network diagram, the root bacterial communities of tobacco and radish crop rotation treatments in different years exhibited different modules, among which the module with the highest connectivity was the first module (the purple module in Fig. 4). In the FY network diagram, there were 21 nodes in the first module, 14 of which were not identified. The identified key node microorganisms belonged to the genera Nocardioides, Terrabacter, Streptomyces, Phycicoccus, Stenotrophomonas, Sphingomonas, Massilia, etc. Ralstonia was in the grey module with the lowest connectivity (bottom right of Fig. 4). In the SY network diagram, the key node microorganisms identified in the first module belonged to Sphingomonas, Nitrospira, Pseudolabrys, Mle1-7, Bacillus, Rhizorhapis, Bryobacter, Pseudonocardia, Candidatus, P. aenarthrobacter, Subgroup, Gaiell and Tumebacillus. In the TY network diagram, the key node microorganisms identified in the first module belongs to Sphingomonas, Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium, Ensife, Blastococcus, Sphingobium and Bradyr hizobium, Pseudolabrys, RB41, Lysobacter, mle1-7, Mesorhizobium, Gaiella, etc. The node microorganisms belonging to Ralstonia did not appear in the SY and TY co-occurrence network. In the CK network diagram, the key node microorganisms identified in the first module were Ralstonia, Nocardioides, Roseisolibacter, Novosphingobium, Nocardia, RB41, Sphingomonas, Micromonospora and Gaiell, Terrabacter, Leifsonia, Rhodanobacter and other bacteria. These results indicated that tobacco–radish rotation treatment reduced the influence of Ralstonia on the bacterial community, thereby reducing the disease resistance pressure on tobacco rhizosphere microorganisms.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that tobacco bacterial wilt can be reduced by rotation of tobacco and radish. The disease index was the lowest after two years of tobacco–radish rotation, followed by radish rotation for three years. Furthermore, 16 S amplicon sequencing revealed that the relative abundance of Ralstonia was higher in the CK than in the SY and TY.

It is well known that deteriorating soil physical properties lead to the decline in tobacco yields (Xiao et al. 2024).But rotation can improve the supply of soil nutrients, which may be related to changes in soil enzyme activity, thus alleviating the continuous cropping barriers(Liu et al. 2024). Our results revealed the changing trend of available nutrients in continuous cropping tobacco field with the different years of tobacco-radish rotation. The tobacco-radish rotation increased the content of pH, OP, AK in soil, the results of post-test showed that the difference of pH was significant (P < 0.05).However, the content of AN and OM was significantly decreased, The tobacco-radish rotation system yields more crops per unit of land area, which to some extent increases the pressure on the soil nutrient supply, to some extent, this nutrient uptake pressure was magnified with the increase of rotation years. In the process of tobacco-radish rotation, it is necessary to pay attention to the supplement of nitrogen fertilizer and organic matter, this is conducive to the formation of a more stable soil nutrient status to ensure the growth of subsequent plant needs.

Soil microbial diversity plays a crucial role in supporting multiple ecosystem functions (Wu et al. 2019). Our results showed that crop rotation significantly increased bacterial community richness in different years. The PCoA analysis showed that the treated sample groups were significantly separated, with FY being closer to CK, followed by SY, and TY being further from the control sample point, showing a trend towards greater differences between the bacterial communities and the control with increasing crop rotation years. The composition of the bacterial community was the same as that of the CK, but the abundance of bacteria differed. The relative abundance of Firmicutes in the FY, SY and TY treatments was greater than that in the tobacco monocropping treatment. Studies have shown that the relative abundances of Firmicutes, Chlorobi and Zygomycota decrease in soils that are susceptible to bacterial wilt, while in soils with disease inhibition properties, the relative abundances of those bacterial phyla rised (Gong et al. 2024). The relative abundance of Ralstonia decreased after different years of tobacco–radish rotation, while the relative abundance of Sphingomonas increased in the FY, SY, and TY treatments increased the most, which was significantly greater than that in the CK. In this study, Sphingomonas tended to increase in soil with reduced disease (Zhang et al. 2017a). Moreover, the differences between the bacterial genera norank_f__Gemmatimonadaceae and raul’s species were negatively correlated, which could be due to the inhibitory action of potential bacterial wilt. At present, large amounts of sphingosine have been proven to increase plant growth and improve plant resistance. The results showed that the tobacco- radish rotation was beneficial to the formation of microbial community, which decreased the relative abundance of pathogenic bacteria, and increased the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria, etc. At the same time, with the increase of tobacco-radish rotation years, this“Effect” was amplified.

The coexistence network can be used to study interactions between microorganisms, thus helping us to understand the response of microbial communities to external disturbances (de Vries et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2019b; Yi et al. 2023). The analysis of the coexistence network revealed that Ralstonia appeared in the CK network (purple module) and TY network (grey module) but not in the SY and TY networks. Moreover, compared with those in the control group, Streptomyces appeared in the first module of the FY network, Bacillus and Tumebacillus appeared in the first module of the SY network, while no bacterial genera were found in the first module of the CK network. The results showed that tobacco antagonism was more important in the SY network. The number of nodes, variables, and connectivity of the SY network were greater than those of the CK network, and tobacco–radish rotation was conducive to the formation of a more stable network structure in soil, which enhanced the resistance of rhizosphere microorganisms to disturbances. The results showed that tobacco-radish rotation was beneficial to the formation of a more complex bacterial community network, and enhanced the “Communication” and “Cooperation” of soil microorganisms, rotation also reduces the “Importance” of pathogens in the network and increases the role of beneficial bacteria in the network, which may also be responsible for the reduction of disease.

Changes in soil chemical conditions affect the structural composition of soil microorganisms to a certain extent (Zheng et al. 2023). Previously reported that soil acidification can reduce the abundance of carbon- and nitrogen-metabolizing bacteria such as Nitrospirae, Firmicutes, Chlorobi and Zygomycota, which are prone to bacterial wilt (Liu et al. 2022; Niu et al. 2017b; Shen et al. 2018).These effects are believed to be due to its nitrogen fixation, phosphate dissolution and plant growth hormone production functions (Asaf et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2020), and Sphingomonas have also been developed into antagonistic agents for the prevention and control of tobacco bacterial wilt (Li et al. 2021), which is consistent with the conclusions reached in this study. Changes in bacterial community composition and structure are mainly driven by changes in soil properties (Zhang et al. 2017a). The results of the RDA showed that pH and alkali-hydrolysed nitrogen were the main reasons for the difference in the bacterial community composition between the tobacco and radish rotation treatments and the control. The results of correlation analysis showed that the change of soil physical and chemical properties may be the important reason for the increase of beneficial bacteria genius and the decrease of pathogen pressure.

In summary, the tobacco-radish rotation can improve the physical and chemical properties of soil, reshape the soil bacterial community, enrich the beneficial microorganisms in the soil rhizosphere, reduce the number of pathogenic bacteria in soil, and alleviate the occurrence of tobacco bacterial wilt (Fig. 6).
