Towards the genome-scale discovery of bivariate monotonic classifiers
Authors: Océane Fourquet, Martin S. Krejca, Carola Doerr, Benno Schwikowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBivariate monotonic classifiers (BMCs) are based on pairs of input features. Like many other models used for machine learning, they can capture nonlinear patterns in high-dimensional data. At the same time, they are simple and easy to interpret. Until now, the use of BMCs on a genome scale was hampered by the high computational complexity of the search for pairs of features with a high leave-one-out performance estimate.ResultsWe introduce the fastBMC algorithm, which drastically speeds up the identification of BMCs. The algorithm is based on a mathematical bound for the BMC performance estimate while maintaining optimality. We show empirically that fastBMC speeds up the computation by a factor of at least 15 already for a small number of features, compared to the traditional approach. For two of the three smaller biomedical datasets that we consider here, the resulting possibility of considering much larger sets of features translates into significantly improved classification performance. As an example of the high degree of interpretability of BMCs, we discuss a straightforward interpretation of a BMC glioblastoma survival predictor, an immediate novel biomedical hypothesis, options for biomedical validation, and treatment implications. In addition, we study the performance of fastBMC on a larger and well-known breast cancer dataset, validating the benefits of the BMCs for biomarker identification and biomedical hypothesis generation.ConclusionfastBMC enables the rapid construction of robust and interpretable ensemble models using BMC, facilitating the discovery of gene pairs predictive of relevant phenotypes and their interaction in that context.AvailabilityWe provide the first open-source implementation for learning BMCs, a Python implementation of fastBMC in particular, and Python code to reproduce the fastBMC results on real and simulated data in this paper, at https://github.com/oceanefrqt/fastBMC.

Introduction

High-throughput RNA sequencing, together with ever-larger public cohorts, has fueled the hope that molecular measurements obtained at diagnosis can drive precision medicine, [1, 2]. Supervised machine learning (ML) techniques are indispensable for this agenda because they capture subtle multivariate signatures that escape simpler statistics. However, the price is often opacity: As dimensionality increases, most ML models quickly become black boxes whose decision logic is inaccessible to domain scientists [3]. Opacity is not merely a philosophical inconvenience—in translational biomedicine, it blocks mechanistic hypothesis generation, slows or prevents experimental validation, and hampers clinical adoption.

We highlight three concrete interpretability obstacles that appear when a classifier relies on dozens, hundreds, or thousands of molecular features: Cognitive overload Even if a model with a large number of features can be visualized (for example, via t-SNE or SHAP), biologists must mentally integrate the information associated with all features to assess the relevance of a particular item. Monotonicity is not an arbitrary constraint: in many biological contexts, risk or phenotype is thought to change in a single direction as transcript levels rise or fall. Requiring the decision surface to be monotone in each gene [i] excludes pathological zigzag boundaries, [ii] facilitates interpretability in the context of external knowledge, and [iii] reduces the hypothesis space, facilitating learning. Conceptually, the monotonicity assumption generalizes the TSP idea (a rank-based monotone rule on two genes) from ordinal to continuous data while maintaining cognitive simplicity. Each decision boundary is a two-dimensional monotonic function (cf. Fig. 1 below); even an ensemble of BMCs remains intelligible because each component can be independently inspected or discarded.

Although BMCs can be viewed as a specialized form of decision tree, they occupy an unusual corner of the bias-variance spectrum. By restricting every rule to exactly two features and imposing monotonicity, BMCs achieve a rare combination of statistical power (the model can still capture nonlinear interactions) and direct interpretability (the decision surface is a single step in 2D). Moreover, unlike generic tree-induction heuristics, each two-gene BMC can be constructed optimally with Stout’s dynamic programming algorithm for isotonic regression, whose complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta (n log_2(n))$$\end{document} is a first important reason why exhaustive genome-scale searches now become feasible even on modest hardware.

The original naïveBMC algorithm [10] uses Stout’s algorithm and already demonstrated biological utility on a dengue cohort, but is still computationally costly to apply on a genome-scale. Here we present fastBMC, a mathematically sound pre-selection scheme that prunes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge \!97\%$$\end{document} of the search space while preserving optimality. The resulting speed-up (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge \!15\times $$\end{document} on three clinical cohorts) enables genome-scale searches and can uncover biologically compelling pairs, such as SDC4/NDUFA4L2 in glioblastoma (section “Interpretability, hypothesis generation, validation options, and treatment implications”) that would likely not be identified using standard classification approaches.

Contribution This paper provides three key contributions. First, it introduces a Preselection Algorithm that drastically accelerates the identification of high-performing BMCs (Algorithm 1), based on a lower bound of the BMCs estimate (Theorem 1), producing a new version of naïveBMC called fastBMC. Second, it provides a reassessment of the efficacy and contributions of BMCs and ensembleBMC for biomedical research. Third, with an open-source implementation of fastBMC, it provides the first open-source implementation to computer BMCs.

Bivariate monotonic classifiers and the naïveBMC approach

Bivariate monotonic classifiers (BMCs) are based on general monotonic functions as decision boundaries [11]. BMCs operate on continuous features to predict binary outcomes. In [10], BMCs were used to classify transcriptomic patient data to predict the severity of dengue infections from blood transcriptomes in Cambodian dengue patients.

Figure 1 shows one of the best BMCs for the prediction of dengue severity in the transcriptomic dataset from [10]. Interestingly, the known biological functions of both transcripts overlap in the highly specific context of T cell co-stimulation [12, 13], which is known to be crucial in the response to the dengue virus [14]. Therefore, this classifier can be interpreted as a complex biological association, in which simultaneously low levels of OX40 and CD40 ligand transcripts are associated with severe dengue infection.Fig. 1Visualization of a bivariate monotonic classifier. To capture more associations that involve more than two transcripts, multiple BMCs can be combined into an ensemble classifier (ensemble BMC), while constrained not to contain BMCs using the same transcripts (only disjoint BMCs). Nikolayeva et al. [10] applied such an ensemble BMC to the classification of clinical dengue infections into mild and severe, according to the blood transcriptome at hospital admission. Their approach empirically performed as good as or better than other classifiers. In its native version, this approach is designed to be data-frugal, meaning it can effectively work with few data points while still generating robust hypotheses.

The ensemble BMC by Nikolayeva et al. [10] was built from BMCs with the best empirical performance estimate, which requires the computation of this estimate for each possible pair of features. As this number grows quadratically with the number of features, this construction (which we refer to as naïveBMC) is computationally expensive and, in practice, can only be used on small subsets of typical genome-scale datasets. Nikolayeva et al. [10] were using two performance estimates for each BMC. The first one corresponds to the error rate when the BMC is trained on all the data (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}). The second one, more robust but also more time-consuming, is the error rate computed with a leave-one-out cross-validation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document}). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document} is an integral part of the method for building the ensemble BMC. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) is especially advantageous when sample sizes are limited, as it maximizes the use of available data: Each observation serves once as the test case, while the rest form the training set. This strategy yields nearly unbiased performance estimates, since every sample is evaluated independently on a model not trained on it. However, LOOCV incurs higher computational cost than k-fold cross-validation, which partitions the dataset into a fixed number of folds and averages performance across them. While k-fold is often preferred for efficiency, LOOCV offers finer resolution and is particularly appropriate when the dataset is small or highly heterogeneous.

fastBMC is an extension of the naïveBMC. It introduces the Preselection Algorithm in the various steps used to build the ensemble BMC. fastBMC differs from naïveBMC in that it reduces the number of BMCs for which the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document} is calculated. Empirical evaluation of fastBMC on simulated datasets

This section presents an experimental evaluation of fastBMC on simulated datasets. We introduce the simulation model in section “Simulated datasets and experimental setup”, compare running times between naïveBMC and fastBMC, and compare the performance of fastBMC with other classification algorithms in section “Speed-up and comparison with other classification algorithms”.

Simulated datasets and experimental setup

Following the idea that certain gene pairs may allow monotonic classifiers, our simulation model includes (potentially noisy) “monotonic interactions” between certain “monotonic pairs” of features (i.e., pairs of features whose labels, in the absence of noise, are monotonic in both features). The labels of all other features are chosen independently at random from {0, 1} with probability 0.5 each. Input features are sampled uniformly at random from the unit interval. We vary the number of data points (30, 50, 100, 150, and 200), the number of features (50, 100, 150, 200, 250), and the noise parameter p in monotonically related feature pairs (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5).

Each simulated dataset, independently of the number of features, contains 14 monotonic pairs, whose labels are determined as follows. Between 2 and 5 randomly selected data points are designated as boundary points. Given a particular pair of features and a boundary point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y = (y_1, y_2)$$\end{document}, a data point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X = (x_1, x_2)$$\end{document} is said to dominate Y if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_1 \ge y_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_2 \ge y_2$$\end{document}. Data points that dominate at least one of the boundary points are given a label of 1, and 0 otherwise.

To enhance computational efficiency, our code is fully parallelized, utilizing 23 CPU cores for simultaneous processing. This parallelization allows us to handle larger-scale computations more effectively, reducing overall processing time and improving the throughput of our analyses.

In this study, we compared fastBMC with naïveBMC on all datasets. Our primary objective was evaluate the efficiency gain achieved by fastBMC. Additionally, we also aimed to assess the robustness of fastBMC under varying levels of noise. Furthermore, we compare fastBMC’s performance against other classic classification algorithms, providing a broader view of its competitiveness and utility in practical scenarios. We choose to abort any calculation when it took more than a day of wall clock computation time.

Empirical evaluation of fastBMC on small biomedical datasets

This section provides an overview of the small biomedical datasets and the experimental settings employed (section “Datasets and experimental setup”). We evaluated the efficiency of fastBMC, focusing on key aspects such as the improvement in running time (section “Obtained speed-up”), a comparative analysis of its performance relative to naïveBMC and other classical algorithms (section “Does the speed-up translate into better classifiers?”). Finally, in section “Interpretability, hypothesis generation, validation options, and treatment implications”, we examine the interpretability of the results in one of the biomedical datasets in light of existing knowledge, hypotheses, and outline possible validation options and implications for treatment.

Datasets and experimental setup

This section describes the three datasets used in the empirical evaluation, as well as the parameterization of fastBMC.

Description of the datasets

Even if transcriptomic datasets usually contain around 20,000 transcripts for coding genes, filtering is often performed to reduce the number of transcripts. Not more than a few thousand genes are typically required to capture the variation present in transcriptomic datasets, even when considering highly diverse biological conditions, experimental technologies, and different species [17].

Dengue dataset This dataset comes from the study by Nikolayeva et al. [10], in which an RNA signature predicted the severity of dengue in young patients. The set contains data from 42 patients, of whom 15 developed a severe form of dengue and 27 a non-severe form. In Nikolayeva et al. [10], the 2653 features with the highest variance were used.

Leukemia dataset This dataset comes from the study by Golub et al. [18], showing how leukemia cases can be classified into acute myeloid leukemia and acute lymphoblastic leukemia. 38 samples were used for training and 34 for testing. The entire data comprises 7129 features for 72 samples. It is accessible at https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/crawford/gene-expression. This dataset represents a well-known and easy classification task.

Glioblastoma dataset This dataset comes from the study by Reifenberger et al. [19] aimed at identifying transcriptomic markers of long-term glioblastoma survival. It comprises 23 samples from long-term (overall survival more than or equal to 36 months) and 16 from short-term survivors (overall survival less than or equal to 12 months). Data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database at http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/ (accession number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ GSE 53733$$\end{document}). We used only the 16,065 features corresponding to coding genes. To further reduce the number of features while retaining the most relevant information, we first eliminated those features with empirical variance below a fixed threshold. Varying this threshold allowed us to [i] generate subdatasets of different sizes, [ii] to profile the speed-up obtained by fastBMC on these subdatasets (section “Obtained speed-up”), and [iii] to examine whether ability to process larger datasets using fastBMC results in improved classification performance (section “Does the speed-up translate into better classifiers?”). In section “Interpretability, hypothesis generation, validation options, and treatment implications” the variance threshold is fixed so that the resulting subdatasets contain around 1000 transcripts.

As BMCs do not change under monotonic transformations of the input features, there was no need to apply such transformations to any of the above datasets.

Parametrization

In the naïveBMC and also in the fastBMC, this parameter is determined in order to maximize the performance of the ensemble BMCbetween a range of 1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\text{max}} \in {\mathbb {N}}$$\end{document}. Both naïveBMC and fastBMC take \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\text {max}}$$\end{document} as a parameter, i.e., the maximal number of disjoint BMCs that we want to test. In this study, as an example for concrete use case, such as identifying a gene pair signature, a reasonable and practical parameter is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\text {max}} = 20$$\end{document}. This constraint is imposed to ensure that the resulting signature remains interpretable and manageable, allowing for meaningful analysis and downstream applications, while also mitigating the risk of overfitting or information overload.

Does the speed-up translate into better classifiers?

Firstly, we aim to investigate whether exploring a larger feature space is worthwhile, and if it enables the discovery of more accurate classifiers. Following this initial assessment, we conduct a comparative analysis of the performance of our proposed method, fastBMC, against other classic techniques.

Comparison with naïveBMC

We now turn to the question of whether the increase in the number of features that can be processed by fastBMC allows the discovery of better classifiers. To do this, we compute the AUC score of fastBMC and naïveBMC for varying subdatasets of increasing size across our three biomedical datasets. As before, we aborted any analysis that took more than 1104 h (user running time).

Figure 7 shows the result.Fig. 7AUC performance on three biomedical datasets, of ensemble BMCs constructed using fastBMC or naïveBMC, and for varying numbers of features

AUC performance on three biomedical datasets, of ensemble BMCs constructed using fastBMC or naïveBMC, and for varying numbers of features

First, we see that the performance of naïveBMC and fastBMC for the same input size is generally comparable. The elimination of certain low-performing models by the Preselection Algorithm does not appear to have a strong influence on classification performance. Second, we see that, for both approaches, a larger input size does not necessarily result in better performance. This observation is in line with the overfitting that can also be observed in other machine learning methods when too many features are provided. Third, for the dengue and leukemia datasets, the best performance is achieved for input sizes larger than what naïveBMC can handle within our time limit. However, for the glioblastoma dataset, the best performance is achieved using only a few features, which is within the capabilities of naïveBMC (albeit, using much more computation time). Note that naïveBMC is sometimes performing better than fastBMC for a given number of features. This difference lies in the fact that the performance of BMCs and their ensembles are only statistical estimates that will vary with the slightly different choices naiveBMC and fastBMC make at the ensemble level.

On the glioblastoma dataset, fastBMC, LR, and RegNet achieve similar predictive performance. Unlike LR, which relies on all genes, both fastBMC and RegNet identify small, interpretable subsets of genes. RegNet selects 15 genes,1 although only some of them are connected within its inferred network. Notably, the genes CNPY1 and DPEP1 are selected by both fastBMC and RegNet. However, the way in which these genes are used is significantly different. fastBMC combines CNPY1 and DPEP1 in two distinct pairs with other genes, whereas RegNet combines them together with 13 other genes to form a 15-dimensional separating linear hyperplane between the glioblastoma survival classes. Here, we do not attempt to further attempt a discussion of the interpretability of the RegNet classifier, but we believe that the following section leaves no doubt about the uniqueness of the way in which BMCs can uncover simple nonlinear relationships that provide significant downstream research opportunities.

Prior knowledge in the glioblastoma context

SDC4 (Syndecan-4) is a transmembrane heparan sulfate proteoglycan involved in cell–matrix interactions, focal adhesion formation, and the regulation of cell migration and invasion [21]. In glioblastoma, high expression of SDC4 has been associated with enhanced invasive behavior and increased cell motility, which are key drivers of tumor progression [22]. Elevated SDC4 can amplify intracellular signaling cascades, such as those mediated by protein kinase C (PKC) and RhoA, that promote cytoskeletal rearrangements and invasive phenotypes [23, 24].

NDUFA4L2 is a hypoxia-inducible gene encoding a mitochondrial protein associated with complex I of the electron transport chain [25]. Its expression is upregulated under low oxygen conditions, a hallmark of the glioblastoma microenvironment [26]. High levels of NDUFA4L2 are thought to facilitate metabolic adaptation to hypoxia, allowing tumor cells to maintain energy production and survive in oxygen-deprived niches [25]. This hypoxic tolerance is frequently associated with resistance to treatment and aggressive tumor behavior [27].

Biological interpretation and implied hypothesis

When expressed in terms of the above prior knowledge, the data-driven SDC4/NDUFA4L2 BMC model corresponds thus to the following hypothesis:In the clinical context, glioblastoma cells may sustain tumor proliferation and progression through two distinct, yet complementary, mechanisms: an invasive phenotype driven by SDC4-mediated signaling and hypoxic tolerance facilitated by NDUFA4L2. In tumors where either pathway is active, aggressive behavior is maintained. However, when both SDC4 and NDUFA4L2 are expressed at low levels, the tumor lacks both a robust invasive capability and an efficient hypoxic adaptation mechanism, resulting in reduced aggressiveness and improved long-term survival.In essence, this hypothesis, which we term Double Defense Hypothesis, posits that glioblastoma cells have two adaptive defense strategies: enhancing invasiveness or increasing hypoxic tolerance, to overcome hostile microenvironmental conditions. The absence of both strategies (i.e., low expression of SDC4 and low expression of NDUFA4L2) leaves tumor cells less equipped to proliferate and disseminate, thus correlating with better clinical outcomes.

Hypothesis validation using TCGA data

As a simple first test of the Double Defense hypothesis, we apply it to the independent ovarian cancer dataset from TCGA [31]. Using the ad hoc choice of the 75th percentile of the SDC4/NDUFA4L2 expression in the TCGA cohort as the threshold between “low” and “high” expression, the Double Defense Hypothesis predicted 83 TCGA patients as long-term survivors and 71 and others as short-term survivors.

The resulting BMC in Fig. 10a separates short and long survival in the TCGA much less accurately than the one in Fig. 9. One of the reasons for poorer performance may lie in the differences between the two cohorts. For example, while the TCGA cohort contains “classic” primary glioblastomas, the Reifenberger et al. [19] cohort was specifically designed to capture extremes of the survival distribution. Therapeutic implications

Agents targeting invasiveness and hypoxia adaptation in glioblastoma already exist. Cilengitide is a drug designed to disrupt SDC4-mediated adhesion and migration, yet failed in a Phase III trial to achieve the desired endpoint [32]. Apatinib effectively targets NDUFA4L2 [26] and was shown to have a certain level of antitumor activity [33].

If validated, the Double Defense Hypothesis suggests that effective glioblastoma therapies should simultaneously target both invasiveness and hypoxic adaptation to increase survival, in particular in those patients whose tumors exhibit simultaneous high expression of SDC4 and NDUFA4L2. Moreover, the joint low expression of these genes could serve as a prognostic biomarker to identify patients with inherently less aggressive tumors who might benefit from less intensive treatment regimens.

In-depth biological evaluation of fastBMC with a larger dataset: METABRIC

While section “Empirical evaluation of fastBMC on small biomedical datasets” focuses on small biomedical datasets to demonstrate the data frugality of fastBMC, it is important to note that fastBMC can be effectively applied to larger datasets with minimal adjustments. By replacing leave-one-out cross-validation with k-fold cross-validation, the robustness of the model can be maintained, while improving computational efficiency. We first describe the METABRIC dataset in section “Description of the METABRIC dataset” as well as the adjustments performed to fastBMC in section “Setting and adaptation for a larger dataset”. Section “The ensembleBMC resulting from fastBMC” presents the ensembleBMC resulting from fastBMC, and goes into a brief description of the resulting gene pairs. Finally, in section “Validation and comparison of the ensembleBMC to other classic methods”, we validate and compare the performance to other classic classification algorithms (analogous to Table 2).

Description of the METABRIC dataset

The Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) is a comprehensive genomic study designed to classify breast cancer into distinct subtypes based on molecular characteristics. Published in 2012, the METABRIC study analyzed the genomic profiles of over 2000 breast cancer samples using gene expression, copy number variation, and mutation analysis [34]. The METABRIC dataset is available at https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=brca_metabric. DNA and RNA were extracted from each sample and matched for analysis. Transcriptional profiling was performed on the Illumina HT-12 v3 platform. The transcriptomic data is already normalized. We applied the median absolute deviation [35] technique with a threshold of 0.7, resulting in the selection of 1708 genes. Our goal is to predict the relapse-free status (RFS) of patients. To this end, we focused on patients who experienced a recurrence, totaling 630 patients. We consider only the patients with the most extreme RFS values. Patients with an RFS of less than the first quartile (20.9 months) are classified as having a short RFS (158 patients), while those with an RFS greater than the third quartile (89.9 months) are classified as having a long RFS (158 patients). This approach allows us to use patients with intermediate RFS for validation purposes.

Setting and adaptation for a larger dataset

As an estimation technique, LOOCV provides a robust assessment of model performance, but to reduce computation time, k-fold cross-validation (CV) can be used. Scenario 1: LAD1/SEC14L2 (Fig. 11, upper left). The interplay between LAD1 and SEC14L2 in breast cancer provides an intriguing opportunity to explore how these well-known biomarkers might interact, particularly since high levels of LAD1 combined with low levels of SEC14L2 are associated with shorter RFS. LAD1 plays a crucial role in transcriptional regulation, influencing which genes are turned on or off within the cell. This function is essential for controlling various cellular processes, including growth and division. SEC14L2, on the other hand, is involved in lipid metabolism and cellular signaling, which are vital for maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to external stimuli. Given their known roles, SEC14L2 could affect how cells manage lipids and signal within the tumor microenvironment, potentially affecting the growth and behavior of cancer cells. The observation that high LAD1 expression coupled with low SEC14L2 expression is associated with shorter recurrence-free survival suggests a complex relationship between these genes. High LAD1 levels might lead to excessive transcriptional repression or activation of specific genes, while low SEC14L2 levels could disrupt normal lipid metabolism and signaling. This dual dysregulation might create an environment that fosters aggressive cancer growth and reduces the effectiveness of treatments.

Scenario 2: PSD3/PHYHD1 (Fig. 11, upper right). Short RFS (poor outcomes) is associated with mid- or low-level gene expression. The low expression of PSD3 and PHYHD1 in breast cancer may signify that these genes perform tumor suppressor-like functions, where their reduced expression leads to the dysregulation of essential cellular processes. PSD3 plays a critical role in vesicle trafficking, which is vital for proper cell signaling and maintenance. When PSD3 expression is reduced, it can result in aberrant trafficking of growth factor receptors, adhesion molecules, and other signaling components, thereby fostering uncontrolled cell proliferation and metastasis. Similarly, PHYHD1 is integral to mitochondrial function, ensuring energy homeostasis and maintaining genomic integrity. Lower expression of PHYHD1 can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, contributing to increased metabolic stress, genomic instability, and resistance to apoptosis in cancer cells. The interconnected roles of PSD3 and PHYHD1 in maintaining cellular homeostasis may explain why their concurrent low expression is associated with a poor prognosis. Dysregulation in vesicle trafficking (mediated by PSD3) and mitochondrial function (mediated by PHYHD1) can synergistically compromise cellular functions, accelerating disease progression. Since proper mitochondrial function is essential for the energy and metabolic intermediates necessary for vesicle trafficking, disruptions in PHYHD1 can indirectly affect PSD3-mediated processes, culminating in increased cellular stress and a shorter RFS.

Scenario 3: MAPT/FAM241A (Fig. 11, top of second column). The low expression of MAPT and FAM241A in breast cancer suggests a possible link between these genes and short RFS, offering an opportunity to better understand the role of FAM241A, whose functions are not yet fully characterized. MAPT is known for its involvement in microtubule assembly and dynamics, which are crucial for cell structure and division. Although MAPT alterations are commonly associated with neurological diseases, emerging research indicates that they may also play a significant role in cancer. Specifically, MAPT could influence cell signaling and interactions within the tumor microenvironment, processes that are vital for cancer cell survival and proliferation. Given that low levels of both MAPT and FAM241A are associated with a worse prognosis, studying their joint function can provide insight into the role of FAM241A. FAM241A is involved in various cellular processes and its interaction with MAPT could shed light on how these processes contribute to cancer progression. Understanding how FAM241A interacts with MAPT in the context of microtubule dynamics and cell signaling could help uncover its role in the tumor environment. This knowledge could improve our understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving breast cancer and potentially identify new therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes.

Formal description of the methods

This section is a formal description of the methods presented in this paper. It includes naïveBMC, whose detailed description has been omitted in Nikolayeva et al. [10] (section “naïveBMC”), as well as the Preselection Algorithm and fastBMC (section “The Preselection Algorithm and fastBMC”).

naïveBMC

This section breaks down the construction of BMCs (section “Classification setting and bivariate monotonic classifiers”) and the various stages that make up the naïveBMC (sections “Ensemble bivariate monotonic classifiers” and “Prediction error estimate by leave-one-out cross-validation”).

Classification setting and bivariate monotonic classifiers

Formally, the input data in [10] is given as a vector of values for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m\in {\mathbf{N}}_{\ge 1}$$\end{document} transcriptomic features, for each of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n\in {\mathbf{N}}_{\ge 1}$$\end{document} patients. Each patient also has a disease label in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0, 1\}$$\end{document}. The problem aims to predict, for a new patient, the disease label from the transcript values.

Nikolayeva et al. [10] used bivariate monotonic (binary) classifiers (BMCs), i.e., functions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f:{\mathbb {R}}^2 \rightarrow \{0,1\}$$\end{document} that are monotonic in both dimensions.2 Any function that increases monotonically in x or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-x$$\end{document}, and in y or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-y$$\end{document} is a BMC. An optimal BMC f is one such that f minimizes the classification error (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}) of the input with respect to its labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(v_i)_{i = 1}^{n} \in \{0, 1\}^{n}$$\end{document}, that is, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{i = 1}^{n} |f(x_i, y_i) - v_i|$$\end{document}. The monotonic function is determined from the optimal regression algorithm developed by Stout [11]. Briefly, the algorithm uses dynamic programming, recursively adding a best-possible position for a point where the monotonicity changes, based on the already computed optimal results for the subproblems. The monotonic function is retrieves by backtracking these decisions, resulting in a set of points that indicate when monotonicity changes. We break ties in the recurrence relation by choosing positions with the smallest index.

Since a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}-minimal f might not be unique, we follow the approach of Nikolayeva et al. [10] and define an optimal BMC as one that classifies the most points in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{R}}^2$$\end{document} as 1. Formally, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}-minimal BMC \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f^*$$\end{document} constructed over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \subset {\mathbf{R}}^2$$\end{document} is considered optimal if and only if for all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}-minimal BMCs f constructed over S holds that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\{x \in {\mathbf{R}}^2 \mid f^*(x) = 1\}| \ge |\{x \in {\mathbf{R}}^2 \mid f(x) = 1\}|$$\end{document}.

The CE of the BMC in Fig. 1 is 1, as exactly one blue triangle is within the red area and no red point falls in the blue area. The red area cannot be further increased without increasing the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}. Therefore, this BMC is optimal.

A \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}-minimal BMC for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document} points can be efficiently calculated by dynamic programming in time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Theta (n \log ^2 n)$$\end{document} [11]. Since this algorithm only constructs monotonically increasing BMCs, we compute four different \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}-minimal BMCs for each transcriptomic feature pair—one for each possible orientation of each of the two axes. We then choose a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {full}}$$\end{document}-minimal BMC among these four options.

Note that although BMCs can be described as decision trees, common greedy algorithms for decision trees typically do not identify classifiers such as those in Fig. 1. This may explain the poorer performance of the decision trees that were computed in Nikolayeva et al. [10] for comparison.

Nikolayeva et al. [10] explain how to combine multiple BMCs into an ensemble classifier of at most \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\text {max}}$$\end{document} BMCs. Such an ensemble BMC is a set of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\in {\mathbf{N}}_{\ge 1}$$\end{document} BMCs, whose predicted label is the majority of the binary labels predicted by the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} BMCs.3 The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} BMCs for the ensemble BMC are selected according to their individual performance (see section “Prediction error estimate by leave-one-out cross-validation”). The BMCs with the lowest error rates are chosen, provided that the ensemble BMC contains the features only once. This greedy selection approach could be optimized to maximize information complementarity between BMCs.

The classification performance of any given BMC is estimated by removing a patient in turn from the input, training a BMC on the reduced dataset, and evaluating the absolute difference (0 or 1) between the predicted and the true label of the removed patient (this is called leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV)). The sum of these values is the BMC’s LOOCV error, or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${ER}_{{loocv}}$$\end{document} (section “Prediction error estimate by leave-one-out cross-validation”).

Otherwise, we evaluate the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document} of the BMC. During this evaluation, since we have a threshold t, if we see that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document} is worse than t, we stop the LOOCV and ignore the BMC. We check this in the same way as in Phase 2. If this is the case, we remove all BMCs with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document} of t, and we update t to the new worst \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ER}_{\text {loocv}}$$\end{document} among all BMCs that we still keep.

At the end of the Preselection Algorithm , we return all BMCs that were kept.

fastBMC

The fastBMC algorithm is an extension of naïveBMC that incorporates the Preselection Algorithm . The Preselection Algorithm is called within each of the three phases of naïveBMC mentioned in section “Ensemble bivariate monotonic classifiers”. Each of these phases conducts an exhaustive LOOCV. During each of these LOOCV s, immediately after every sample that is excluded for testing, the Preselection Algorithm is called. This means that the Preselection Algorithm is called multiple times per phase. The reason for calling the Preselection Algorithm repeatedly is that this ensures that we do not bias the overall LOOCV with respect to which patient we leave out. In more detail, the Preselection Algorithm does not introduce bias into the subsequent computations because it does not have access to the data point that is used for testing.

Discussion

Our empirical evaluation on three biomedical datasets suggests that fastBMC produces models that perform similarly to naïveBMC, in drastically less time, thus allowing the analysis of significantly larger sets of features, which, in turn, is sometimes critical to discover better classifiers.

In this article, we introduce an extension of an existing method by Nikolayeva et al. [10], which we call fastBMC. This extension is based on a Preselection Algorithm (Algorithm 1) that operates on a set of optimal bivariate monotonic classifiers (BMCs) for transcriptomic feature pairs and efficiently determines those with the lowest leave-one-out cross-validation error. Such sets are invaluable for constructing optimal ensembles of BMCs, which are not only highly accurate, but also easily interpretable. When combining the Preselection Algorithm with the approach by Nikolayeva et al. [10] to compute BMCs, we empirically find that for datasets of 250 and more features, fastBMC is at least 15 times faster than the original approach (naïveBMC), and that this speed-up is also required in our empirical evaluation to discover optimal classifiers in reasonable computation time, in two out of three datasets. Our study acknowledges that an increasing number of features does not necessarily translate to better classification performance, and in fact, when the number of features far exceeds the number of samples, the curse of dimensionality can lead to overfitting, making it easier to fit a model to noise rather than meaningful patterns. This concern is particularly relevant when using fastBMC, which can handle larger numbers of features and thus increase the risk of overfitting. Compared with classic classification methods, fastBMC performs as well as the other methods. The benefit lies in the interpretability and visualization of the model. Thus, fastBMC represents a considerable advance for the discovery of BMCs especially in transcriptomic but potentially also data-richer biological applications, where even more features are available. The software we used for this study is the first open source library, in Python, that allows the discovery of BMCs and their ensembles. It is available at https://github.com/oceanefrqt/fastBMC.

Direct interpretation of the identified glioblastoma BMC SDC4/NDUFA4L2 in the context of prior knowledge immediately suggested a novel plausible and experimentally testable clinical hypothesis and downstream implications for glioblastoma treatment (section “Interpretability, hypothesis generation, validation options, and treatment implications”).

It is important to temper our observation with an acknowledgment of the dynamic complexity inherent in glioblastoma. These tumors are characterized by extensive intratumoral heterogeneity and rapid clonal evolution, factors that may not be fully captured by a single pretreatment transcriptomic snapshot. In practice, the aggressive and adaptive nature of glioblastoma means that small subpopulations of cells with distinct gene expression profiles could emerge over time, potentially altering the tumor behavior despite a favorable initial baseline signature. Therefore, even if the SDC4/NDUFA4L2 signature provides valuable prognostic information, it should be viewed as one piece of a larger and more complex puzzle, in which the two physiological aspects brought together by the BMC may also, biologically, be more closely related than initially thought [49].

Perhaps due to these complexities, our initial validation in the TCGA cohort could confirm a statistical association of SDC4/NDUFA4L2 with survival, but not the originally much stronger association in a more recent and more homogeneous cohort. Future studies in other cohorts employing single cell sequencing and longitudinal monitoring may help characterize those subtypes of glioblastoma in which the association can be observed, how stable the SDC4/NDUFA4L2 signature remains over time, and how they integrate with other dynamic molecular changes that drive the progression of glioblastoma.

Motivated by the performance and interpretability in our glioblastoma case study, our methodological efforts are now directed towards further refinement of fastBMC and exploration of its applicability to biological and non-biological datasets. Currently, BMCs produce binary output. To enhance the practical value of BMCs, it would be interesting to estimate the probability of correctness for each prediction. This probability could be defined, for example, on the basis of the distance of a point from the classification boundary by a method such as Platt scaling [50]. Platt scaling is a calibration technique used in machine learning to convert the output of a classifier into well-calibrated probability scores, typically by applying a logistic function to model predictions.

Alternatively, a monotonic multiclass model could be used. Using any inherent order of training data, a multiclass approach represents a form of ordinal classification, typically used in bioinformatics for tasks such as cancer staging or prediction of disease progression. Beyond biology, ordinal classification is useful in other domains such as satisfaction surveys [51], bank failure prediction [52], and quality control [53].
