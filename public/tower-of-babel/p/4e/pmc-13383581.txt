Vessel wall imaging in adult moyamoya disease with presumed acute in situ thrombotic occlusion: illustrative cases
Authors: Takeshi Funaki, Hideo Chihara, Tomoki Sasagasako, Masakazu Okawa, Yohei Mineharu, Sachi Okuchi, Taichi Ikedo, Chiaki Sakai, Yoshiki Arakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAlthough the hemodynamic mechanism of ischemic symptoms is widely accepted in moyamoya disease (MMD), acute thrombotic occlusion at a preexisting stenotic site may cause unexpected ischemic stroke. This phenomenon has rarely been addressed, particularly in relation to vessel wall MRI findings.OBSERVATIONSAmong 247 patients with MMD, 3 adults (1.2%, age range 33–59 years) exhibited radiological findings compatible with acute in situ thrombotic occlusion, defined as imaging-documented acute occlusion at a preexisting stenotic site and/or spontaneous recanalization on serial imaging. All 3 patients developed sudden-onset ischemic stroke despite relatively early angiographic stages and without preceding transient ischemic attacks. Two patients showed infarct patterns atypical for purely hemodynamic stroke, with basal ganglia involvement. Vessel wall MRI revealed marked contrast enhancement of the arterial wall at the site corresponding to the acute occlusion in all 3 patients. Neurological deficits remained after stroke in 2 patients, whereas the patient who had been receiving antiplatelet therapy had no residual deficits.LESSONSThese cases highlight presumed acute in situ thrombotic occlusion as a possible mechanism of ischemic stroke in adult MMD and suggest a potential role for vessel wall MRI in risk stratification and management. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26208 Keywords: moyamoya disease, cerebral infarction, acute thrombotic occlusion, magnetic resonance imaging, vascular wallABBREVIATIONS: ACA = anterior cerebral artery, DWI = diffusion-weighted imaging, ICA = internal carotid artery, MCA = middle cerebral artery, MMD = moyamoya disease, MRA = MR angiography, TIA = transient ischemic attack

Ischemic symptoms, including transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) and ischemic stroke, are common clinical manifestations of moyamoya disease (MMD). Hemodynamic insufficiency caused by chronic steno-occlusive arterial lesions is considered the primary mechanism.1 This hemodynamic mechanism is supported by unique triggers for TIAs, including hyperventilation, and by the watershed infarcts commonly observed in MMD.2 The evidence supporting antithrombotic therapy remains limited in the disease.1,3–6 Nevertheless, acute arterial occlusion at a preexisting stenotic segment—suggesting an in situ thrombotic process—has been reported in a small number of adult patients with MMD.7,8 The incidence and pathophysiology of this phenomenon remain poorly understood. Here, we report 3 adult patients with MMD who exhibited serial radiographic findings suggestive of acute occlusion at preexisting stenotic sites, with emphasis on vessel wall MRI findings.9

Illustrative Cases

Between 2018 and 2025, 247 patients with MMD (age range 2–70 years) were admitted to our hospital. The first author retrospectively reviewed their medical records and serial imaging studies and identified 3 adult patients (1.2%) with radiographic findings compatible with acute thrombotic occlusion at a preexisting stenotic site (Table 1). Two patients (cases 1 and 2) demonstrated spontaneous recanalization of the occluded arteries on follow-up imaging after stroke. In the remaining patient (case 3), acute occlusion was incidentally documented on serial imaging obtained within a short interval (≤ 1 week). None of the 3 patients had a history of TIAs. Two patients (cases 1 and 3) were diagnosed with asymptomatic MMD prior to the index stroke, whereas the remaining patient (case 2) was diagnosed with MMD only after stroke onset. During the same period, 38 patients were diagnosed with asymptomatic MMD; within this asymptomatic subgroup, those who exhibited radiological findings compatible with acute thrombotic occlusion accounted for 5.3% (2/38).

Case 1

A 59-year-old woman was referred to our hospital after incidental detection of intracranial arterial stenosis on screening MRI. The patient had a history of mild dyslipidemia and hypertension but no episodes of TIAs. Neurological examination revealed no abnormalities. Autoantibody screening was negative, and RNF213 genotyping revealed heterozygosity for the RNF213 p.R4810K variant. MR angiography (MRA) demonstrated stenosis at the terminal portions of the bilateral internal carotid arteries (ICAs) (Fig. 1A) with collateral vascular networks at the base of the brain. The patient was diagnosed with asymptomatic MMD. MRA showed no apparent periventricular anastomosis, a hemorrhage-prone collateral pathway. A statin was prescribed for dyslipidemia, whereas antiplatelet therapy was not initiated because the patient had no ischemic symptoms.

Eight months after the initial visit to our clinic, the patient was found unconscious at home and was transported by ambulance to another hospital. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) revealed acute infarction in the left frontal lobe and bilateral basal ganglia (Fig. 1B). MRA obtained at the same time demonstrated signal loss in the bilateral anterior cerebral arteries (ACAs) and left middle cerebral artery (MCA), attributable to occlusion of the left A1 segment of the ACA and the left M1 segment of the MCA (Fig. 1C). The patient was treated with antiplatelet therapy. Follow-up MRA performed 1 week after stroke onset demonstrated spontaneous recanalization of the ACAs (Fig. 1D).

After acute-phase management and rehabilitation, the patient was referred to our hospital. Vessel wall MRI demonstrated marked contrast enhancement at the left A1 segment of the ACA (Fig. 1E), corresponding to the transient occlusion site. The vessel wall of the left M1 segment of the MCA was also enhanced. Conventional angiography findings were consistent with MMD and were classified as Suzuki stage 4 in the right hemisphere (Fig. 1F and G). In the left hemisphere, severe stenosis of the A1 and M1 segments with moyamoya vessels was observed (Fig. 1H and I); however, antegrade flow in the ACA and MCA was maintained (Suzuki stage 2). The stroke resulted in cognitive dysfunction. Bypass surgery is being considered at the time of writing.

Case 2

A 33-year-old man with no history of TIAs was urgently admitted to an outside hospital with sudden-onset impaired consciousness, aphasia, and right hemiparesis while performing manual labor. Although the patient’s brother had MMD, the patient had not been diagnosed with MMD. The patient had no conventional vascular risk factors. DWI revealed acute infarction in the left frontal lobe (Fig. 2A), and MRA revealed occlusion at the terminal portions of bilateral ICAs (Fig. 2B). Antiplatelet therapy was initiated. Follow-up MRI performed 1 month after stroke onset demonstrated established infarction in the left frontal lobe and left putamen (Fig. 2C); however, MRA performed at the same time showed spontaneous recanalization of the left MCA (Fig. 2D). The patient was then referred to our hospital for further evaluation.

Autoantibody screening was negative, and RNF213 genotyping revealed heterozygosity for the RNF213 p.R4810K variant. Vessel wall MRI demonstrated marked contrast enhancement at the left M1 ­segment of the MCA (Fig. 2E), corresponding to the transient occlusion site. Conventional angiography findings were consistent with MMD (Suzuki stage 4 in the right hemisphere and 2 in the left hemisphere) (Fig. 2F–I), and antegrade flow in the MCA was maintained. No apparent periventricular anastomosis was observed. The patient underwent staged bilateral direct bypass surgery, and the perioperative course was uneventful. Although the patient’s hemiparesis improved, motor aphasia persisted.

Case 3

A 43-year-old woman was referred to our hospital after incidental detection of intracranial arterial stenosis on screening MRI. The patient had neither a history of TIAs nor conventional vascular risk factors. Neurological examination revealed no abnormalities. Autoantibody screening was positive for anti-SS-A antibody, and RNF213 genotyping revealed heterozygosity for the RNF213 p.R4810K variant. Conventional angiography findings were consistent with MMD (Suzuki stage 2 in the right hemisphere and 3 in the left hemisphere), and no apparent periventricular anastomosis was observed (Fig. 3A–D). Both ACAs were supplied via the left A1 segment of the ACA, which showed stenosis (Fig. 3C). Vessel wall MRI demonstrated marked contrast enhancement at the terminal portion of the left ICA and the left A1 segment of the ACA (Fig. 3E). She was diagnosed with asymptomatic moyamoya syndrome associated with Sjögren’s syndrome. Cilostazol was initiated as antiplatelet therapy.

The patient remained clinically stable at the eighth outpatient visit (21 months after the initial visit), and MRA performed at the same time revealed no interval change with preserved antegrade flow in the ACAs (Fig. 3F). Four days later, the patient developed severe headache and transient motor weakness in the right extremities during a business trip. The patient then returned to our clinic and underwent emergency MRI and MRA. DWI showed an acute infarct in the left frontal lobe (Fig. 3G). MRA demonstrated signal loss in the bilateral ACAs, attributable to occlusion of the left A1 segment of the ACA (Fig. 3H). The patient ultimately underwent staged bilateral direct bypass surgery, and no neurological deficits remained. The patient expressed gratitude for the favorable outcome.

Discussion

Observations

The present 3 cases share unique clinical and radiological features. First, ischemic stroke occurred suddenly in adult patients without preceding TIAs. Second, this occurred despite relatively early angiographic Suzuki stages. Third, acute occlusion at a preexisting stenotic site and/or recanalization was documented on serial imaging. In cases 1 and 2, the infarct patterns on MRI were atypical for purely hemodynamic stroke. Taken together, these findings suggest that an in situ thrombotic process may have contributed to acute arterial occlusion. Interestingly, all 3 cases showed marked contrast enhancement of the arterial wall at the site corresponding to the acute occlusion. Caution is warranted, however, in interpreting vessel wall enhancement as predictive of acute in situ thrombotic occlusion. Because vessel wall MRI was performed after stroke onset in cases 1 and 2, the observed vessel wall enhancement may have represented a reactive phenomenon after occlusion in those cases.

The present cases bear some resemblance to previous reports that have addressed contralateral acute arterial occlusion after revascularization surgery for MMD. Sussman et al. reported 8 adult cases (0.6% of their cohort) in which angiography performed within 48 hours after surgery demonstrated acute complete occlusion of the ICA contralateral to the operated hemisphere.7 Their case series included 2 cases in which a preexisting mild stenosis acutely progressed to complete occlusion. The authors speculated that postoperative hypercoagulability and an enhanced thrombotic process might underlie this phenomenon. Ogawa et al. also reported an adult case with moyamoya syndrome, in which the contralateral ICA occlusion developed 4 hours after surgery and was successfully treated with aspiration thrombectomy, with angiographic visualization of thrombus distal to a stenotic segment.8

The available histopathological and imaging evidence suggests that an in situ thrombotic process may contribute, at least in part, to ischemic events in MMD. Hosoda et al., in a histopathological study of 31 autopsy cases, reported that mural thrombi were frequently identified (17/31) in major cerebral arteries and that their distribution correlated with the intimal proliferative lesions.10 Kim et al. also reported, in a multimodal MRI study of adult MMD, that the thromboembolic infarct patterns were the most common.11 More recently, Yoshimoto et al. discussed that RNF213 might be a key regulator of cerebral endothelial integrity and that its polymorphism reduces endothelial barrier functions.12

We observed marked contrast enhancement of the arterial wall at the site corresponding to the acute occlusion in all our cases. This interesting observation is in line with several studies that have identified vessel wall enhancement as a predictor of ischemic events.13–17 Our observation may also provide a clue to the pathophysiology of acute in situ thrombotic occlusion, which we defined radiologically as acute occlusion at a preexisting stenotic site and/or spontaneous recanalization confirmed on serial imaging. Kathuveetil et al. discussed that vessel wall enhancement may reflect proliferative neovascular changes, an active angiogenic microenvironment, and an accompanying inflammatory response.17 We therefore speculate that these hypothesized pathological changes increase thrombogenicity, thereby predisposing to acute in situ thrombotic occlusion.

Our findings are hypothesis generating regarding the possible role of prophylactic antiplatelet therapy in selected asymptomatic patients with MMD. Current guidelines indicate that the clinical benefit of antiplatelet therapy in MMD remains uncertain and is not firmly established,3–5 although several cohort studies have suggested potential benefits in adult patients with ischemic MMD.6 The Japanese guideline also cautions against the use of antiplatelet agents in asymptomatic patients because of the potential risk of hemorrhagic events.3 Given our findings, however, future comparative studies should examine whether prophylactic antiplatelet therapy might be considered for asymptomatic adult patients with marked vessel wall enhancement in the absence of periventricular anastomosis and microbleeds, both of which are reported predictors of de novo hemorrhagic stroke.18 Antiplatelet therapy may not increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage in MMD,19,20 although it does increase the risk in the general population.21

According to the patient-level data reported by Wang et al., vessel wall enhancement was observed in 14 of 24 patients without acute infarction at baseline, whereas all patients with acute infarction in that study showed vessel wall enhancement.13 Furthermore, some in situ thrombotic processes may occur asymptomatically. Taken together, vessel wall enhancement alone may be insufficient for risk stratification of acute in situ thrombotic occlusion or for guiding management, particularly regarding prophylactic antiplatelet therapy. Further studies are warranted to identify additional risk factors for acute in situ thrombotic occlusion and to clarify how vessel wall MRI findings should be integrated into patient risk stratification and management.

Lessons

The present cases illustrate unique clinical and radiographic findings compatible with acute thrombotic occlusion at a preexisting stenotic site. Although further case accumulation is required, our findings suggest that vessel wall MRI may aid risk stratification and facilitate clinical decision-making regarding the prevention of this phenomenon. The possible role of prophylactic antiplatelet therapy in asymptomatic patients with relevant risk factors, including vessel wall enhancement, should be examined in future studies.
