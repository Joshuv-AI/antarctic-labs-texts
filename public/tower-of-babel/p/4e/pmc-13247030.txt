Behavioural states control binocular vision through input-specific mechanisms
Authors: Katayun Cohen-Kashi Malina, Stav Shtiglitz, Dahlia Kushinsky, Alon Rubin, Ivo Spiegel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBinocular vision is essential for high-acuity stereopsis, depth perception and goal-directed behaviours, but whether binocular visual processing is modulated according to an animal’s behaviroal state remains unknown. By combining behavioural tracking with calcium imaging in layer 2/3 excitatory neurons in the binocular zone of the primary visual cortex (bV1) of adult mice, we demonstrate that binocularity and binocular integration change rapidly in a state-dependent manner via eye-/input-specific cellular mechanisms: responses to contralateral eye stimulation are strongly enhanced during high arousal states, much more so than ipsilateral responses, and binocular integration shifts from sublinear to supralinear summation, which both are explained by the differential modulation of thalamic and callosal inputs to bV1 neurons. We thus propose that the state-dependent modulation of bV1 neurons adapts an animal’s visual perception to its behavioural demands by reducing the relative impact of binocular vision during high-arousal states while enhancing the impact of peripherally-derived monocular vision.Subject terms: Striate cortex, Sensory processing

Introduction

The integration of visual information from both eyes allows for stereoscopic vision and depth perception1–3. The behavioural importance of binocular vision is evident during goal oriented visually-guided tasks such as navigation and hunting in animals with forward-facing eyes that have large binocular visual fields (humans, monkeys, cats, etc)2. Notably, animals with sideward facing eyes and relatively narrow binocular visual fields (e.g., mice have a binocular visual field of only 40°;4) also use binocular vision for skilled behaviours such as prey catching5–7. While binocular vision conveys advantages in skilled visually-guided tasks, under certain behavioural conditions - e.g., during high arousal states such as when an animal perceives a sudden threat by a rapidly approaching predator - it might be advantageous to enhance peripheral vision and to reduce binocular vision. Whether binocularity can be modulated in a rapid manner depending on the animal’s behavioural state remains unknown.

Binocular vision emerges from the integration of visual information from both eyes by excitatory neurons in the primary visual cortex (V1). In mice and other mammals, visual information is conveyed to the rest of the brain via the axons of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). About 5% of the RGCs project ipsilaterally in rodents8–10, while the remaining 95% cross the midline in the optic chiasm. Most RGC axons synapse onto neurons in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) in the thalamus which, in turn, projects to V1. Binocularity metrics such as the Ocular Dominance Index (ODI)11–14 measure the relative responsiveness of visual cortex neurons to stimuli presented to the contra- and ipsi-lateral eyes. Neurons that respond to visual stimuli presented to both eyes individually are called binocular neurons; in rodents, these neurons are concentrated in the binocular zone of the primary visual cortex (bV1)15. While the responses of bV1 neurons to inputs from the contralateral eye are driven primarily by thalamic inputs from the dorsal LGN (dLGN), responses to inputs from the ipsilateral eye are largely conveyed by callosal inputs from the contralateral bV1 (i.e., 50–80% of the response of bV1 neurons to ipsilateral eye stimulation)16–20. Notably, the integration of inputs from the contra- and ipsi-lateral eyes is sublinear21 and thought to maintain orientation selectivity in bV1 neurons17.

The molecular and cellular mechanisms that control the establishment of binocular circuitry in the developing brain are still not fully understood. Classic experiments in cats22, primates23 and mice11 have revealed that visually-evoked sensory experience over multiple days and weeks is necessary for the development of these circuits. Furthermore, a recent study based on longitudinal calcium imaging of excitatory neurons in layer 2/3 (L2/3) of bV1 in mice revealed that the binocularity of single neurons changes in a sensory-dependent manner during a period of postnatal hyperplasticity (“critical period”) but remains stable in the adult24. Thus, these observations are consistent with other studies that indicate that the average ocular dominance (i.e., binocularity) of large excitatory neuron populations in the adult visual cortex is fixed25,26.

We and others have found that behavioural states such as arousal and locomotion alter how sensory information is processed in cortical circuits27–34. For example, in the monocular zone of the mouse V1 (mV1), the strength of visually evoked responses in L2/3 excitatory neurons scales with an animal’s arousal state28,35. However, studies of visual and auditory detection in mice have demonstrated that sensory perception is actually optimal at intermediate - but not low or high - arousal states30,36,37, suggesting that perceptual abilities do not scale directly with the strength of the sensory-evoked response. This might suggest that those aspects of sensory processing that mediate high-resolution perceptual tasks - e.g., binocular vision that is required for prey catching - are reduced at high arousal states as well. However, whether binocularity and binocular integration in visual cortex neurons are modulated according to an animal’s behavioural state has not been explored.

Here, we combined in vivo two-photon (2 P) calcium imaging in bV1 layer 2/3 excitatory neurons with behavioural tracking and computational modelling to investigate how binocularity and binocular integration are modulated by an animal’s arousal state. We find that binocularity decreases during high arousal states, that binocular integration transitions from a sublinear regime during low arousal to a supralinear regime during high arousal states, and that these effects are driven at least in part by the selective state-dependent enhancement of thalamic inputs from the contralateral eye. Together with previous studies in mV1, our findings indicate that the relative impact of binocular vision decreases during high-arousal states while the impact of peripherally-derived monocular vision increases.

Results

Arousal differentially modulates contra- and ipsi-lateral inputs to bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons

The goal of our study was to assess whether binocularity and binocular integration in bV1 is affected by an animal’s behavioural state. To this end, we expressed the genetically encoded calcium indicator (GECI) jGCaMP7f38 via an AAV-vector in L2/3 excitatory neurons in the adult mouse bV1 and recorded their responses to visual stimuli in awake head-fixed mice that were placed on a linear treadmill under a 2-photon (2 P) microscope (Fig. 1A). Visual stimuli (i.e., sinusoidal drifting gratings at four different orientations, see Methods) were presented on an LED screen placed in front of the mice and were restricted in size to the animals’ binocular field of view (FOV). To present visual stimuli to each eye separately, as required for studying binocular vision, we established an automated shutter system that was placed in front of the mouse’s eyes thereby allowing for interleaved randomised presentation of visual stimuli to each eye separately (i.e., only to the ipsi- or contra-lateral eye relative to the recording site in bV1) or both eyes simultaneously. We did not observe any visually-evoked neural responses in bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons when the shutters blocked both eyes, indicating the shutter system is highly effective (Fig. 1A). During the imaging sessions, we monitored the animals’ behavioural state by simultaneously recording the movement and pupil size of both eyes, their whisker pads (for subsequent quantification of facial movement), and their running speed39–41. Thus, our experimental setup allowed us to correlate multiple measures of the animals’ arousal state with the visually-evoked neural responses of their bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons.

We first assessed whether behavioural states affect the visually evoked responses of bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons differently when visual stimuli are presented to each eye separately. Based on our pupillometry data, we divided the animals’ arousal states during visual stimuli presentation into “Low”, “Mid” or “High” periods and parsed the neurons’ responses accordingly (Fig. 1B). We found that the visual responses of single bV1 neurons to contra- versus ipsi-lateral monocular stimulation was differentially affected by behavioural states: while the neurons’ responses to contralateral eye stimulation increased at high arousal state, their responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation were less affected by the animals’ behavioural state (Fig. 1B). The differential effect of arousal state on neuronal responses to inputs arriving from either the contra- or ipsi-lateral eye was evident both in the distribution of the neurons’ responses to the respective eye stimulation (Fig. 1C, Supplemental Fig. 1A) as well as in the neurons’ population means (Fig. 1D): on average, the strength of the responses to contralateral inputs significantly increases as the animal becomes more aroused (Low vs. High: p < 0.0001; Friedman test followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction), with a median increase of 76%. Although ipsilateral responses also showed a significant increase (Low vs. High: p = 0.0123), the effect was markedly smaller, with a median increase of only 17%. Notably, shuffle analysis confirmed that the differential shifts in the distribution and mean of the responses were not due to sampling size bias (Supplemental Fig. 1B, C). To directly quantify and compare the degree of arousal-dependent modulation of visual responses to contra- and ipsi-lateral stimulation, we first calculated a “State-Dependency Index” (SDI, see Methods) separately for the contra- and ipsi-laterally evoked responses of each neuron. This analysis revealed that the contralateral SDI of the majority of neurons (~69%) is above 0 (i.e., their contra-laterally evoked responses are stronger at high arousal than at low arousal), while similar fractions of neurons exhibit an increase or decrease (52% and 48%, respectively) in their ipsilateral SDI (Fig. 1E). Moreover, the SDI values were significantly higher for contra- than ipsi-laterally evoked responses (p < 0.0001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), confirming that behavioural state modulates contralateral responses more strongly. Notably, at the single neuron level, when we analyzed the relation between responses amplitude and pupil size (i.e, treating state as a continuous measure) we found that the responses to contralateral eye stimuli show stronger arousal state-dependent modulation than responses to ipsilateral stimuli, with steeper positive slopes relative to pupil diameter at the time of stimulus presentation (Supplemental Fig. 1D, E). Moreover, at the individual animal level, we observed a significant reduction in the average slopes of the linear regressions between neuronal responses to contralateral eye stimulation and pupil size as compared to ipsilateral slopes, thus demonstrating that our results are not driven by a specific subset of animals in our study (Supplemental Fig. 1F). Next, we tested whether the state-dependent modulation of contra- and ipsi-lateral responses of a given bV1 L2/3 excitatory neuron occurs in a coordinated manner. However, we did not observe a correlation between contra- and ipsi-lateral SDI-values within single neurons (r = 0.083, Pearson correlation coefficient; Fig. 1F), and there was no correlation between the slopes of the linear regression between each neuron’s responses to contra- or ipsi-latera eye stimulation and the mean pupil size at the time of stimulation (Supplemental Fig. 1G).

Since pupil dilation and whisking strongly correlate with each other in mice (39 and Fig. 1A, Supplemental Fig. 1H–I), we next tested whether a similar state-dependent modulation of the bV1 neurons’ responses to ipsi- and contra-lateral eye stimulation is observed when the animals’ behavioural states is assessed via measurement of their facial movements (i.e., whisking41) instead of pupil dilation. Indeed, when we parsed our calcium imaging data according to the animals’ amount of whisking, we observed a similar state-dependence as when these data were parsed by pupil dilation (Supplemental Fig. 1J): the responses to contralateral eye stimulation increase significantly at high state (Low vs. High, p < 0.0001) whereas the responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation remain, on average, unaltered (Low vs. High, p = 0.4013). These results demonstrate that pupil size and whisking capture arousal states similarly and can both be used as reliable readouts of an animal’s arousal state. Of note, we found that pupil diameter and whisking each explained, on average, ~5% of the bV1 neuronal response variability while locomotion contributed smaller but non-negligible fractions (~2.5%). A combined model, which included all three factors simultaneously, accounted for ~10% of the response variance (Supplemental Fig. 2).

One possible explanation for the stronger responses of bV1 neurons to visual stimulation at high arousal might be that the pupil’s dilation at high arousal leads to enhanced activation and/or differential recruitment of retinal photoreceptors upon presentation of visual stimuli42. To test for this possibility, we repeated the experiment in a subset of mice after administering Atropine to dilate their pupils to eliminate potential differences in the amount of photoreceptors activated across arousal states (Fig. 1G). Since pupillometry cannot be used to determine the animals’ behavioural state in these experimental conditions, we assessed behavioural state by measuring facial movement (i.e., whisking). This experiment revealed that the bV1 neural response to contralateral eye stimulation increases significantly during high arousal states while the response to the ipsilateral eye stimulation remained, on average, unchanged both prior to (Low vs. High, contra: p < 0.0001, Ipsi- p = 1; Friedman test) and after Atropine application (contra: p < 0.0001 Low vs. High; Ipsi- p = 0.0584 Low vs. High; Fig. 1H). In addition, a significantly larger proportion of neurons showed stronger responses to contra- versus ipsi-lateral eye stimulation during high compared to low arousal (68% vs. 57%; p = 0.0274, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Supplemental Fig. 1K). We therefore conclude that the state-dependent modulation of the responses of bV1 excitatory neurons cannot be explained solely by the increased luminance or differential recruitment of photoreceptors in the retina at high arousal state. Notably, this state-dependent modulation can be observed despite the neurons’ enhanced responses to ipsi- or contra-lateral eye stimulation after the administration of Atropine (Fig. 1H, control vs. Atropine).

Taken together, our analyses reveal that the responses to both contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation in bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons are modulated by arousal, but that the responses to contralateral eye stimulation are more strongly and consistently enhanced than those to ipsilateral stimulation. Of note, in single bV1 neurons, the state-dependent modulations of the responses to contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation occur independently of each other.

Binocularity of bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons is modulated by arousal

The binocularity of bV1 neurons is determined by the relative strength of the responses to visual inputs arriving from the ipsi- and contra-lateral eyes. Thus, our finding that responses to contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation are differentially modulated by behavioural states suggests that a neuron’s binocularity changes in a state-dependent manner. To test this idea, we analysed the ODI of the imaged neurons during low, mid and high arousal states. This revealed that the binocularity of individual neurons is not fixed but can change rapidly in a state-dependent manner (Fig. 2A) whereby their ODI values shift, on average, towards the contralateral eye at high arousal (Fig. 2B). Indeed, we found that the neurons’ average ODI increased monotonically with arousal (Fig. 2C), indicating that the overall neuronal population becomes significantly more contralaterally driven as the animal’s arousal increases (p < 0.0001 Low vs. High). Notably, the observed state-dependent changes in ODI were not due to sampling bias, as confirmed by shuffle data analysis (see Methods and Supplemental Fig. 3A, B). Furthermore, we observed a similar state-dependent shift in binocularity when we used whisking as a readout of the animal’s arousal state, both at the single-cell level (Supplemental Fig. 3C) and at the population level (Fig. 2D, control; p = 0.0003 Low vs. High). Finally, we also observed a significant shift of the ODI toward the contralateral eye after Atropine administration (Fig. 2D, Atropine; p = 0.0152 Low vs. High; Friedman test), again confirming that the changes we observe are unlikely to be explained solely by differences in luminance due to pupil dilation at high arousal state. Notably, running also induces a significant shift in ODI towards the contralateral eye (Supplemental Fig. 3D; p = 0.00614; Wilcoxon signed rank test) which might be expected since running activity is correlated with pupil dilation and increased whisking40.

Our observation that binocularity decreases at high arousal (i.e., ODI shifts towards the contralateral eye) is consistent with our observation that responses to contralateral eye stimulation are enhanced at this behavioural state while the responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation are only weakly affected. Nevertheless, changes in binocularity can also occur due to other factors such as non-aligned eye movements at high arousal. To test for this possibility, we conducted binocular eye-tracking and measured the relative positions of the two eyes at different behavioural states (Supplemental Fig. 4A, top). Consistent with previous reports43–46, we found that the eyes’ movements in head-restrained mice are limited and mostly restricted to maximum shifts of up to ±10 degrees from the centre of the visual field which is more narrow than the visual stimuli presented in our experiments (30 degrees, see Methods and Supplemental Fig. 4A, bottom). Furthermore, we found that the distributions of eye movements are similar during low, mid and high arousal states for both eyes (Supplemental Fig. 4A, bottom), with a small yet significant average convergence of the eyes at high arousal state towards the centre of the visual field (Supplemental Fig. 4B). Since eye movements and/or behavioural state might also change the neurons’ receptive fields, we repeated the experiments with a centred full-field drifting grating stimulation. This revealed that upon full-field stimulus presentation, the differential effects of arousal on the neurons’ responses to contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation and their binocularity are similarly state-modulated as in our other experiments where the visual stimulus was restricted to 30 degrees in the centre of the animals’ visual field (Supplemental Fig. 4C, D). Thus, the shift in ODI toward the contralateral eye at high arousal cannot be explained by state-dependent lateral eye movements or changes in the neurons’ receptive field.

Finally, since nonlinearities in the readout of the calcium signal in our GCaMP imaging could lead to distorted measurements of the visually evoked responses upon ipsi- and contra-lateral eye stimulation at different behavioural states47, we examined two subpopulations of neurons: (i) neurons that at low arousal state responded equally to either ipsi- or contra-lateral eye stimulation (ODI = ~0; see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 5A–C), and (ii) neurons that responded stronger to ipsilateral eye stimulation at low arousal state (ODI < 0; see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 5D–F). We reasoned that if our results stem from nonlinearities in our calcium measurement, we will see in the first group (ODI = ~0) a similar state dependency of the responses to ipsi- and contra-lateral eye stimulation, and hence their binocularity will remain stable across behavioural states. In the second group, in the case of effects of nonlinearities, we expect to see at high state the strengthening of the responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation while the responses to contralateral eye stimulation would remain mostly unchanged, thus leading to a shift in binocularity toward the ipsilateral eye. However, when we analysed the state-dependent responses of neurons in these two subgroups we found that, similar to our findings in the general population, behavioural states modulated the responses to inputs arriving from contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation differentially and led to a shift in ODI toward the contralateral eye. To further rule out potential biases arising from nonlinearities, we binned the neurons’ responses according to their absolute mean responses (dF/F) to contra- or ipsi-lateral eye stimulation at low arousal. Within each response bin, we then compared activity across arousal states. This analysis revealed that, for the same absolute response amplitude, responses to contralateral eye stimulation significantly increased at high arousal (Low vs. High: p = 0.0078, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), whereas ipsilateral responses did not change on average (Low vs. High: p = 0.1309, Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Supplementary Fig. 5G, H). Lastly, although the neurons’ mean absolute responses were correlated with their ODI values (Supplementary Fig. 5I, J), we did not observe any correlation between a neuron’s response magnitude to contralateral eye stimulation and its SDI (r = 0.028, p = 0.4656, Pearson correlation), and only a weak correlation between responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation and the corresponding SDI (r = 0.083, p = 0.0407).

Taken together, our data and analyses indicate that transitions in behavioural states modulate the ODI of single bV1 neurons, leading to an overall decrease in an animal’s binocularity at high arousal states and to a corresponding increase in monocular vision.

Binocular integration is modulated by behavioural states

Having found that the binocularity of bV1 excitatory neurons decreases at high arousal, we next asked whether binocular integration in these neurons is affected by behavioural states as well. For this, we first analysed the responses of bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons to visual stimuli when both eyes are open (i.e., during binocular stimulation). Similar to observations by us and others in mV1 neurons27–29,31,35,48–50, we found that the responses of bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons to binocular stimulation are increased at high arousal and that this occurs both in the distribution of the responses across different arousal states (Fig. 3A) and in the mean population response (p < 0.0001 Low vs. High, Friedman test; Fig. 3B). As with monocular eye stimulation (Fig. 1H), this increase in binocularly-evoked visual responses at high arousal state was not driven by state-dependent pupil dilation (Fig. 3C). When we then calculated the neurons’ SDI we found that the majority of the cells (~66%) had a positive SDI (Supplemental Fig. 6A), thus indicating stronger evoked responses at high arousal. Also, we did not observe a correlation between the SDIs for binocular responses and ipsilateral eye responses (Fig. 3D, left, Pearson correlation, p = 0.2809) but found a significant positive correlation between the SDIs for binocular and contralateral responses (Fig. 3D, right, Pearson correlation, p = 7.6e-08), thus indicating a stronger contribution of the contralateral inputs to the binocular responses. Consistent with the findings for contralateral eye stimulation, a similar fraction of the variance in binocular responses was explained by the different behavioural variables (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Since the integration of inputs from the contra- and ipsi-lateral eyes during binocular stimulation was previously shown to be sublinear21, we next asked whether binocular integration itself is modulated by behavioural states. To this end, we analyzed separately the neurons’ normalised responses according to the stimulated eye (contralateral, ipsilateral or binocular eye stimulation), sorted these responses according to the pupil size at the time of visual stimulus presentation, and calculated moving averages of these responses (Fig. 3E); in addition, we calculated the linear sum of the normalised responses to contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation (Fig. 3E, “Sum”). This analysis revealed that the neurons’ responses to binocular stimulation transition from sublinear summation at low arousal to nearly linear summation at mid arousal and to supralinear summation at high arousal. Interestingly, this shift occurs even though the responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation are only weakly modulated by arousal, and these state-dependent effects on binocular integration can be observed also in individual mice (Fig. 3F). Importantly, similar results were obtained when analyzing neurons’ absolute mean responses (dF/F) rather than normalised values (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Moreover, when we compared the absolute summed input (i.e., sum of the responses to separately presented contra- or ipsi-lateral stimuli) across arousal states, we found that for the same summed input, the measured binocular response (both eyes open) was consistently stronger at high arousal (Supplemental Fig. 6C). Taken together, these results demonstrate that arousal affects not only the relative strength of the responses of bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons to the inputs from each eye but also modulates the neurons’ responses to the combined inputs, thereby shifting binocular integration from a sublinear regime at low arousal to a supralinear regime at high arousal.

State-dependent changes in afferent inputs or in bV1 neurons can drive shifts in visually evoked responses and binocular integration

Having found that binocular integration in bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons is modulated by behavioural states, we next sought to identify the underlying cellular-circuit mechanisms. For this, we considered two mechanisms that are not mutually exclusive: (i) differential state-dependent modulation of feedforward inputs, whereby contralateral inputs are more strengthened at high arousal than ipsilateral inputs while the input-output function of bV1 neurons (i.e., transformation from synaptic input to spiking activity) is not affected by the animals’ arousal state, and (ii) the afferent inputs are not modulated by state but the neurons’ input-output function is modulated. When we modelled these two mechanisms (Fig. 4A, B), we found that both can qualitatively account for the differential state-dependent modulation of neuronal responses to contra- and ipsi-lateral inputs as well as for the shift in binocular integration from a sublinear to a supralinear regime at high arousal. Thus, the state-dependent modulation of the afferents to bV1 excitatory neurons as well as state-dependent changes in these neurons themselves might be at play.

Thalamic and callosal axons are differentially modulated by the arousal state

Based on our modelling analyses, we next sought to test experimentally whether the state-dependent changes in the binocularity and binocular integration in bV1 neurons might be driven by differential effects of arousal on contra- and ipsilateral inputs. Responses to contralateral eye stimulation in bV1 neurons are driven primarily by inputs from the dLGN, and these thalamic inputs to mV1 were previously shown to be modulated by an animal’s behavioural state51–53. In contrast, responses to ipsilateral eye stimulation are driven to a large degree (50–80%) by callosal inputs from bV1 in the opposite hemisphere. However, it remains unknown how either of these inputs to bV1 are modulated by an animal’s behavioural state. Thus, to test whether callosal inputs to bV1 are state-modulated, we expressed GCaMP6s in bV1 excitatory neurons in one hemisphere and imaged the callosal axons’ activity in the opposite hemisphere (Fig. 5A). In agreement with previous findings in the somatosensory cortex54, and in contrast to bV1 excitatory neurons, we found that the ongoing activity of callosal axons in bV1 (i.e., activity in the absence of visual stimulation) was negatively correlated with arousal (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B; mean correlation = −0.082, p < 0.0001, Pearson correlation coefficient). When we then analysed the callosal axons’ responses upon presentation of visual stimuli to each eye separately, we found that the axons’ responses are biased toward the ipsilateral eye (relative to the imaged hemisphere; mean ODI = −0.1671; Fig. 5B); notably, most imaged axons (91%) conveyed inputs from both eyes and only a small fraction were monocular (9%). Unlike in bV1 excitatory neurons (Figs. 1D, 3B), we found that the responses of callosal axons to both contra- and ipsi-lateral eye stimulation were only weakly modulated by arousal state (Fig. 5C), with a similar fraction of axons showing a positive SDIs (55% and 50% for ipsi- and contra-lateral responses, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 7C). Consistent with this, we found that when we presented the visual stimuli to both eyes, the axons’ binocular visually-evoked responses remained, on average, stable across arousal states (Fig. 5D; p = 0.9993, Low vs. High; Friedman test). When we then analyzed LGN-derived inputs in bV1 (Fig. 5E), we found that, in contrast to the callosal inputs, the ongoing activity of LGN inputs was strongly correlated with arousal (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B) and that their responses to monocular visual stimulation were biased toward the contralateral eye (mean ODI = 0.251, Fig. 5F). Moreover, we found that both contra- and ipsi-lateral responses of these thalamocortical axons were significantly enhanced during high-arousal states (p < 0.0001 for both, Low vs. High, Friedman test; Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 7D), and that also the binocular visually evoked responses of these LGN axons were enhanced at high arousal (p = 0.006, Low vs. High, Friedman test; Fig. 5H).

Taken together, our experiments reveal that the inputs from the LGN to bV1 excitatory neurons, which convey primarily inputs from the contralateral eye, are enhanced at high arousal51,52 while inputs from callosal axons, which convey primarily inputs from the ipsilateral eye, are minimally modulated by arousal state. Thus, these findings support the idea that differential state-dependent modulation of ipsi- and contra-lateral inputs to bV1 neurons underlies at least in part the state-dependent changes in binocularity and binocular integration (Fig. 5I).

Discussion

Sensory processing is rapidly modulated by behavioural states to adapt an animal’s sensory perception to its changing behavioural demands. Previous studies in mV1 of rodents found that the activity and sensory evoked responses of excitatory neurons are enhanced during high arousal states27–29,31,35,48–50. Here, we showed that there are also state-dependent effects of arousal on binocular vision. Focusing on excitatory neurons in layer 2/3 of the adult mouse bV1, we found that (i) arousal differentially and independently affects the responses to inputs from each of the two eyes, with strongly enhanced responses to contralateral eye stimulation during high arousal states and significantly less enhanced responses upon ipsilateral stimulation, (ii) the binocularity of single neurons is not fixed but can change rapidly across behavioural states such that the overall binocularity of bV1 decreases during high arousal states, (iii) binocular integration shifts from sublinear to supralinear summation during high arousal states, i(v) the state-dependent shifts in binocularity and binocular integration are driven at least in part by the differential effect of arousal on the thalamic and callosal inputs to bV1 neurons. Importantly, we demonstrate that these rapid changes in binocularity and binocular integration result from transitions in the animal’s behavioural state and that, consistent with previous studies30,55, these changes are not explained simply by more light impinging onto the retina due to dilated pupils at high arousal or due to state-dependent eye movements.

These findings may have important implications for our understanding of the ethological functions of monocular and binocular vision across arousal states and they raise fundamental questions about the underlying cellular and circuit mechanisms. Binocular vision conveys information about the centre of an animal’s visual field (i.e., binocular visual field) while monocular vision conveys information about the periphery of an animal’s visual field. Accordingly, binocular and monocular vision serve different behavioural purposes: binocular vision is important for skilled behaviours that require high visual acuity and precise depth perception (e.g., when trying to thread a needle and/or when lunging onto a prey) while peripheral vision is important for surveying an animal’s surroundings (e.g., to gage the approach vector of a putative danger and/or to plot an escape route). Thus, our observation that at high arousal the relative gain of peripheral vision increases while the impact of binocular vision decreases makes sense from an ethological point-of-view: under these conditions, e.g., when an animal is startled by the sound of an approaching predator, detailed binocular vision is less essential than identifying the putative threat as rapidly as possible and plot an escape route. Similarly, tasks that require precise depth perception and high visual acuity (e.g., lunging onto a prey) are best solved when the animal is alert and aroused to a certain degree but not overly so30. Thus, it is interesting to speculate that the decrease in binocular vision at high arousal is particularly prominent in animals with side-wards facing eyes such as rodents since (i) these animals tend to be prey rather than predators2,56, (ii) the monocular field of view of these animals is relatively large and comprises a relatively large fraction of the animals’ total visual scene, and iii) these animals tend to be afovic (i.e., without a fovea) thus increasing the need for rapid compensatory neural mechanisms2. Future experiments based on recordings of single (binocular) V1 neurons and direct mapping of the neurons’ receptive field as well as recordings in non-rodent species combined with behavioural tracking might allow for testing these ideas.

Our observation that the binocularity of neurons in the adult visual cortex can change rapidly within seconds might seems at odds with previous findings demonstrating that changes in binocularity happen during postnatal development in a sensory-dependent manner over days and weeks and that once established, binocularity remains fixed in the adult visual cortex24,26. Since head-fixed mice in 2 P imaging sessions are mostly in low and mid arousal states and only transition to high-arousal states for relatively short periods of time, the effects that we observed in our study cannot be observed in a dataset that does not use pupillometry or related methods to specifically measure the animals’ arousal states. Thus, while our findings reveal a previously unidentified feature of adult mouse binocular processing, they do not contradict previous reports.

Our experiments reveal that an animal’s behavioural state affects not only the strength of the bV1 neurons’ responses to each input individually but also the integration of these two streams of information. Since sublinear integration is thought to preserve feature selectivity (e.g., orientation selectivity17), and since visually guided tasks seem to be performed best at mid-arousal states30, it is interesting to speculate that the shift from sub- to supra-linear summation at high arousal and the potentially resulting decrease in neuronal feature selectivity may help explain this counterintuitive finding since decreased feature selectivity is expected to reduce the animal’s performance. However, whether feature selectivity indeed decreases at high arousal state remains unclear: while some studies reported a slight yet non-significant decrease in orientation selectivity at high arousal40, other studies observed no change28,31 or even a state-dependent increase57 in orientation selectivity. These contradictory findings might be explained by the relatively low abundance of high arousal states during typical recording sessions which, in turn, might lead to more noisy measurements of orientation selectivity at high arousal - thus, longer imaging sessions in future studies might help resolve this point.

An important question concerns the cellular and circuit mechanisms that drive the rapid state-dependent changes in the binocularity and binocular integration of single visual cortex neurons observed by us: our modelling analyses reveal that two non-exclusive mechanisms could be at play, including (i) the differential state-dependent modulation of afferent ipsi- and contra-lateral inputs to bV1 neurons, and (ii) the state-dependent modulation of the input-output function of bV1 neurons themselves. Our findings, combined with the results of previous studies51,52 and in agreement with studies showing distinct effects of arousal on specific aspects of visual processing51,58,59, lend support to the first of these two mechanisms: while the visually-evoked responses of LGN neurons and their thalamocortical efferent axons to visual stimulation are enhanced during active behavioural states (Fig. 5G)51,52, the responses of callosal axons are only moderately state-modulated (Fig. 5C). Thus, since LGN neurons and their axons convey information primarily from the contralateral eye and callosal axons convey information primarily from the ipsilateral eye, these observations are consistent with the idea that the state-dependent modulation of binocularity and binocular integration in bV1 excitatory neurons are driven at least in part by the differential state-dependent modulation of their ipsi- and contra-lateral inputs (Fig. 5I). Notably, callosal axons originate from neurons in bV1 in the opposite hemisphere which themselves are modulated by state (i.e, enhance their responses at high arousal state). Thus, our results (i.e., moderate state-dependent modulation of callosal axon activity) are consistent with a recent study in the mouse somatosensory cortex54 which demonstrated that the activity of callosal axons is suppressed when an animal is in an active state (i.e., when whisking). However, since ipsi- and contra-lateral visual information is present at each stage of the visual pathway15 and since each of these stages is affected by behavioural states and/or state-dependent neuromodulatory systems27,29,55,59,60, we cannot rule out the potential contribution of additional state-dependent modulation of binocularity along the visual pathway to the observations reported here. Future experiments will have to explore this and other putative cellular and circuit mechanisms as well.

Method

Experimental model and subject details

Mice

All procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Weizmann Institutional Animals Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Recordings were made from adult mice (P70-P90) of either sex, individually housed in a 12/12 h reverse dark/light cycle. All mice used in this study were C57Bl6/J wild-type mice (Envigo, Israel).

Methods

AAV stocks

The AAV-CaMKII-jGCaMP7f-WPRE plasmid was generated by subcloning of jGCaMP7f from pGP-AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7f-WPRE (Addgene plasmid #104492) into a CaMKII AAV backbone using standard cloning methods. AAVs for this plasmid were packaged in pDJ essentially as described61,62. AAV.CamKII.GCaMP6s.WPRE.SV40 was a gift from James M. Wilson (Addgene viral prep #107790-AAV; http://n2t.net/addgene:107790; RRID:Addgene_107790).

Viral injections and cranial window implantations

Mice were anaesthetised with isoflurane at an initial concentration of 4% and maintained at a level of 1.5–2%. Mice were fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus (Kopf Instruments, model 942) and ophthalmic ointment (Duratears) was applied to the eyes. Body temperature was kept at 37 °C with a heating blanket (RWD Life science). Before the surgical procedure, local anaesthesia (2% Lidocaine) was injected under the scalp and the skin was scrubbed with 70% ethanol and betadine. A craniotomy 4 mm was made above the left bV1 (centred 2.9 mm posterior and 2.7 mm lateral to the bregma). For imaging of bV1 L2/3 excitatory neurons, 2–3 injections of 300 nL of AAV-CaMKII-jGCaMP7f-WPRE were made using a bevelled glass micropipette at a depth of 300 μM with a microsyringe pump (UMP3T-2, WPI). For the imaging of callosal axons, a single injection of 300 nL of AAV-Syn1-mScarlet-KASH-2A was made to further identify the area under the 2P. A cover glass was glued to the skull using VetBond (3 M) and a custom-made head-post was glued to the skull (Krazy glue). For imaging of callosal axons, a craniotomy was made above the right bV1 and a total of 600nL of AAV.CamKII.GCaMP6s.WPRE.SV40 was injected in three different depths (300, 500 and 700 μM). For imaging of thalamic axons, a single craniotomy was made at 2.2–2.3 mm posterior and 2–2.2 mm lateral from bregma and a total of 60 nL of AAV.CamKII.GCaMP6s.WPRE.SV40 was injected at a depth of 2.6 mm. Following surgery, the animals were administered analgesics (0.1 mg/kg of buprenorphine and 5 mg/kg of Carprofen).

2-photon microscopy for in vivo calcium imaging

Imaging was performed at least three weeks after AAV injection and following the habituation of the mice to head-fixation, running on the treadmill, and usage of the motorised shutter system (see visual stimulation section). Imaging was done using a two-photon microscope with a 12 kHz resonant-galvo scanhead (Bergamo microscope, ThorLabs) at three planes using a piezoelectric linear actuator (P-726, Physik Instrumente) to sequentially image 3 z-planes, resulting in an acquisition rate of 11.4 Hz. Frame size was 512 × 512 pixels. Illumination was provided by a Mai Tai DeepSee laser at 930 nM. Data was acquired using ThorImageLS (version 4.0).

Behavioural monitoring

Facial videography

During imaging, right and left pupils and facial movement were monitored simultaneously by placing two CMOS cameras angled at 58–60° relative to the animals’ antero-posterior axis. The eyes were illuminated with an IR-light source (M940L3, Thorlabs) and imaged at 33 Hz. Data was synchronised with stimulus presentation using pClamp (Molecular Devices).

Running speed

mice were placed on a liner treadmill (Arnold, Jon (2023). Rodent Belt Treadmill. Janelia Research Campus. Physical object. 10.25378/janelia.24691311), and running speed was monitored using an encoder which tracks the movement of the belt.

Atropine experiments

In a cohort of 5 mice, a drop of 30 μl of Atropine (1%, Fischer Pharmaceuticals Ltd) was applied to each of the animals’ eyes before the imaging session. Once the pupils were fully dilated (~1 h after Atropine application) the animals were placed in the experimental setup for the imaging session.

Visual stimulation

Visual stimuli were created using MATLAB with Psychophysics Toolbox (http://psychtoolbox.org) and presented on a gamma-corrected LCD screen. The monitor was positioned 20 cm from the centre of the eyes and a circular patch of drifting sinusoidal gratings on a grey background (set to 30° of the animal’s visual field) stimulated the mouse’s binocular visual field. Prior to full recordings, the binocular zone of V1 was determined by receptive field mapping. Mapping stimuli, sinusoidal drifting bar gratings of 1 s with a spatial frequency of 0.04 cycles per degree, temporal frequency of 2 Hz, and 100% contrast were displayed in 6 evenly spaced orientations presented in a pseudorandom order. Each stimulus type was presented 15 times. Mapping stimuli were presented only to the ipsilateral eye and responses were analysed to determine the optimal field of view. On the day of the experiment, eyes were stimulated using a motorised shutter system that allowed for interleaved stimulation of each eye separately as well as two additional stimulation conditions: one when both eyes are open and the second when both eyes are blocked by the shutters (used as a control condition to make sure that the shutters indeed block the eyes). The transition between shutter combinations occurred 5 s before the presentation of visual stimuli. Sinusoidal drifting bar gratings of 2 s were shown with an interstimulus interval of 4 s in which a grey screen of mean luminance was presented. Stimuli were shown with a spatial frequency of 0.04 cycles per degree, temporal frequency of 2 Hz, and 100% contrast, displayed in 4 evenly spaced orientations, presented in a pseudorandom order. Each stimulus type was presented 30–40 times per shutter combination in a pseudorandom order. At the end of the visual stimulation session recording, ongoing activity was recorded for 15 min when no visual stimuli were presented and a grey screen of mean luminance was displayed.

In the first model, the afferent input varies as a function of the animal’s state (indicated by pupil size), while the neuron’s input-output function remains state-independent. In the second model, the afferent input is constant (state-independent), but the neuron’s input-output function is modulated by the animal’s state. We show that both models can equally account for the observed state-dependent ocular dominance and binocular integration.

Data analyses

Following registration, segmentation, and extraction of fluorescence values, all data were analysed using custom-written software in MATLAB (The MathWorks). Pupil size and facial movement (whisking) were analysed using custom-written software in LabView (NI). Unless otherwise noted, the sample size (n) for each experimental group was consistent throughout all analysis and all error bars represent mean ± s.e.m.

Pupil size measurement

Frames were filtered using a median filter and thresholded to low IR light reflectance areas. The resulting regions were then filtered based on circularity and size until only the region corresponding to the pupil remained, as verified manually during each analysis. Consequently, an ellipse was fitted to this region by setting its major and minor axes equal to the longer and shorter lines of symmetry of the bounding rectangle. The pupil diameter was estimated as the geometric mean of the major and minor axes. Pupil diameter for each animal was smoothed with a Savitzky–Golay filter with a first-order polynomial and a window size of 11 points. Pupil size was normalised to the maximum pupil size during the recording and thresholded to determine low (norm. pupil <0.15), mid (0.15 <norm. pupil <0.35), and high state (norm. pupil >0.35) of the animal (see Fig. 1B). Maximum pupil dilation (normalised pupil value of 1) was determined by an induced high state of each mouse.

Eye movement

Eye movements were measured by first converting the relative movement of the pupil from the centre (determined as the most frequent position of the pupil centroid) from pixel to mm (m). For measuring the movement in terms of degree of visual angle (α) we assume that the centre moves on the surface of a sphere with the diameter(d) = 3.4 mm63 α = 360 m/πd.

Whisking measurement

Whisking data was extracted from the facial movies as previously described54. ROIs from both whisker peds were selected using a costume-written GUI in LabView. The whisking movement was computed as the squared difference in intensity between the pixels of each pair of consecutive frames. The whisking signal was normalised to the maximum during the recording, averaged over the 2 s of visual stimulation, and thresholded to determine the low (lower 30 percentile values), mid, and high (upper 30 percentile values) state of the animal.

Running periods

Running periods were defined as periods when the treadmill speed was greater than 0.5 cm s−1 after smoothing the treadmill signal, during visual stimulus presentation.

Analysis of Ca2 + 2-photon imaging data

Raw calcium movies were analysed using Suite2p64. Neuropil corrected signals were resampled to 100 Hz using a linear interpolation. For cross-correlation measurements, F signals were smoothed with a Savitzky–Golay filter with a first-order polynomial and a window size of 11 points. Cross-correlation coefficient (CC), at 0 time-lag, of ongoing activity was calculated between all possible pairs per recordings and between each ROI and the calculated pupil diameter (see below) for a time window of 15 min (obtained in a separate imaging session). Shuffle correlations were computed as the correlation between each imaged ROI and the inverse signal of the pupil or one of the ROIs in the cell-to-cell correlation. Baseline fluorescence (F0) was calculated as the mean fluorescence of the 1 s preceding presentation of visual stimulation. Responses <0 were set to be 0. To determine if a cell was significantly responsive to the drifting grating stimulus, a one-way Kruskal Wallis test was computed comparing the evoked responses to baseline fluorescence. For measuring binocularity (ODI, see below), a cell was called visually responsive if P < 0.05 and its average response >6% to either contra or ipsi eye stimulation. For each cell, the preferred orientation was defined as the orientation in which the gratings elicited the largest response to either one of the eyes and was considered as matched between the eyes65. For measuring binocular integration, a cell was considered as visually responsive when both eyes were open if P < 0.05 and its average response >6%. Each trial was defined as a low, mid, or high state based on the average normalised pupil diameter measured 1 s after the start of visual stimulation for a total duration of 2 s so that it matched the duration of the visual stimulus27.

Shuffled data

For each visually responsive cell, after splitting its responses to the 3 different states according to the mean pupil size at the time of visual stimulation, we randomly draw an equal number of responses to the visual stimulus at each state ignoring the corresponding pupil size. The cell responses were then averaged across all the trials in each pseudo-state and ODIs were calculated.

Variance analysis

To quantify the contribution of different behavioural variables to response variability, we fit a multiple linear regression model to the activity of each recorded bV1 neuron, using pupil diameter, whisking, and locomotion (as well as their combinations) as predictors. Mice without documented running velocity were excluded from analyses that included locomotion as a predictor. partial r² values were averaged across neurons, per condition, per mouse, and then across mice.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The number of experimental recordings and animals used in each experiment is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical tests were performed using MATLAB. For comparisons of responses across the three experimental conditions throughout the manuscript, we used the Friedman test followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. P-values and statistical tests used are indicated in the figure legends.
