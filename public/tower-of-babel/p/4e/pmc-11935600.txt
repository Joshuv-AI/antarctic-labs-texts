Viral Infection and Ischemic Stroke: Emerging Trends and Mechanistic Insights
Authors: Michael Clarke, Sarina Falcione, Roobina Boghozian, Raluca Todoran, Yiran Zhang, Maria Guadalupe C. Real, Alexis StPierre, Twinkle Joy, Glen C. Jickling
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Population studies have suggested that viral infections may be contributing to risk of ischemic stroke, although the mechanisms for this are unclear. In this review, we examine the epidemiological evidence supporting the involvement of viral diseases, including influenza, COVID‐19, chronic herpesvirus infections, and hepatitis C in current trends of stroke incidence. To support these associations, we highlight the virus–host interactions that are critical in the context of stroke, including direct effects of acute and persistent viral infections on vascular function, inflammation, and thrombosis. Additionally, we evaluate the systemic changes that occur during viral infection that can predispose individuals to ischemic stroke, including alterations in blood pressure regulation, coagulation, and lipid metabolism. Our review emphasizes the need to further elucidate precise mechanisms involved in viral infections and stroke risk. Future research will inform the development of targeted interventions for stroke prevention in the context of viral diseases.

Vascular Effects of Infection in Pediatric Stroke

Viral infections have been increasingly recognized as contributors to complex diseases. Ischemic stroke is often preceded by vascular injury or dysfunction, which compromises blood supply to the brain. The recent COVID‐19 pandemic has brought attention to viral interactions within our bodies that may contribute to ischemic stroke. This relationship is mediated through a complex interplay of endothelial cell injury, inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and immune interactions.

A rise in ischemic stroke incidence has been observed during colder months, parallelling seasonal infections and suggesting viruses may play some role in stroke cause. 1 Clinical observations have observed trends in influenza and COVID‐19, reporting a possible correlation to ischemic stroke incidence. 2 , 3 Further support for this association comes from reporting of reduced stroke risk as a result of vaccination to influenza, COVID‐19, chickenpox, or shingles. 4 , 5 , 6 The potential changes to stroke risk, as related to viral infection or immunization, appear to be more pronounced among a younger population. 3 , 4

Several viruses are known to play a direct role in conditions that precede an ischemic stroke event. For example, complications arising from chickenpox, shingles, and other common herpetic lesions can manifest as vasculopathies in cerebral arteries, which change the properties of the blood vessel walls, reducing blood supply within the brain. 7 , 8 Other viral illnesses, such as mononucleosis or respiratory illness may play a role in systemic vascular damage, blood clotting, homeostatic changes, and immune function. For each change, the risk of an ischemic stroke event is increased.

Despite many clinical reports that implicate a virus in stroke, investigations into the mechanisms of acute viral infections and ischemic stroke pathogenesis have returned mixed results. Viral contributions to ischemic stroke may not all be the result of a recent acute infection, but rather a consequence of repeated interactions between virus and host tissues over time. The Northern Manhattan Study, which compared 5 common infections (3 of them viral) to stroke incidence over an 8‐year period, found that individual infections did not significantly change stroke risk; however, the cumulative “infectious burden” of multiple infections resulted in a significant risk increase. 9

In this review, we summarize the role human viral infections, whether acute or persistent, may have in ischemic stroke. This includes examples from common viruses, and their contribution to a state of increased risk of ischemic stroke.

Seasonal Trends in Stroke

A pattern of increased stroke incidence has been observed during the colder months of fall and winter. This pattern exists across many studies, representing multiple countries, climates, and time periods. 1 There are multiple proposed explanations for this trend, including reduced physical activity, changes in diet, and higher blood pressure due to cold and dry weather.

When comparing rates of ischemic stroke and influenza‐like‐illnesses over multiple seasons, the rise in illness during the winter months significantly correlates to a rise in hospitalization due to ischemic stroke, peaking at 15 to 30 days following illness. 3 , 27 There is overlap between seasonal viral infections that may cause an influenza‐like‐illness and those that have been observed to promote a stroke‐related condition. Among these viruses, are influenza A (IAV) and B, parvovirus B19 (B19V), and SARS‐CoV‐2. 28 , 29

Vaccination and Stroke Risk

Lower rates of stroke among individuals with specific prophylactic or therapeutic treatments for viral infection provide further support for a viral relationship with stroke.

The increased risk of ischemic stroke following influenza infection is supported by a wealth of data from large‐scale population studies comparing vaccination status and stroke incidence. In a Canadian study of public health records, a total of over 4 million residents in the province of Alberta were evaluated for stroke incidence based on vaccination status. 4 Importantly, covariate analysis for common risk factors of stroke, age, sex, residence, and income level was included, determining that a reduction in stroke risk was present in consideration of most other factors. Notably, the highest reduction in risk was observed in ischemic stroke when an individual had received multiple yearly influenza vaccinations. A previous population study in France had found this effect, with similar results across covariate subgroups, including a pattern for increasing reduction of stroke risk with more regular vaccination over a 5‐year period. 10 From these results, vaccination may be preventing viral mechanisms from triggering ischemic stroke or compounding other risk factors. Additionally, a reduced history of infection through regular vaccination may prevent harmful effects from multiple interactions with viruses.

Since 1995, the chickenpox vaccine has been in use in the United States. Primary infection with varicella‐zoster virus (VZV) often occurs in childhood and presents as varicella (chickenpox). Although rare, VZV is a significant contributor to ischemic stroke in children. Among a cohort study of 70 children with ischemic stroke, 31% presented with varicella in the 12 months preceding their stroke, whereas the age‐matched varicella rate among the general population was estimated at only 9%. 15 Further research, and perhaps aging of the vaccinated population is needed to determine if this vaccine can lower ischemic stroke incidence.

Reactivated VZV infection in adults presents as herpes zoster (shingles). Multiple studies have shown a significant increase in stroke risk following zoster. Occasionally, complications including viral vasculopathy accompany zoster, which may play a role in this increased risk. From 2006 to 2017, a live‐attenuated zoster vaccine was approved for use in the United States, which reduced zoster incidence in elderly individuals by approximately 51% to 65%. The approval of a recombinant vaccine in 2017 saw this effect rise to approximately 90%. 6 The effect of these vaccinations on ischemic stroke incidence is yet to be determined. However, population studies have evaluated the incidence of ischemic stroke in vaccinated and unvaccinated elderly populations who (irrespective of vaccination status) presented with zoster. The incidence of ischemic stroke within 1 year following zoster has been reduced in vaccinated populations, with the greatest effect being observed in those who had received the recombinant vaccine. 6 , 30 These findings may suggest that other manifestations of VZV reactivation, not protected by the earlier vaccine, may be related to ischemic stroke. The shingles vaccine is currently recommended for use in older patients (>50) to reduce the presentation of zoster rash and any subsequent complications such as postherpetic neuralgia and vasculopathy. Further research on vaccinated populations is required to determine the effectiveness of the shingles vaccine on reducing complications, including ischemic stroke. In complicated cases of VZV reactivation, antiviral drugs may be used to reduce the severe adverse effects that often arise in these cases. No reduction in stroke risk has been confirmed in patients who have received these antiviral treatments, suggesting that vaccination may still be the most effective approach to reducing the stroke‐associated viral effects of VZV. 17

Although epidemiological evidence is not fully established, ischemic stroke risk may be increased following infection with SARS‐CoV‐2, the causative agent of COVID‐19. Multiple neurological complications have been reported following COVID‐19, including ischemic stroke. 31 Given the recency of this pandemic, the available data to implicate SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in stroke cause, and the ability of vaccination to alter rates of ischemic stroke, remain to be determined. However, a retrospective analysis of >60 000 patients with COVID‐19 in Korea found that full vaccination resulted in a 60% risk reduction of reported ischemic stroke at a 90‐day follow‐up as compared with unvaccinated controls. 13

Trends in Age‐Related Stroke

Complications arising from the mother or fetus during pregnancy may contribute to perinatal acute ischemic stroke. As a result, the risk of stroke in neonates is higher relative to older children. 32 Maternal‐borne viral infections like herpes simplex virus (HSV), cytomegalovirus, and B19V may trigger an inflammatory response in the placenta, fetal membranes, and within the fetus itself. 33 Inflammation is a risk factor for perinatal acute ischemic stroke, as it may lead to thrombosis or vascular injury, which compromises fetal blood supply. Inflammation of the fetal membranes, known as chorioamnionitis, has been linked to a significant increase to the risk of perinatal acute ischemic stroke. 34 Exact mechanisms linking viral infections to chorioamnionitis or other inflammatory conditions associated with pregnancy are still unconfirmed.

From childhood on, the risk of stroke increases with age. Ischemic stroke during childhood is rare; however, viral infections have been associated with an increased risk. The VIPS (Vascular Effects of Infection in Pediatric Stroke) study was a large‐scale case–control examination of multiple factors in pediatric stroke. In this study, serological evidence of primary infection with VZV, HSV, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), and B19V was more present among children with ischemic stroke. 14 , 21 These infections are normally acquired as a child or young adult without causing disease and remain in the body throughout life. The higher representation of infection within children with ischemic stroke may indicate a higher risk of complications during infancy.

The rate of ischemic stroke among younger adults (<55) is on the rise. 35 Evidence supports a corresponding shift in established risk factors into younger populations, which contributes for a significant part of this trend. 36 These risk factors are on average less progressed in the younger population, making the association less clear. However, ischemic stroke with rare or undetermined causesis highest in the younger population. 37 Viral infections may contribute to this trend as distinct causes or as compounding factors to existing risk factors. Boehme et al. observed increased risk of ischemic stroke following influenza‐like‐illnesses. In this study, using 36 975 records from the 2009 California inpatient database, the strongest correlation occurred in the youngest patient group (18–45), with a decreasing risk of 7% for every 10‐year age interval beyond 45. 3 A meta‐analysis of ischemic stroke reports following VZV reactivation observed the same trend of decreasing ischemic stroke risk with age. 38 In the Canadian study of influenza vaccine and stroke risk, the highest relative reduction in risk was observed among the youngest cohorts. 4 These reports support an increased contribution of virus‐related factors in ischemic stroke incidence among young adults.

Virus–Host Interactions and the Relationship to Stroke

A virus can have different interactions with a human host, which are important to consider when examining the relationship to stroke. Common acute viral infections include respiratory infection or gastroenteritis. The acute infection can induce inflammation, enhance thrombosis, and disrupt endothelial function, which may contribute to ischemic stroke as discussed in greater detail subsequently.

Some viruses establish a persistent infection in humans, which have long‐standing interactions with the host immune system. Herpesviruses such as HSV and VZV establish latent infections. Periodic reactivation of a latent virus results in acute infection and inflammatory response that can also contribute to ischemic stroke. Chronic viral infections such as HIV‐1 or hepatitis C virus (HCV), maintain low levels of activity, progressively influencing vascular health and immune response, which may be important to ischemic stroke risk over time. 22 , 24

Other viral interactions occur normally throughout the body as viruses are a significant part of the microbiome, called the virome. Along with bacterial, plant, and animal viruses, many human viruses are normal to the body and are important to immune development. Multiple enteric viruses are found within the healthy human gut but have the capacity to become pathogenic, potentially contributing to stroke‐related pathologies. 26 , 39 We present these data in a subsequent section including a discussion of the viruses within the gut virome and the role they may play in ischemic stroke.

Acute Infection and Stroke

Inflammation during acute viral infection can increase the risk of ischemic stroke through changes in blood pressure, coagulation, immune response, and endothelial function. Respiratory infections such as influenza and COVID‐19 result in sharp increases of inflammatory markers associated with vascular pathologies. 40 Mouse stroke models with respiratory infection show increased brain damage and risk of intracerebral hemorrhage following recanalization. However, in the presence of cytokine blockers, stroke outcome is normalized. 41 Here we discuss acute viral infections and their association to stroke.

IAV and influenza B virus are respiratory viruses that predominantly cause seasonal influenza‐like illness. As previously mentioned, this illness may have an effect on ischemic stroke incidence. 3 A Scottish study supported this finding by showing that laboratory‐confirmed influenza virus infections contributed to a 5‐ to 7‐fold increase in risk of ischemic stroke within the following month. 42 Among the respiratory viruses found in patients of this study, the effect of influenza (of either type) was the most significant.

SARS‐CoV‐2 causes the acute respiratory disease COVID‐19. Early reporting began describing neurological symptoms in patients hospitalized with COVID‐19. 43 Often, these results found an increased risk of ischemic stroke but were not able to exclude common risk factors. A recent meta‐analysis assessed risk of ischemic stroke over a large multinational cohort and did not find a significant association between COVID‐19 and ischemic stroke. 44 However, this study did find that stroke among patients with COVID‐19 was less attributable to traditional risk factors. SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is shown to cause chronic inflammation, thrombosis, and changes to vascular function, suggesting a potential role in ischemic stroke causemay still exist. 11 , 12 To explore this, further mechanistic study and long‐term analysis are needed.

B19V is the causative agent of erythema infectiosum (“fifth disease”), which is a mild rash syndrome found commonly in children. Adults can get a mild or subclinical respiratory infection. Stroke is a commonly cited complication in children with sickle‐cell disease who get B19V infection. This is potentially attributable to the combined hypoxic effects of the underlying disease and the depletion of B19V‐infected erythroid progenitor cells. 45 In the VIPS study, 6% of children who had ischemic stroke had serologically confirmed B19V infections, as compared with 0% of controls without stroke. 14 However the presence of sickle‐cell disease in the patient cohort was not assessed or excluded. Although not associated with erythema infectiosum, interactions between the disseminated virus and nonerythroid tissues may have off‐target effects that correspond to ischemic stroke risk. 46 Of interest, polymerase chain reaction detection of parvovirus DNA long after the acute phase has suggested that the virus may persist in these off‐target tissues. This persistence has been implicated in several chronic inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, including damage to endothelium. 47

Persistent Viral Infections and Stroke

Persistent viral infections occur following an acute period. A virus may be able to persist within the body by infecting specific tissues or changing infection dynamics. Chronic infections establish a chronically active state that presents as long‐term disease. Viruses such as HCV and HIV‐1 are examples of chronic infections that have been studied in the context of increased stroke risk. Another persistent form of infection is latency. This is distinguishable from chronic infection by the intermittent appearance of detectable active virus within the body during reactivation. Reactivation may be accompanied by clinical symptoms, some of which have been implicated in stroke cause. All herpesvirus infections can establish latent infection and persist for the life of the host. These infections, such as HSV, VZV, cytomegalovirus, and EBV, are endemic within the human population, with increased activity with advancing age, possibly contributing to vasculopathy, thrombosis, and stroke.

Human herpesviruses are divided into 3 subfamilies, which have important differences in tissue tropism and host reservoir for latency. These differences may determine the mechanisms by which the virus contributes to ischemic stroke. HSV and VZV are herpesviruses belonging to the same alphaherpesvirus subfamily and can reside in ganglions of the peripheral nervous system. After primary infection, viral particles migrate to sensory ganglia, the typical sites of latency. Reactivation of latent virus involves traveling along axonal routes to peripheral tissues like the skin. Owing to this neurotropism, these viruses can often trigger neurological symptoms. Both primary infection and reactivation of VZV have been implicated in ischemic stroke risk. In a study of childhood ischemic stroke, 31% of cases had a varicella rash within the 12‐month period preceding stroke event, representing a 3‐fold increase in varicella incidence over the general population. 15 Similarly, risk of stroke was increased by >30% for the 12‐month period following zoster in adults. 16 Importantly, these studies predated the use of either varicella or zoster vaccinations in their respective countries. In the VIPS study, acute HSV infection was the most common herpesvirus infection found in children with ischemic stroke. 21 Although reactivation of HSV typically causes perioral (HSV‐1) or genital herpetic lesions (HSV‐2), neurological complications including stroke can occur. In a retrospective case study of herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE), 5.6% of patients had ischemic stroke during hospitalization for HSE. 18

Cytomegalovirus is a member of the second subfamily, betaherpesvirus. Infection can initially cause flulike symptoms (mononucleosis) followed by a state of latency residing in hematopoietic cells. Reactivation occurs over time, often with few, if any clinical symptoms. Such subclinical reactivation can affect endothelial function and may contribute to vascular disease progression. 48 Cytomegalovirus infection of vascular tissue leading to the development of atherosclerotic plaques is a known complication in transplant patients, leading to graft rejection and poor long‐term outcomes. The use of antiviral treatment during transplantation is effective in reducing rates of graft rejection and atherosclerosis. 20 Although transplant patients are immunosuppressed, some evidence supports cytomegalovirus may cause progression of vascular disease in healthy patients as well. A study that analyzed serological positivity to cytomegalovirus among patients with stroke as compared with controls found that a recent active cytomegalovirus infection independently increases the risk of stroke incidence by 3‐fold. 19

EBV is a gammaherpesvirus, the third subfamily. Like cytomegalovirus, EBV is also a causative agent of mononucleosis; however, illness is followed by establishing latency in B‐cells. As such, EBV has been associated with immune dysregulation, lymphomas, and autoimmune diseases. 49 Although evidence linking EBV to stroke is scant, several case reports documented stroke following EBV infection. 50 The international VIPS study also found acute EBV infection to be twice as prevalent among children with ischemic stroke. 21

Chronic viral diseases cause gradual degenerative changes that may increase ischemic stroke risk over extended periods of time. Chronic hepatitis associated with HCV is a known chronic inflammatory condition and risk factor in cardiovascular disease. In patients with HCV, other traditional risk factors for stroke are often present, and potentially compounded by the viral infection. Analysis of HCV infection among patients with stroke shows a 2‐fold increase in ischemic stroke risk when compared with a control population matched for risk factors. 22 In retrospective analysis, the use of interferon‐based therapy on patients with chronic hepatitis in Taiwan resulted in a significant reduction of stroke. 23

HIV‐1 causes another chronic life‐long infection and is reported to accelerate vascular disease and atherosclerosis, thus contributing to stroke risk. HIV‐associated neurological disease can cause white matter injury, which may affect microvascular circulation leading to ischemia. In a patient cohort study, patients infected with HIV‐1 had an increased risk of stroke as compared with uninfected controls, independent of traditional risk factors. 24 Additionally, the use of antiretrovirals which reduce circulating HIV‐1 virus has been demonstrated to reduce the risk of stroke occurrence upon follow‐up with treated patients. 25 It is important to note that in addition to the direct effects of viral infection, progressive AIDS predisposes the body to multiple coinfections, including HCV and herpesviruses, which may contribute to vascular injury and stroke.

Virome and Stroke

Resident viruses of the body interact with our tissues and immune system, potentially contributing to disease. These viruses, collectively known as the virome, include bacteriophages, diet‐acquired plant and animal viruses, and human viruses. The gastrointestinal tract is host to much of the known human virome, which often includes multiple species of enterovirus. The Enterovirus genus includes both highly pathogenic viruses, like poliovirus, as well as nonpolio enteroviruses, which include the causative agents of mild disease such as hand, foot, and mouth disease. Multiple potentially pathogenic enterovirus species have been found in stool and blood samples of healthy individuals indicating the presence of other factors in a diseased state. 51 Some proposed disease causesinvolve the persistence of enteroviruses in the intestine and other tissues, suggesting that they may be carried asymptomatically but have immunomodulatory effects that are being studied in diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease and type 1 diabetes. 52

Like polioviruses, many nonpolio enteroviruses are neurotropic and are also known to cause encephalitis and meningitis. 53 These pathologies overlap viral infections with a more direct link to ischemic stroke cause, involving cerebral vasculopathy, neuroinflammation, and atherosclerosis, although evidence is currently limited to clinical reporting. In a virome analysis study of patients with stroke, enteroviral RNA was identified in the sera of 23.6% patients with stroke compared with only 2.9% of healthy controls. 26 Although in this case, the stroke event may have led to opportunistic viral infection, ~18% of the polymerase chain reaction‐positive patients in this study had negative serology (IgM or IgG), perhaps indicating an absence of newly established acute enteroviral infection. As such, further work is needed to better understand the relationship of human virome to stroke and vascular disease.

Viral Mechanisms in Vasculopathy

Vasculopathy is a broad term that refers to disease of the blood vessel. Viruses can produce vasculopathy by altering endothelial function and initiating inflammatory processes. Such changes may resemble the acute or chronic inflammatory pathologies of vascular disease, a risk factor of stroke. Changes in cranial arteries or brain microvasculature cause stenosis and present an immediate risk for ischemia, whereas vasculopathy in peripheral sites can result in plaque formation and embolism. Vasculopathy That Affects Cerebral Arteries

Multiple viruses are thought to trigger vasculopathy, but evidence of this within the brain is lacking. Alphaherpesvirus infections (VZV, HSV‐1, and HSV‐2) are acquired through cutaneous routes but have the unique ability to establish latency in neurons of sensory ganglia. These viruses are understood to travel along axons to cutaneous sites following reactivation, manifesting as herpetic lesions. Other routes may be possible, including viral spread along perivascular nerve fibers toward cerebral and temporal arteries resulting in vasculopathy. 7 Herpesvirus vasculopathies can often present as perivascular swelling causing stenosis but are also seen as localized or multifocal lesions on cerebral vessel walls. 66 Differences in disease presentation, as well as the ability to reach the central nervous system or cerebral arteries may be related to the site of latency or cellular factors of the neurons within. Studying reactivation is challenging because latent virus and the diseased arteries reside in deep neural tissue, which is hard to access. Postmortem tissue can be used; however, tissue death may activate latent virus. In addition, the species specificity of these viruses prevents the use of natural animal models.

Cases of childhood stroke are often accompanied by focal cerebral arteriopathy (FCA). This vasculopathy is almost exclusively observed in children and has been correlated through observational reports with recent herpesvirus infection. 21 Compared with other viral‐associated vasculopathies discussed here, FCA is unifocal and more localized. FCA affects the distal internal carotid artery and its branches, most commonly the M1 branch of the middle cerebral artery. 34 VZV and HSV‐1 have been implicated in the development of FCA, as the trigeminal ganglion, which innervates the internal carotid artery, is a preferred site of latency for these viruses. Supporting this relationship, FCA is frequently observed following primary VZV infection (varicella), in which case it is referred to as postvaricella arteriopathy. The increased risk of ischemic stroke borne by postvaricella arteriopathy coincides with the previously discussed risk period following varicella in children. However, the clinical manifestation of reactivated VZV, herpes zoster, rarely precedes the associated ischemic strokes in childhood cases. To address this, Grose et al. have proposed a “round trip” model where a portion of VZV remains active through transmission to the trigeminal ganglion and continuing to the cerebral arteries to cause postvaricella arteriopathy. 67 FCA is still observed in children who do not have evidence of VZV infection, or are vaccinated for varicella, which suggests multiple possible causes of FCA, including other herpesviruses like HSV‐1. 34

Vasculopathy is also a known complication following herpes zoster. This condition, known more broadly as VZV vasculopathy, is observed mostly in the vasculature of the brain. This specificity for arteries of the brain suggests that VZV may not be reaching the arteries through circulation. Human brain vascular adventitial fibroblasts can be productively infected in vitro with VZV, resulting in migration, release of cytokines and virus‐containing microparticles. 55 These findings support a model for VZV vasculitis where reactivated virus travels along axons to cranial arteries and through the arterial wall via adventitial fibroblasts. Activated fibroblasts migrate toward the intimal layer where cytokine release and viral spread triggers inflammatory pathways in endothelial cells. The absence of an external elastic lamina in cerebral arteries (as compared with systemic arteries) may allow for this migration to occur, and explain why this vasculopathy is not typically seen in other tissues. 7

Temporal arteritis (TA) is an inflammatory vasculopathy within temporal arteries and is often found as a precursor to ischemic stroke in older adults. 54 A tissue biopsy of superficial temporal arteries can be taken from a live patient, providing a unique opportunity for in vivo detection of active viruses within the brain circulation. 68 Multiple infections have been implicated in causing TA; however, antigen isolation of TA biopsies most significantly supports VZV involvement. 66 Further supporting this, a targeted RNAseq analysis of TA biopsies showed vascular remodeling and inflammation in the presence of VZV antigen. 54

Although HSV‐1 and HSV‐2 are commonly associated with herpetic lesions of the mouth and genitals (respectively), these lesions can be caused by either virus. Similarly, HSV‐1 is most commonly found in cases of HSE but can likely present with either infection. 8 HSV accounts for most cases of infectious encephalitis and can result in cerebrovascular complications including stroke. 18 HSE is observed in the presence or absence of detectable virus in the central nervous system. 8 There is some evidence that HSV is present in TA biopsies; however, substantially less is known about this potential cause. In cases of HSE, vascular inflammation and intimal layer thickening can be observed through MRI, resembling the presentation of VZV vasculopathy. 69 In a review of case reports of HSE, hemorrhagic stroke was more often associated with HSV‐1 infection, whereas multifocal ischemia caused by a large vessel vasculitis was associated with HSV‐2. 70 This may indicate the role that primary site of infection and site of latency have in the presentation of disease.

Like VZV, HSV may travel to cerebral arteries through axonal transport, but less is known about mechanisms for viral dissemination through this route. HSV can infect endothelial cells directly, and some evidence supports a role in atherosclerosis in other tissues. 56 , 71 Active HSV may disseminate following lesions in cutaneous sites, reaching cerebral circulation and crossing the blood–brain barrier. HSV gains entry to endothelial cells by attaching the viral envelope to galectin‐1 protein. 72 Infected endothelial cells are dysfunctional, with increased leukocyte adhesion, impaired transport ability, and increased expression of coagulation cascade effectors. 73 , 74 , 75 This dysregulation results in granulocyte‐mediated injury to the infected endothelium, thrombus formation and vasculopathy. He et al. reported downregulation of the Golgi‐associated protein GM130 within HSV‐1 infected endothelial cells, leading to Golgi fragmentation, cell apoptosis, and downregulation of tight junction proteins. 73 Disruption of tight junction proteins was already demonstrated within infected cerebral arteries, resulting in the disruption of the blood–brain barrier, a feature present in HSE. 57 Loss of barrier integrity, in conjunction with other inflammatory processes, may explain the perivascular inflammation, hemorrhage, and entry of virus into cerebral vascular tissue.

Systemic Vasculopathy That Affects Stroke

Vasculopathy in tissues outside of the brain also increases the risk of stroke. Diseased systemic arteries can lead to embolism. Although systemic arteries are less proximal to the brain, narrowing vessel walls may elevate blood pressure or cause ischemia through reducing cerebral perfusion pressure. Multiple processes during infection can contribute to these pathologies; however, viruses such as cytomegalovirus and B19V, which can infect vascular tissue, are of interest as direct triggers for vasculopathy.

Cytomegalovirus can infect endothelial cells causing inflammatory response and dysregulated cellular function. 76 This may lead to vascular injury, atherosclerosis, and thrombosis. Initial cytomegalovirus infection is often subclinical but may present as mononucleosis before entering latency. Reactivated virus has been found in myeloid progenitors and in the short‐lived blood monocytes, suggesting that the sites of latency are hematopoietic cells of the bone marrow. 58 Much is still unknown about cytomegalovirus latency, but this model provides a basis for disseminated virus and access to vessel walls. In addition, mouse models (using murine cytomegalovirus) have discovered genes for persistence and possibly latency within endothelial cells and macrophages. 77 The persistence within these cells would support a direct role in chronic inflammation of vessels and atherosclerosis.

Cytomegalovirus reactivation tends to increase with age, paralleling other stroke risk factors. 48 Reactivation is a major concern in posttransplant or immunocompromised cases. In transplant patients who are cytomegalovirus positive, vascular tissue damage and vessel wall thickening is observed in immunosuppressed tissue, which can affect blood supply to transplanted tissue and result in rejection. 76 This observation has also been recapitulated in mouse xenografts, revealing that infected grafts showed greatly increased markers for endothelial adhesion, vascular remodeling, and monocyte infiltration. 59 Healthy mouse models infected with murine cytomegalovirus have viral antigen within sections of aortic walls, coupled with focal accumulation of inflammatory cells, resembling early atherosclerotic plaque formation. 78

B19V targets erythroid progenitor cells within the bone marrow, establishing an acute infection that results in transient disruption of erythropoiesis. Following acute phase, a secondary viremia occurs with high levels of virus in the peripheral blood. This secondary viremia also allows for the systemic distribution of the virus and can lead to late clinical manifestations of infection. 79 There is no well‐defined mechanism for viral persistence, although the B19V DNA genome has been found in nonerythroid tissues long after the acute period. 80 The main receptor used for viral entry into erythroid progenitor cells is the glycosphingolipid globoside (also known as blood type P‐antigen). This receptor is present on many different cell types, so there are likely cell‐specific coreceptors or cellular functions involved in entry. In vitro, B19V on its own has a low level of viral entry into endothelial cells. However, with the addition of anti‐B19V antibodies, uptake of virus increased by up to 4000‐fold, indicating that antibody‐mediated enhancement is facilitating entry. 81

Positive serology and detection of B19V genomes within diseased tissues have suggested a link between B19V and some chronic diseases including multiple vasculopathies. 46 In case reports of Henoch–Schönlein purpura, Kawasaki disease, and systemic lupus erythematosus, markers for B19V within affected vascular tissue have been observed. 60 , 82 A control–case study of TA biopsies even found a significantly higher prevalence of B19V DNA in the inflamed temporal arteries. 83 Identifying the role of B19V in each of these vasculopathies is challenging. There is a strong humoral response to initial infection, resulting in IgG antibodies that may persist for life. Therefore, anti‐B19V antibody presence may not indicate viral involvement in disease. As well, the virus spreads to many tissues during periods of viremia and can infect multiple cell types in an abortive manner. 82 Like an antibody test, detection of viral DNA may still be a remnant of an initial infection and unrelated to the diseased tissue. There is, however, evidence to indicate that parvovirus‐infected endothelial cells are dysregulated and may contribute to vasculopathy. Magro et al. observed both viral proteins and transcripts localized to endothelium in skin biopsies from patients with vasculitis. 60 In a follow‐up study, B19V‐infected endothelial cells were found to be apoptotic, and the degenerative effect of endothelium was correlated with the levels of detected viral protein. 47 Given the widespread dissemination of B19V within the body during primary infection, and the potential effects of persistent viral genome within abortively infected endothelial cells, B19V may be contributing to vasculopathies within the body and increasing the risk of stroke.

Coxsackie group B viruses (CVB) are a subset of nonpolio enteroviruses commonly found within the human population. These viruses may be carried asymptomatically within the gut, becoming pathogenic if transmitted from carriers through the fecal–oral route. Most often, an acute infection with CVB results in gastroenteritis but can develop into myocarditis and other more severe complications. 84 Research into the cardiomyopathies related to CVB infection have revealed that endothelial cells are permissive to infection. 85 Circulating CVB may be able to reach secondary sites of disease through infection of endothelial cells, which has been shown to modulate endothelial cell activity resulting in dysfunction. 61 In vitro infection of human microvascular endothelial cells demonstrated viral entry of the virus with only low levels of lysis. Virus was detected for extended periods within the human microvascular endothelial cells, suggesting a persistent infection had been established, although the mechanism for this has yet to be uncovered. Increased expression of ICAM‐1 (intercellular adhesion molecule‐1) and inflammatory cytokines IL‐6 (interleukin‐6) and IL‐8 by human microvascular endothelial cells show activation of the cells and a basis for chronic inflammatory processes and vasculopathy.

CAR (coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor) is the main receptor for invasion of endothelial cells by both CVB and adenovirus subtypes. As a cell‐adhesion molecule, CAR plays a multifaceted role in endothelial cells, primarily influencing endothelial resistance to mechanical stress, and inflammation. CAR is also a component of tight junctions, where it contributes to the maintenance of vascular function and tissue homeostasis. 86 Like CVB, several adenoviruses use CAR to infect endothelial cells potentially contributing to vasculopathy. Isolated atherosclerotic plaques are found to contain viral signatures from both groups. Additionally, CAR has been shown to be significantly upregulated in atherosclerotic plaques, which may further facilitate the entry of coxsackie and adenoviruses into cells within the plaque, potentially contributing to plaque instability. 39 Further examination of the effect that viral infection of vascular cells has on CAR expression and function is required to better understand the relationship between CVB, adenoviruses, and vasculopathy.

Extracellular Interactions Causing Vasculopathy

Some viral factors cause vasculopathy either independently or without infecting vascular cells. This may occur through interactions between circulating virus and vascular tissue or inflammation that specifically induces pathogenic changes to vasculature. These changes impair vascular function and manifest as endothelial dysregulation, perivascular inflammation, and an increased risk of stroke.

In early acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, the highest levels of viral replication occur within the nasal ciliated epithelial cells. Entry into target cells is mediated by the host cell angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor. Although the respiratory epithelial cell is the dominant site of acute infection, ACE2 is expressed in higher levels in other tissues, such as the heart and small intestine. This suggests possible mechanisms for the multiorgan complications documented in severe cases of COVID‐19. Endothelial dysfunction and injury are observed in the brain vasculature of patients with COVID‐19. 62 The level of expression of ACE2 on endothelial cells and the ability of the virus to invade them during infection are debated. In vitro addition of viral titers to endothelial cells from different tissues yields little evidence of entry, possibly due to transient expression of ACE2 or lack of appropriate coreceptors. 87 Pericytes are cells embedded within the capillary walls of the brain that perform multiple functions, including maintenance of the blood–brain barrier, and play a part in regulating cerebral blood pressure. These cells express ACE2 and may be sites of SARS‐CoV‐2 interaction within blood vessels. The viral surface spike protein interacts with ACE2, causing lowered expression on the pericyte surface and results in contraction of the microvascular capillaries. 63 This narrowing of pericyte‐adjacent sections of the brain microvascular presents risk for ischemia, stalling of circulating neutrophils, and stroke.

Excessive cytokine release during respiratory infections has been implicated in worsening the progression of disease. This effect, called “cytokine storm” has been identified in both SARS‐CoV‐2 and IAV infection. One of the central mediators identified in cytokine storm during IAV pneumonia is the endothelial cell response to viral presence. IAV, with the exception of the highly pathogenic H1N1 strain, has not been shown to directly infect endothelial cells in vivo. 65 However, the endothelial cell receptor S1P1 interacts with viral proteins in the extracellular environment, leading to high levels of cytokine production, and related vasculopathies such as atherosclerosis. 88

The H1N1 strain of IAV alters the expression of endothelial factors in pulmonary vascular endothelium in rats, indicating that the virus can cause endothelial dysfunction. A study observed changes in the expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1, and tissue plasminogen activator in the pulmonary vascular endothelium after infection. 89 Other common IAV strains can also induce apoptosis in endothelial cells, particularly when pretreated with tumor necrosis factor‐α. 64 Although it is not determined if this mechanism is unique only to the H1N1 strain, this suggests that virus interactions with vascular endothelial cells leads to perforation of the endothelial layer through cell loss.

Systemic Changes during Viral Infection

There are many physiological changes that occur during viral infection. Some of these changes are the result of immune responses, which are intended to clear the infection but can cause pathologies in a dysregulated or chronic state. Other changes are mediated by viral proteins themselves that may act beyond the intracellular environment of the target host cell and cause pathological changes to systems of the body. High blood pressure is a principal risk factor in stroke and can be increased through viral interactions with host tissues that regulate blood pressure. Circulating blood cells like platelets and monocytes that are involved in coagulation can be prompted by viral components to create clots on the surface of blood vessels, which may travel to the brain and cause stroke. Lipids and cholesterol are also important homeostatic mechanisms for the vascular system and can be important factors in the development of atherosclerotic plaques. Multiple viruses can dysregulate this system as well by disrupting cellular functions.

Blood Pressure

Viral infections can disturb the renin‐angiotensin system (RAS), which is responsible for regulating electrolyte balance and blood pressure. The RAS works through 2 main pathways: the ACE/angiotensin II (Ang II)/Ang II type 1 receptor pathway and the ACE2/Ang (1–7)/MAS receptor pathway. RAS dysregulation can trigger changes in vascular contraction, which may induce or compound the effects of hypertension. These changes may also cause endothelial dysfunction, impairing the management of oxidative stress and inflammation causing vasculitis and atherosclerosis and contributing to stroke risk. 90 , 91

Because of the ubiquity of RAS effectors in multiple tissues, viral presence can disrupt blood pressure regulation on a systemic level. 92 For example, binding of viral surface antigens to ACE2 can lead to a decrease in ACE2 activity, exacerbating pathophysiological mechanisms such as Ang II/ACE2 regulation imbalance. 93 As was discussed in the previous section, SARS‐CoV‐2 can cause lowered expression of ACE2 on ACE2‐expressing cells through surface interactions with the viral spike protein. This dysregulation of the RAS results in higher Ang II activity, promoting vasoconstriction, inflammation, and oxidative stress. This functional shift in RAS is linked to acute lung injury and respiratory distress in patients with COVID‐19. 94 Other respiratory viruses such as respiratory syncytial virus and IAV types H7N9 and H5N1 involve dysregulation of the RAS system in causing acute lung injury. During the course of these viral infections, ACE2 shows significant downregulation in affected tissues combined with higher systemic Ang II. 95

Blood Clotting

The coagulation system can be activated during infection to limit spread of a virus and aid with clearance. However, some viruses can affect the coagulation system in a way that promotes thrombosis and stroke, including induction of tissue factor, antibody‐mediated thrombosis, and effects on fibrinolysis.

Influenza infections directly modulate effectors of coagulation. Influenza virus relies on the proteolytic cleavage of its hemagglutinin membrane protein to facilitate infectivity. This cleavage is mediated by the anticoagulant protein plasmin, which is an important factor for influenza infection of target cells. Preventing plasminogen processing into it plasmin through an inhibitor, 6‐aminohexanoic acid, is protective against influenza lethality in mice models. 96 Within the body, PAI‐1 (plasmin activator inhibitor‐1) inhibits the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin and therefore is involved in regulating coagulation. PAI‐1 expression has been shown to increase during influenza infection, and has even been characterized as an antiviral effector, because inhibition of plasmin prevents proteolytic cleavage of the viral hemagglutinin protein. The upregulation of PAI‐1, however, inhibits normal fibrinolysis and the clearance of clots and is implicated in disseminated intravascular coagulation. 97

Tissue factor (TF) is an inflammatory effector that is present on monocytes, vascular smooth muscle, and endothelial cell surfaces. TF expression can initiate the coagulation cascade and is associated with many forms of thrombosis. In addition to the other vasculopathic effects that have been found in COVID‐19, SARS‐CoV‐2 has been found to induce disseminated intravascular coagulation. 98 This may be a result of an increase in levels of circulating TF during infection. In a study using a SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein pseudovirus, infection of human macrophages increased TF procoagulant activity at the cell surface and stimulated release of TF‐coated extracellular vesicles. 99 This suggests that spike protein interactions with the cell surface may play a direct role in the increase of circulating TF found in patients with severe COVID‐19. Although there is not yet significant evidence to link this to the development of ischemic stroke, higher circulating tissue factor is associated with stroke risk through analysis of clinical reports. 100

Antiphospholipid syndrome is an autoimmune disorder that can lead to a hypercoagulable state and an increased risk of antibody‐mediated thrombosis. Antibody‐mediated thrombosis occurs through the development of antibodies specific to phospholipid‐binding plasma proteins or cell surface phospholipids directly. Proposed mechanisms for this autoimmune effect include cross‐reactivity of virus‐specific antibodies, as well as the formation of large immune complexes during activation of vascular tissues. 101 Thrombosis resulting from antiphospholipid syndrome is associated with a significant number of ischemic stroke events, particularly in patients under the age of 45. 102 Multiple viral infections are implicated in the development of antiphospholipid syndrome including HCV, B19V, and SARS‐CoV‐2. 101 , 103 , 104 Presence of anticardiolipin antibodies is significantly higher in patients with chronic HCV. 105 Although this contributes to an increased risk of thrombosis, a direct mechanism relating HCV infection to ischemic stroke remains to be elucidated. Antibodies specific for the B19V capsid protein, VP1, may cross‐react with phospholipids, as reported in mouse models for antiphospholipid syndrome. 106 Antiphospholipid antibodies have also been identified in patients with severe COVID‐19. through an increase in Fc‐γ RIIA immunoglobulin and increase in prothrombotic platelets. 107

Low‐Density Lipoprotein and Cholesterol

Maintaining low‐density lipoprotein and cholesterol levels in the blood is crucial in the prevention of atherosclerosis due to their direct role in the development of plaques within vessel walls.

Cytomegalovirus is associated with atherosclerotic tissues and has been isolated from plaques, suggesting a direct role in atherosclerosis. 108 In murine models using murine cytomegalovirus, low‐density lipoprotein associated cholesterol was significantly elevated in infected groups. This effect was independent of dietary factors, as the increase was still observed when mice were given a high‐cholesterol diet. 78 Although the closely related murine cytomegalovirus virus is a good model for the study of human cytomegalovirus, physiologically, mice are more resistant to atherosclerosis than humans. An apolipoprotein‐E knockout mouse model is susceptible to atherosclerosis and used as an analog for the development of the disease in humans. In this model, murine cytomegalovirus was significant in initiating atherosclerotic plaques in a similar fashion to humans. 109

The percentage of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, the major lipid contributor to atherosclerotic plaques, was significantly increased in the serum of murine cytomegalovirus‐infected mice, whether or not the mice were fed a high‐cholesterol diet. Human cytomegalovirus significantly increased the esterified cholesterol component of the total cholesterol in a human arterial smooth muscle cell line infected in vitro with cytomegalovirus. 110

Conclusions

Viral infections may be underappreciated in their contributions to stroke, as much is still unknown about the mechanisms by which a virus may increase the risk of stroke. It is often difficult to isolate a single infection as a causative agent in this case, although epidemiological data support a significant effect. Emerging trends in stroke cause, including seasonal variation, reduced risk following vaccination, and a lower mean age of incidence, support a contribution of viral infection to stroke. The research summarized in Table 1 provides a basis for further investigation into this relationship.

Our review of mechanistic studies on viral–host interactions began with vasculopathies following viral infection, which are summarized in Table 2. In these cases, direct viral interactions with vascular tissues causing changes to structure and function may be associated with an increased risk of stroke. However, the conditions that often precede a stroke, including atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and chronic inflammation, are often progressive and involve multiple risk factors. This review has additionally examined the multifaceted relationship between viral infections and the risk of ischemic stroke. Acute viral diseases such as influenza, COVID‐19, mononucleosis, and gastroenteritis often present mildly but may initiate local and systemic vascular changes to promote stroke risk. This effect has gained some prominence with the emergence of SARS‐CoV‐2 and the further study of pathogenic cytokine signaling. Chronic viral diseases have long‐standing relationships with affected tissues and our immune system. The interactions that take place during the persistence of a virus make progressive changes that mimic the development of other vascular risk factors of stroke, potentially resulting in viral contributions being overlooked.

In this regard, a more comprehensive approach to assessing risk of ischemic stroke must be used with consideration for multiple viral infections across the medical history of the patient. Such an analysis would include the effects of both acute and persistent infections, as well as a total “infectious burden” in increasing the risk of future stroke.
