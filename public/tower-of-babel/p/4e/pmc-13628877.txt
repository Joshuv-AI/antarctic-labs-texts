Process optimization for protoplast preparation from Grifola frondosa and development of a high-yield mutant with enhanced mycelial growth and protein production
Authors: Qiaoyu Wang, Ziteng Zhao, Qiuyu Lu, Ke’er Xiao, Lingyu Gao, Mukaddas Sai, Lili Guan, Linna Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Grifola frondosa is a highly valued medicinal mushroom with diverse bioactive properties; however, its low mycelial biomass remains a major bottleneck affecting the efficiency of liquid fermentation. To address this issue, protoplast-mediated mutagenesis was employed in this study to breed a high-performance G. frondosa mutant with superior fermentation traits. Following optimization using intelligent strategies, the highest yield of protoplasts was achieved (6.573 × 106 protoplasts/g fresh weight) under suitable isolation parameters (fungal age of 7.9 days, lywallzyme concentration 2.1%, enzymatic hydrolysis time 5.2 hours, enzymatic temperature of 30 °C, and mannitol concentration of 0.6 mol/L). Subsequently, DES-induced protoplast mutagenesis yielded a stable G. frondosa mutant (D24) showing 62.78% greater mycelial biomass and 111.11% higher protein yield relative to the parental strain. Furthermore, fermentation kinetics profiling and phenotypic observation suggested that the mutant D24 exhibited a shortened lag phase, enhanced hyphal dispersion, and denser mitochondrial distribution compared with the original strain. Additionally, genome resequencing identified 57,216 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in D24 relative to the original strain, including 4,068 non-synonymous SNPs. GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment indicated that the mutated genes were potentially involved in amino acid synthesis and metabolism, lipid metabolism, and others. These candidate mutations may be associated with the increased mycelial biomass and protein content. Collectively, this study has developed a novel high-yielding mutant of G. frondosa, which may serve as a promising material for future pharmaceutical and biotechnological applications.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01197-z.

Introduction

Recently, continuous global population growth has led to a sharp rise in protein demand. However, despite being a complete protein source, animal-based production is unsustainable due to its high land use, significant greenhouse gas emissions, and associated health risks [1]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop efficient and environmentally friendly alternative protein sources. Microbial protein has thus emerged as a highly promising solution, offering advantages such as high productivity, low cost, and environmental friendliness, while providing the high-quality protein necessary for human physiological functions [2]. As an important member of eukaryotic microorganisms widely distributed worldwide, mushrooms have been used as medicine and food for a long time [3]. Among the 2000 characterized mushroom species, approximately 700 are identified as safe for human consumption, possessing nutritional and/or medicinal value [4]. Grifola frondosa (Dicks.: Fr.) SF Gray (Maitake), an important mushroom belonging to the family Grifolaceae Jülich, has received widespread attention due to its great edible, medicinal, and cosmetic potential [5, 6]. It is evident that the health benefits of G. frondosa are closely related to its abundant active components. A variety of valuable metabolites have been identified in this fungus [7, 8]. Notably, the protein content present in the mycelium or fruiting bodies of G. frondosa underlies its nutritional value and supports its consideration as a potential source of high-quality microbial protein [9]. Additionally, accumulated evidence has revealed the various pharmacological properties of G. frondosa, including anti-tumor [10], anti-obesity [11], anti-inflammatory [5], and others [12]. These findings collectively position G. frondosa as a significant reservoir of diverse bioactive constituents, leading to a marked increase in demand for this macrofungal species.

To meet the growing demand for G. frondosa, it is particularly important to establish a suitable cultivation process. With the gradual recognition of their extremely high economic value, the cultivation technology of mushrooms has been rapidly improved [13]. Nowadays, solid-state cultivation and submerged fermentation are the two main approaches used for mushroom production [14]. However, solid-state cultivation for mushroom production at the industrial level faces great challenges as its production efficiency is easily affected by various factors such as temperature, moisture, pH, heat transfer, etc [15]. Compared with solid-state cultivation, submerged fermentation—a technique for high-density cultivation of mushroom mycelium grown in liquid medium supplemented by aeration and agitation [16]—exhibits various advantages, including controllable process parameters, high production efficiency, short production time, and stable product quality. Thus, submerged fermentation technology has become a promising strategy for producing mushroom mycelium and its metabolites. In our previous study, liquid fermentation technology was also attempted to be used for the production of G. frondosa. However, preliminary results showed that slow hyphal growth and low protein yield have greatly limited its further application. Therefore, it is extremely important to select and breed novel G. frondosa strains with fast growth rates and high yields of proteins.

Nowadays, various traditional and modern breeding strategies (mutagenic breeding, protoplast fusion, genetically engineered breeding, mono-mono mating, genome shuffling, etc.) have been proposed to improve the efficiency of mushroom production [17–19]. Compared with other methods, artificial mutagenesis is considered a simpler, more effective, and low-cost strategy for strain improvement [20]. Mutagenesis breeding technology using physical or chemical mutagenic factors has been successfully used in the breeding of many fungi, including Flammulina velutipes [21], Antrodia camphorata [22], and Volvariella volvacea [23], etc. to improve their growth, active substance production, and adaptability to adverse external environments [24]. Another advantage of this strategy is that it does not require a detailed understanding of the target strain’s gene regulatory network. For filamentous fungal breeding, protoplasts are commonly used as the starting material. Due to the loss of the cell wall, protoplasts are more sensitive to external mutagens, facilitating the isolation of desirable mutants [25]. Hence, combining protoplasts with mutagenesis is widely regarded as an important means to increase the frequency and efficiency of mutations.

However, the preparation of protoplasts is a complex process, and its efficiency is easily affected by different factors [26]. Therefore, to improve the mutation efficiency of G. frondosa, the preparation process of protoplasts from G. frondosa was first optimized in the present study using a series of intelligent strategies. Subsequently, an attempt was made to breed a novel G. frondosa strain with a fast growth rate and high yield of proteins by protoplast-mediated diethyl sulfate (DES) mutagenesis. Furthermore, phenotype analysis, fermentation kinetics profiling, and genome resequencing of the wild (WT) and selected mutant were conducted. The findings presented herein provide candidate strain for the industrial application of G. frondosa.

Materials and methods

Fungal strain and cultivation

G. frondosa CGMCC 5.404, the parental strain used in this study, was bought from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (Beijing, China). This fungal strain was cultured under conditions identical to those described in our prior study, utilizing the same medium and cultivation parameters [27].

Preparation of protoplasts from G. frondosa

The protoplasts of G. frondosa were isolated according to a previous study with minor modifications [28]. Briefly, after seven days of growth, 0.1 g of G. frondosa mycelium was harvested by filtering the fermentation broth through four layers of gauze, then washed with sterile water and osmotic stabilizer, and subsequently incubated in 2% lywallzyme solution (dissolved in osmotic stabilizer, 1 mL) at 30 °C for 5 hours. Afterwards, the suspension was centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 10 minutes, and the resulting precipitate was resuspended in osmotic stabilizer. Finally, the number of protoplasts was counted using a hemocytometer.

Optimization of protoplasts preparation parameters

Single-factor test

To systematically evaluate the impact of different parameters (fungal age, lywallzyme concentration, enzymatic hydrolysis time, enzymatic temperature, and osmotic stabilizer) on protoplast yield, single-factor tests were employed. For fungal age, the mycelium grown for 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 days were harvested and then subjected to hydrolysis using 2% lywallzyme dissolved in 0.6 mol/L mannitol at 30 °C. After five hours, the protoplast yield was quantified using a hemocytometer. For lywallzyme concentration, eight-day-old mycelia were hydrolyzed with 1%, 1.5%, 2%, 2.5%, and 3% lywallzyme solutions, respectively, while all other isolating parameters were kept constant as described above. Subsequently, the optimal enzymatic hydrolysis time was evaluated over a range of 2 to 6 hours, with the other parameters set as follows: fungal age of 8 days, lywallzyme concentration of 2%, enzymatic temperature of 30 °C, and 0.6 mol/L mannitol as the osmotic stabilizer. For enzymatic temperature, mycelia (8 days) were treated with 2% lywallzyme solution under various temperatures (26 °C, 28 °C, 30 °C, 32 °C,·34 °C) for 5 h, with all other isolation conditions maintained as described above. Finally, different osmotic stabilizers (MgSO4, KCl, sucrose, glucose, and mannitol) at a concentration of 0.6 mol/L were tested for protoplast isolation under fixed conditions (fungal age of 8 d, lywallzyme concentration of 2%, enzymatic hydrolysis time of 5 hours, and enzymatic temperature of 30 ℃). After quantifying protoplast yield, the optimal osmotic stabilizer was determined. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Design matrix and results of the BBD experiment

For RSM, to describe the relationship between these four factors and protoplast yield from G. frondosa, a multivariate quadratic regression (MQR) equation was established using the software Design-Expert 13. Furthermore, statistical analysis of this model was carried out referring to another article [30]. Additionally, feed-forward ANN model, an artificial intelligence learning tool inspired by the neuronal connections of the human brain, was also built using MATLAB software (R2006a, Math-Works Inc., Natick, MA, USA) to fit the data obtained from the BBD test [31]. In the present work, the architecture of the ANN consisted of an input layer containing four independent variables, a hidden layer containing multiple neurons, and an output layer (protoplast yield). However, due to the key role of neurons in the hidden layer in the performance of ANN, its optimal value was determined using the degree of approximation (Da) function, as described in previous studies [32]. In this formula, the constant was set to 5. After building a suitable ANN model, optimization was carried out using MATLAB software with a genetic algorithm to determine the optimal values of these four parameters. The parameters of the GA optimization process were set to be the same as those in a previous study [33]. Briefly, during the optimization process, the genetic algorithm was run according to the following parameter settings: the population adopted a double-vector form with a size of 20 and was randomly initialized. The stochastic uniform function was chosen for selection, with an elite count of 2 and a crossover fraction of 0.8, using a scattered crossover function. The migration fraction was 0.2, occurring every 20 generations.

Verification test and observation of protoplast morphology

The optimum preparation parameters predicted by RSM and ANN-GA were verified via verification tests. Briefly, the protoplasts were isolated from G. frondosa five times in parallel under the conditions predicted by RSM and ANN-GA, respectively, and the average protoplast yield was assessed. The performance of the RSM and ANN models was evaluated using several parameters: the coefficient of determination (R2), standard deviation (S.D.), mean square error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), chi square (χ2), and standard error of prediction (SEP) [34]. Furthermore, G. frondosa protoplasts were prepared under optimal conditions, and their morphology was photographed at 1, 3, and 5 hours using a microscope (TS2, Nikon, Japan), respectively. To assess the vitality of the isolated protoplasts, the protoplasts were subjected to trypan blue staining using the method described by Yu et al. [35].

Regeneration of protoplasts

After protoplast isolation, the protoplast concentration was adjusted to 1 × 108/mL using 0.6 mol/L mannitol. Three independent replicates were performed. Then, 0.1 mL of protoplast suspension was spread onto the fermentation medium containing 0.6 mol/L mannitol [36] and allowed to regenerate the cell wall at 25 ± 1 °C. After 10 days, the number of colonies grown on the medium was counted. Meanwhile, the protoplasts suspended in sterile water were also spread onto a fermentation medium without osmotic stabilizers (CONT group). Finally, the regeneration rate of protoplasts was calculated using the formula mentioned in Patil et al.’s study [37].

Mutagenesis of G. frondosa using diethyl sulfate (DES)

DES Treatment for protoplasts isolated from G. frondosa

Protoplasts isolated from G. frondosa were gently resuspended in 0.6 mol/L mannitol (pH 7.0) and subjected to mutagenesis with DES at a final concentration of 2 mg/mL. Subsequently, the mutagenesis reaction was conducted under controlled conditions (25 °C, 135 r/min). Following DES exposure for varying durations (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 min), protoplasts were immediately harvested, and the reaction was quenched by 200 μL of 25% (w/v) sodium thiosulfate solution. Furthermore, the protoplasts were spread onto the regeneration medium according to the method described in Sect. “Regeneration of protoplasts”, and the mortality rate for each duration was calculated. Three biological replicates were conducted for each treatment duration.

Screening of suitable mutants

To screen fast-growing mutants, the colonies of mutants and the wild strain (WT) were aseptically punched out using a 7-mm diameter sterile puncher and inoculated into equally sized wells on solid fermentation medium. The plates were then incubated at 25 °C for 15 days. Then, the colony diameters of each mutant and the parental strain were measured in triplicate. Following initial screening, mutant strains exhibiting significantly larger colony diameters than the parental strain were selected for secondary evaluation through submerged fermentation in Erlenmeyer flasks. Briefly, the selected strains were activated as described above. Then, the strains were inoculated into 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 40 mL fermentation medium [27]. The remaining cultivation parameters were set as follows: inoculation volume of 10% (v/v), temperature of 25 °C, agitation rate of 150 r/min, and a growth period of 8 days. After fermentation, the mycelium of each strain was harvested by filtration through four layers of sterile gauze and subsequently washed twice with deionized water. The collected mycelium was lyophilized using a lyophilizer (Genesis 2000 SQ, VIRTIS, USA) and ground into a fine powder (60 mesh). The dry mycelial weight was then measured, and the total protein content was determined. All the tests were carried out in triplicate.

Analyzing the genetic stability of mutant strains

Following the methodology of Lian et al., the selected mutants were subjected to ten consecutive subcultures at a 10% (v/v) inoculum to evaluate their genetic stability [38]. Additionally, the dry mycelial biomass and total protein content of the mutant strains were quantified at every second subculture passage. All the tests were carried out in triplicate.

Protein extraction and content quantification

Proteins were extracted from the mycelium of selected strains and the wild strain using the ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction method described by Zhang et al. with minor modifications [39]. Briefly, lyophilized mycelial powder (0.1 g) was homogenized in deionized water (9 mL), followed by pH adjustment to 10.0 using 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution. The resulting suspension was then subjected to ultrasonic treatment using an ultrasonic bath system (ultrasonic power: 400 W, ultrasonic temperature: 40 °C, ultrasonic time: 5 min). After ultrasonic treatment, the mixture was centrifuged (3000 r/min, 15 min, 4 ℃), and the supernatant was collected as the protein extract. Then, the protein concentration in the extract was determined using the Bradford kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fermentation kinetics analysis of G. frondosa original strain and mutant strain D24

To compare the differences in mycelial growth kinetics and protein accumulation between the parental strain and mutant strain D24, growth curves and protein accumulation curves were constructed after batch fermentation over a period of 15 days. Briefly, the mutant strain D24 and the parental strain were cultivated in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL fermentation medium. Other experimental conditions were set based on the above with minor modifications. The liquid fermentation medium consisted of glucose (25 g/L), peptone (12 g/L), yeast powder (10 g/L), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (2.5 g/L), magnesium sulfate (1.5 g/L), and vitamin B1 (0.05 g/L). The agitation rate was set at 150 r/min. During cultivation, the fermentation broth was harvested at 24-hour intervals to detect the pH, residual sugar concentration, mycelial biomass, and intracellular protein content in accordance with the methods described by other studies [40]. All above experiments were performed in triplicate. Origin 2022 software (Origin Lab, America) was used for nonlinear fitting of mycelial growth, protein accumulation, and substrate consumption.

Moreover, given the important role of fermentation kinetics in controlling microbial growth and enhancing product accumulation [41], the Logistic kinetic model was employed to describe the hyphal growth and protein accumulation of both G. frondosa strains. The calculation formulas of these three models are based on other research [42].

Macroscopic and microscopic morphological observation of hyphae

The mycelia of G. frondosa mutant D24 and the WT strain were cultured according to the liquid fermentation method described above for 10 days. The fermentation broth was then filtered through four layers of sterile gauze to collect the mycelia. The harvested mycelia, along with the resuspension solution (mycelia were resuspended in 30 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) were transferred into a 15 mm petri dish for morphological observation and image capture.

The hyphae of the G. frondosa mutant D24 and the parental strain were collected at two time points (4 and 6 days) during liquid fermentation. Then, scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi-SU8600, Hitachi High-Tech) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi-HT7800, Hitachi High-Tech) were used to observe the microscopic morphology of both strains [43]. These morphological observations were performed in triplicate.

Whole-genome resequencing analysis of the G. frondosa D24 and the original strain

Mycelia of G. frondosa mutant D24 cultured for 6 days, along with those of the parental strain (cultured under the same conditions), were collected by filtration and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each strain. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using a fungal genomic DNA extraction kit and sent to Annoroad Gene Technology (Beijing, China) for whole-genome resequencing. A DNA library with an insert size of 500 bp was constructed for each sample, and genome resequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing platform. Low-quality reads were filtered from the raw data to obtain high-quality reads (clean reads), which were then aligned to the G. frondosa reference genome (ASM168373v1, available at: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_001683735.1/) using BWA software (version 0.7.12). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (InDels) were detected using GATK software (v3.5) and then annotated using ANNOVAR. Subsequently, SNP sites with non-synonymous mutations in the mutant D24 relative to the parental strain were selected, and the corresponding genes were subjected to enrichment analysis against the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database and GO (Gene Ontology) databases.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 based on one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California).

Results and discussion

Impact of different parameters on the generation of protoplasts in G. frondosa

As is well known, the establishment of standardized preparation parameters is essential for high-quality protoplast production. Due to interspecific variability in fungal cell wall composition, optimal protoplast isolation conditions must be determined for each organism. In this study, to evaluate the influence of different parameters on protoplast yield from G. frondosa, single-factor tests were introduced.

Fungal age of G. frondosa

Hyphae of G. frondosa cultured for different durations were collected for protoplast isolation, and the corresponding results are presented in Fig. 1A. It is evident that the protoplast yield exhibited significant variation depending on the age of G. frondosa, indicating that fungi in different physiological states or growth stages have different levels of sensitivity to lytic enzymes. Although others found that young or exponentially growing hyphae are suitable for protoplasts isolation, the ideal fungal age varies among species [44]. For instance, the optimal ages for protoplast isolation from Cordyceps militaris [45], Antrodia cinnamomea [46], and Lyophyllum decastes [47] were 4 days, 30 hours, and 10 days, respectively. Moreover, previous results have indicated that both very young and aged hyphae are not conducive to the release and survival of protoplasts, which aligns with the findings presented here [45]. In the present work, results showed that as the age of G. frondosa increased from 5 to 8 days, the protoplast yield gradually increased. Referring to other articles, it is speculated that this may be due to the fact that the growth time was too short, resulting in less mycelium for enzymatic hydrolysis and therefore insufficient protoplasts [48]. In other words, although young G. frondosa mycelia possess thin cell walls that facilitate enzymatic hydrolysis, their inherently slow growth rate limits biomass accumulation, thereby reducing protoplast yield [47]. However, a reduction in protoplast amount was observed from hyphae cultured for more than 8 days, potentially attributable to cellular senescence, decreased mycelial vitality, increased cell wall thickness, and reduced susceptibility to lytic enzymes. This observation is consistent with the previous findings [49]. Concentration of lywallzyme

Numerous studies have revealed that the enzyme concentration is one of the limiting factors affecting protoplast formation. Thus, based on the above results, the proper lywallzyme concentration was determined. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, the highest protoplast yield was found in the 2% lywallzyme treatment group, and statistically significant differences were observed between this group and the hyphae treated with 1% or 1.5% lywallzyme (p < 0.05). Furthermore, increasing the lywallzyme concentration from 2% to 3% resulted in a slight reduction in G. frondosa protoplast yield (p > 0.05). Referring to previous studies, the decrease in protoplasts yield might be attributed to damage of cell membrane glycoproteins and nucleic acids [47]. Similar trends have also been observed in the preparation of protoplasts from other fungi, including Gaeumannomyces tritici [50] and Hirsutella sinensis [51]. The above data indicate that the suitable concentration of lywallzyme was 2%.

Enzymatic hydrolysis time

The impact of different enzymatic hydrolysis times (2, 3, 4, 5, 6 h) on protoplast yield was investigated. Fig. 1C showed that prolonged lywallzyme treatment resulted in a biphasic trend in protoplast yield, with an initial increase followed by a subsequent decline. The maximal protoplasts yield was achieved after 5 hours of incubation, demonstrating statistically significant differences compared to the 2-, 3-, and 6-hour treatment groups (p < 0.05). The results showed that with the increase of enzymatic hydrolysis time, the cell wall of the mycelium gradually lysed, thereby enhancing protoplast yield. However, the decrease in the amount of protoplasts caused by further prolonged enzymatic hydrolysis might be related to its destructive effect on the cell membrane structure of protoplasts [51]. These findings were in accordance with other previous studies. For instance, after enzymatic hydrolysis of Ganoderma boninense for different hours (1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 hours), the 2-hour treatment group showed significantly higher protoplast amounts [52]. Additionally, Ishikawa and coworkers found that the effect of enzymatic hydrolysis time on fungal protoplasts production was also related to the enzyme concentration [53]. They found that the trend of protoplast production varied with the extension of enzymatic hydrolysis time when the concentration of lysing enzyme was 10, 20, and 30 mg/mL. Hence, the enzymatic hydrolysis time used in the following single-factor tests was selected as 5 h.

Enzymatic temperature

It is widely known that temperature is a key factor affecting the rate of enzymatic reactions. Therefore, it is undoubted that finding the optimal enzymatic temperature is particularly important for enhancing the protoplast yield. Results showed that the highest protoplasts yield (6.08 × 106 protoplasts/g FW) was obtained at 30 °C, which was markedly higher than at other incubation temperatures tested (Fig. 1D, p < 0.05). This change might be explained as an increase in enzyme activity and reaction rate with rising temperature, leading to an increase in the amount of protoplasts. However, once the temperature exceeded 30 °C, the rate of enzymatic hydrolysis by lywallzyme decreased. Thus, the suitable enzymatic temperature for protoplast preparation from G. frondosa was 30 °C.

Osmotic stabilizers

Suitable osmotic stabilizers are particularly important for preventing osmotic lysis of protoplasts and maintaining their stability [54]. To date, many osmotic stabilizers have been used in the preparation of plant and microbial protoplasts, such as inorganic salts, sugars, etc. [55]. Indeed, the suitable osmotic stabilizer for different microorganisms is not entirely consistent. For Metarhizium anisopliae var. Majus, 0.7 mol/L KCl was demonstrated to be the optimal osmotic stabilizer [56]. Taking Sclerotium rolfsii as another example, Fariña et al. found that the highest protoplast yield was observed in the presence of MgSO4 as an osmotic stabilizer [57]. In the present work, five osmotic stabilizers were employed to isolate the protoplasts from the target fungus. Significant differences were observed in the number of obtained protoplasts using different osmotic stabilizers (Fig. 1E). Clearly, 0.6 M mannitol, as a widely used stabilizer, produced the highest protoplast yield of up to 6.14 × 106 protoplasts/g FW, followed by MgSO4, KCl, and sucrose, while glucose was the least effective stabilizer for G. frondosa protoplasts. Similarly, other researchers have found that mannitol is suitable for the isolation of protoplasts from many microbial species, including Ganoderma lucidum [58], Cordyceps cicadae [59], etc. Therefore, mannitol was employed to be used for subsequent experiments.

Development of the ANN model and optimization using GA

Numerous studies have mentioned that, compared to RSM, ANN coupled with GA have demonstrated higher prediction accuracy in many optimization processes [62]. Due to its superior parameter generalization and remarkable validation accuracy, ANN-GA has gradually gained popularity and is widely used as a powerful hybrid intelligent technique in various fields, such as food, pharmaceuticals, engineering, etc. [63, 64]. Therefore, ANN-GA was also employed to further optimize the suitable values of four variables.

In this study, a feed-forward ANN network with three layers (input layer, hidden layer, output layer) was developed, in which the output layer was the protoplasts yield from G. frondosa, and four investigated variables were used as four input layers. To select the appropriate number of hidden neurons, the ANN network was trained with varying numbers of neurons in the hidden layer (ranging from 3 to 29), and the corresponding Da value was calculated. As depicted in Fig. 3A, the suitable number of hidden neurons was determined to be 20, as it yielded the maximum Da. The ANN network with 20 neurons in the hidden layer exhibited a highly R2 value (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the analysis of mean square error against iteration numbers revealed that the model reached a lower MSE (0.40095) after three iterations (Fig. 3C). Comparison of RSM and ANN

To evaluate the fitting and predictive efficiency of the two optimization strategies, a total of six parameters were employed, including the coefficient of determination (R2), standard deviation (S.D., %), mean square error (MSE), root mean square error (RMSE), chi square (χ2), and standard error of prediction (SEP, %). As illustrated in Table S2, the R2 values of the two models were both higher than 0.95, indicating a high consistency between the actual results and the values predicted by these two models. Nevertheless, the R2 value of RSM was higher than that of ANN, suggesting superior accuracy of RSM. Additionally, lower MSE, S.D., RMSE, χ2, and SEP values were observed for the RSM model compared to the ANN model, indicating that the predictive ability of the RSM model was superior to that of the ANN model in this study [65]. The predictive performance of well-established RSM models is known to depend on factors like experimental scale and treatment type. Generally, RSM can sometimes provide relatively accurate predictions even with a small number of experiments, whereas ANN performs robustly regardless of the data size. Given the limited dataset (n = 29) used in this work, RSM proved to be more effective, although ANN still yielded efficient predictions. Similar findings have been reported by Zeng et al. (2020) and Naderloo (2020) when comparing the performance of RSM and ANN models [66, 67].

Moreover, the protoplast morphology was observed and photographed during the enzymatic hydrolysis process. As is well established, specific enzymes can recognize and degrade cell wall components, such as chitin and cellulose, leading to cell wall disruption. In the absence of cell wall support and constraint, protoplasts naturally assume a spherical morphology. Obviously, as the enzymatic hydrolysis time increased, the hyphae were progressively hydrolyzed, leading to a corresponding increase in spherical protoplasts and a reduction in hyphal fragments (Fig. 4A). To assess protoplast viability, trypan blue staining was employed, as this dye selectively penetrated non-viable cells. As depicted in Fig. 4B, the protoplasts exhibited larger, rounded morphology with minimal trypan blue staining, indicating high viability and ensuring optimal regeneration potential. On the basis of the above results, the prepared protoplasts were allowed to regenerate cell walls in regeneration medium, and their regeneration rate was calculated to be 3.62 × 10−1% (Fig. 4C). Nevertheless, despite the fact that low protoplast regeneration rates have been documented in previous studies [68], the regeneration rate obtained in this study appears to be relatively low in comparison with previously reported values. As reported, the regeneration rates of protoplasts for C. militaris [45] and G. lucidum [58] reached 36.5% and 3%, respectively, both of which were higher than the rate observed in the present work. It is possible that the nutrient composition, osmotic stabilizer, and pH of regeneration medium may account for this discrepancy, warranting further optimization [46]. For instance, the protoplast regeneration rate of L. decastes differed significantly among the five regeneration media tested [47]. In the case of H. sinensis, for instance, the protoplast regeneration rate was below 1% when mannitol served as the osmotic stabilizer in the regeneration medium [51]. Meanwhile, previous study have found that the agar concentration in the solid medium was associated with the protoplast regeneration rate [51]. Additionally, referring to other research [69], the conditions employed during protoplast preparation, such as enzyme composition, digestion time, and osmotic stabilizer, may also influence the regeneration efficiency and could partially explain the low regeneration rate observed in G. frondosa. The biomass and protein production of selected G. frondosa mutant strains

The mutant strains selected from the preliminary screening were further evaluated using liquid fermentation in flasks for 8 days, and their mycelial biomass and protein yield were determined (Fig. 6C and D). Regarding mycelial biomass, in comparison with the WT strain, obvious differences were found in 8 mutant strains, including 7 positive mutant strains and 1 negative mutant strain (Fig. 6C). Among these, the D24 mutant strain exhibited the highest mycelial biomass, which was 1.64 times that of the original strain. Moreover, Fig. 6D depicts the protein production of each mutant strain. There were 6 mutant strains with significantly higher protein production than that of the WT strain (p < 0.05). Specifically, the protein production of mutant strains D6, D15, D18, D24, D32, and D48 increased by 78.95%, 84.21%, 84.21%, 100%, 42.11%, and 105.26%, respectively, compared with the parental strain. The highest protein production (0.39 g/L) was observed in the mutant strain D48, which was significantly higher than that of the WT strain (0.19 g/L). Based on comprehensive comparative analysis of mycelial biomass and protein production among the different mutant strains, seven mutant strains (D6, D15, D18, D24, D25, D32, and D48) were selected for further investigation of their genetic stability.

Genetic stability analysis of candidate G. frondosa mutant strains and antagonistic reaction

To assess the genetic stability of candidate mutant strains, both the selected mutants and the WT strain were subjected to 10 sequential subcultures, with mycelial biomass and protein yield quantified at alternating generations. As summarized in Table 4, mutant strain D24 demonstrated superior mycelial biomass and protein accumulation while maintaining genetic stability, as evidenced by consistent production yields across multiple subculture generations (p > 0.05). Therefore, the mutant strain D24 was selected to use in further studies. Notably, the mycelial biomass of D24 obtained in this work is comparable to or higher than those reported in several liquid fermentation studies of G. frondosa. For instance, Sim et al. reported a mycelial biomass of 1.3 g/L for G. frondosa after liquid fermentation for 14 days [75]. Taking G. frondosa CCRC36911 as an example, a higher mycelial biomass of 2.32 g/L was achieved when yeast extract was used as the nitrogen source [76]. Conversely, some studies have reported higher mycelial biomass of G. frondosa through liquid fermentation compared to that of mutant D24. Cui et al. Obtained a mycelial biomass of G. frondosa GF9801 as high as 17.61 g/L after optimizing the liquid medium [77]. Beyond flask culture, fermenter-based production of G. frondosa mycelium has also been explored. Shih et al. found that the mycelial dry weight of G. frondosa was enhanced to 8.23 g/L in a 5-L fermenter using glucose fed-batch fermentation [78]. In addition, a mycelial dry weight as high as 18 g/L was achieved for G. frondosa mutant strain (M270) after fermentation in a 20-L bioreactor [79]. However, compared to mycelial biomass production of G. frondosa via liquid fermentation, protein production remains comparatively underexplored. In the study by Sim et al., for example, the soluble protein yield was 283.6 µg/mL under liquid fermentation conditions [75]. It is well established that the antagonistic reaction serves as a convenient test for evaluating somatic incompatibility [80]. Generally, antagonism is considered an incompatible reaction occurring at the hyphal contact zone, manifesting as pigment formation, dense hyphal protrusions, or sparse hyphal growth. When an antagonistic reaction occurs between two fungi, it indicates that they possess distinct genetic characteristics [81]. An antagonistic test conducted between the mutant strain D24 and the parental strain revealed a clear antagonistic reaction, suggesting that mutant strain D24 is genetically different from the WT strain (Fig. S2).

Comparative analysis of fermentation kinetic characteristics between the original strain and mutant strain D24

Accumulated evidence reveals that studying fermentation kinetics provides a theoretical basis for optimizing fermentation process control, scaling up laboratory-scale data, and transitioning from batch to fed-batch fermentation. Consequently, this research area has garnered increasing attention from biochemical engineering scholars worldwide [82]. To compare fermentation kinetics between the wild strain and the mutant strain D24, batch cultures were established with periodic monitoring of mycelial biomass, protein production, pH dynamics, and residual sugar concentrations. As depicted in Fig. 7A, with the extension of cultivation time, the biomass of both the WT strain and mutant strain D24 gradually increased before stabilizing. Notably, compared with the WT strain, mutant strain D24 demonstrated significantly enhanced biomass productivity from Day 2 to Day 15 (p < 0.01). Moreover, mutant strain D24 displayed a higher growth rate, as evidenced by a steeper slope, indicating stronger environmental adaptability and a shorter lag phase. To further analyze mycelial growth, the data were nonlinearly fitted using Origin 2022. Microbial growth kinetics are commonly modeled using several models, such as the Monod equation or the Logistic equation. Among these models, the Logistic model exhibits a typical S-shaped curve, effectively capturing the inhibitory effect of increasing cell density on microbial growth during batch fermentation. It provides a strong fit for the cell growth dynamics observed in batch fermentation processes [83, 84]. Therefore, the Logistic model was adopted in this study. As shown in Fig. 7B, the Logistic model was effective for fitting the hyphal growth of mutant D24 (R2 = 0.9965) and the original strains (R2 = 0.9988). Notably, the mutant D24 demonstrated significantly enhanced fermentation performance compared to the wild-type strain, achieving higher maximum biomass and superior maximum specific growth rate (μmax: 0.5803 ± 0.0256 h−1 vs WT 0.5278 ± 0.0138 h−1). In terms of protein yield, both the wild strain and mutant strain D24 exhibited similar overall trends (Fig. 7C). During the first 7 days and 8 days of fermentation, the proteins of wild and mutant strains accumulated rapidly, respectively. Subsequently, the protein accumulation rate of the two strains clearly slowed down. Moreover, markedly higher protein yield was detected in the mutant strain D24 compared to the original strain after two days of fermentation (p < 0.001). Similarly, the Logistic model was also introduced and successfully fitted the protein accumulation results of mutant D24 (R2 = 0.9922) and the parental strain (R2 = 0.9947) (Fig. 7D). Moreover, the D24 mutant outperformed the wild-type strain in both protein production and maximum specific protein production rate (μmax improved from 0.5334 ± 0.0306 h−1 to 0.6669 ± 0.0466 h−1). These results provide a basis for the scaled-up production of G. frondosa mycelium in subsequent studies.

It is well established that sugar serves as the primary carbon source for microbes during fermentation, playing a crucial role in both microbial growth and product accumulation. Therefore, changes in the residual sugar levels in the fermentation broth were monitored during the fermentation of both strains. As shown in Fig. 7E, residual sugar levels in both groups gradually decreased with increasing cultivation time, which is consistent with previous studies [85]. Given that a certain amount of residual sugar remained at the end of fermentation, it is possible that glucose was not the limiting factor in G. frondosa fermentation at this stage. However, at all sampling time points except days 1 and 15, the residual sugar in the mutant strain group was higher than that in the WT strain group, especially from day 4 to day 11 and on day 13. Similar observations have been reported by other researchers. For instance, Picon et al. observed that an Escherichia coli mutant strain exhibited a higher protein production ratio than the wild-type strain, along with a lower glucose consumption rate [86]. These results suggest that the G. frondosa mutant strain D24 may possess enhanced metabolic efficiency, potentially directing glucose more effectively toward target product biosynthesis and mycelial growth, while possibly reducing carbon flux into competing byproduct pathways.

Additionally, the pH profiles of the two strains were recorded daily during fermentation. The results showed that the pH values of both fermentation broths gradually decreased from an initial value of 6.2 and stabilized at around 4, which aligns with findings from a previous study [85]. Moreover, it can be noted in Fig. 7F that the pH of the mutant strain’s fermentation broth was not significantly different from that of the original strain (p > 0.05). According to previous studies, variations in culture pH can markedly alter the synthesis of active substances and overall biomass in some fungi [87, 88]. However, the absence of significant pH differences between the two strains in this study suggests that pH fluctuations were unlikely to be the sole driving factor behind the higher biomass and protein production observed in the mutant strain D24.

Previous studies have demonstrated that dense hyphal pellets can restrict substrate and oxygen diffusion, impairing metabolic activity and reducing yield [89]. Statistical analysis and annotation of genomic SNPs and InDels

To investigate the genetic variation of the mutant strain D24, genome resequencing analysis was performed on both the parental strain and the mutant strain D24. After filtering, the clean reads were obtained and aligned with the reference genome, which were summarized in Table S3. It can be clearly noted that the mapping rates for the WT strain and mutant strain D24 were 83.09% and 77.17%, respectively. The proportion of Q30 in each strain sample was demonstrated to be higher than 90%. Moreover, highly genome coverage rates (exceeding 92%) were found in two strains. These data indicate good reference genome coverage and suitability for variant detection analysis.

Furthermore, two common mutational events, namely InDels and SNPs, were introduced to analyze the obtained data. Compared to the reference genome, there were 1,125,718–1,130,586 SNPs and 165,904–166,500 InDels identified in the WT as well as D24 strains, respectively, indicating there were differences between the two strains and the reference genome (Fig. 9A). For the distribution of variant regions, it can be noted that these two strains exhibited a generally similar distribution pattern, and the highest number of SNPs was found to be located in the intronic and exonic regions, while the highest number of InDels was found to be located in the upstream and intronic regions (Figs. 9B and 9C). Additionally, six variation types of SNPs were identified, among which C: G > T: A and T: A > C: G were the most frequent, while T: A > A: T transversions were the least frequent (Fig. 9D). Both strains displayed comparable distributions of SNPs across mutation classes, with the exception of C: G > T: A. It can be seen that, compared with the original strain, the mutant strain D24 had fewer C: G > T: A. Additionally, based on whether they caused amino acid changes, the mutations in the coding region were further classified, including nonsynonymous SNPs, synonymous SNPs, stopgain, stoploss, etc. As illustrated in Table 5, the wild strain had 42 less SNPs with nonsynonymous mutations than the mutant strain D24. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to elucidate the differences in functional regulation of the mutant strain at the metabolic pathway level (Fig. 10D). The analysis revealed that 34 genes in mutant D24 were involved in amino acid-related pathways (pathways such as tryptophan metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis), which may be related to the supply of precursors for protein synthesis. It is well known that amino acids are indispensable nutrients that sustain various life activities. Organisms can utilize these compounds as carbon sources for energy production or as nitrogen sources to support growth and development. Additionally, specific amino acids participate in signal transduction as signaling molecules and serve as protective agents against environmental stress [92]. Additionally, 16 genes are involved in terpenoid and alkaloid metabolism (pathways such as indole alkaloid biosynthesis, diterpenoid biosynthesis, and isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis), suggesting that secondary metabolic pathways might be altered. In addition, some genes are also involved in carbohydrate metabolism and ABC transporters. Furthermore, 29 genes are involved in lipid metabolism (pathways such as linoleic acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, and fatty acid degradation), which may affect cell membrane composition and energy metabolism. Of particular note, the linoleic acid metabolism pathway was the most significantly enriched one. The linoleic acid metabolism pathway is a core metabolic network in living organisms that converts the essential fatty acid linoleic acid into a range of metabolites, including oxylipins. Oxylipins are oxygenated lipid species generated from polyunsaturated fatty acids through either enzymatic or non-enzymatic oxygenation reactions, including hydroxyoctadecaenoic acid (HODE), hydroperoxyoctadecadienoic acid (HPODE), etc. [93]. Previous studies have revealed that oxylipins exhibit crucial biological activities, functioning as signaling factors for communication both within and between cells and as regulators of microbial metabolism [94, 95]. In present work, two genes were enriched in this pathway, including PpoC and CYP3A-like gene. Among them, PpoC encodes linoleate 10 R-lipoxygenase, a LOX (lipoxygenases) enzyme that catalyzes the oxidation of linoleic acid to produce hydroperoxyoctadecadienoic acid (HPODE) [96]. This intermediate can then be further metabolized by other enzymes into oxylipins (hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HODE) and di-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (diHODE)) [97]. Meanwhile, it was reported that deletion of the PpoC allele also reduced spore development in Aspergillus nidulans [96]. Additionally, a gene encoding a CYP3A-like cytochrome P450 (CYP450) was identified in this pathway. In fungi, linoleic acid can be oxidized by linoleate 8-dioxygenase (8-DOX) into hydroperoxides, which are then converted by the CYP450 enzymes into diols, compounds that promote hyphal branching and development in filamentous fungi [97]. Taken together, it is speculated that genetic variations in CYP3A-like and PpoC may influence the linoleic acid metabolism, leading to increased production of oxylipins, including diols, thereby promoting hyphal growth of G. frondosa. Although the absolute yield of D24 requires further improvement before direct industrial application, the candidate mutations identified here offer valuable targets for future strain engineering, whether through introduction into superior backgrounds or via application of the same mutagenesis pipeline to other strains.

Conclusions

In this study, a high-efficiency protoplast preparation and DES mutagenesis system was established for G. frondosa, yielding a genetically stable mutant strain, D24, with significantly improved mycelial biomass and protein yield. Phenotypic and fermentation kinetic analyses revealed that D24 exhibits accelerated growth kinetics, altered hyphal morphology, and enhanced mitochondrial abundance, which may indicate a shift toward more active metabolic and biosynthetic activity. Additionally, genome resequencing identified 57,216 SNPs in the D24 strain compared with the WT strain, including 4,068 non-synonymous mutations involving 2,505 genes. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses further revealed that these genes were potentially involved in pathways related to amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, terpenoid and alkaloid synthesis, and other biological processes. Overall, this study provides a novel high-yielding mutant strain of G. frondosa, offering promising material for the development of G. frondosa. Nevertheless, further research is still needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms responsible for this action of the mutant strain. Additionally, the biofunctional properties of this novel strain require additional characterization.
