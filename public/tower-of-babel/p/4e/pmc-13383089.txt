Navigating the small airways with tezepelumab
Authors: Jo Vi Lim, Chris RuiWen Kuo, Rory Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shareable abstractOscillometry-defined small airway dysfunction (SAD) is prevalent in severe asthma and may improve with tezepelumab. This real-world study supports SAD as a clinically relevant, treatable trait and proposes oscillometry as a complementary assessment tool. https://bit.ly/4r1sIAf

The small airways, defined as those with an internal diameter of <2 mm, have historically been termed the “silent zone” due to the persistent challenges clinicians face in assessing and managing small airways dysfunction (SAD) [1]. SAD is associated with increased asthma exacerbation risk, poorer symptom control, and greater disease severity [2]. Conventional spirometry may overlook SAD, as the forced expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of the forced vital capacity (FEF25–75) is highly dependent on age and lung volume [3]. The pathophysiology of SAD is thought to involve mucus plugging, subepithelial fibrosis, and bronchial smooth muscle hypertrophy, all of which lead to inflammation and obstruction of the peripheral airways [4, 5]. A major barrier to effective treatment of SAD is achieving adequate drug deposition within these distal airways. One proposed strategy is the use of systemically administered therapies, such as biologics, which circumvent the limitations of aerosol delivery from inhaler devices.

In this regard, the recent phase 4 randomised controlled VESTIGE trial demonstrated that dupilumab, an anti-interleukin 4 receptor alpha biologic, significantly attenuates SAD as assessed by oscillometry, with improvements in peripheral airway resistance (Rrs5-20) and reactance (AX) exceeding biological variability thresholds and thereby indicating clinical relevance compared with placebo [6, 7]. Whereas spirometry requires a forced expiratory manoeuvre, oscillometry relies solely on tidal breathing, making it a more physiological assessment of airway function. In oscillometry, resistance reflects airflow limitation, whereas reactance provides an index of lung compliance [8].

Tezepelumab is a novel monoclonal antibody targeting the epithelial-derived alarmin thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) and has demonstrated efficacy in type 2 (T2) high asthma, with more modest benefits observed in T2 low disease [9]. Tezepelumab shares mechanistic similarities with dupilumab through downstream suppression of key inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13, which play an important role in small-airway inflammation, mucus plugging and airway hyperresponsiveness [10, 11]. It may therefore be hypothesised that upstream inhibition of TSLP could exert effects on SAD comparable to those observed with dupilumab. However, the phase 2 randomised controlled CASCADE trial did not demonstrate significant improvements in SAD with tezepelumab compared with placebo, although it was not enriched with patients with SAD a priori [12]. Nonetheless, emerging real-world evidence suggests that tezepelumab is associated with significant improvements in SAD among patients with severe persistent asthma [13, 14].

We therefore read with interest the recent paper by Kuperschmidt et al. [15], a single-centre prospective observational study of 34 patients with severe asthma treated with tezepelumab and followed longitudinally for 6 months (figure 1). Notably, seven patients (21%) had previously received another biologic therapy, 9% were receiving maintenance oral corticosteroids, and 9% had comorbid chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps. Using pragmatic cut-points of 0.10 kPa·L−1·s−1 and/or 1.0 kPa·L−1 for Rrs5-20 and AX, respectively, 76% of the cohort demonstrated oscillometry-defined SAD [16]. Treatment with tezepelumab was associated with significant improvements in SAD, reflected by mean reductions in Rrs5-20 and AX that exceeded established biological variability thresholds (0.04 kPa·L−1·s−1 and 0.39 kPa·L−1, respectively) [6]. The observed alignment between physiological improvements in oscillometry parameters; significant improvements in asthma control test score exceeding minimal clinically important difference of three units; and reductions in exacerbation frequency provides further evidence that SAD is not only clinically meaningful but also a predictable treatable trait [17]. Notably, improvements in forced expiratory volume in 1 s 1 and FEF25–75 also exceeded biological variability thresholds of 150 mL and 210 mL·s−1, respectively [6].

Tezepelumab inhibits thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) thereby preventing activation of downstream inflammatory cytokine pathways. 6 months of tezepelumab therapy resulted in statistically significant improvements in small airway resistance (Rrs5-20), lung compliance (AX), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and mid-expiratory flow (FEF25–75), all exceeding established biological variability thresholds. Clinically meaningful reductions in exacerbation rates were observed, with improvements in asthma control test (ACT) scores surpassing the minimal clinically important difference. The study's main strength included its real-world prospective design, which complements and extends the applicability of findings beyond the highly selected patient groups typically enrolled in randomised controlled trials with strict inclusion criteria. Extended follow-up will be essential to determine whether the observed benefits are maintained over time. However, there was a notable absence of exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FENO) measurements, which play a pivotal role in identifying patients most likely to benefit from tezepelumab [9]. As a result, the study population may not fully reflect real-world prescribing practices in which FENO is routinely considered. Second, among the seven biologic-experienced patients, three had previously received dupilumab, a therapy known to improve SAD [7]. This may have confounded the observed treatment effects, particularly given the relatively small sample size. Third, the lack of a placebo control raises the possibility that some of the observed improvements may be attributable to regression to the mean. Nevertheless, accurate identification and treatment of SAD are important, as it is associated with dynamic hyperinflation and worse symptoms [18].

The current literature highlights several important questions that warrant further investigation. Not all patients experienced resolution of SAD, highlighting the heterogeneity of distal airway involvement and the need for predictors of response. At baseline, 76% of patients exhibited SAD, decreasing to 44% at 6 months. Residual SAD despite biologic therapy may reflect fixed structural remodelling of the small airways rather than ongoing inflammation alone. We would be particularly interested to determine whether resolution of SAD translates into sustained clinical remission and reduced exacerbation rates in patients treated with tezepelumab. A randomised controlled trial powered specifically on oscillometry-defined SAD is now required to determine definitively whether tezepelumab attenuates SAD. A recently completed placebo-controlled study (NCT05280418) evaluating the effects of anti-TSLP therapy on airway structure and function has incorporated oscillometry as an additional outcome, and its results are awaited with interest.

Future trials should enrich for patients with baseline oscillometry-defined SAD, incorporate biologic-naive populations, and include longitudinal oscillometry endpoints alongside clinical outcomes. In this context, it would also be of interest to explore whether other upstream anti-alarmin therapies, such as the anti-IL-33 monoclonal antibody itepekimab, exert similar effects on the small airways. Beyond treatment initiation, key uncertainties remain regarding treatment optimisation. Specifically, the impact of inhaled corticosteroid dose reduction on SAD following stabilisation with tezepelumab is unknown. The upcoming ARRIVAL (NCT06473779) study will partially address this, though it is primarily powered for loss of asthma control rather than SAD. Finally, it remains unclear whether biologic dosing intervals can be safely extended without deterioration in small airways physiology [19], an issue with important implications for long-term disease control, treatment burden, and healthcare costs.
