Preparedness of family caregivers for elderly patients with pressure injuries: a study of current status and predictors
Authors: Jia Jia, Ya-shuang He, Qiu-yuan Wu, Yi-yuan Ren, Xiao-fen Mai, Xinfeng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily caregivers are pivotal in the care of elderly patients with pressure injuries (PIs). Their preparedness is crucial for patient outcomes, yet its determinants are not fully understood. This study aimed to assess preparedness among family caregivers of elderly PIs patients and identify its key predictors.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in a general hospital from May 2024 to March 2025. A total of 428 family caregivers of elderly patients with PIs completed structured questionnaires, including the Caregiver Preparedness Scale (CPS), Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), and Pressure Ulcer Knowledge Assessment Scale (PUKAS). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression.ResultsThe mean CPS score was 28.04 ± 4.03 (range 0–32), indicating moderate-to-high preparedness. Caregiver burden was negatively correlated with preparedness (r=-0.333, p < 0.05), while PI knowledge was not significantly associated. Multiple regression revealed that higher caregiver education (β = 0.215–0.254, p = 0.002), receipt of moderately detailed disease-related education (β = 0.152, p = 0.002), and urban residence (vs. town, β=-0.127, p = 0.005) were significant predictors of preparedness, whereas higher burden predicted lower preparedness (β=-0.302, p < 0.001).ConclusionsFamily caregivers of elderly PIs patients in China report generally high preparedness, which is influenced by educational level, residence, received education, and caregiver burden. Interventions should move beyond knowledge transfer to include hands-on training, emotional support, and burden alleviation, with particular attention to socioeconomically disadvantaged caregivers.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Pressure injury, Family caregivers, Caregiver preparedness, Caregiver burden, Influencing factors

Introduction

The global demographic shift towards an aging population presents a significant challenge to healthcare systems, particularly in the management of chronic conditions [1]. This trend is especially pronounced in China, which has entered a deep aging society with over 300 million people aged 60 and above [1]. A direct consequence is the rising prevalence of frailty and limited mobility among older adults, which significantly increases their susceptibility to PIs [2]. PIs, defined as localized damage to the skin and underlying tissue resulting from sustained pressure or shear forces, not only cause significant patient suffering and increase infection risks but also impose a heavy economic burden on healthcare systems [3]. The prevalence of PIs remains alarmingly high worldwide, with rates in China’s long-term care and home-based settings reaching as high as 10.87% to 30%, highlighting the pervasiveness and severity of this issue [4, 5].

In line with a strategic shift in healthcare delivery from institutional to home- and community-based care, the responsibility for PI prevention and management has increasingly fallen upon family caregivers. These non-professional caregivers are often assume a primary caregiving role, requiring them to perform complex nursing tasks such as regular repositioning, skin care, nutritional support, and wound observation. From a nursing perspective, these family members are essential, yet often under-supported, extensions of the healthcare team. Consequently, their capacity has become a critical determinant of clinical outcomes for elderly patients with PIs. Within this context, the concept of caregiver preparedness is of paramount importance. Defined as a caregiver’s perceived readiness to manage the multifaceted demands of care across physical, emotional, and logistical domains [6], adequate preparedness is directly linked to enhanced caregiver confidence, more effective care behaviors, and improved patient outcomes [7]. Conversely, a lack of preparedness can lead to role strain, psychological exhaustion for the caregiver, and potential threats to patient health and safety [7].

According to the Stress and Coping Theory by Lazarus and Folkman, caring for a patient with a PI can be viewed as a major stressor [8]. A caregiver’s level of preparedness essentially reflects their cognitive appraisal of this stressor and their perceived resources to manage it. High preparedness enables caregivers to more positively appraise the caregiving situation and mobilize effective coping strategies. While the importance of caregiver preparedness is well-documented in other chronic conditions such as cancer and stroke, systematic research specifically targeting the unique and complex context of elderly PI care remains scarce, particularly within the Chinese socio-cultural landscape. The factors that influence preparedness among this specific caregiver population are not yet well understood, which hinders the development of targeted support strategies.

Therefore, this study was designed to address this knowledge gap. The primary objectives were to systematically assess the current status of preparedness among family caregivers of elderly patients with PIs in China and to identify the key influencing factors. The findings are expected to provide an empirical basis for developing targeted support programs and interventions. Specifically, this research aims to generate actionable evidence for clinical nurses, who are at the forefront of patient and family education. Such initiatives aim to empower family caregivers, alleviate their burden, and ultimately enhance the quality of care and life for elderly patients with PIs.

Methods

Study design and setting

We employed a cross-sectional study design to investigate the preparedness of family caregivers for elderly patients with PIs. The study was conducted from May 2024 to March 2025 in a tertiary Grade-A hospital in Zhuhai, Guangdong, China. As a large tertiary referral center, this hospital manages a substantial number of elderly patients with complex wounds, including severe PIs, thereby ensuring an adequate pool of potential participants.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. The minimum sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software. Based on a medium effect size (f² = 0.08), ten predictor variables, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a statistical power (1 − β) of 0.90, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 318 [9]. To account for potential invalid responses or attrition, the target sample size was increased by approximately 10% to 350 participants.

Inclusion criteria for patients were: (1) diagnosed with a pressure injury according to established clinical guidelines [10]; (2) duration of illness > 3 months; and (3) age ≥ 65 years. Patients were excluded if they had severe cognitive impairment or consciousness disorders.

Inclusion criteria for family caregivers were: (1) a spouse, child, son/daughter-in-law, or other relative serving as the primary caregiver; (2) providing care for > 8 h per week; (3) age ≥ 18 years; (4) capable of verbal communication and comprehension; and (5) willing to voluntarily participate in the study. Caregivers with a paid employment relationship with the patient or a history of mental illness (e.g., anxiety, depression) were excluded.

A total of 435 caregivers were initially approached, of whom 428 completed the survey, resulting in a valid response rate of 98.39%.

Instruments

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising four sections:

General information questionnaire

This self-designed questionnaire collected sociodemographic and clinical data. It included patient information (age, gender, education level, mobility status, PI stage) and caregiver information (age, gender, marital status, education level, employment status, place of residence, relationship to the patient, average monthly household income, cumulative caregiving duration, co-caregiving status, perceived financial impact of the patient’s illness, and extent of disease-related education received). Among them, the classification of the residence is " Rural”, " Town” or " Urban”, which is determined based on the participants’ self-reports.

Caregiver preparedness scale (CPS)

Caregiver preparedness was measured using the Chinese version of the Caregiver Preparedness Scale, originally developed by Archbold et al. [11] and translated and validated by Liu et al. [12]. The scale consists of 8 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all prepared”) to 4 (“very well prepared”). The total score ranges from 0 to 32, with higher scores indicating a greater level of perceived preparedness. The Cronbach’s α for the Chinese version was reported to be 0.925 [12]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for CPS was 0.942.

Zarit burden interview (ZBI)

Caregiver burden was assessed using the Chinese version of the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), developed by Zarit et al. [13] and validated by Wang et al. [14]. This 22-item scale evaluates burden across two dimensions: personal strain and role strain. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“nearly always”). The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with scores categorized as follows: 0–19 (little to no burden), 20–39 (mild burden), 40–59 (moderate burden), and ≥ 60 (severe burden). The Chinese version of the ZBI demonstrated good reliability in previous studies (Cronbach’s α = 0.958) [14]. In the present study, the scale also showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.953).

Pressure ulcer knowledge assessment scale (PUKAS)

Caregiver knowledge regarding PIs was measured using a questionnaire developed by Zhou Dongmei [15]. While the original instrument contains three dimensions, only the “PI-related knowledge” dimension was used for this study, as it was most relevant to the research aims. This dimension includes 25 true/false items. A correct answer (“yes”) was scored as 1 point, while incorrect (“no”) or uncertain (“not sure”) answers were scored as 0. The total score ranges from 0 to 25, with higher scores reflecting a better knowledge of PI prevention and care. The scale has demonstrated good test-retest reliability and a Cronbach’s α of 0.77 [15]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for “PI-related knowledge” dimension of PUKAS was 0.828.

Data collection

A pilot study was first conducted with 50 caregivers to test the feasibility and clarity of the questionnaire, and minor adjustments were made accordingly. The research team received standardized training. Eligible caregivers were approached in person by a trained researcher within the hospital wards. We employed a electronic data collection approach to enhance efficiency and accuracy. After providing written informed consent, caregivers were invited to scan a QR code to access and complete the questionnaire on their own mobile devices via a secure online survey platform (Wenjuanxing). This approach minimized data entry errors, allow for real-time completion checks, and facilitate participation for caregivers who might find paper-based forms cumbersome. Crucially, this was not an open online survey; participation was strictly limited to those who were first screened and consented in person by our research staff, ensuring that all respondents were indeed the eligible primary caregivers. Research staff remained present to clarify questions, provide assistance if needed, and immediately verify the completeness of each submission. This process guaranteed the reliability and authenticity of the collected data.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. [2024] (k256-1)). All participants were fully informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before they commenced the survey. Anonymity and confidentiality of the data were strictly maintained.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize the characteristics of the patients and caregivers, as well as the scores of the main study variables. The normality of the distribution of continuous variables was assessed. To identify factors associated with caregiver preparedness, univariate analyses were conducted using independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between caregiver preparedness, caregiver burden, and PI knowledge. Finally, variables that were statistically significant (P < 0.05) in the univariate analyses were entered into a multiple linear regression model to identify the independent predictors of caregiver preparedness. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Predictors of caregiver preparedness

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent predictors of caregiver preparedness. All variables that were significant in the univariate analyses were entered into the model as independent variables. The final model was statistically significant (F = 11.161, P < 0.001) and explained 27.6% of the variance in caregiver preparedness (Adjusted R² = 0.276).

As shown in Table 4, compared with caregivers of patients with an educational level of primary school or below, those caring for patients with secondary school education had significantly lower caregiver preparedness (β = -0.146, P = 0.011). Relative to caregivers with primary school education or below, those with secondary school education (β = 0.215, P = 0.002) and college/university education or above (β = 0.254, P = 0.002) demonstrated significantly higher preparedness. Compared with urban caregivers, those residing in towns had significantly lower preparedness (β = -0.127, P = 0.005), while the difference in preparedness between rural and urban caregivers was marginally significant (β = -0.160, P = 0.072). Caregivers who received moderately detailed disease-related education exhibited significantly higher preparedness than those who obtained only brief guidance (β = 0.152, P = 0.002), whereas no significant difference was observed between those who received very detailed education and those with brief guidance (β = 0.061, P = 0.159). Furthermore, caregiver preparedness was significantly and negatively associated with caregiver burden (total score), such that higher burden was associated with lower preparedness (β = -0.302, P < 0.001).

Discussion

The primary motivation for this research was to address a critical and growing problem in healthcare: the increasing reliance on family members to manage complex chronic conditions like PIs in an aging population. With the shift towards home-based care, the preparedness of these non-professional caregivers becomes a direct determinant of patient safety and quality of life. This study aimed to move beyond anecdotal evidence to systematically assess caregiver preparedness and identify actionable predictors that could inform support strategies.

Principal findings and the preparedness landscape

This study found that family caregivers of elderly PIs patients in China report a moderate-to-high level of preparedness, with a mean score of 28.04 out of 32. This relatively optimistic result may contrast with findings in more acute care contexts like stroke [16], possibly because the chronic nature of PIs allows caregivers to gradually accumulate experience and adapt to their roles. However, this overall score masks crucial disparities. The core finding of our research is that this sense of preparedness is not uniform; it is significantly shaped by a caregiver’s socioeconomic background and their direct interactions with the healthcare system. The multiple linear regression analysis was pivotal, identifying five key independent predictors: patient’s educational level, caregiver educational level, place of residence, receipt of disease-related education, and caregiver burden.

Socioeconomic disparities as barriers to preparedness

The predictive power of both educational level and place of residence underscores a significant socioeconomic dimension to caregiver preparedness. Consistent with research on caregivers in other chronic disease settings [17], our finding that higher education predicts better preparedness points. Caregivers with higher education are often better equipped to understand complex medical information, problem-solve effectively, and advocate for the patient within the healthcare system [18]. Caregivers of patients with a secondary school education exhibited significantly lower preparedness than those caring for patients with a primary school education or below. A plausible explanation is that patients with higher education level may possess greater capacity for self-management of their condition, thereby requiring less intensive caregiver assistance.

Our finding regarding the urban-rural disparity, based on the self-reported residence classification, underscores a critical gap in healthcare equity. It suggests that caregivers’ perceived preparedness is shaped by the broader healthcare ecosystem to which they have access. Urban caregivers likely benefit from a denser infrastructure of medical resources, including specialized clinics and community nursing services, which can bolster their confidence and skills. This aligns with findings that rural caregivers face significant barriers, including limited access to professional guidance and support networks [19]. From the perspective of Lazarus and Folkman’s Stress and Coping Theory [8], urban caregivers may have more external resources to draw upon during their secondary appraisal of the caregiving stressor, leading to a more positive assessment of their ability to cope and thus a higher sense of preparedness [20].

The power of education and the caregiver burden

The analysis revealed that the receipt of disease-related education was a positive predictor of preparedness, while caregiver burden was a significant negative predictor. This contrast has important implications for clinical practice. The positive impact of formal education confirms that preparedness is a modifiable attribute. When healthcare professionals provide structured, detailed guidance, they are not just transferring information; they are building caregiver self-efficacy. This is strongly supported by intervention studies demonstrating that targeted training improves caregivers’ knowledge and practical skills in PIs prevention [21].

Conversely, the negative relationship between burden and preparedness highlights the depleting nature of the caregiving role. This finding is consistent with research in stroke caregiving [22] and suggests a potentially vicious cycle: unprepared caregivers may find their tasks more stressful, leading to higher burden, which in turn depletes their emotional and cognitive resources, further eroding their sense of preparedness [23]. High burden can lead to burnout, a state antithetical to feeling ready and capable. This underscores that interventions cannot solely focus on education; they must simultaneously incorporate strategies to alleviate burden, such as connecting caregivers to social support systems and respite services [24].

The “knowing-doing” gap and unmeasured factors

A notable and statistically significant finding was the lack of correlation between caregivers’ objective PIs knowledge scores and their subjective, self-reported preparedness. This “knowing-doing gap” suggests that possessing factual knowledge is insufficient to foster a true sense of readiness. Preparedness is a multidimensional construct that encompasses not just knowledge, but also practical skills, emotional readiness, and problem-solving confidence [25]. A caregiver may know that a patient needs to be turned every two hours but may lack the physical confidence or skill to do so safely. This highlights the limitations of purely didactic educational approaches and points to the need for interventions that include hands-on training, simulation, and emotional support. It also indirectly validates the distinct construct of our primary outcome—the Caregiver Preparedness Scale—which measures perceived readiness rather than just knowledge.

Furthermore, it is crucial to acknowledge that our model, while statistically significant, explained 27.6% of the variance in preparedness (Adjusted R² = 0.276). This indicates that while the identified predictors are important, other factors account for the majority of the variance. Future research should investigate the role of psychosocial variables such as caregiver resilience, coping styles, self-efficacy, and family dynamics. Patient-related factors like the patient’s cognitive status, pain level, and cooperation with care, along with system-level factors like the quality of hospital discharge planning, could also be significant unmeasured confounders.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is one of the earlier projects that adopted well-validated multi-dimensional scales to systematically study the coping abilities of family caregivers for elderly PI patients. The relatively large sample size enhances the statistical power of our predictor analysis. Furthermore, by assessing knowledge, burden, and preparedness simultaneously, our study identifies a critical “knowing-doing gap,” highlighting that subjective readiness is a more complex construct than objective knowledge alone. This provides a nuanced understanding that can better inform intervention design.

However, several limitations must be considered. First, the use of a convenience sampling method in a single tertiary hospital in Zhuhai limits the generalizability of our findings. Caregivers in community settings, primary care, or other regions of China may have different experiences and levels of preparedness. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality. For instance, while we found a strong negative association between burden and preparedness, we cannot determine the direction of this relationship; it is likely bidirectional and can only be clarified through longitudinal studies. Finally, as all measures were self-reported, social desirability and recall bias are potential limitations.

Implications for nursing practice and future research

Despite its limitations, this study provides clear, actionable insights for nursing practice, as nurses are the primary healthcare professionals responsible for patient and family education and support. Our findings call for a proactive, multi-faceted approach to empowering family caregivers.

Develop Targeted, Nurse-Led Support Programs: Nurses should use our findings to proactively identify at-risk caregivers—particularly those with lower educational attainment, those from non-urban areas, and those reporting high burden. Support should not be “one-size-fits-all.” Instead, nurse-led programs should be tailored to the specific needs of these vulnerable groups.

For future research, longitudinal studies are urgently needed to untangle the causal relationship between caregiver burden, knowledge, and preparedness over time. Multi-center studies involving diverse settings (including community and primary care) are necessary to enhance the generalizability of these findings. Most importantly, there is a pressing need for intervention studies that test the efficacy of tailored, nurse-led support programs based on the predictors identified here. The ultimate goal of such research should be to determine if enhancing caregiver preparedness translates into the most important outcomes: improved healing, reduced suffering, and better quality of life for elderly patients with PIs.

Conclusions

This study reveals that while family caregivers of elderly patients with PIs in China perceive themselves as moderately-to-highly prepared, this preparedness is significantly contingent on key modifiable factors. Caregivers with lower educational levels, those residing in rural areas, and those experiencing higher levels of caregiving burden are significantly less prepared. Furthermore, the receipt of detailed, systematic health education is a strong positive predictor of preparedness. These findings underscore that caregiver preparedness is not an inherent trait but a state that can be cultivated. Healthcare professionals should move beyond passive information-giving and develop targeted, multi-faceted interventions that provide practical skills training, address socioeconomic disparities in access to resources, and actively work to alleviate caregiver burden. Empowering these vital caregivers is a direct and necessary pathway to improving the quality of care and clinical outcomes for the growing population of elderly patients with PIs.
