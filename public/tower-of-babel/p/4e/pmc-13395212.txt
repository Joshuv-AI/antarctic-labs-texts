A cost-minimization analysis of telemedicine vs standard induction for buprenorphine: Evidence from a randomized clinical trial in India
Authors: Amudeep Singh, Harpreet S. Dhillon, Neha Purohit, Abhishek Ghosh, Shalini Naik, BN Subodh, Debasish Basu, Prinja Shankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:In-person buprenorphine induction poses substantial cost and access barriers in India’s opioid agonist treatment programs, while telemedicine-assisted induction may offer a scalable, lower-cost alternative.Aim:To compare the societal costs of telemedicine-assisted buprenorphine induction (TABI) with standard care (SOC), while evaluating treatment retention and quality-of-life (QoL) outcomes.Methods:An economic evaluation was embedded within a randomized controlled trial at a tertiary addiction treatment center, adopting a societal perspective. Health-system costs were estimated using a mixed top-down/bottom-up micro-costing approach for all participants (n = 138; SOC = 70, TABI = 68), while direct out-of-pocket and indirect wage-loss costs were assessed on day 7 in a predefined sub-sample (n = 57; SOC = 28, TABI = 29). Primary outcomes were 1-week retention and QoL; as effectiveness was comparable between arms, a cost-minimization analysis (CMA) was conducted.Results:Mean health-system cost per induction was INR 3,545 (USD 42.26) for SOC vs INR 2,690 (USD 32.06) for TABI (24% lower). In the out-of-pocket expenditure (OOP) sub-sample, mean direct patient costs were INR 1,857 (USD 22.13) for SOC vs INR 776 (USD 9.24) for TABI (P < 0.001); mean indirect costs were INR 1,502 (USD 17.91) vs INR 766 (USD 9.13). Total mean societal cost per patient was INR 6,904 (USD 82.33) for SOC vs INR 4,232 (USD 31.88) for TABI (39% saving). Treatment retention was similar between groups (SOC: 76% vs TABI: 82%; P = 0.339), and both demonstrated comparable improvements in QoL at 1 week (84.00 vs 84.17; P = 0.912).Conclusion:Compared with standard induction, TABI substantially reduces health-system and patient costs, while maintaining comparable short-term retention and QoL.

INTRODUCTION

Opioid use disorder constitutes is a critical public health concern in India, with approximately 2.06% of the population reporting opioid use, of which 0.55% (nearly 6 million individuals) report harmful or dependent use category and require structured treatment.[1]

The mainstay of pharmacological management of opioid use disorders in India is opioid agonist maintenance treatment (OAMT) with buprenorphine–naloxone, delivered under National AIDS Control Organization clinics, Integrated Rehabilitation Centers for Addicts, and hospital-based de-addiction centers.[2] Buprenorphine has strong evidence for reducing illicit opioid use, improving retention, and lowering mortality.[34] However, buprenorphine induction—the initial phase of treatment initiation involving starting buprenorphine during opioid withdrawal and titrating the dose until clinical stabilization is achieved—remains a critical bottleneck in the Indian OAMT system.

Current induction protocols require multiple in-person visits during the early stabilization phase to monitor withdrawal, titrate doses, and prevent precipitated withdrawal.[5] These requirements impose substantial patient costs (transportation, lost wages, caregiver time) and contribute to high attrition before stabilization. In rural and semi-urban regions, travel distance further compounds these barriers, often delaying or preventing initiation altogether. Thus, while buprenorphine is clinically effective, the induction process itself limits scalability and access in India.[6]

The COVID-19 pandemic created an opportunity to re-examine induction practices. The Telepsychiatry Operational Guidelines (2020) authorized the use of telemedicine in addiction psychiatry, including for follow-up of OAMT patients.[7] Pilot experiences during the pandemic suggested that remote consultations for OAMT—including counseling, dose adjustment, and follow-up can serve as a viable service delivery model for community-based OAMT clinics, not only during emergencies but also in routine care.[8] It offers potential benefits such as reducing time and financial costs, facilitating access to medical prescriptions for general health conditions, and providing referral guidance for complex clinical needs.[910] Globally, studies have echoed that telemedicine-assisted buprenorphine initiation improves retention, reduces overdose risk, and is cost-effective compared to in-person induction.[11]

Despite India’s expanding digital health infrastructure under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, economic evidence for telemedicine-assisted OAMT is lacking. No study has assessed whether telemedicine-assisted buprenorphine induction (TABI) offers a cost advantage over standard care (SOC) when clinical outcomes—such as treatment retention and quality-of-life (QoL) are comparable. Given the substantial unmet need and structural barriers to in-person induction, assessing whether TABI can reduce health system and patient costs, while maintaining or improving retention and QoL is crucial for scaling OAMT in India.

METHODS

Study site

The current study was undertaken at the out-patient unit of Drug Deaddiction and Treatment Centre (DDTC) of a tertiary-care hospital in North India. The DDTC has a typical OPD setting catering to the needs of patients with substance use disorders.

Study design

A cost-minimization analysis (CMA) was conducted, assuming equivalent outcomes between comparators. The analysis was undertaken from a societal perspective, capturing all relevant costs regardless of the payer, including health system costs and patient-level costs. The analysis compared TABI with SOC for OST.

The primary outcomes were treatment retention and QoL at baseline and 1-week post-initiation. As no significant differences in retention or QoL were observed, clinical equivalence was assumed, and the analysis focused on comparing total costs between TABI and SOC.

Health-system costs were estimated using a mixed top-down/bottom-up micro-costing approach recommended by Drummond et al.[12] All resources utilized during induction were identified, measured, and valued separately for both groups. Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) was assessed using a Cost-of-Illness approach, classifying OOPE into direct and indirect medical costs.[13] Direct medical costs included user fees, diagnostics, drugs, and consumables; direct non-medical costs covered transportation, food, and boarding. Indirect costs reflected wage loss for patients.

Study parameters: Population, criteria, timeline

The study included adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with opioid use disorder. Data collection covered the period from October 2023 to September 2024. Data collected included demographic information, OOPE using a structed tool and QoL using WHO BREF.

All patients received an initial in-person intake visit, as required by protocol during the study period. After intake, patients in SOC group continued to receive medication management and follow-up care on site, while those in the TABI group received these services through real-time audio-visual visits. After the first visit, in SOC, a typical patient would then visit the OPD on Day 2, Day 3, and Day 7; while for the TABI group patient would visit the OPD on Day 7 only. Patients were randomly allocated to either of the two groups using stratified random sampling. The same pool of providers consisting of physicians and nurse practitioners delivered care across both modalities.

Sample size and patient allocation

The study sample comprised a sub-sample from a randomized clinical trial that included 138 participants.[14] The cost-analysis component was added later to the existing protocol. Hence, only a sub-sample of 54 participants was included for the analysis of out-of-pocket spending; however, all 138 participants were included for the health system cost analysis, which was based on the existing records from various hospital departments.

Data collection

Health system cost

Information on space utilization was obtained from the hospital engineering department using facility maps. Prices and year of purchase for capital items and equipment were sourced from the procurement unit, while useful life estimates were gathered from the technical officer and validated through literature review. Furniture quantities and prices were collected through observation and interviews with officer-in-charge. Drug quantities prescribed to each patient were recorded, and unit prices were retrieved from central pharmacy records.

Staff salary details were obtained from hospital administration, and time allocation for induction activities was assessed through interviews. Human resources included a resident doctor, nurse, counsellor, social worker, hospital and sanitary attendants, receptionist, laboratory technician, and security staff. Gross annual salaries were apportioned based on time devoted to OAMT induction. Data on diagnostics including type and number of routine investigations as well as urine drug sensitivity tests were recorded for each patient. Data on price per unit investigations was procured from personnel in charge of the laboratory. Utility costs (electricity, water, maintenance, and overheads) were extracted from hospital records and apportioned by patient volume across groups.

Out-of-pocket expenditure

Face-to-face interviews were conducted by the clinician on day 7 of patient recruitment, in a predefined sub-sample of the RCT (SOC = 28, TABI = 29). Data were collected using a semi-structured tool covering: (i) general information (health-facility visits, distance, transportation); (ii) direct medical expenses (consultation fees, diagnostics, medicines); (iii) direct non-medical expenses (transportation, food, lodging); and (iv) indirect costs. The indirect costs were captured by enquiring about the lost hours of their productive time due to OAMT induction therapy in the last 7 days, and their monthly wages. Indirect cost for a patient was then calculated as:

Indirect cost = Total number of workdays foregone × daily wage rates,

An 8-hour workday was assumed for estimating productivity loss, consistent with International Labor Organization standards and prior research.[1516] Interviews were conducted in the local language, and responses were entered digitally. Missing data were addressed using standard data-cleaning procedures.

Retention and quality-of-life

Retention in treatment was operationally defined as the patient presenting to seek treatment on day 7 following induction. QoL was assessed for SOC and TABI groups using the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire,[17] a validated 26-item tool measuring four domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in regional language at baseline and after 1 week of intervention. Any missing responses were addressed according to the WHOQOL-BREF scoring guidelines.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were used to assess both sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the sample. Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Washington, USA), and SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) were used to analyze the data.

Health system cost

To estimate diagnostic costs, the unit price of each test was multiplied by the number of times that test was performed. Similarly, drug costs were calculated by multiplying the unit price of each prescribed medication with the quantity prescribed. The aggregated values were used to derive the total expenditure on diagnostics and medicines for participants in each group. For capital costs of building or space, rental price was multiplied by the floor area to estimate opportunity cost of building being used. Annualized cost of capital resources was estimated considering the life of the item and a discount rate of 3%. In case of joint or shared costs, either capital or recurrent, the cost of the overall resource that was used for OST induction was apportioned using suitable statistics. Human resource costs were apportioned based on the time allocated for the study participants. It is noteworthy that for TABI patients the medicines prescribed are dispensed on Day 1 of induction and patients are followed up over call (tele-consultation) to review. Patients come directly on Day 7 to complete the induction process. The costs involved in tele-consultation are therefore added to TABI group only. In SOC group patients would come on all four days of induction viz Day 1, Day 2, Day 3, and Day 7. Total cost from health system perspective for cost heads including human resource, building, drugs, furniture, equipment and overheads for both SOC and TABI were estimated. Unit costs for induction for both the groups were estimated. Additionally, day specific unit costs were computed by dividing the total cost incurred for a particular day by the total number of patients visiting that day.

Out-of-pocket expenditure

On day 7 following OST induction, participants were asked to recall and report all patient-level costs incurred during the induction process. Reported expenditures included direct medical costs (consultation fees, investigations, medicines), direct non-medical costs (transportation, food, accommodation), and indirect costs (loss of wages). Costs were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and group comparisons between SOC and TABI were conducted using independent sample t-tests to assess differences in direct costs borne by patients.

Retention and quality-of-life

The primary dependent variable, retention to buprenorphine treatment, was dichotomized as “retained” or “not retained” based on the retention definition of remaining in treatment ≥7 days. The mean QoL in different domains of the WHOQOLBREF was derived using the standard methods of analysis. The OOPE on medical (medicines, diagnostics) and nonmedical components (travel and food) for outpatient consultation was calculated on a per visit basis. Domain-wise and overall scores were calculated and compared between the groups using independent sample t-tests. P values of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before collecting OOPE data. Patient anonymity and confidentiality were maintained, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. The clinical trial was registered with the Clinical Trial Registry, India (CTRI/2023/10/058173).

RESULTS

Health systems cost

Total costs

The study estimated that a total cost of INR 2,18,600 (USD 2605.98) incurred upon the health system for SOC group (n = 70) and INR HE 1,74,799 (USD 2082.84) for TABI group (n = 68) [Tables 1 and 2]. The share of human resources in total cost was around 15% for both SOC and TABI groups, while building or capital costs contributed to approximately 39% and 26% share in total costs for SOC and TABI groups, respectively. The share of diagnostics was around 42% in SOC and 52% in TABI groups. Furniture and equipment contributed a 2% share for TABI group, while it was negligible in SOC group. The share of consumables and overheads was negligible (<1%) for both groups [Supplementary Figures 1 and 2].

Unit costs

Visit-wise unit costs for induction on OAMT in the SOC group were INR 1808 (USD 21.55), INR 484 (USD 5.77), INR 561 (USD 6.69), and INR 691 (USD 8.24) for visits 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. For the TABI group, the corresponding unit costs were INR 1,811 (USD 21.58), INR 72 (USD 0.86), INR 133 (USD 1.59), and INR 675 (USD 8.05). On average, patients in the SOC group incurred a cost of INR 3,545 (USD 42.26) per induction, whereas those in the TABI group incurred INR 2,690 (USD 32.06) per induction [Table 3]. This represents a reduction of INR 855 (USD 10.20) per patient, corresponding to a 24% cost saving with the TABI approach.

The clinical characteristics too did not differ between SOC and TABI groups [Supplementary Table 4].

Retention rates

At one week following induction, treatment retention was comparable between the two groups. In the SOC arm, 53 participants (76%) remained retained in treatment, while in the TABI arm, 56 participants (82%) were retained. The difference between groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.339) [Supplementary Figure 3].

Quality-of-life outcomes

Quality-of-life (QoL) outcomes in RCT sample

QoL was assessed using the WHOQoL-BREF tool at baseline and 1-week post-induction. At baseline, there were no significant differences between the SOC and TABI groups across all domains, including overall QoL, overall health, physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). After 1 week, both groups demonstrated improvements in all QoL domains. However, there were no statistically significant differences between SOC and TABI groups in any domain [Supplementary Table 5].

Indirect costs

We separately estimated the indirect costs attributable to loss of patient wages. A total of 21 patients in the SOC group and 20 patients in the TABI group reported loss of work due to treatment. The aggregate wage loss was INR 31,550 (USD 376.13) in the SOC group and INR 15,325 (USD 182.70) in the TABI group. Correspondingly, the mean wage loss per patient was INR 1,502 (USD 17.91) in the SOC group and INR 766 (USD 9.13) in the TABI group.

Thus, from a patient perspective, total mean expenditure per patient was INR 3,358.79 (USD 40.04) in the SOC group compared to INR 1,541.17 (USD 18.37) in the TABI group forming a 54% cost reduction.

Cost-minimization analysis (CMA)

A CMA was conducted to compare SOC and TABI, assuming equivalent outcomes based on comparable treatment retention and 1-week QoL. The overall per-patient costs were INR 6,904 (USD 82.30) for SOC and INR 4,232 (USD 50.43) for TABI. Category-wise and total costs for both modalities are presented in Table 5. Given similar outcomes and substantially lower costs with TABI, telemedicine-assisted induction was identified as the less costly alternative.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that while both TABI and SOC induction yield comparable quality of life and retention outcomes, TABI offers a significant reduction in both health system and patient level costs. The QoL assessments at baseline and 1-week post-intervention revealed no significant differences between the SOC and TABI groups across all domains, including physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environment. These findings align with previous studies indicating that telemedicine interventions do not compromise patient-reported outcomes. For instance, a CMA in another similar study[18] found no significant differences in maternal health outcomes between telehealth-integrated and conventional antenatal care, despite a reduction in patient costs with telehealth.

From a health system perspective, the mean cost per patient for induction was INR 3,545 (USD 42.26) in the SOC group compared to INR 2,690 (USD 32.06) in the TABI group, reflecting a 24% cost saving. From a patient perspective, the total mean expenditure per patient was INR 3,358.79 (USD 40.0) in the SOC group compared to INR 1,541.17 (USD 18.4) in the TABI group, reflecting more than a 50% reduction. When both patient and health system costs are considered together, the mean societal cost per patient was INR 6,903.79 (USD 82.33) in the SOC group versus INR 4,231.17 (USD 50.45) in the TABI group, representing a saving of INR 2,672.62 (USD 31.88) or nearly 39%. These findings mirror earlier prior evidence demonstrating that telemedicine can offer cost advantages while maintaining comparable clinical outcomes. For instance, a CMA[19] demonstrated that telehealth consultations for chronic pain management resulted in similar costs per patient but with reduced time and steps in the clinical workflow compared to in-clinic visits. Another study of South Korea[20] found that telemedicine reduced societal costs per consultation by USD 7.92 for chronic diseases, USD 27.30 for dementia care, and USD 9.61 for rehabilitation, primarily due to reductions in productivity losses and transportation expenses.

Our findings align with emerging evidence highlighting the economic benefits of telehealth models for OAMT for increasing treatment engagement and retention. Similarly, another study[21] reported no significant differences in retention between telehealth and office-based care, with retention rates of 42% and 51%, respectively. Importantly, their analysis demonstrated that telehealth incurred substantially lower overall costs per patient retained, reinforcing the potential economic advantages of remote induction models.

These studies collectively suggest that telemedicine not only maintains clinical efficacy but also offers substantial cost savings, making it a viable alternative to traditional in-person care. However, reduced patient costs may shift financial burden to providers or insurers, highlighting the need for comprehensive economic evaluations to assess benefits and sustainability telemedicine interventions.

Several limitations should be noted. The short time horizon restricts assessment to the induction period, excluding potential long-term costs or outcomes. The small OOPE sample size and reliance on self-reported data may limit generalizability and introduce recall bias. Broader system-level costs such as tele-consultation training and administrative support were not included. Differences in technology access, digital literacy, wages, and transport costs may also affect applicability to other settings. Intangible benefits like convenience or reduced stigma were not monetized, and inflation or currency fluctuations may influence comparability. The absence of qualitative interviews limited exploration of patients’ experiential and perceived benefits. Finally, as no significant differences in retention or QoL were observed, a cost-minimization approach was used, limiting conclusions beyond cost comparisons.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that TABI is a cost-saving alternative to SOC for initiating OAMT. Given comparable retention and QoL outcomes observed between the two modalities, the CMA showed that TABI incurred substantially lower costs than SOC, while achieving similar clinical results. These results highlight the potential of telemedicine-assisted induction models to enhance access, reduce economic burden, and strengthen the scalability of OAMT in resource-limited healthcare settings. Future research should validate these findings across diverse geographic regions, programmatic contexts, and longer follow-up periods.
