Fault diagnosis of surge arresters via electro-thermal coupled simulation and gradient boosting tree
Authors: Ying Yan, Zhijie Tang, Zuyi Peng, Jiahui Chen, Yaoheng Xie, Zhenming Cai, Xing Xu, Lipeng Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Metal-Oxide Arrester (MOA) is a crucial device for ensuring the safety of power grids, but its fault diagnosis faces the challenge of scarce actual fault samples. In this study, a 3D electro-thermal coupled simulation model is developed to reconstruct four operating conditions: internal moisture, aging of the insulating sleeve, wet contamination, and normal operation. Specifically, fault mechanisms are physically modeled by altering the conductivity of the insulating sleeve for aging, mapping conductive layers with varying coverage on the porcelain housing for wet contamination, and attaching geometric water bands to the valve discs for internal moisture. Based on this model, current and temperature signals are obtained to analyze the electro-thermal characteristics under different faults. Subsequently, a CatBoost-based fault diagnosis framework is introduced to construct a fault diagnosis model. By preprocessing data and integrating features such as temperature statistics and geometric quantities, the model’s recognition performance is significantly improved. Comparative experiments against XGBoost, fitcnet, and Support Vector Machine (SVM) demonstrate the superiority of the proposed method. The final model achieves an accuracy of 99.3%, outperforming XGBoost (98.6%), fitcnet (96.5%), and SVM (90.0%). This study provides an effective solution for MOA fault diagnosis under sample-scarce conditions, enhancing the intelligent assessment and maintenance of power grids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46258-7.

Introduction

MOAs serve as the primary overvoltage‑limiting devices in power grids, protecting electrical equipment from transient overvoltage hazards and thereby safeguarding the operational security of power systems1. During service, however, MOAs are susceptible to various failure mechanisms, including internal moisture ingress, surface accumulation of wet contaminants, and component aging. These faults can lead to abnormal temperature rises or uneven voltage distribution and, in severe cases, may culminate in thermal breakdown or explosive failure2.

Timely and accurate fault identification is essential for preventing such incidents, which first necessitates a clear understanding of the prevailing fault types. The most common failure arises from seal degradation, which allows moisture to penetrate the arrester chamber. For instance, aged end‑flanges and sealing rings permit water vapor to enter and form a conductive film on the valve plates. This film establishes a parallel leakage path alongside the valve plates, distorting the inter‑section voltage distribution. If unchecked, the process can evolve along a “moisture ingress → temperature rise → thermal instability” trajectory, eventually leading to complete puncture failure3,4.The insulating sleeve, subjected to prolonged combined electrical and thermal stresses, also undergoes material aging. This increases its surface conductivity and promotes the formation of longitudinal leakage channels that bypass the voltage‑withstanding function of the valve plates. In mild cases, segmented temperature differences and localized infrared hot‑spots emerge; in more severe scenarios, surface flashover or external insulation breakdown may occur5.Externally, MOAs installed outdoors readily accumulate contaminants such as dust, rain, and snow on their surfaces. This deposition markedly lowers the equivalent resistivity of the porcelain sheath6, thereby perturbing the external electric field distribution. Concurrently, capacitive coupling between the external insulation and the internal valve plates intensifies, giving rise to localized temperature elevations7. Furthermore, wet contamination exacerbates the potential difference between internal and external regions, generating excessive radial electric fields that can trigger internal partial discharges and ultimately degrade the insulation integrity of the valve plates8.

These fault types differ substantially in their underlying mechanisms, locations, and symptomatic manifestations, implying that reliable identification requires acquisition of sufficient state variables. Nevertheless, field data from faulty MOAs remain scarce, and available records often lack consistent formats, posing a significant challenge for systematic analysis. Existing research on MOA fault mechanisms and monitoring has advanced primarily along two parallel paths: experimental investigation and numerical simulation. On the experimental front, studies have struggled to obtain comprehensive and consistent current and temperature signatures across different fault scenarios. Early diagnostic approaches relied heavily on detecting increments in leakage current, yet such methods show limited sensitivity to faults like wet contamination that are unrelated to moisture ingress or aging9. Subsequent techniques based on resistive current analysis are often hampered by three‑phase coupling effects and practical measurement inaccuracies, restricting their broader engineering applicability10,11. More critically, experimental data are frequently incomplete: faults induced by insulating‑sleeve aging develop over decades, making full experimental replication impractical12; samples for moisture‑ingress faults are limited, and systematic temperature monitoring of porcelain sheaths is often lacking13; while Arup Kumar Das et al. captured infrared images of MOAs with varying degrees of pollution for fault diagnosis; however, they did not simultaneously acquire current signals14. Consequently, a unified experimental database encompassing multiple fault types with synchronized current and temperature information remains elusive. Moreover, precisely localizing faulty sections in multi‑unit MOA assemblies continues to be a technical difficulty15.Simulation‑based studies, on the other hand, have been constrained by limited measurability in relation to practical monitoring. For internal moisture ingress, Zhang Pipei et al. examined the correlation between moisture content and the voltage‑bearing ratio of valve plates, yet directly measuring the potential of individual valve plates in operating arresters remains challenging16. Research on wet contamination has largely focused on how pollution severity influences the radial electric field, with comparatively few works adopting an integrated multi‑physics modeling approach17. Regarding insulating-sleeve aging, most studies have been confined to elucidating fundamental mechanisms without establishing quantifiable indicators for fault assessment18. In the broader field of industrial health management, data-driven frameworks have demonstrated exceptional capability in handling complex data scenarios. For instance, the semi-supervised sequential variational autoencoder with self-attention mechanisms (SAM-SVAE)19 has successfully leveraged unlabeled degradation data to enhance prediction robustness, while the transferable topology-aware graph pooling network20 has effectively addressed the challenges of domain shifts under cross-domain conditions. Although He Tao et al. applied neural‑network models for online MOA condition evaluation, the underlying fault mechanisms were not thoroughly investigated21. In summary, simulation outcomes for diverse fault conditions often exhibit weak measurability and are therefore not readily transferable to field monitoring applications. Thus, a key unresolved challenge lies in leveraging simulation tools to generate well‑formatted, easily measurable current and temperature signals, and subsequently utilizing these signals to construct a robust, practically viable MOA fault identification framework.

To address the above limitations, this paper focuses on three predominant MOA fault origins: internal moisture ingress, insulating-sleeve aging, and porcelain-housing wet contamination. Initially, a 3D finite element model for the 220 kV MOA electro-thermal field is developed. In this model, precise fault reconstruction is achieved by physically simulating the specific failure mechanisms: varying the material conductivity to represent insulating-sleeve aging, mapping conductive layers with specific coverage angles and heights on the porcelain housing to model wet contamination, and attaching geometric water bands with adjustable thickness and length to the resistor discs to mimic internal moisture ingress. Using the acquired leakage current and temperature data as diagnostic indicators, a fault recognition framework is established based on the CatBoost model. This framework incorporates newly proposed statistical and geometric temperature features, with its effectiveness validated through comparative analyses against fitcnet, SVM, and XGBoost.

Simulation model and parameter configuration of a 220 kV arrester

In this section, a 3D simulation model of a 220 kV lightning arrester is constructed using finite element software (COMSOL Multiphysics, Version 6.2, https://www.comsol.com), with the specific implementation steps as follows: first, complete the construction of the 3D geometric model, then define the material property parameters of each component, subsequently select the current and solid heat transfer modules, and finally set the boundary conditions for calculation.

Three-dimensional simulation model of the arrester

The 220 kV MOA is composed of a voltage grading ring, connecting flange, base, and two identical MOA units. Each MOA unit consists of a porcelain housing, an insulating sleeve, zinc oxide resistor discs, aluminum spacers, and an epoxy resin rod. Each unit contains 32 ZnO resistor discs, with each disc having a thickness of 24 mm, an inner diameter of 15 mm, and an outer diameter of 33.5 mm. Every three resistor discs are separated by one aluminum spacer. A three-dimensional simulation model was built according to the actual dimensions of the 220 kV MOA produced by Nanyang Jinguan Industrial Co., Ltd., as shown in Fig. 1.

To ensure high-fidelity simulation results, the 3D model was discretized using a physics-controlled mesh with a Finer element size setting. The final mesh consisted of 433,843 domain elements, with a minimum element quality of 0.1261. The coupled electromagnetic-thermal equations were solved under a Frequency Domain study step using the MUMPS direct solver with an automatic Newton-Raphson algorithm. A strict convergence criterion with a relative tolerance of 0.001 was enforced to ensure numerical stability.

Fault simulation setup

Constructing a physical experimental platform presents substantial difficulties, and the required timeframes for physical simulations are excessively long. For instance, the natural aging process of an insulating sleeve typically takes decades, making it impractical to reproduce such faults experimentally in the short term. Consequently, this study currently utilizes finite element analysis (FEA) to simulate these three fault conditions. Subsequent research will look into conducting relevant physical experiments to verify the simulation findings.

This paper simulates three fault conditions: insulation sleeve aging, internal moisture ingress, and wet pollution contamination. For the insulation sleeve aging fault, disassembly cases have shown that the resistance of the insulation sleeve can drop to 0.005% of its normal value, causing the current to primarily flow through the aged insulation sleeve rather than the ZnO resistor discs12. During simulation, the conductivity of the aged insulation sleeve is set within the range of 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁶ S/m, with a step size of 4.5 × 10⁻⁷ S/m. Parametric scanning is performed on both conductivity and aging location to simulate varying degrees of insulation sleeve aging.

When the MOA’s sealing system is compromised, leading to internal moisture ingress, the hydrophobic nature of the ZnO resistor discs causes water vapor to condense and form water bands on their surfaces. These water bands create parallel conductive paths with the discs, thereby reducing the overall resistivity of the MOA16.To simulate internal moisture faults of varying severities, a parametric sweep was conducted on the geometric dimensions of the water bands. The definitions of the water strip’s geometric parameters are illustrated in Fig. 2. Specifically, the water band thickness ranges from 1.5 mm to 3.0 mm with an increment of 0.5 mm, while the height varies from 102 mm to 1020 mm with a step size of 102 mm. Based on the full combination of these parameters, a total of 40 simulation models with distinct geometric configurations were constructed.

The severity of moisture ingress directly influences the increment of the total leakage current. Consequently, the degree of moisture is categorized based on the percentage increase of the total current amplitude. The specific classification criteria are defined as follows:

Light Moisture: ΔI < 50% (current amplitude < 0.94 mA).

Moderate Moisture: 50% < Δ I < 100% (0.94 mA ≤ current amplitude < 1.25 mA).

Severe Moisture: Δ I > 100% (current amplitude ≥ 1.25 mA).

When MOAs operate outdoors, factors such as weather and environmental conditions lead to the formation of a pollution layer—primarily composed of salt and dust—on the porcelain housing surface after several years26. This paper simulates wet pollution contamination on the porcelain housing under varying severity levels by altering the location, coverage height, coverage angle, and conductivity of the wet pollution layer. The contamination height is set to 1/4 and 1/2 of the unit height. For the 1/4 unit height case, each unit is divided into 4 locations, while for the 1/2 unit height case, each unit is divided into 3 locations. The coverage angles are set to 360° and 180°. The conductivity values of the wet pollution are set to 0.0286 S/m, 0.00104 S/m, and 0.00435 S/m, respectively8.

Considering influencing factors such as power-frequency voltage fluctuations and outdoor wind speed, the operating voltage under normal conditions is set to 0.95–1.05 times the rated voltage, and the heat transfer coefficient h0 of the porcelain housing is set to 5–10 W/(m²·K)27. Analysis of electro-thermal characteristics under fault conditions

Electric field analysis

The amplitude and phase angle of the total current are important and effective characteristic quantities for judging the operating state of the MOA. Therefore, this study uses the total current as the fundamental representation for electric field analysis. After applying the rated power-frequency voltage to the established model, the total current measured from the bottom of the MOA has an amplitude of 0.626 mA and a phase angle of 84.70°.

Figure 3 illustrates the variation in the amplitude and phase angle of the total current under different degrees of insulation sleeve aging. As the aging severity increases and the sleeve conductivity rises, a more pronounced axial leakage path develops, leading to a gradual increase in the total current amplitude. Regarding the phase angle, during the initial aging stage, the leakage path is predominantly resistive. Consequently, the resistive component of the total current increases relatively, while the capacitive component remains largely unchanged, causing the current phase angle to decrease initially. However, as the sleeve conductivity further increases, the voltage across the faulty section decreases, resulting in a corresponding voltage rise across the healthy section. This shifts the dominant current component towards the capacitive current flowing through the healthy section, thereby causing the current phase angle to trend upward.

To elucidate the physical mechanism, the equivalent circuit model in Fig. 4 models the water band as an impedance branch in parallel with the lower valve discs. This parallel structure theoretically explains how moisture reduces the equivalent impedance, directly accounting for the observed surge in leakage current amplitude and phase angle shift.

As the water band height increases, the proportion of the parallel conductive path increases, leading to a decrease in the capacitive component and an increase in the resistive component. A decrease in the cross-sectional area of the water band due to reduced thickness results in a decrease in the capacitance and an increase in the resistance of the water band section, thereby increasing the resistive component. In summary, an increase in water band height and a decrease in water band thickness both cause a reduction in the phase angle of the total current.

Through the above analysis, it can be observed that the amplitude and phase angle ranges of the three types of operating conditions exhibit significant overlap. For instance, the amplitudes for both mild aging and mild wet pollution fall within the range of 0.65–0.75 mA. The phase angles for moisture ingress with a thin water band and low height almost coincide with those of wet pollution. Relying solely on the amplitude or phase angle of the total current makes it difficult to reliably distinguish between fault types, necessitating the introduction of new state variables for effective discrimination.

Thermal field analysis

MOA fault conditions often lead to an increase in resistive current, resulting in a certain degree of heating. Therefore, this paper further focuses on changes in the thermal field, and calculated temperature measurement points from bottom to top on the porcelain sleeve surface under different operating conditions are shown in Fig. 7.

Fig. 7Schematic diagram of temperature measurement points on the porcelain housing during normal operation.

Schematic diagram of temperature measurement points on the porcelain housing during normal operation.

Figure 8, using a fault in the lower section as an example, compares the surface temperature of the porcelain sleeve under different operating conditions. Under normal operation, considering the influence of capacitance to ground, the current in the upper section is greater than that in the lower section, resulting in a slightly higher temperature rise in the upper section compared to the lower section, with an overall low temperature rise for the porcelain sleeve. During insulation sleeve aging, the temperature rise in the faulty section is significantly greater than that in the normal section, exhibiting strong asymmetry between sections. For internal moisture ingress, a longitudinal high-temperature channel forms near the moisture band, leading to a continuous temperature rise zone along the band direction, and the porcelain sleeve shows a noticeable temperature increase. Finally, for wet pollution contamination, the wet pollution on the porcelain sleeve surface alters the uniformity of the electric field distribution, thereby changing the temperature distribution of the porcelain sleeve. When a lower section fault occurs, the voltage sharing in the normal section increases, so the temperature rise in the normal section is greater than that in the faulty section, with a relatively small overall temperature rise for the porcelain sleeve.

CatBoost clustering algorithm for MOA fault condition diagnosis

This paper addresses MOA fault diagnosis by constructing a diagnostic system framework based on the CatBoost clustering algorithm, with the specific steps illustrated in Fig. 12. First, the collected data samples undergo preprocessing: the phase angle of the total current for each sample is converted from degrees to radians, and the temperature measurement points on the MOA porcelain sleeve are divided into two groups according to the sections. Subsequently, features are extracted from the porcelain sleeve temperatures, which can be categorized into temperature statistical features and temperature geometric features. These extracted features, along with the original data, are then input into the CatBoost model for training. The reliability and accuracy of the model are evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation. Finally, a hold-out dataset not involved in the training process is employed as a test set to validate the model and evaluate its generalization capability for MOA fault diagnosis.

All data processing and machine learning modeling, were implemented in Python (Version 3.13.7, https://www.python.org). The CatBoost classification model was constructed utilizing the catboost package (Version 1.2.8, https://catboost.ai), while model evaluation and stratified K-fold cross-validation were performed using the scikit-learn library (Version 1.7.2, https://scikit-learn.org).

Parameter tuning and optimization

To balance overall accuracy and balanced performance across all categories, this paper adopts CatBoost as the final classification model within a multi-class framework. The evaluation metrics employed are accuracy (Acc) and macro-average F1-score. During the training process, an early stopping strategy based on the number of iterations is implemented—training halts if the loss function shows no significant improvement over 50 consecutive iterations28.

Optimization of model hyperparameters followed a two-stage strategy progressing from cross-validation to full-dataset training. Initially, a conservative parameter range was evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation to ensure consistent performance across folds. Subsequently, tree depth was moderately increased while the number of trees was slightly reduced during full training set optimization, enhancing the model’s expressive power while mitigating overfitting risks. The key hyperparameters ultimately adopted are listed in Table 6. Specifically, the learning rate was fixed at 0.05, the L2 regularization coefficient was set to 3.0, and the random seed was standardized at 42 to ensure reproducibility.

Conclusion

This study systematically established simulation models for four representative operating conditions of MOA: normal operation, insulation sleeve aging, internal moisture ingress, and porcelain sleeve wet contamination. The electro-thermal behavior under each condition was comprehensively analyzed. Electrically, the amplitude of the total leakage current was found to increase with aging severity, moisture height, and contamination coverage. However, significant signal overlap was observed among normal, lightly moistened, and contaminated states, indicating the limitation of relying solely on current-based features for accurate classification. Thermally, distinct spatial patterns were identified in the temperature distribution along the porcelain sleeve. Specifically, insulation aging and internal moisture ingress led to a higher temperature rise in the faulty section compared to the healthy section, whereas wet contamination resulted in the opposite thermal signature, providing a clear basis for fault discrimination.

Leveraging these insights, a feature set combining statistical and geometric attributes of temperature profiles was constructed. A CatBoost-based fault diagnosis model was developed, which achieved an average accuracy of 99.3%. Comparative results demonstrate that the proposed model outperforms baselines, including XGBoost, fitcnet, and SVM. Furthermore, it achieved perfect 100% accuracy on a separate test set, demonstrating robust performance and strong generalization ability.

Looking forward, future research could focus on integrating real-time environmental data, such as ambient temperature and humidity, into the fault diagnosis framework to enhance model adaptability under varying operational conditions.
