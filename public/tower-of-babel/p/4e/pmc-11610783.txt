N-acetyl Cysteine to Mitigate Liver Damage in Patients with Deranged Liver Function Undergoing Surgery on Cardiopulmonary Bypass
Authors: Mukul Chandra Kapoor
Published: 2024

Patients with mitral and aortic valve disease usually have right-sided pressure/volume overload due to reduced forward flow. Reduced forward flow leads to right heart failure and, consequently, congestive heart failure (CHF). Central venous congestion develops in multiple organ systems, and the congestion in the liver leads to hepatic dysfunction. High central venous pressure and increased hepatic vein pressure decrease portal venous inflow.[1] Congestion reduces hepatic blood flow, increases venous pressure, and lowers oxygen saturation to cause liver parenchymal injury. Exudation into the space of Disse impairs oxygen and nutrient diffusion to hepatocytes.[2]

The elevated central venous in CHF causes hepatic venous system congestion. Low cardiac output impairs liver perfusion, leading to hepatic dysfunction.[3] Patients develop signs of congestion, raised central venous pressure, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, hypoperfusion, and low cardiac output. The terms “cardio-hepatic syndrome” and “cardiac hepatopathy” have been proposed for this interaction between the heart and the liver.[4] Patients for valvular surgery commonly have tricuspid regurgitation (TR) from volume/pressure overload caused by valvular heart disease and the effects on the pulmonary circulation. Severe TR is associated with laboratory changes in gamma-glutamyl transferase and bilirubin due to congestive cardiac hepatopathy.[5] Hepatic dysfunction increases surgical morbidity and mortality.[6]

In this issue, Ram Kiran et al.[7] present a randomized controlled trial (RCT) showing a lower degree of hepatic dysfunction after double valve replacement (DVR) +/—tricuspid valve repair in patients with mitral and aortic valve disease. The control group received standard therapy, while the trial group received n-acetyl cysteine prophylactically. They report a smaller rise in bilirubin, serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase, and serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase in the patients who received n-acetyl cysteine as prophylaxis. The difference from the control was statistically significant.

A transient derangement of liver function is seen after cardiac surgery. Intraoperative factors aggravating this liver dysfunction include CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, and operation time.[8] About 10% of patients exposed to cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) have hepatic injury, while about 4% have severe liver dysfunction.[9] The average CPB time for a double valve replacement (DVR) is about 2 hours (although it varies with surgeon experience, patient disease, and the center’s capability). A long exposure to extracorporeal circulation of patients with pre-existing hepatic dysfunction aggravates the dysfunction.

Open heart surgery compromises the liver’s homeostatic milieu as CPB imposes physiological, immunological, and metabolic demands.[10] This compromised milieu results from congestion, decreased perfusion, and hemolysis, which manifests as an elevation of all liver function parameters, including bilirubin, serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase, serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase, and alkaline phosphatase.[11] Administration of n-acetyl cysteine perioperatively may attenuate the physiologic response and reduce post-CPB complications as it is a free radical scavenger.[12] Prophylactic intravenous n-acetyl cysteine therapy has also been shown to be associated with significantly lower levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), a marker of systemic inflammation, and a lower incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation after CABG surgery.[13] Similar benefits of reduced inflammation after cardiac surgery have been reported by other studies.[1415]

N-acetyl cysteine is used to limit hepatic injury during liver transplantation and has shown benefit in paracetamol-related liver dysfunction and multiple other conditions. It stimulates glutathione biosynthesis by providing cysteine; thus, its use increases glutathione levels. It is a major antioxidant, and its benefits are attributed to this property.[16] Oxidative stress is one of the pathogenetic mechanisms causing liver damage. Data suggests that n-acetyl cysteine can protect against hepatic injury by limiting the effects of oxidative stress and inflammation.[17] In chronic liver disease, excessive oxidant formation leads to disruption of redox signaling and molecular damage. Nitric oxide contributes to liver ischemia-reperfusion injury. N-acetyl cysteine modulates the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase in hepatocytes stimulated by proinflammatory cytokines. N-acetyl cysteine is a potential treatment option for acute and chronic liver diseases.[1819]

The anesthetic management of patients with hepatic dysfunction can be a clinical challenge. Intraoperative management of patients with coexisting liver derangement undergoing major cardiac surgery on CPB includes optimizing splanchnic perfusion and preventing further hepatic decompensation. Catecholamine release on initiation of CPB decreases hepatic perfusion by 20% and hepatic arterial flow by up to 45%.[11] The benefits of n-acetyl cysteine in mitigating liver dysfunction in noncardiac surgery are relatively well established. More RCTs must be performed to establish the benefit of prophylactic use of n-acetyl cysteine in patients with liver dysfunction undergoing surgery on CPB.
