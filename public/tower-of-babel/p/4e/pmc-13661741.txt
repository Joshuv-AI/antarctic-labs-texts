Comparison of traditional and new treatments for fibrous dysplasia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Lucie Levaillant, Guillaume Mabilleau, Adèle Malagié, Sixtine Donat, Régis Coutant, Patrice Rodien, Claire Briet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFibrous dysplasia, alone or associated with McCune-Albright syndrome, is a rare bone disorder that can cause pain, deformity, fractures, and thus affect quality of life. Various treatments have been tried, starting with bisphosphonate administration, and now targeted therapies are being developed. Due to the rarity of the disease, there are only a few randomised clinical trials, making it difficult to understand the efficacy of each treatment. The aim of this systematic review is to provide an overview of what has been done in the past and what has been published recently on new targeted therapies.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE and Web of Science up to February 3, 2026 for all oral, subcutaneous or intravenous therapies in patients with fibrous dysplasia. Data extraction and analysis followed PRISMA guidelines. The review was registered on PROSPERO, no. 42024602268.ResultsFifty-six studies were included, most focusing on nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, followed by new targeted therapies, mainly denosumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting RANKL. Overall, almost three-quarters of the studies showed a reduction in lesion size or activity, with only a few studies quantifying this reduction by various means (X-rays, CT, MRI, or Na[18F]F PET-CT). Almost all showed a reduction in circulating bone markers (n = 45/47), with a weighted mean reduction of 31 ± 18% in alkaline phosphatase (n = 30). The new targeted therapies looked promising, in particular denosumab showing a 66 ± 15% weighted mean reduction in fibrous dysplasia lesion activity on Na[18F]F PET-CT, but some patients have experienced serious side effects, which need to be better understood in order to prevent them more effectively.ConclusionDenosumab has shown promising results in recent years in terms of reducing the size and activity of fibrous dysplasia lesions and improving bone markers. Further research is needed to better prevent the serious side effects sometimes associated with this treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-07918-6.Keywords: Fibrous dysplasia, Denosumab, Burosumab, Treatment, Efficacy, Safety, McCune-Albright syndrome

Introduction

Fibrous dysplasia/McCune-Albright Syndrome (FD/MAS) is a rare disease with an incidence that is difficult to estimate, thought to be around 3.6 per 1,000,000 person-years in 2015–2018 in the Danish population [1]. Fibrous dysplasia (FD), with or without McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS), is characterised by the replacement of haematopoietic and adipose bone marrow by fibrous stroma under the influence of Gsα activation, which induces an increase in cAMP levels in mutated mesenchymal stem cells derived from FD lesions [2]. The proliferation of this abnormal stroma leads to the formation and expansion of FD lesions, which can cause pain, deformity, fractures, and sometimes neurosensory complications (through nerve compression of the optic and auditory nerves, in the case of cranial involvement). The impact of FD varies greatly depending on the patient, ranging from minor in cases where a monostotic lesion is discovered incidentally, to significant, affecting both function and psychological well-being, as well as overall quality of life [3]. Histologically, FD lesions are characterized by irregular, branching, and anastomosing trabeculae of woven bone, often exhibiting the typical C- or S-shaped configurations, without conspicuous osteoblastic rimming. These trabeculae are embedded within a fibrotic marrow stroma composed of unmineralized collagenous matrix [4]. Higher magnification reveals abnormal accumulation of thick osteoid seams, indicative of defective bone mineralization [5]. Extensive areas of the trabeculae in direct contact with the fibrous stroma show signs of active osteolysis, whereas smaller regions display early bone formation. This juxtaposition suggests recurrent cycles of impaired bone growth and excessive matrix deposition, accompanied by unrestrained osteoclast activity [6]. Abnormal vascularisation is also another hallmark of FD lesions [7]. Although the pathophysiology is complex and yet poorly understood, the disruption of bone remodelling is believed to be primarily responsible for the histopathological remnants of trabecular bone in the dysplastic fibrous stroma [6].

Various treatments have been used to control FD lesions. Bisphosphonate administrations have been used for pain for many years, but their efficacy in reducing the size or progression of FD lesions has not been proven [8]. New targeted therapies have been investigated in patients with FD with the aim of limiting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 (IL-6) receptor inhibitor, acts on a signalling pathway that promotes osteoclastogenesis under inflammatory conditions. Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody against receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL), has also been employed to suppress osteoclastic activity and reduce lesion progression in FD. FD lesions are also characterized by elevated production of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), which drives renal phosphate wasting. Hypophosphatemia resulting from excessive FGF23 secretion is a key determinant of disease severity and is associated with an increased risk of fractures, even in skeletal regions not directly affected by dysplastic bone. Notably, the use of anti-resorptive agents such as bisphosphonates and denosumab is contraindicated in patients with hypophosphatemia, due to the risk of further impairing bone mineralization, which complicates the clinical management of FD. In this context, Burosumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting FGF23, has also been investigated as a therapeutic approach in FD.

These new targeted therapies have renewed hope of successfully controlling the most severe forms of FD, as shown by published case reports with promising responses. However, the rarity of the disease makes randomised clinical trials difficult to carry out, with efficacy difficult to assess. The main aim of this study is to compare all oral or intravenous aetiological treatments used in patients with fibrous dysplasia, firstly in terms of reduction in lesion size, but also in terms of reduction in bone markers and in bone fractures, qualitative improvement in mobility and pain, and occurrence of adverse events.

Methods

A systematic review in line with PRISMA guidelines was undertaken to compare all oral, subcutaneous or intravenous aetiological treatments in patients with fibrous dysplasia [9]. The review was registered a priori on PROSPERO, no. 42024602268. All original articles were included, including case reports, observational studies, non-randomised interventional studies and randomised controlled trials.

Search strategy

The search was carried out on 3 February 2026, in MEDLINE and Web of Science, with the queries indicated in Supplementary Table 1.

Screening of literature

Data selection and extraction were carried out independently by two people (AM, SD). In case of disagreement, another researcher was involved (LL). All extracted data was then verified (LL). The literature was first reviewed based on the title and abstract against inclusion and exclusion criteria. The remaining full-text articles were assessed for final inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were all studies of patients receiving oral, subcutaneous or intravenous treatments for fibrous dysplasia. Exclusion criteria were systematic reviews, studies published in languages other than English or French, and studies of non-etiological treatments (such as analgesics).

Outcomes analysed

The primary outcome was the percentage reduction in lesion size or activity in the different fibrous dysplasia treatments. This is a radiological outcome, with a reduction in the size of the lesion observable on X-ray, CT or MRI, and a reduction in the activity of the lesion observable on Na[18F]F PET-CT. The secondary outcomes were the reduction in bone markers between the different treatments for fibrous dysplasia, the reduction in bone fractures, the qualitative improvement in mobility and pain, and the occurrence of adverse events.

Data collection process

The following data have been extracted from the included studies:

- First author, publication year, title of the study, publication type.

- Sex, age, type of disease (McCune-Albright syndrome, hypophosphatemia).

- Type of medication.

- Percentage reduction in lesion size assessed by each imaging technique, and time after treatment.

- Percentage reduction in each bone marker, and time after treatment.

- Percentage of patients reported to have had bone fractures before and after the start of treatment (self-reported).

- Qualitative improvement in mobility.

- Qualitative improvement in pain.

- Occurrence of adverse events, including serious adverse events.

Quality evaluation

The quality of the included studies was critically appraised using JBI for case reports and observational studies [10], and SIGN for randomised controlled trials (RCTs) [11]. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-E for observational studies[12], ROBINS-I V2 for non-randomised interventional studies [13], and the Cochrane RoB tool version 2 for randomised controlled trials [14]. The quality of evidence was assessed using GRADE for observational studies, non-randomised interventional studies, and randomised controlled trials[15]. These scores are composed of closed-ended questions, designed to help focus on the essential concepts of the study evaluation. The quality of the study was then judged as “good”, “fair”, or “poor” based on the answers to the questions. Where opinions differed, a consensus was reached after discussion with another researcher (LL).

Data synthesis

All-quality data are summarised. Descriptive tools are used such as means and percentages. All means shown are weighted means.

Results

One hundred and fifty-nine articles were identified in the Medline database and 209 in the Web of Science database. After eliminating duplicates, 238 articles were retained (Fig. 1). After exclusion based on title, abstract, or full text, or due to unavailability of full text, 57 studies were included (Table 1) [16–72]. All included studies were given an overall quality rating of good (Supplementary Table 2). No studies were excluded for quality reasons.

Of the 57 articles included, 34 were case reports, 20 observational studies, two non-randomized interventional study, and only two randomized clinical trials. The most commonly studied drugs were intravenous bisphosphonates reported in 22 studies, followed by denosumab in more recent years, in 16 studies. Previous bisphosphonate use was reported in almost all patients receiving denosumab, burosumab, or tocilizumab (96%, 88%, and 100%, respectively), but not in the patient treated with calcitonin. Only 6 studies used burosumab and 1 used tocilizumab, calcitonin or imatinib. Overall, all the studies included 542 patients: 409 patients were treated with bisphosphonates, 99 with denosumab, 16 with tocilizumab, 17 with burosumab, 1 with calcitonin and 1 with imatinib. With regard to bisphosphonate treatment, 193 patients were treated intravenously (zoledronate dose between 0.025 mg/kg and 5 mg, every 3 to 6 months) and 45 orally. When described (n = 56), these studies included 78% women, with a weighted mean age at treatment of 30 ± 12 years. In 21 studies (38%), the mean age at the time of treatment was under 18. Of these, 166 patients (47%) had McCune-Albright syndrome (reported in 39 studies), and 94 (32%) had hypophosphatemia (reported in 25 studies).

The population differed between the different drugs. The 17 patients treated with burosumab naturally had at least one hypophosphatemia, and most of them were children with McCune-Albright syndrome, whereas the patients included in the tocilizumab trial were the oldest, with an average age of 50 and included 31% of patients with McCune-Albright syndrome (Table 2).

Imaging

Of the 34 studies that investigated reduction in lesion size and/or activity by imaging, 74% found a reduction. The vast majority assessed lesion size by radiography (n = 25), followed by CT (n = 5), Na[18F]F PET-CT (n = 5) and MRI (n = 3). Only three studies quantified the reduction in the lesion, after denosumab treatment, by Na[18F]F PET-CT (n = 2) and MRI, CT and radiography (n = 1 each). One study of two patients showed a 23% reduction in lesion size on CT and 9% on X-ray, and a reduction in lesion activity of 37% on Na[18F]F PET-CT (Fig. 2A). Another study found, in 8 patients treated by denosumab, a 73% reduction in lesion activity on Na[18F]F PET-CT in 8 patients. The final study of one patient treated by denosumab showed a 32% reduction in lesion size on MRI.

Bone mineral density (BMD) has been assessed primarily in studies with bisphosphonates (n = 17/19), and also in denosumab and burosumab studies (n = 1 each). When reported, an increase in BMD (n = 17/19) was found, with a weighted mean increase of 17% ± 20 after a weighted mean of 30 months ± 12 of bisphosphonate treatment (n = 8). In addition, BMD outcomes were reported in one study for denosumab and in one study for burosumab, with observed increases of 9.9% after 19 months of treatment and 6.1% after 24 months, respectively.

Bone markers

Almost all studies, regardless of the pharmacological treatment used, reported a reduction in bone markers (n = 45/47). The vast majority of studies reported a decrease in alkaline phosphatase (n = 34/36), with a weighted mean reduction of 31 ± 18% (n = 30) after 15 ± 14 months of treatment (n = 26). When analyzed by drug class, bisphosphonates reduced alkaline phosphatase levels, with a weighted average reduction of 28 ± 12% (n = 15), whereas the weighted average reduction were of 33 ± 18% for denosumab (n = 8), 51 ± 6% for burosumab (n = 6) and 35% for calcitonin (n = 1) (Fig. 2B). There was no decrease for tocilizumab. More recently, six studies with denosumab assessed procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP), with a weighted mean reduction of 67 ± 18% (n = 6), after 12 ± 4 months' treatment (n = 5).

Sixteen studies, performed with intravenous bisphosphonate, denosumab or tocilizumab, investigated C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen (CTX), which also decreased in almost all cases (n = 16/18), with a weighted mean reduction of 48 ± 18% (n = 14) after 10 ± 11 months of treatment (n = 12). When analyzed by drug class, there was a weighted average reduction of 41 ± 10% with bisphosphonates (n = 7) at a weighted average of 8 ± 14 months of treatment, whereas the weighted average reduction was of 58 ± 22% for denosumab at a weighted average of 12 ± 4 months of treatment (n = 7). Six studies with oral or intravenous bisphosphonate reported a weighted mean reduction of N-terminal telopeptide (NTX) of 29 ± 13% after 25 ± 12 months of treatment.

Bone fractures, pain and mobility

Fifty-nine percent of patients had fractures before the treatment (n = 94/159 patients). Only 12 studies described the number of patients having fractures before and after treatment (self-reported): with a weighted percentage of 57% of patients having fractures before the treatment evaluated, and 54% having new fractures after the treatment (self-reported), but with great disparities between studies (all patients had fractures after treatment in 4 studies, whereas 4 studies showed no fractures after treatment). Also, 96% of the studies reported a qualitative improvement in pain (n = 45/47), and 88% reported a qualitative improvement in mobility (n = 14/16).

Occurrence of adverse events according to type of drug

Thirty-one studies reported side effects. Most of these were mild or moderate and were reported in 30 studies. Only 5 studies reported serious adverse events, all related to denosumab, representing 7% of all patients reported (n = 7/99): 4 patients developed severe hypercalcemia on discontinuation of treatment, 1 patient developed osteonecrosis of the jaw, 1 patient reported oral blisters. The last patient developed secondary hyperparathyroidism and hypophosphatemia under denosumab treatment.

Discussion

In this review of pharmacological intervention used in FD/MAS, which includes 49 studies, most of them focus on intravenous bisphosphonates, followed by the new targeted therapies, primarily denosumab. Overall, almost three quarters of studies showed a reduction in lesion size or activity on imaging, with only a few studies quantifying this reduction by various radiological methods (X-rays, CT, MRI or Na[18F]F PET-CT). They also almost all showed a reduction in bone markers (n = 45/47), with a weighted mean reduction of 31 ± 18% in ALP (n = 30). In terms of results according to type of drug used, the new targeted therapies seem promising, in particular the reduction in lesion activity obtained with denosumab, with a weighted mean reduction of 66 ± 15% on Na[18F]F PET-CT. The same is true for bone markers, with a reduction in ALP with denosumab as great as with bisphosphonates, and even greater with burosumab, with a mean reduction of 51 ± 6% when the patient had associated hypophosphatemia. However, relatively frequent serious side effects have been reported with denosumab, mainly severe hypercalcemia several weeks after treatment discontinuation.

These serious side effects are the main concern regarding denosumab. Recent research has identified the reasons for this phenomenon: denosumab is an anti-resorptive agent that targets RANKL to prevent its binding to the RANK receptor, thereby inhibiting osteoclast differentiation. This is, however, a temporary effect: studies in mice have shown that these osteoclasts can differentiate several weeks after withdrawal (around 6 weeks in mice), and resorb a lot of bone, leading to an increase in tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase, followed by usual bone markers, and finally hypercalcemia [73]. Recent study in a mouse model have shown that sequential and multiple treatment with zoledronate upon discontinuation of denosumab can limit this phenomenon [74]. It can therefore be imagined to add new therapies to control this phenomenon, such as sequential treatment when denosumab is stopped, and perhaps as neoadjuvant therapy (for example, an injection of bisphosphonate before the introduction of denosumab). Denosumab has been approved as a treatment for osteoporosis for 15 years, as it has been shown to reduce bone markers, increase BMD after treatment and reduce fractures [75]. However, some studies have also shown the difficulties associated with discontinuing treatment, as this leads to excessive bone resorption, resulting in a rebound in bone markers upon discontinuation, a decrease in BMD, and an increased risk of new vertebral fractures in these osteoporotic patients in the absence of post-denosumab treatment [76]. Currently, almost 100 patients with FD are reported in the literature, but none of these studies have described worsening of FD lesions upon discontinuation of treatment or an increase in fractures after discontinuation. However, BMD has only been reported during treatment in one patient [53], and only one study reported fractures in 3/13 patients undergoing treatment (without describing the fracture rate before treatment) [62]. Future studies should describe BMD and systematically report fracture rates before, during, and after treatment in order to assess these risks. However, none of the therapies reported in this systematic review target the pathophysiological defect of this disease, i.e., the increase in cAMP in certain bone marrow stromal cells. The need for new therapies is therefore still relevant, and other therapies could improve fibrous dysplasia with, hopefully, fewer side effects, such as molecules that can specifically inhibit cells with a gain of mutated Gsα function or gene therapy [77].

Of note, most of the included studies were case reports or observational studies, with only two clinical trials (4%), due to the rarity of the disease and the difficulty of carrying out a clinical trial. Clearly, case reports and observational studies have many missing data and do not assess all patients in the same way. Also, many studies provide a qualitative assessment of lesion size and/or activity under treatment, but few quantify this reduction, with only three studies quantifying it by different means (X-ray, CT, MRI or Na[18F]F PET-CT). This leads to patients being managed primarily based on expert opinion. FD lesions were primarily assessed at diagnosis using bone scintigraphy with 99mTc-labelled bisphosphonate, which allowed the FD skeletal burden score to be calculated. However, its use during follow-up was controversial and showed no improvement after bisphosphonate administration in a study [78]. Na[18F]F PET-CT has only recently been used in non-cancerous diseases, as it is becoming more accessible and less expensive. This interesting tool has shown a strong correlation between volume-based parameters (such as Fluoride Tumor Volume (FTV)) and bone markers (such as ALP, N-telopeptides, and P1NP), providing new quantitative data for assessing disease burden [58]. Systematic quantitative assessment of lesion size would be beneficial for improving evidence-based decisions in fibrous dysplasia. Several trials are underway, one open-label with burosumab, 2 with denosumab (one open-label (NCT05419050), the other placebo-controlled (NCT05966064)), in adults and children. These three studies will effectively quantify lesion size and/or activity by Na[18F]F PET-CT and provide the expected evidence-based data.

This study has certain limitations: we did not report cases with local treatments such as embolization of the lesion, as we chose to focus on systemic treatment. Moreover, each drug regimen was different from one study to another, with different dosages and different intervals between doses. Similarly, the time between treatment and assessment also varied considerably between studies. We have chosen not to report on these differences, but they probably contribute to the variation in results between studies.

In conclusion, the new targeted therapies have shown promising results, whether in reducing lesion activity in fibrous dysplasia for denosumab, or in improving bone markers for denosumab and burosumab. Further research is needed to improve tolerance and acceptability of treatment. Finally, new therapies should be sought, such as drugs that specifically inhibit constitutively activated Gsα.
