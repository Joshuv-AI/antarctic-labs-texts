Image navigator–based, automated coronary magnetic resonance angiography for the detection of coronary artery stenosis
Authors: Gregory Wood, Reza Hajhosseiny, Alexandra Uglebjerg Pedersen, Simon Littlewood, Tina Juul Hansen, Radhouene Neji, Karl P Kunze, Jens Wetzl, Bjarne Linde Nørgaard, Jesper Møller Jensen, Michael Maeng, Per Lav Madsen, Niels Vejlstrup, Claudia Prieto, René M Botnar, Won Yong Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is recommended as the first-line diagnostic imaging modality in low-to-intermediate-risk individuals suspected of stable coronary artery disease (CAD). However, CCTA exposes patients to ionizing radiation and potentially nephrotoxic contrast agents. Invasive coronary angiography is the gold-standard investigation to guide coronary revascularisation strategy; however, invasive procedures incur an inherent risk to the patient. Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA) avoids these issues. Nevertheless, clinical implementation is currently limited due to extended scanning durations, inconsistent image quality, and consequent lack of diagnostic accuracy. Several technical CMRA innovations, including advanced respiratory motion correction with 100% scan efficiency (no data rejection), fast image acquisition with motion-corrected undersampled image reconstruction, and deep-learning–based automated planning, have been implemented and now await clinical validation in multi-center trials.MethodsThe objective of the image navigator–based, automated CMRA prospective multi-center study is to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of a newly developed, state-of-the-art, standardized, and automated CMRA framework compared to CCTA in 201 patients undergoing clinical investigation for CAD. The study protocol mandates the administration of oral beta-blockers to decrease heart rate to below 60 bpm and the use of sublingual nitroglycerine spray to induce vasodilation. Additionally, the study incorporates the utilization of standardized postprocessing with sliding-thin-slab multiplanar reformatting, in combination with evaluation of the source images, to optimize the visualization of coronary artery stenosis.DiscussionIf proven effective, CMRA could provide a non-invasive, needle-free, yet also clinically viable, alternative to CCTA.Trial RegistrationThis study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05473117).Keywords: Coronary magnetic resonance angiography, Coronary artery disease

Background

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide and atherosclerotic coronary artery disease (CAD) is the main contributor to cardiovascular disease [1]. As CAD is a disease that can largely be effectively treated through lifestyle changes and with targeted medical treatment, identifying those individuals with the disease who require treatment before serious complications occur is of importance [2], [3].

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is recommended as a first-line diagnostic imaging modality in symptomatic individuals with possible chronic coronary syndrome [2], [3]. However, CCTA is limited by exposure to ionizing radiation and the need for contrast agents, which hampers repeated monitoring scans beyond the initial assessment of CAD. Furthermore, the presence of coronary calcification may be associated with low diagnostic specificity of CCTA [4], [5]. Invasive coronary angiography (ICA) combined with the measurement of fractional flow reserve (FFR) is considered the gold standard for decision-making as to whether to perform coronary revascularisation in patients with stable CAD. However, as an invasive procedure, these tests may be associated with increased patient discomfort and inherently carry a small but significant risk of complications to the patient.

Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA) is an alternative for coronary atherosclerosis imaging which is non-invasive, and free of ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast agents. The potential of CMRA has previously been demonstrated in multiple single-center trials [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11] and very few multi-center trials [12], [13]. However, clinical translation of CMRA remains hitherto very limited due to prohibitively long scan times needed to achieve the required high-spatial resolution currently achievable with CCTA [14], image degradation due to cardiac and respiratory motion, and the lack of dedicated postprocessing and analysis software. Furthermore, standardized protocols for CMRA are lacking, which limits the application of CMRA to very few specialized research sites. Several technical innovations have been implemented in recent years in an attempt to combat some of these issues.

One method to overcome this hurdle is the “free running” acquisition approach, which samples and uses all data irrespective of the cardiac and respiratory motion states, with separation of these during image reconstruction [15], [16]. While this method has numerous benefits that have been shown in multiple publications, it has not yet been widely distributed and tested clinically because it requires high-end computing, long reconstruction times, and often the use of contrast agents.

An alternative approach employs image navigator (iNav)–based respiratory motion correction without the need for data rejection (100% respiratory scan efficiency) combined with faster image acquisition and non–rigid motion-compensated image reconstruction with patch–based low-rank denoising [17], which now await clinical validation in multi-center trials [18]. In two single-center studies, we have recently demonstrated the potential of a novel sub-millimeter CMRA framework yielding high negative predictive value (NPV) and moderate positive predictive value (PPV) in comparison to CCTA and ICA in 50 and 45 patients, respectively [19], [20]. To further facilitate clinical implementation of CMRA and reduce the degree of user input required, automatic deep-learning (DL)–based scan planning including three-dimensional (3D) volume and iNav placement as well as patient-specific determination of the cardiac acquisition window has been developed [21], [22]. This novel sub-millimeter CMRA framework in combination with automated DL–based scan planning is referred to as iNav-AUTO CMRA (image navigator–based, automated coronary magnetic resonance angiography). Table 1 provides an overview of the key technical improvements with the iNav-AUTO CMRA framework compared to the two previous multi-center trials of CMRA which utilized 1.5T scanners and non–contrast-enhanced sequences. Finally, clinical implementation of CMRA requires systematic and standardized reporting for assessing CAD. The Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System (CAD-RADS) has been created for CCTA to ease and standardize the grading of CAD severity [23]. However, this has yet to be implemented when assessing CMRA images.

Methods

Overall study design

This study is a prospective, international multi-center study with the purpose of evaluating the accuracy of the iNav-AUTO CMRA to detect and rule out significant obstructive CAD defined by CCTA as the non-invasive reference standard. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05473117). Patients ≥18 years of age with symptoms suggestive of stable angina referred to CCTA for assessment of suspected CAD may be enrolled. Further inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Table 2. Five investigative sites in Denmark (n = 3) and the United Kingdom (n = 2) will participate in the study. A total of 201 subjects will be entered into the study, with no single center permitted to enroll >50% of the total number of subjects.

Patient recruitment

At the time of referral to CCTA, patients will be approached inviting them to participate in the study. An appointment on the same day as the CCTA will then be provided for the CMRA scan, if possible. If this is not possible, CMRA will be performed within 4 weeks of CCTA. Recruitment is planned to commence in mid-2024 and is expected to take 12−18 months. A study timeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Main outcome and measures

The primary endpoint is defined as a per-patient NPV >80% by CMRA compared to CCTA for the detection of CAD at a threshold of CAD-RADS ≥4.

In patients referred to ICA, assessment of the per-patient and per-vessel diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, NPV, and PPV in detecting coronary diameter stenosis by CMR and CCTA against quantitative ICA using a stenosis threshold ≥50% will be determined.

To further enhance image quality for future studies, other measures include quantitative [24] and qualitative assessment of CMRA image quality, as well as identifying clinical variables, such as body mass index (BMI), heart rate, and sex, that may affect CMRA image quality. Also, assessment of inter-reader agreement and evaluation of CMRA image quality across various sites will be conducted.

. Patient preparation

In accordance with standard clinical practice, patients will be instructed to abstain from all substances containing caffeine and refrain from smoking on the day of the CMRA examination. Patients will be required to have a heart rate of <60 bpm to obtain the optimal CMRA images [25]. In the event of resting, heart rate >60 bpm registered on a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) 50–100 mg of atenolol will be administered 2 h before scanning unless contraindicated. To ensure heart rate stability, patients with a resting heart rate of 50–60 bpm will be premedicated with 25 mg of atenolol 2 h before scanning. Individuals who have contraindications to atenolol will be excluded from the study (Table 2). If on attendance for CMRA pulse rate is >75 bpm, then participants will be administered intravenous metoprolol in 5 mg doses up to a maximum dose of 20 mg. This will be administered with medical supervision. If the pulse rate fails to fall below 75 bpm following oral and/or intravenous beta-blocker, patients will be excluded from the study, as there will not be a sufficient acquisition window to facilitate CMRA [12]. All patients will receive 0.8 mg of sublingual nitroglycerine to dilate the coronary arteries for better visualization immediately before scanning.

CMRA acquisition

The automated, DL-derived planning of CMRA acquisition has previously been described [17]. All CMRA scans will be performed on a 1.5T clinical MR scanner (MAGNETOM Sola or Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a 32-channel cardiac coil and an 18-channel body coil. A three-lead vector ECG will be used for cardiac synchronization.

Following an initial localizer scan to locate the heart within the thoracic cavity, a research implementation of a DL-based approach will automatically place the imaging slab and field of view [21]. Furthermore, the 2D iNav will be automatically placed around the left ventricle of the heart (Fig. 2). A free-breathing four-chamber cine scan will then be performed, and an automated tracking of the right coronary artery will determine the mid-diastolic rest period and the end-systolic rest period, which will then adapt the acquisition window length and shift the onset of the acquisition window to the beginning of the detected resting phase in the 3D CMRA scan (Fig. 3). Operators will be able to make manual adjustment of the scan planning if they consider it necessary as well as repeat each step of acquisition planning in cases of an unsuccessful first attempt. All participants will have two acquisitions performed, once using the diastolic rest period and once the systolic, as acquisition in both cardiac phases has been demonstrated to improve overall diagnostic accuracy [26].

Automated high-resolution CMRA image acquisition and reconstruction

A high-resolution CMRA (iNav-AUTO CMRA) will be performed using the novel research sequence previously described [17]. In brief, an undersampled free-breathing 3D whole-heart ECG-triggered, balanced steady-state free precision sequence with a 3D variable density spiral-like Cartesian trajectory with golden-angle rotation will be used with a scan resolution of 0.9 mm3. To reduce respiratory motion artifacts, a 2D iNav is used allowing for 100% respiratory scan efficiency and 2D translational motion correction of the heart on a beat-to-beat basis and 3D non–rigid motion correction on a bin-to-bin basis [27]. Image contrast is improved by applying two different pre-pulses. A spectrally selective spectral presaturation with inversion recovery fat saturation pulse with a constant flip angle of 130° to improve coronary depiction by minimizing fat-related aliasing artifacts will be used. An adiabatic T2 preparation pulse (duration = 40 ms) [28] in each heartbeat will be used to enhance the contrast between blood and cardiac muscle, thereby avoiding the use of extracellular contrast agents.

The image reconstruction framework consists of three different steps (i) beat-to-beat respiratory binning and intra-bin translational motion correction using iNav; (ii) bin-to-bin 3D non–rigid motion estimation; and (iii) non–rigid motion-corrected 3D patch–based low-rank reconstruction [29]. Image reconstruction will be performed inline and will take around 3–4 min per CMRA acquisition. A comparison of the CMRA protocol with CCTA is illustrated in Table 3.

CCTA protocol

The CCTA scan will be performed using a second- or third-generation dual-source computed tomography (CT) technology according to best practice societal guidelines [14] and utilized in a previous multi-center study of CCTA [30]. Acquisition will preferably use a step-and-shoot prospectively gated pulsing window set to 65%–75% of the R-R cycle with a rotation time of 250–280 ms. Automatic tube current modulation and tube voltage selection will beused to adjust radiation exposure according to patient size. Images were reconstructed at a maximum field of view of 200 mm, a matrix of 512 × 512 pixels, and slice thickness/interval of 0.75/0.7 mm. In case of high levels of coronary calcification, a minimum tube voltage of 120 kVp to reduce calcium blooming recommended. Contrast volume will be determined according to patient weight with an infusion rate of 4.5–6 mL/s. The radiation dosage used will be determined in adherence to the as low as reasonably achievable principle. Depending on local clinical practice, CT-FFR will be performed in those individuals with moderate luminal stenosis.

ICA protocol

ICA will be performed in accordance with the current consensus guidelines for quantitative estimation of coronary luminal stenosis [31], [32]. Two angiographic views will be obtained for each vessel at a minimum of 30 degrees from one another and, where possible, avoiding overlap with other branches. Image acquisition will occur at a frame rate of 15 frames per second, at the best available resolution. FFR with intracoronary infusion of adenosine will be calculated to determine disease significance in those with stenosis in the range from 50% to 70% identified in either the left main or left anterior descending and 70% to 90% in the right coronary or left circumflex arteries, with a value of 0.80 functioning as the threshold to determine the suitability of patients for revascularisation [33] or not [34]. Total or subtotal vessel occlusion will be labeled as a value of 0.5, as has been utilized in previous studies [35], [36].

CMRA and CCTA analysis

Both the CMRA and CCTA 3D coronary dataset will be analyzed using a nine-coronary segment model [12], [17]. The coronary segments in the nine-segment model include the left main stem coronary artery, left anterior descending coronary artery (proximal, middle, and distal), right coronary artery (proximal, middle, and distal), and left circumflex (proximal and mid artery). To assess diagnostic performance, the CAD-RADS classification will be used to calculate disease severity on a per-patient basis. Furthermore, each individual coronary segment will be visually inspected and categorized as having no or minor luminal stenosis <50% or significant coronary artery stenosis defined as luminal diameter narrowing of ≥50% or ≥70%.

The image quality of CCTA and CMRA images will be systematically evaluated for the presence of image artifacts. Specifically, phase misalignment, respiratory motion, coronary motion, calcium blooming, and/or hard-beam artifact for CCTA [32]. Respiratory and/or coronary motion imaging artifacts will be evaluated for CMRA [37]. The scale that will be used for assessment of image quality will be as follows, previously utilized by Bustin et al. [17] and also in assessment of CCTA [29], [30]: 0, non-diagnostic; 1, poor (limited coronary vessel visibility or noisy image); 2, average (coronary vessel visible but diagnostic confidence low); 3, good (coronary artery adequately visualized and diagnostic quality image); and 4, excellent (coronary artery clearly depicted). If the presence of an artifact means that assessment of a coronary segment cannot be performed to ascertain the presence of stenosis, then this segment will be classified as having no stenosis. However, this will be registered in the image quality assessment as 0, non-diagnostic. All coronary segments will be included for the evaluation regardless of the image quality to avoid overestimation of the diagnostic accuracy.

All datasets will be anonymized and transferred electronically to secure servers for storage and analysis at the core laboratories. CCTA data will be analyzed at Aarhus University Hospital (AUH). All CCTA scans will be independently analyzed blinded to the patient’s clinical information in our core laboratory by two experts in cardiac CT analysis who have more than 15 years of experience in clinical cardiac CT. All CCTA data analysis will be performed on syngo.via software (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). CMRA data will be analyzed at AUH. The CMRA scans will be analyzed by the core laboratory at AUH by two independent experts with level III accreditation who are blinded to the patient’s clinical information. All CMRA data analysis will be performed using CVI42 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada). Multiplanar reconstruction with adjustment of the slab thickness will be used to optimize the visualization of the full length of each coronary artery in 3D. The evaluation of coronary artery stenosis will be done visually from orthogonal planes. An example of a patient with proximal left anterior descending coronary artery stenosis who underwent both CMRA, CCTA, and ICA is shown in Fig. 5. In case of disagreement between the observers, a consensus approach will be used. In addition, the principal investigator from each site will perform an analysis of the CMRA dataset, blinded to the CCTA data, for later comparison with the core laboratory readings.

ICA analysis

Angiographic data will be analyzed at AUH by a core laboratory utilizing Medis Suite 4.0.50.2. Quantitative analysis will be performed to determine luminal diameter narrowing of ≥50% and ≥70% in each of the nine coronary segments.

Statistical analysis

Continuous demographic variables will be presented as mean ± standard deviation and assessed using a two-tailed t-test. Ordinal categorical demographic variables will be presented as median, range, and interquartile range and assessed using either a Wilcoxon signed rank test or a Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. McNemar’s chi-squared test will be used to test the primary endpoint, which is that CMRA has an NPV of >90% in predicting significant CAD as compared to CCTA with a threshold of CAD-RADS ≥4 on a per-patient basis. Similar analysis will be done with a CAD-RADS threshold of ≥3. For those patients referred to ICA, the NPV, PPV, sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy will be calculated with 95% confidence intervals for CMRA and CCTA compared with ICA as the reference standard. Per-vessel and per-segment analysis with a coronary stenosis threshold of ≥50% and ≥70% in comparison to CCTA and, where available, ICA will also be performed.

Image quality will be graded on a per-patient and per-vessel basis on a scale of 0–4, as previously described [19], [20]. This will be compared between vessels and between investigator sites using a Mann-Whitney U test. Clinical and hemodynamic variables that may affect image quality such as heart rate, BMI, and gender will be assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

A p-value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Sample size calculation

To meet the primary endpoint defined as a per-patient NPV >80% by CMRA compared to CCTA for the detection of CAD at a threshold of CAD-RADS ≥4 with 90% power, a sample size of 183 is required. However, to account for a 10% drop-out rate due to inadequate image quality, a total of 201 patients will be included in the study to reject the null hypothesis that NPV is ≤80% in favor of the alternative hypothesis that NPV is >80%. This calculation is based on preliminary, unpublished data from our research group, showing that the NPV of CMRA is 88% as compared to CCTA with a prevalence of CAD-RADS ≥4 of 23%.

Study organization

The study organization includes a steering committee consisting of W.Y.K., R.M.B., and G.W. Safety monitoring will be performed by the principal investigator from each site and reported to the steering committee. The data management will be handled by the core laboratory at AUH where there is an established data infrastructure that securely stores and manages bot imaging as well as clinical data. Each site will report the clinical patient data into a Research Electronic Data Capture database, hosted at Aarhus University [38], [39]. Every included subject must provide written informed consent. Each participating center will undergo review at the core laboratory of the CMRA, CCTA, and ICA acquisition protocols and determination of scan quality acceptability from three scans for each imaging modality before initiation of enrollment.

Discussion

This will be the first multi-center study implementing state-of-art technological CMRA innovations integrating advanced non–rigid motion correction with 100% respiratory scan efficiency, image acceleration, and DL–based automated scan planning tool. Previous studies have illustrated the efficacy of the CMRA sequence as compared to both CCTA and ICA when employed at a single specialist center [19], [20]. However, the technical developments utilized in the present study, easing image planning and acquisition, mean that non-specialist centers such as those included in the iNav-AUTO CMRA study can feasibly implement CMRA. This multi-center design will determine whether implementation and more consistent image accuracy are achieved by use of the DL-assisted protocol and whether this iteration of CMRA is of sufficient quality to enable clinical implementation.

A prolonged scanning time limits the utility of conventional CMRA in clinical practice because of several issues including: [1] the large volume of patients that typically need to undergo investigation for CAD, [2] the impact on image quality since long scan times typically reduces image quality due to involuntary patient motion and less optimal effect of vasodilatation from sublingual nitroglycerine, and [3] patient discomfort. Due to the automated nature of the scanning protocol and 100% respiratory gating efficiency, scan time within this study will be reduced as compared to previous multi-center studies of CMRA [12], [13], with the expectation that this also results in an improvement in image quality. Meticulous pre-scan preparation of patients involving oral administration of beta-blockers to reduce the heart rate to below 60 bpm is crucial for extending the cardiac rest period and facilitating motion-free image acquisition [25]. The utilization of sublingual nitroglycerine is equally important as it promotes vasodilation, thereby facilitating the detection of luminal stenosis [40], [41].

At present, there is a lack of dedicated postprocessing software designed explicitly for CMRA scan analysis. To standardize the analysis of CMRA in our study, we have opted to use commercially available postprocessing software incorporating sliding-thin-slab multiplanar reformatting, in conjunction with the examination of source images. By adopting this approach, we aim to evaluate the level of inter-reader agreement in the interpretation of CMRA scans and, ideally, enhance consensus among readers. As such, if the novel iNav-AUTO CMRA scan can produce images of diagnostic quality and shows robustness with acceptable inter-reader agreement, it would further facilitate CMRA becoming a diagnostic option in clinical practice.

This study will also assess the effect of acquisition in both the end-systolic and mid-diastolic rest periods. Recent data have indicated that this improves the specificity of CMRA [26], which has lagged behind the high sensitivities achieved in previous studies [19], [20]. However, this study did not use beta-blocker for control of heart rate, which has previously been shown to increase the length of the acquisition window [12], and improve image quality [19]. Therefore, we will utilize beta-blockers for heart rate control in this study.

Given the, albeit small, risk of invasive complications, ICA should only be performed when high-risk CAD is suspected. There are also limits on the number of occasions in which patients can safely undergo CCTA, due to the use of ionizing radiation, which is of particular concern in younger adults and women [42], as well as the risk of renal impairment with contrast use in elderly or comorbid patients [43], [44]. This is of particular concern as recent guidelines advised that CCTA results remain valid for a period of only 2 years [3]. In addition, in patients with non-obstructive CAD, CCTA can be used on an ongoing basis for determining plaque progression and guiding decision-making [3], which indicates that increasing numbers of patients will require repeated re-investigation. Given that approximately 25% of patients undergoing their first CCTA are under the age of 50 [45], a large number of individuals will incur repeated exposure to ionizing radiation over the course of their lives. Since CMRA is completely non-invasive and does not use ionizing radiation, patients could alternatively be safely investigated via CMRA instead of CCTA.

If the clinical feasibility of CMRA is established through the current study, we suggest from the aspect of radiation hygiene that initial patient group to consider for CMRA would be women under 50 years of age. According to the Western Denmark Heart Registry that would represent approximately 10% of the cohort of patients referred to CCTA [45]. If individuals of both genders below the age of 50 are taken into consideration, they would account for approximately 25% of patients [45]. From the perspective of health economics, replacing CMRA with CCTA would depend on the local reimbursement rate for the two imaging modalities. Furthermore, it is plausible that CMRA is not going to replace CCTA one-to-one but may instead find utility as an initial diagnostic modality in a subset of symptomatic patients at low risk or even as a screening tool in asymptomatic high-risk patients if proven cost-effective.

In this study, we have chosen CCTA as the reference method for the detection of coronary artery stenosis. CCTA is chosen as the reference standard since current guidelines recommend CCTA as the first-line diagnostic test in symptomatic patients with low-to-intermediate pre-test likelihood of CAD [46]. This is due to the well-documented very high NPV of CCTA that effectively rules out significant obstructive CAD [35]. As such CCTA is nowadays considered the primary gate-keeper to ICA. ICA is still considered the gold standard for diagnosis mainly due to its superior spatial and temporal resolution without limitations concerning heart rate or arrhythmias. As such, a subanalysis of those referred to ICA will also be performed, to assess the diagnostic accuracy of CMRA compared to CCTA in relation to the ICA results.

Finally, this study plans on utilizing CAD-RADS for the assessment of CAD severity. Previous studies of CMRA have only utilized luminal stenosis grading, which is considered less effective compared to CAD-RADS for guiding patient management [47], [48]. This study will report both CAD-RADS and luminal stenosis cutoff values, to clarify if CAD-RADS can effectively be implemented with CMRA and is superior to assessing luminal stenosis alone.

Limitations

This study utilizes CCTA rather than ICA as the investigation to compare with CMRA. Although CCTA is the non–invasive gold standard for investigation of CAD, ICA remains the gold standard for assessment of potential for revascularisation and therefore it could be argued that this should be used to compare to CMRA, as CCTA itself is not 100% accurate in comparison to ICA [35]. However, CCTA is used as the comparative measure as this better reflects the way that CMRA will likely be used in clinical practice. Second, no adjustment to ensure a 50:50 gender enrollment has been performed. However, given that approximately 55% of patients referred to CCTA are male [49], it is very likely that the results of this study without adjustment will be representative of both genders.

In summary, the objective of this investigation is to conduct a real-world evaluation to determine if CMRA has progressed adequately to justify its integration into routine clinical practice. CMRA holds the potential to offer a safer diagnostic evaluation for patients referred with suspected obstructive CAD. The outcomes of this study will enhance our understanding of the potential role of CMRA as an alternative or complementary imaging modality for the evaluation of CAD.

Conclusions

The objective of the iNav-AUTO CMRA trial is to determine the clinical efficacy of the most up-to-date CMRA technology utilizing DL–based automated scan planning as compared to CCTA. If results demonstrate that the diagnostic accuracy of CMRA is approaching that of CCTA, then CMRA may be on the brink of routine implementation in clinical practice.

Ethics approval and consent

The study was conducted in strict obedience to Domestic and International laws, the principles of Good Clinical Practice and with respect to the Helsinki II Declaration. The study protocol has been approved by each participating center in the UK by the local institutional review board and by the Danish Research Ethics Committees in Denmark (case number: 1-10-72-57-22). The study is listed on www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05473117). Written consent was obtained before study inclusion, and a copy was stored at the inclusion site. Since all study subjects are already scheduled to undergo CCTA, we believe that the potential risk for the patients is related strictly to the additional non–contrast CMRA scan. The CMRA scan was performed by experienced radiographers and study participants were carefully screened for any contraindications to CMRA, such as metallic foreign bodies in the eye and pacemakers. Therefore, we believe that the study carries minimal risk for the participating individuals.
