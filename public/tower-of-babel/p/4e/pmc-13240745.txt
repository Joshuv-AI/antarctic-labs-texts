Nonbrachial Plexopathy Diagnoses Seen in a Brachial Plexus Injury Specialty Clinic
Authors: James S Lin, Allen T Bishop, Robert J Spinner, Nicholas Pulos, Alexander Y Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeA subset of patients referred to brachial plexus clinics are eventually diagnosed with conditions unrelated to the plexus. This study characterizes these individuals to provide insights into the types of patients encountered by referring clinicians and surgeons caring for brachial plexus injuries (BPIs).MethodsA retrospective review of all new consultations at a quaternary multidisciplinary BPI clinic over a 10-year period was undertaken. Fifteen (15%) patients underwent surgical intervention with our team, including tendon transfers (n = 7), nerve transfers (n = 5), arthrodesis (n = 3), exploration with neurolysis (n = 2), neurorrhaphy (n = 2), tumor resection involving a peripheral nerve (n = 2), and nerve biopsy (n = 1).ConclusionsApproximately 1 in 14 patients referred for brachial plexopathy were ultimately diagnosed with a nonplexus condition. A considerable proportion of these individuals were still deemed to benefit from surgical treatment. These findings highlight the value of comprehensive evaluation and management by a multidisciplinary team. The number of adult brachial plexus injuries (BPIs) worldwide each year is estimated to exceed the number that established centers can manage.1 Subsequently, an increasing number of institutions and surgeons in the United States are managing brachial plexopathy. Anecdotally, despite being a “brachial plexus clinic,” a number of patients without classic traumatic BPI often referred to these specialty clinics. Analysis of the epidemiology of patients with BPI presenting to an established multidisciplinary center found that the most common etiologies were traumatic injuries (61%), followed by iatrogenic and obstetric injuries (or brachial plexus birth palsy) (both 11%).2 Another category of brachial plexopathy often seen is the inflammatory etiology—commonly referred to as Parsonage-Turner syndrome, idiopathic brachial neuritis, or neuralgic amyotrophy—which may have an annual incidence as high as one in 1,000 in the primary care setting.3

A subset of patients referred to brachial plexus clinics are ultimately diagnosed with conditions unrelated to the plexus. Timely and accurate recognition of these patients is essential for providing targeted care that suits their needs, which may involve additional evaluation and treatment both within and outside a multidisciplinary brachial plexus team. This study aims to characterize these individuals to offer insights to referring clinicians and surgeons managing patients with BPIs regarding the types of patients they may encounter.

Methods

After institutional review board approval, we retrospectively reviewed all new consultations at a quaternary brachial plexus specialty clinic over 10 years from 2013 to 2023. The original intended patient population for this clinic was patients with traumatic, iatrogenic, and obstetric cases of brachial plexus injury. Its objective was not designed to manage chronic pain from brachial plexopathy. Prior to evaluation, our BPI clinic attempts to triage spinal etiologies, primary neoplasms, and thoracic outlet syndrome and refer these cases to the appropriate specialists at our institution. We do see surgical complications of spine surgery such as C5 palsies as well as more extended injuries, which were classified as iatrogenic cases of BPI (rather than nonplexus conditions).2 For inflammatory cases of brachial plexopathy, we also attempt to triage and see selective cases who may be candidates for secondary reconstruction, as we do not typically perform early surgery in these cases.

All patients underwent multidisciplinary evaluation from our brachial plexus team, which included orthopedic hand and microvascular surgeons, a neurosurgeon, a team of neurologists, and physical medicine and rehabilitation specialists.4 Patients were included for analysis if their final diagnosis was determined not to involve a brachial plexus lesion. Patients with traumatic, obstetric, iatrogenic, oncologic, and inflammatory (eg, Parsonage-Turner syndrome) brachial plexopathy were excluded.

Demographic, diagnostic, and treatment data were manually obtained from the electronic medical record. Final diagnoses were determined from the senior brachial plexus surgeons’ and neurology consultation notes. All relevant medical records are reviewed by the surgeons prior to the first appointment at our BPI clinic, and any previous evaluations by providers of various specialties were determined through these records. Types of surgical intervention were categorized as tendon transfers, nerve transfer, neurorrhaphy (defined as primary repair or nerve grafting), exploration with neurolysis only, and arthrodesis.

Results

Over a 10-year period, 1,415 new patient consultations were conducted, of whom 99 (7.0%) were diagnosed with conditions other than brachial plexus issues as the primary cause of their symptoms, forming the cohort of this investigation. The mean age in this group at the time of index evaluation by our team was 47.1 years (SD, 20.4; range, 9 months to 88 years). Sixty (60.6%) were male, and 39 (39.4%) patients were female. Demographics of Cohort

Most patients (n = 83, 83.8%) had records of previous evaluations for their condition prior to presenting to our BPI clinic. Forty-six (46.5%) had been seen by a nonhand orthopedic surgeon. Twenty (20.2%) patients had previously been evaluated by a hand surgeon or peripheral nerve specialist. Thirty-two (32.3%) patients had previously seen a neurologist, 22 (23.2%) by a physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist, 17 (17.2%) by a neurological surgeon, 17 (17.2%) by a primary care physician, and 16 (16.2%) by an emergency medicine provider. All of these patients were evaluated outside our institution.

A total of 15 (15.2%) patients underwent surgical intervention by our team, with four patients requiring a second-stage surgery. The types of procedures performed included tendon transfers (n = 7), nerve transfers (n = 5), arthrodesis (n = 3), exploration with neurolysis (n = 2), neurorrhaphy (n = 2), tumor resection around a peripheral nerve (n = 2), and nerve biopsy (n = 1).

Primary cervical spine pathology

Primary cervical spine conditions were most commonly encountered (Table 2). Of these patients, 15 had symptoms consistent with cervical radiculopathy, two had Hirayama disease5 (also known as monomelic amyotrophy, characterized by progressive deficits involving the muscles innervated by the C7 to T1 spinal segments), and one patient had cervical myelopathy. Once it was determined that the symptoms were due to a cervical spine issue, patients were often referred to our spine center for treatment.

Three patients with severe, longstanding cervical radiculopathy ultimately underwent surgery by our BPI team. One patient with radiculopathy who had already undergone three cervical spine procedures (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, posterior decompression and fusion, and revision posterior fusion) underwent transfer of the flexor carpi ulnaris branch of the ulnar nerve to the biceps branch of the musculocutaneous nerve (Oberlin transfer), which improved elbow flexion from a modified Medical Research Council grade 1 to 2+ at the latest follow-up, 1 year postoperatively. Another patient had previously undergone two spine procedures: C2 to T2 decompression and posterior spinal fusion, followed by a C5 corpectomy with C4 to C6 anterior fusion, and continued to experience C5 to C6 dysfunction with paralysis of elbow flexion. She underwent bilateral Oberlin transfers and bilateral long head triceps branch transfers to the anterior division of the axillary nerve. At 1 year postoperatively, her elbow flexion and shoulder abduction improved from no activation to 70° of abduction with a grade 3 elbow flexion on the left and 30° abduction with grade 2 elbow flexion on the right. The third patient exhibited chronic C8 to T1 radiculopathy with severe intrinsic atrophy. He previously had a paraspinal abscess with osteomyelitis and had undergone a surgical procedure, which was described as anterior C7 to T1 debridement of osteomyelitis, diskitis, or paraspinal abscess. Upon presentation to us, his primary concern was improvement of thumb flexion, pinch opposition, and clawing of his ulnar digits. He underwent thumb interphalangeal joint arthrodesis, extensor indicis pollicis opposition transfer, and an anticlaw tenodesis.

One of the two patients with Hirayama disease underwent transfer of the extensor carpi radialis longus to flexor digitorum profundus tendons of digits 2 to 5 to improve grip strength, followed by thumb interphalangeal joint arthrodesis.

Isolated peripheral nerve lesions

Isolated lesions of distal peripheral nerves included four cases of radial nerve palsy, four with ulnar nerve lesions, two cases of posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) palsy, one case of median neuropathy, one with long thoracic neuropathy, one with axillary neuropathy, and one with injury to the lateral antebrachial cutaneous and radial sensory nerves.

Four of these patients underwent surgery by our BPI team. One patient with a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor of the radial nerve underwent resection followed by radial nerve tendon transfers: the pronator teres to the extensor carpi radialis brevis, middle finger flexor digitorum superficialis to a rerouted extensor pollicis longus (EPL), and flexor carpi radius (FCR) to extensor digitorum communis (EDC). One patient sustained an iatrogenic PIN injury during a distal biceps tendon repair. Our team performed an exploration and repair of the PIN using a sural nerve autograft, which failed to restore any function. Consequently, the patient underwent a subsequent surgery that included tendon transfers of the pronator teres to the EPL, as well as FCR to the EDC and extensor digiti minimi. Another patient had a PIN palsy secondary to a periosteal lipoma. This patient underwent tumor resection and transfer of the supinator branch to the distal PIN by our team. Similarly, he underwent tendon transfers in a second-stage procedure, including palmaris longus to a rerouted EPL and FCR to EDC. Finally, one patient underwent resection of a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor of the ulnar nerve followed by interpositional sural nerve autograft reconstruction.

Primary neurologic disorders

Patients with primary neurologic disorders included three diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and one patient with an unspecified upper and lower motor neuron disease. Two patients were diagnosed with early Parkinson’s disease, and two were diagnosed with a neurologic movement disorder. One patient was diagnosed with multifocal motor neuropathy. One was diagnosed with anterior horn disease. One was noted to have spastic hemiparesis secondary to a stroke, and one was found to have sequela from polio.

Two patients underwent reconstructive surgery with our brachial plexus injury team. The patient with an unspecified upper and lower motor neuron disease underwent nerve transfers to restore elbow flexion, and the patient with multifocal motor neuropathy underwent a median nerve biopsy.

Mechanical shoulder conditions

Nine patients were found to have a mechanical shoulder condition as the primary source of their symptoms. These patients had issues such as glenohumeral instability and scapulothoracic dysfunction. These patients were referred to our shoulder specialists for management.

Spinal cord injury (SCI)

Four patients sustained spinal cord injuries, which were the cause of their symptoms, rather than a brachial plexus lesion. Of these patients, one underwent tendon transfers for reconstruction of pinch and grasp by one of our colleagues specializing in tetraplegia.

Psychogenic etiologies

Four patients were deemed to have symptoms secondary to a functional movement disorder or dystonia without an identifiable organic or neurologic cause. None of these patients underwent surgical intervention. They were referred to physical medicine and rehabilitation specialists or our neurology colleagues who specialize in movement disorders and dystonia.

Myelitis

Four patients were diagnosed with myelitis or an inflammatory condition affecting the spinal cord. This could either be flaccid myelitis or transverse myelitis, depending on the distribution of spinal cord involvement. One patient underwent tendon transfers to restore hand function.

Other

A diverse group of 34 patients was diagnosed with various other conditions, such as missed compartment syndrome, distal crush injuries, or otherwise unknown conditions not consistent with a brachial plexus lesion. Out of these, 14 patients experienced severe pain in their upper extremity, but no brachial plexopathy was identified.

Four patients in this category ultimately underwent surgery by our brachial plexus team. One patient with a missed forearm compartment syndrome underwent exploration with neurolysis followed by arthrodesis of the thumb interphalangeal and index through small finger PIP joints to improve grasp. Another patient with a remote history of forearm compartment syndrome with loss of wrist and finger flexors underwent transfer of the wrist and finger common flexor mass with allograft lengthening to the flexor digitorum profundus tendons of the index through small fingers using gracilis and semitendinosus allograft lengthening, brachioradialis to flexor pollicis longus tendon transfer, and median and ulnar nerve neurolysis. One patient was found to have a lipoma of the subscapularis, and he underwent reconstruction of the rhomboid major and minor, levator scapulae, and serratus musculature following tumor resection. Finally, one patient with upper extremity pain of an unknown etiology underwent nerve transfers to restore hand function.

Discussion

Patients with a history of trauma, upper extremity weakness, and sensory disturbances, along with supportive studies, should be considered for a presumptive diagnosis of brachial plexus injury and referred to a specialized BPI clinic. Despite these criteria, a subset of these patients is ultimately diagnosed with conditions unrelated to the plexus. Prior reports have noted the diagnostic complexity of differentiating brachial plexopathy from cervical spine pathology,6 peripheral nerve lesions,7,8 and central neurologic disorders.9 Because timely identification of these alternative diagnoses is essential for guiding patients to the correct subspecialty care, this study aimed to characterize individuals referred to an established and dedicated BPI clinic who were eventually diagnosed with nonbrachial plexus causes. In this 10-year retrospective review of 1,415 new consultations, 7% of patients were ultimately diagnosed with nonplexus disorders. These patients exhibited substantial diagnostic heterogeneity—including cervical spine pathology, isolated distal peripheral nerve lesions, primary neurologic disease, mechanical shoulder dysfunction, myelitis, spinal cord injury, psychogenic etiologies, and several miscellaneous conditions—consistent with the breadth of conditions previously described in tertiary peripheral nerve referral patterns.10 A considerable proportion had undergone evaluation by multiple specialists before referral. A notable subset ultimately did undergo surgical intervention by our multidisciplinary brachial plexus injury team.

The primary aim of this study was to determine the types of nonbrachial plexus conditions encountered in patients referred for presumed brachial plexopathy. Cervical radiculopathy emerged as the most common alternative diagnosis, aligning with prior reports illustrating the overlapping patterns of pain, numbness, and weakness that can complicate differentiation from lesions of the brachial plexus.11 Careful consideration of clinical findings, electrodiagnostic evaluation, and targeted imaging may help identify the correct etiology. In cervical radiculopathy, pain often involves the neck and radiates down the upper extremity in a dermatomal distribution that may be provoked by maneuvers such as Spurling’s test.12 Brachial plexopathies typically do not involve symptoms in the neck, and deficits do not follow a single dermatome or myotome. Magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography myelogram evaluation is recommended to confirm sources of compression such as spondylolysis or disc herniation in cervical spine patients who are candidates for potential interventional or surgical management.12 Although electromyography may offer prognostic value in predicting surgical outcomes for cervical radiculopathy,13 it is not routinely obtained in this setting if clinical symptoms correlate with imaging findings. Moreover, the North American Spine Society contends there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of electromyography for patients in whom the diagnosis of cervical radiculopathy remains unclear after clinical and magnetic resonance imaging evaluation.12 In contrast, patients at our BPI clinic routinely undergo neurologist evaluation and neurodiagnostic testing the day before their initial consultation, as our team performs these tests. Electrodiagnostic testing is instrumental in differentiating the etiology and determining the location and extent of a brachial plexus lesion.4 Nevertheless, three patients with deficits from chronic cervical radiculopathy did undergo surgery from our team, which included procedures such as nerve and tendon transfers. Salvage of cervical spine-related deficits through distal reconstruction has been previously described.5,14 These data further emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration with spine surgeons, as there may be a role for hand and microvascular reconstruction to improve upper extremity function in longstanding cases in which deficits may not fully correct with intervention at the level of the cervical spine alone.

Our findings also help illustrate the breadth of additional diagnoses encountered in contemporary BPI clinics. The spectrum of conditions exhibited here—such as distal peripheral nerve lesions, primary neurologic disorders, mechanical shoulder abnormalities, and psychogenic disorders—demonstrates the diagnostic complexity and underscores the need for multidisciplinary expertise when evaluating and treating these patients. The purpose of this study is not to discourage referrals to a BPI center when the diagnosis is unclear, as a considerable proportion of these patients still underwent reconstructive upper extremity surgery with our plexus team to improve function.

Limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. As a retrospective review from a well-established single quaternary care BPI center in the Midwestern United States, this study is susceptible to selection bias. Referral patterns, institutional reputation, and evolving subspecialty practices are factors noted by prior authors to influence BPI clinic populations, which likely shaped the patients who presented to this center.2,15, 16, 17 Transfer bias was also unavoidable, particularly given the significant travel distances for many patients; loss to follow-up and subsequent care obtained at outside institutions may limit the capture of complete outcomes. Although our experience may not be generalizable for all surgeons aiming to establish new centers treating BPI, we feel that this information is valuable in providing insight into conditions that they can expect to encounter.

In conclusion, these data demonstrate that a meaningful minority of patients referred to a dedicated brachial plexus injury clinic are ultimately diagnosed with disorders outside the brachial plexus. Cervical spine conditions, distal peripheral nerve lesions, and primary neurologic disorders were the most frequently diagnosed conditions. For providers evaluating suspected brachial plexopathy, these findings underscore the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis, performing thorough neurologic and musculoskeletal examinations, and ensuring access to multidisciplinary subspecialty evaluations. Future studies may aim to develop validated diagnostic pathways or decision-support tools that incorporate clinical history, imaging characteristics, and electrodiagnostic findings to improve timely appropriate care of these complex patients.
