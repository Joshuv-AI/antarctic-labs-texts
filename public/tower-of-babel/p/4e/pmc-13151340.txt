Development and psychometric validation of the pre-earthquake illusion of control perception scale
Authors: İsmet Çelebi, Emrah Gökkaya, Ömer Demirbilek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarthquakes are highly destructive, and their impact depends largely on prior preparedness. Beyond knowledge and experience, psychological perceptions—especially the illusion of control, the unrealistic belief that one can influence uncontrollable events—can reduce motivation to prepare and weaken disaster readiness. This study aimed to develop a valid and reliable instrument to measure adults’ pre-earthquake illusion of control.MethodsThe scale development process followed established psychometric procedures, including content validation, item analysis, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Background

Earthquakes pose significant physical, economic, and psychological risks for societies living on active fault lines, such as Turkey [26]. Preparedness for such natural disasters is directly related not only to structural precautions but also to psychological perceptions. In particular, individuals’ erroneous belief that they have control over external and uncontrollable events—such as earthquakes—referred to as the illusion of control, may reduce preparedness behaviors and negatively affect risk management [23, 26, 27]. National and international studies have shown that, beyond knowledge level alone, subjective perceptions of control play an important role in disaster preparedness [4, 29].

Earthquake risk perception plays a central role in motivating preparedness behaviors at the household level, including emergency kit preparation, structural reinforcement, and evacuation planning. However, inflated perceptions of personal adequacy may weaken the motivational effect of risk awareness. Thus, illusion of control may act as a moderating cognitive bias that reduces the behavioral consequences of perceived risk [12, 17, 21, 24, 25, 30].

The illusion of control is defined as “a state in which individuals believe unrealistically that they have control over events determined entirely by external factors.” [15]. As a coping strategy against uncertainty, such an illusion may be temporarily comforting; however, it can have risky long-term consequences [27]. Research shows that this illusion previous research suggests that illusion of control may influence individuals’ risk perceptions and preparedness decisions [7, 23, 26]. In sudden and destructive disasters such as earthquakes, falling into the illusion of control constitutes a serious barrier to taking precautionary actions. Similar cognitive biases have also been reported in other disaster contexts such as floods, pandemics, and climate-related hazards, where individuals may overestimate their personal capacity to avoid harm despite objective risks [25].

The present study is conceptually grounded in the distinction between Rotter’s [22] Locus of Control theory and Langer’s [15] Illusion of Control framework. Rotter conceptualized locus of control as a relatively stable personality orientation reflecting generalized expectations about internal versus external control of reinforcement. In contrast, the illusion of control represents a context-specific cognitive heuristic in which individuals overestimate their influence over objectively uncontrollable events. Unlike locus of control, which concerns broad expectancy patterns, the illusion of control operates as a situational bias [2, 3, 14]. In disaster contexts, this bias may function as a psychological buffer that neutralizes risk perception and weakens motivation for preparedness behaviors.

Previous disaster research also indicates that demographic factors such as gender, education level, and prior disaster experience may influence preparedness behaviors and perceived control [21, 30]. Young adults are an important population to examine in disaster preparedness research, as this developmental period is often associated with higher risk-taking behaviors and lower levels of disaster preparedness reported in previous studies. Young adulthood (approximately 18–30 years) is a developmental period during which individuals take responsibility for decision-making, develop independent living skills, and exhibit higher levels of risk-taking. Studies show that this age group tends to have low levels of disaster preparedness but high levels of self-confidence [4, 23]. For example, research conducted with university students in Türkiye found that even individuals with earthquake experience often display low preparedness levels, which may be linked to unrealistic beliefs in personal control [26]. Similarly, a study among nursing students reported that although students had high disaster awareness and risk perception, this awareness did not translate into action, resulting in low preparedness behavior [4]. This suggests that regardless of how high individuals’ knowledge and risk perception may be, cognitive biases such as the illusion of control can strongly influence preparedness behavior. Additionally, Whang et al., [29] found that disaster management competence among nurses was associated not only with knowledge but also with preparedness beliefs and perceived control.

All these findings highlight the need for a unique and valid measurement tool that assesses individuals’ perceptions of control in the pre-earthquake period. In this context, the development of the Earthquake Pre-Event Illusion of Control Perception Scale aims to measure young adults’ perceived control prior to disasters and to evaluate the relationship between this perception and both risk assessment and preparedness behaviors. Moreover, this scale is expected to contribute to scientific and practical studies in disaster psychology, public health, and disaster education.

In the present study, “control” does not refer to the ability to influence seismic events directly, but to subjective beliefs of adequacy, invulnerability, and behavioral competence in the absence of objective preparation.

Methods

Research design

This study aims to examine how individuals’ perceptions of control prior to an earthquake are shaped independently of their actual preparedness levels. Accordingly, the goal was to develop a valid and reliable psychometric instrument capable of measuring individuals’ pre-earthquake illusion of control. Methodologically, the research follows a scale development design ([6]; DeVellis [8]),.

Scale development process

The overall scale development process and the steps followed are summarized conceptually in Fig. 1. The stages proposed by Boateng et al., [6] and DeVellis [8] were taken into consideration throughout the development of the scale.

Scale Development Process

Participants

This study was carried out within the scope of developing a scale to measure the illusion of control prior to an earthquake. Data were collected from voluntary participants aged 18 and older, representing the general population. A convenience sampling method was used. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being 18 years or older, (2) voluntary participation, and (3) completing the scale form fully.

In scale development studies, it is recommended that the sample size be at least five times the number of items [11], while some authors suggest ten times the number of items [1, 6]. Based on the 14-item initial item pool, a minimum of 140 participants was targeted. A total of 406 participants were reached, which exceeds this requirement substantially (406/14 = 29 participants per item). No data loss occurred during the data collection process because incomplete data transmission was disabled, and all data was included in the analysis.

Demographic analysis showed that 259 participants (63.8%) were female, and 147 (36.2%) were male. Regarding age distribution, 201 (49.5%) were 18–19 years old, 158 (38.9%) were 20–22 years old, and 47 (11.6%) were 23 years or older. Most participants were single (94.6%), while 22 participants (5.4%) were married. In terms of earthquake experience, 47 (11.6%) reported no experience, 196 (48.3%) had experienced a non-destructive earthquake, and 163 (40.1%) had experienced a destructive earthquake. Additionally, 222 (54.7%) had received earthquake-related training, while 184 (45.3%) had not.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a questionnaire consisting of two main sections: (a) the Sociodemographic Information Form and (b) the draft version of the Earthquake Pre-Event Illusion of Control Perception Scale.

a. Sociodemographic Information Form: This form included five questions designed to obtain basic demographic information from participants, including age, gender, marital status, prior earthquake experience, and participation in earthquake-related training programs.

The initial item pool consisted of 20 items developed based on an extensive review of the literature on illusion of control, self-efficacy bias, risk perception, and disaster preparedness beliefs [15, 19, 28]. The preliminary items were evaluated by 13 experts in disaster management, psychology, and measurement and evaluation using Lawshe’s content validity technique. Based on the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) results, six items were removed, resulting in a 14-item draft scale, which was subsequently administered to the main study sample.

Before the main data collection, a pilot test (n = 10) was conducted to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and linguistic appropriateness of the items. Minor wording adjustments were made based on participant feedback prior to the final administration of the questionnaire.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted between September 15 and October 15, 2025, using an online questionnaire administered through Google Forms. All survey items were set as mandatory in order to prevent missing responses. The survey link was distributed through multiple online channels, including social media platforms (Instagram, X, and LinkedIn), university student communities, online discussion forums, and the researchers’ professional networks.

Prior to participation, individuals were provided with information about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of their responses. Informed consent was obtained electronically before participants could access the questionnaire. Participation was anonymous, and no personally identifiable information was collected. Completion of the questionnaire required approximately 3–5 min.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 and AMOS 24. First, descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics. Second, the content validity of the initial item pool was assessed using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) based on expert evaluations following the method proposed by Lawshe [16]. Items that did not meet the recommended CVR threshold for the given number of experts were removed from the scale.

Third, item analyses were conducted to evaluate item performance. For each item, means, standard deviations, corrected item–total correlations, and Cronbach’s alpha if-item-deleted values were calculated. Corrected item–total correlations were examined to assess the contribution of each item to the overall construct [9].

Fourth, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was performed to examine the underlying factor structure of the scale. Prior to factor extraction, the suitability of the dataset for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. KMO values above 0.60 were considered acceptable, while values above 0.80 indicated very good sampling adequacy. A statistically significant Bartlett test was interpreted as evidence that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis (Hair et al., 2014).

Factor extraction was conducted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Direct Oblimin rotation, as correlations between latent factors were theoretically plausible. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained, and the factor structure was further evaluated using the scree plot and conceptual interpretability of the factor solution. Items with factor loadings below 0.40 were considered for removal. In addition, items showing cross-loadings greater than 0.30 on multiple factors were evaluated as potentially problematic in terms of discriminant contribution (Hair et al., 2019). After each item removal, the analysis was re-run to ensure stability of the factor structure, and this iterative process continued until a conceptually coherent solution was achieved.

Fifth, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS to test the factor structure identified through EFA. Model fit was evaluated using multiple goodness-of-fit indices, including χ²/df, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI). Following commonly accepted recommendations, χ²/df values below 5, CFI, IFI, and TLI values above 0.90, and RMSEA values below 0.08 were considered indicative of acceptable model fit [10, 13]. Model modifications were applied only when theoretically justified.

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. According to commonly accepted guidelines, alpha values of 0.70 or higher indicate acceptable reliability, values above 0.80 indicate good reliability, and values above 0.90 indicate excellent internal consistency [10, 18].

Results

In this section, the statistical analyses conducted in the study are presented in three stages. First, the content validity analyses conducted to evaluate the relevance and representativeness of the scale items are reported. Second, findings related to item analysis, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) are presented to examine the construct validity of the scale. Finally, reliability analyses assessing the internal consistency of the scale and its subdimensions are reported.

Content validity analyses

The content validity of the initial item pool was evaluated based on expert judgments using Lawshe’s content validity technique [16]. A total of 13 experts in disaster management, psychology, and measurement and evaluation assessed the 20 items included in the preliminary scale form. Experts rated each item using a three-point scale: “essential,” “useful but not essential,” and “not necessary.” For each item, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated based on expert evaluations. According to Lawshe’s criteria, items with CVR values less than or equal to zero are generally considered for elimination. In the present study, no items were removed at this stage. Subsequently, the CVR values were compared with the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.538, which corresponds to a panel of 13 experts. As a result, six items with CVR values below this threshold were removed, yielding a 14-item scale. Following item-level evaluation, the Content Validity Index (CVI) of the scale was calculated as the mean CVR of the remaining items. The resulting CVI value was 0.610, which exceeds the critical value of 0.538 for 13 experts. Therefore, the overall content validity of the 14-item scale was considered acceptable.

Construct validity analyses

Item analysis

Following the completion of content validity assessment, item analysis was conducted as a preliminary step prior to factor analysis in order to evaluate item performance and internal consistency of the scale. Item analysis examined the relationship between each item and the total scale score, as well as the contribution of each item to overall reliability. For each item, mean, standard deviation, corrected item–total correlation coefficients, and Cronbach’s alpha if-item-deleted values were calculated.

Corrected item–total correlation coefficients ranged between 0.468 and 0.713, exceeding the commonly recommended minimum threshold of 0.30 (DeVellis [8]), . Values above 0.40 are generally considered indicative of good item discrimination, suggesting that the items meaningfully contribute to the measurement of the construct.

Additionally, the Cronbach’s alpha if-item-deleted values did not indicate a substantial increase in the overall reliability of the scale if any item were removed. These findings suggest that all items demonstrated satisfactory discrimination and contributed appropriately to the internal consistency of the scale. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the two-factor structure identified through the exploratory factor analysis. Model fit was evaluated using several commonly reported fit indices in structural equation modeling, including χ²/df, RMSEA, NFI, CFI, GFI, and TLI (Fig. 3). Modification indices were examined to further improve model fit. Based on theoretical considerations and similarity in item content, covariances between the error terms of several item pairs were allowed (Items 3–4, 3–14, 6–14, and 13–14). These modifications resulted in improved model fit while maintaining the conceptual integrity of the two-factor structure.

Discussion

In this study, the Earthquake Pre-Event Illusion of Control Perception Scale was developed to measure how individuals’ perceived control prior to an earthquake is shaped independently of their actual preparedness levels. The scale development process followed psychometric measurement principles ([6]; DeVellis [8]), and a multi-stage validity-based analytical approach.

The findings revealed that the scale has a two-dimensional structure that aligns with core components of the illusion of control described in the literature [15]. The first dimension reflects individuals’ tendency to overestimate their personal preparedness, which corresponds to self-efficacy illusion and inflated perceptions of personal capability [20]. The second dimension captures avoidance-based responses to earthquake risk, representing a cognitive and emotional strategy through which individuals distance themselves from threatening information. These mechanisms are particularly important for understanding the well-documented knowledge–behavior gap in disaster preparedness research. Previous studies have shown that individuals may possess high levels of risk awareness or disaster knowledge, yet fail to translate this awareness into concrete preparedness actions ([5]; Paton & Johnston, 2001). The present findings suggest that unrealistic control beliefs may contribute to this paradox. When individuals perceive themselves as personally capable of managing earthquake consequences without formal preparation, the perceived need for precautionary behavior decreases. In addition, the avoidance dimension appears to function as an emotion-regulation strategy. Although avoidance can temporarily reduce anxiety associated with disaster risk, it simultaneously reduces cognitive engagement with preparedness planning. As a result, individuals may remain in a psychologically “frozen” state in which awareness exists but is not translated into preventive action. In this sense, avoidance operates both as a short-term psychological comfort mechanism and as a barrier to long-term behavioral preparedness. This may help explain why even individuals with direct earthquake experience do not necessarily demonstrate higher preparedness levels.

The two-factor structure identified through factor analyses aligns well with the theoretical model. Results from both Exploratory Factor Analysis and Confirmatory Factor Analysis demonstrated statistically significant and conceptually coherent clustering of items. The acceptable fit indices [10, 13] verify the construct validity of the developed scale. The emergence of this two-factor structure suggests that perceived control in the pre-earthquake period operates through two distinct cognitive mechanisms: subjective preparedness beliefs and avoidance-based control illusions.

Reliability analyses showed that Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for both subdimensions and the overall scale were high. This indicates that the scale possesses sufficient measurement sensitivity and internal homogeneity [18]. High item-total correlations also support the contribution of each item to the targeted construct.

The validated and reliable Earthquake Pre-Event Illusion of Control Perception Scale consists of 12 items and two subdimensions. It is a five‑point Likert-type instrument, with scores ranging from 12 to 60. Higher scores indicate a stronger illusion of control, while lower scores indicate a weaker illusion of control. The scale demonstrates satisfactory psychometric properties within the present sample and provides a preliminary tool for assessing pre-earthquake illusion of control perceptions.

The findings of this study help address an important gap in measuring the illusion of control within the context of disaster psychology. In countries such as Türkiye, where disaster frequency is high, understanding cognitive biases influencing preparedness behaviors is essential for enhancing the effectiveness of risk communication and disaster education programs [5]. This scale offers a scientific foundation for identifying the degree of unrealistic confidence and inflated perceptions of control during the pre-disaster period, facilitating targeted interventions and educational strategies. One of the most valuable contributions of this study is the operationalization of the illusion of control in a disaster-specific context.

Beyond its measurement function, the developed scale offers practical applications for disaster risk communication. Identifying individuals with high illusion of control scores can facilitate the design of targeted “anti-bias” strategies. Instead of simply increasing technical knowledge about earthquake risk, communication campaigns can directly address unrealistic patterns of confidence and cognitive avoidance. Specific interventions that gently question perceptions of personal control can help bridge the information-action gap and strengthen behavioral preparedness.

However, several limitations should be noted. The scale development study was conducted within a specific sample group; therefore, the psychometric properties of the scale should be retested in populations with varied sociodemographic characteristics. Additionally, although the scale was administered to adults aged 18 and older, the majority of participants were between 18 and 22 years old. Therefore, the current psychometric properties primarily reflect young adults and should be interpreted within this demographic context. Future studies are encouraged to examine the indirect effects of the illusion of control on disaster preparedness by including behavioral preparedness indicators, which may strengthen the external validity of the model. Additionally, the present study did not include direct behavioral preparedness indicators (e.g., emergency kit ownership, evacuation planning). Future research should examine the predictive validity of the scale by testing its relationship with observable preparedness behaviors.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop a scale that measures how individuals’ perceived control and preparedness beliefs prior to an earthquake are formed independently of their actual preparedness levels. As a result of the analyses, the Earthquake Pre-Event Illusion of Control Perception Scale was developed, consisting of two dimensions and 12 items with strong psychometric properties.

The two-dimensional structure of the scale enables the differentiation of individuals’ subjective preparedness beliefs and their cognitive avoidance tendencies toward earthquake-related thoughts. Analyses conducted throughout the study demonstrated that the scale shows satisfactory validity and reliability. The factor structure aligns with theoretical foundations, and internal consistency coefficients indicate high reliability. The developed scale provides an initial and psychometrically supported tool for measuring individuals’ pre-earthquake control illusions.

This scale fills an important gap in disaster psychology and risk communication by providing a structured way to measure cognitive mechanisms that influence preparedness behaviors. In settings where earthquake risk is high, such as Türkiye, understanding the perceptual and cognitive factors that weaken disaster preparedness is crucial. The scale provides a preliminary tool for identifying individuals with heightened illusions of control and may support the development of targeted educational and risk communication strategies in future research.

Future research should test the scale in different samples and cultural contexts, and to investigate how the illusion of control interacts with behavioral preparedness indicators in longitudinal studies.

However, it should be noted that the current psychometric properties of the scale primarily reflect young adult populations, as the majority of participants were between 18 and 22 years of age. Therefore, the generalizability of the scale should be further examined in more demographically diverse samples in future research.

Another limitation of the present study is that convergent, divergent, and predictive validity indicators were not examined. The study focused primarily on establishing the initial psychometric properties of the scale through content validity, factor structure, and internal consistency analyses. Future studies should test the relationships between illusion of control perceptions and theoretically related constructs such as disaster risk perception, preparedness behaviors, and behavioral preparedness indicators to further strengthen the external validity of the scale. In conclusion, the Earthquake Pre-Event Illusion of Control Perception Scale represents an original, valid, and reliable instrument that provides a promising foundation for future research and applications in disaster psychology and disaster management.
