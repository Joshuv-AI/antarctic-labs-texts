Molecular Characterization of Pericardial Mesothelioma with Constrictive and Recurrent Pericarditis

Recurrent pericarditis may present with nonspecific clinical symptoms and imaging findings such as pericardial thickening and constrictive physiology, often mimicking inflammatory or infectious etiologies. In cases unresponsive to conventional therapies, neoplastic causes, including pericardial mesothelioma, should be considered. When histopathology reveals atypical mesothelial proliferation and traditional biomarkers such as BAP1 and CDKN2A are intact, molecular profiling, including assessment for TAZ amplification, can support diagnostic accuracy, inform prognosis, and guide targeted therapeutic strategies. Malignant pericardial mesothelioma (MPM) is a rare and aggressive neoplasm arising from the mesothelial cells lining the pericardium, with nonspecific clinical presentations.1 Although the etiology of MPM remains unknown, genetic predispositions appear to contribute to its pathogenesis, including BAP1 (BRCA1-associated protein 1) loss1,2 and CDKN2A (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A) homozygous deletion,3 paralleling mechanisms in pleural mesothelioma. However, these markers are not universally present, and their absence can complicate distinction from reactive mesothelial proliferations. Here, we describe a rare case of MPM presenting as recurrent pericarditis, with challenging histopathologic and clinical features. Molecular profiling revealed a unique genomic mechanism, highlighting a potential novel mechanism of oncogenesis.

History of Presentation

A 73-year-old man presented with 9 months of recurrent fevers, chills, night sweats, fatigue, and pleuritic chest pain found to be in atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. Laboratory tests demonstrated white blood cell count of 23,500 (1.4% bands), hemoglobin 8.9, platelets 379,000, sodium 128, potassium 4.7, creatinine 0.50, anion gap 11, with normal liver function tests. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (85 mm/h), C-reactive protein (218 mg/L), and D-dimer (1,629) were elevated. Troponin was negative, with electrocardiogram showing nonspecific T-wave abnormalities. Additional work-up including urinalysis, blood cultures, respiratory pathogen panel, HIV, treponema antibody, Lyme antibody, and blood parasite smear were negative. Computed tomography angiography showed circumferential thickening of the ascending aorta and a trace pericardial effusion. Both transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography confirmed a small pericardial effusion, grade II diastolic dysfunction, and pericardial thickening (1.2 cm) without constrictive physiology.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of tobacco use disorder in remission, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation on apixaban, hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and recurrent pericarditis refractory to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and colchicine. He denied alcohol use or recreational drug use. He was born in Italy and worked in a chemical plant with exposure to acetone and toluene, then worked with plumbing and piping.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included inflammatory/rheumatologic (eg, autoimmune aortitis, systemic lupus erythematosus, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody–associated vasculitis, giant cell arteritis, IgG4-related disease, Behcet disease, sarcoidosis, ankylosing spondylitis), infectious (eg, tuberculosis [TB] pericarditis, Lyme carditis, viral pericarditis/Coxsackie), neoplastic (eg, lymphoma, metastatic malignancy), and idiopathic (eg, autoinflammatory syndrome, idiopathic recurrent pericarditis) etiologies.

Investigations

Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)–avid nodular thickening from the ascending aorta to the aortic root, concerning for active pericarditis and aortitis (Figure 1). Rheumatologic, infectious, and neoplastic work-ups were largely negative except for positive interferon-gamma release assay, HLA-B51, and prostate-specific antigen (Table 1). The positive Quantiferon Gold test raised concern for TB pericarditis; however, imaging did not demonstrate pulmonary. Given the diagnostic uncertainty and to officially rule out TB pericarditis, the patient underwent a pericardial window with biopsy, which showed dense adhesions between the pericardium and epicardium. Immunostains were positive for keratin cocktail, CK7, WT1, D2-40, calretinin, and INI1 (expression retained) and were negative for fungal or acid-fast stains but revealed foci of atypical mesothelial cell proliferation suspicious for mesothelioma (Figure 2). Expert pathology review, however, favored a benign reactive process given intact BAP1 immunostaining in mesothelial cells and lack of CDKN2A deletion by FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridization) (present in 12% of cells; threshold for positivity: 20%).

Management

Given no active symptoms and negative imaging for pulmonary TB, the infectious team deferred inpatient treatment, and the patient was discharged with outpatient follow-up. Because of persistent diagnostic uncertainty however, empiric treatment was initiated for TB aortitis with rifabutin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, and pyridoxine, as well as for large-vessel vasculitis with prednisone and the IL-6 receptor antagonist tocilizumab.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was rehospitalized for recurrent fever and chest pain. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (48%), grade III diastolic dysfunction, and features of constrictive physiology (eg, pericardial thickening, septal bounce, elevated right atrial pressure, annulus reversus). Computed tomography angiography revealed worsening pericardial effusion and new bilateral pleural effusions. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated extensive nodular pericardial thickening with 2 pericardial base masses involving the subepicardial myocardium (Figure 3). Repeat PET-CT showed progression with increased FDG-avid pericardial involvement extending to the ascending aorta/proximal arch and new FDG-avid mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy. Endobronchial ultrasound with lymph node biopsy confirmed metastatic mesothelioma. OncoPanel revealed primarily tier III/IV variants of uncertain significance, including low-frequency TP53 splice-site mutation and moderate-frequency variants in genes such as MTAP. Additionally, amplification was noted in TAZ and DKC1.

Anakinra, an IL-1 receptor antagonist, was initiated for recurrent pericarditis, leading to symptom resolution and normalization of inflammatory markers. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated resolution of constrictive physiology, although it recurred intermittently. After 2 weeks, repeat PET-CT showed reduced FDG uptake in the pericardium and intrathoracic lymph nodes, with near-complete resolution of pericardial and pleural effusions. The patient subsequently started palliative chemotherapy with carboplatin and pemetrexed.

Discussion

MPM is a rare neoplasm, representing <1% of all mesotheliomas.1 Diagnosing primary pericardial mesothelioma (PM) is challenging given its rarity, nonspecific presentation, and overlap with inflammatory and infectious pericardial conditions that also present with recurrent pericarditis, effusion, and constrictive physiology.1 This often leads to delayed or incorrect diagnosis. In the current case, recurrent pericarditis with episodic fevers, pleuritic chest pain, and elevated inflammatory markers initially suggested large-vessel vasculitis, supported by early imaging indicating aortitis. Concurrently, latent TB infection raised suspicion for TB pericarditis, a known cause of constrictive pericarditis. The patient received immunosuppressants and antitubercular therapy but remained symptomatic. Ultimately, metastatic disease was confirmed via endobronchial ultrasound–guided lymph node biopsy, highlighting the diagnostic challenge in distinguishing benign from malignant pericardial disease.4

Although FDG PET-CT can localize disease and guide biopsies, it cannot reliably differentiate inflammation from malignancy,5 as vasculitic and TB pericarditis can demonstrate FDG uptake.5 Histopathology adds further complexity owing to reactive mesothelial proliferations mimicking malignancy.4,6 In this case, cytology was initially negative, and biopsies showed atypical mesothelial proliferation, with definitive diagnosis only achieved through lymph node biopsy. The literature supports the low diagnostic yield of pericardial fluid cytology, with only 24% of cases showing malignant cells, and false-negative biopsy rates of ∼45%.4 Therefore, PM diagnosis requires high clinical suspicion and multidisciplinary evaluation. Cardiologists should consider malignancy in steroid-refractory or relapsing pericarditis, especially with nodular pericardial thickening, FDG-avid lesions, or persistent effusion. This case illustrates the importance of careful imaging and pathology interpretation as well as targeted biopsy with extensive immunohistochemical and molecular testing4,6 to avoid misdiagnosis. As such, we suggest an adapted and expanded algorithm in cases of atypical mesothelioma where histopathology is inconclusive and common tumor markers are absent (Figure 4).

Molecular profiling in this case revealed absence of BAP1 and CDKN2A (p16), commonly used to distinguish reactive mesothelial proliferation from malignant mesothelioma.7 In fact, loss of BAP1 expression has previously been reported in approximately 65% of epithelioid mesotheliomas,2 and homozygous deletion of CDKN2A by FISH is seen in over 90% of malignant mesothelioma, particularly pleural and peritoneal subtypes.3 Notably, 12% of cells in this case harbored CDKN2A deletion, below the standard diagnostic threshold (<20%), suggesting early genomic instability and mesothelial transformation, with molecular alteration preceding overt histologic malignancy.

However, PMs are under-represented in molecular studies, and their early genetic evolution is less well characterized. In this case, amplification of TAZ, a transcriptional coactivator regulated by the Hippo signaling pathway, was identified. The Hippo pathway plays a critical role in organ size control and tissue homeostasis by inhibiting TAZ and its paralog YAP (yes-associated protein) through phosphorylation, thereby preventing their nuclear localization and transcriptional activation.8 Dysregulated Hippo signaling results in TAZ accumulation, promoting cell proliferation, survival, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and tumorigenesis.8 Although the role of Hippo pathway aberrations is well documented in sarcomatoid and biphasic pleural mesotheliomas, with evidence linking them to oncogenesis and aggressive clinical behavior,8 data in PM are scarce. This case is among the first to report TAZ amplification in PM, providing novel evidence that Hippo pathway dysregulation may also underlie pathogenesis in this rare tumor subtype.

Collectively, intact BAP1 expression and subthreshold CDKN2A deletion in this case highlights the heterogeneity of molecular alterations in mesothelioma and suggests that TAZ amplification may represent a critical oncogenic driver in BAP1-intact tumors. As such, the Hippo-TAZ axis represents a promising therapeutic target for pericardial mesotheliomas with intact BAP1 and CDKN2A expression. Such pathway modulators, including inhibitors of TEAD (a TAZ-binding transcription factor), are currently in preclinical and early clinical trials for pleural mesothelioma,9 but they should also be considered for MPM. Given the high mortality associated with MPM and the limited efficacy of cisplatin/pemetrexed chemotherapy, our study suggests an alternative therapeutic pathway for PM.

Finally, the patient's response to IL-1 blockade (anakinra) raises questions about the role of inflammation in mesothelioma. Prior research shows that asbestos-induced pleural mesothelioma may respond to IL-1 inhibitors, indicating immunogenicity.10 This, combined with the current case, supports a role of inflammation in modulating therapy and suggests that YAP/TAZ amplification may serve as a biomarker for predicting mesothelial immunogenicity. Future studies should explore this, including why IL-1 blockade was effective when IL-6 inhibition was not, to further to guide targeted therapies for mesothelioma.

Conclusions

This case contributes to the limited MPM literature by highlighting the diagnostic challenges of PM presenting with concurrent pericarditis as well as identifying TAZ amplification and Hippo pathway involvement in tumor pathogenesis. These findings underscore the importance of molecular profiling in rare mesothelioma subtypes to inform diagnosis and treatment. Future research should explore the molecular landscape of PM further and investigate targeted therapies against Hippo-TAZ signaling components.
