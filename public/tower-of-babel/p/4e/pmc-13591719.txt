Single-cell transcriptomics reveals MAFB-driven macrophage reprogramming and immune divergence in recurrent glioblastoma
Authors: Yijing He, Jiaming Yu, Meng Huang, Peng Lu, Bo Liu, Chunxiao Zhang, Chuanxia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive and most common type of primary brain tumor, with poor prognosis despite standard therapies. The mechanisms driving recurrence remain poorly understood. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) dominate the GBM microenvironment and promote tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression, yet their activation states and transcriptional programs are not well defined.ResultsBy integrating large-scale single-cell transcriptomic datasets, we delineate profound immune microenvironmental divergence in recurrent GBM, characterized by the emergence of a distinct CXCL3⁺ tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) population. These CXCL3⁺ TAMs exhibit a robust pro-inflammatory cytokine program, intensified interactions with malignant and immunosuppressive immune cells, and a strong association with poor patient survival. Regulatory network analysis identifies MAFB as the central transcriptional regulator of this TAM state. MAFB is selectively enriched in macrophages, markedly upregulated in recurrent tumors, and strongly correlated with pro-inflammatory gene signatures. Immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunofluorescence further demonstrate an expanded population of MAFB⁺ mesenchymal-like TAMs in recurrent GBM. Depletion of MAFB in patient-derived GBM cells suppresses intracranial tumor growth and prolongs survival in vivo.ConclusionsOur study highlights profound immune remodeling in primary and recurrent GBM. We further define a MAFB-driven CXCL3⁺ macrophage program that shapes the immune landscape of recurrent GBM and underscore this axis as a promising target for microenvironmental reprogramming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16321-7.

Background

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and among the most aggressive primary malignant brain tumors in adults [1]. Despite maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide (TMZ), recurrence is nearly universal, and survival after relapse remains dismal [2]. Although O6-methylguanine–DNA methyltransferase (MGMT)–mediated repair of TMZ-induced lesions contributes to chemoresistance, this mechanism accounts for only a fraction of treatment failure [3–5]. The biology of recurrent GBM is far more complex, driven by tumor-intrinsic evolutionary pressures together with extensive remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) that collectively establish a therapy-resistant niche [6].

However, the molecular basis of chemoresistance in recurrent GBM remains incompletely understood and continues to be debated [7]. Genomic and transcriptomic studies have shown that recurrence is accompanied by dynamic reprogramming of cellular states, including enrichment of mesenchymal-like, therapy-resistant tumor populations [7–9]. Single-cell and single-nucleus analyses of matched primary and recurrent tumors further reveal transitions toward mesenchymal and inflammatory programs linked to invasion, immune dysfunction, and poor clinical outcome, as well as increased T-cell infiltration with heightened exhaustion signatures [9–11]. However, existing studies lack the scale or resolution to systematically dissect microenvironmental changes at recurrence [12]. Many single-cell datasets under-sample recurrent tumors or focus primarily on malignant cells, leaving the immune compartment—particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and T-cell subsets—poorly resolved. As a result, the identity of the immune cell states that emerge upon recurrence, and their mechanistic contribution to disease progression and patient outcome, remains incompletely defined.

Among the immune populations, TAMs are the most abundant immune cells, capable of constituting up to half of the tumor mass [13]. Macrophages exhibit remarkable functional plasticity, and recent studies have identified subsets with mesenchymal-like properties that facilitate extracellular matrix remodeling, promote angiogenesis, and sustain an immunosuppressive niche [14–16]. Recent single-cell and spatial analyses have revealed substantial TAM heterogeneity, spanning microglia-derived, monocyte-derived, mesenchymal-like, and metabolically specialized populations [17]. Monocyte-derived immunosuppressive TAMs appear to accumulate preferentially in recurrent GBM and have been implicated in resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy [18]. Yet the specific TAM subsets selectively engaged during recurrence, the transcriptional circuits that establish and maintain these states, and their crosstalk with T cells in shaping a therapy-refractory ecosystem remain largely unresolved. This gap has hindered the development of TAM-targeted therapies, such as CSF1Rinhibitors [19] and PI3Kγblockade [20], which show limited or context-dependent efficacy—underscoring the need for a deeper mechanistic understanding of TAM regulation in recurrent GBM.

To address these challenges, we integrated five publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets encompassing both primary and recurrent GBM, complemented by independent bulk transcriptomic cohorts and functional validation in patient-derived models. Through this multi-cohort analysis, we uncover extensive immune remodeling at recurrence, characterized by heightened T-cell exhaustion and expansion of regulatory T-cell populations. We further identify a CXCL3⁺ TAM subset with mesenchymal-like and tumor-promoting features that correlates with poor patient survival, and we delineate a MAFB-centered transcriptional network that governs this population. Together, these findings define a MAFB-driven CXCL3⁺ macrophage program that shapes the immune landscape of recurrent GBM and nominate MAFB-dependent TAM states as promising targets for therapeutic reprogramming of the GBM immune niche.

Results

Integrated single-cell atlas of primary and recurrent glioblastoma

To systematically define cellular heterogeneity in glioblastoma (GBM) and delineate microenvironmental differences between primary and recurrent disease, we constructed a unified single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) atlas by integrating five publicly available datasets using the Seurat workflow (Fig. 1A). After stringent quality control, we retained 273,098 high-quality cells from 62 patients, including 189,035 cells from primary tumors and 84,063 from recurrent tumors (Supplementary Table 1). Batch variation across studies was effectively mitigated using the Harmony algorithm. Following normalization and principal component analysis (Supplementary Table 2), we applied uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization and identified eight major cell populations based on canonical markers: T cells, B cells, macrophages, proliferating macrophages, fibroblasts, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, and tumor cells (Fig. 1B, C; Extended Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 3). Notably, primary and recurrent cells intermingled across clusters without batch-driven segregation (Extended Fig. 1B and C), supporting the robustness of the integrated atlas.

To interrogate recurrence-associated microenvironmental remodeling, we compared cell-type compositions between primary and recurrent tumors. Recurrent GBM exhibited a significantly increased proportion of T cells, whereas primary tumors were relatively enriched in tumor cells (Fig. 1D, E; Extended Fig. 2A). Moreover, T cells from recurrent tumors displayed markedly elevated exhaustion scores compared with those from primary GBM (Fig. 1F; Extended Fig. 2B), indicating acquisition of a more dysfunctional, immunosuppressive phenotype at recurrence. In parallel, cell-cycle analysis revealed a greater fraction of S/G2M-phase cells in recurrent samples, suggesting heightened proliferative dynamics or increased representation of transitional cell states within the recurrent microenvironment (Fig. 1G).

CD4⁺ regulatory T cells are expanded and exhibit enhanced checkpoint signaling in recurrent GBM

To more deeply resolve immune microenvironmental differences between primary and recurrent GBM, we further partitioned T cells into CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and other T cell subtypes (Fig. 2A, B). We then assessed the expression of key inhibitory receptors (IRs) across these subsets to identify populations potentially shaping responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade. CD4⁺ regulatory T (CD4+ Treg) cells exhibited markedly elevated expression of multiple IRs—including TNFRSF18, CTLA4, and TIGIT, which are canonical mediators of immunosuppression (Fig. 2C). Importantly, recurrent tumors harbored a substantially greater proportion of CD4⁺ Treg cells, together with higher IR expression levels, compared with primary tumors (Fig. 2D, E).

Differential expression analysis revealed extensive transcriptional reprogramming within the CD4⁺ Treg cells compartment at recurrence (Fig. 2F). Recurrent Treg cells upregulated immune checkpoint–associated genes (CTLA4, LAG3, RUNX3) and inflammatory mediators such as S100A9, consistent with an immunosuppressive and potentially dysfunctional phenotype. By contrast, CD4⁺ Treg cells from primary GBM expressed higher levels of cytoskeletal and translational regulators, including members of the SEPTIN family. Pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that CD4⁺ Treg cells in primary tumors were associated with antiviral defense responses, lipid complex remodeling, and neuronal projection regeneration, whereas recurrent Treg cells were enriched for pathways related to T-cell activation and migration, leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, and adaptive immune responses (Fig. 2G).

Distinct T-cell composition and proliferative trajectories in primary and recurrent GBM

We next compared T-cell subset distributions between primary and recurrent GBM (Fig. 3A). The analysis revealed striking tissue-associated patterns. CD8⁺ tissue-resident memory (Trm) cells, heat shock protein–associated (HSP) T cells, and natural killer T (NKT) cells were preferentially enriched in primary tumors, whereas CD4⁺ Treg cells and CD4⁺ naïve T cells predominated in recurrent tumors. Quantitative comparison confirmed significantly higher abundance of CD8⁺ Trm1/2 subsets in primary GBM (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that primary tumors maintain more robust effector-memory and proliferative CD8⁺ T-cell compartments, whereas recurrent tumors are characterized by expansion of immunoregulatory CD4⁺ subsets and depletion of CD8⁺ effector populations.

To investigate how CD8⁺ T-cell states diverge across disease stages, we reconstructed differentiation trajectories using Slingshot (Fig. 3C, D). Two major lineages emerged, reflecting distinct CD8⁺ T-cell activation states. The majority of CD8⁺ T cells occupied lineage 1, forming a continuum from less activated states toward a terminal proliferative phenotype. Mapping exhaustion-, proliferation-, and cytotoxicity-associated gene signatures (Supplementary Table 4) along this trajectory revealed strong enrichment of proliferation-associated markers at the terminal branch, indicating the presence of a proliferative CD8⁺ T-cell subset within lineage 1 (Fig. 3E). To further characterize the exhaustion program, we examined the expression of canonical transcriptional regulators including TOX and EOMES. Both genes exhibited overall low expression levels across T-cell subsets, including the exhausted CD8⁺ T-cell population. Despite their established roles in terminal exhaustion, their low expression in our dataset suggests that the dysfunctional phenotype observed here may not fully correspond to a classical TOX/EOMES-driven terminal exhaustion state (Extended Fig. 2C).

To define signaling pathways underlying this proliferative phenotype, we performed pathway activation inference. CD8⁺ T-cell proliferation was significantly positively associated with G2/M checkpoint activity and showed partial enrichment of MYC target genes (Fig. 3F), consistent with known roles of MYC in driving cell-cycle progression through coordinated regulation of cell cycle–related gene networks.

CXCL3⁺ tumor-associated macrophages define a pro-tumorigenic program in GBM

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are major regulators of the GBM immune microenvironment and influence T-cell dysfunction and therapeutic resistance through pathways such as MHC-II–mediated antigen presentation, IL-10/TGF-β signaling, CCL2/CXCL chemokine axes, CSF1–CSF1R interactions, and the SIRPα–CD47 checkpoint. To characterize TAM heterogeneity and identify recurrence-associated states, we re-clustered 150,035 TAMs, resolving nine transcriptionally distinct subsets, including CXCL3⁺, CXCL10⁺, and E2F8⁺ TAMs (Fig. 4A). All subsets were detectable in both primary and recurrent tumors (Fig. 4B). However, their distributions showed clear tissue biases: RRAGC⁺, GZMA⁺, and FYB⁺ TAMs were enriched in primary GBM, whereas FCER1A⁺, E2F8⁺, DLL3⁺, and CXCL3+ populations were markedly expanded in recurrent disease (Fig. 4C). These patterns suggest a shift from relatively immune-activated macrophage phenotypes in primary tumors toward immunosuppressive and tumor-supportive states at recurrence.

To further characterize the functional heterogeneity of TAM subsets, we compared the expression of immune suppressive receptors (IRs), inflammatory factors, and MHC-I/II–related genes across different TAM subsets. Interestingly, IR markers such as PDCD1, CTLA4, TIGIT, and HAVCR2 were prominently expressed in GZMA⁺ TAMs (Fig. 4D), which were primarily enriched in primary GBM. In contrast, we previously observed that these IR markers are mainly upregulated in CD4⁺ Treg cells from recurrent GBM. This apparent difference may reflect cell type–specific and stage-dependent immune regulation, suggesting that during disease progression, immunosuppressive programs may shift from myeloid-driven to lymphocyte-driven mechanisms. Among these subsets, CXCL3⁺ TAMs displayed a unique transcriptional profile characterized by high expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines coupled with mesenchymal-like gene signatures (Fig. 4E), consistent with a hybrid inflammatory–mesenchymal state that may promote tumor progression and reinforce an immunosuppressive niche.

To evaluate their clinical significance, we analyzed macrophage gene signatures in the TCGA-GBM cohort. A high CXCL3⁺ TAM signature score was strongly associated with poorer overall survival (Fig. 4F), underscoring the prognostic relevance of this subset and suggesting that CXCL3⁺ TAMs contribute to an aggressive, therapy-resistant microenvironment.

Differential tumor cell states between primary and recurrent GBM

To further dissect tumor-intrinsic heterogeneity contributing to disease progression, we analyzed tumor cells and identified seven transcriptionally distinct sub-clusters (Fig. 5A, B). Among these, cluster C0 was significantly enriched in recurrent tumors, whereas cluster C4 and C6 predominated in primary GBM (Fig. 5C). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that C0 cells were strongly associated with glial lineage programs and central nervous system developmental pathways, suggesting lineage-specific remodeling and state transitions accompanying tumor recurrence (Fig. 5D).

Cell–cell communication analysis revealed a markedly altered interaction landscape between primary and recurrent GBM (Fig. 5E). Across the global signaling network, CXCL3⁺ TAMs emerged as prominent communication hubs, engaging in extensive bidirectional interactions with multiple T-cell subsets—particularly CD8⁺ TEM/Cyto and CD4⁺ Treg cells. Several immune-regulatory interactions exhibited higher signaling strength in primary tumors compared with recurrent tumors, indicating dynamic rewiring of immune–tumor crosstalk as GBM evolves under therapeutic pressure.

Strikingly, CellChat inference of the MHC-II signaling pathway demonstrated a broad reduction in antigen-presenting interactions among TAM subsets in recurrent GBM, whereas MHC-II–directed signaling toward CD4⁺ Treg cells was selectively enhanced (Fig. 5F). These findings suggest that recurrence is accompanied by functional reprogramming of antigen presentation, shifting from broadly immune-stimulatory interactions toward preferential engagement of immune-regulatory T-cell populations, thus promoting an increasingly suppressive and therapy-resistant microenvironment.

MAFB regulates mesenchymal and immune programs in CXCL3⁺ TAMs

Transcription factors (TFs) orchestrate cellular identity and functional states. To identify regulatory networks underlying TAM and tumor cell phenotypes, we applied SCENIC to infer TF regulons across GBM populations. This analysis revealed highly distinct TF signatures: KLF4, CEBPB, MAFB, ETS2, and FOSB were specifically enriched in CXCL3⁺ TAMs, whereas HOXB3, POU3F3, SMAD1, and FOXG1 emerged as dominant regulators within the C0 cluster (Fig. 6A). Regulon activity scoring further highlighted TFEC, MAFB, and KLF4 as master regulators in CXCL3⁺ TAMs, with MAFB also being the major regulator of GZMA+ TAMs (Fig. 6B).

Among these regulators, MAFB emerged as a central node controlling the mesenchymal-like transcriptional program of CXCL3⁺ TAMs. Consistent with this regulatory role, MAFB expression was predominantly macrophage-restricted and significantly elevated in recurrent GBM (Fig. 6C, D). Correlation analysis demonstrated strong positive associations between MAFB and pro-inflammatory genes (Fig. 6E), indicating that MAFB coordinates inflammatory signaling within TAMs.

Interestingly, along the pseudotime trajectory of lineage 1 CD8⁺ T cells, MAFB-associated gene expression patterns paralleled proliferation-related trends (Fig. 6F). Although MAFB is expressed lowly in T cells, these correlated patterns suggest that MAFB-dependent TAM programs may modulate the proliferative and functional states of neighboring CD8⁺ T cells, highlighting a potential role for MAFB in macrophage–T-cell crosstalk. Functional pathway analysis revealed that MAFB-high CXCL3⁺ macrophages exhibit enhanced antigen-presentation signaling, particularly within the MHC-II pathway, accompanied by increased macrophage–T cell communication strength (Extended Fig. 3A-C). Multiplex immunofluorescence results showed that MAFB⁺ macrophages were primarily localized in the perivascular region. These cells were mainly distributed in the GFAP⁺ tumor area, suggesting enrichment in the tumor parenchyma rather than the invasive periphery, and showed no significant spatial association with CD3⁺ T cells (Extended Fig. 4). These findings suggest that MAFB may potentiate immunoregulatory macrophage functions rather than merely marking their presence.

MAFB inhibition suppresses GBM progression in vivo

To define the functional contribution of MAFB to the immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting microenvironment of GBM, we first examined its expression in paired primary and recurrent human tumor specimens. Immunohistochemistry revealed markedly elevated MAFB protein levels in recurrent tumors compared with matched primary lesions, consistent with transcriptomic analyses identifying MAFB upregulation as a recurrence-associated signature (Fig. 7A). Multiplex immunofluorescence using macrophage marker (CD68), Epithelial–Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) marker (Vimentin, N-cadherin, and E-cadherin) further demonstrated that MAFB localized predominantly to mesenchymal-like TAMs in recurrent tissues (Fig. 7B, Extended Fig. 5A and C), reinforcing its role as a key regulator of TAM state transitions.

(F) Kaplan–Meier survival curves of mice implanted with control or MAFB-knockdown GSC5 cells, indicating prolonged survival following MAFB depletion (n = 6 per group). P values were determined using the log-rank test. (***P < 0.001). (G) Representative hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained brain sections from xenograft-bearing mice, with tumor boundaries outlined. Reduced tumor size is evident in MAFB-knockdown groups. Scale bar: 1 mm

We knocked down MAFB in GSC5 glioma stem cells (Fig. 7C), and found that MAFB depletion significantly impaired sphere formation and self-renewal (Extended Fig. 5D and E). We next assessed the functional consequences of MAFB loss using intracranial xenograft models. Implantation of patient-derived GSC5 glioma stem cells stably expressing MAFB-targeting shRNA resulted in significantly reduced tumor growth and prolonged survival relative to control mice (Fig. 7D–F). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining confirmed markedly smaller tumor masses in the MAFB-silenced group (Fig. 7G), establishing MAFB as a critical driver of GBM progression in vivo.

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of MAFB, we analyzed independent transcriptomic datasets. MAFB expression was substantially elevated in GBM compared with normal brain tissue and showed additional upregulation in recurrent tumors (Extended Fig. 6A, B). Importantly, higher MAFB expression was significantly associated with shorter overall survival (Extended Fig. 6C), underscoring its prognostic importance and supporting a model in which MAFB-driven TAM programs contribute to an aggressive, therapy-resistant disease state.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated five publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets with independent bulk transcriptomic profiles and experimental validation to generate a comprehensive atlas of the immune microenvironment in primary and recurrent GBM. This integrative approach revealed extensive remodeling of both T-cell and myeloid compartments during recurrence, identified a recurrence-enriched CXCL3⁺ TAM population with mesenchymal-like features, and uncovered a MAFB-centered regulatory network that links macrophage state transitions to tumor progression and poor clinical outcome (Fig. 8, Supplementary Table 5). Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic insight into how therapeutic pressure drives immune dysfunction in GBM and highlight MAFB-driven CXCL3⁺ TAMs as compelling targets for microenvironmental reprogramming.

Fig. 8Differential signaling networks underlying immune remodeling in primary and recurrent GBM

Differential signaling networks underlying immune remodeling in primary and recurrent GBM

A central observation of this work is the marked shift in T-cell composition and phenotype between primary and recurrent tumors. Primary GBM contained relatively abundant CD8⁺ tissue-resident memory and proliferative T-cell populations, whereas recurrent disease was characterized by expansion of CD4⁺ regulatory T cells and naïve CD4⁺ subsets accompanied by diminished effector CD8⁺ representation. Pseudotime analysis further delineated a trajectory in which CD8⁺ T cells transition from tissue-resident memory states toward a proliferative, MYC-associated phenotype with reduced inflammatory signaling. In parallel, CD4⁺ Treg cells in recurrent GBM exhibited increased frequency and heightened expression of multiple immune checkpoint receptors—including CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT—signatures consistent with potent immunosuppressive activity. These features collectively define a microenvironment in which T cells display transcriptional hallmarks of activation yet are functionally constrained by exhaustion and regulatory programs. The shift from an effector-dominant landscape in primary tumors to a more immune-regulatory configuration in recurrent disease likely reflects adaptive immune evasion under therapeutic pressure and suggests that immunotherapeutic strategies for recurrent GBM will require simultaneous reinvigoration of exhausted CD8⁺ T cells and attenuation of Treg-mediated suppression.

Our analysis of macrophage heterogeneity underscores the central role of myeloid cells in recurrence-associated immune remodeling. We identified multiple transcriptionally distinct TAM subsets with clear tissue biases, among which CXCL3⁺ TAMs were preferentially expanded in recurrent tumors. These cells displayed a paradoxical hybrid phenotype characterized by high expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines alongside mesenchymal-like transcriptional programs, yet reduced expression of MHC class I and II molecules. This combination—chronic inflammatory signaling coexisting with impaired antigen presentation—suggests a tumor-supportive macrophage state capable of promoting angiogenesis, invasion, and extracellular matrix remodeling while attenuating effective anti-tumor T-cell priming. Cell–cell communication analysis positioned CXCL3⁺ TAMs as major signaling hubs in recurrent GBM, engaging extensively with tumor cells, exhausted or cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells, and CD4⁺ Treg cells. Notably, MHC-II–mediated interactions were redistributed away from broad antigen-presenting networks toward preferential engagement of Tregs in recurrent tumors, further reinforcing an immunoregulatory niche.

Through SCENIC-based regulatory network inference, we identified MAFB as a critical transcription factor defining the CXCL3⁺ TAM state. MAFB regulon activity was selectively enriched in CXCL3⁺ TAMs, and MAFB expression was predominantly localized to macrophages, with significantly higher levels in recurrent compared with primary GBM. Correlation analyses linked MAFB with inflammatory gene signatures, suggesting that MAFB integrates immune-regulatory and mesenchymal programs in a context-dependent manner. MAFB⁺ macrophages preferentially localize in the perivascular region, suggesting that they may play a role in the vascular-associated microenvironment of the tumor microenvironment. Immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed that MAFB protein is enriched in mesenchymal-like TAMs in human recurrent GBM, and functional studies using intracranial xenograft models demonstrated that MAFB knockdown in patient-derived glioma stem cells reduced tumor burden and prolonged survival. These findings, together with the association between high MAFB expression and poor clinical outcome, support a model in which MAFB-driven transcriptional programs promote tumor-supportive macrophage states that facilitate recurrence.

Our results have several implications for the development of immunomodulatory therapies in GBM. First, the coexistence of T-cell activation signatures with high levels of inhibitory receptors and Treg expansion suggests that strategies focusing solely on enhancing T-cell infiltration are unlikely to succeed in recurrent disease. Instead, effective approaches will require combinatorial therapies that simultaneously reinvigorate cytotoxic T cells, restrain Treg-mediated suppression, and reprogram TAMs away from immunosuppressive and mesenchymal-like phenotypes. Second, the identification of CXCL3⁺ TAMs as a recurrence-enriched, pro-tumorigenic subset raises the possibility that targeting CXCL3-associated signaling pathways or their downstream effectors could disrupt inflammatory circuits that support invasion and therapeutic resistance. Third, the central role of MAFB in orchestrating CXCL3⁺ TAM identity highlights this transcription factor—and its upstream regulators and downstream pathways—as an attractive focus for macrophage-directed interventions (Supplementary Table 6). Although direct pharmacologic inhibition of transcription factors remains challenging, targeting the epigenetic and signaling networks controlled by MAFB may be more feasible and could synergize with immune checkpoint blockade or radiotherapy. However, this study has inherent limitations. First, the single cell datasets analyzed here were derived from multiple public repositories, and variations in sample processing, sequencing depth, and library preparation across studies may influence the detection of certain cellular states. Second, spatial transcriptomic data were not included, which limits our ability to fully resolve the spatial organization of the tumor microenvironment. Third, although we performed in vivo modeling and immunofluorescence staining to validate key findings, some regulatory relationships remain to be elucidated through more detailed mechanistic experiments. The translational relevance of our findings would benefit from validation in more physiologically relevant experimental models, such as co-culture systems or in vivo models recapitulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Despite these limitations, the major trends identified in our integrated analysis remained robust. Future studies incorporating larger clinical cohorts and spatially resolved or cell type–specific perturbation approaches will be essential to further validate the MAFB-driven TAM transcriptional program in tumor recurrence.

Conclusions

In summary, our study delineates the immune microenvironmental divergence between primary and recurrent GBM at single-cell resolution and identifies a MAFB-driven CXCL3⁺ TAM population as a central orchestrator of recurrence-associated immune dysfunction. By linking specific T-cell and macrophage states to regulatory networks and clinical outcome, we provide a conceptual and mechanistic framework for understanding immune failure in recurrent GBM and for developing microenvironment-focused therapeutic strategies. Targeting MAFB-dependent TAM programs—together with approaches to restore cytotoxic T-cell function and limit Treg suppression—may offer a promising avenue to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in this highly refractory disease.

Methods

Ethics statement

All research described in this study was conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national ethical guidelines. Animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Guangdong Academy of Medical Sciences) under approval number KY2024-1011-01, and all procedures adhered to institutional standards for animal welfare. The permitted maximum tumor size was 2,000 mm³, and this threshold was not exceeded in any experiment. Human sample collection and use were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approval number 202103150) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Cell culture and clinical samples

HEK293T cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCellBio) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Cat. no. 15140163).

All tumor samples were obtained from GBM patients undergoing surgical resection at Xiangya Hospital. Tumor tissues were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions and cultured under serum-free, stem cell–permissive conditions to enrich for glioblastoma stem-like cells (GSCs), as previously described [21]. Briefly, freshly resected tumor tissues were minced and digested with DNase I (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) and collagenase IV (Gibco, Vacaville, CA, USA). Resulting cells were plated in ultra-low attachment six-well plates (Corning) and maintained in Neurobasal medium (Gibco, Cat. no. 21103049) supplemented with 20 ng ml⁻¹ bFGF (PeproTech, Cat. no. 100-18B-500), 20 ng ml⁻¹ EGF (PeproTech, Cat. no. AF-100-15-500), GlutaMAX (Invitrogen), and B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. no. 12587010). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and passaged every 2–5 days.

Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored using the MycoAlert Detection Kit (Lonza), and all cell lines used in this study were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

GSC5, derived from a female GBM patient, was characterized by the following molecular and immunohistochemical profile: MGMT unmethylated, GFAP (+), Ki67 (~ 40%), P53 (−), IDH1 (−), H3K27M (−), Olig2 (+), EGFR (+), and ATRX (+).

Acquisition of scRNA-Seq and bulk datasets

Tumor samples were selected based on pathologically confirmed GBM diagnosis without other malignancies and sufficient tissue availability for single-cell RNA sequencing. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets of glioblastoma were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession numbers GSE182109 [22], GSE103224 [23], GSE138794 [24], GSE139448 [25], and GSE162631 [26]. A total of 62 samples were included in the analysis, comprising 42 primary GBM and 20 recurrent GBM specimens (Supplementary Table 1). Bulk RNA-seq datasets were acquired from GEO (GSE145645 [27], GSE222515 [28]) and from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).

All scRNA-seq datasets were processed and integrated using the Seurat R package [29] (version 5.0.1). For each sample, genes detected in fewer than three cells were removed, and cells containing fewer than 200 expressed genes or more than 10% mitochondrial transcript content were excluded. Low-quality cells and doublets were removed using scDblFinder, resulting in a curated dataset of 273,098 high-quality cells. Highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function, and the top 2,000 most variable genes were retained for principal component analysis (PCA). Gene expression values were then normalized, scaled, and integrated across datasets using the RunHarmony [30] workflow to mitigate batch effects. Dimensionality reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with a clustering resolution of 0.6.

Cell identities were assigned based on canonical marker expression. T cells were defined by CD3D and CD3E; B cells by CD79A and CD79B; endothelial cells by CLDN5 and VWF; oligodendrocytes by TF and MAG; macrophages by FCER1G and TYROBP; proliferating macrophages by MKI67 and TOP2A; fibroblasts by COL1A1 and DCN; and tumor cells by PTPRZ1 and SOX2. Annotation consistency was verified across datasets to ensure robust classification.

Differential expression and enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between cell clusters were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat’s FindMarkers function, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they exhibited a minimum log₂ fold change ≥ 1 and a Bonferroni-adjusted P value < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were performed using the clusterProfiler package [31]. The Hallmark gene sets (h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and used as the reference for enrichment analyses. Pathways with an adjusted P value (p_adj) below 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle phase assignment was conducted using the Seurat package. Canonical S-phase and G2/M-phase marker genes (Seurat::cc.genes.updated.2019) were applied via the CellCycleScoring function to compute cell cycle scores for each cell and classify them into G1, S, or G2/M phases based on relative expression patterns. The proportion of cells in each phase was subsequently quantified across sample groups to evaluate differences in proliferative states.

Trajectory analysis

Trajectory inference for CD8⁺ T cells was performed using the Slingshot package [32] (version 2.8.0). CD8⁺ T cells were extracted from the Seurat object and converted into a SingleCellExperiment object prior to analysis. Slingshot was applied to the UMAP embedding to infer developmental lineages and estimate pseudotime distributions. The distribution of CD8⁺ T-cell subsets along the inferred pseudotime trajectories was visualized using ridge density plots. Lineage paths were subsequently overlaid onto the UMAP embedding, with distinct colors assigned to each CD8⁺ T-cell subset to facilitate visualization of differentiation trajectories.

Cell–Cell interaction analysis

Cell–cell communication analysis was conducted using the CellChat package [33] (version 2.1.2) in R. Separate CellChat objects were generated for primary and recurrent GBM samples, and ligand–receptor interactions were inferred using the default CellChatDB database. Overexpressed signaling genes and interactions were identified with the identifyOverExpressedGenes and identifyOverExpressedInteractions functions. Communication probabilities were calculated using computeCommunProb and subsequently aggregated into pathway-level signaling using computeCommunProbPathway. Interaction networks were summarized with aggregateNet, and cell-type centrality metrics were computed using netAnalysis_computeCentrality to characterize the contribution of each cell population to global signaling structure. To compare signaling differences between primary and recurrent tumors, the two CellChat objects were merged using mergeCellChat, and differential interaction strengths were visualized. Specific pathways of interest, including MHC-II signaling, were displayed using circular layout plots to facilitate intuitive interpretation of intercellular communication patterns. Cell–cell communication strength and signaling centrality were quantified using the CellChat package. Outgoing signaling strength and overall information flow were compared between CXCL3⁺ MAFB-high and CXCL3⁺ MAFB-low macrophage states.

Gene regulatory network inference and regulon activity analysis

Gene regulatory network reconstruction and transcription factor (TF) activity profiling were performed using the SCENIC workflow [34], implemented with pySCENIC (v0.11.2) in Python (version 3.8) and subsequently processed in R. To reduce technical noise, genes expressed in fewer than 100 cells were removed prior to analysis, after which the filtered expression matrix was converted into .loom format for input into pySCENIC. Co-expression modules were inferred using the grnboost2 algorithm with a curated list of human TFs (allTFs_hg38.txt). These initial modules were refined through cis-regulatory motif enrichment analysis using the cisTarget database. Cell-level regulon activity scores were then quantified using the AUCell algorithm.

The resulting aucell.loom file was imported into R for integration with single-cell metadata. Regulon activity matrices were extracted using the get_regulons_AUC function in the AUCell (version 1.22.0) package. To quantify cell type specificity, Regulon Specificity Scores (RSS) were computed using the calcRSS function, where higher RSS values indicate stronger enrichment of a regulon within a given cell population. All data visualizations were generated using standard R plotting workflows, and intermediate files were retained to ensure full reproducibility.

Construction of cluster signature scores and prognostic evaluation

To assess the prognostic relevance of distinct cellular subpopulations, marker genes for each cluster were identified from the integrated single-cell dataset. Marker selection criteria included: (i) significantly elevated expression within a given subset (avg_log₂FC > 0.25); (ii) expression in at least 25% of cells (pct.1 > 0.25); and (iii) statistical significance after multiple testing correction (adjusted P < 0.05). For each patient in the TCGA-GBM cohort, the mean expression level of marker genes corresponding to each subpopulation was used to calculate a signature score.

Prognostic associations were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression, implemented with the survival R package (version 3.5-5). For each signature score, hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals, and P values were extracted to determine the prognostic significance of specific cellular subpopulation signatures.

Immunohistochemistry assay

Paraffin-embedded GBM tissue sections were baked at 60 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene (10 min × 2), and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100% × 2, 95%, 80%, 75%) to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer using a microwave for 8 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, then incubated with 3% H₂O₂ for 30 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against MAFB (Proteintech, Cat. no. 20189-1-AP), washed three times in PBS, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. no. 7074). Signal was developed using DAB, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, differentiation in 1% HCl/75% ethanol, rinsing, dehydration through graded ethanol and xylene, and mounting with neutral resin.

Multiplexed immunofluorescence assay

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized using an eco-friendly xylene substitute and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Servicebio, WGXFHA0008), followed by PBS washes (pH 7.4). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% H₂O₂ in methanol for 25 min at room temperature. After encircling tissues with a hydrophobic barrier pen, sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min and sequentially incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min at room temperature. After each antibody cycle, slides were washed with PBS and subjected to tyramide signal amplification (TSA), with antibody stripping performed between rounds to enable multiplex staining. Following the final staining round, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min, and autofluorescence was quenched using a commercial reagent. Slides were mounted with anti-fade medium and imaged using whole-slide scanning (3DHISTECH). Antibodies included anti-MAFB (Proteintech, 20189-1-AP), anti-CD68 (HUABIO, HA601115), anti-Vimentin (HUABIO, EM0401), E-cadherin (Servicebio, GB12083), N-cadherin (Servicebio, GB15135), GFAP (Servicebio, GB15096), CD31 (Servicebio, GB150217), CD3 (Servicebio, GB13014), HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG (CST, 7074), and HRP-linked anti-mouse IgG (CST, 7076).

Lentiviral production and generation of knockdown cell lines

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with psPAX2, pVSVG, and shRNA-expressing plasmids using StarFect transfection reagent (Genstar, Cat. no. C101), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plasmid ratio used for transfection was 5:3:2 (shRNA: psPAX2:pVSVG). At 8 h post-transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh GSC culture medium. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h after transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm filters (Biosharp), and supplemented with 10 µg ml⁻¹ polybrene (Sigma) to enhance infection efficiency.

Glioblastoma stem-like cells (GSCs) were infected with lentiviral supernatants at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10–20 for 24 h. After infection, cells were cultured in fresh medium for at least 48 h. When robust GFP fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope, cells were subjected to puromycin selection (2–5 µg ml⁻¹, Sigma) for 5–7 days to establish stable knockdown cell lines. Knockdown efficiency was validated by immunoblotting analysis at least three independent biological replicates.

An empty vector control (pLV3-U6-CopGFP) was used as a negative control. Two independent shRNAs targeting MAFB were used to minimize potential off-target effects. Immunoblotting assay

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. no. 89900) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (GLPbio, Cat. no. GK10009). Equal amounts of protein were separated by electrophoresis on 4–12% gradient gels (FuturePAGE, ACE) in MOPS-SDS running buffer at 90 V for 90 min, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, Cat. no. 162–0177). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, washed, and probed with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (NCM Biotech, Cat. no. P10300), and signals were captured using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Antibodies included anti-MAFB (Proteintech, 20189-1-AP), anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A2228), HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG (CST, 7074), and HRP-linked anti-mouse IgG (CST, 7076).

Neurosphere formation assays

1,000 GSC cells were plated into ultra-low attachment 96-well plates (Corning, Cat. no. 3474). After incubation for 7 days, neurosphere formation was evaluated using a ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager (Bio-Rad). Quantification and analysis of neurosphere areas were performed using ImageJ software.

Limiting dilution assay

Tumour cells were plated at different densities into 96-well ultra-low attachment plates. After incubation for 14 days, the frequency of stem-cell formation was determined using Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis software (available from https://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/).

In vivo orthotopic GBM mouse model

GSCs expressing shRNA constructs were stereotactically implanted into the brains of 6–8-week-old male NSG mice (Nuohang Biotechnology). A total of 5 µl of cell suspension was injected at a rate of 1.5 µl min⁻¹ at the following coordinates relative to bregma: 1 mm anterior, 2 mm lateral, and 3 mm depth from the cortical surface. Tumor progression was monitored by bioluminescence imaging following intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin under isoflurane anesthesia. On day 21 post-implantation, mice were euthanized, and brains were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

For survival analyses, mice were observed daily, and humane endpoints were defined as the onset of neurological impairment, > 20% body weight loss, or signs of severe distress. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to evaluate survival differences between groups, and significance was assessed using the log-rank test (GraphPad Prism v9; p < 0.05). All mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions on a 12-h light–dark cycle, with autoclaved food, bedding, and water provided ad libitum. All procedures involving animals complied with institutional and national guidelines for laboratory animal care.

Statistical analyses

No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes; our sample sizes were chosen based on standards established in prior publications. Data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. Statistical tests used for each experiment are specified explicitly within the corresponding figure legends. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1), GraphPad Prism v9 and ImageJ (v1.54). For normally distributed data comparing two groups, unpaired two-sided Student’s t-tests (Welch’s t-test when appropriate) were applied. For multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method or ANOVA, as appropriate. Correlations between immune-related genes and MAFB expression were assessed using Spearman correlation. Kaplan–Meier survival estimates were obtained using the survfit function from the survival R package, and corresponding curves were visualized using the ggsurvplot function from the survminer package. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.
