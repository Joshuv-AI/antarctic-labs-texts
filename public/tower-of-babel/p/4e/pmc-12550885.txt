Levofloxacin-induced myopathy of the iliac and gluteal muscles
Authors: Yukiko Kato, Takahiko Tsutsumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLevofloxacin-induced myopathy is a relatively under-recognized adverse effect. Here, we present a case of myopathy affecting the iliac and gluteal muscles caused by the administration of levofloxacin. A man in the 70s was admitted to the hospital with bilateral thigh pain and difficulty walking following levofloxacin use. Creatine kinase levels were not elevated, but MRI revealed inflammation in the iliac and gluteal muscles. His symptoms improved after levofloxacin discontinuation, and he may have had levofloxacin-induced myopathy. This case report seeks to raise awareness of levofloxacin-induced myopathy by adding to the small number of reports in the literature. Levofloxacin-induced myopathy should be considered in the differential diagnosis when there are musculoskeletal symptoms in elderly patients receiving levofloxacin.Keywords: Musculoskeletal and joint disorders, Drugs and medicines

Background

Levofloxacin is a widely used antibiotic valued for its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and excellent tissue penetration. It’s often used to treat various infections, including community-acquired pneumonia and urinary tract infections.1 Severe adverse effects of levofloxacin include QT interval prolongation and seizures.2 3 Reported musculoskeletal side effects commonly include Achilles tendinitis and tendon rupture.4 In contrast, reports of levofloxacin-induced myopathy remain scarce,5 6 indicating a potential under-recognition of this condition and highlighting the need for increased vigilance. Herein, we present a case of myopathy involving the iliac and gluteal muscles in an elderly patient after levofloxacin administration.

Case presentation

A man in the 70s who had been undergoing dialysis for end-stage renal disease was referred to our hospital diagnosed with appendicitis. Initial treatment was conservative with intravenous cefmetazole, a third-generation cephalosporin. Cefmetazole was administered at a dose of 1 g every 24 hours after dialysis sessions on dialysis days, for a total of 6 days. Thereafter, therapy was switched to oral levofloxacin and metronidazole. Levofloxacin was administered at an initial dose of 500 mg on a non-dialysis day. On the second day, there was no administration, and the patient underwent routine haemodialysis in the afternoon. On the third day, a reduced dose of 250 mg was given. Metronidazole was prescribed at a dose of 500 mg three times daily from the beginning. All antibiotic dosages were appropriate for a patient undergoing dialysis.

The day after the initial levofloxacin dose, he developed bilateral thigh and heel pain. The next day, the pain had progressed to the extent of causing difficulty in walking. On the fourth day, at the scheduled outpatient visit, levofloxacin was discontinued for suspected drug-induced myalgia. Subsequently, he was referred to the internal medicine department due to persistent pain and difficulty walking.

His medical history was significant for end-stage renal disease secondary to polycystic kidney disease necessitating dialysis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hypertension and insomnia. His regular medications included carvedilol, methyldopa, ramelteon and suvorexant. Family history was unavailable.

On presentation, his vital signs were: blood pressure 172/72 mmHg, pulse rate 78 bpm, body temperature 36.7°C, respiratory rate 16 breaths/min and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Abdominal examination revealed no tenderness or rebound tenderness. To assess for recurrence of appendicitis, iliopsoas signs were evaluated. The iliopsoas resistance test was positive, whereas the iliopsoas extension test was negative, suggesting the pain was not due to appendicitis. There was no significant muscle weakness or tenderness on leg palpation, and the straight leg raise test was negative. The Achilles tendons exhibited no tenderness, and there were no cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis, such as the shawl and Gottron signs. The shawl sign is a photosensitive erythematous rash over the upper back, while the Gottron sign refers to erythematous macules or papules over the extensor surfaces of joints such as the elbows and knees.

Investigations

Laboratory findings were as follows: white blood cell count of 5800/μL with a neutrophil percentage of 78.5% and lymphocytes at 10.8%, haemoglobin level of 9.2 g/dL, haematocrit of 30.4% and platelet count of 283,000/μL. Blood urea nitrogen was 36 mg/dL and serum creatinine was 5.7 mg/dL. Electrolytes, including sodium, potassium, phosphorus and calcium, were within normal ranges. The creatine kinase level was 188 U/L. Liver enzymes and thyroid function were normal. Abdominal plain CT revealed enlargement of the bilateral iliopsoas muscles without evidence of worsening appendiceal wall thickening or increased fat density around the appendix. Further MRI evaluation demonstrated high signal intensity in the iliac and gluteal muscles on short T1 inversion recovery (STIR) images (figures1 2). Anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase antibodies testing for dermatomyositis/polymyositis was negative.

Differential diagnosis

Physical examination and laboratory findings suggested a low likelihood of electrolyte disturbances or endocrine disorders as the cause. Due to the confinement of symptoms to the proximal muscles, evaluation by electromyography was challenging, and the anatomical location made muscle biopsy impractical. MRI findings indicated inflammation of the iliopsoas and gluteal muscles, suggestive of an underlying myopathy. The Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale7 yielded a score of 4, indicating a possible drug-induced myopathy, so discontinuation of levofloxacin was maintained.

Treatment

Levofloxacin therapy was discontinued, and pain management was administered using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and acetaminophen. Rehabilitation was promptly begun during the early phase of hospitalisation.

Outcome and follow-up

Symptoms steadily improved, and follow-up MRI 6 weeks later showed significant resolution of high signal areas on STIR imaging (figures3 4). Myalgia completely resolved 8 weeks after onset with no recurrence to date.

Discussion

Levofloxacin is one of the prototypical fluoroquinolone antibiotics. In this section, we discuss the case with reference to the wider evidence on the fluoroquinolone class. The pathophysiology of fluoroquinolone-induced myopathy remains inadequately understood. However, there have recently been reports identifying myopathy as a clinical manifestation.8 A PubMed search restricted to the title/abstract field using the terms ‘myopathy’ or ‘rhabdomyolysis’ in combination with ‘levofloxacin’, ‘fluoroquinolone’, ‘ciprofloxacin’, ‘moxifloxacin’, ‘ofloxacin’, ‘norfloxacin’, ‘delafloxacin’, ‘gemifloxacin’, ‘gatifloxacin’ or ‘quinolone’, together with a title-restricted Google Scholar search using the same keywords, identified 13 reports in which detailed information relevant to the present case could be confirmed, eight of which involved levofloxacin (data as of August 2025).69,20

Drug-induced myopathy arises through various mechanisms, including direct myotoxicity, immune-mediated inflammatory myopathy, indirect muscle damage or a combination of multiple mechanisms.21 22 The precise mechanism of fluoroquinolone-induced myopathy is not known, but it is hypothesised that abnormal muscle metabolism caused by fluorine compounds may play a role.23 Additionally, it is suggested that the mechanism underlying fluoroquinolone-induced tendinitis may also be applicable to myopathy.4 This hypothesis posits that fluoroquinolones impair integrin function in musculoskeletal tissues by chelating magnesium ions or induction of direct cytotoxic effects through increased production of reactive oxygen species and matrix metalloproteinases involved in tendon cell remodelling.24,26

A recent observational study identified risk factors for fluoroquinolone-induced adverse events, including advanced age, renal impairment, organ transplantation and corticosteroid use.27 In the present case, end-stage renal disease and advanced age may have been risk factors. In dialysis patients, approximately 24% of administered levofloxacin is removed during the procedure, but during the interdialytic period, its half-life is prolonged due to the reduced systemic clearance.28 In this case, a 500 mg dose of levofloxacin was initially administered, and it is thought that until dialysis was performed the following day, the plasma concentration of levofloxacin remained elevated compared with individuals with normal renal function.

Fluoroquinolone-induced myopathy has been reported to manifest within 1 week of initial administration,4 with reports also describing symptom onset as early as 2 days after the first levofloxacin dose.12 The condition predominantly presents as proximal muscle pain, which may occur with or without muscle weakness.4 In severe instances, the disease may progress to rhabdomyolysis. Symptom severity varies, ranging from being asymptomatic to having significant muscle pain and weakness, resulting in marked difficulty with ambulation.5 11 On physical examination, muscle tenderness is a characteristic finding, irrespective of the presence or absence of muscle weakness.5 6

In this case, particular attention was paid to this patient’s symptoms having appeared as early as the day after the initial dose of levofloxacin. Although this onset is earlier than that documented in most reports, previous descriptions of fluoroquinolone-induced tendinopathy (a related musculoskeletal adverse event) have noted symptom onset as quickly as 2 hours after the initial exposure.4 The emergence of myopathy within 1 day of administration, as observed in this case, therefore remains a plausible scenario.

Regarding symptoms confined to the iliopsoas and gluteal muscles, reports have described levofloxacin-induced myopathy limited to the proximal muscles of the lower limbs,11 14 suggesting that this adverse effect may also be restricted to specific muscle groups.

Moreover, the absence of elevated creatine kinase levels despite severe muscle symptoms causing gait disturbance, as seen in this case, has also been documented in an earlier report of levofloxacin-induced myopathy.14 Such a clinical presentation is therefore considered to be entirely plausible.

These findings suggest that fluoroquinolone-induced myopathy exhibits a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations.

Differential diagnoses for conditions with predominant proximal muscle involvement, as in our case, include drug-induced myopathy, autoimmune myopathies such as polymyositis/dermatomyositis, inclusion body myositis and polymyalgia rheumatica, as well as infectious myopathies, metabolic myopathies and electrolyte disturbances. Diagnostic evaluations may involve testing for autoantibodies, electromyography and muscle biopsy.

In the present case, we considered the differential diagnoses noted above, but electromyography and muscle biopsy were unfeasible due to the anatomical location of the symptoms. Moreover, given the advanced age of the patient, it was challenging to exclude polymyalgia rheumatica. MRI was therefore performed to assess the affected area, which revealed findings highly suggestive of myopathy. Although the differential diagnosis of myopathy is broad, neither blood test results nor the pathophysiological mechanism in this case strongly supported alternative aetiologies, and the clinical course pointed towards a drug-induced myopathy.

Cefmetazole and metronidazole were also administered, so they too must be considered as potential causative agents. However, no musculoskeletal adverse effects have been reported in association with cefmetazole. Metronidazole, on the other hand, has been associated with side effects such as paraesthesia and myalgia, but these are typically attributed to neuropathic mechanisms rather than to direct myotoxicity.29 We therefore deemed levofloxacin to be the most likely causative agent responsible for the myopathy in this case.

When fluoroquinolone-induced myopathy is suspected, initial management should include immediate discontinuation of the causative agent, rest for the affected area, initiation of rehabilitation and avoidance of concurrent steroid use.4 Symptoms generally improve within 1–4 weeks after drug withdrawal, but there are cases where symptoms have persisted for up to 6 months.4 Early detection is thus critical to prevent a decline in activities of daily living.

Fluoroquinolones are widely used, with their usage continuing to rise.30 Moreover, the global ageing population has reached 10% and is expected to increase.31 The number of patients requiring dialysis worldwide has reached 3.73 million and is projected to grow.32 Consequently, the incidence of fluoroquinolone-induced myopathy is anticipated to rise. Nevertheless, awareness of this condition remains limited. Functional recovery becomes increasingly challenging with advancing age,33 and prolonged hospitalisation further exacerbates the decline in activities of daily living,34 so early detection is paramount to prevent deterioration. Enhancing recognition and understanding of this disease is therefore considered to be of urgent importance.
