Optimizing Methane Control in Composite Goaf Areas: Insights from Multisource Emission Analysis and Numerical Modeling in the Tongxin Coal Mine
Authors: Chungang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoal mine methane poses a threat to both coal production and the well-being of workers. This study investigates the complex challenge of managing methane emissions from multiple sources in coal mines prone to gas. Coal mining creates multiple air leakage channels in the mine goaf, increasing the risk of gas explosions. Tracer SF6 measurements were employed to identify multiple air leakage channels. A comprehensive numerical model, incorporating multiple air leakage channels, was developed to simulate methane distribution in the goaf and upper corner. By adjusting the combination of gas drainage rate (U) and working face ventilation flux (Q), the concentration of methane at the upper corner is expected to be decrease, ensuring safe production at the Tongxin mine. A total of 48 simulation cases were conducted, determining that U = 1080 m3/min and Q = 2250 m3/min are recommended for the 8201 working face in the Tongxin coal mine. This combination provides not only an effective solution but also a cost-efficient one for methane control. The findings offer a new insight into effective methane control providing a foundation for improving mine safety.

Introduction

More than 51% of underground coal mines in China are characterized as gassy mines.1 Coalbed methane refers to the emitted methane in the underground mining process.2 Methane seriously threatens the safety of coal mine production and the lives of workers. The greenhouse effect of methane is 26–38 times greater than that of carbon dioxide.3−6 In the extraction of closely situated coal seams, the collapse of the lower coal seam’s roof can create cracks that connect to the upper goaf.7,8 Moreover, the mined-out areas in the coal seams are typically interconnected, resulting in composite goafs with multiple air leakage channels.9−12 These leakage channels create the potential for gas explosions and spontaneous coal combustion.13−15 Methane in the working face originates not only from the current goaf but also from surrounding goafs, posing a serious challenge to methane control.16 Li et al.17 explored the impact of dual-side roof cutting technology on the combined disaster of methane and spontaneous combustion in goaf, revealing the sensitivity of the evolution of the disaster area. Xia et al.18 developed a further final element (FE) software to simulate the gas flow behavior of goaf. Juganda et al.19 proposed a hybrid approach to modeling the methane distribution in the mine goaf using computational fluid dynamics (CFD). Lolon et al.20 highlighted how atmospheric pressure changes can influence the formation of explosive gas zones. Wang et al.21 investigated the coupling of gas explosions and spontaneous combustion under coal mine goaf gas drainage, observing the migration of the coupled hazard zone to deeper areas. Hu et al.22 discovered that methane enrichment zones in underground coal mines consistently exhibit a “hexagon’ shape”, with the widths of methane-enriched zones in the coal increasing with the coal bed angle. Wang et al.23 developed CFD models for goaf gas flow, incorporating operating vertical boreholes to simulate methane distribution and gas flow pathways. To prevent or minimize the risks of methane emissions, methane drainage approaches have been adopted by coal mines.24 Zhang et al.25 established a three-dimensional numerical model of the mining area under U-shaped ventilation. They found that the gas concentration in the upper corner was reduced by 43.9% after employing a large borehole to extract methane gas. Lei26 discovered that the method of drilling large-diameter boreholes greatly reduces the methane concentration in the upper corner, with values increasing as the borehole length increases deeper and is farther away from the upper corner. Li et al.27 investigated the superposition effect of the air leakage and coal combustion-induced chimney effect on methane dilution in the upper corner. It reveals the disaster prevention effect of ventilation dilution. They found that the two effects on methane dilution are opposite. Tutak and Brodny28 performed three ventilation cases for reducing the methane concentration in the upper corner. Meng et al.29 predicted the methane concentration in the upper corner of the Qianyingzi coal mine by three machine learning methods. They found that combining deep learning with classical time series analysis can provide accurate methane concentration prediction.

As coal resources are mined, some goafs undergo composite status, leading to diverse gas sources. However, there is limited research addressing the issue of multisource methane. In this study, the tracer SF6 measurements were conducted in the 8021 working face of the Tongxin coal mine to investigate multiple sources of methane. Utilizing the actual conditions of the 8201 working face, a numerical model was developed to analyze multisource air leakage. It is expected to explore the influence of different air volumes in the working face and gas extraction from the high-drainage roadway on methane concentration at the upper corner. This research provides valuable information for methane control in composite goaf areas.

Introduction

Overview of the 8201 Working Face

The Tongxin coal mine is located in Datong City, China. It has a designed production capacity of 1.5 Mton per year. The main coal produced is gas coal. The coal being mined is about 500 m deep and belongs to the Carboniferous Permian coal seam. The Tongxin coal mine is an underground mine and faces significant methane-related challenges, especially in goafs with multiple leakage channels. This study focuses on the 8201 working face of the Tongxin coal mine. The 8201 working face is situated in the northern part of the Tongxin coal mine’s Panel II. The strike length and inclination length of the 8201 working face are 2138 and 217 m, respectively. The coal seam thickness ranges from 2.31 to 23.42 m, with an average thickness of 14.09 m and a dip angle ranging from 3° to 11° with an average of 6°. The mining method is top coal caving.

The ventilation method of the 8201 working face is a U-shaped exhausting ventilation. The western and northern parts of the 8201 working face correspond to the goafs of 8202 and W8101 working faces, respectively. There is a goaf above the 8201 working face. The eastern part of the working face is an unmined area. The Tongxin coal mine adopts an exhausting ventilation system, causing methane from multiple sources of air leakage to accumulate in the upper corner, resulting in gas over the limit and posing a serious threat to mine safety. Furthermore, the width and height of the inlet airway are 5.5 and 3.5 m, respectively. The return airway is 5.5 m in width and 3.7 m in height. The cross-sectional dimensions of the working face are 9.1 m in width and 3.7 m in height. To address the issue of gas over the limit at the upper corner, a high-drainage roadway is arranged on the return air side, with cross-sectional dimensions of 4.2 m in width and 2.6 m in height. The distance between the high-drainage roadway and the coal seam roof is 6–32 m with an average of 15 m. There are six 2BEC-120 liquid ring vacuum pumps installed on the ground, forming three sets of low suction pressure systems. The drainage rate of a single pump under working conditions is 1120 m3/min. In addition, the 2BEC72 Liquid ring vacuum pumps are installed in the cavern, with a maximum drainage rate of 550 m3/min. A surface low suction pressure system and an underground gas drainage system serve the 8201 working surface. The maximum gas drainage rate is 1670 m3/min. The methane emission quantity in the working face is predicted as 49 m3/min. After verification, the minimum ventilation flux of the 8201 return airway is Qrmin = 1060 m3/min. Therefore, the minimum ventilation flux of the 8201 working face is Qmin = Qrmin + U, where U is the gas drainage rate.

Tracer SF6 Measurement for Air Leakage

In this study, we applied tracer gas SF6 measurement (DKJL-H SF6, Dingsheng Electric Power Automation Co., Ltd.) to identify air leakage channels and subsequently determine the methane source. SF6 is a common tracer gas used for ventilation studies due to the fact that it is easy to be detected at low concentrations and has very low toxicity.30 The SF6 cylinder is connected to the pressure reducing valve, stabilizing valve, and flowmeter in turn. SF6 is continuously released. Figure 1 shows the schematic of the 8201 working face. Figure 1b is a plane view illustrating the testing of air leakage in the goaf of the 8201 working face. SF6 was released by bundle tube at point A in 8201 goaf and 5 m from the low corner. The duration of release is 2 h, with a flow rate of 10 L/min with a total volume of 1200 L. Measurements of SF6 concentration taken at different positions (A′, 21 points) on the working face are shown.

Tracer gas measurement for air leakage: (a) overall plane layout, (b) A–A′ air leakage channel, (c) B–B′ air leakage channel, and (d) C–C′ air leakage channel.

Figure 1c is a vertical view examining air leakage conditions from the upper goaf to the 8201 goaf. SF6 was released at point B, on the return air side, 370 m from the working face. The SF6 release tube was inserted and sealed in a drainage hole by sealing gel. The drainage hole connected the upper goaf and the return airway of the 8201 working face. The drainage hole (45 m in the length and 50 mm in the diameter) was previously used for draining water in the upper goaf area. After testing, the inside of the hole was blocked. A CJG10 methane detector (Shaanxi ASTTAR Explosion-proof Safety Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to test the methane in the hole, which was almost 0. After hole operation, the methane concentration quickly increased to more than 1%. After measurement by the dilution method, the methane concentration was 21%. It is believed that the upper goaf is a methane source. The SF6 receiving point of B′ was positioned near the release point but on the windward side. The release duration was 10 min, and the release flow rate was 25 L/min with a total volume of 250 L.

Figure 1d is a plane view assessing air leakage conditions in the adjacent goaf. A borehole was drilled from the surface to the release point of C, 165 m from the 8201 return airway and 182 m from the open-cut line of the 8202 working face. The length and diameter of the relief borehole are 350 and 150 mm, respectively. The relief borehole used to be a methane drainage hole, and its air tightness has been checked. The methane from the borehole was drained, and that concentration was up to 18%. SF6 gas was injected into point C through the borehole. The receiving point C′ was located in the 8201 return airway, 1950 m away from the open-cut line of the 8201 working face. The duration of SF6 release had two stages: the first stage lasted 15 min with a flow rate of 10 L/min (total volume of 150 L), and the second stage had a release duration of 5 min with a flow rate of 10 L/min (total volume of 50 L).

The above three SF6 measurements were carried out at different times to eliminate interference from the test pieces. In addition, while measuring the SF6 concentrations at 32 measuring points (A′), methane detection was performed at 21 measuring points evenly distributed in the working face by a CJG10 methane detector.

Result of SF6 Tracer Measurement

Figure 2 shows the concentrations of SF6 and methane at different positions on the 8201 working face. SF6 and methane concentrations approximately increase exponentially with the distance from the measured point to the low corner. SF6 is almost undetectable between 0 and 70 m, indicating that airflow in the working face can enter the goaf and goaf gases hardly enter the working face. However, the methane concentration detected at 0–70 m of the working surface is around 0.05%. This is due to the methane released from the coal seams and rocks surrounding the roadways before the working face. Then, SF6 can be intermittently detected in the range of 70–125 m of the 8201 working face, with an average SF6 concentration of 23 ppm. The methane concentration in this section of the working face increases with an average of 0.7%. It implies that the airflow from the working face can enter the goaf, and a small amount of goaf methane enters the working face through the air leakage channels. In the range of 125–217 m, SF6 can be continuously detected, and with increasing distance from point A′ to the lower corner, the SF6 and methane concentrations show a rapid increase trend. It indicates that with increasing distance, more goaf gas leaks into the working face. A large amount of methane enters the working face with the leaked air, gathers in the vortex formed in the upper corner, and appears as gas over the limit.

Methane and SF6 concentration versus the distance measured points to the low corner.

After the tracer gas release at point B, SF6 was first detected at point B′ after 14 min of release, and the detection continued for 20 min. The O-shaped circle of goaf indicates that void zones above the mined-out area will form dynamic fractures during the mining process. Due to the exhausting ventilation system in the mine, gases from the upper goaf will enter the 8201 goaf through the connected fractures. Based on estimates, the average air velocity from the release point to the upper corner is 0.51 m/s, indicating that some methane gas in the 8201 working face and return airway originates from the upper goaf. Additionally, for the point C′, SF6 can be detected 27 min after SF6 release at point C. This indicates that the methane from the 8202 goaf can enter the 8201 working face through leakage airflow. Therefore, there are three sources of methane in the 8201 working face: the 8201 goaf, the adjacent 8202 goaf, and the upper goaf of the 8201 working face.

Geometric Model

As shown in Figure 3, according to the actual conditions of the 8021 working face, the geometric model was built. COMSOL Multiphysics was applied to simulate the methane transfer in the mine goaf. After presimulation, the geometric length and height of the 8201 goaf were set as 300 and 20 m, respectively. The length of 8201 working face is 217 m. The cross-sectional areas of the 8201 working face, intake airway, and return airway are 9.1 m × 3.7 m, 5.5 m× 3.5 m, and 5.5 m × 3.7 m, respectively. The details of the high-drainage roadway are described in Section 2.1.

The air leakage of the 8201 goaf is complex, and it is difficult to model each air leakage passage. Therefore, a simplified model was applied that omits the 8202 goaf and the upper goaf, instead building two-dimensional plane sources of air leakage and methane release on the top and side of the 8201 goaf.38,39 Therefore, three air leakage passages were set up on the side of the 8201 goaf. Two leakage passages on the roof of the 8021 goaf were laid out due to the SF6 measurement and the misalignment between the upper goaf and the 8201 goaf. The cross section of all air leakage passages was set to 3 m × 3 m. The gravity effect was considered, and the angle between the gravity direction and the xy plane was 84°. Grid independence was checked by carrying out three sets of elements. Element numbers of the models without and with leakage channels are 421 343 and 442 976, respectively. Taking 442 976 elements, for example, the statistics are as follows, mesh vertices: 83 179; tetrahedra: 442 976; triangles: 56 996; edge elements: 1 290; vertex elements 80; minimum element quality: 0.022 14, average element quality: 0.668 8; element volume ratio: 3.374 × 10–6; mesh volume 995 800 m3. Assume that a methane emission rate of 8021 goaf is 30 m3/min. The air leakage from the side and roof of 8201 goaf were set as 7 m3/min. Thus, the velocity of leakage passages was set as 0.78 m/s. Methane concentrations of the leakage airflows for the side and top of the 8201 goaf were set as 21% and 18%. Figures 3 c and 3d show the porosity and permeability of the 8201 goaf. The porosity and permeability near the coal pillar and working face are relatively high, while the porosity and permeability inside the goaf are low.

To investigate the effects of the ventilation flux of the working face, Q, and gas drainage rate of high drainage roadway, U, on the distribution of methane concentration in the goaf, combined with the actual conditions of the Tongxin coal mine, a total of 48 cases (Qrmin = 1060 m3/min, Q = 1060, 1500, 1850, 2250, 2625, 3000, 3375, and 3750 m3/min, U = 0, 180, 360, 540, 720, 900, 1080, and 1440 m3/min, Q > Qrmin + U) were conducted. In the upper corner near the coal wall and the goaf side, the airflow velocity is low, leading to localized vortex flow. It hinders the dispersion of methane emitted from the goaf into the main airflow. The high-concentration methane accumulates near the upper corner, forming an area of gas over the limit in the vortex area. According to Coal Mine Safety Regulations of China, mining work must be halted and personnel evacuated when the methane concentration at the upper corner exceeds 1%. The high-drainage roadway is generally located above the upper corner, effectively addressing the issue of methane exceedance in this area. This study applies the methane concentration at the upper corner as a criterion to determine the optimal airflow flux of the 8201 working face and the gas drainage rate of the high-drainage roadway.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Multisource Leakage on Methane

Figure 4 shows the methane concentration and airflow in the 8201 goaf under Q = 1850 m3/min and U = 720 m3/min. Figures 4a is the case without air leakage, and Figure 4b is the case with air leakage. A part of the airflow from the intake airway enters the goaf and exits on the return air side. As shown in Figure 4a, in the absence of multisource leakage, even under the negative-pressure effect of the high-drainage roadway, the distribution of airflow streamlines in the goaf is approximately symmetric. The methane concentration in the goaf is higher on the return air side, while the methane concentration across the entire working face remains relatively low. The maximum methane concentration in the upper corner is 0.57%, nearly identical to that of the intake airway. It indicates that the methane generated in the goaf is effectively extracted by the high-drainage roadway, reducing the methane concentration of the upper corner. However, in the presence of multiple channels of air leakage, airflow from the leaking sources enters the goaf, flowing toward the return air side. The air leaks carry methane into the 8201 goaf, causing a rapid increase in methane concentration. Most of the goaf space reaches a concentration of 20%. Despite the high-drainage roadway taking a large amount of methane from the goaf, the maximum methane concentration in the upper corner is 5.03%. The value is significantly higher than that without multisource leakage. Therefore, the airflow flux of U = 720 m3/min cannot meet the requirements for normal coal mining operations. Adjusting the ventilation flux of the working face, Q, and the gas drainage rate of the high-drainage roadway, U, is necessary to ensure that the methane concentration in the upper corner is lower than 1%.

Distributions of methane concentration in the goaf and airflow direction (z = 3 m): (a) methane concentration without air leakage; (b) methane concentration with air leakage.

Effect of the Air Ventilation Flux in the Working Face

Figure 5 shows the methane distribution in the goaf with different ventilation flux in the working face: Q = 1850, 2250, 3000, 3375, and 3750 m3/min, at the gas drainage rate U = 720 m3/min. Increasing Q can significantly alter the methane concentration in the return airway and upper corner. As Q increases, the low gas concentration area in the goaf gradually expands. This phenomenon has been observed in ref (40). The greater Q, the larger the pressure difference formed inside the goaf, and more fresh air enters the goaf, causing the high methane concentration area to move deeper. However, the increase in Q dilutes the methane concentration, resulting in a reduction in methane concentration in the working face and upper corner. For the cases with Q = 1850, 2250, 3000, 3375, and 3750 m3/min, the methane concentrations in the upper corner are 5.03%, 3.07%, 2.57%, and 2.81%, respectively. In the range of Q = 1850–3000 m3/min, increasing the Q can effectively reduce the methane concentration in the upper corner. However, a continuous increase in Q leads to a slight increase in the methane concentration in the upper corner. This indicates that (i) increasing Q cannot continuously reduce the methane concentration in the upper corner and needs to be combined with gas extraction measures, and (ii) an increase in Q increases the leakage of airflow near the goaf, causing a large influx of gas into the working face. It results in an increase in the methane concentration in the upper corner. Furthermore, increasing Q means an increase in the mine exhausting ventilation system, consuming more electricity and increasing the risk of gas explosions and spontaneous combustion of coal mine goaf. Therefore, it is necessary to adjust the ventilation flux in the working face and the drainage rate in the high-drainage roadway to solve the problem of methane concentration in the upper corner.

Simulated methane distribution with different working face ventilation at drainage rate U = 720 m3/min.

Effect of the Drainage Rate of the High-Drainage Roadway

Figure 6 shows methane distribution in the goaf with different gas drainage rates of the high-drainage roadway, U = 0, 360, 720, and 1080 m3/min, at the ventilation flux Q = 2250 m3/min. Gas extraction hardly affects the methane concentration distribution on the intake side of the goaf. It is mainly influenced by the strong airflow leakage from the working face and a large amount of air from leakage channels entering the high-drainage roadway. However, changes in methane extraction significantly alter the methane concentration near the upper corner. This result expresses a good agreement with those of Lei26 and Fan.41 Due to the leakage channels in the present model, the distribution of high methane concentration in the goaf is wider. When U = 0, a large amount of methane from the goaf flows into the working face and accumulates at the upper corner, causing the methane concentration to exceed the safety limits. In the case of U = 0, the methane concentration at the upper corner is 19.02%, far exceeding the mine’s specified standard and posing a threat to mine safety. For the cases with U = 360, 720, and 1080 m3/min, the methane concentrations at the upper corner are 13.53%, 3.07%, and 0.62%, respectively. It indicates that an increase in the drainage rate in the high-drainage roadway significantly reduces the methane concentration at the upper corner, allowing a large amount of methane to be discharged from the mine through the high-drainage roadway. The high-drainage roadway proves to be an effective method for addressing the issue of excessive methane at the upper corner. When Q = 2250 m3/min, only U = 1080 m3/min can make the methane concentration in the upper corner less than 1% and ensure the safety of coal mining. U cannot be larger than 1080 m3/min in order to ensure the ventilation flux in the return airway being sufficient.

Simulated methane distribution with different drainage rates at working face ventilation flux Q = 2250 m3/min.

To determine the optimal ventilation flux of the working face, Q, and gas drainage rate of the high-drainage roadway, U, 48 cases were carried out to investigate the effects of two parameters on the methane concentration of the upper corner. As shown in Figure 7, when there is no methane drainage, no matter how large Q is, the methane concentration of the upper corner exceeds 15%, which is very dangerous. When Q ≤ 1850 m3/min, on the premise of ensuring the ventilation flux of the 8201 working face, the high-drainage roadway cannot solve the problem of gas over the limit. When U ≤ 360 m3/min, the methane concentration of the upper corner decreases approximately linearly with the increase in Q. In the range of U = 360–720 m3/min, the methane concentration of the upper corner decreases rapidly with the increase in U. The methane concentration of the upper corner for Q = 3750 m3/min exceeds that of Q < 3750 m3/min when U = 900 m3/min. Coal Mine Safety Regulations of China stipulates that the methane concentration at the upper corner cannot exceed 1%. Eleven combinations are considered to meet the methane concentration requirements for safe production. Specifically, U = 900 m3/min, Q = 2625 and 3000 m3/min; U = 1080 m3/min, Q ≥ 2250 m3/min; and U = 1440 m3/min, Q ≥ 2625 m3/min are identified as safe configurations. However, when U > 1080 m3/min, the methane concentration in the upper corner is not sensitive to variations in either Q and U. Therefore, to save costs and reduce the risk of spontaneous combustion in the goaf, we recommend U = 1080 m3/min, Q = 2250 m3/min. This configuration can not only reduce the methane concentration at the upper corner to less than 1% but also can minimize air leakage in the goaf. The optimal configuration is crucial for mitigating the risk of methane explosions. It holds significant implications for the safety and efficiency of mining operations of the Tongxin coal mine.

Conclusion

The 8201 working face of the Tongxin coal mine confronts a pressing issue—multisource methane emissions within the intricate network of interconnected goaf. The urgency to manage methane-related risks is paramount.

Tracer SF6 measurements were conducted to identify distinct air leakage channels of the 8201 working face, including the 8201 goaf, the neighboring 8202 goaf, and the upper goaf. SF6 was first detected at a distance of 70 m from the low corner, showing an average value of 23 ppm in the range of 70–125 m. Methane and SF6 concentrations of the 8201 working face are positive. A mathematical model, encompassing multiple air leakage channels, has been established to simulate methane concentration in goaf areas, working faces, and upper corners. This model can determine effective control strategies for gas over the limit in the mining environment.

By identifying optimal combinations of gas drainage rate (U) and working face ventilation flux (Q), the research ensures safer working conditions by reducing methane concentrations. In general, as U and Q increase, the methane concentration at the upper corner decreases. Q can influence the methane concentration distribution on the intake side of the goaf, while U has minimal impact on it. To ensure the methane concentration at the upper corner remains below the safety threshold of 1%, 11 combinations of U and Q have been determined. Ultimately, a recommended optimal combination of U = 1080 m3/min and Q = 2250 m3/min is proposed for the 8201 working face to achieve safe gas concentrations. It offers not only an effective but also a cost-efficient solution for methane control. The significance of these results lies in their potential to ensure safer working conditions.
