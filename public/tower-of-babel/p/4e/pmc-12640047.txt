Characteristic of flavor in soup of pork stewed with Lactarius hatsudake
Authors: Shuyu Liang, Pingsheng Zhong, Guopu Ren, Bo Zhou, Jiali Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mushroom-stewed-meat is one of the main traditional delicacy in China, however, different mushrooms give different flavor and taste to stew meat. The effects of Lactarius hatsudake (L. hatsudake) on the principal taste active and volatile compounds in soup of stewed pork has been investigated in this work. The results indicated that 14 kinds of free amino acids (FAAs) have been identified, and their maximum contents (2130.32 μg/g) was reached after stewing 5 h, and then gradually decreased. The combined concentration of 5′-inosine monophosphate (IMP) and 5′-guanosine monophosphate (GMP) reached a maximum of 98.15 μg/mg after 3.5 h of stewing. The stewing enhanced the umami of the soup, and the equivalent umami concentration (EUC) reached the maximum (0.44 g/100 g) after 5 h. The content of soluble sugars (polyols) had a similar trend to that of EUC. Minerals reached a dissolved equilibrium after 6 h of stewing. The main flavor components of the soup was aldehydes. Heating increased the richness of the flavor characteristics in soup of pork stewed with L.hatsudake, and the overall flavor was best at 5 h. Consequently, an optimal balance between nutrient dissolution, taste and flavor profiles in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake was achieved after 5 h of stewing. This work provides guidance for the industrial processing of pork soup stewed with L.hatsudake.

Introduction

With a history tracing back to the Paleolithic period (Kosečková et al., 2024), stewing is one of the oldest cooking methods in the world. Broths, which are among the most nutritious ways to cook meat and bones, hold an important place in the Chinese diet. Appropriate ingredients play a significant role in regulating the overall flavor and enhancing the palatability of soup (Lv et al., 2024). Edible mushrooms are an ingredient often added to stews, with rich nutrients and umami substances (França et al., 2022), which help to improve the flavor of meat products. Dermiki et al. (2013) obtained extracts rich in umami compounds by soaking dried shiitake mushrooms in hot water, which can be used to enhance the flavor of meat products.

Lactarius hatsudake (L. hatsudake), a wild edible mushroom with a medicinal and food homology, easy to deterioration, was found to be rich in nutrients such as proteins, dietary fibers, along with biologically active ingredients such as polyphenols, polysaccharides (Gao et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2025; Yang, Zhang, Qin, Luo and Ren, 2022). The use of L. hatsudake primarily involves fresh sale, cooking, salting, drying and mushroom oil production. However, deep processing and comprehensive utilization of L. hatsudake remain uncommon.

As a special seasonal dish with a unique flavor, soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake is an ideal product for industrial production. However, because soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake is mostly made in homes and private restaurants, there are very few products on market, and the flavor compounds have been little studied. During the stewing process, nutrients and volatile flavor compounds from the raw ingredients are released into the soup. We all know that soups need to be stewed for the right amount of time to reach the best nutrition and flavor. However, different researchers have different opinions on the optimal stewing time for different raw materials. Meng et al. (2022) found the richest concentrations of volatile flavor compounds in bone soup at 4 and 5 h of stewing. Fu et al. (2023) found that the contents of amino acids, volatile flavor compounds, titratable acids, antioxidant capacity and total sugar were significantly higher at 40 min of simmering than at other simmering times in the Morchella soup. The effect of time on volatile and nonvolatile aromatic compounds in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake has been investigated in this work. It is expected that the results will provide theoretical support for soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake making.

Materials and method

Materials

Streaky pork came from Castrated male pig (Sus scrofa, Ningxiang breed), which originates from Hunan Liusha River Flower Pig Ecological Husbandry Co. (Changsha, China). Castrated male pig was slaughtered at 180 ± 5 days of age after being fed a corn-soybean basal diet. Pig was stunned electrically and exsanguinated. Carcasses were chilled at 4 °C for 24 h, aged at 0–2 °C for 48 h, then transported under a controlled cold chain. Fresh pork packed by polyethylene (PE) plastic bags and then cut into 1.5 × 1.5 × 3 cm size for further use. Fresh L. hatsudake has been collected from Hubei province.

Sample preparation

Fresh streaky pork was cleaned using flowing water and then placed in boiled water to remove blood stains before stewing. L. hatsudake was cleaned with flowing water to remove sand and then dried in a ventilated area. The stewing condition as following: the ratio of L. hatsudake and streaky pork was 2:1 (w/w), material-liquid ratio was 1:2 (w/v)，the mixture was placed into an electric casserole dish (Guangdong Midea Group, China) covered by pot and then stewed for 6 h under 98.59 kPa of atmospheric pressure. After sampling 50 mL every half hour, the 50 mL of water was added into electric casserole dish at the same temperature to ensure that the total volume of soup remains constant during the stewing process. The temperatures of the samples were measured using a food thermometer (DM-1015, Hangzhou Delixi, China) while sampling, and the temperatures of the 13 samples were 23.8 °C, 43.2 °C, 62.6 °C, 78.7 °C, 91.2 °C, 98.5 °C, 98.6 °C, 98.6 °C, 98.5 °C, 98.6 °C 98.4 °C, 98.6 °C and 98.4 °C, respectively. The samples were numbered from S0 to S12 according to sampling time, and stored at −24 °C for later use.

Free amino acids (FAAs) analysis

The contents of FAAs in samples were determined according to the method previously reported by Qian et al. (2023) with minor modification. 5 g soup sample was mixed with 20 mL boiled water (100 °C), and then extracted at 95 °C for 10 min. After cooling to 25 °C, the extract was, which was added ultra-pure water to 25 mL, filtered with 0.45 μm nylon membrane (Biosharp, Beijing Labgic Technology Co., Ltd.) and the supernatant was submitted to OPA FMOC pre-column derivatization. FAAs were determined using a high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1260, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a Poroshell HPH-C18 column (4.6 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) and a UV detector set at 338 nm, with the column temperature maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases A and B were buffer solution (10 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 10 mmol/L Na2B4O7, pH 8.2) and methanol: acetonitrile: water (45:45:10, v/v/v), respectively. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. The gradient eluent program was as following: 0–0.35 min (98 % A: 2 % B), 0.35–13.40 min (43 % A: 57 % B), 13.40–13.50 min (0 % A: 100 % B), which maintained to 15.70 min, 15.70–17.80 min (98 % A: 2 % B) then maintained to 20.00 min.

5′-Nucleotides analysis

The content of 5′-nucleotides in the samples was measured according to the method described by Yang, Zhang, et al. (2022) with minor modifications. The sample (2 g) was centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 5 min and the supernatant was then filtered with 0.22 μm MCE membrane (Biosharp, Beijing Labgic Technology Co., Ltd.). The nucleotides were determined using a high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1260, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with Kinextex C18 100 Å column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Fenomax) and a UV detector (254 nm) at 25 °C of column temperature. The mobile phases A and B were a buffer solution (1.4 mmol/L C16H37NO4S, 0.01 mmol/L KH2PO4, pH 3.1) and methanol, respectively. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min using an isocratic mobile phase of 95 % A and 5 % B. Standards of 5′-nucleotides were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, Mo., USA).

Calculation of equivalent umami concentration (EUC)

The EUC (g/100 g) reflects the concentration of monosodium glutamate (MSG) equivalent to that of a mixture of MSG and 5′-nucleotides. The EUC value was calculated using the following formula:Y=∑aibi+1218∑aibi∑ajbjwhere Y is the EUC of the mixture (g/100 g); ai and aj are the concentrations (g/100 g) of the respective umami amino acids (Asp or Glu) and 5′-nucleotides (IMP, GMP, or AMP), respectively. bi and bj are the relative umami concentrations (RUC) of the umami amino acids (Glu, 1; Asp, 0.077) and umami 5′-nucleotides (IMP, 1; GMP, 2.3; AMP, 0.18), respectively; and the synergistic constant is 1218 based on the concentration (g/mg) used (Wang et al., 2022).

Mineral analysis

2 mL sample was added to a microwave digestion tube with 5 mL of ultra-pure nitric acid and sealed for 1 h. The sample was then digested for 71 min in a microwave digestion system (Ultra Wave, Leibtech Instruments Co., Ltd., China) by the following heating procedures: heating up to 120 °C from 0 to 8 min, then increasing to 160 °C in 8 min and holding for 10 min, subsequently raising the temperature to 210 °C in 15 min and maintaining it for 30 min. Finally, the digested solution was fixed with ultra-pure to 25 mL. K, Ca, Na and Mg concentrations were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Agilent 7700× ICP-MS, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The power of radio frequency was 1.6 kW, and the speeds of plasma gas, carrier gas, dilution gas, and argon gas were 15 L/min, 0.6 L/min, 0.5 L/min, and 5 L/min, respectively. All multi-element standard solutions used in this work were purchased from Guobiao (Beijing) Testing & Certification Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Soluble sugars (polyols) analysis

Soluble sugars (polyols) were determined by a LC-30 A HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an ELSD-LT II evaporative light-scattering detector (Shimadzu, Japan) and an Athena NH2 column (120 Å, 250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Shimadzu, Japan) at 40 °C of column temperature. The mobile phase was made up with acetonitrile and ultra-pure water (85:15, v/v) with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The ELSD-LT II was run with nitrogen as the nebulizing gas at 80 °C. Mannitol and trehalose standards were purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds were analyzed by solid phase microextraction (SPME) and gas chromatography (Shimadzu GC-2010, Japan)-mass spectrometry (Shimadzu QP-2010, Japan) (GC–MS) equipped with an SH-Rtx-1 column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm; Shimadzu, Japan). Approximately 5.0 g of each soup sample was transferred into a headspace bottle, followed by the addition of 5 μL of cyclohexanone (947 μg/kg; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.) as the internal standard. Then, a 50/30 μm divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) (Supelco, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) fiber was inserted into the headspace of the vial at 60 °C for 40 min with magnetically stirred. After extraction, the fiber was inserted into the GC injector and desorbed at 250 °C, split ratio 10:1. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The GC oven temperature program was as follows: 35 °C for 1 min (desorption period), then increased at a rate of 0.5 °C/min to 40 °C, and finally increased further at a rate of 10 °C/min to 260 °C, where it was maintained for 7 min. The mass spectrometer was set to the electronic impact mode at 70 eV in a range of 30–500 m/z.

The volatile compounds were tentatively identified by comparing their mass spectra fragmentation patterns with those in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST 11) Mass Spectral Library. The individual volatile compounds, including both identified and unidentified peaks, were tentatively quantified based on their peak areas relative to that of the internal standard.

Electronic nose (E-nose) analysis

Aromatic information for the samples was obtained using a PEN3 portable electronic nose (E-nose) (Airsense, Schwerin, Germany). The E-nose is equipped with 10 metal oxide gas sensors: W1C (aromatic), W5S (nitrogen oxides), W3C (ammonia and aromatic molecules), W6S (hydrogen), W5C (alkanes and aromatic), W1S (short-chain alkanes), W1W (sulfur compounds), W2S (alcohols, ethers, aldehydes, and ketones), W2W (aromatics, sulfur organic compounds), and W3S (long-chain alkanes). Each soup sample (5 g) was placed in a 20 mL headspace bottle, and equilibrated at 25 °C for 30 min. The time of detection and cleaning times were all 90 s. After detection by the electronic nose, the characteristic value of each sample from the 55th to 57th seconds was obtained.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range tests in the SPSS software (version 19.0, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). PCA and OPLS-DA of the volatile compounds were performed using the software SIMCA 14.1 (Sweden). The compounds with variable importance in the projection (VIP) score > 1, p (corr) > 0.7 in the OPLS-DA analysis. Differences were considered significant when P values ≤ 0.05.

A total of 14 FAAs were detected in the soup, including 6 essential amino acids and 8 non-essential amino acids (Table 1). The contents of essential and non-essential amino acids ranged from 51.60 μg/g to 656.10 μg/g and from 125.06 μg/g to 1479.59 μg/g, respectively. As the stewing time increased, the total FAAs content in the soup initially increased and then decreased (Table 1). The content of total FAAs was the highest (2130.32 μg/g) when the soup was stewed for 5 h, and then decreased with the extension of stewing time. A similar trend was found by previous studies on duck soup (Wang et al., 2024). According to previous studies, the FAAs in soup may originate from the initial FAAs in raw materials (L. hatsudake and pork), or they may be derived from the degradation of proteins and peptides in the raw materials (Li et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024).

Amino acids can be divided into 4 categories according to the different taste characteristics, including monosodium glutamate-like (MSG-like), sweet, bitter and tasteless (Qi et al., 2021). Among them, umami and sweet amino acids contributed the most significantly to the taste of soup (Aisala et al., 2020). The presence of both aspartic acid (Asp) and glutamic acid (Glu) can enhance umami taste of soup by yielding a taste that similar to MSG, thus improving the overall taste of foods. The results showed that the stewing process had an important impact on the content of MSG-like and sweet FAAs (P < 0.05). Table 1 showed that stewing for 5 h had the highest content of MSG-like (236.45 μg/g) and sweet amino acids (865.47 μg/g) in the soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake. However, the contents of MSG-like FAAs and Glu was rapidly decreased from 236.45 μg/g (5 h) to 56.76 μg/g (5.5 h) and from 198.36 μg/g (5 h) to 19.98 μg/g (5.5 h) after 5 h, respectively. A previous report also observed a similar phenomenon during the cooking process of Agaricus bisporus soup. This may be attributed to the fact that Glu not only contributes to the umami flavor of the soup, but also is an important precursor for the formation of volatile compounds.

5′-Nucleotide and EUC changes in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake

5’-Nucleotides are typical taste compounds that significantly enhance umami flavor. 5′-Adenosine monophosphate (AMP) provides a salty and sweet taste while suppressing bitterness in foods (Feng et al., 2023), 5′-guanosine monophosphate (GMP) could give a meaty flavor and 5′-inosine monophosphate (IMP) alone presents taste and plays a complementary role with monotonous glutamate to enhance umami in foods (Hu et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2025). The importance of GMP in mushroom umami has been widely demonstrated, its taste intensity tends to be roughly equivalent to 2.3 times than that of IMP (Meng et al., 2022), and even higher when compared to MSG based on the EUC formula (Tu et al., 2024).

Umami 5′-nucleotides and amino acids have a synergistic effect on umami flavor, which can be evaluated by the EUC value. Generally, a higher content of umami substances corresponds to relatively higher EUC values. The EUC values of pork soup stewed with L. hatsudake at different stewing times were shown in Table 2. The EUC values increased significantly with stewing time, reaching a maximum of 0.44 at 5 h and then decreased significantly to 0.05 at 5.5 h. Therefore, the umami taste of pork soup stewed with L. hatsudake mainly depended on 5′-nucleotides and the synergistic effects between 5′-nucleotides and FAAs.

Soluble sugars (polyols) and mineral changes in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake

Soluble sugars (polyols) are substances that significantly contribute to the taste of soup, providing a pleasant sweet taste (Tsai et al., 2007). As shown in Fig. 1A, trehalose and mannitol were found in the soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake, maximum content of trehalose (1193.96 mg/L) and mannitol (4923.90 mg/L) all reached at 5 h of stewing. Previous reports have shown that mannitol is an important substance for the sweetness of mushrooms (Mau et al., 1997) and regarded as the major polyol in many species of mushrooms (Tsai et al., 2008). Mannitol is the main polyol accounted for around 80 % of the total sugars (polyols) in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake in this work. Stewing enhances the degradation of soluble sugars. Li et al. (2019) found that soluble sugars (polyols) gradually transferred from raw Lentinus edodes to soup during cooking. In this work, we found that the levels of trehalose and mannitol in the soups initially increased with longer stewing times, followed by a relative stabilization of their levels. This phenomenon can be due to the dissolution of soluble sugars (polyols) and the occurrence of the Maillard reaction during heat treatment.Fig. 1Changes in soluble sugars (polyols) (A) and minerals (B) in soup of pork stewed with L.hatsudake.Fig. 1

The levels of Mg, Ca, K and Na were presented in Fig. 1B, and their dissolution reached equilibrium after 4.5 h of stewing. Significant increases in mineral concentrations were observed as stewing time extended, which was supported in a previous report (Meng et al., 2022). K was the richest element in the soup, followed by Na. Minerals are inherently salty or bitter, which can interact with other non-volatile components or assist in flavor enhancement. Cations generally have a salty taste; for example, Na has a purely salty taste, while K has a salty and bitter taste. However, the intrinsic taste of a mineral only becomes perceptible when its concentration exceeds its sensory threshold. Consequently, minerals mainly affect taste through synergistic interactions with other components. For example, the addition of Ca, K, and Na to MSG solutions significantly lowers the Glu threshold. The presence of Na could increase the umami taste of meat synergistically by interacting with 5′-nucleotides and FAAs. The results supported the fact that stewing increased the content of minerals, and hence, influence the taste of soups.

Volatile profiles,TAV and OAV changes in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake

As shown in Table S1, 153 volatile compounds were identified in the soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake, including 22 alcohols, 15 ketones, 27 aldehydes, 22 alkanes, 19 alkenes, 27 esters, 9 acids and 12 others, varying in kind and content at different stewing time.

The number and content of flavor compounds varied with stewing time, as shown in Fig. 2. Alcohols (3987.29 μg/kg) and esters (1109.72 μg/kg) were relatively more abundant after 0.5 h of stewing, while ketones (1448.61 μg/kg), alkenes (1252.29 μg/kg) and others compounds (743.87 μg/kg) were relatively more abundant after 5.5 h of stewing (Fig. 2A). Aldehydes had a higher relative content (1846.98 μg/kg) at stewing for 4.5 h than that for others species. The highest total relative content of volatile components (8933.64 μg/kg) was observed at 4.5 h of stewing. Among the kinds analyzed (Fig. 2B), the 1 h mark contained the highest number of alcohols (15), while esters (18) were relatively more abundant at 0.5 h. Volatile compounds were produced from non-volatiles and fats (Zeng et al., 2019). Alcohols are primarily products of fat oxidation and reduction of carbon-based compounds, they have higher thresholds than aldehydes and ketones, but contribute less to overall flavor (Meng et al., 2022). Additionally, some unsaturated alcohols, such as 3-octanol, linalool and isobutanol, have lower thresholds and significantly contribute to the overall flavor of soups (Wang et al., 2024). Aldehydes also play a major role in soup flavor due to their high concentration and low threshold. Aldehydes are primarily produced through the oxidation and degradation of fatty acids; for example, the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) generates compounds such as heptanal, nonanal, and trans-2,4-decadienal (Ying et al., 2016). In addition, the Strecker degradation of FAAs produces aldehydes such as 2-methylpropanal, derived from valine; 2-methylbutanal, derived from isoleucine; phenylacetaldehyde, derived from phenylalanine; and benzaldehyde, derived indirectly from phenylalanine (Gu et al., 2025). Ketones are considered as the source of aromatic oils in cooked meat. The number and content of alcohols decreased with stewing time, indicating that alcohols may be unstable and prone to diffusion or degradation at high temperature (Yang et al., 2016). As stewing time extended, the degradation of esters resulted in a reduction in both their types and contents, leading to a significant increase in aldehydes and alkanes. Based on the above analysis, it can be concluded that longer stewing time increased the content of volatile flavor substances, but relatively decreased the diversity.

The E-nose effectively analyses olfactory characteristics and can distinguish subtle variations in volatile compounds (Cao et al., 2025). Differences were observed in the signal responses of the 13 samples across the 10 sensors (Fig. 2C), indicating that the volatile flavor compounds detected by the sensors varied. Overall, the soups showed higher response values for sensors W1W, W2W, W2S, W1C and W3S, which could be attributed to the abundant flavor compounds, such as aldehydes, alcohols, ketones and aromatic compounds, present in the samples (Lv et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). The response values also varied among the different samples, likely due to variations in the flavor profiles of the soups caused by the different stewing times.

The amount of flavor substances alone is insufficient to determine a food's characteristic flavor. The key flavors are identified by a combination of the concentration and threshold of flavor components, not all of which are active characteristic flavors. The TAV and OAV values are widely used to identify the active taste and key aroma compounds in food. When the TAV or OAV value exceeds 1.0, it indicates that the substance has a significant effect on the food's flavor (Fu et al., 2023; Song & Liu, 2018; Wang et al., 2023; Yang, Lv, Liu, Bi and Zhang, 2022). The larger the TAV or OAV value, the greater contribution. Aldehydes are the main characteristic flavor substances that provide olfactory attributes to soups, such as green, fatty, fruity, and floral aromas. 3-Octanol contributes a mushroom flavor, while 2-pentylfuran has a baking aroma, which is an important flavor characteristic in the soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake.

Orthogonal partial least squares discrimination analysis (OPLS-DA) for volatile flavor characteristic

OPLS-DA in SIMCA 14.1 was used to analyze the relationship between changes in volatile components and stewing time. R2X and R2Y indicate the explanation rate of X and Y, while Q2 refers to the predictive ability of the model. The R2X, R2Y and Q2 of this OPLS-DA were 0.806, 1.0 and 0.837, respectively, indicating that the model had credible interpretive and predictive ability.

To identify differences in the composition of volatile compounds among the 13 samples, the samples were divided into two categories. Samples S0 to S1 were set as group 1 and samples S2 to S12 as group 2. As shown in Fig. 4A, the score dispersion points of the two groups were well separated. The horizontal coordinate “t” represents the principal component score of the samples, indicating compositional differences between the two groups. The vertical coordinate “to” represents the orthogonal score of the sample composition, indicating the difference between samples within the same group. As shown in Fig. 4A, there is a significant difference in composition between the two groups (p < 0.05). To better understand the relationship between non-volatile and volatile components during the stewing process of pork stewed with L. hatsudake, a correlation analysis was conducted between non-volatile components (FAAs and soluble sugars (polyols)) and differential volatile components with VIP > 1 and OAV > 1, as shown in Fig. 5. The blue and red lines represented a positive and negative correlations, respectively. The correlation cluster heatmap was divided into two categories. Heptanal, (E)-2-nonenal, (E,E)-2,4-decadienal, 2-pentylfuran, and hexanal were positively correlated with non-volatile components, whereas 3-octanol, 3-octanone, (E)-2-octenal, linalool, ethyl butyrate, and 1-octanol were negatively correlated. According to literature reports, C8 volatile compounds significantly contribute to the aroma of edible fungi (Aisala et al., 2019). Among the 6 substances with negative correlations, 4 belong to the C8 compounds. These compounds may be inherent components of L. hatsudake, and their concentrations decrease during the stewing process. This reduction may result from the thermal volatilization of alcohols and the oxidative degradation of esters into other compounds during heating and stewing. Notably, the correlation analysis highlights several potential relationships between volatile components and their possible precursors from a statistical perspective. However, many of these correlations might not hold true in real-world scenarios (Gu et al., 2025). One possible explanation for this discrepancy could be the non-linear patterns observed in precursor consumption and volatile compound formation during stewing.Fig. 5Correlation analysis between the differential non-volatile compounds and volatile compounds.Fig. 5

Conclusion

The stewing time showed a significant effect on the flavor characteristics in soup of pork stewed with L. hatsudake. The stewing time enhanced the umami of the soup, and EUC reached the maximum at 5 h. 14 FAAs were identified in all samples, and the concentration of total FAAs reached a maximum at 5 h then gradually decreased. IMP + GMP reached a maximum concentration at 3.5 h of stewing. Minerals reached a dissolved equilibrium. The main flavor components of the soup were aldehydes, which changed significantly with stewing time. Heating increased the richness of the flavor characteristics of pork soup mixed with L. hatsudake. The increase in stewing time improves the dissolution of some nutrients, but it has a negative impact on the diversity of flavor compounds that are detrimental to the total flavor of the soup. Overall, 5 h demonstrated superior flavor attributes.
