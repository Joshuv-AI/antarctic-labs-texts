Background

The term "expertise" refers to a high level of knowledge or skill relating to a particular subject or activity [1]. In its broadest sense, the concept of expertise has been the subject of extensive research in cognitive psychology, with medical expertise being a frequent area of investigation [2]. It is essential for physicians, in initial training and continuing education, to pursue medical expertise, encompassing not only knowledge and technical skills but also socio-cognitive skills [3, 4]. The latter include clinical reasoning, i.e. the capacity to efficiently reason through clinical challenges, arrive at accurate diagnoses, and devise appropriate treatment strategies. The concept of clinical reasoning expertise has been extensively examined and supported by various theories [5].

Anaesthesia is indispensable in numerous surgical and medical procedures, playing a critical role in hospital care. Anaesthesiologists have traditionally been considered as technical specialists serving surgeons [6, 7]. However, as Nobel Prize-winning Daniel Kahneman points out “if an anaesthesiologist says, ‘I have a feeling something is wrong’, everyone in the operating room should be prepared for an emergency” [8]. The expertise of anaesthesiologists is likely as cognitive as it is technical [9]. Recent literature underscores the pivotal role of anaesthesiologists' non-technical skills, particularly in mitigating risks and managing crises [10, 11]. Experienced anaesthesiologists may employ specific reasoning strategies to quickly recognize patterns and make effective decisions [12]. Beyond their technical duties, anaesthesiologists operate as clinical reasoners in acute, high-pressure situations, prone to cognitive biases [13, 14]. This cognitive view of expertise encompasses various aspects of the anaesthesiology practice, including situational awareness (attention, perception, anticipation), decision making (problem solving, judgement) and reducing cognitive biases (error, mistake).

The concept of anaesthetic expertise is recurrently invoked across educational research [15] and clinical guidelines development [16]. However, defining anaesthetic expertise, especially its cognitive, non-technical dimensions, remains challenging. The absence of a standardized definition hampers the generalizability of studies reliant on this concept. A clear concept synthesis of anaesthetists’ clinical reasoning expertise could foster cohesion in research, education, and guideline development, ultimately enhancing patient safety.

This study aims to identify, appraise, and synthesize how anaesthetists’ clinical reasoning expertise is conceptualized in the literature. The secondary objective is to highlight the variations of these conceptualizations over time, and across different anaesthetic fields or research areas. Accordingly, we use a systematic review with qualitative thematic synthesis, allowing not only mapping but also appraisal and conceptual integration.

Methods

We present the current protocol according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement [17]. We registered it on PROSPERO on February 17, 2024 (CRD42024510184). Ethical approval is not required for this study.

The main research question will be: “How is clinical reasoning expertise among anaesthetists is conceptualized in the literature?” Secondary questions will be “How have these conceptualizations evolved over time?” and “Do distinctive patterns or emphases in these conceptualizations emerge according to specific anaesthetic subspecialties or research domains?”.

Systematic review

Database querying

To comprehensively identify relevant scientific publications, we plan to search the Medline, Embase, and Cochrane databases from their inception to July 1, 2024, for peer-reviewed papers published in English or French. We will structure the search strategy around the three themes of interest (i.e. anaesthesiology, expertise, and clinical reasoning) and consider related terms (e.g. excellence, cognition, decision making). Eventually, the list of potentially relevant publications will be enriched by articles that are cited by, co-cited with, and those that cite the selected articles. This will be achieved using full-text references, the Cocite® software, and the Google Scholar® search engine, respectively. Study selection

The study screening and selection process will be reported using the PRISMA flow chart [17]. Query results from the three databases will be processed using the Rayyan® online software for systematic reviews [18]. After duplicate removal, two independent reviewers will screen all titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant publications. They will independently examine full texts from potentially relevant publications against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, for final inclusion. As inclusion criteria, we will consider all publications (essays, observational or experimental studies, and reviews) that address the concept of clinical reasoning expertise among anaesthetists (whether through exploration, description, or use). We will exclude publications from non–peer-reviewed sources journals or non-official reports ("grey literature"), as well as studies focusing exclusively on procedural expertise (i.e., technical skills such as intubation). Any disagreements will be discussed and, if necessary, solved by a third reviewer.

Data extraction

For each included publication, the following information will be systematically extracted: references (title, first author, country, journal, year), type of article (e.g. experimental study, essay, review), research area (e.g. clinical, educational, translational), anaesthetic field (e.g. specific category of patient or technique), and how clinical reasoning expertise is described. In cases where clinical reasoning expertise is not explicitly defined, we will also extract descriptions, explanations, or contextual uses that provide substantive insight into how clinical reasoning expertise is understood, including conceptual, functional, or inferred characteristics. If necessary, corresponding authors will be reached to obtain additional information about their study. All the data will be collected using a standardised form and compiled before their analysis.

Bias assessment

Given the conceptual and qualitative nature of the textual data analysed in this review, a traditional risk of bias assessment, as applied to quantitative or empirical qualitative studies, is not appropriate. In accordance with the revised Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for systematic reviews of textual data, we will employ the Narrative, Opinion, Text Assessment and Review Instrument (NOTARI) checklist to critically appraise the quality of the included publications [19]. This checklist enables the evaluation of the rigor of argumentation, the credibility of the authors (including their expertise and affiliations), consistency with other data or theories, and consideration of alternative perspectives. This structured approach ensures a relevant appraisal of evidence derived from non-empirical texts, thereby enhancing the reliability of our synthesis. In case of experimental or quantitative study included, quality appraisal will be conducted using regular appropriate tools.

Qualitative analysis

To achieve an operational concept synthesis of how anaesthetists’ clinical reasoning expertise is addressed in the literature, we plan to conduct a two-stage qualitative analysis under the supervision of an experienced co-investigator (MG).

Semantic analysis

First, a comprehensive semantic analysis will be conducted using Alceste® software [20]. Descriptive statistical analyses, including word frequency, semantic classes, and interclass relationships, will highlight recurrent structures and term associations within the corpus. The identified patterns and thematic clusters will provide valuable insights for the next stage.

Thematic synthesis

Secondly, we will conduct a formal thematic synthesis following the Thomas and Harden approach [21]. Two reviewers (JNE and DF) will independently conduct a sentence-by-sentence free-coding of each text item based on meaning and content. The codes will then be grouped into categories and further into descriptive themes, resulting in a multi-layered tree structure. After comparing and discussing the classifications to reach a consensus, the other investigators will draft and revise a preliminary summary of findings, arranged by descriptive themes. Up to this point, we will have produced a synthesis closely aligned with the original findings of the included papers.

The final step will involve generating analytical themes to move beyond the original content and directly address the objectives of this review. Each investigator will do this independently and then collectively through an iterative process aimed at emerging new themes sufficiently abstract to describe and explain all our initial descriptive themes on anaesthetists’ clinical reasoning expertise, as well as inferred barriers, facilitators, and implications for intervention development.

Discussion

The proposed systematic review and qualitative thematic synthesis aims to clarify the often ambiguously defined concept of clinical reasoning expertise among anaesthetists. The study will draw data from diverse scientific literature, including experimental studies and essays that provide nuanced, non-quantified insights. This broad and heterogeneous collection of sources will reflect the complex and multifaceted nature of clinical reasoning in anaesthesiology, an inherently multidimensional concept that is difficult to quantify. A broad research strategy will be adopted to capture the full range of relevant evidence, employing a qualitative approach with rigorous thematic analysis to thoroughly examine the intricate aspects of clinical reasoning. This tailored methodology will ensure a robust synthesis of anaesthetic expertise’s complex and unquantifiable elements, providing a comprehensive understanding of the subject.

To our knowledge, this project is original, as no similar study has been conducted in other medical specialties. However, comparing our results with findings from different medical and non-medical fields will help identify commonalities and specificities of anaesthesiology [2]. This study will be the first to propose a global approach that accounts for the evolving and multi-dimensional nature of the concept. Given the complexity of clinical reasoning, primary research alone may not capture all relevant literature [22]. Therefore, we will supplement the initial list of potentially relevant publications with articles cited by, co-cited with, and those that cite the selected articles.

This concept synthesis will be essential for facilitating the comparability and extrapolation of studies, enabling the identification of complementary actions that can collectively enhance expertise. A comprehensive, multidimensional definition of clinical reasoning expertise would contrast with the current fragmented approach, providing a foundation for effective pedagogical interventions. Improving clinical reasoning will lead to more accurate diagnoses, better decision-making, and improved patient outcomes while also reducing medical errors and increasing patient safety. Understanding the learning mechanisms of professionals in training will foster the development of innovative teaching tools and insights into factors like time, fatigue, emotional processes, and trauma, enabling personalized training pathways. Additionally, integrating this data into artificial intelligence models capable of replicating expert-level clinical reasoning will enhance training, education, and clinical decision-making, providing robust AI-driven support systems. Technological advancements and the advent of machines designed to assist or replace humans are transforming the expectations and roles of experts, and this study will contribute significantly to this evolving landscape.

For the first time, clinical reasoning expertise within anaesthesiology will undergo a comprehensive inventory and concept synthesis, opening new possibilities to address gaps in research, education, and clinical practice.
