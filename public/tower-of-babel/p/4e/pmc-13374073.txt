Optimal dietary nano-VD₃ enhances growth performance and bone development in broilers through remodeling the gut microbiome and metabolites
Authors: Naifei He, Jiamin Tian, Jiahui Wu, Liyou Tan, Yapeng Wang, Hongwei Dai, Yan Chen, Jianxiong Lu, Guohua Zhang, Susu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkeletal maldevelopment is a significant challenge in broiler production, causing substantial economic losses. Vitamin D₃ (VD₃) plays a critical role in poultry skeletal health, but its optimal dietary inclusion level for medium-growth broilers remains to be determined due to bioavailability differences among forms. This study investigated the effects of dietary VD₃ levels (conventional vs. nano-formulated) on growth performance, bone development, and gut microbiota composition and metabolite profile in broilers. A total of 420 one-day-old male Luhua broilers were randomly assigned to four groups in an 84-day experiment: a control group fed a basal diet, and three treatment groups supplemented with 3,750 IU/kg conventional VD₃ (CVD), 2,500 IU/kg low-dose nano-VD3 (LNVD), or 3,750 IU/kg high-dose nano-VD3 (HNVD).ResultsBoth CVD and LNVD significantly enhanced average daily gain (ADG) and bone development by improving bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mineral density (BMD), mechanical properties (yield strength, stiffness, Elastic modulus), increasing calcium and ash content, and upregulating osteogenic gene expression (ALP, OC, OPG, BMP1) in the femur and tibia. Compared to CVD, LNVD led to significantly higher ADG from days 1–84 and greater bone indices at days 28, 56 and 84, including fresh bone weight (FBW), fat-free dry weight (FFDW), yield strength, elastic modulus, and calcium and ash content in the femur and tibia. In contrast, HNVD significantly decreased ADG and bone indices. Furthermore, cecal microbiome and metabolomics analysis showed that LNVD increased the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Ligilactobacillus, Muribaculaceae, NK4A214_group) and key metabolites (e.g., butyric acid, kynurenic acid, glutathione), while reducing harmful taxa (e.g., Desulfovibrio, Campylobacter_jejuni) and detrimental metabolites (e.g., leukotriene E3, 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal-Cys-Gly conjugate). These shifts significantly correlated with improved growth and bone traits.ConclusionsIn summary, 2,500 IU/kg nano-VD₃ is recommended as the optimal supplementation level for Luhua broilers under the conditions of this study, offering a strategy to enhance VD₃ nutrition and skeletal health.Keywords: Nano-vitamin D₃, Skeletal traits, Broiler chicken, Microbiomics, Metabolomics

Introduction

While intensive poultry farming has significantly enhanced growth and feed efficiency of broilers in recent decades [1], skeletal development frequently fails to keep pace with rapid body mass gain [2], exhibiting reduced bone mineral density, diminished bone strength, and disrupted balance between cancellous and cortical bone [3]. This discrepancy has led to an increased incidence of leg disorders (such as tibial dyschondroplasia, femoral head necrosis, rickets, and skeletal deformities) that impair locomotion, feeding behavior, and overall welfare, ultimately resulting in higher mortality and reduced carcass yield and meat quality [4].

Bone development in poultry is regulated by multiple factors, including lighting conditions and dietary mineral levels and sources [5]. Vitamin D₃ (VD₃) serves as a key endocrine regulator of calcium–phosphate homeostasis and bone mineralization, functioning in synergy with parathyroid hormone (PTH) and fibroblast growth factor 23 [6]. However, in intensive poultry production systems, limited sunlight exposure severely restricts endogenous vitamin D (VD) synthesis, necessitating dietary supplementation. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of dietary VD₃ and its extremely low aqueous solubility in gastrointestinal fluids results in poor bioavailability [7], with substantial inter-individual absorption variability [8]. These challenges underscore the need for optimized delivery strategies to enhance VD₃ efficacy [9]. Emerging delivery systems include nanoemulsions, nanoencapsulation platforms, lipid-based carriers, and protein-based carriers [10]. VD₃ Nanoemulsions (< 100 nm) improved gastrointestinal bioavailability and prolonged release [11], with promising outcomes demonstrated in human and rodent studies [12]. Nevertheless, excessive VD₃ intake led to renal calcification [13] and induced nanoparticle-related cytotoxicity [14]. Consequently, optimal supplemental dosages for broilers, especially medium-growing breeds, require further investigation.

VD₃ exerted immunomodulatory effects, preserving intestinal barrier integrity and reshaping gut microbiota composition [15]. Growing evidence showed that gut microbiota and their metabolites regulated bone turnover by modulating osteoblast and osteoclast activity [16]. Through the gut-bone axis, microbial metabolites derived from dietary nutrients inhibited bone resorption, promoted bone formation, and reduced bone loss [17]. This functional interplay suggests that dietary VD, through its microbiome-modulating effects, may offer synergistic benefits for skeletal health. In this study, we systematically evaluated the effects of commercial conventional VD₃ and nano-VD₃ at varying dosages on growth performance and bone development to explore optimal supplemental levels in Luhua broilers, a medium-growing breed. Furthermore, we analysed the cecal microbiome and metabolome to investigate how nano-VD₃ supplementation affects bone development via the gut–bone axis. Our study aims to provide an actionable VD nutritional strategy to enhance growth and bone health in broilers.

Materials and methods

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Ministry of Science and Technology, China, 2004) and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Northwest Minzu University (Permit No. xbmu-sm-20210130). Commercial conventional VD3 (500,000 IU/kg, Realcan Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Yantai, China) and nano-VD₃ (5,000,000 IU/kg, Luoxing Biology Studio, Shanghai, China) were used.

Structural characterization of VD₃ and nano-VD₃

The microstructural features of conventional VD₃ and nano-VD₃ were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; EVO 18, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Powder samples were mounted on aluminum stubs with conductive double-sided carbon tape, and loosely adhered particles were gently removed with an air blower. A 5–8 nm Au film was deposited via sputter coating (SC7620, Quorum Technologies) at a current of 10 mA. Micrographs were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV with a working distance of 5.6–7.3 mm.

For aqueous preparations, conventional VD₃ and nano-VD₃ powders were separately dispersed in distilled water at the same concentration and sonicated for 10 min under the same conditions to improve dispersion and minimize agglomeration. A 5–10 µL aliquot of each suspension was drop-cast onto carbon-tape-covered SEM stubs and air-dried at room temperature for approximately 24 h to allow complete solvent evaporation. The dried films were then sputter-coated as above and imaged under the same SEM conditions.

Animals and experimental design

A total of 420 one-day-old male Luhua broilers, purchased from Lanzhou Hualong Poultry Breeding Co., Ltd. (Lanzhou, China), were randomly assigned to four treatment groups, each with seven replicates (15 birds per replicate). The treatments were as follows: (1) CON, basal diet; (2) CVD, basal diet supplemented with 3,750 IU/kg conventional VD3; (3) LNVD, basal diet supplemented with 2,500 IU/kg nano-VD₃; and (4) HNVD, basal diet supplemented with 3,750 IU/kg nano-VD₃. The ‌84-day feeding trial‌ was conducted, with a basal diet formulated according to the ‌Chinese Chicken Feeding Standard (GB/T 5916 − 2020, China). The trial was conducted at ShunHe broiler breeding farm (Lanzhou, China) using a flat net-rearing system. Each replicate was kept in an individual pen (200 × 100 cm; 70 cm above the concrete floor) constructed from a stainless-steel frame covered with flat wire mesh and equipped with a round feeder pan and five nipple drinkers. All birds were housed in a building with controlled ventilation and a wet-curtain cooling system. The coop temperature was maintained at 35–37 °C with 50% relative humidity (RH) during the first week and was reduced by approximately 2 °C per week until reaching 24 °C with 45% RH. The lighting schedule provided 23 h light/1 h dark per day for chicks aged 1–28 days, and 16 h light/8 h dark per day for broilers aged 29–84 days. The feeding trial lasted for 84 days, during which birds had ad libitum access to feed and water. Newcastle disease and infectious bursal disease polyvalent vaccines were administered on days 7 and 14, respectively.

Growth performance

All broilers were weighed at days 1, 28, 56 and 84 after a 12 h fast. Feed intake was measured daily for each replicate. Body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and F/G (feed/gain) were calculated. Mortality was recorded and performance parameters were corrected for mortality.

Sample collection

At days 28, 56 and 84, after a 12-h fast, two broilers per replicate with body weights close to the group average were selected from each treatment. Blood samples (5 mL) were collected from the wing vein of each broiler, and serum samples were separated for the determination of calcium and phosphorus concentrations. The broilers were then euthanized via intravenous sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg body weight). The abdomen was disinfected with 75% ethanol and immediately opened. The entire intestine was carefully removed from the abdominal cavity, and the ceca were separated with a sterile scalpel. The cecal contents were collected, immediately homogenized, transferred to cryogenic vials, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until processed for microbial DNA analysis.

The legs were removed, and the fibula, cartilage, skin, and muscles were excised. The left femur and tibia were used for morphological analysis and stored at −20 °C for subsequent mechanical testing, while the right-side bones were used to determine mineral content.

Bone morphological characteristics

Frozen tibias were thawed at room temperature for 2 h before analysis. Bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mineral density (BMD) were determined using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DCS-600; Aloka, Tokyo, Japan) with settings optimised for small animal models [18].

The left femur and tibia were weighed, then dried at 100 °C for 24 h. Soxhlet reflux extraction was performed for 48 h at 70 °C using 333 mL of 95% ethanol and 667 mL benzene. Fat-free bones were dried at 100 °C for 24 h and reweighed to determine fat-free dry weight (FFDW). Bone length and width were measured using a digital caliper (Fig. 1).

Bone biomechanical characteristics

The femora and tibiae used for X-ray measurements were subsequently subjected to a 3-point bending test, according to the method described by Jungmann et al. [19], using an Instron electromechanical universal testing machine (Instron, Norwood, MA, USA). Related indices of calcium and phosphorus

Serum calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) concentrations were measured using a Beckman LX-20 automatic biochemical analyser (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Fullerton, CA, USA) by the ion-selective electrode method. Dried femur and tibia were crushed, ashed on an electric stove, and incinerated in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 12 h (SX2-4–10; Shanghai Gengfa Pharmaceutical Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The ash was dissolved in nitric and perchloric acids; Ca content was determined by titration with ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), and P content was determined by the molybdenum yellow colorimetric method according to AOAC [22]. Ash, Ca, and P contents were expressed as a percentage of fat-free dry weight (FFDW).

Gene expression assays

Total RNA was extracted from femoral and tibial bone tissues using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa CAS. No. 9190). RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis, and RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer based on A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios. cDNA was synthesized with a PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China). Amplification was carried out in a total volume of 10 µL containing 5 µL of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (SparkJade, Shandong, China), 1 µL of cDNA, 0.4 µL of each forward and reverse primer, and 3.2 µL RNase-Free ddH2O. qRT-PCR was performed on a LineGene9660 real-time PCR system (BIOER, Hangzhou, China) with the following reaction conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The relative mRNA expression of target genes was calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method and β-actin as an internal control. The information of primers used in this study was listed in Table 2.

16S rRNA sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was isolated from the cecal contents using the TGuide S96 kit (DP812; Tiangen Biotech Co., China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and quality of the DNA were verified in 0.8% agarose gels. Subsequently, the full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers (27 F, AGRGTTTGATYNTGGCTCAG and 1492R, TASGGHTACCTTGTTASGACTT). The purified PCR products were used to construct the single-read sequencing library on the PacBio platform (Biomarker-Technologies Co., China), following the manufacturer’s specifications. SMRT-Link v8.0 was used to correct the original subreads to obtain Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS) sequence. The lima v1.7.0 software was used to identify the CCS sequence of different samples through the barcode sequence and remove the chimera (UCHIME v4.2), and obtain effective-CCS sequences. The generated datasets were analyzed using USEARCH v10.0. High-quality sequences were clustered as operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on 97% similarity. BMK Cloud (www.biocloud.net) was used for alpha diversity, beta diversity, and microbial composition analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics of cecal contents was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS system coupled to a Xevo G2-XS QTof high-resolution mass spectrometer (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA). Separation used an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm). Data were acquired in MSE mode with alternating low (2 eV) and elevated (10–40 eV) collision energies; the scan time was 0.2 s. Electrospray ionization (ESI) settings were: capillary voltage 2.0 kV (positive-ion mode) or −1.5 kV (negative-ion mode), cone voltage 30 V, source temperature 150 °C, desolvation temperature 500 °C, cone gas 50 L/h, and desolvation gas 800 L/h.

Raw data acquired with MassLynx v4.2 were processed in Progenesis QI for peak picking, alignment, and related data processing. Putative metabolites were annotated by matching to the METLIN database and an in-house library (Biomarker) with theoretical fragment matching and a mass error ≤ 100 ppm. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Spearman correlation were used to assess within-group repeatability and the stability of quality-control (QC) samples. Identified compounds were annotated for class and pathway information using KEGG, HMDB, and the LIPID MAPS database.

Statistical analysis

Routine statistical analyses were performed in SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Unless otherwise stated, data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 8.02 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). For two-group comparisons, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied; for multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used (α = 0.05). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the ropls package in R, and model robustness was assessed with a 200-permutation test. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were obtained by cross-validation. Differential metabolites were identified based on fold change (FC ≥ 2.0), VIP > 1.0, and P < 0.05. KEGG pathway enrichment of the differential metabolites was evaluated using the hypergeometric test. Correlations between the microbiota and metabolites were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, with |r| ≥ 0.80 and P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Characterization of VD₃ and nano-VD₃

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of powders and aqueous suspensions of VD₃ and nano-VD₃ are shown in Fig. 2. The dry VD₃ powder exhibited relatively coarse, aggregated particles with occasional smooth regions and adhered fragments. In contrast, the dry nano-VD₃ powder displayed a smooth, spherical morphology with much finer and more uniformly distributed nanoparticles, consistent with their smaller particle size. The VD₃ aqueous suspension formed irregular, porous, and loosely connected structures, while nano-VD₃ suspension showed a homogeneous dispersion with numerous nanoscale spherical particles embedded in a continuous network (Fig. 2D). These morphological characteristics suggest an enhancement in the solubility and bioavailability of nano-VD₃.

Growth performance

There was no significant difference (P > 0.05) in the mortality of broilers among groups (data not shown). The effects of VD₃ supplementation on growth performance in broilers were presented in Table 3. There were no significant differences (P > 0.05) in body weight (BW) on days 1 and 28, and ADG, ADFI, and F/G from day 1 to 28 among the groups. Compared with the CON group, LNVD significantly increased (P < 0.05) BW on days 56 and 84, ADG from day 29 to 56, day 57 to 84, and day 1 to 84, and ADFI from day 57 to 84, while showing a better (P < 0.05) F/G from day 29 to 56. CVD significantly increased (P < 0.05) BW on day 84, ADG and ADFI from day 57 to 84, and ADG from day 1 to 84, with an improved (P < 0.05) F/G from day 29 to 56; however, HNVD significantly reduced (P < 0.05) BW on day 84 and ADG from day 57 to 84. Additionally, LNVD treatment showed significantly increased (P < 0.05) BW at day 56 and 84, and ADG from day 1 to 84 compared to CVD. These results indicated that both CVD and LNVD enhanced the growth performance of broilers, with LNVD showing a greater improvement; in contrast, HNVD had detrimental effects.

Bone morphological characteristics

To investigate the effect of VD₃ supplementation on bone development and biological structure in broilers, morphological characteristics of femur and tibia were measured (Fig. 3; Table 4). Compared with CON, LNVD significantly increased (P < 0.05) FBW, FFDW, width, BMC, and BMD of the femur and tibia in broilers at day 28; increased (P < 0.05) BMC and BMD of both femur and tibia at day 56; and increased (P < 0.05) FBW and FFDW of the femur and tibia, BMC and BMD of the femur, and BMC of the tibia at day 84. CVD significantly increased (P < 0.05) BMC and BMD of the tibia at day 28, BMC and BMD of both femur and tibia at day 56, as well as FBW and BMC of the femur and FBW and FFDW of the tibia at day 84. However, HNVD significantly decreased (P < 0.05) FBW, FFDW, and BMD of the tibia at day 28; and decreased (P < 0.05) FFDW of the femur, and FBW of the tibia at day 84. Additionally, LNVD treatment showed significantly increased (P < 0.05) FBW, FFDW, width, BMC, and BMD of both femur and tibia at day 28, FBW, FFDW and BMD of the femur at day 84 compared to CVD. These results indicated that both CVD and LNVD enhanced skeletal development and bone quality, with LNVD demonstrating superior efficacy, whereas HNVD had detrimental effects.

Discussion

Under intensive farming conditions, calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) deficiencies and the resulting skeletal disorders are highly prevalent in broilers due to their physiological characteristics and the low VD bioavailability, leading to significant economic losses. Studies have shown that dietary supplementation with various forms of vitamin D3 and its metabolites promoted broiler nutrient utilization, growth, and skeletal development [23]. However, the effects of VD₃ are limited by its form and the dosage supplemented. Supplementation 2,400 and 3,600 IU/kg of VD₃ improved the growth performance and carcass traits of yellow-feathered broilers during the early stage, and only 1,200 IU/kg of VD3 decreased the abdominal fat percentage [24]. 25 µg/kg of 25(OH)D₃, increased the BW and ADG, while improving the F/G at different phases of growth [25]. Sheikhsamani et al. [26] reported an optimal supplementation level of 800 IU/kg for nano-VD₃ and a maximum recommended level of 1600 IU/kg in Cobb 500 broiler chickens. Discrepancies observed in studies probably stem from species-specific metabolic differences or factors like dietary calcium and/or phosphorus levels and ultraviolet light in experimental designs.

In this study, supplementation with conventional VD₃ (CVD) at 3,750 IU/kg or nano-VD₃ (LNVD) at 2,500 IU/kg both significantly increased ADG from day 57 to 84 and from day 1 to 84, and ADFI from day 57 to 84, while reducing F/G from day 29 to 56. However, the LNVD group showed a significantly greater increase in BW at days 56 and 84, and ADG from day 57 to 84 and day 1 to 84, compared to the CVD group. This suggests that the LNVD dosage at 2,500 IU/kg could have a more favorable effect on growth performance than the higher-dose CVD. The results indicate that both CVD and nano-VD₃ supplementation can enhance the growth performance of Luhua broiler chickens, but lower dosage nano-VD₃ (2,500 IU/kg) appears to be sufficient due to its higher bioavailability. Morphological analysis by scanning electron microscopy showed that nano-VD₃ particles were more uniform and smaller, with homogeneous dispersion in suspension, which improved gastrointestinal stability and bioavailability [27]. Nano-VD₃ has demonstrated greater improvements in growth performance and bone quality compared to conventional micro-VD₃ [26]. Nano-delivery enhanced gastrointestinal stability and dispersion of VD₃, promoted its mucosal and lymphatic transport, thereby increasing circulating 25(OH)D₃ levels and bioactive exposure at equivalent or lower doses compared to conventional formulations [11].

Optimized VD₃ supplementation programs enhanced growth and tibial traits, however, excessive VD intake reduced body weight and relative bone weight despite improving mineralization [28]. In this study, nano-VD₃ supplementation at 3,750 IU/kg led to decreased BW at day 84 and ADG from day 56 to 84, indicating that this dosage is supra-optimal for broiler growth. Collectively, these findings demonstrate a form- and dose-dependent effect of VD₃ on broiler growth.

Broilers are prone to skeletal developmental abnormalities due to insufficient mineral supply or low bioavailability, particularly Ca and/or P. The structural, morphological, and mechanical characteristics of bones largely determine animal morphology and physical capabilities [29]. Bone morphological characteristics, such as FBW, FFDW, BMC, BMD, length, and width, provide comprehensive insights into bone development, mineralization, and structural integrity. Poor bone morphological indices were associated with compromised bone health and increased fracture risk, reflecting their importance in assessing broiler skeletal quality [30]. Dietary factors like Ca, P, and VD levels influenced bone quality, with optimal levels enhancing femur BMD and breaking strength (BBS) [28]. In this study, LNVD significantly increased FBW, FFDW, BMC, and BMD of the femur and tibia in broilers at days 28 and 84, as well as BMC and BMD at day 56. Similarly, CVD increased BMC and BMD of the tibia at days 28 and 56, as well as FBW and BMC of the femur and FBW and FFDW of the tibia at day 84. However, neither LNVD nor CVD affected femoral or tibial length and width. These findings align with studies showing that tibia ash content, BMC, and BMD were sensitive indicators of dietary Ca and VD levels in broilers [31]. VD form (e.g., D3 and 25(OH)D3) and supplement dosage did not influence femur length and width, although BBS and ash weight increased with higher dosage [32]. These results showed that both LNVD and CVD promoted bone development and improved bone quality by enhancing mineral deposition and matrix accrual.

Bone mechanical properties, including elastic modulus, yield strength, and toughness, are fundamental to skeletal integrity, which in turn critically influences locomotion and growth performance. The bone structure adapts to locomotor demands. Specific adaptations, such as increased elastic modulus in the hind femur of jumping animals, mitigated fracture risk by allowing controlled deformation during high-stress activities [33]. Mechanical properties are dynamic during growth. Sarcomere division in muscle fibers, triggered by skeletal growth and contractions, ensures mechanical integrity and synchronizes tissue development [34]. Impaired mechanical properties lead to disorders like tibial dyschondroplasia (TD), reducing bone strength and density [29]. Our results demonstrated that both CVD and LNVD improved the mechanical competence of long bones, particularly during the starter and finisher phases, as evidenced by increased yield strength, fracture energy, and elastic modulus of both the femur and tibia at days 28 and 84. However, CVD had no significant effect on bone mechanical indices at day 56, while LNVD increased femur stiffness and energy to fracture. This could be related to the growth and development process of broiler chickens. In medium-growth broiler chickens, the grower phase (29 to 56 days) is optimal for nutritional metabolism and health status, as it is characterized by high nutrient utilization efficiency. Consequently, VD3 supplementation may have a relatively limited effect during this period. This aligns with evidence that dietary VD3 positively affected the growth-immunity and bone development of broilers during the early stage [24].

In this study, LNVD elevated serum calcium levels without affecting phosphorus levels, whereas CVD had no significant impact on either calcium or phosphorus levels. Calcium plays crucial roles in bone metabolism, nerve signal regulation, and neurotransmitter release. Elevated serum calcium indicates improved calcium homeostasis, which is essential for skeletal health and other physiological functions. In poultry, due to their rapid growth and development, adequate calcium is particularly important for bone development. Studies show that calcium and VD synergistically regulate calcium absorption and bone mineralization through the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) pathway [35]. VD enhanced intestinal calcium absorption by upregulating calcium transporters (e.g., TRPV6 and CaBP-D28k) [36], which accounted for the observed increase in serum calcium level with LNVD supplementation. However, studies found no association between serum VD and phosphorus levels [37]. Dietary 25(OH)D3 improved bone quality in poultry without consistently affecting serum phosphorus [38]. VD primarily modulated calcium homeostasis, while phosphorus regulation involved complex pathways (e.g., FGF23, renal transporters) that did not always respond to VD supplementation [39]. This suggested LNVD could improve calcium homeostasis by enhancing calcium absorption, whereas supplementation with conventional VD₃ at 3,750 IU/kg was insufficient to achieve adequate calcium absorption in Luhua broilers.

The improvement of bone mineral and calcium content in broilers has significant impacts on bone quality and health. High mineral content (particularly Ca and P) directly enhanced bone stiffness and mechanical strength [40]. Studies suggested that elevated mineral content led to increased BMD, BBS, and cortical thickness [39, 41]. Mineral accumulation also enhanced trabecular structure by increasing trabecular number and promoting osteoblast proliferation and differentiation [42]. VD3 supplementation significantly increased tibial calcium content and femoral mineral content in broilers [43]. The 25(OH)D3 enhanced bone quality by improving calcium transport and deposition [38]. Dietary interventions with VD showed dose-dependent linear relationships with BMD at key skeletal sites [44]. Mechanistically, VD3 upregulated calcium-binding proteins such as calbindin-D28k and epithelial transporters, thereby facilitating skeletal mineral entry [45]. In this study, LNVD increased ash and calcium content in both the femur and tibia at days 28 and 84, while CVD increased tibial ash content at day 84. These results indicated that VD3 supplementation enhanced bone mineralization by improving calcium absorption and homeostasis, thereby improving bone density and strength in broilers. Nevertheless, nano-VD₃ showed superiority in the improvement of performance and bone mineral traits due to its enhanced bioavailability.

Bone development constitutes a dynamic process orchestrated by the coordinated activities of osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Osteoblasts, as the primary bone-forming cells, are responsible for synthesizing organic bone matrix components and facilitating subsequent mineralization through the secretion of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and other regulatory factors. In contrast, osteoclasts execute bone resorption via the secretion of protons and proteolytic enzymes, thereby creating a conducive microenvironment for osteoblast-mediated bone formation [46]. This tightly regulated reciprocal interaction between bone-forming and bone-reabsorbing cells ensures the continuous turnover and remodeling of bone tissue. The function of osteoblasts is regulated by multiple factors, including ALP, osteocalcin (OC), and osteoprotegerin (OPG). OPG is a key regulator of bone remodeling that functions as a soluble decoy receptor for receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL). By competitively binding to RANKL, OPG blocks its interaction with RANK on osteoclast precursors, thereby inhibiting osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption [47]. ALP facilitates bone mineralization by hydrolyzing organophosphates to release inorganic phosphate for hydroxyapatite formation [48]. OC, a calcium-binding protein primarily synthesized by osteoblasts and odontoblasts, binds to hydroxyapatite in bone to regulate calcium metabolism and bone mineralization [49]. Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), as members of the TGF-β superfamily, regulate skeletal tissue formation and regeneration by promoting mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) differentiation into osteoblasts and chondroblasts [50]. In this study, ‌LNVD significantly enhanced mRNA expression of ALP, OC, OPG, and BMP1 in both femur and tibia at days 28 and 84, whereas CVD upregulated their expression exclusively at day 84. Studies have shown that dietary 25(OH)D₃ increased OPG expression and lowered the RANKL/OPG ratio in broilers under heat stress [51]. VD₃ promoted osteoblast activity (ALP, OC) and lowered the RANKL/OPG ratio to restrain resorption by VDR signaling [52]. This suggested that LNVD could promote bone development by enhancing bone formation and mineralization through upregulating the expression of osteogenic genes, while CVD had a limited effect.

Consistent with our findings, Sommerfeld et al. [53] demonstrated that the nano-delivery system enhanced gastrointestinal stability and dispersion, promoted mucosal and lymphatic transport, and increased circulating 25(OH)D₃ levels at equivalent or lower inclusion doses. However, HNVD supplementation ‌lowered‌ bone calcium and ash content, ‌compromised‌ several mechanical properties, and ‌thereby reduced‌ bone quality in this study. This negative effect indicated that the 3,750 IU/kg supplemental dose of nano-VD₃ might be excessive for broilers. Studies have demonstrated that supraphysiological active VD disrupted the calcium-phosphorus balance and mineral distribution, leading to an impaired bone mineral content and strength [54, 55], whereas moderate supplementation increased bone ash and strength [56].

The gut microbiota, functioning as the microbial barrier of the intestinal tract, plays a critical role in host metabolism, nutrition, and immune processes ‌[57]. Certain probiotic bacteria influenced skeletal development through the gut-bone axis [58]. Our study revealed that LNVD supplementation significantly enhanced cecal microbiota richness and diversity in broilers during the early growth stage. Although Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were the dominant bacterial phyla across all three growth stages of broilers, LNVD significantly reduced the relative abundance of Desulfobacterota and Actinobacteriota at day 56 while increasing that of Bacteroidota at day 84, suggesting a persistent modulation of the microbial community composition by LNVD. This aligns with studies showing that 25(OH)D₃ modulated gut microbiota composition in laying ducks at later stages [59].

We further analyzed shifts at the genus level to identify the bacterial taxa responsible for LNVD-induced improvements in growth and bone properties in broilers. Among dominant bacterial genera, LNVD increased the abundance of Phascolarctobacterium at day 56 and Bacteroides at day 84. Bacteroides degraded complex polysaccharides, producing fermentable substrates that were subsequently fermented into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [60], while Phascolarctobacterium, an asaccharolytic genus, utilized succinate provided by other microbes to generate propionate [61]. SCFAs signaled through GPR41/43 receptors to suppress osteoclastogenesis and promote osteoblast activity, thereby enhancing bone BMC, BMD, and mechanical strength [62, 63]. ‌A LEfSe analysis also revealed that LNVD supplementation altered the composition of cecal microbiota taxa at the genus and species levels in an age-dependent manner, thereby progressively remodeling the gut microbial structure. Among bacterial taxa with significantly increased abundance at day 28, Ligilactobacillus was positively correlated with BW, serum and femur calcium, and various bone phenotype indices, such as BMC, BMD, energy to fracture, and elastic modulus. This is supported by a study showing that enrichment of Ligilactobacillus had a protective effect against trabecular bone loss in Murine models of osteoporosis [64]. Unclassified_Clostridia_vadinBB60_group showed a positive correlation with serum calcium, femur calcium, energy to fracture, and elastic modulus. It has been demonstrated to facilitate pyruvate fermentation to butanoate in broilers [65], thereby modulating immune and bone metabolism [66]. At day 56, NK4A214_group, unclassified_Muribaculaceae, and unclassified_Prevotellaceae were positively correlated with ADG and some bone phenotype indices (e.g., energy to fracture, stiffness). These SCFAs-producing bacteria enhanced the production of SCFAs and butyrate, promoting the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) to modulate local and systemic immune homeostasis, thereby influencing bone metabolism [67]. This also facilitated a bone-beneficial ecological shift in the gut microbiota, which alleviated tibial dyschondroplasia in broilers [68]. Furthermore, Ligilactobacillus_salivarius has been reported to improve tibial bone parameters (such as breaking strength and mineral indices) in broilers [69]. Its enrichment prevented osteoporotic phenotypes in mice, suggesting a potential osteoprotective role through the gut-bone axis [70]. Similarly, supplementation with Lactobacillus_johnsonii enhanced skeletal development in broilers by increasing circulating insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), a known regulator of long-bone growth [71]. Additionally, LNVD increased the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria at day 84, including Bacteroides, unclassified Erysipelatoclostridiaceae, Lactobacillus intestinalis, and Enterococcus. These findings demonstrated that LNVD enhanced SCFA production by promoting gut probiotics proliferation, thereby improving growth performance and skeletal development in broilers.

In contrast, LNVD led to a reduction in unclassified_Desulfovibrionaceae and Campylobacter_jejuni at day 28, Desulfovibrio at day 56, and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 at day 84. The reduction in Desulfovibrio alleviated the disruption of tight junctions caused by hydrogen sulfide [72], while the decrease in Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 ‌was associated with increased expression of tight junction markers and decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in broilers [73]. Campylobacter_jejuni colonizing the intestinal mucus layer causes gastroenteritis and diarrhea by inducing pro-inflammatory responses and releasing cytokines and chemokines [74]. This indicated that LNVD promoted the growth and bone health of broilers by reducing the abundance of harmful gut bacteria, maintaining intestinal barrier function, and suppressing inflammatory responses. In summary, LNVD reshapes the gut microbiota into a healthier profile by increasing the abundance of probiotics and reducing harmful bacteria, which supports growth and bone development in broilers.

The gut microbiota closely communicates and interacts with the host through its small-molecule metabolites. The gut microbiota metabolizes dietary substrates into bioactive small-molecule metabolites that influence the liver, kidney, brain, and skeleton [75]. Our study revealed that LNVD significantly modulated cecal metabolic profiles in an age-dependent way. At day 28, LNVD upregulated butyric acid and kynurenic acid, which exhibited a positive correlation with BW, serum calcium, and various bone phenotype indices, such as BMC, BMD, FFDW, energy to fracture, and elastic modulus. Studies have shown that elevated butyrate levels and activation of butanoate metabolism enhanced SCFA production, thereby promoting osteoblast differentiation and inhibiting osteoclastogenesis, which ultimately increases bone mass [76]. Kynurenic acid stimulated osteoblastogenesis and inhibited osteoclastogenesis by reducing NF-κB p65 phosphorylation through activation of the GPR35 receptor [77]. In contrast, LNVD decreased the abundance of leukotriene E3 and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (HNE)-Cys-Gly conjugate, which exhibited a negative correlation with ADG, serum calcium, and some bone phenotype indices, such as BMC, BMD, FFDW, energy to fracture, and elastic modulus. Leukotriene E3, as a metabolite of arachidonic acid and a leukotriene produced by the 5-lipoxygenase pathway, promoted bone resorption and accelerated bone loss through its pro-inflammatory effects [78]. The HNE-Cys-Gly conjugate induced bone tissue damage through its role in promoting oxidative stress [79]. The downregulation of HNE-Cys-Gly, a conjugate formed from the lipid peroxidation product HNE and the dipeptide cysteine-glycine, indicated enhanced glutathione-dependent detoxification, thereby reducing oxidative stress and cellular damage. The metabolism of alpha-linolenic acid contributed to the suppression of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators, including leukotrienes and HNE conjugates, thus attenuating inflammatory signaling that promotes osteoclast activation [80].

At day 56, LNVD upregulated 15-cis-phytoene and 3-dehydro-L-threonate, which were positively correlated with NK4A214_group and unclassified_Muribaculaceae, as well as ADG and some bone phenotype indices, such as energy to fracture and stiffness. 15-cis-phytoene, a specific stereoisomer of phytoene, is a key intermediate in carotenoid biosynthesis in plants and microbes. Phytoene demonstrated protective effects against oxidative damage and amyloid-β proteotoxicity [81]. 3-dehydro-L-threonate, as part of the ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathway, likely influenced bone metabolism through its role in oxidative stress reduction, inflammation modulation, and metabolic regulation. Additionally, decreased leukotriene E3 showed a significant negative correlation with enhanced growth performance and certain bone phenotype indices.

At day 84, LNVD significantly increased the abundance of glutathione (GSH), peracetic acid, and all-trans-13,14-dihydroretinol (atDR). These metabolites exhibited positive correlations with growth performance (BW, ADG) and various bone phenotype indices, such as multiple skeletal phenotypes, particularly femur and tibia biomechanical properties (e.g., energy to fracture, stiffness, and elastic modulus) and mineralization-related indices (e.g., BMC and BMD). The enhanced microbial GSH synthesis observed in low-oxidative-stress cows compared with high-oxidative-stress cows suggests a protective role of microbiota-derived GSH against oxidative damage [82]. Studies have demonstrated that Sunset Yellow (SY)-induced gut microbiota-dependent GSH metabolic dysregulation promoted colitis in mice, whereas GSH supplementation reversed the SY-induced growth inhibition [83]. Exogenous GSH alleviated liver injury in piglets through host-microbiome interaction and a constitutive androstane receptor (CAR)-dependent signaling pathway [84]. atDR was produced through bacterial conversion of dietary vitamin A (VA), primarily involving commensal bacteria such as Lactobacillus and other anaerobic gut microbes [85]. This metabolite was functionally linked to retinol metabolic pathways and contributes to host immune homeostasis and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions [86]. Peracetic acid, derived from the hydrolysis of encapsulated precursors in feed, modulated gut microbiota composition and metabolic pathways, thus improving growth performance in poultry [87]. To sum up, LNVD alters cecal metabolic profiles by promoting the growth of probiotics while suppressing the proliferation of harmful bacteria, thereby improving growth performance and bone development in broilers.

Conclusion

In summary, supplementation with both 3,750 IU/kg conventional VD3 (CVD) and 2,500 IU/kg nano-VD₃ (LNVD) enhanced the growth performance and bone development in broilers by improving morphological and mechanical properties, increasing calcium and ash content, and upregulating osteogenic gene expression in the femur and tibia. Notably, LNVD demonstrated superior effects on growth and bone properties compared to CVD, whereas 3,750 IU/kg nano-VD₃ (HNVD) supplementation had detrimental effects. Furthermore, cecal microbiome and metabolomics analysis suggested that LNVD promoted growth and bone development via the gut-bone axis by increasing beneficial bacteria (e.g., Ligilactobacillus, Muribaculaceae, NK4A214_group) and key metabolites (e.g., butyric acid, kynurenic acid, glutathione) while reducing harmful bacteria (e.g., Desulfovibrio, Campylobacter_jejuni, Prevotellaceae_UCG-001) and metabolites (leukotriene E3 and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal-Cys-Gly conjugate). Based on these findings, we recommend that 2,500 IU/kg nano-vitamin D₃ is the optimal supplementation level for medium-growing Luhua broilers, enhancing growth performance during the grower and finisher phases and on bone health. However, this study was conducted under specific dietary calcium and phosphorus levels, and variations in these levels may influence the actual vitamin D3 requirement in broilers.
