Service utilization patterns in cocaine use disorder: A data-driven study
Authors: Carlotta Riemerschmid, Larissa Schwarzkopf, Gabriele Koller, Elena Gomes de Matos, Esther Neumeier, Sally Olderbak, Eva Hoch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver the past decade, cocaine use disorders (CoUD) have become increasingly prevalent in Europe’s outpatient addiction care settings. This study examines clinically meaningful subgroups among individuals seeking outpatient care for CoUD in Berlin, a metropolitan area with particularly high prevalence rates to inform treatment strategies.MethodsUsing 2024 person-level routine data of the Berlin Addiction Care Statistical Service, latent class analyses (LCA) were conducted to identify subgroups among clients entering outpatient care with primary CoUD (n = 1223) based on patterns of substance use disorder (SUD) comorbidity and cocaine use prior to admission. Identified classes were subsequently compared with respect to sociodemographic characteristics and treatment-related variables applying Holm-Bonferroni adjusted group comparisons.ResultsA three-class solution best fitted the data: (1) CoUD-only (low comorbidity), (2) CoUD+Alcohol Use Disorders (AUD), and (3) CoUD+Multiple SUDs. The CoUD+AUD class was more likely to be employed and tertiary educated. The CoUD-only class showed more first-time treatment entries, shorter treatment duration, and lower rates of scheduled completion and symptom improvement. The CoUD+Multiple SUDs class showed comparable treatment outcomes to the CoUD-only class.DiscussionThese findings underscore the importance of recognizing heterogeneity in care needs among individuals with CoUD. Tailored treatment approaches, including early-stage interventions for less complex cases and integrated strategies for those with comorbid SUDs, may enhance treatment effectiveness and optimize resource allocation.Keywords: Outpatient addiction care, Cocaine, Cocaine dependence, Cocaine treatment, Latent class analysis, Comorbidity, Substance use disorders

Introduction

Cocaine availability and use rates have increased across Europe over the past decade (for an overview see: European Union Drugs Agency, 2025). More recently, cocaine-related treatment demand has been rising in Western and Central Europe (Antoine et al., 2021), including Germany (Riemerschmid et al., 2025).

Several review articles examined the efficacy of available treatment options for Cocaine Use Disorders (CoUD)6 in recent years (Bentzley et al., 2021, Brady, 2019, De Crescenzo et al., 2018, Kampman, 2019, Schwartz et al., 2022, Volkow et al., 2019). In contrast to other substance use disorders (SUDs), there is currently no approved pharmacotherapy for CoUD (Bentzley et al., 2021). Psychosocial interventions represent the international standard of care (Schwartz et al., 2022) and encompass a variety of treatment options. Contingency Management (CM) and specialized cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) are often named as particularly effective (Kampman, 2019, Schwartz et al., 2022), with CM showing the most consistent evidence for reducing cocaine use (Bentzley et al., 2021). However, CM is rarely implemented in routine care often due to financial constraints or ethical concerns (Bentzley et al., 2021, Kampman, 2019, Ronsley et al., 2020). Despite the effectiveness of certain psychosocial interventions in clinical trials, high rates of treatment discontinuation, return to use and readmissions are reported for individuals in treatment for CoUD (Dacosta-Sánchez et al., 2024, Kampman, 2019, Palazón-Llecha et al., 2024, Poireau et al., 2022). Heterogeneity in treatment outcomes has been linked to several factors, such as age, years of use, use prior to treatment entry, craving, and comorbid substance use disorders (Kampman, 2019, Palazón-Llecha et al., 2024).

To uncover latent heterogeneity in populations with SUDs, person-centered statistical approaches such as latent class analysis (LCA) have gained attention (Bonfiglio et al., 2022, Lanza and Rhoades, 2013). Findings from LCAs among people who use cocaine (Liu et al., 2019) and individuals with CoUD (Stiltner et al., 2023) have shown that polysubstance use patterns meaningfully differentiate clinical profiles, as comorbid SUDs can increase the severity of dependence (Ford et al., 2009).

Different combinations of SUDs may also shape needs for care (Ellis et al., 2023) and affect treatment outcomes (Wang et al., 2017). Most person-centered approaches in the context of cocaine use and CoUD have been implemented in population-based samples. Profiles of individuals seeking treatment may differ from population-based samples (Connor et al., 2013), as most individuals with CoUD only seek help once their disorder has significantly progressed (Requena-Ocaña et al., 2021). Identifying latent subgroups within treatment-seeking populations may provide a basis for more targeted treatment approaches and better alignment of available services with individual needs.

This is particularly relevant for CoUD, where, unlike other SUDs, no clinical practice guideline currently exists in Germany. Outpatient addiction care facilities represent a key entry point into the addiction care system for individuals with CoUD (Schwarzkopf et al., 2020). Services comprise a continuum of interventions ranging from low-threshold and outreach services to addiction counseling and psychosocial support, and further to specialized outpatient medical rehabilitation (Diedenhofen, 2026). Low-threshold services and counseling are generally available free of charge and without formal preconditions, whereas more intensive interventions, such as outpatient medical rehabilitation, require formal approval by statutory health or pension insurance (Deutsche Hauptstelle für Suchtfragen (DHS),2019). Care is person-centered, with individuals retaining the right to choose their treatment facility and type of service. Accordingly, patterns of service utilization across these settings can be understood as reflecting different levels of care. Overall, outpatient addiction care services are accessed by individuals from diverse social backgrounds, with heterogeneous clinical profiles, comorbidities, and treatment needs. Berlin is Germany’s largest metropolitan area and has one of the highest prevalence rates of cocaine use nationwide (Olderbak et al., 2023a, Olderbak et al., 2023b) and a widely differentiated outpatient addiction care system. These factors make Berlin a particularly suitable setting to examine real-world service utilization patterns. The present study aims to:

(1)Identify distinct subgroups in a sample of care-seekers with primary CoUD

Identify distinct subgroups in a sample of care-seekers with primary CoUD

(2)Compare the identified subgroups regarding their sociodemographic characteristics, clinical profiles, and treatment outcomes.

Method

Setting and data source

Data were derived from the Berlin Addiction Care Statistical Service (Berliner Suchthilfestatistik [BSHS]) which collects socio-demographic, clinical, and treatment-related data of individuals in addiction-specific inpatient and outpatient facilities in the federal city state of Berlin, Germany on an annual basis. The dataset comprises the total care volume with newly initiated as well as terminated care episodes within the respective reporting year. The BSHS routine documentation is standardized and adheres to the nationwide documentation standards of the German Core Dataset (DHS, 2023). It includes, but is not limited to, parameters of the pan European Treatment Demand Indicator (TDI, EMCDDA 2012). Within BSHS, each data entry refers to a distinct, newly initiated care episode and not to an individual help-seeker. Assignment of care episodes to distinct individuals is possible based on a unique-person identifier code. Primary and secondary addiction-related diagnoses are assessed at the beginning of each care episode based on the preceding 12 months, with the primary diagnosis reflecting the central reason for care-seeking. Diagnoses comprise the following ICD-10 codes: F10 – F19, F50, F55, F63.0, F63.8, F68.0 (Dilling et al., 2013). The data collection is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2013). The pseudonymization processes are in accordance with regional (Berlin Data Protection Law), national (German Data Protection Law), and international (European General Data Protection Regulation) data protection regulations and does not require formal ethics committee approval.

Sample selection

For the present analysis the sample was derived from the BSHS in 2024. Analyses were restricted to records of outpatient addiction care facilities. In total, 54 facilities (80.6% of all registered facilities in Berlin) participated.

Inclusion criteria

Terminated care episodes initiated between January 1, 2023, and December 30, 2024, were included. The two-year timeframe was chosen to ensure temporal consistency and minimize the impact of changes in care-seeking behavior or documentation practices. Care-seekers were required to have had at least two contacts with the facility. CoUD was required as the primary ICD-10 based diagnosis, defined as harmful cocaine use (F14.1) or cocaine dependence (F14.2; (Dilling et al., 2013)).

Exclusion criteria

Episodes with another primary diagnosis, missing person identifier code, or age below 18 years at admission were excluded. For individuals with more than one episode, only the first terminated episode in 2024 was retained. For details see Fig. 1.

Analysis

Latent class analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (RStudio IDE; (R Core Team, 2024)). We performed latent class analyses (LCAs) using the poLCA-package (Linzer and Lewis, 2011). Analytical procedures followed best practice guidelines and recommendations (Nylund-Gibson and Choi, 2018, Sinha et al., 2021, Weller et al., 2020).

Overall, six binary indicators (no = 1/ yes = 2) were defined according to literature. These were secondary ICD-based SUD diagnoses (F1X.1 misuse or F1X.2 dependence): (1) F10. Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD), (2) F11. Opioid Use Disorder (OUD), (3) F12. Cannabis Use Disorder (CUD), (4) F15. Stimulant Use Disorder (StUD; stimulant use disorders other than cocaine), (5) F17. Tobacco Use Disorder (TUD) (Aharonovich et al., 2005, Crummy et al., 2020, Ford et al., 2009, Kohut, 2017, Liu et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2019, Stiltner et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2017), and (6) self-reported cocaine use in the 30 days before admission (Palazón-Llecha et al., 2024).

The prevalence of all indicator variables was checked prior to the LCA as every category should at least represent 10% of the sample (Sinha et al., 2021). The assumption of local independence was assessed using contingency tables and Cramér’s V with a cut-off value of.50 (see Appendix A). (Sinha et al., 2021)

LCA models with k = 1–6 classes were estimated using the expectation maximization algorithm. Each model was estimated with multiple random starts and 30 iterations to avoid local maxima. To ensure convergence, the maximum number of iterations was set to 1000 (Nylund-Gibson and Choi, 2018). Model selection was primarily based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) with the lowest BIC indicating the best-fitted model as recommended for sample sizes above n = 500 (Sinha et al., 2021). Additionally, the sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC) was reported. To prevent overfitting, an elbow plot was inspected to determine the point at which adding an additional class did not substantially improve model fit (see Appendix B). For a comprehensive picture, smallest class size and entropy (closeness to 1) were examined to ensure sufficient class separation as well as the interpretability of the identified classes (Nylund-Gibson and Choi, 2018). The final model solution was visually inspected for the so-called “Salsa effect,” which occurs when classes reflect the same underlying pattern of indicator responses but differ only in their severity levels (Sinha et al., 2021). Average posterior probabilities were calculated for each latent class across all estimated models to assess classification certainty. These probabilities reflect the likelihood that individuals are correctly assigned to their most probable class. Average probabilities above.70 indicate acceptable classification certainty, values above.80 suggest good posterior classification, and values above.90 indicate excellent classification certainty (Nylund-Gibson and Choi, 2018).

Additionally, two sensitivity analyses (SA) were calculated: SA 1, treating missing values as a distinct category in the indicator variable “cocaine use”, and SA 2, excluding the two least prevalent comorbid SUDs (StUD and OUD), as their rates were below the recommended 10% threshold. Finally, the final class solution was described and labeled based on prevalences of indicator variables and the class-specific conditional response probabilities.

Class description and comparisons

Probabilities for indicator variables (comorbid SUDs, cocaine use) are reported descriptively. The final classes of the selected model were described and compared based on the following sociodemographic characteristics at admission: age (M, SD), “female” share, employment status (“employed”, “unemployed”, “other”), and school education level (“higher”, “primary or secondary”, “never completed primary school”). Furthermore, the following treatment-related characteristics were contrasted: CoUD severity (“F14.1 harmful use”, “F14.2 dependence”), age at first-time cocaine use (M, SD), lifetime problematic crack use (“yes”, “no”), prior contact to the addiction care system (“yes”, “no”), and primary treatment modality (“low threshold services”, “addiction counseling”, “outpatient medical rehabilitation”, “treatment in detention”, “other”). Additionally, the following treatment outcomes at termination were analyzed: duration of treatment (M, SD), treatment termination (dichotomized as "scheduled" [termination as planned or with clinical agreement] vs. "unscheduled" [premature termination without clinical agreement, disciplinary discharge, or unplanned transfer]), and symptomatology assessed by staff (dichotomized as "improved" vs. "stable", "worsened", or "newly developed").

For all variables external to the class model, global group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVAs for continuous variables and Pearson’s Chi-squared tests for categorical variables. For continuous variables, variance homogeneity was assessed using Levene’s test. In case of variance heterogeneity, Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. If global tests indicated significant group differences, pairwise post-hoc comparisons between latent classes were conducted. Starting with an initial alpha-level of.05, all p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Holm–Bonferroni method, with adjustments applied separately for baseline variables, outcome variables, and post-hoc pairwise comparisons.

Results

Between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2024, 1374 CoUD-related treatments were terminated. These episodes were assigned to 1359 individual care-seekers, treated in n = 34 facilities. After exclusion of additional episodes and individuals aged < 18, a total of n = 1223 episodes were included in the conducted LCAs (see Fig. 1). Overall, n = 138 episodes had missing values in the indicator variable “cocaine consumption”. This was addressed in SA 1.

Prevalences for indicator variables are presented in Table 1. Comorbid OUD (5.5%) and StUD (8.7%) were retained as separate indicators due to their theoretical and clinical relevance (Maahs et al., 2024), despite prevalence rates below the recommended threshold of 10%. The low prevalences were addressed in SA 2. All indicator variables showed low to moderate pairwise associations. Hence, local independence of the indicator variables was assumed. The strongest association was observed between AUD and TUD with a Cramer’s V of 0.38 (see Appendix A).

Class description

Conditional item response patterns are presented in Fig. 2. Class 1 represented 50.4% of the sample (n = 617) and showed very low probabilities for all comorbid SUDs (< 5%), combined with moderate probability for use of cocaine before admission (59.8%). This class was labeled “CoUD-only”.

Conditional item response patterns for indicator variables in the 3-class model (N = 1223).

Class 2 included 26.9% of the sample (n = 329). All individuals had a comorbid AUD (100%), and a moderate proportion had a comorbid TUD (45.9%), while probabilities of other SUDs were very low (< 6%). Individuals in this class showed a high probability of cocaine use before admission (80.5%). This class was labeled “CoUD+AUD”.

Class 3 included 22.6% of the sample (n = 277). Individuals in this class had a high probability of comorbid CUD (64.2%) as well as moderate probabilities of comorbid AUD (43.1%), TUD (22.5%), StUD (19.1%) and OUD (11.9%). The probability of cocaine use before admission was moderate (53.8%). Given the high prevalence of comorbid SUDs, this class was labeled “CoUD+Multiple SUDs”.

The three classes differed significantly regarding the level of school education (χ² (4) = 59.9, p < .001) and employment status (χ² (4) = 96.4, p < .001). The proportion of individuals with a tertiary school education was significantly higher among the “CoUD+AUD” class than in the other two classes. Significant differences were observed between the three classes regarding CoUD severity (χ² (2) = 27.3, p < .001), with a higher likelihood for harmful use in the “CoUD-only” class compared to the other two classes. The proportion of care-seekers with prior contact to the addiction care system differed significantly as well (χ² (2) = 15.2, p = .002), with the “CoUD+AUD” class having more readmissions than the “CoUD-only” class. The classes also significantly differed regarding lifetime crack use (p = .002, Fisher’s Exact Test), with a significantly lower prevalence within the “CoUD+AUD” class compared to the other two classes.

The classes additionally differed regarding the primary treatment form (χ² (8) = 63.4, p < .001). Here the “CoUD-only” class showed lower proportions of care-seekers in outpatient rehabilitation than the other two classes. Additionally, the group had higher rates of care-seekers in addiction counseling than the “CoUD+Multiple SUDs” class. The “CoUD+AUD” presented with a lower rate of care -seekers that received treatment in detention than the other two classes. The groups also differed significantly in their treatment durations (χ² (2) = 7.5, p = .002), with the “CoUD-only” class having shorter average durations than the other two classes. Finally, the proportions of scheduled endings (χ² (2) = 6.9, p = .031) and improved symptomatology at termination also differed significantly (χ² (2) = 12.5, p = .004). Here, the “CoUD-only” class in both instances presented a lower share than the “CoUD+AUD” class.

Discussion

Three classes with distinct sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were identified and labelled as CoUD-only, CoUD+AUD, and CoUD+Multiple SUDs. The characteristics and treatment-related profiles of each class are discussed below.

CoUD-only

Comprising half of the sample, the “CoUD-only” class represented most care-seekers with primary CoUD in outpatient addiction care. The class had less prior experience with the addiction care system and exhibited a higher prevalence of harmful cocaine use rather than dependence than the other classes. This pattern, together with the low prevalence of comorbid SUDs, is consistent with evidence linking polysubstance use to more severe and chronic CoUD trajectories (Ford et al., 2009). Counseling was most frequently reported as the primary treatment form, potentially reflecting lower perceived service needs, as this class also showed the shortest treatment duration. However, the group showed the lowest rates of scheduled terminations and symptomatic improvements, which were significantly lower than in the "CoUD+AUD" class. While counseling might be sufficient for individuals with lower clinical complexity, the overall poorer treatment outcomes indicate unmet needs for individuals with more severe CoUD. Care provision in the German system is person-centered and largely self-directed, and care-seekers may gravitate toward less intensive options even when more structured approaches are clinically indicated. CM as the most evidence-based intervention (Bentzley et al., 2021) for CoUD relies on structured contact and incentive-based reinforcement of abstinence (Higgins et al., 2000) in contrast to counseling which primarily targets individual needs and self-responsibility (DHS,2019). Earlier implementation of more targeted, cocaine-specific programs may therefore be warranted for individuals presenting with more severe CoUD.

CoUD+AUD

This class represented a quarter of the sample and showed the highest employment rates and educational attainment, as well as the lowest proportion of individuals receiving treatment in detention. These characteristics altogether indicate a high level of social functioning. Employment has been identified as positive predictor for treatment success among people who use cocaine (Higgins et al., 2000). This aligns with the high rates of scheduled treatment terminations and improved symptoms in this class. The high level of social functioning could also explain the comparatively high rate of outpatient medical rehabilitation, as access to this treatment form depends on stable living conditions (Deutsche Rentenversicherung, 2024). In addition, medical rehabilitation is generally recommended for individuals with AUD (Kiefer et al., 2022). At the same time, the presence of a dual diagnosis of AUD and CUD is associated with clinical challenges (Heil et al., 2001). The combined use of alcohol and cocaine can lead to increased consumption of both substances (Gossop et al., 2006), as they are often used to enhance or counteract each other’s effects (Pennings et al., 2002). Treatment should therefore address the risks of combined use. Alcohol consumption is associated with return to cocaine use in individuals with CoUD (McKay et al., 1999), and consuming high amounts of both substances can lead to the formation of coca ethylene, which is associated with increased cardiovascular risks (Crummy et al., 2020, van Amsterdam et al., 2024).

CoUD+multiple SUDs

The third class represented the last quarter of the sample and was defined by the high percentage of individuals with comorbid SUDs, primarily CUD. The proportion of individuals using cocaine in the month prior to admission was comparatively low. Given the high prevalence of comorbid CUD, some individuals in this group may have temporarily reduced cocaine use through cannabis consumption, possibly perceiving it as a substitution strategy (Liu et al., 2021). Self-reported studies indicate that this strategy is often perceived as beneficial by people wo use cannabis and cocaine (Daldegan-Bueno et al., 2021). Clinical findings contradict this perception as cannabis use can heighten the risk to return to cocaine use and hinder long-term abstinence (Aharonovich et al., 2005). Polysubstance use patterns are often linked to more complex clinical profiles and poorer treatment outcomes (e.g. Bonfiglio et al., 2022; Ellis et al., 2023). Contrary to this assumption, this class did not perform worse regarding treatment outcomes than the "CoUD-only" class and showed no distinct service utilization patterns, suggesting that the additional complexity of multiple SUDs was not associated with poorer outcomes in this routine care setting.

Limitations and strengths

The following limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, comorbid SUDs variables were only available in Boolean format, where cases coded as “0” may reflect either absent comorbidity or a lack of documentation, introducing potential misclassification. This limited variability and potential under documentation may have contributed to the relatively low entropy values observed in the LCA models, suggesting challenges in achieving clear class separation. Nevertheless, the resulting classes were consistent with previous findings and showed meaningful differentiation in service utilization patterns.

Second, psychiatric comorbidities are known to play an important role in differentiating clinical subgroups among individuals with CoUD (Stiltner et al., 2023, Ware et al., 2026), yet they are rarely reported in outpatient addiction care settings, suggesting substantial under documentation. Given their well-established clinical relevance, their systematic inclusion should be prioritized in future research, as they are likely to account for additional variance in service utilization among individuals with CoUD.

Third, the ICD-10 F14 diagnosis covers cocaine misuse and dependence, regardless of the route of administration. Although clinical differences between people who use powder cocaine and crack cocaine are well documented, the very low prevalence of lifetime crack use in this sample suggests most individuals presented with powder cocaine–related care demand. Crack use may be more common in populations with other primary SUDs, especially OUD (Deimel et al., 2025; Stewart et al., 2014). Additionally, route of administration may not always be differentiated in routine documentation, potentially leading to misclassification. Sensitizing clinical staff for this differentiation seems warranted, as people who use crack cocaine represent underserved population with severe addiction-related needs. (Butler et al., 2017) and elevated access barriers to care offerings (Duopah et al., 2024).

Fourth, treatment outcome measures are assessed and recorded by staff, introducing potential subjectivity and variability across providers, which should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Fifth, the dataset does not capture information on specific interventions delivered within each episode, limiting the interpretability of findings regarding treatment fit and intervention effectiveness.

The present study also has strengths as it relies on a large-scale routine data set covering multiple types of addiction care services. It reflects real-world care structures and utilization. As most outpatient addiction care services contribute to the BSHS data set, the sample provides a broad and highly representative picture of care-seeking individuals with CoUD in a large urban setting (here in Berlin). Additionally, LCA was chosen as a model-based approach that allows for probabilistic class assignment and the formal evaluation of model fit, making it particularly suitable for identifying unobserved subgroups in heterogeneous clinical populations that were subsequently linked to treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

Findings of this study underscore the importance of identifying clinically meaningful subgroups rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach in addiction care. Individuals with CoUD-only differed from those with comorbid SUDs in both service utilization and treatment success, although the existence of comorbidity was not consistently associated with poorer outcomes.

For cases with CoUD-only, current treatment approaches may lack sufficient substance specificity, pointing to the need for measures ranging from early intervention to cocaine-specific treatment strategies, with additional focus given to the form of use (crack cocaine versus powder cocaine). Individuals with comorbid AUD showed the best treatment outcomes, suggesting an overall good fit between needs and treatment strategies. However, it should also be noted that they had a favorable starting point due to high social integration. Individuals with multiple SUDs did not show worse outcomes than individuals with CoUD-only, but their more complex clinical profiles may still require more integrated and coordinated care approaches. Overall, the findings provide a basis for hypothesis-driven, subgroup-specific care planning in CoUD - moving beyond a one-size-fits-all model.
