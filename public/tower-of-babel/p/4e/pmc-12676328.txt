Surfactant Ratio-Driven Modulation of Caffeine Loading and Release in Nigella sativa Oil Nanostructured Lipid Carriers
Authors: Nindya Indah Damayanti, Nicholas M. H. Khong, Celine Valeria Liew, Romchat Chutoprapat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The delivery of hydrophilic actives such as caffeine through lipid-based topical carriers remains a challenge due to their poor affinity for lipophilic matrices and the barrier properties of the stratum corneum. This study investigated how the Tween 80/Span 20 surfactant ratio modulates interfacial organization, caffeine encapsulation, and release behavior of caffeine-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) formulated with Nigella sativa oil. Five NLC formulations were prepared with fixed lipid and surfactant concentrations, varying only in Tween 80/Span 20 ratio: NLC 1 (Tween 80 only), NLC 2 (Span 20 only), NLC 3 (1:2), NLC 4 (1.5:1.5), and NLC 5 (2:1). Physicochemical and functional properties were assessed, including particle size, zeta potential, entrapment efficiency, morphology, in vitro release, and occlusion factor. Intermediate surfactant ratios (1:2 and 1.5:1.5) achieved the highest entrapment efficiency (59.57 ± 5.43% and 63.22 ± 2.98%, respectively) with controlled release, suggesting interfacial structures that favor caffeine retention without excessively restricting diffusion. In contrast, Tween-rich systems showed lower entrapment efficiency and occlusion, consistent with looser, more hydrated interfaces. Zeta potential and transmission electron microscopy confirmed surfactant ratio–dependent interfacial structuring, while occlusion data suggested that intermediate surfactant ratio systems tended to form denser films, enhancing water retention. These findings demonstrated that surfactant ratio is not merely a stabilizing factor but a tunable design element governing the nanoscale organization of lipid–aqueous interfaces. By adjusting Tween 80/Span 20 ratios, formulators can balance retention, release, and occlusion, providing a rational framework for designing next-generation NLCs with improved performance and skin compatibility.

Introduction

Topical delivery of hydrophilic compounds such as caffeine presents a persistent challenge in pharmaceutical and cosmetic formulations. Despite caffeine’s well-documented dermatological benefits, including modulation of microcirculation, antiedematous effects, and barrier enhancement, − its physicochemical properties (low log P, high aqueous solubility) hinder its retention and controlled release from lipid-based carriers. , These limitations are particularly critical when attempting to target the stratum corneum, a lipid-rich barrier that favors lipophilic penetration while impeding aqueous diffusion.

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), as second-generation lipid nanoparticles, offer an adaptable platform for topical drug delivery. Their biphasic lipid matrices improved drug-loading potential, while their nanoscale size and lipophilic character facilitate close contact with the stratum corneum, enabling controlled release of actives. , NLCs can encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic compounds, including caffeine, while maintaining biodegradability and biocompatibility. , The versatility of NLCs has been demonstrated across diverse actives and applications. For example, voriconazole, a hydrophobic antifungal drug, has been successfully incorporated into NLC hydrogels, improving topical antifungal delivery by providing sustained release and excellent skin compatibility. , Similarly, NLC-based transdermal patches of dolutegravir sodium, another hydrophobic drug, achieved nearly a twofold increase in bioavailability compared with the oral route, confirming the ability of NLC systems to overcome permeability and pharmacokinetic limitations of challenging drugs. , Quercetin, a highly hydrophobic flavonoid with strong antioxidant and photoprotective potential, has also been effectively delivered through NLC-embedded hydrogels using pomegranate oil as a liquid lipid, resulting in enhanced entrapment efficiency, stability, and UVB protection. , In addition to hydrophobic molecules, several studies have explored the encapsulation of hydrophilic bioactives. For instance, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), the major catechin in green tea, was successfully incorporated into NLCs, which enhanced its absorption and oral bioavailability, supporting potential applications in the food and nutraceutical industries. Likewise, saffron extract, well-known for its antioxidant activity but limited by inherent chemical instability, was encapsulated in thyme–pennyroyal essential oil–based NLCs. Optimization of lipid and surfactant composition yielded particles with small size, low polydispersity, and high encapsulation efficiency, while also providing oxidative and microbial stability. Beyond versatility, NLCs offer practical advantages such as ease of production, scalability, and cost-effectiveness, making them attractive for commercial cosmetic applications. , Their performance is further enhanced by incorporating liquid lipids, which disrupt crystallinity within the matrix and improve loading capacity. Nigella sativa oil represents a particularly promising yet underexplored option for NLC formulations. N. sativa oil is distinguished by its rich composition of bioactive fatty acids, particularly its high concentration of linoleic acid. This abundance of linoleic acid is especially significant for topical applications, as it demonstrates remarkable capabilities in enhancing skin hydration, strengthening the skin barrier function, and alleviating conditions such as atopic dermatitis. ,

In conventional NLC design, surfactant pairs are typically chosen from the same homologous series, such as Tween 80/Span 80 or Tween 60/Span 60, with their ratios adjusted to achieve the desired Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance (HLB). This approach, however, prioritizes emulsification efficiency while overlooking the nanoscale interfacial organization that governs entrapment and release of the active. Here, we propose a paradigm shift by employing a divergent pair of nonionic surfactantsTween 80 (HLB ≈ 15) and Span 20 (HLB ≈ 8.6)instead of homologous pairs. We define intermediate surfactant ratios as mixing ratios of Tween 80 and Span 20 (e.g., 1.5:1.5 or 1:2) that generate midrange mixed HLB values (∼11–12), compared with the extremes of single-surfactant systems. Such ratios are hypothesized to promote a more cohesive interfacial film, thereby enhancing entrapment efficiency, whereas Tween-rich systems with higher HLB values may reduce interfacial affinity and accelerate caffeine diffusion. Although classical Tween/Span combinations are known to improve particle size reduction, the mechanistic impact of divergent pairings such as Tween 80/Span 20 on the localization and release of borderline hydrophilic actives like caffeine within NLCs remains poorly understood.

To address this knowledge gap, physicochemical characterization plays a central role in linking surfactant composition to formulation performance. In lipid-based delivery system development, various analytical techniquessuch as Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and X-ray diffraction (XRD)are commonly employed to elucidate molecular interactions, lipid crystallinity, and thermal behavior, thereby providing insights into formulation stability and drug–excipient interactions. − In the present work, however, emphasis was placed on particle size, zeta potential, and entrapment efficiency, which are most directly relevant to the study objectives. Particle size and its distribution are critical determinants of drug release behavior and therapeutic performance in lipid-based carriers, with smaller particles and higher surface area-to-volume ratios typically accelerating release. Zeta potential is closely related to colloidal stability, where higher absolute values reduce aggregation via electrostatic repulsion, thereby supporting particle integrity during release and film formation. Entrapment efficiency reflects the extent of drug retained within the lipid matrix, a key factor in ensuring therapeutic effectiveness.

Given these considerations, this study was designed to elucidate how varying Tween 80/Span 20 ratios govern the interfacial environment of N. sativa-based NLCs and, in turn, regulate particle size, zeta potential, entrapment efficiency, and the release behavior of caffeine. In particular, we investigate whether intermediate surfactant ratios promote pseudobilayer interfacial structures that transiently immobilize the active, thereby enhancing entrapment efficiency and enabling controlled release.

Materials and Methods

N. sativa oil (containing ∼56% linoleic acid) was sourced from Botanicessence, Thailand. Tween 80 was purchased from S. Tong Chemicals Co., Ltd., Thailand. Span 20 was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry, Japan. Cetyl alcohol was acquired from Ajax Finechem, Australia. Caffeine anhydrous (99.9%) was procured from Loba Chemie, India. Ethanol (99.7%) was purchased from RCI Labscan, Thailand. Potassium dihydrogen phosphate and sodium dihydrogen phosphate monohydrate were obtained from Carlo Erba, Italy. Sodium hydroxide and potassium chloride were sourced from Merck, Germany. A cellulose membrane (MW = 14,000) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich, USA.

Preparation of Caffeine-Loaded Nanostructured Lipid Carriers

The nanostructured lipid carriers were prepared using a modified method from Shiehzadeh et al., combining high-shear homogenization and ultrasonication techniques. The formulation comprised a 5% lipid phase consisting of cetyl alcohol and N. sativa oil. The lipid phase, together with Span 20 as a lipophilic emulsifier, was heated to 65 °C. Simultaneously, two aqueous phases were prepared separately: one containing Tween 80 and deionized water, and another containing the caffeine solution, both heated to 80 °C. The preparation involved a stepwise emulsification process. First, the caffeine solution was gradually incorporated into the lipid phase under constant stirring at 900 rpm for 2 min to form a water-in-oil (w/o) emulsion. Subsequently, the hydrophilic emulsifier solution was added to the primary emulsion under identical conditions for an additional 2 min. The resulting water-in-oil-in-water (w/o/w) double emulsion then underwent high-shear homogenization (IKA Ultra Turrax Digital 25) in two stages: initially at 15,000 rpm for 5 min, followed by 18,000 rpm for 2 min. Final size reduction was achieved using probe sonication (Vibra Cell) at 20% amplitude for 2 min. The detailed composition of the formulation is presented in Table . Samples were allowed to undergo equilibration for 1 day prior to observation.

Characterization of Caffeine-Loaded Nanostructured Lipid Carriers

Physical Appearance and pH Analysis

The physical appearance of the prepared NLCs were evaluated through visual observation. The pH measurements were conducted using a calibrated digital pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Germany) at 25 °C.

Particle Size, Polydispersity Index, and Zeta Potential Analysis

The particle size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential were determined using a Zetasizer ZS (Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. Prior to measurement, the NLC dispersion was diluted with ultrapure water (1:100 v/v) to achieve optimal scattering intensity.

Entrapment Efficiency Study

The entrapment efficiency study was performed based on Makky et al. with some modification. Approximately 3 mL of the NLC formulation was loaded into a tube and subjected to ultracentrifugation (Hitachi CP 100 NX) at 8.5 °C for 45 min at 60,000 rpm. After centrifugation, the supernatant, which appeared clear, was collected from the upper portion, while the sediment phase, appearing white to yellowish in color, settled at the bottom. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and diluted with ethanol. The diluted solution was analyzed using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 60, USA) at 273 nm, applying the appropriate dilution factor to determine the caffeine content in the supernatant. In Vitro Release Study

The in vitro drug release study of caffeine-loaded NLCs was conducted using Franz diffusion cells (Model VTC-200, LOGAN Instruments Corp., USA), following methods adapted from previous studies. − A cellulose membrane with a molecular weight of 14,000 Da (D9777 – 100 FT, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was positioned on the donor compartment. The mounted membrane had an active surface area of 2.37 cm2, while the receptor compartment was filled with 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 maintained at 32 °C using a circulating water bath. The PBS medium in the receptor compartment was continuously stirred at 100 rpm. Samples (approximately 1 mL) were collected from the receptor compartment at 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 8, and 24 h, with an equal volume of fresh buffer added to maintain a constant volume. Caffeine release was quantified using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 60, USA), and the data were tabulated into a Table as percentage release versus time.

Morphological Analysis

The morphological characterization of NLCs was performed using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL, JEM 1400, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 160 kV. Sample preparation involved dispersing 2 g of the NLC formulation in 20 mL of water. A single drop of the dispersed sample was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to dry at room temperature for 5 min. The sample was then negatively stained with 1% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid solution for 2 min. Excess stains were removed using filter paper, and the grid was examined under the microscope at appropriate magnifications.

In Vitro Occlusion Study

The occlusion study was adapted from established research protocols. − The procedure involved filling a glass beaker with 50 mL of distilled water. A piece of Whatman filter paper (No. 42, USA) was secured over the beaker’s opening and carefully sealed with Teflon tape to prevent water evaporation. The test sample (200 mg) was uniformly distributed across the filter paper’s surface, covering a precisely measured area of 18.8 cm2. The prepared beakers were placed in an incubator (MaxQ 6000, Thermo Fisher, USA) and maintained at 32 °C ± 1 °C to simulate human skin surface temperature. After a 48 h incubation period, the beakers were weighed to quantify water loss. For experimental validation, petroleum jelly served as a positive control, while a filter paper without any test sample acted as a negative control. The occlusion factor (F) was calculated using the following equation: 2 F=[A−B)/A]×100 where A represents the water loss from the negative control (filter paper without sample), B represents the water loss from the test sample, and F represents the occlusion factor, which indicates the sample’s ability to prevent water loss. Higher values of F indicate greater occlusion effects.

Quantification of Caffeine

Quantification of caffeine was performed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 60, USA) where absorbance measurements were taken at 273 nm. The calibration curve for caffeine was established for entrapment efficiency and in vitro drug release studies by plotting the absorbance values against the concentrations of the caffeine standard solutions. A standard caffeine solution (100 μg/mL) was prepared, and serial dilutions were made within the following concentration ranges: 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 μg/mL in ethanol for the entrapment efficiency study, and 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 20 μg/mL in PBS (pH 7.4) for the in vitro drug release study.

Statistical Analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were analyzed using ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 28.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Caffeine-Loaded Nanostructured Lipid Carriers

Physical Appearance and pH

All formulations appeared as milky to yellowish dispersions upon preparation (Figure ). Notably, NLC 1 (Tween 80 only) exhibited physical instability, showing heterogeneity in dispersion and a turbidity gradient between the upper and lower portions, along with white particulates indicative of lipid aggregation after 8 days of storage at room temperature. In contrast, NLC 2 (Span 20 only), and NLC 3, NLC 4 and NLC 5, prepared with Tween 80/Span 20 mixtures, maintained physical stability and homogeneity throughout the observation period, with no signs of phase separation or instability.

Physical appearance of different NLC formulations (NLC 1–NLC 5): (A) After 1 day of equilibration following preparation (Day 1 observation), and (B) After 8 days of storage at room temperature (Day 8 observation).

This difference in macroscopic behavior, though often overlooked, provides early evidence for how surfactant pairing influences the interfacial integrity and colloidal stabilization of NLCs. As previously reported by Witayaudom & Klinkesorn (2017), formulations with appropriately balanced surfactant systems are better able to instantaneously adsorb at the oil–water interface during emulsification, forming a more stable interfacial film that suppresses coalescence and gravitational separation. In our study, the combination of Tween 80 and Span 20 appeared to fulfill this role more effectively than Tween 80 only, likely due to enhanced interfacial packing and HLB complementarity.

The pH values across all formulations ranged from 6.11 ± 0.12 to 6.43 ± 0.04, with no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). These values fall within the physiologically acceptable range for dermal application and are compatible with the mildly acidic environment of the stratum corneum (typically pH 5.0–7.0). Importantly, the consistency of pH across formulations suggests that variations in surfactant ratio did not result in measurable shifts in bulk acidity or basicity, supporting the view that observed performance differences are attributable to structural interfacial effects rather than bulk chemical changes. Accordingly, NLC 2, NLC 3, NLC 4, and NLC 5 were selected for further investigation based on these observations.

Particle Size, Polydispersity Index, and Zeta Potential Analysis

The average particle size of caffeine-loaded NLCs ranged from 102.68 ± 8.39 nm (NLC 4) to 136.77 ± 28.49 nm (NLC 5), with no statistically significant differences among the groups (p > 0.05) (Table ). All formulations achieved sub-200 nm dimensions, suitable for topical delivery. A modest increase in size was observed at higher Tween 80 content (notably NLC 5, 2:1 Tween 80/Span 20), which may reflect looser interfacial packing due to Tween 80's bulky polyoxyethylene chains occupying greater surface area. Overall, varying the Tween 80/Span 20 ratio exerted only a minor effect on particle dimensions; nevertheless, the consistent production of sub-200 nm particles suggests that mixed-surfactant systems promote stable nanoscale dimensions.

This stabilizing effect can be explained by two complementary mechanisms: first, mixed surfactants form a denser interfacial layer around droplets, preventing agglomeration; and second, they effectively reduce interfacial tension. Comparable results have been reported in other NLC systems, such as cinacalcet HCl formulations where particle size remained below 200 nm despite formulation variability, and in the work of Khan et al. (2016), where combining Solutol HS15 (HLB 15) with lecithin (HLB 4) yielded particles <100 nm using solvent emulsification–sonication and high-pressure homogenization.

Polydispersity index values ranged from 0.41 ± 0.04 (NLC 3) to 0.53 ± 0.12 (NLC 5), indicating moderately uniform but not monodisperse systems (Table and Figure ). Although the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), the trend toward higher polydispersity index in NLC 5 may again reflect a less ordered interfacial film, where the dominance of a single high-HLB surfactant (Tween 80) may reduce interfacial cohesion. In contrast, the narrower distribution in NLC 3 (1:2 Tween 80/Span 20) suggested that intermediate surfactant ratios promote more efficient droplet stabilization, likely due to synergistic interfacial interactions between the two surfactants. Although polydispersity index values below 0.3 are typically preferred for high uniformity, values under 0.5 are still considered acceptable in lipid-based nanocarriers, especially those fabricated via ultrasonication. The absence of significant variation across groups (p > 0.05) implies that the emulsification process was consistent; however, the slight broadening of particle size distributions may reflect microscopic interfacial heterogeneity, particularly at surfactant ratios where molecular packing at the interface is less optimal. This observation is in agreement with Belgheisi et al. (2020), who noted that ultrasonic energy input can generate uneven cavitation zonesconcentrated near the probe tipresulting in transient local fluctuations in particle breakdown and surface coverage. In the context of our study, such heterogeneity may have been compounded by differences in surfactant self-assembly behavior, depending on the balance between the hydrophilic head groups of Tween 80 and the more lipophilic Span 20.

The zeta potential values provided further mechanistic insight into surfactant interfacial behavior. NLC 2, containing Span 20 only, exhibited the most negative zeta potential (−38.50 ± 7.88 mV), whereas NLC 5, with the highest proportion of Tween 80, had the least negative value (−28.97 ± 1.31 mV). NLC 3 (1:2 Tween 80/Span 20) and NLC 4 (1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20) fell between these extremes, indicating a progressive reduction in surface charge with increasing Tween 80 content. This trend suggests not merely charge dilution, but reorganization of the interfacial surfactant layer. Tween 80, with its bulky polyoxyethylene chains, forms a hydrated steric barrier that masks the negative charges of fatty acids (e.g., linoleic acid from N. sativa oil), thereby lowering the measured zeta potential. , In contrast, Span 20 (HLB 8.6), being more lipophilic and less hydrated, tends to embed within the lipid core and expose carboxylate groups, leading to stronger net negative charge. Importantly, all NLCs maintained zeta potential values below −25 mV, a threshold typically considered sufficient to prevent aggregation and ensure colloidal stability.

Taken together, these results suggest that surfactant ratio does not significantly alter particle size or dispersity, but strongly influences interfacial packing and surface charge. Intermediate Tween/Span ratios (1:2 and 1.5:1.5) appear to promote more cohesive and stable interfaces. Accordingly, zeta potential may serve as an indirect yet sensitive indicator of interfacial organization, highlighting how subtle adjustments in surfactant ratio translate into meaningful changes at the oil–water boundary.

Entrapment Efficiency

Previous studies have shown that increasing surfactant concentration enhances entrapment efficiency by forming thicker stabilizing layers around nanoparticles. In this work, the total surfactant concentration was fixed at 7.5% to specifically examine the effect of Tween 80/Span 20 ratio on entrapment efficiency. As shown in Figure , intermediate surfactant ratios, particularly NLC 3 (1:2 Tween 80/Span 20) and NLC 4 (1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20), exhibited significantly higher entrapment efficiency values (59.57 ± 5.43% and 63.22 ± 2.98%, respectively) whereas single-surfactant systems (NLC 2, Span 20 only: 55.43 ± 3.64%; NLC 5, Tween-rich 2:1: 50.87 ± 4.59%) showed lower values (p < 0.05).

At intermediate ratios (1:2 and 1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20), which correspond to midrange mixed HLB values (∼10.7–11.8), the surfactant blend likely reduced polarity mismatch at the oil–water interface, forming a denser and more elastic film that stabilized droplets during emulsification and sonication. This cohesive interfacial structure may have minimized caffeine migration into the aqueous phase, thereby improving its partitioning within the lipid matrix. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that systems rich in high-HLB surfactants promote the loss of polar drugs to the external phase, leading to lower entrapment efficiency. , The relatively low entrapment efficiency of NLC 5, despite its higher HLB, suggests that excess Tween 80 produces a hydrated and permeable interface that facilitates outward diffusion of caffeine. Conversely, the Span 20-only system (NLC 2) likely suffered from weak emulsification dynamics and poor interfacial stabilization, resulting in insufficient entrapment.

Taken together, the results emphasize that neither extremepurely hydrophilic nor purely lipophilic surfactant systemsfavors optimal encapsulation. Instead, intermediate surfactant ratios provide the most balanced interfacial environment, maximizing retention of the active. This trend serves as both a quantitative indicator of formulation performance and a functional proxy for nanoscale interfacial behavior. It further validates the premise that surfactant ratio is not a neutral formulation choice but an interfacial engineering tool that directly shapes drug distribution within nanocarriers. Accordingly, attention to surfactant ratio is essential when designing NLCs for hydrophilic actives, not only to ensure colloidal stability but also to fine-tune the physicochemical microenvironment that determines whether the active is retained or lost during emulsification.

Overall, the entrapment efficiency results highlight how surfactant ratio governs the interfacial architecture of NLCs, determining the extent to which hydrophilic caffeine is retained within the lipid matrix. However, particle size and zeta potential also play complementary roles, influencing the diffusional path length and surface energetics at the oil–water interface. To translate these physicochemical attributes into functional performance, it is essential to examine the release behavior of the active. Accordingly, the in vitro release study was conducted to evaluate how surfactant-driven differences in entrapment, particle size, and surface charge manifest in the cumulative release of caffeine over time.

In Vitro Release Study

The release profiles of caffeine showed a clear dependency on surfactant ratio, with the Tween 80/Span 20 proportion determining both the extent and rate of release over 24 h (Table ). NLC 5 (2:1 Tween 80/Span 20) exhibited the highest release (104.1 ± 1.27%), followed by NLC 4 (1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20) (94.3 ± 0.93%), whereas NLC 3 (1:2 Tween 80/Span 20) and NLC 2 (Span 20 only) showed significantly lower values (82.76 ± 5.77% and 79.68 ± 3.28%, respectively) (p < 0.05).

These results point to a surfactant ratio–driven trade-off between retention and release. Notably, the relationship with entrapment efficiency was not strictly inverse: NLC 4 combined relatively high entrapment efficiency with the high release, indicating that interfacial hydration and reduced cohesiveness introduced by Tween 80 can override the retention effect typically associated with higher entrapment efficiency, consistent with the observations of Lv et al. (2018).

Mechanistically, higher Tween 80 content appeared to produce a more hydrated and less cohesive interfacial shell, facilitating outward diffusion of caffeine. Tween 80, with its polyoxyethylene chains and high HLB (15), likely formed a looser interfacial barrier that accelerated partitioning of the active into the aqueous medium. In contrast, increased Span 20 produced a denser interfacial matrix that restricted diffusion despite higher retention within the lipid core.

Kinetic modeling using the Korsmeyer–Peppas equation (Table ) further supported this interpretation. All formulations followed diffusion-dominated release (R 2 > 0.90), with release exponents (n values) ranging from 0.402 to 0.491, consistent with Fickian diffusion from spherical particles. Notably, NLC 4, which showed the second-highest release, also displayed the highest n value (0.491), suggesting relatively faster early stage diffusion, although still within the diffusion-controlled regime.

Integration with other physicochemical data provided further insight into release behavior. Although NLC 4 and NLC 5 showed lower zeta potential values, both remained within the ± 25 mV stability threshold, ensuring colloidal stability while still permitting enhanced release. This agrees with previous findings that colloidal stability and interfacial hydration can decouple from slower release kinetics. , By contrast, NLC 2 illustrates the opposite scenario: its Span-rich interface produced both low entrapment and low release, reflecting poor emulsification dynamics and a denser, less permeable barrier. Taken together, these comparisons showed that release behavior cannot be predicted from a single parameter such as zeta potential or entrapment efficiency alone, but rather from the combined effects of surfactant ratio on interfacial packing, charge shielding, and permeability.

Overall, these results substantiate the central hypothesis that the nature and density of the surfactant interface, shaped by the Tween/Span ratio, govern the permeability landscape of the lipid carrier and ultimately control release behavior. Rather than relying solely on lipid content or loading of the active, surfactant ratio emerges as a critical design parameter for regulating the release of hydrophilic actives such as caffeine. The gradual release observed here can prolong caffeine availability at the stratum corneum interface, which may translate into improved skin hydration and barrier functionproperties highly desirable for topical applications.

Morphological Analysis

Transmission electron microscopy images of the NLC formulations (Figure ) reveal spherical to slightly ellipsoidal particles with well-defined boundaries, confirming successful nanoparticle formation. The formulations displayed uniform external morphology, with no observable aggregation or fusion, supporting the particle size and stability data presented earlier. The transmission electron microscopy observations were consistent with particle size analysis, showing that NLC 4 had the smallest particles, while NLC 5 exhibited the largest.

In Vitro Occlusion Study

The occlusion factor, an important indicator of a formulation’s ability to reduce transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and maintain hydration at the skin surface, varied significantly across the different NLC systems, showing a direct relationship with surfactant composition. As shown in Figure , NLC 4 (1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20) exhibited the highest occlusion factor (41.01 ± 2.87%), followed by NLC 3 (1:2 Tween 80/Span 20) (40.50 ± 14.97%) and NLC 2 (Span 20 only) (35.02 ± 3.29%). In contrast, NLC 5 (2:1 Tween 80/Span 20), which contained the highest proportion of Tween 80, showed a lower occlusion factor (33.47 ± 7.48%). Despite these apparent trends, statistical analysis revealed no significant differences among the formulations (p > 0.05).

Occlusion factors of caffeine-loaded NLCs after 24 h intervention. Different letters indicate the significant difference compared with others group at p < 0.05.

These data suggest that intermediate surfactant ratios (NLC 3, 1:2 Tween 80/Span 20; and NLC 4, 1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20) favor higher occlusivity, most likely through the formation of a denser and more compact lipid film at the skin surface. Span 20, with its lower HLB value and greater lipophilicity, can integrate into the lipid phase and enhance surfactant packing at the oil–water interface. However, when used alone (NLC 2), Span 20 did not provide the highest occlusion, suggesting that an optimal balance with Tween 80 is necessary to achieve cohesive interfacial organization. By contrast, a higher proportion of Tween 80 (NLC 5, 2:1 Tween 80/Span 20) appeared to disrupt this organization, as the hydration and steric repulsion of its polyoxyethylene headgroups likely produce a more permeable and less occlusive interfacial layer. This interpretation aligns with the findings of Chutoprapat et al. (2024), which demonstrated that surfactant–coemulsifier pairing with cetyl alcohol significantly influences the formation of a cohesive interfacial film in lipid-based carriers, thereby enhancing occlusivity.

The observed occlusion pattern showed an inverse tendency with the caffeine release profiles. NLC 5, the Tween-rich system, exhibited the highest release but the lowest occlusion, whereas NLC 4 demonstrated the highest occlusion and still released less caffeine than NLC 5. NLC 3, despite having a comparably high occlusion, showed a lower release value than both NLC 4 and NLC 5. This suggests a shared mechanistic basis in the interfacial organization, where tighter surfactant packing not only reduces moisture loss but also slows caffeine diffusion, a dual benefit in dermal delivery aimed at hydration-sensitive conditions. In addition, the occlusion effects of the NLCs may also be attributed to the small particle size (less than 200 nm) of the NLCs. Previous research has established that nanoparticles with sizes between 200 and 400 nm demonstrate superior occlusivity compared to larger particles, with those exceeding 600 nm showing a significant reduction in occlusive properties. Moreover, it has been shown that occlusion by lipid nanoparticles is not solely dependent on particle size, but on surface characteristics and lipid crystallinity, both of which can be modulated by surfactant arrangement.

Taken together, these findings emphasize that the Tween 80/Span 20 ratio modulates interfacial structure, which in turn governs both moisture retention and release of the active. By adjusting this ratio, formulators can fine-tune NLCs for specific therapeutic objectives, ranging from sustained release with occlusive protection to more dynamic, diffusion-driven delivery in less occlusive systems. While the present data provide indirect evidence of interfacial reorganization, future studies incorporating ex vivo skin permeation together with complementary techniquessuch as FTIR and DSC for probing lipid–surfactant interactions and crystallinity, and interfacial tension analysis for direct interfacial evaluationwill be valuable to confirm the proposed mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study provided insights into how the Tween 80/Span 20 surfactant ratio modulates the physicochemical and functional performance of caffeine-loaded NLCs. While particle size and polydispersity index values remained relatively consistent across the formulations, surfactant ratio had a pronounced influence on entrapment, release kinetics, surface charge, and occlusion trends. Entrapment efficiency was significantly higher at intermediate surfactant ratios (1:2 and 1.5:1.5 Tween 80/Span 20), suggesting that these combinations promote a more cohesive interfacial arrangement capable of retaining hydrophilic actives such as caffeine. In contrast, Tween-dominant systems exhibited lower entrapment, consistent with a more hydrated and permeable interface. Caffeine release mirrored these effects, with faster diffusion from Tween-rich carriers and slower release from Span-rich systems. Although occlusion values did not differ significantly, intermediate-ratio and Span-rich formulations showed a tendency toward higher occlusivity, consistent with denser interfacial packing and relatively lower caffeine release. Collectively, these findings established a framework for considering surfactant ratio not as a formulation constraint but as a design tool for fine-tuning the retention and release of the active, and the barrier-related properties in dermal nanocarrier systems.
