Impact of type 2 and steroid-Induced diabetes mellitus on prognosis in patients with multiple myeloma
Authors: Ji-Heng Wang, Pu Xiang, Er-Jiang Zhao, Li-Na Liu, Yu-Zhang Liu, Li-Jie Liang, Yu-Shan Cui, Bai-Jun Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and steroid-induced diabetes mellitus (SIDM) with prognosis in patients with multiple myeloma (MM).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients initially diagnosed with MM at Henan Provincial Cancer Hospital between January 2005 and December 2016. Patients were categorized into three groups: MM without diabetes mellitus, MM with T2DM, and MM with SIDM.ResultsA cohort of 1,463 patients were included in this study, comprising 818 patients with MM without diabetes mellitus, 217 with MM and T2DM, and 428 with MM and SIDM. Independent predictors of overall survival (OS) in the entire cohort included age (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.011, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.007–1.016, p < 0.001), International Staging System (ISS) stage III (HR = 1.463, 95% CI: 1.273–1.681, p < 0.001), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) (HR = 0.334, 95% CI: 0.286–0.390, p < 0.001), chromosomal abnormalities (HR = 11.999, 95% CI: 9.688–14.860, p < 0.001), T2DM (HR = 1.606, 95% CI: 1.409–1.829, p < 0.001), and SIDM (HR = 1.224, 95% CI: 1.038–1.444, p = 0.016). Among patients with MM and SIDM, age (HR = 1.047, 95% CI: 1.037–1.057, p < 0.001), ISS stage III (HR = 1.796, 95% CI: 1.389–2.322, p < 0.001), lactate dehydrogenase (HR = 1.004, 95% CI: 1.002–1.006, p < 0.001), HSCT (HR = 0.284, 95% CI: 0.213–0.382, p < 0.001), and chromosomal abnormalities (HR = 14.59, 95% CI: 9.822–21.674, p < 0.001) were independently associated with OS. In patients with MM and T2DM, ISS stage II (HR = 2.008, 95% CI: 1.418–2.844, p < 0.001), ISS stage III (HR = 3.126, 95% CI: 2.110–4.631, p < 0.001), HSCT (HR = 0.264, 95% CI: 0.173–0.403, p < 0.001), and chromosomal abnormalities (HR = 32.677, 95% CI: 17.632–60.560, p < 0.001) were independently associated with OS.ConclusionBoth T2DM and SIDM were independently associated with poorer prognosis in patients with MM.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a malignant clonal disorder of bone marrow plasma cells, leading to bone destruction and marrow failure due to excessive monoclonal protein production [1–3]. Risk factors include genetic predisposition and premalignant conditions [4–6]. In China, the 5-year relative survival rate for patients with MM was 24.8%, lower than in Japan (33.3%) and the United States (46.7%), according to CONCORD-3 2018 statistics [7]. Prognostic factors encompass tumor burden, cytogenetic abnormalities, molecular characteristics, clinical factors such as renal dysfunction, age, and comorbidities, as well as treatment response [2, 8–11]. The prevalence of comorbidities rises with age [12, 13]. A study indicated that a frailty score based on age, Activities of Daily Living (ADL), Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) predicts overall survival (OS) in newly diagnosed patients with MM ineligible for autologous stem cell transplantation (OS was defined as the duration from the date of initial diagnosis to the date of death from any cause.) [10].

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent endocrine disorder in adults, characterized by varying degrees of insulin resistance and insufficient secretion, leading to hyperglycemia [14, 15]. Additionally, glucocorticoids are widely used across various medical fields, including chemotherapy, to manage specific conditions [16]. Steroid-induced diabetes mellitus (SIDM) refers to an abnormal rise in blood glucose levels associated with glucocorticoid use, occurring in patients with or without a prior history of diabetes [17]. Nearly half of the patients receiving glucocorticoids develop SIDM [18, 19]. Diabetes mellitus can lead to complications such as cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, and increased mortality, all of which may impact the outcomes of patients with cancer [14, 15].

Determining the precise impact of diabetes on MM prognosis is challenging, as many MM clinical trials do not report diabetes-related data [20]. However, epidemiological evidence indicates a high prevalence of diabetes in patients with MM, with T2DM and SIDM rates of 11–18% and 31.7%, respectively, though these findings are based on small sample sizes [21–23]. Diabetes is generally associated with a poorer cancer prognosis, increasing the risk of death by 42% and recurrence by 21% [24, 25]. In hematological malignancies, diabetes raises the risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.36) [26]. Among patients with MM, elevated post-load glucose levels correlate with higher MM mortality risk (HR = 3.06), whereas self-reported diabetes has shown no significant association [27, 28]. Patients with MM and diabetes are less likely to qualify for autologous HSCT [29]. Some studies indicates that diabetes does not elevate mortality risk in patients with MM, yet it has been linked to complications in this population [30–32]. Additionally, antidiabetic medication usage has been associated with a reduced risk of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) progression to MM [33]. Thus, the current data on diabetes and MM prognosis remain limited and inconsistent [20].

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between T2DM and SIDM and the prognosis of patients with MM.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study enrolled patients who were initially diagnosed with MM at Henan Provincial Cancer Hospital between January 2005 and December 2016. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial Cancer Hospital, which waived the requirement for individual consent due to its retrospective design.

The inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of multiple myeloma (MM) based on NCCN guidelines and an age of ≥ 18 years [1, 34]. Patients with incomplete clinical data were excluded.

Data collection

Patient charts were retrieved from the medical records system, and data were extracted, including age, sex, MM typing, International Staging System (ISS) stage, kidney injury, extramedullary infiltration, lactate dehydrogenase, β2-microglobulin, plasma cell proportion, hemoglobin levels, use of bortezomib-containing chemotherapy regimens, secondary fungal infections, HSCT, blood glucose levels, and chromosomal abnormalities. Cytogenetic analysis was performed using G-banding and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) on bulk bone marrow samples. Due to evolving clinical practices during the study period (2005–2016), CD138 + cell sorting was not routinely performed. Chromosomal abnormalities were classified as ‘present’ if any of the following were detected: aneuploidy, translocations, deletions, or amplifications. The absence of standardized high-risk abnormality screening (e.g., del(17p), t(4;14)) across the cohort precluded stratified analysis; thus, abnormalities were analyzed as a composite variable, consistent with prognostic models in retrospective studies when granular data are limited [35].

Curative effect indices were collected, including stringent complete response (sCR), complete response (CR), very good partial response (VGPR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). Overall survival (OS) was also assessed. Progression-free survival (PFS) data were incomplete due to retrospective limitations and were not analyzed [35]. OS was defined as the duration from therapy initiation to death from any cause [36].

The patients were categorized into three groups: MM without diabetes mellitus, MM with T2DM, and MM with SIDM. T2DM was diagnosed based on the WHO 2019 classification of diabetes mellitus [37]. Since compliance with glucose control methods could not be assessed due to data limitations, and elevated blood glucose levels could not be solely attributed to glucocorticoid use or poor compliance, SIDM was defined as the absence of a prior diabetes mellitus diagnosis before MM and at least two instances of random blood glucose > 200 mg/dL or the initiation of antidiabetic therapy after starting glucocorticoids for MM [21].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) and analyzed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by the Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as medians (ranges) and assessed using the Wilcoxon test. Categorical data were expressed as n (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test. Kaplan-Meier curves were utilized to compare OS among patients with different types of diabetes mellitus. Univariable analysis was performed using Cox regression analysis, and significant variables from univariable analyses were included in the multivariable Cox regression analysis model. The forward conditional method was applied in the multivariable analysis to identify factors associated with OS. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

This study included 1,463 patients, comprising 818 with MM without diabetes mellitus, 217 with MM and T2DM, and 428 with MM and SIDM. Patients in the MM with T2DM group were older than those in the other two groups (p = 0.001). Compared with the other groups, the MM with SIDM group had higher β2-microglobulin levels (p = 0.002), while the MM with T2DM group had the highest plasma cell percentage (p < 0.001). The MM with SIDM group exhibited lower hemoglobin content (p = 0.001), and blood glucose levels were lowest in the non-diabetic group (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

For ISS stages, the median survival was 68.6 months (95% CI: 65.4–71.8) for stage I, 63.8 months (95% CI: 60.9–66.7) for stage II, and 42.3 months (95% CI: 39.8–44.8) for stage III. Patients who underwent HSCT had a significantly longer survival than those who did not (96.0 months [95% CI: 92.6–99.4] vs. 51.8 months [95% CI: 49.6–53.9], log-rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). In younger patients (< 65 years), only T2DM (HR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.10–1.97, p = 0.009) was significant, while SIDM showed a trend (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.98–1.52, p = 0.077).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the impact of T2DM and SIDM on the prognosis of patients with MM. The findings suggest that both T2DM and SIDM are independently associated with poorer prognostic outcomes in patients with MM. These results may offer valuable insights for optimizing the management of patients with MM and diabetes.

In this study, 14.8% of patients had T2DM, and 29.3% had SIDM, which is consistent with previously reported rates of 11–18% and 31.7% [21–23]. According to the official definition, SIDM is characterized by elevated blood glucose associated with glucocorticoid use, regardless of prior diabetes history [17]. However, in this study, due to the inability to assess compliance with glucose control methods and the lack of definitive causal data in patient charts, SIDM was defined as the presence of elevated glucose during glucocorticoid treatment in patients without a prior history of diabetes mellitus. This definition may have influenced the observed prevalence of diabetes in this study. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to further investigate this aspect.

Diabetes adversely affects cancer prognosis in general [24, 25, 38]; however, data on MM remain limited, and the evidence is less conclusive than for other cancers, as reviewed by Tentolouris et al. in 2022 [20]. A major contributing factor to this gap is the insufficient reporting of diabetes in clinical trials on MM [20]. Therefore, future clinical trial designs for MM should incorporate diabetes as a key variable.

The key finding of this study is that both T2DM and SIDM are independently linked to poorer OS in patients with MM. This is significant, as most patients with MM also have T2DM or SIDM, particularly given that glucocorticoids are commonly used to manage chemotherapy-related side effects, inflammation, and edema in patients with cancer [16]. However, Sanchez et al. reported that DM did not impact MM prognosis, but their study included only patients who underwent HSCT, whereas the present study did not apply HSCT-based selection criteria [30]. In fact, this study employed minimal eligibility restrictions to enhance generalizability. Since diabetes can affect eligibility for HSCT, the findings of Sanchez et al. may be biased and not fully representative of all patients with MM [29, 30]. In this study, HSCT rates did not differ significantly among the three groups. A study of 196 patients suggested that patients with MM and diabetes had shorter survival than those without diabetes, supporting the present findings [39]. Additionally, diabetes has been shown to increase the risk of complications such as infections in patients with MM, which could contribute to higher mortality depending on the severity of complications [31, 32]. Unfortunately, due to the nature of the available data and the fact that patients were from a wide geographic area and could seek care at different hospitals, this study could not determine the occurrence of complications or the exact causes of fatality. Previous research has indicated that antidiabetic medication reduces the conversion rate of MGUS to MM, but its influence on MM progression remains unclear [31].

In addition to diabetes, this study identified age, ISS stage III, HSCT, and chromosomal abnormalities as independent factors associated with OS. Age is a well-established predictor of poor prognosis in MM [10]. Similarly, tumor burden (reflected by ISS stage) and cytogenetic or molecular abnormalities are recognized prognostic factors, indicating that the analyzed patient population is representative of MM cases [2]. Moreover, HSCT was linked to improved OS, consistent with existing literature [40].

This study had several limitations. Due to documentation bias, data on antidiabetic drugs and complications were unavailable for analysis. The potential interaction between antidiabetic and MM drugs could not be assessed. Additionally, because many patients received diabetes treatment at other hospitals, glycemic control was not analyzed. The single-center design restricted the sample size, and local practices, guidelines, and policies may have introduced bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings. In interpreting the results of our study, it is notable that blood glucose level did not emerge as a significant prognostic factor in the analyses of patients with MM and SIDM or MM and T2DM. This apparent inconsistency may be attributed to the complex interplay between diabetes and MM, where the overall impact on prognosis is influenced by multiple factors beyond glycemic control alone. For instance, the presence of diabetes may affect the eligibility for certain treatments or increase the risk of complications, thereby indirectly influencing survival outcomes. Additionally, the retrospective nature of our study and the potential for unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the observed associations. Further investigation into the specific mechanisms through which diabetes affects MM prognosis, including the role of glycemic control, is warranted to better understand this relationship. This study has several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective nature of the study introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding variables. For instance, patients in the diabetes mellitus (DM) groups exhibited higher plasma cell percentages and lower hemoglobin levels, which may have contributed to the poorer prognosis observed in these groups. These baseline differences could reflect underlying disease severity or other unmeasured comorbidities, rather than being solely attributable to diabetes. Secondly, the inability to fully control for all potential confounding factors limits the ability to definitively conclude that diabetes is the sole driver of the observed differences in prognosis. Thirdly, the single-center design and potential influence of local policies may have introduced bias, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other populations or settings. This highlights the need for multicenter studies to validate our results. Future prospective studies with more rigorous control of confounding variables are needed to validate our findings and further elucidate the impact of diabetes on the prognosis of multiple myeloma. Additionally, this study lacks detailed cytogenetic risk stratification (e.g., high-risk vs. standard-risk) and data on key agents like lenalidomide, which may influence prognosis. Future studies should incorporate cytogenetic risk profiling and treatment-specific analyses (e.g., proteasome inhibitors vs. immunomodulatory drugs) to better delineate the impact of diabetes on MM outcomes. Finally, cytogenetic data were analyzed as a binary variable (abnormal vs. normal) due to inconsistent documentation of specific high-risk abnormalities (e.g., del(17p), t(4;14)) over the 12-year study period. This limitation precludes risk stratification by current molecular standards. Future studies should incorporate standardized FISH panels on CD138 + cells to address this gap.

Conclusion

Both T2DM and SIDM are independently linked to poorer prognosis in patients with MM. These findings indicate that tailored management strategies may be necessary to improve survival outcomes in patients with MM and diabetes. However, this study did not assess glycemic control. Future prospective trials should investigate the influence of glycemic control on the prognosis of patients with MM and diabetes.
