One-year dynamics of clinical activity during biologics therapy in patients of ulcerative colitis
Authors: Yizhe Tie, Fengyuan Su, Jianwu Zhang, Minhui Zheng, Ningyi Li, Jieqi Zheng, Minhu Chen, Rirong Chen, Li Li, Shenghong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLongitudinal trajectories and the optimal timing for evaluating clinical activity remains unclear in ulcerative colitis (UC) patients receiving biologics.MethodsThis study included 2705 UC patients from 5 trials and collected 28 807 records of partial Mayo scores (PMS) from baseline to week 52. Latent class growth mixed model was performed to construct 1-year PMS trajectories. Logistic regression assessed the association between PMS trajectories and 1-year therapeutic outcomes, and endoscopic remission was the primary outcome. Spearman correlation was utilized to identify the timepoints of PMS most correlated with the trajectories.ResultsFive trajectories of clinical activity were identified, including rapid remission (n = 590, 21.8%), delayed remission (n = 316, 11.7%), mild improvement (n = 748, 27.7%), slow improvement (n = 915, 33.8%), and relapse-fluctuation (n = 136, 5.0%) groups. Endoscopic and histological remission rates exhibited a progressive decline across trajectory groups, with the highest probability in the rapid remission group and sequentially lower probabilities in the delayed remission, mild/slow improvement, and relapse-fluctuation groups (P < .001). Week-20 PMS demonstrated the strongest correlation with clinical activity trajectories (correlation coefficient = 0.7867, P < .001), with an area under curve of 0.9311 (95% CI: 0.9183, 0.9440). Furthermore, week-20 PMS could independently predicted 1-year endoscopic (aOR [95% CI] 0.59 [0.54, 0.65], P < .001) and histological (aOR [95% CI] 0.61 [0.53-0.70], P < .001) remission.ConclusionsUC patients receiving biologics would exhibit 5 distinct trajectories of clinical activity. Week 20 is a potential intermediate timepoint for clinical activity assessment to inform long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic and recurrent inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).1 The prevalence of UC has exceeded 0.3% in Western countries, with increasing incidence rates in newly industrialized countries, thereby positioning UC as a disease of global concern.2 UC patients usually endure prolonged relapsing-remitting disease courses, which significantly impairs quality of life.3 Therefore, it is imperative to identify optimal strategies for controlling disease activity, improving prognosis and alleviating the overall disease burden in UC.

Nowadays, biologics serves as an important first-line therapy for moderate-to-severe UC patients when conventional treatment fails, providing effective solutions for controlling disease activity and inducing subsequent remission.4 However, although biologics have improved the prognosis of UC, some patients may experience suboptimal therapeutic outcomes, such as primary non-response or secondary loss of response.5 Therefore, timely monitoring disease activity and predicting therapeutic response are vital, especially in the initial year of biologic treatment. As one of the most convenient and feasible measurements, clinical activity assessment exerts an essential role in the management of UC.6 Several prior studies have observed that achieving symptomatic improvement before week 16 could help to predict long-term outcomes, illustrating the importance of longitudinal clinical activity monitoring in prognostic stratification as well as personalized and precision medicine.7,8 However, there are few studies illustrating the dynamics of clinical activity over time or investigating its association with other therapeutic outcomes. In addition, the optimal timing for clinical activity assessment has not yet reached a unified consensus, with different studies utilizing varying time.9

To address this challenge, our study integrated 28 807 clinical activity records from 2705 UC patients to: (1) identify 1-year trajectories of clinical activity during biologics therapy; (2) investigate the association between these trajectories and long-term outcomes, including endoscopic and histological remission; and (3) determine the optimal timepoint for assessing trajectories of disease activity.

Methods

Study design

This post hoc study utilized data from 5 large-scale clinical trials, including UNIFI (Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT02407236), PURSUIT (Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT00488774, NCT00487539 and NCT00488631), ACT-1(Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT00036439), GEMINI 1 (Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT00783718), and VARSITY (Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT02497469), which evaluates the therapeutic efficacy of ustekinumab, golimumab, infliximab, adalimumab, and vedolizumab in moderate-to-severe active UC patients.10–14 And the design of these 5 trials could be found in the original publications.

Our study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.15

Participants

We included those UC patients receiving biologics during induction and maintenance period in our analysis. Considering the requirement for constructing trajectories, we screened for those patients with at least 3 records of clinical activity at different times. Participants who received placebo during induction or maintenance therapy as well as those lacked 3 times of clinical activity evaluation were excluded. The flowchart for screening patients was shown in Figure S1.

Variables

The primary variable of interest was clinical activity at specific timepoints and their trajectory during the first year of biologic therapy, as assessed by the partial Mayo score (PMS). To ensure data consistency given the varying follow-up intervals across different studies, we established 15 predefined assessment timepoints: baseline (week 0), as well as 2, 4, 8 ± 2, 12 ± 2, 16 ± 2, 20 ± 2, 24 ± 2, 28 ± 2, 32 ± 2, 36 ± 2, 40 ± 2, 44 ± 2, 48 ± 2, and 52 ± 2 weeks after initiating biologics. When more than one PMS was available within any given time window, the mean score of them was calculated to represent the clinical activity for that interval.

Other variables of interest include sex, age, disease duration, disease extension, previous biologic therapy, concomitant medication (corticosteroid, 5-aminosalicylic acid, and immunomodulator), baseline disease activity indexes (C-reactive protein [CRP], fecal calprotectin [FC], PMS, and Mayo endoscopic score [MES]), and types of biologics.

Outcomes

The outcomes of this study included clinical, endoscopic, and histological outcomes at the end of the maintenance therapy (around 52 weeks). Clinical remission was defined as PMS <3 and no subscore >1. Endoscopic outcomes included endoscopic improvement (MES ≤ 1) and endoscopic remission (MES = 0). Histological remission (highest Geboes score < 2.0) and histological improvement (highest Geboes score < 3.2) at the end of maintenance stage were only available in patients from UNIFI and VARSITY trails. Endoscopic remission was the primary outcome in our analysis. Withdrawal from therapy was considered as failure to achieve the predefined outcomes.

Statistical analyses

Latent class growth mixed models (LCGMM) were employed to construct the clinical activity trajectories during 1-year biological therapy, which hypothesized the existence of latent subgroups with distinct growth patterns and fits linear or polynomial curves to characterize trajectories among subgroups.16,17 We developed LCGMMs with 2 to 5 classes base on linear, quadratic, and cubic functions. The optimal trajectory model was selected based on: (1) minimum Bayesian information criterion value; (2) each trajectory class comprising ≥5% of participants; and (3) the average posterior probability of assignment >70% for all trajectory classes.16

Continuous variables were described as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables were described using frequencies with proportions and compared by chi-square tests. Multinomial logistic regression was conducted to explore the association between baseline characteristics and PMS trajectories, and binary logistic regression was utilized to assess the correlation between trajectories and therapeutic outcomes at the end of the maintenance therapy, in which multivariate analyses were used to adjust for 8 confounding factors (sex, age, disease duration, previous biologic therapy, baseline CRP, baseline PMS, baseline MES, and types of biologics) identified through prior literature and univariate analysis (see Table S1).18 Results were presented as odds ratios (OR)/adjusted OR (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Spearman correlation was utilized to identify the timing of PMS most correlated with PMS trajectories. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to assess area under the curve (AUC) and Youden index was used to select the optimal cutoff point. Internal validation was conducted using the bootstrap method (resampling = 1000).19 Subgroup analyses evaluated interactions with age (<40 or ≥40 years), sex, prior biologic exposure, and biological type. Sensitivity analysis were conducted to construct clinical activity trajectories separately for patient treated with anti-Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents (golimumab, infliximab, and adalimumab) and non-anti-TNF biologics (ustekinumab and vedolizumab) to verify if these 5 trajectories remain stable across different drug classes. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.6.3). P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The data were previously collected anonymously so that no need of additional ethical approval was required.

Results

Study population

After excluding those receiving placebo in induction or maintenance period (N = 1849) or those lacking 3 times of PMS (N = 49), we finally included 2705 UC patients into the analysis, and patient characteristics were shown in Table 1. Among the enrolled patients, 58.8% were male and 23.7% had prior biologic exposure. The median (IQR) age and disease duration were 40(30,50) and 4.9(2.2,9.24) years, respectively. Regarding baseline disease activity, the median (IQR) of PMS and MES were 6(5,7) points and 3(2,3) points in our study. As for types of biologics, there are 380(14.1%), 231(8.5%), 713(26.4%), 409(15.1%), and 972(35.9%) patients receiving adalimumab, infliximab, golimumab, ustekinumab, and vedolizumab, respectively. The patient characteristics of different biologics were shown in Table S2.

Dynamic trajectories for clinical activity

Based on those 2705 patients, 28 807 records of PMS from baseline to week 52 has been used to construct trajectories of clinical activity based on LCGMM. The number and percentage of patients, as well as missing percentage at each predefined assessment timepoints were shown in Table S3. According to the selection criteria of optimal trajectory, the best-fitting model for PMS required cubic polynomial function fitting with 5 classes (see Table S4).

These trajectories were designated as rapid remission group (n = 590[21.8%]), delayed remission group (n = 316[11.7%]), mild improvement group (n = 748[27.7%]), slow improvement group (n = 915[33.8%]), and relapse-fluctuation group (n = 136[5.0%], see Figure 1). The PMS trajectory in rapid remission group had relatively low baseline PMS and rapidly declined to low level (PMS < 1) after week 12, which sustained this low level throughout 1-year follow-up. The delayed remission group exhibited high baseline PMS but showed gradual decline to low level (PMS < 1) by week 24, remaining stable thereafter. The mild improvement group improved steadily before week 8 but maintained stabilized PMS around 3 after that. The slow improvement group displayed gradual and marginal decrease in PMS, with persistent moderate activity at 1 year. The relapse-fluctuation group was characterized by precipitous declines during induction period followed by quick relapse during maintenance. Violin plots of observed PMS over time for each trajectory group were summarized in Figures S2-S6, which demonstrated reasonable agreement between visual inspection of observed PMS and model-estimated curves throughout follow-up.

Trajectories of clinical activity for UC patients among 1-year biologics.

Among different trajectory groups, significant differences in previous biological exposure, disease duration, concomitant medication, baseline disease activity, trials, and types of biologics were observed (see Table S5). Notably, regarding the percentages of biologic types across different trajectories, the delayed remission group exhibited a relatively higher proportion of patients receiving ustekinumab (31.7%), whereas golimumab was more prevalent in the mild improvement (26.1%), slow improvement (36.7%), and relapse-fluctuation (27.9%) groups. Vedolizumab, which was the most frequently used biologic overall (35.9% of the enrolled population), accounted for a substantial proportion across most trajectory groups, particularly in the relapse-fluctuation group (41.2%). Compared with the rapid remission group, association between baselines and other trajectories were shown in Table S6. For example, trajectories were significantly associated with type of biologics. With infliximab as the reference, patients treated with adalimumab exhibited lower possibility of being classified into the delayed remission group (aOR 0.190 [0.118, 0.306], P < .001) and the relapse-fluctuation group (aOR 0.313 [0.152, 0.647], P = .002). Golimumab was associated with increased possibility of the mild improvement group (aOR 1.580 [1.128, 2.212], P = .008) and the slow improvement group (aOR 2.637 [1.865, 3.727], P < .001), compared to infliximab. In contrast, both ustekinumab and vedolizumab predicted significantly lower odds of delayed remission, mild improvement, slow improvement, and relapse-fluctuation groups (all P < .001).

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that, in both the anti-TNF and non-anti-TNF groups, the optimal group-based clinical activity trajectory model was a 5-class solution with a cubic polynomial function (Tables S7 and S8). Furthermore, similar patterns were observed in both groups, including the rapid remission group, delayed remission group, mild improvement group, slow improvement group, and relapse-fluctuation group (Figures S7 and S8). Notably, distinct distribution of patients across trajectories was observed, particularly for the rapid remission group, which comprised 29.61% of patients in the anti-TNF subgroup compared with 13.61% in the non-anti-TNF subgroup.

Association between clinical activity trajectories and long-term therapeutic outcomes

We compared the percentage of achieving therapeutic outcomes at the end of the maintenance therapy among the 5 clinical activity trajectories (see Figure 2) and found that different trajectories were associated with distinct therapeutic outcomes, including endoscopic remission (P < .001), clinical remission (P < .001), endoscopic improvement (P < .001), histological remission (P < .001), and histological improvement (P < .001). Notably, patients in rapid remission group achieved the most favorable prognosis, in which the rate for endoscopic remission, endoscopic improvement, clinical remission, histological remission, and histological improvement were 53.16%, 78.95%, 88.42%, 33.23%, and 74.12%, respectively. The delayed remission group showed relatively inferior outcomes, achieving endoscopic remission rates of 39.16%. Mild improvement group, slow improvement group, and relapse-fluctuation group exhibited further suboptimal improvement, in which the rates of endoscopic remission were all less than 20%. Notably, in relapse-fluctuation group, the percentages across all therapeutic endpoints were only approximately 5%.

Percentage of achieving therapeutic outcomes at the end of maintenance therapy between 5 clinical activity trajectories.

Furthermore, logistic regression showed that rapid remission group was more likely to achieve all therapeutic endpoints (see Table 2). Compared to rapid remission group, aOR[95% CI] for endoscopic remission in mild improvement group, slow improvement group, and relapse-fluctuation group were 0.19([0.14,0.26], P < .001), 0.12([0.08,0.17], P < .001), and 0.04([0.01,0.11], P < .001), respectively. Although the likelihood of achieving endoscopic (aOR [95% CI]: 0.70 [0.49,1.01], P = .055) as well as histological remission (aOR [95% CI]: 0.75 [0.46,1.20], P = .232) in delayed remission group were lower than that of rapid remission group, there were no statistical significance. The likelihood of achieving histological remission in mild improvement group (aOR [95% CI]: 0.30 [0.19,0.46], P < .001) and slow improvement group (aOR [95% CI]: 0.08 [0.03,0.17], P < .001) were significantly lower than that of rapid remission group. No patients in relapse-fluctuation group achieved histological remission. Analysis for endoscopic improvement, clinical remission and histological remission showed that rapid remission group had significantly higher likelihoods of achieving therapeutic endpoints than all other groups (P < .001). After adjusting for the confounding factors, 5 trajectories could predict therapeutic outcomes at the end of maintenance, with an AUC[95% CI] of 0.7710[0.7467,0.7953] in predicting endoscopic remission. After internal validation by Bootstrap, the 5-trajectory model demonstrated good discriminative ability for predicting therapeutic outcomes at the end of maintenance, with an AUC[95% CI] of 0.7615[0.7375,0.7851] for endoscopic remission (see Table S9).

The optimal timing for evaluating and predicting clinical activity trajectories

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between PMS at different timepoints and clinical activity trajectories, which observed highest correlation at week 20 (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient = 0.7867, adjusted P < .001; see Figure 3; Table S10).

Performance of partial Mayo score at different timepoints for distinguishing clinical activity trajectories.

Furthermore, according to 1-year therapeutic outcomes among distinct clinical activity trajectories, trajectories were classified into low-risk trajectories (rapid remission group and delayed remission group) and high-risk trajectories (mild improvement group, slow improvement group, and relapse-fluctuation group). We explored the performance of PMS at different timepoints for distinguishing high risk trajectories and observed that week-20 PMS exhibited highest Youden index, with a sensitivity of 0.9029 and a specificity of 0.8308 (see Figure 3, Table S11). Although the AUC of week-28 PMS was slightly higher than that of week-20, there was no statistical significance. Considering that earlier prediction provides more clinical benefit of avoid suboptimal outcomes induced by delayed treatment escalation, week 20 might be a suitable time for mid-term evaluation of clinical activity.

PMS at week 20 for predicting long-term therapeutic outcomes

Week-20 PMS demonstrated significant predictive value for endoscopic remission (aOR [95% CI]: 0.59 [0.54, 0.65], P < .001), endoscopic improvement (aOR [95% CI]: 0.59 [0.55, 0.64], P < .001), clinical remission (aOR [95% CI]: 0.50 [0.46, 0.54], P < .001), histological remission (aOR [95% CI]: 0.61 [0.53, 0.70], P < .001), and histological improvement (aOR [95% CI]: 0.61 [0.56, 0.67], P < .001) at the end of the maintenance therapy (see Table 3). Subgroup analysis indicates no significant interaction between sex and week-20 PMS, while age, prior biologic exposure, and types of biologics interacted (see Tables S12-S15). Furthermore, we observed that AUC[95% CI] of week-20 PMS for predicting endoscopic remission, endoscopic improvement, clinical remission, histological remission, and histological improvement were 0.7430[0.7151,0.7710], 0.7568[0.7311,0.7824], 0.8169[0.7941,0.8398], 0.7711[0.7341,0.8082], and 0.7384[0.7065,0.7702], respectively. Bootstrap internal validation demonstrated stable model performance, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.7257-0.8102 among 5 outcomes, indicating minimal overfitting and good robustness (see Table S16).

Based on Youden index, week-20 PMS < 3 served as the optimal cutoff for predicting endoscopic remission, with a sensitivity of 0.8265 and a specificity of 0.5461. And bootstrap internal validation demonstrated consistent model performance (see Table S18).

Discussion

This study characterized 5 distinct clinical activity trajectories during 1-year biologic therapy in UC patients and versified their association with clinical, endoscopic, and histological outcomes at the end of maintenance therapy. Besides, week-20 PMS exhibited highest predictive capacity for trajectory classification and could effectively predicted therapeutic outcomes at the end of maintenance period. These findings highlight the importance of clinical activity monitoring in UC patients receiving biologics, and provide evidence to the key timepoints for assessments, which could promote personalized and precision medicine in UC management.

In this study, we constructed the trajectories of clinical activity based on LCGMM, which has been used to analyze the dynamics for individuals with longitudinal data and identify subgroups with potential analogic trajectories.20 A prospective study has described the trajectory of fatigue score of 849 IBD patients during a 9-year follow-up, and found that there was a significant correlation between different trajectory groups, and disease activity.21 Some previous studies have summarized the trajectories of CRP, FC, fecal lactoferrin, and albumin during biological therapy in UC.22,23 Serving as one of the most convenient and accessible disease activity measurements, clinical activity is very suitable for repeated assessments and longitudinal monitoring. For filgotinib, one of the small molecule drugs, group-based trajectory modeling has been conducted to construct 5 distinct subgroups of clinical activity trajectories in UC, which aligns with our study.24 As far as we know, this study reveals the dynamic change trajectory of clinical activity of UC patients during the biological treatment for the first time, which could provide evidences to identify disease course, monitor disease progression, and improve long-term prognosis for UC patients.

Although 15%-38% of UC patients achieve clinical remission within 2 weeks after biologic initiation, current clinical practices consider to defer definitive assessment of treatment response to avoid premature discontinuation, given the potential for delayed therapeutic benefit.9,25 Clinical trials demonstrated that 8-12 weeks of continued therapy yields delayed responses in initial non-responders.26 For example, UNIFI study administered additional ustekinumab to week-8 non-responders, with week-16 responders classified as delayed responders eligible for maintenance therapy.12 And subsequent study found that week-16 responders had higher baseline inflammatory burden but achieved comparable 1-year clinical, endoscopic, and histological outcomes compared to week-8 responders.27 Similar patterns were observed in adalimumab, underscoring the importance of reassessing disease activity beyond the induction phase, particularly in patients with a high inflammatory burden.28 In this context, our study identified week 20 as the optimal intermediate timepoint for evaluating 1-year clinical activity trajectories and predicting subsequent therapy outcomes. This timing aligns with prior evidence showing that clinical remission at approximately 6 months after vedolizumab initiation is strongly associated with sustained remission in inflammatory bowel disease.29 Additional studies have also highlighted the predictive value of serological markers around week 20.30 Furthermore, several clinical trials have adopted 20 ± 2 weeks as the standardized assessment window for evaluating therapeutic efficacy in UC.31,32

Importantly, week-20 PMS should not be interpreted as a justification for delaying treatment optimization in patients with inadequate early response. Rather, it serves as an intermediate prognostic marker for patients who remain on therapy after the initial induction assessment. Within a treat-to-target framework in UC clinical practice, early evaluation and timely treatment adjustment remain essential. In this setting, week-20 PMS may serve as a valuable adjunct to existing early treatment assessments by further refining risk stratification, identifying patients at increased risk of unfavorable long-term outcomes despite ongoing therapy, and informing subsequent therapeutic decision-making. Nevertheless, the clinical utility of week-20 PMS warrants further validation in large-scale prospective real-world studies.

There are some limitations in our study. First, all patients included in our analysis were all moderate and severe UC patients treated with biologics in strict accordance with the prescribed dose and intervals, which might not fully reflect the complex and changeable clinical situation and disease state in the real world.33 For example, therapeutic drug monitoring has emerged as an efficacious and safe strategy for treatment optimization in routine clinical practice, in which some UC patients previously classified as slow improvement group may shift toward rapid remission group through dose escalation. Therefore, real-world study in future is needed to further verify its generalizability. Second, histological outcomes were available only from the UNIFI and VARSITY trials. Accordingly, the association between clinical activity trajectories and histological outcomes should be interpreted with caution, particularly in patients treated with biologics other than adalimumab, ustekinumab, or vedolizumab. Furthermore, although cubic function has been used to fit the trajectories of clinical activity, the fitting result may not accurately reflect the actual value of clinical activity at certain point. Finally, due to the limitation of follow-up time, our study failed to explore the changes of clinical activity after 1 year of biological treatment and its association with hospitalization, extension of disease location, and colectomy.

Conclusion

To sum up, this study described the dynamic change trajectory of clinical activity in UC patients within 1 year after treatment with biological agents, and found that week-20 PMS could help to identify different trajectories and predict therapeutic outcomes at the end of maintenance period, which provided evidence for clinical disease course in UC patients and key timing of clinical activity evaluation.
