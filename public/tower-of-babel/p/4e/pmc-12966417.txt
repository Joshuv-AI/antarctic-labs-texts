Human papillomavirus16 E7 enhances cell stemness by regulating the APC2/SPIN4/β-catenin axis in cervical cancer
Authors: Tao Shen, Yuejiang Ma, Tingting Wu, Zhu Cao, Peng Yi, Xiufeng Huang, Shizhou Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is a causal factor in cervical cancer, driving the cancer’s initiation and progression. Although cancer stem cells (CSCs) have been implicated in maintaining the stemness and malignancy of cervical cancer cells, the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood. In this study, we modulated gene expression in Caski and SiHa cervical cancer cells using siRNA and overexpression approaches. Functional assays, including MTT, transwell, RT-qPCR, western blotting, immunohistochemistry, luciferase reporter, immunofluorescence, and sphere formation, were performed to evaluate target gene expression. Additionally, transcriptome sequencing was used to analyze the impact of silencing HPV16 E7 on SiHa cells, and a xenograft model was assessed for in vivo effects. Our transcriptome sequencing reveals substantial changes in gene expression profiles upon HPV16 E7 silencing in cervical cancer. Notably, we identified APC2 as a key downstream target transcriptionally activated by HPV16 E7 through the transcription factor E2F1, and its elevated expression is associated with poor prognosis in cervical cancer. Surprisingly, APC2 exhibits oncogeneic properties in cervical cancer by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and its overexpression reverses the inhibitory effects of HPV16 E7 silencing on malignancy and CSC properties. Additionally, SPIN4 is identified as a pivotal downstream target of the HPV16 E7/APC2 axis, positively modulating cervical cancer progression. Our study reveals a novel HPV16 E7-APC2-SPIN4 axis as a key driver of cervical cancer. In this pathway, APC2 unexpectedly functions as an oncogene by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling to promote tumorigenesis and CSC properties. Subject terms: Cervical cancer, Cancer stem cells

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the most prevalent and leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women of developing countries, linked to high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), especially HPV16 (50-60% cases) [1]. Screening and HPV vaccination have remarkably reduced cervical cancer incidence in high-income countries, yet remain uncommon in low-income regions due to limited implementation [2, 3]. HPV-driven E6/E7 oncoproteins propel cervical cancer progression and confer chemoresistance and radioresistance [4, 5], highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets. While our previous studies implicate high-risk HPV16 E7 in cellular stemness [6, 7], its precise mechanism requires further exploration.

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) possess the ability for indefinite self-renewal, differentiate into various tumor types, and may originate from either normal stem cells or mutated differentiated cells, leading to uncontrolled growth [8]. Their identification and characterization in cervical cancer is vital, as they govern tumor initiation, progression, recurrence, and therapeutic resistance [9, 10]. Distinct CSC markers (CD44, CD133, ALDH1 [11], and surface antigens [12]) interface with regulatory pathways (Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and Hedgehog [13–16]) and growth factors [17], controlled by transcription factors (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog [18]). Understanding these markers and pathways enables the development of novel CSC-targeted therapies in cervical cancer, potentially overcoming resistance and improving patient outcomes.

Genomic analyses improve cancer diagnosis/prognosis [19] and implicate Numerous differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in cervical carcinogenesis [20], yet high-risk HPV E7’s stemness regulatory networks remain unclear in cervical cancer. Based on our previous findings [6, 7], this study employed transcriptome sequencing, bioinformatics analysis, and experimental approaches in HPV16 E7-silenced cervical cells to uncover the molecular mechanisms regulating stemness, providing insights into the role of HPV oncoproteins in cervical cancer development.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

In this study, C33A (YDT-0108), Caski (YDT-7048), and SiHa (YDT-0595) cervical cancer cell lines were obtained from INDIT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s minimum essential medium (DMEM, BD Bio, L100-500, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, SERANA, S-FBS-MX-015, AUS) in a 37 °C incubator supplied with 5% CO2. All cell lines were confirmed mycoplasma-free, used within 10 passages post-revival, and harvested during the logarithmic growth phase at approximately 80% confluence for experiments.

Western blotting (WB)

Protein expression was assessed by WB assays. Briefly, lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer for protein extraction, and total protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo, 23228, USA). Proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and electrophoretically transferred onto 0.45 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk, membranes were probed with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized by an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Biosharp, BL520B, Hefei, China) and quantified by ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), normalized to β-actin [Treatment group (indicator/inner reference)]/[Control group (indicator/inner reference)]. Cell transfection

Gene manipulation in SiHa and Caski cells was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668-019, USA)-mediated transfection. For overexpression, the APC2 coding sequences (NM_005883) were cloned into pcDNA3.1 vectors (Youbio, VT1001, China) and transfected into SiHa and Caski cells for 24 h. For silencing, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting HPV16 E7 (siR-E7), APC2 (siR-APC2), SPIN4 (siR-SPIN4), or non-targeting control (siR-NC) (Tsingke Bio, Beijing, China) was transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 and cultured for 24 h. siRNA sequences are provided in Table 3.

To achieve stable gene silencing, lentiviral short hairpin RNA (shRNA) vectors targeting APC2 (Lenti-sh-APC2) and a non-targeting control (Lenti-sh-NC) were constructed from validated siR-APC2 sequences. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK-293T cells (YDT-0019), and viral supernatants were used to transduce SiHa cells at 60–70% confluence. Stable clones were selected with puromycin (2.5 µg/mL, Solarbio, P8230, Beijing, China) for 7–10 days. Subsequently, the transfection efficiency was confirmed by WB assays.

Methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) proliferation assay

To evaluate cell proliferation, the MTT assay was performed on Caski and SiHa cells. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well (100 μL/well) and cultured for 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. At each time point, 50 μL of MTT solution (1 mg/mL; Sigma, M2128, USA) was added. After 3 h of incubation at 37 °C, the MTT solution was aspirated, and 150 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Hushi, 30072418, Shanghai, China) was added per well to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo, 51119570, USA).

Transwell experiments

Treated Caski and SiHa cells were seeded into 24-well transwell inserts (2 × 104 cells/well) with 8 μm PET membranes (Corning, 3422, USA). The lower chamber contained DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 hours of migration, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A, Hefei, China) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Aladdin, C110703, Shanghai, China) for 40 min. Migrated cells were quantified by counting three random fields per well under a microscope (Nikon, E100, Japan). For invasion assessment, membranes were pre-coated with Matri-gel (Corning, 356234, USA) for 24 h before cell seeding. Subsequent steps followed the migration protocol.

Sphere formation assay

To assess CSC properties, treated Caski and SiHa cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/mL in DMEM/F12 medium (BD Bio, 01-172-1ACS, USA), which was replaced every 3 days. After two weeks of culturing, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (1 mL/well) for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (1 mL/well; Aladdin, C110703, Shanghai, China) for 40 min. Microscopic images of the stained cells were captured, and ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) was used for quantification. The sphere area was selected through the Adjust->Threshold module of ImageJ for area quantification.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in compliance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China. Based on the experimental procedures from the previous study, some minor modifications have been made [21]. Briefly, 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Slaccas, Shanghai, China) were acclimatized for 1 week under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (12/12-h light/dark cycle, 22 °C). For tumor induction, twenty-four mice (N = 6) were randomly divided and subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 SiHa cells stably transfected with APC2-silencing constructs (Lenti-sh-APC2), SPIN4-silencing constructs (Lenti-sh-SPIN4), or control vectors (Lenti-sh-NC). The mice’s weight and the tumor volume were monitored every three days starting from the seventh day. Upon reaching the tumor volumes measuring 1500 mm³ (calculated as length × width²/2), mice were humanely euthanized, and body weights were recorded. Their tumor masses were excised, photographed, and weighed. Tumor dimensions were measured using digital calipers (SYNTEK, SY-119-200, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paired tumor and adjacent normal cervical tissues were collected from 10 patients with cervical cancer at the Women’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, with informed consent and the approval of the Institutional Review Board (No. IRB-20230172-R). Clinical and xenograft tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol and xylene series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm for IHC analysis.

Briefly, 5 μm sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and underwent antigen retrieval using phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) with shaking for 15 min. After incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 25 min, sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio, A8020, Beijing, China) for 30 min. Primary antibodies (Table 1) were then applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by 1-h incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 25 °C, Diaminobenzidine (DAB, Servicebio, G1212) staining, and hematoxylin (Servicebio, G1004) counterstaining. IHC-stained sections were imaged using a slide scanning imaging system SQS-12P (TeksqRay, China). Three representative fields per sample were captured at ×200 and ×400 magnification. Qualification of IHC results was addressed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). The mean optical density (MOD) was measured for each image after color deconvolution to separate DAB (brown) and hematoxylin (blue) signals. MOD values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent fields per sample.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from six independent samples (three siR-E7 and three siR-NC replicates of SiHa cells). After verifying RNA integrity, samples were sent to LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China) for transcriptome sequencing. To screen for DEGs of HPV16 E7 silent transcript in cervical cancer, the R software “Deseq2” package was used with the condition: padj <0.05, | log2fc |> 0.35. The R software “pheatmap” package was used to show the expression of DEGs in each sample. A fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) bar plot was utilized to quantify DEGs changes. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis for DEGs using the R software “clusterProfiler” package.

Bioinformatics analysis

Cervical cancer RNA-seq data and the corresponding clinical data were downloaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) for a total of 309 samples. The data were divided into two groups: tumor (306 samples) and healthy (3 samples) population. Further, tumor patients were divided into two groups with low APC2 expression according to the median expression of the target gene APC2. The screening conditions were: p-value < 0.05, | log2fc |> 0.35.

Besides, our study further explored the association of APC2 expression with multiple clinical variables. DEGs from the comparison of APC2 (high vs. low) were intersected with those from HPV16 E7 silent transcripts (siR-E7 vs. siR-NC), visualized using a Venn diagram from the R software “ggscatter” package. Correlation between SPIN4 and APC2 expression in tumor samples was also assessed with “ggscatter”. Further, the tumor patients were divided into two groups with high and low SPIN4 expression according to the median expression of the target gene SPIN4. The screening conditions were: p-value < 0.05, | FC | > 1.5. Gene expression box plot (SPIN4 in cervical cancer vs. normal tissues) was generated using the “ggplot2” package. Survival analysis, performed with the “survival” and “survminer” packages, determined the optimal cutoff for SPIN4 expression groups. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to obtain the enriched pathways of differentially expressed proteins.

Luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910, USA). Briefly, SiHa cells were plated in 24-well plates and grown to approximately 80% confluence. The cells were then co-transfected with the reporter plasmid (pGL3-APC2-WT or pGL3-APC2-MUT constructs) and a plasmid expressing the E2F1 transcription factor (pcDNA3.1-E2F1) or an empty vector control (pcDNA3.1) using Lipofectamine 2000. After 24 h, cells were lysed, and the cleared lysates were analyzed. Following the instructions in the manual, firefly luciferase activity (from the reporter) was measured after adding LAR II, followed by measurement of renilla luciferase activity (from an internal control) after adding Stop & Glo® Reagent. Relative activity was calculated as the ratio of Firefly to Renilla luminescence.

Flow cytometry

To analyze ALDH1A1 activity, cervical cancer cells transfected with siR-APC2 or siR-NC (negative control) were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and fixed using an IC Fixation Buffer (Invitrogen, 00-8222-49) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized with 1× Permeabilization Buffer (Invitrogen, 00-8333-56) and then incubated with a directly conjugated anti-ALDH1A1 antibody (Abcam, ab52492, UK; diluted in 1× Permeabilization Buffer at 1:20) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in 200 µL PBS for immediate analysis. Flow cytometry was performed on an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo, A24858, USA), and the percentage of ALDH1A1-positive cells was quantified using Attune NxT software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were seeded on glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Acros Organics, 215680010, Belgium) for 5 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 90 min, the coverslips were incubated with primary antibodies (APC2, Proteintech, 13559-1-AP, 1:200) diluted in 5% BSA overnight at 4 °C. The following day, cells were washed and incubated with appropriate fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies (CoraLite®488-Conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, Proteintech, SA00013-2, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (20 µg/mL; Servicebio, G1012) for 5 min. After final washes, the coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using an anti-fade mounting medium. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica, STELLARIS8, Germany) (magnification times: 400×).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the representative data was executed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 software. The t-test was employed for pairwise comparisons, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized for comparisons among multiple groups. Additionally, Cohen’s d method defined the effect size calculations in the in vivo experiments (https://calculatorfree.net/zh-cn/math/effect-size-calculator.html). All experiments included appropriate controls and were repeated a minimum of three times to ensure reproducibility. Statistical significance was defined by thresholds of *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling reveals HPV16 E7-mediated regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and APC2 expression in cervical cancer

Based on our previous finding that HPV16 E7 sustains stemness in cervical cancer cells (Caski and SiHa) to regulate tumor malignant phenotypes [6], we further demonstrated that this effect is mediated through RTKN2-dependent activation of the NF-κB pathway [7]. Intriguingly, we now report a novel crosstalk between HPV16 E7 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis, a key regulator of cell stemness [22], suggesting a more complex mechanistic network underlying stemness maintenance in cervical cancer. Specifically, we conducted a comprehensive transcriptome sequencing on HPV16 E7-silenced SiHa cells (siR-E7) and the corresponding control cells (siR-NC) to identify the gene changes induced by HPV16 E7 silencing. The silencing efficiency (~31% in Caski and ~30% in SiHa cells; **p < 0.01) was verified in Fig. 1A. Volcano plot analysis identified 39 upregulated and 292 downregulated genes in HPV16 E7-silenced SiHa cells vs. controls (Fig. 1B). A heatmap visualized differential expression patterns (Fig. 1C), highlighting several stemness-related DEGs. Notably, APH1B, BBC3, and CDKN1A were upregulated (influencing cell cycle and apoptosis [23, 24]), while PHF19, APC2, KREMEN2, ASPM, E2F1, BIRC5, RACGAP1, and EZH2 were downregulated (involved in cell proliferation, chromatin modification, and the Wnt signaling [25–29]) (Fig. 1D). A FPKM values confirmed these changes (Fig. 1E). GO analysis highlighted mitotic processes and ATP-dependent DNA activities (Fig. 1F), and KEGG analysis identified pathways including cell cycle, DNA replication/repair, p53 signaling, and the Wnt pathway (Fig. 1G), suggesting potential disruptions of these processes following HPV16 E7 silencing in cervical cancer. Critically, silencing of HPV16 E7 in Caski and SiHa cells reduced β-catenin protein expression level (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, sequencing data revealed a significant downregulation of APC2, a recognized critical negative regulator of the Wnt pathway [29, 30], in cervical cancer cells with silenced HPV16 E7, prompting further investigation into this gene. Given the Wnt pathway’s pivotal role in regulating CSC properties, including stemness [13], our data propose a plausible mechanism wherein HPV16 E7 may enhance CSC traits by modulating APC2 to activate the Wnt pathway, thereby promoting cervical cancer malignancy.

APC2 is downregulated by HPV16 E7 silencing and associated with altered transcriptional profiles and poor prognosis in cervical cancer

Interestingly, our investigation into the mechanism underlying HPV16 E7-mediated changes in APC2 expression revealed a putative E2F1 binding site within the APC2 promoter (Sequence information is provided in Supplementary Data 1). This discovery was confirmed using luciferase reporter assays that E2F1 overexpression significantly increased APC2 promoter-driven luciferase activity in SiHa cells, while mutation of the putative E2F1 binding site (APC2 MUT: the reverse complementary sequence of the E2F1 binding site, with sequence information provided in Supplementary Data 1) markedly reduced this activity (Fig. 2A). This finding aligns with our previous work and existing models proposing an indirect role for HPV16 E7 in modulating cellular pathways via E2F1 [7, 31, 32]. Next, we investigated the role of APC2 in HVP 16 E7-driven cervical carcinogenesis. First, we compared APC2 expression in cervical cancer cell lines with different HPV16 statuses. WB analysis revealed detectable APC2 in all tested lines (HPV16-positive SiHa and CaSki; HPV16-negative C33A), with relative expression levels of 0.83, 0.57, and 0.60, respectively (Fig. 2B). Silencing of HPV16 E7 further significantly reduced APC2 protein expression by approximately 40% in Caski (**p < 0.01) and 56% in SiHa cells (*p < 0.05), as confirmed by WB analysis (Fig. 2C). To further investigate APC2’s functional role, IF staining revealed that APC2 is primarily localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2D).

Furthermore, to explore the APC2’s impact on cervical cancer gene expression, we analyzed APC2-related DEGs in cervical cancer from TCGA database, with a volcano plot showing 1135 upregulated and 3255 downregulated genes (Fig. 2E). Additionally, GO and KEGG analyses of APC2-related DEGs in cervical cancer highlighted enriched biological processes, molecular functions, and signaling pathways, including the Wnt signaling and pathways related to immune, metabolic, and developmental processes (Fig. 2F, G). Clinical correlation revealed that APC2 expression was significantly associated with advanced regional lymph node (N0 stage) (p = 0.011) and stage I (p = 0.034) in cervical cancer (Table 4). These findings suggest that APC2 may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting the aggressiveness and prognosis of HVP 16 E7-driven cervical cancer.

Silencing of APC2 of cervical cancer inhibits stemness and malignancy through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling in vivo and in vitro

To further investigate the functional role of APC2, siRNA-mediated silencing of APC2 in Caski and SiHa cells significantly reduced protein expression by approximately 48% (p < 0.01) and 44% (p < 0.05), respectively, compared to controls, as confirmed by WB analysis (Fig. 3A). Subsequent analysis revealed significant phenotypic changes in APC2-silenced cervical cancer cell lines. Specifically, APC2-silenced cells exhibited decreased proliferation rates (Fig. 3B), and cells’ migratory and invasive capacities were significantly impaired (Fig. 3C). Notably, APC2 silencing in cervical cancer cells markedly reduced stemness characteristics, as evidenced by decreased the proportion of ALDH1+ cells and tumor spheroid formation, as well as downregulated expression of stem cell markers (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog) (Fig. 3D–F). Importantly, APC2 silencing in cervical cancer cells significantly reduced β-catenin expression (Fig. 3F), suggesting inactivation of the Wnt pathway. Subsequently, IHC analysis revealed a significant upregulation of stemness-related markers (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog), APC2, and HPV16 E7 in cervical cancer tissues vs. para-carcinoma tissue (Fig. 3G), reinforcing their carcinogenic roles in cancer progression.

To investigate the role of APC2 in vivo, SiHa cells with stable APC2 silencing (~63% reduction, p < 0.05; Fig. 4A) were subcutaneously inoculated to establish a xenograft tumor for in vivo functional analysis. Our data showed that tumors derived from APC2-silenced SiHa cells exhibited significant growth inhibition (Fig. 4B), as evidenced by decreased tumor volume (**p < 0.01; with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 4.68) and weight (**p < 0.01; with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 3.93) compared to controls (Fig. 4C). Notably, the silencing of APC2 did not affect overall mouse body weight (Fig. 4D), indicating minimal systemic toxicity. Additionally, IHC analysis of APC2-silenced xenograft tumors confirmed reduced APC2 expression, along with decreased levels of stemness markers (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog) and the Wnt pathway activity (β-catenin) (Fig. 4E). Our findings indicate that APC2 promotes tumor progression by sustaining cancer stemness and activating the Wnt signaling in cervical cancer.

APC2 overexpression restores the inhibitory effects of silenced HPV16 E7 through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling on cervical cancer cell aggressiveness

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed phenotypic changes, we successfully constructed APC2 overexpression in SiHa cells for subsequent restoration experiments (Fig. 5A). Combined APC2 overexpression and silenced HPV16 E7 induced marked phenotypic alterations in SiHa cells. Specifically, while E7 silencing alone exerted inhibitory effects, the combination resulted in effectively enhanced proliferation (Fig. 5B). Consistently, APC2 overexpression rescued silenced HPV16 E7-mediated suppression of migration, invasion, and sphere-forming capacities (Fig. 5C, D). Additionally, by detecting the protein expressions of cell stemness indicators (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog) and the Wnt pathway marker (β-catenin), we found that APC2 overexpression significantly reversed the impact of HPV16 E7 silencing on these marker levels in SiHa cells (Fig. 5E). Our findings suggest that HPV16 E7 positively regulates APC2 to activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby promoting the malignant phenotype and stemness of cervical cancer, at least partially in this context.

HPV16 E7/APC2 axis positively regulaties SPIN4 expression in cervical cancer

To identify downstream targets of the HPV16 E7-APC2 axis, overlap analysis via a Venn diagram identified a cohort of APC2-related and silenced HPV16 E7-related DEGs. including NKPD1, DIO2, APC2, NCR3LG1, SPIN4, GNG13, B3GNT9, ZFR2, SMC1B, RPS27L, RSPH1, NEFH, and AXL (Fig. 6A). Gene expression, quantified by FKPM values (Fig. 6B), showed significantly reduced SPIN4 upon HPV16 E7 silencing, confirmed in cervical cancer cells by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6C) and WB assays (Fig. 6D). Analysis of publicly datasets revealed a strong positive correlation between APC2 and SPIN4 expression (p = 0.022) (Fig. 6E). Critically, IHC staining further showed significantly increased SPIN4 expression in cervical cancer tissues but decreased expression in APC2-silenced xenograft tumors, compared to their respective controls (Fig. 6F,G). Additionally, manipulating APC2 levels directly regulated SPIN4 expression in cervical cancer cells, confirming their regulatory relationship (Fig. 6H). Our findings indicate a critical role for the HPV16 E7/APC2 axis in cervical cancer progression by modulating SPIN4 expression, revealing a novel signaling pathway.

HPV16 E7/APC2 axis promotes the malignancy and stemness of cervical cancer by positively regulating SPIN4 expression in vitro and in vivo

To explore SPIN4’s role in cervical cancer, analysis of TCGA RNA-seq data revealed significant upregulation in cervical tumor vs. Normal tissues (p = 0.027) (Fig. 7A) and association with poorer overall survival [p = 0.037; HR = 2.54 (95% confidence interval: 0.93-6.98); Fig. 7B]. GO and KEGG analyses revealed SPIN4-related DEGs in cervical cancer involved in various biological processes, functions, and cancer pathways, with the Wnt signaling being a notable finding (Fig. 7C, D). To validate SPIN4’s role, we silenced it in SiHa cells (silencing efficiency of~32% as confirmed by WB; Fig. 7E), and co-expressed APC2 with SPIN4-targeting siRNA. While APC2 overexpression promoted malignant phenotypes (proliferation, migration, invasion, and spheroid formation), combined overexpressed APC2 and silenced SPIN4 partly reverted these effects (Fig. 7F–H). Stemness markers (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog) and the Wnt pathway component (β-catenin) also followed this pattern, with SPIN4 silencing passivating APC2’s induction (Fig. 7I).

In vivo, we established a xenograft model using SiHa cells with stable SPIN4 sliencing. Examination of xenograft tumors revealed a pronounced size reduction in the Lenti-sh-SPIN4 group relative to the Lenti-sh-NC controls (Fig. 8A), a finding well-tolerated by the mice as evidenced by stable body weights (Fig. 8B). Quantitative analysis confirmed the significant inhibitory effect of SPIN4 knockdown on tumor growth, as shown by decreased tumor volume (**p < 0.01; with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 2.45) and weight (**p < 0.01; with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 2.76) (Fig. 8C). IHC analysis of the resultant tumors revealed a marked reduction in stemness markers (OCT4, SOX2, and Nanog) and the Wnt pathway component (β-catenin) upon SPIN4 sliencing (Fig. 8D), consistent with our in vitro observations. These findings suggest SPIN4 as an effector of the HPV16 E7/APC2 axis driving cervical cancer malignancy and stemness through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation in vitro and in vivo.

Discussion

High-risk HPV, particularly HPV16, drives cervical cancer [2] and enhances CSC traits, including self-renewal and therapy resistance [8], by dysregulating pathways related to cancer, such as the Wnt/β-catenin, Hedgehog, and the PI3K/AKT signaling [33–36]. Our previous studies have hinted at the pivotal role of HPV16 E7 in regulating cell stemness in cervical cancer [6], yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Building upon our previous finding that HPV16 E7 promotes stemness via RTKN2-mediated activation of NF-κB signaling [7]. This study reveals its additional role through the APC2/SPIN4 axis to modulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, underscoring a novel mechanistic crosstalk that enhances stemness and tumor progression in cervical cancer.

Among the HPV16-encoded proteins, HPV16 E6 and E7 oncoproteins have attracted considerable attention due to their well-documented roles in carcinogenesis [1]. The HPV16 E6 protein has demonstrated that it interacts with tumor suppressor protein p53, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation, disrupting the cell cycle and DNA repair, leading to genomic instability and cancer progression [37, 38]. Similarly, the HPV16 E7 protein destabilizes the retinoblastoma (Rb) tumor suppressor protein via ubiquitin-proteasome degradation, dysregulating the cell cycle and enabling uncontrolled cell proliferation, crucial for the HPV16-associated malignancies [39, 40]. Beyond its known role in Rb inactivation, HPV16 E7 may promote cervical cancer through multi-pathway synergy. Our transcriptomic profiling showed HPV16 E7 silencing disrupts cell division, DNA repair, and p53 signaling, linking Rb loss to replication stress and DNA damage [41]. Furthermore, this study provides significant insights into HPV16 E7’s role in cervical cancer progression, as the concurrent Wnt/β-catenin inactivation following HPV16 E7 silencing suggests a non-canonical stemness mechanism in cervical cancer cells. Based on our previous work and existing literature [7, 31, 32], we propose that HPV16 E7 indirectly impacts APC2 via E2F1, a key downstream effector released upon HPV16 E7-mediated pRb inactivation to facilitate the proliferation of cervical cancer [42]. Luciferase reporter assays identified a putative E2F1 binding site in the APC2 promoter, suggesting direct transcriptional regulation. Interestingly, our data reveal APC2 expression differences among HPV16-positive and -negative cervical cancer cell lines. Despite both being HPV16-positive, the varying APC2 levels in Caski and SiHa imply other influencing factors, with these expression contrasts offering new insights into APC2 regulation in HPV16-driven cervical cancer, enhancing our understanding and suggesting directions for future research. Importantly, silencing HPV16 E7 in cervical cancer cells led to significant downregulation of APC2, universally identified as a critical negative regulator of the Wnt pathway [30, 43]. Innovatively, we demonstrate APC2’s oncogenic role in cervical cancer: its silencing reduced proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness, accompanied by the Wnt pathway inactivation (lower β-catenin). Xenograft model confirmed these effects, contrasting APC2’s typical role as a tumor suppressor in other cancers [44–46].

APC2 has been verified to interact with the Wnt pathway, especially β-catenin [43]. In the inactive Wnt signaling, a cytoplasmic β-catenin destruction complex (AXIN/APC/GSK-3β/CK-1α) phosphorylates β-catenin for degradation, inhibiting target genes [47]. Dysfunctional APC2 disrupts this, causing nuclear β-catenin accumulation, oncogenic β-catenin/TCF/LEF complex formation, and increased cell proliferation/migration/invasion [30, 44, 48, 49]. APC2, an APC homolog, draws attention in cancer for its Wnt pathway-dependent and independent roles [50]. Daly et al. reported functional redundancy between APC and APC2 in Wnt signaling, homeostasis, and tumorigenesis [51]. Notably, while APC inhibits ligand-independent Wnt via clathrin endocytosis, APC2 loss does not induce receptor activation [52]. Crucially, our results suggest it may exert a context-dependent, oncogenic function in HPV-driven cervical cancer. This discrepancy highlights the complex, tissue-specific nature of carcinogenesis and the ability of viral oncoproteins to reprogram host cell functions [53]. The molecular landscape of HPV-positive cervical cancer is uniquely shaped by the persistent expression of the oncoproteins [54], an altered environment in which the conventional functions of host cell proteins may be subverted. APC2 overexpression effectively reversed the tumor-suppressive effects of HPV16 E7 silencing in cervical cancer, promoting malignancy stemness and the Wnt pathway activity. We hypothesize that in viral oncogenesis, HPV E7-mediated upregulation of APC2 may confer non-canonical functions, such as stabilizing a protein complex that enhances Wnt signaling or engaging alternative pathways to promote stemness, as mentioned NF-κB Pathway in our previous study [7]. Notably, this nuanced view emphasizes that protein function cannot be extrapolated across all cancer types without considering the specific oncogenic drivers and cellular context.

Subsequently, we explored the mechanism behind this innovative result and identified SPIN4 as a downstream target of the HPV16 E7/APC2 axis. SPIN4, a spindle and kinetochore-associated protein, plays a crucial role in cellular health and disease [55], including cancer [56]. It is vital for mitotic spindle formation and chromosome segregation, ensuring genomic stability. Disruptions in SPIN4 function can lead to genomic instability, a key feature of cancer. Moreover, histone 3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) binding activity of SPIN4 suggests a unique role in gene expression regulation [57]. The recent study focusing on an X-linked overgrowth disorder that involves SPIN4 has provided evidence indicating that SPIN4 activates canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling [55]. Chang et al. reported that elevated expression of SPIN4 is associated with advanced nodal status and poorer prognosis in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma [56]. However, the role of SPIN4 in cervical cancer still lacks investigation. Our bioinformatics and experimental analyses demonstrated for the first time that the HPV16 E7/APC2 axis positively regulated SPIN4 expression, with higher SPIN4 expression correlating with poorer overall survival of cervical cancer. Furthermore, SPIN4 silencing could significantly reverse the effects of APC2 overexpression on proliferation, migration, invasion, stemness, and the Wnt pathway in cervical cancer cells. The in vivo tumorigenesis experiments directly confirmed that SPIN4 is required for efficient tumor growth and provided critical functional evidence supporting its role as a key downstream effector of the HPV16 E7/APC2 oncogenic axis to regulate the Wnt pathway in cervical cancer progression, at least partially. While our study provides insightful findings into the mechanisms underlying HPV16 E7-mediated cervical cancer progression, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, to fully appreciate the significance of this Wnt/β-catenin axis regulation, it must be viewed in the broader context of other signaling networks known to be influenced by HPV16 oncoprotein. Notably, extensive prior research has demonstrated HPV16 oncoprotein’s potent ability to activate the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, a well-characterized driver of cell proliferation, survival, and self-renewal, hallmarks of stemness [35, 58, 59]. This raises a critical question: What is the comparative contribution and functional relationship between the Wnt and PI3K/AKT pathways downstream of HPV16 oncoprotein? Future combinatorial targeting is needed to dissect their distinct and synergistic roles in regulating stemness, a finding with significant therapeutic implications. Additionally, our results highlight meaningful clinical implications, particularly in the realm of targeted therapy and biomarker development. The consistent upregulation of APC2 in HPV16-positive tumors and its association with early disease stage and poor prognosis position APC2 as a promising prognostic biomarker. Moreover, the functional demonstration that APC2 silencing inhibits tumor growth and CSC properties without significant systemic toxicity suggests that therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring APC2 function or targeting its downstream effectors (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin signaling) could offer novel avenues for treating HPV-driven cervical cancer, especially in cases resistant to conventional therapies. Finally, while our transcriptomic data (Fig. 2D) revealed numerous DEGs, APC2 was prioritized for validation due to its established role as a key regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a signaling axis critically involved in cancer stemness and progression. The significant downregulation of APC2 upon E7 silencing, coupled with its clinical relevance in cervical cancer prognosis, strongly supported its potential as a central mediator of E7-driven oncogenicity. Further investigation into the intricate molecular interactions within the HPV16 E7/APC2/SPIN4 axis, as well as the functional roles of other DEGs identified in our screening, will be an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals a novel mechanism by which HPV16 E7 promotes cervical cancer malignancy and CSC properties by interacting with the Wnt signaling pathway, involving APC2 and its downstream target SPIN4. This study deepens our understanding of cervical cancer development and highlights potential therapeutic targets for intervention strategies.
