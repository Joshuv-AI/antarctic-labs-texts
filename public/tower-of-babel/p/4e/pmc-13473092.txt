MAPK pathway inhibitors enhance radioiodine sensitivity in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma through promoting NIS expression and ARF4-mediated NIS membrane transport
Authors: Junfang Wang, Zhenzhen Zeng, Shasha Xu, Xiaoxian Wang, Keliu Wu, Yongguang Chang, Guanzheng Wang, Baoping Liu, Ruihua Wang, Xingmin Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) patient develops resistance to radioactive iodine (RAI) due to diminished sodium-iodide symporter (NIS) expression and mislocalization to the cell membrane. MAPK pathway inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing RAI avidity in radioiodine-refractory papillary thyroid carcinoma and ATC. Elucidating this mechanism may lead to novel therapeutic approaches for ATC treatment. The cytotoxic effects of three MAPK pathway inhibitors (selumetinib, vemurafenib, dabrafenib) were assessed in ATC cell lines and xenograft models via viability assays and 18F-FDG PET/CT. We investigated their impact on radioiodine metabolism by analyzing the expression of key genes (NIS, TSHR, and TPO) and trafficking regulators (ARF4, PIGU, and β-catenin) via Western blotting, and assessing NIS localization and function through immunofluorescence and uptake assays. The protein interaction between NIS and ARF4 was examined via co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and immunofluorescence. MAPK pathway inhibitors reduced the viability of ATC cell lines. ATC cells exhibited restored iodine metabolism-related gene expression after treatment, and pronounced increases in membrane-localized NIS were detected via the promotion of ARF4 expression. Furthermore, MAPK pathway inhibitors increased radioiodine uptake in ATC cells. The MAPK pathway inhibitors enhance NIS function through two mechanisms: upregulation of NIS expression and increased ARF4-mediated NIS membrane transport.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54570-5.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is a common endocrine malignancy. It is histopathologically classified into papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC), medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), and ATC1,2. Among these, ATC is the least differentiated and most aggressive subtype. It is characterized by rapid progression, high metastatic potential, and poor prognosis. Epidemiological data indicate that ATC has a median survival of approximately 9.4 months, and its dismal 2-year overall survival rate is only 25%, making it the most lethal form of thyroid cancer3. Radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy is a well-established treatment for differentiated thyroid cancers. However, its efficacy in ATC remains limited due to the loss of iodine avidity. Consequently, recent research has focused on strategies to restore RAI sensitivity by promoting redifferentiation in ATC.

The BRAFV600E mutation, the most prevalent genetic alteration in PTC, is also implicated in approximately 40–50% of ATC cases4. This mutation constitutively activates the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, leading to uncontrolled proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis5. Moreover, activation of the MAPK pathway downregulates critical iodine-metabolizing genes, including NIS, thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR), and thyroid peroxidase (TPO), through epigenetic silencing mechanisms. Specifically, the BRAFV600E gene mutation induces histone deacetylation at the NIS promoter, suppressing its transcriptional activity and impairing NIS expression6.

Recent studies have explored strategies to restore RAI avidity in poorly differentiated and anaplastic thyroid cancers, including epigenetic modulation, NIS gene delivery, and MAPK pathway inhibition, with promising preclinical results7. The use of MAPK pathway inhibitors has emerged as a prominent strategy for treating anaplastic thyroid cancer, with a primary focus on BRAF and MEK inhibitors8. An animal study demonstrated that inhibiting the MAPK pathway with MEK inhibitors alone reduced the tumor burden in mice with ATC in the short term. This was accompanied by the upregulation of NIS transcripts and significantly increased radioiodine uptake in the thyroid following treatment. The enhanced radioactivity subsequently improved therapeutic efficacy9. Another study revealed that selumetinib, a MEK inhibitor, restored radioiodine efficacy in patients with radioiodine-refractory thyroid cancer by promoting radioiodine uptake10. These findings indicate that targeting MEK has therapeutic potential in poorly differentiated and anaplastic thyroid cancers. Additionally, treatment with the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib in radioiodine-refractory thyroid cancer patients upregulated thyroid-specific gene expression and radioiodine uptake via suppression of the MAPK pathway. Another BRAF inhibitor, Dabrafenib, is also believed to facilitate redifferentiation in thyroid cancer11,12. However, the specific mechanism of ATC remains understudied.

Clinical studies have demonstrated that the BRAF-mutation in PTC exhibits diminished NIS expression and accurate membrane localization. Transient BRAF overexpression in thyroid cells significantly reduces both NIS levels and membrane targeting13. These findings suggest that BRAF-driven MAPK activation disrupts NIS functionality, contributing to RAI insensitivity in thyroid cancer. In previous studies, immunohistochemical analysis of patient samples revealed that approximately 70–80% of thyroid cancers express or even overexpress NIS. However, the expression is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm rather than at the plasma membrane. In terms of post translational regulation, the impaired function of NIS may not only result from reduced or absent expression but also from defective membrane targeting and insufficient retention of NIS in the membrane14,15. Notably, RAI resistance is not solely attributable to reduced NIS expression but may also result from defective NIS membrane trafficking and retention. Proper iodine uptake requires not only NIS expression but also its correct localization and stability at the plasma membrane. Critically, a recent study revealed that ADP-ribosylation factor 4 (ARF4) binds to NIS via its C-terminal VAPK motif, promoting NIS vesicular transport to the plasma membrane. The overexpression of ARF4 in PTC cells increased membrane-localized NIS, whereas vesicular transport inhibitors reduced NIS membrane trafficking16. ARF4 is a member of the Ras superfamily of small GTPases, localizes to the Golgi apparatus and regulates vesicular trafficking, facilitating protein transport to the plasma membrane17,18. Emerging evidence implicates ARF4 in tumor progression and therapy resistance. For instance, Budhiraja demonstrated that ARF4-mediated retrotranslocation of EGFR enhances glioblastoma resistance to temozolomide19, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. These findings suggest that ARF4 may play a key role in restoring RAI uptake in thyroid cancer.

In this study, we evaluated the effects of MAPK pathway inhibitors on ATC via cell proliferation assays. Western blotting was employed to detect the expression of NIS, TPO, TSHR, and PAX8, and iodine uptake assays were performed to assess whether MAPK pathway inhibitors could restore radioactive iodine avidity in ATC. Furthermore, by knocking down ARF4 and applying MAPK pathway inhibitors, we investigated the role of these inhibitors in the membrane trafficking of NIS. Our findings provide new insights into restoring RAI sensitivity in ATC.

Results

MAPK pathway inhibitors inhibit the proliferation of anaplastic thyroid cancer cells in vitro

To evaluate the anti-proliferative effects of MAPK pathway inhibitors on ATC cells, we treated OCUT-2C, C643, and HTh-7 cells with various concentrations of selumetinib, vemurafenib, and dabrafenib for 48 h. Then cell viability was assessed by using the CCK-8 assay, and the results revealed that all three drugs potently inhibited cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A–C). The IC50 values for each drug in the three cell lines are summarized in Table 1. Notably, all ATC cell lines exhibited high sensitivity to these inhibitors, and vemurafenib showed particularly strong growth-inhibitory efficacy even at low concentrations. The result of the CCK-8 cell proliferation assay also showed a significant reduction in the proliferative capacity of the cells treated with MAPK pathway inhibitors compared with that of the DMSO control (Fig. 1D–F). Consistent with the above results, assessment of cell proliferation using the EdU incorporation assay confirmed that treatment with MAPK pathway inhibitors markedly attenuated the proliferation of OCUT-2C, C643, and HTh-7 cells. The reduction in cell viability was more pronounced in OCUT-2C and C643 cells following selumetinib treatment (Figure S1A-D). MAPK pathway inhibitors suppress the growth of anaplastic thyroid cancer xenografts in nude mice.

To further evaluate the antitumor efficacy of MAPK pathway inhibitors in vivo, we established a nude mouse xenograft model using OCUT-2C cells. Once the subcutaneous tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): vehicle control, selumetinib treatment, vemurafenib treatment, and dabrafenib treatment. No significant drug-related toxicity or weight changes were observed throughout the study, indicating that the tumor-bearing mice were well tolerated. The mice were treated with the corresponding drugs for 2 to 3 weeks. Compared with the control group, all three drugs treatment groups exhibited significant antitumor effects (Fig. 2A, B). It was further confirmed by a notable reduction in both tumor volume and weight at the endpoint (Fig. 2C, D). To further assess the in vivo inhibitory effects of these three compounds on the growth of transplanted tumors, we performed 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging. Notably, the maximum standard uptake value (SUVmax) of the control group was also greater than that of the drug-treated groups (Fig. 2E). Therefore, after treating ATC cells with three inhibitors, we further isolated the cell membrane components and detected the expression of NIS on the cell membrane and the cell cytoplasm. To further explore the mechanism by which MAPK inhibitors influence NIS expression and localization, we examined key regulatory proteins. As expected, in anaplastic thyroid cancer cells (OCUT-2C, C643, and HTh-7) phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK), a downstream effector of the MAPK pathway, was significantly reduced after inhibitor treatment. The expression of ADP-ribosylation factor 4 (ARF4), which is related to the cell membrane transport of NIS16, was significantly upregulated. In contrast, the expression levels of two other proteins implicated in NIS transport, phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis class U (PIGU) and β-catenin, remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5C, D). These results suggest that MAPK pathway inhibitors may play a role in the membrane transport of NIS predominantly by activating ARF4 molecules rather than PIGU or β-catenin.

ARF4 specifically interacts with NIS in vitro

To clarify the role of ARF4 in NIS transport in ATC cells, we first performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay to detect whether there was specific binding between ARF4 and NIS. The results demonstrated a direct interaction between ARF4 and NIS in both the OCUT-2C and C643 cell lines (Fig. 6A, B). To further investigate whether ARF4 is involved in the cell membrane localization of NIS, we evaluated the subcellular distributions of ARF4 and NIS via an immunofluorescence assay. As expected, we observed significant colocalization between ARF4 (red signal) and NIS (green signal) in OCUT-2C and C643 cells, represented by yellow signal overlap (Fig. 6C). MAPK pathway inhibitors regulate NIS membrane translocation via ARF4

Our previous results suggested that the MAPKi-mediated upregulation of membrane NIS might be due to the upregulation of ARF4. To test this hypothesis, we transfected ARF4 siRNA into OCUT-2C, C643, and HTh-7 cells, and assessed the membrane and total NIS protein levels. Indeed, ARF4 knockdown led to a marked decrease in membrane-localized NIS, but resulted in a modest reduction in total NIS protein levels. This disproportionate reduction in membrane NIS compared with total NIS suggests that ARF4 facilitates NIS trafficking to the plasma membrane. ARF4-knockdown cells were subsequently treated with MAPK pathway inhibitors. While inhibitor treatment increased the cell membrane NIS in these cells, the magnitude of this increase was significantly attenuated compared to inhibitor-treated cells with intact ARF4 expression (Fig. 7A–B). The total NIS levels exhibited minimal changes under these conditions. Meantime, in three anaplastic thyroid cancer cell lines, knockdown of ARF4 significantly reduced iodine uptake. However, treatment with vemurafenib in ARF4-knockdown cells restored iodine uptake function（Fig. 7C）. Discussion

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway plays a critical role in essential cellular functions such as differentiation, proliferation, survival, autophagy, and apoptosis. Activation at any step within the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK cascade can lead to downstream signaling and oncogenesis. The BRAFV600E mutation is one of the most important and frequent genetic alterations in thyroid cancer, and the presence of the BRAF mutation is associated with more aggressive tumor features, including extrathyroidal extension, advanced stage at presentation, and metastasis in ATC2045. Targeting the MAPK pathway has become a highly regarded therapeutic strategy for ATC, and several inhibitors are under active investigation. Key agents in this class include the BRAFV600E inhibitor and the MEK inhibitor, which have been prominently featured in both clinical and preclinical studies in recent years.

MEK inhibitors specifically suppress MAPK signaling, inhibit growth and promote apoptosis in PTC and ATC cells21,22. BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib significantly inhibits the migration and invasion of anaplastic thyroid cancer cells while also inducing apoptosis23. Previous studies have shown that MAPK pathway inhibitors can significantly increase radioiodine sensitivity in ATC in both in vivo and in vitro models10–12. Although reduced radioiodine sensitivity is largely due to dysregulated expression of iodine metabolism genes and mislocalization of NIS, and prior work has focused on the transcriptional upregulation of NIS as the primary means to restore avidity2446, Nagarajah et al., we demonstrate that MAPK pathway inhibitors can increase radioiodine sensitivity through an alternative mechanism—by facilitating the correct membrane localization of NIS at the posttranslational level.

In this study, we focused on investigating the mechanism of action of three MAPK pathway inhibitors—selumetinib, vemurafenib, and dabrafenib—in ATC. We used three human ATC cell lines: OCUT-2C (harboring the BRAFV600E mutation), C643 (harboring the HRAS mutation), and HTh-7 (harboring the NRAS mutation). We found that all three inhibitors effectively reduced ATC cell survival, primarily by suppressing proliferative activity. Consistent with our results, Camilla et al. reported that under 3D culture conditions mimicking the tumor microenvironment, MAPK pathway inhibition affects ATC cell migration and invasion25. More notably, at concentrations equivalent to one-third of the IC₅₀ (which ensures comparable cell survival rates), selumetinib inhibited cell proliferation more potently than the other compounds tested. Furthermore, through in vivo experiments, we confirmed that MAPK pathway inhibitors suppress the growth of ATC xenografts. Consistent with the in vitro proliferation results, all three drugs effectively inhibited tumor growth in nude mice, with selumetinib showing the strongest effect. Further evaluation with 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated a reduction in both tumor volume and metabolic activity in the experimental groups, as indicated by significantly decreased 18F-FDG uptake. Increased glucose uptake in cancer is often due to elevated expression and activity of hexokinase-2 (HK2) and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1)26,27. Previous studies have indicated a link between BRAFV600E mutation and these downstream targets in thyroid cancer, and the BRAFV600E blockade leads to the downregulation of glucose uptake and metabolism28–30. The administration of MAPK pathway inhibitors was shown to attenuate ATC tumor growth, reduce cellular proliferation, and diminish metabolic activity both in vivo and in vitro.

Previous studies have indicated that hyperactivation of the MAPK pathway leads to dysregulation of iodine metabolism-related gene expression. Pharmacological inhibition of MAPK overactivation has been shown to restore the expression of genes involved in iodine metabolism, such as NIS, thereby enhancing radioiodine avidity in thyroid cancer31–33. A unique feature of thyroid follicular cells is their ability to capture and concentrate iodine, which depends on the expression of NIS, TPO, TSHR, and PAX87. NIS mediates iodine uptake at the plasma membrane, whereas TSHR promotes iodine organification and retention within thyroid follicles. Previous reports have shown that drugs such as the estrogen receptor agonist DN200434 or tunicamycin can restore NIS expression and radioiodine uptake, whereas blocking NIS function with KClO₄ markedly inhibits iodide uptake34,35. These findings suggest that the restoration of radioiodine avidity and iodine metabolism gene expression are closely related to normal NIS function. The nuclear transcription factor PAX8 binds to the NIS promoter and regulates its transcription36. We sought to further investigate the role of MAPK pathway inhibitors in promoting redifferentiation and increasing radioiodine sensitivity in ATC. To explore their potential in restoring iodine metabolism gene expression and radioiodine avidity, we evaluated the effects of selumetinib, vemurafenib, and dabrafenib on ATC cells. Treatment with these drugs restored the expression of NIS, TPO, and TSHR in all three ATC cell lines. However, PAX8 expression was restored only in BRAF-mutated OCUT-2C cells. Previous studies have indicated that restoring PAX8 expression can rescue NIS expression suppressed by BRAF mutation37, which is consistent with our data. Interestingly, in the other two cell lines with different mutations, although PAX8 was not restored, NIS and other iodine metabolism genes were upregulated, and iodide uptake function was recovered. The differential effects of selumetinib and BRAF inhibitors on iodide uptake in OCUT-2C cells likely reflect concurrent activation of BRAF V600E and PI3K signaling. AKT regulates iodide uptake mainly by controlling NIS membrane function rather than expression. Sustained AKT activation promotes NIS internalization and disrupts membrane retention via effects on cytoskeletal organization and epithelial polarity38. In contrast, BRAF inhibitors may reduce MAPK signaling and limit compensatory PI3K/AKT activation, and affect NIS membrane function39. Thus, the inability of selumetinib to restore iodide uptake likely reflects its limited effect on AKT-dependent NIS trafficking rather than NIS expression levels.

Previous studies have suggested that radioiodine sensitivity is associated primarily with the expression of iodine metabolism-related genes, and that enhancing the recovery of genes such as NIS is considered a key approach for increasing radioiodine sensitivity34,35,40. However, recent research has increasingly highlighted the importance of NIS membrane localization in determining radioiodine sensitivity. Several experiments have demonstrated that promoting NIS translocation to the plasma membrane through pharmaceutical interventions or activation of specific signaling pathways can reduce the malignancy of thyroid cancer and significantly enhance radioiodine uptake38,41; however, the effect of such inhibition on the functional transport mechanism of NIS remains unclear. We observed that MAPK pathway inhibition significantly increased the level of NIS localized at the plasma membrane. We examined several molecules(ARF4, PIGU and β-catenin) known to influence NIS membrane trafficking42. ARF4 has been shown to promote the directional transport of NIS-containing vesicles to the plasma membrane, thereby increasing membrane NIS levels and radioiodine uptake in PTC16. Amit et al. reported that PIGU, which is involved in protein anchoring, also affects NIS trafficking. Downregulation of PIGU inhibited NIS membrane localization and reduced radioiodine avidity in PTC, whereas PIGU overexpression increased membrane NIS more prominently than total NIS43. Another study by Ling Lan demonstrated that β-catenin activation alters NIS subcellular localization and impairs radioiodine uptake, whereas β-catenin knockdown restores both NIS localization and iodide uptake, indicating its negative regulatory role44. In our experiments, MAPK pathway inhibitor treatment significantly increased ARF4 expression, but did not markedly alter PIGU or β-catenin levels. These findings suggests that MAPK inhibitors may regulate NIS membrane trafficking primarily through ARF4. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that MAPK pathway inhibitors might regulate the membrane trafficking of NIS by influencing the expression of ARF4. To investigate whether a functional interaction exists between NIS and ARF4, we performed co-IP and protein colocalization assays, which confirmed a direct binding relationship between the two proteins. These findings lay the groundwork for further mechanistic exploration. To further validate our hypothesis, we knocked down ARF4 and observed that, as expected, the abundance of NIS on the cell membrane was significantly reduced. Correspondingly, upregulation of ARF4 protein expression via MAPK inhibitor treatment resulted in a concomitant recovery of NIS localization at the plasma membrane. Collectively, our data suggest a model in which MAPK pathway inhibitors enhance NIS plasma membrane localization—and thereby radioiodine avidity in ATC-by upregulating ARF4 expression and promoting ARF4-mediated NIS trafficking. This finding offers a new perspective for improving radioiodine uptake in ATC: modulating molecules that influence NIS membrane targeting may further increase radioiodine sensitivity, potentially providing new therapeutic approaches.

Although we have shown that the MAPK pathway inhibitor can increase both NIS expression and ARF4-mediated NIS membrane trafficking, thereby improving radioiodine avidity in ATC, the precise role and regulatory mechanisms of ARF4 in thyroid cancer remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies should explore the molecular mechanism of ARF4 in thyroid cancer in greater depth.

In summary, our study demonstrated that MAPK pathway inhibitors enhance radioiodine avidity in anaplastic thyroid cancer not only by upregulating iodine metabolism gene expression but also by promoting ARF4 expression and subsequent NIS plasma membrane localization. These findings further clarify the mechanism of action of MAPK-targeting drugs and highlight the role of ARF4 in iodine transport.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and drug treatment

OCUT-2C, C643, and HTh-7 anaplastic thyroid carcinoma cell lines were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). C643 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Clark Biosciences, Australia) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, China). OCUT-2C and HTh-7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with an equivalent amount of medium. All the cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was performed to authenticate all cell lines, and no cross-contamination was detected. The STR authentication reports and corresponding Research Resource Identifiers (RRIDs) are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

MAPK pathway inhibitors (selumetinib, vemurafenib, and dabrafenib; MedChemExpress, USA) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Solarbio, China) for in vitro and in vivo applications. Stock solutions (10 mmol/L) were stored at -20 °C and diluted in culture media immediately before use.

Cytotoxicity and 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assays

Cell viability was evaluated via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan). The cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3,000 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. The treatments included: serial dilutions of the inhibitors (20, 50, 100, or 200 μmol/L), and a vehicle control (0.1% DMSO). The mixture was incubated for 24, 48, or 72 h. CCK-8 reagent (100 μL/well) was added at the endpoint, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo 488 Kit (Beyotime, China). After drug treatment, the cells were incubated with 10 μmol/L EdU for 2 h at 37 °C. The subsequent steps followed the manufacturer’s protocols: fixation: 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min, RT); permeabilization: 0.3% Triton X-100 (20 min, RT); Apollo® reaction (30 min, dark); and nuclear counterstaining: Hoechst 33,342 (5 min); Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blot

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight. Drug treatments were administered in complete medium for 48 h. The cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, China). Protein concentrations were determined via a BCA assay (Solarbio, China). Equal protein amounts (20 μg) were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After being blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Detailed information for all primary antibodies, including their corresponding Research Resource Identifiers (RRIDs), is provided in Table S1. Following three 10-min washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via the Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, USA) with ECL Prime detection reagent (Cytiva).

Immunofluorescence staining

The cells were grown on a confocal dish and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After three 5-min washes with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum (Solarbio, China) for 1 h. Primary antibodies (Table S1) were applied overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Following three washes with PBS, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) were applied for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL; Beyotime, China) for 5 min. Images were acquired using a ZEISS fluorescence microscope with LAS X software (Leica Microsystems). Due to the use of high magnification, each field contained a limited number of cells; therefore, multiple fields were analyzed to ensure sufficient sampling. In total, approximately 30–50 cells were included per condition across independent fields.

In vitro radioiodine uptake assay

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates (4 × 105 cells/well) and incubated overnight. Following 48 h of treatment with 10 μM selumetinib, vemurafenib, or dabrafenib, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 2 mL of serum-free medium containing 2 μCi Na131I for 60 min at 37 °C. Reference standards (0.2 μCI Na131I in 200 μL of serum-free medium were prepared in triplicate. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and lysed with 500 μL of 0.3 M NaOH. The lysates were transferred to a plastic tube, and the radioactivity of the cells and the reference tube was determined using a γ-counter (CAPINTEC CAPRAC-t, USA). The normalized RAI uptake was then expressed as a percentage of the total added radioactivity, according to the following formulas: Normalized RAI uptake (CPM/mg protein) = (Sample CPM-Background CPM)/Protein concentration(mg); RAI uptake rate (%) = Normalized RAI uptake (CPM/mg protein)/Total added RAI CPM × 100%.

Subcutaneous xenograft model

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Life Science Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. ZZU-LAC20250418-01) and were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines and were reported following the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Tumor volume was measured every three days using digital calipers and calculated using the following formula: (length × width2)/2. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group). Selumetinib, vemurafenib, and dabrafenib were prepared in a vehicle containing 40% PEG 300 (Solarbio, China), 5% Tween 80 (Solarbio, China), 53% saline, and 2% drug stock solution. Control mice received vehicle containing 2% DMSO instead of the drug. Drugs were administered by oral gavage twice daily, five days per week, for 2–3 weeks. At the experimental endpoint, mice had body weights ranging from 16 to 23 g. The mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital and euthanized by cervical dislocation prior to tumor harvesting. Tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for further analyses.

18F-FDG PET/CT imaging and analysis

18F-FDG PET/CT imaging was performed using a Siemens Inveon micro-PET/CT system (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). The radiochemical purity of the 1⁸F-FDG exceeded 95% (tested via radio-TLC). Mice (n = 5 per group) were fasted for 6 h before intravenous tail vein injection of 5.5 MBq (150 μCi) 1⁸F-FDG. After 60-min of uptake under isoflurane anesthesia (1.5% in 100% O₂), continuous anesthesia was maintained during imaging. PET acquisition lasted 10 min, followed by low-dose CT (80 kVp, 500 μA) for attenuation correction. Images were reconstructed using Inveon Acquisition Workplace (IAW v2.0) with OSEM3D/MAP algorithm (2 iterations, 18 subsets). The tumor SUVmax was calculated as: SUVmax= [tissue activity concentration (kBq/mL) × body weight (g)] / injected dose (kBq).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in GraphPad Prism. Due to the relatively small sample size, the normality test has limited statistical power; however, the data were considered approximately normally distributed. Statistical significance among multiple groups was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
