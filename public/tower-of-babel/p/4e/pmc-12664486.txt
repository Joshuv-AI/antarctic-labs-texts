Machine learning-based predictive model for atrial arrhythmia following transcatheter atrial septal defect closure
Authors: Xander Jacquemyn, Alexander Van De Bruaene, Joris Ector, Peter Haemers, Pieter De Meester, Cedric Manlhiot, Werner Budts, Bert Vandenberk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial septal defects (ASDs) are frequently closed percutaneously. Despite successful closure, many patients still develop atrial arrhythmias. There is inconsistent data on the risk factors associated with these atrial arrhythmias. As such, we aimed to develop a machine learning (ML) model predicting atrial arrhythmias following transcatheter ASD closure in adolescents and adults.MethodsPatients with secundum-type ASDs undergoing transcatheter closure between 2008 and 2024 at a single center were retrospectively analyzed. Patients with prior atrial arrhythmias were excluded. A deep neural network, adapted via transfer learning from a large external ECG dataset, was used to extract predictive features from preprocedural 12-lead ECGs. These features were combined with clinical, demographic, biochemical and hemodynamic variables in ensemble survival models. Model performance was assessed using the integrated Brier scores and the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC).ResultsA total of 148 adult patients (median 44.4 years [30.6–57.8], 105 females [70.9 %]) were eligible for included. There were a total of 1055 person-years of follow-up (median follow-up 7.3 [3.1–11.3]), during which 28 patients (18.9 %) developed atrial arrhythmias. The final ensemble ML model incorporating ECG-derived features demonstrated strong predictive performance (integrated Brier score 0.044, mean AUC 0.823). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the model across various patient profiles.ConclusionsWe developed a novel ML-based risk model using a transfer learning approach to predict atrial arrhythmias after transcatheter ASD closure. Further research and external validation are needed to refine the proposed risk stratification prior to clinical implementation.Keywords: Atrial septal defect, Atrial arrhythmias, Machine learning, Artificial intelligence, Prediction model, Congenital heart disease

Introduction

Atrial septal defect (ASD) are among the most common congenital heart defects, occurring in approximately 1.43 per 1000 live births, and, due to advances in diagnosis and treatment, now associated with an anticipated 97 % likelihood of survival into adulthood [1,2]. During childhood and adolescence, these ASDs often go unrecognized due to their typically asymptomatic nature. In some cases, they are identified incidentally during imaging performed for unrelated reasons. However, in a subset of patients, particularly older individuals, atrial arrhythmias may be the initial presenting symptom, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality among those with congenital heart disease (CHD) [3].

From a physiological perspective, certain ASDs can lead to a hemodynamically significant shunt resulting in a volume overload in the right side of the circulatory system, including the right atrium (RA), right ventricle (RV), and pulmonary vasculature. To prevent further right-sided deterioration, including volume and/or pressure overload, and the potential progression to subsequent heart failure, closure of the ASD may be required [4,5]. Past experiences from surgical closure of ASDs in childhood has consistently resulted in excellent long-term outcomes, marked by a low incidence of late atrial arrhythmias [6]. However, adults undergoing surgical ASD repair are at a notable risk of developing atrial arrhythmias during long-term follow-up [7]. The introduction of novel catheter-based percutaneous closure devices for ASDs have yielded outcomes consistent with previous surgical experiences, demonstrating that individuals at older ages exhibit increasing risk of late atrial arrhythmias [8]. Another interesting observation is that ASD closure does not reduce the occurrence of atrial arrhythmias [8], highlighting that patients with and without arrhythmias prior to closure may represent distinct, heterogeneous phenotypes. In essence, the "ideal" ASD treatment should address both the elimination of the intracardiac shunt, and the reversal of geometric changes induced by cardiac overload [9]. Furthermore, closure of an ASD-based left-to-right shunt not only induces geometrical but also electrical remodeling of the RA and RV [10]. These aforementioned changes may contribute to the development of atrial arrhythmias, making the assessment of this risk pivotal for effective clinical management. It not only holds valuable prognostic information for patients but also identifies high-risk individuals who may benefit from more intensive follow-up or prophylactic treatment.

Historically, conventional statistical models have been formulated to predict these outcomes, demonstrating respectable predictive accuracy, however, they are not integrated into clinical practice [11]. Machine learning (ML) models, such as deep neural networks (DNN), present the potential for superior predictive performance compared to these conventional models [12]. Although machine learning risk scores are widely utilized across various cardiology domains, including the prediction of outcomes in heart failure patients or coronary artery disease, there is currently no existing ML tool specifically tailored to predict late atrial arrhythmias following ASD repair in adolescents and adults [13]. The development of such a tool has the potential to refine prognostic precision, ultimately optimizing clinical care for individuals undergoing ASD repair in the later stages of life. As such, in this work we aimed to develop a ML algorithm capable of predicting new-onset atrial arrhythmias in patients undergoing percutaneous ASD closure.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective, single-center study evaluated all adult patients with secundum-type ASD referred for closure at our institution between 2008 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were patients with a secundum-type ASD referred for transcatheter closure, a minimum of 6 months of follow-up at our institution, and availability of baseline demographic, clinical, biochemical data, and a preprocedural 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 18 years, surgical closure of the atrial septal defect, the presence of associated CHD, and a history of atrial arrhythmias. Additionally, patients exhibiting any atrial arrhythmia on the preprocedural 12-lead ECG were excluded for the primary analysis. Follow-up evaluations for each patient included clinical assessment, transthoracic echocardiography, and a standard 12-lead ECG. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement: guidelines for reporting observational studies and TRIPOD + AI statement for the development of clinical prediction models (Supplemental Table 1) [14,15]. Approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee at KU Leuven (reference number: MP030877), in compliance with the ICH-GCP guidelines (International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice), the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all relevant laws and regulations. Because of the retrospective nature of the study, informed patient consent was waived. A full in-depth description of the methods is provided in the supplemental methods section.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of any atrial arrhythmia during follow-up. Atrial arrhythmia was defined as atrial flutter or atrial fibrillation documented during routine follow-up, as identified by a standard 12-lead electrocardiogram or, when clinically indicated, ambulatory Holter monitoring.

Data preprocessing

Data was collated in a standardized data collection form. Variables with more than 50 % of missing data were removed and not considered for analysis. Low-variance numerical features (standard deviation < 0.2) and categorical features with only one unique value were removed. Missing data was handled using a simple imputer for categorical variables and an iterative imputer with decision tree regressor for continuous variables. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded. Features were standardized using z-score normalization. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and features with VIF >10, were removed. For the ECG analysis, raw 12-lead ECG signals were extracted from structured files at an original sampling frequency of 250 Hz. Each lead was trimmed or zero-padded to a fixed length and subsequently resampled to 4096 time points at a target frequency of 400 Hz to ensure uniform input dimensions for the DNN.

Algorithm development

Deep neural network architecture and training

For ECG classification and risk prediction of the primary outcome, we employed a pre-trained “ResNet1d” model developed on the Clinical Outcomes in Digital Electrocardiology (CODE) dataset, comprising over 2.3 million 12-lead ECGs from 1.5 million patients in Brazil collected between 2010 and 2017 [16]. Dataset details are described elsewhere [17]. The model was originally developed to classify three categories: absence of AF, presence of current AF, and risk of future AF, expressed as three probabilities [16,18]. In brief, the model architecture consists of an initial convolutional layer, followed by five residual blocks with 2 convolutional layers per block, and this output is fed into a fully connected (dense) layer with a sigmoid activation function, σ, whose output is passed through a softmax function and provides probabilities for the three classes. A more detailed description of the DNN architecture and training is provided in the Supplemental Methods. We adapted the model through a transfer learning approach, freezing all layers except the final residual block and output layer, which then provides probabilities for future atrial arrhythmia (Fig. 1). The cohort was divided into training and validation sets in a 70:30 ratio using stratified sampling to preserve class distribution.

Predictive feature importance

The predictive features eligible for inclusion in the final models encompassed demographic, clinical, and biochemical data, invasive hemodynamic measurements, and the probabilities derived from the transfer model based on the 12-lead ECG. A detailed list of the 63 eligible variables, including their data type, mean or median, and range, is presented in Supplemental Table 2. Feature importance was assessed using a permutation-based Random Survival Forest (RSF) approach with stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Shadow features were generated by shuffling the values of each feature column to create random “noise” [19]. Feature importances were averaged across folds and compared to shadow features using Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Across 50 trials, a binomial probability mass function (PMF) estimated the chance of random selection, and features were categorized as highly, moderately, or not important based on selection frequency.

Ensemble model structure

Fig. 1 presents the structure of the ensemble model. Three distinct model types were assessed for the survival analysis: (1) a Cox proportional hazards model with Elastic Net regularization (CoxNet), combining L1 and L2 penalties; (2) a RSF; and (3) a Gradient Boosting (GB) Survival Model. All models were trained and evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Hyperparameter tuning was performed through grid search, and the complete set of candidate hyperparameters is detailed in Supplemental Table 3.

Model evaluation and metrics

The Brier score was used to evaluate predictive performance, alongside the integrated Brier Score (IBS) calculated across the follow-up period. The cumulative dynamic area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and mean AUC was also evaluated. Model calibration was assessed using D-calibration, comparing predicted versus observed event rates. Models were then evaluated at the censoring time, and a prediction threshold was set using logistic regression (LR), with further model evaluation using standard metrics: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, false positive rate, false negative rate, and F1-score.

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the transfer learning ECG model alone. Final performance was also evaluated across patient subgroups (e.g., sex, age groups) using the DeLong test. A final sensitivity analyses including patients with prior SVT, which were excluded for model development, was performed.

Statistical analysis

Time-to-event analyses, using the date of transcatheter closure as baseline, were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method. Associations with the primary endpoint were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. Patients were right-censored at their last clinic visit. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.0) and Python (version 3.11.3). The code repository for this study is publicly available (https://github.com/xjacquemyn/ML4AApostASD).

Results

Patient population

A total of 247 patients were initially deemed eligible after excluding 1626 individuals for various reasons (Fig. 2). Among these, 71 patients were excluded due to limited follow-up, and an additional 28 were excluded because of a prior history of atrial arrhythmias. Consequently, the final study cohort comprised 148 patients (median age at transcatheter closure: 44.4 years [30.6–57.8], 105 females [70.9 %]), with a median follow-up duration of 7.3 years [3.1–11.3]. Over the course of follow-up, 28 patients (18.9 %) experienced an atrial arrhythmia (median time-to-event of 0.7 years [0.1–6.1]). Within this group, 7 cases occurred within the first month post-ASD closure. Among the arrhythmia subtypes, 18 patients developed AF, 8 developed atrial flutter, and 2 patients were diagnosed with a further unspecified supraventricular tachycardia (SVT). Predictive feature importance

Although many variables demonstrated significant associations with the primary outcome in conventional exploratory Cox regression analyses (Supplemental Table 5), their overall predictive contributions were limited. Feature importance analysis revealed that the probability estimates from the transfer learning ECG model were the most predictive of atrial arrhythmia occurrence (Fig. 3A). This was followed by two biochemical markers obtained at the time of transcatheter ASD closure, prothrombin time and serum sodium. In contrast, demographic characteristics, other clinical and biochemical variables, as well as invasive hemodynamic measurements, were not able to accurately predict atrial arrhythmia risk. A detailed summary of the number of times each variable ranked higher than its corresponding shadow feature is provided in Supplemental Table 6. The ECG-derived probabilities also demonstrated the highest permutation importance (Fig. 3B).

Model evaluation

In five-fold cross validation, the GB model demonstrated the lowest prediction error (IBS = 0.044), followed by CoxNet (IBS = 0.055) and RSF (IBS = 0.072). The mean C-index was highest for RSF (0.935 ± 0.027), followed by CoxNet (0.915 ± 0.063) and GB (0.881 ± 0.059). The cumulative dynamic AUC and time-dependent Brier scores are visually represented in Supplemental Fig. 1. All models demonstrated good calibration between predicted cumulative risk estimates and observed event rates (Supplemental Fig. 2). Given its superior IBS score, the GB model was selected as the final model. The predicted compared to the actual freedom from the occurrence of atrial arrhythmias is presented in Fig. 4A. Model performance metrics at the time of censoring or events are reported in Table 2 with their corresponding AUC in the entire population (Fig. 4B). We evaluated model performance metrics using three potential cut-off points: the first to maximize the F1-score, a second threshold to match the prevalence of atrial arrhythmias in predicted and observed samples, and lastly a threshold to maximize sensitivity and specificity (Table 2).

Discussion

In this work, we developed an effective ML algorithm capable of accurately predicting new-onset atrial arrhythmias in patients undergoing percutaneous ASD closure. Our final model, incorporating only three variables demonstrated high specificity alongside strong sensitivity and was predominantly driven by ECGs. In a hypothetical scenario with equal pre-test probabilities, our model meaningfully shifts these to clinically relevant post-test probabilities, highlighting its practical value in decision-making (Supplemental Fig. 6).

The integration of machine learning–based information management holds significant potential to enhance the lifelong care of patients with ASDs. For instance, previous studies have demonstrated the utility of ML approaches in aiding the initial diagnosis, such as through cardiac auscultation, chest radiographs and ECGs [[20], [21], [22], [23]], while our study extends this work further along the clinical trajectory by accurately predicting new-onset atrial arrhythmias. Interestingly, our study is not the first to investigate the prediction of this outcome using ML, as prior research has demonstrated the feasibility of such prediction in a pediatric cohort [24]. The development of such a risk stratifying tool is important, as the majority of patients undergoing these interventions will remain asymptomatic and free of complications throughout long-term follow-up [25]. On the contrary, previous studies, along with our own findings, have shown that late atrial arrhythmias are relatively common, affecting up to one in four patients [11]. Over the past decade, several risk factors for late atrial arrhythmias have been proposed. Among these, older age (>50–60 years of age) has emerged as the most consistently reported and reproducible predictor [11,25]. Additional studies have also highlighted associations with metabolic syndrome, elevated pulmonary pressures, and male sex [11]. Our exploratory analysis also observed increasing risk with age, systolic blood - and pulmonary pressures, potentially reflecting the increased risk of AF associated with subclinical diastolic dysfunction or pulmonary vascular remodeling [26].

Using a novel machine learning framework incorporating time-to-event analysis of feature importances, our findings indicate that ECG-derived data are the most predictive of new-onset atrial arrhythmias. Notably, the transfer learning model alone achieved a dynamic AUC approaching 0.90, which highlights the substantial predictive value embedded within ECG signals. This observation aligns with previous large-scale studies in the general population, which have consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of ECG-based models in accurately predicting incident atrial fibrillation [27].

In comparison to previous traditional risk prediction models, our model outperforms the latter significantly. Although the internal decision-making processes of our "black box" ECG-based model remain hidden, this parallels the current limitations in understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the development of new-onset atrial arrhythmias in these patients. However, many studies have suggested that these complications are a consequence of residual atrial structural and electrophysiological disturbances, even despite defect closure [28]. Several potential explanatory factors have been proposed, including P-wave amplitude, P-wave duration, and P-wave dispersion, markers that may reflect right atrial dilation [10,29]. Ventricular predictors have also been suggested, including QTc interval prolongation, which may occur secondary to right ventricular volume overload in patients with ASD [30]. More recently, advanced analyses incorporating vectorcardiographic variables, such as the ventricular gradient, potentially reflecting RV pressure overload [31], and the spatial QRS-T angle have also been proposed [10]. Following ASD closure, atrial geometrical and electrical reverse remodeling are evident and occur as early as within 24 h [10]. Interestingly, several of the aforementioned variables, such as P-wave dispersion or PQ interval duration, remain unchanged during follow-up in certain patients [32]. For example, P-wave dispersion has been shown to correlate with persistent right atrial enlargement following ASD closure [32], while another study reported that PQ interval duration remained stable over time in patients who subsequently developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation [10]. These findings may partly explain the previously highlighted phenomenon that ASD closure does not uniformly reduce the incidence of atrial arrhythmias [8], further suggesting that patients who develop such arrhythmias may actually constitute distinct and heterogeneous phenotypic subgroups, potentially characterized by distinct patterns of electromechanical remodeling in response to the ASD-associated hemodynamic burden.

In addition to the ECG-derived data, serum sodium and prothrombin time were identified as potential predictors of new-onset atrial arrhythmias. The significance of sodium in arrhythmogenesis may initially seem counterintuitive, yet hyponatremia has been linked to AF in patients with heart failure, with lower serum sodium levels observed in paroxysmal compared to permanent AF [33,34]. Three potential mechanisms may explain the observed association between hyponatremia and increased risk of AF. First, hyponatremia often reflects neurohormonal activation, including increased activity of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and antidiuretic hormone [35]. This leads to fluid retention and may be secondary to underlying comorbidities such as congestive heart failure, which in the context of patients with ASDs may develop due to chronic volume overload. Second, hemodynamic stress resulting from hypervolemia can lead to increased atrial pressure and stretch, which in turn promotes electrical instability [36]. Mechanoelectrical feedback is a well-established contributor to arrhythmogenesis, especially within the pulmonary veins [37]. Finally, serum sodium itself may directly influence atrial electrophysiology. Preclinical studies have shown that low sodium levels can increase triggered activity and burst firing in the pulmonary veins, without affecting the sinoatrial node [38]. Additionally, sodium may interfere with atrioventricular nodal function, potentially diminishing its inhibitory effect on accessory pathways [39]. The role of hypernatremia is even less well understood, though it has been proposed as a marker of poor prognosis [40]. In our cohort, a U-shaped association was observed, with elevated risk of atrial arrhythmias at both low and high extremes of serum sodium concentrations (Supplemental Fig. 7). Secondly, to the best of our knowledge, the role of prothrombin time as a predictor for the occurrence of atrial arrhythmias remains incompletely understood. While prolongation is expected in patients with prior SVT receiving vitamin K antagonists [41], these patients were excluded in our primary analysis. Prior historic studies have shown that AF promotes platelet activation and coagulation, with chronic AF associated with elevated levels of thrombin, D-dimer, antithrombin III complex, and other coagulation factors [42,43]. However, it remains unclear whether these coagulation abnormalities are attributable to AF itself or its predisposing pathology. Additionally, paroxysmal AF lasting >12 h, prothrombin time was significantly shortened, but not in shorter episodes, indicating a time-dependent relationship with coagulation factors [42]. The pathophysiology of hypercoagulability in AF is associated with abnormal atrial wall motion, endothelial injury, and increased platelet aggregation, which activate the extrinsic coagulation cascade and consequently shorten prothrombin time [44]. However, the proposed pathophysiology does not explain why prothrombin time would qualify as a predictor for subsequent atrial arrhythmias, and exploratory analyses in our cohort fail to reveal any clear associations. (Supplemental Fig. 8). As such, both prothrombin time and serum sodium may as well reflect broader physiologic or systemic conditions rather than true direct causal links towards the development of atrial arrhythmias.

Limitations

First, the retrospective design introduces inherent biases. Second, the relatively small sample size and single-center nature of our study, although well-characterized, may not fully capture the global diversity of patients with ASDs undergoing transcatheter closure. In line with the aforementioned, the most significant limitation is the absence of an external validation cohort, however, we have tried to limit overfitting through the use of cross-fold validation. Additionally, the primary outcome was based on routine follow-up, which may have led to the underreporting of asymptomatic atrial arrhythmias. Then, the "black box" nature of our model limits the ability to evaluate the underlying mechanistic reasoning behind the identified risk factors. Finally, while preprocedural echocardiographic variables have demonstrated incremental predictive value, only a small subset (35 %) of our cohort had available echocardiographic images for Echocardiography Core Lab evaluation, and as such we opted to only include ECG-derived data.

Conclusion

A prognostic risk model based on preprocedural data was developed to predict arrhythmia-free survival after transcatheter ASD closure. The integration of a transfer-learning model using a preprocedural 12-lead ECG demonstrated strong predictive performance. The model demonstrated generalizability across different subgroups and exhibited high predictive accuracy in patients with a history of atrial arrhythmias. Clinical implementation of this tool could aid in individualizing patient care through by enhancing preoperative risk communication and optimizing follow-up strategies. Further research and external validation are needed to refine the proposed risk stratification.
