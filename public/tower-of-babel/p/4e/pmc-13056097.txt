R-loop disruption triggers irreversible topoisomerase 1–DNA cleavage complex formation during AID-dependent IgH gene diversification
Authors: Maki Kobayashi, Xi Chen, Tasuku Honjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceClass switch recombination (CSR) and somatic hypermutation are essential for antibody diversity, dependent on activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID). Cytidine-to-uridine (C-to-U) editing by AID creates an irreversible topoisomerase 1 (TOP1)–DNA cleavage complex (TOP1-CC); however, the underlying mechanism has not previously been identified. We have demonstrated that disrupting R-loops within the DNA cleavage region by antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or RNase H results in TOP1-CC formation, even without AID. Our findings reveal R-loop degradation as a critical, previously unrecognized intermediary step between C-to-U editing and DNA cleavage. We propose that C-to-U editing triggers DNA cleavage by introducing a G:U mismatch, which destabilizes the R-loop, rendering it susceptible to nuclear RNases. This work unveils an AID-dependent mechanism that initiates CSR through the R-loop destabilization.

Acquired immunity is supported by two types of gene recombination–based mechanisms in mammals: class switch recombination (CSR), which switches antibodies from IgM to IgG, IgE, or IgA to provide diverse effector functions, and somatic hypermutation (SHM) in the variable (V) region, which increases the affinity of antibodies for antigens (1). CSR requires DNA cleavage and subsequent synapsis, which brings the switch μ (Sμ) region into proximity with a downstream switch region, such as Sγ, Sε, or Sα. This Sμ-Sx synapsis and fusion allow other antibody classes (IgG, IgE, and IgA) to be produced, while maintaining the same antigen specificity encoded by the V-D-J regions. The CSR and SHM mechanisms are dependent on activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) (2). AID induces specific DNA cleavage in the S and V regions in an N terminus domain-dependent manner and DNA S-S synapsis in a C-terminus domain-dependent manner (3). AID-induced DNA cleavage occurs mainly in the V and S regions of the IgH gene, but also at a limited number of other loci. This process drives physiological IgH gene diversification, but can occasionally cause chromosomal translocations that contribute to B lymphomagenesis (4, 5).

AID is a cytidine deaminase that edits single-stranded RNA (6), DNA (7), and DNA/RNA hybrids (8) without possessing intrinsic DNA cleavage activity. This raises a critical question: How does cytidine to uridine (C-to-U) editing by AID result in DNA cleavage [i.e., single-strand breaks (SSB)], which serves as the initiating substrates for SHM and CSR? The classical view is that DNA cleavage and the initiation of SHM are mediated by the base-excision repair and mismatch repair pathways. However, key base-excision repair enzymes, including uracil DNA glycosylase (UNG) and APE1 (apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1), are not required to initiate AID-induced DNA breaks (9–11). Instead, their contribution to CSR lies in subsequent repair steps, such as DNA S-S synapsis and nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ), as shown by knockout and knockdown studies (10, 12). Notably, cytidine-to-thymidine (C-to-T) SHM increases in the loss of function of UNG, which indicates that this enzyme faithfully repairs the uracil generated by AID-mediated cytidine deamination (12–14). Similarly, the mismatch repair endonuclease protein PMS2 is not required for SHM (15) but is required for NHEJ (16). Because SHM is generated by AID-dependent DNA cleavage and subsequent repair by the translesion polymerases (3), intact SHM in PMS2 knockout mice indicates that AID-dependent DNA cleavage does not need PMS2.

Topoisomerase 1 (TOP1) and topoisomerase 2 (TOP2) resolve the helical stress generated during transcription and replication. TOP1 and TOP2 cut single-stranded and double-stranded DNA, respectively; they then rotate the DNA ends and rapidly religate these ends. When TOP1 and TOP2 fail to religate (17), irreversible topoisomerase-DNA cleavage complexes (TOP1-CC and TOP2-CC) are formed, resulting in SSB and double-strand breaks (DSB). Religation is typically interfered by specific inhibitors, such as camptothecin (CPT) for TOP1 or etoposide for TOP2, and can also be suppressed by various endogenous DNA damage. TOP1 is known to become trapped at the sites of various kinds of DNA damage, including those caused by ultraviolet irradiation, ribonucleoside misincorporation, abasic sites, DNA mismatches, alkylating agents, bulky adducts, and oxidative lesions. TOP1 causes genomic instability in repetitive sequence regions in yeast and mammalian cells. This response takes place in a transcription-dependent manner, as transcription induces the formation of non-B-DNA structures in these mutated, repetitive regions (18–20).

We previously hypothesized that the formation of an irreversible TOP1-CC is the underlying mechanism of AID-dependent DNA cleavage, based on the shared features of transcription-dependent SSBs and results from TOP1 knockdown and chemical inhibition (21–23). However, the mechanism by which AID-mediated C-to-U editing induces irreversible TOP1-CC formation remained unclear. Whereas we have previously shown that the AID-dependent, AGO2- and miRNA-mediated reduction of TOP1 alters DNA structures such as non-B-DNA and increases the frequency of DNA breaks by TOP1 itself (23), simple decrease of TOP1 protein amount by TOP1 knockdown in B lymphoma CH12 cells, without AID activation, did not sufficiently increase DNA cleavage. This indicates that another, still unidentified, AID-dependent mechanism must be present to trigger DNA breaks, beyond the regulation of TOP1 protein levels.

Transcription of noncoding RNA in the S region, known as germline transcripts (GLTs), is necessary for DNA cleavage and CSR. GLTs (μ-, γ-, ε-, or α) are initiated from an I promoter, transcribe each S region, and end in each C exon (3). For example, μ-GLT starts from the Iμ promoter, traverses the Sμ region, and ends at the Cμ exon. The necessity of these small transcription units in CSR was first identified in a knockout mouse study of the Iγ2 promoter, where knockout mice lost CSR activity, specifically from IgM to IgG2 (24). The dependence of SHM on transcription is shown in artificial SHM substrates driven by inducible promoters (22, 25). Transcription activity influences chromatin dynamics, including histone exchange during nucleosome disassembly and reassembly, depending on the ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complex and histone chaperones (26, 27). Several knockdown studies in CH12 cells have shown the requirement of chromatin remodeling complex factors, such as SMARCA4, or histone chaperones, including SSRP1, SPT16, and SPT6, for the initiation of TOP1-mediated DNA cleavage and the completion of NHEJ (28–31).

In contrast to the well-established role of chromatin dynamics, the functional contribution of GLTs remains controversial. Most investigations into GLT function have focused on the formation of R-loops —three-stranded structures composed of a DNA/RNA hybrid and a displaced single-stranded DNA segment. Research into RNases that destabilize R-loops has included experiments overexpressing RNase H1 (32–34). This overexpression tends to reduce CSR and SHM through R-loop reduction; however, results have been inconsistent across the relevant published studies. Another line of research has involved the nuclear exosome complex, which possesses endo- and 3’ to 5’ exo-nuclease activities. These studies have shown that deficiencies in factors, including Rrp40/Exosc3, Exosc10, DIS3, or MTR4, produce a prolonged R-loop on the template (antisense) DNA strand (35–38). This indicates that sustained R-loops interfere with the deamination activity of AID. Therefore, excessive R-loops decrease C-to-T mutations of the template strand, while the overall frequency of SHM is not affected. During CSR, the deficiency of these factors causes asymmetric deamination, resulting in inadequate trimming of DSB into blunt ends. This reduces NHEJ and promotes microhomology-mediated (alternative) end joining. Furthermore, knockout of Dis3, the catalytic component of the exosome complex, affects the binding of CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) and cohesin to the IgH gene region and alters higher-order chromatin structures, resulting in reduced CSR. Conversely, reduced expression of dead box helicase 1 (DDX1) decreases R-loop formation by impairing the resolution of G-quadruplexes on GLTs, and reduces AID recruitment (39), which instead demonstrates the necessity of R-loop formation for AID editing.

Structural analysis of the TOP1 protein showed that it surrounds the double-stranded DNA helix with its core, linker, and C-terminal domains (40, 41). Understanding how TOP1 becomes trapped at the transcribed GLT region—a region characterized by R-loops and high topological stress—is both intriguing and key to understanding the connection between AID editing and TOP1-CC formation. To address this, we employed ASOs targeting the Iμ promoter region of the μ-GLT to investigate GLT RNA degradation and its effect on TOP1-CC formation during CSR. Our results showed that ASOs increased DNA cleavage associated with TOP1-CCs. Collectively, these findings suggest that GLT RNA degradation is a critical intermediary step that links AID-mediated C-to-U editing and TOP1-CC formation during IgH gene diversification.

Results

Anti-GLT ASOs Increase CSR from IgM to IgA in ∆Sμ Cells by Enhancing DNA Cleavage.

GLTs contain highly repetitive sequences in S regions, known as the S region “core.” These regions are not essential for CSR, but are important for CSR efficiency, as demonstrated by studies using knockout mice or cell lines with deleted Sμ repeats (42, 43). In this study, we utilized genetically modified CH12 cells lacking a large part of the Sμ region between the Iμ promoter and the Cμ exon (∆Sμ cells), including heavy repeat Sμ core, to exclude the influence of the highly repetitive sequences within the Sμ segment, allowing for more selective evaluation of the roles of GLTs. We designed multiple anti-GLT ASOs to target unique sequences, spanning the Iμ promoter to the 153 bp truncated Sμ region (Fig. 1A). As the R-loop is essential for CSR, we initially hypothesized that GLT degradation by ASOs would decrease CSR. However, we found that two of the five anti-Iμ ASOs (Iμ-AS1 and Iμ-AS2) significantly increased the IgA percentage (IgA%) compared to the control in ∆Sμ cells (Fig. 1B). Notably, these two oligonucleotides resulted in increased IgA% even in nonstimulated cells.

To exclude the possibility of off-target effects on endogenous AID expression, we utilized ∆Sμ cells stably expressing the inducible AID-ER fusion protein (AID fused to the ligand-binding domain of the estrogen receptor mutant, ER-T2) (23). This fusion protein is activated by 4-OHT. Upon AID-ER activation, we found increased IgA levels in Iμ-AS1- and Iμ-AS2-transfected cells (Fig. 1C). Conversely, the IgA% was not affected by the sense-strand negative control oligonucleotides (Iμ-S1 and Iμ-S2) compared with the nontargeting control ASO (Fig. 1C). Similar to the CIT-stimulated ∆Sμ cells, the ∆Sμ-AID-ER cells treated with Iμ-AS1- and Iμ-AS2-ASOs showed an increase in IgA% even without stimulation. The Malat1 knockdown ASO, used as the positive control, showed no change in IgA% compared to the negative control. RT-qPCR confirmed that Iμ-AS1- and Iμ-AS2-ASOs decreased Iμ transcript levels (Fig. 1D), and Malat1 ASO decreased Malat1 levels (Fig. 1E) as expected.

Given the increased frequency of CSR, we next assessed the corresponding DNA cleavage level using the biotinylated linker ligation method (Fig. 1 F and G). The ∆Sμ-AID-ER cells transfected with Iμ-AS2 showed increased DNA cleavage compared to the control oligo-transfected cells stimulated with 4-OHT.

To investigate whether the ASOs induce site-specific DNA breaks near the target region of the Iμ promoter, we analyzed the junction points between 5’ (μ) and 3’ (α) DNA cleavage ends during CSR via Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1H). In the control group, the μ-side junction points were broadly distributed across the Iμ, truncated Sμ, and Cμ regions. However, cells treated with Iμ-AS1 and Iμ-AS2 exhibited a shift, showing more junction points within the Iμ and truncated Sμ, with fewer in the Cμ region. Additionally, Iμ-AS10, which did not result in an increased IgA%, did not significantly alter the distribution of junction points. This suggests that the anti-Iμ ASOs that increase IgA% cause more DNA cleavage sites in the Iμ promoter and converge the junction points toward it.

These junction sequencing results enabled us to quantify the number of mutations in the switched alleles spanning the Iμ, truncated Sμ, and Cμ regions. The mutation frequency per base pair in the switched alleles was not altered by ASO treatment (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Table S1). Across all samples, the mutation frequency of guanine (G) and cytosine (C) was substantially lower than that of adenine (A) and thymine (T). This suggests that the altered translesion polymerases activities in this cell line, such as decreased polymerase θ and increased polymerase η (3). Since the percentage of switched cells increased following Iμ-AS1- and Iμ-AS2-transfection (Fig. 1 B and C), we expected that the total SHM frequency (including both switched and unswitched cells) in the ASO-treated cells would be higher than in the control cells.

Iμ-GLT Region Reduces R-loop Formation but Does Not Alter DNA Single-Strandedness.

The increased DNA cleavage we observed following TOP1 knockdown is associated with an increase in alternative DNA structures, such as negative supercoiling, detectable by bisulfite sequencing (21). To assess whether the ASOs would induce a similar change to the DNA duplex structure, we measured bisulfite sensitivity in the Eμ-Cμ region of the ∆Sμ AID-ER cells (Fig. 2A). Contrary to our expectation, the addition of Iμ-AS1 and Iμ-AS2 did not increase the level of cytosine modification on either the top or bottom strands. This indicates that ASO treatment did not increase negative supercoiling or DNA single-strandedness.

The IgH Sμ region forms an R-loop, a DNA/RNA hybrid structure (44). We evaluated the effects of ASOs on R-loop formation using DRIP with the anti-R-loop antibody S9.6, followed by qPCR in the ∆Sμ AID-ER cells (Fig. 2B). Consistent with their CSR-enhancing effects, both Iμ-AS1 and Iμ-AS2 oligos decreased R-loop frequency compared to the control. This R-loop reduction was pronounced in the Iμ-AS2-treated cells when compared with Iμ-AS1-treated cells, correlating with their stronger effects on CSR (Fig. 1 B and C).

Collectively, these findings that the increase in DNA cleavage by ASOs is not associated with an increase in negative supercoiling or DNA single-strandedness suggest the other DNA structural change. Although enhanced DNA cleavage generally associates with the increased R-loops (45), ASOs induced the reduction/destabilization of R-loops in this case.

RNA Degradation During Transcription Induces Positive Supercoiling and TOP1-dependent DNA Cleavage in Vitro.

As the increased DNA cleavage was not due to negative supercoiling by transcription, the other conformational change such as positive supercoiling was supposed to be involved. To test this possibility, we employed an in vitro transcription assay to evaluate conformational changes in the DNA, specifically positive supercoiling, as well as DNA break structures, such as nicked and linear forms. The mouse Iμ, truncated Sμ, and partial Cμ sequences, along with the artificial T7 terminator, were cloned downstream of the T7 promoter in the pBluescript vector (Fig. 3A). The constructed vector was then used for in vitro transcription, DNA purification, and two-dimensional (2-D) agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE). The typical conformation-dependent distribution of the plasmid is illustrated in Fig. 3B (46). The native, unmodified plasmid DNA recovered from Escherichia coli was negatively supercoiled (Fig. 3 C, Leftmost panel). Transcription by T7 RNA polymerase (T7RNAP) alone did not visibly alter the negative supercoil (Fig. 3C, second panel from Left). The addition of TOP1 shifted the DNA from a negatively supercoiled state toward a more relaxed state (Fig. 3C, third panel from Left); however, the relaxing effect of TOP1 was weaker in the presence of transcription than in the sample without transcription (Fig. 3C, second panel from Right). Crucially, the simultaneous addition of RNase H to TOP1, with or without an ASO, induced stronger TOP1-related relaxation and positive supercoiling effects during transcription. This even resulted in the formation of linear plasmids, indicating DSBs (Fig. 3C, third and fourth from Right). The formation of linear plasmids suggests the creation of DSBs resulting from SSB by TOP1-CC on both strands. This DSB formation did not occur in the T7RNAP (+)/RNase H (+)/TOP1 (–) samples (Fig. 3 C, Rightmost), demonstrating the requirement of both RNA removal and TOP1 activity for DNA cleavage during transcription.

A TOP1–CC Is Formed at the ASO-dependent DNA Cleavage Regions of the IgH Gene in CH12 Cells.

Based on the finding that RNA degradation during transcription induced TOP1-dependent DNA cleavage in this in vitro model system, we next tested the formation of TOP1-CCs in the ASO-dependent DNA cleavage region of the IgH gene in the ∆Sμ AID-ER cells. This was assessed using a ChIP assay with an anti-TOP1-CC antibody (Fig. 3 D–G). Treatment with CPT, which ubiquitously induces TOP1-CC formation in genomic DNA, induced higher TOP1-CC signals in Eμ-Iμ and Malat1 gene regions (Fig. 3E). In contrast, treatment with Iμ-AS2 ASO induced a much higher TOP1-CC signal, particularly with the primers positioned in the Iμ region (Fig. 3F). Importantly, the Malat1 knockdown ASO did not induce TOP1-CC signals with the primers covering its target region. This confirms the specificity of the ASO-dependent TOP1-CC within the IgH gene region. Furthermore, using anti–topoisomerase 1 polyclonal antibody (which recognizes the N-terminal 100 amino acids of human TOP1) revealed similar TOP1 distribution levels across all loci detected by qPCR analysis (Fig. 3G). This latter result shows that TOP1 generally localizes to DNA, without necessarily causing DNA cleavage.

Collectively, our results show that RNA removal by anti-Iμ ASOs during transcription is critical for inducing TOP1-CC formation and subsequent DNA cleavage (Fig. 4). ASOs generally induce RNA degradation via RNase H enzymes (RNase H1 and RNase H2) (47). We propose that the rapid removal of R-loops behind RNA polymerase II (RNAP2) promotes positive supercoiling and the formation of TOP1-CCs. The accumulation of TOP1-CCs results in further DSBs, as suggested by linear plasmids formed in 2-D AGE.

ASO-mediated R-loop disruption induces TOP1-CCs and DSBs in IgH gene. Transcription induces R-loops behind RNAP2. Without the ASOs, the R-loops are reversed to the normal B structure. In the presence of the ASOs, R-loops are disrupted by the ASO and RNase H. TOP1 promotes positive supercoiling and TOP1-CC (single-strand break, SSB) in decreased R-loop condition. Accumulated TOP1-CCs in both strands result in double-strand DNA breaks, as suggested by linear plasmid formation in 2-D AGE.

Tyrosyl-DNA Phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1)-Mediated Processing of TOP1-CCs Is Detrimental to CSR Efficiency.

The resolution of the TOP1-CCs is typically initiated by ubiquitination or sumoylation, and subsequent TOP1 protein degradation by the proteasome (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Fig. 5A). We previously reported that the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib suppresses CSR, indicating that the chemical fixation of TOP1-CC suppresses the progression of DNA cleavage required for CSR (10). To identify the specific TOP1 degradation pathway involved in CSR, we individually knocked down several candidate enzymes and scaffold proteins in CH12 cells: SPRTN (the SprT-like N-terminal domain gene), TEX264 (the testis expressed 264 gene), and TOPORS (the topoisomerase 1 binding, arginine/serine-rich gene) (Fig. 5 B–D) (48–50). Although knockdown efficiency exceeded 80% for all targets, CSR to IgA in the CH12 cells was not affected. This suggests that these canonical degradation pathways do not play a significant role in CSR under these conditions.

We next focused on tyrosine DNA phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1), an enzyme that removes the covalently linked TOP1’s tyrosine residues from 3’-phosphate of DNA break ends (Fig. 5A) (51, 52). This process involves a transesterification reaction, in which the TOP1-derived catalytic tyrosine is replaced with histidine 263 of TDP1 (in humans). This is followed by a hydrolysis step by TDP1 histidine 493 (in humans), which ultimately results in a 3’ phosphate residue on the DNA break end (51). The remaining 3’-phosphate is further processed by several enzymes, including polynucleotide kinase 3’-phosphatase (PNKP). To investigate the role of TDP1, we generated Tdp1 knockout CH12 cells by replacing the 6th exon of Tdp1, which encodes the catalytic histidine 262 (in the mouse), with the EGFP-polyA gene cassette (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Tdp1 knockout clones (B and K) exhibited variable CSR efficiency compared to the parental cells (Fig. 5 E and F), which may be attributable to clonal variation in factors such as AID expression, GLT levels, or NHEJ factors. However, the functional deficiency was validated by the increased sensitivity of several knockout clones to CPT (Fig. 5 G and H). To directly test the contribution of TDP1 to CSR in each clone, we stably expressed human WT TDP1 (WT-rescued) in the knockout cells (Fig. 5 I–K). Crucially, following 24 h of CIT stimulation, the IgA% in the TDP1-rescued cells tended to be lower than in the nonrescued cells (Mock-rescued) (Fig. 5 J and K).

These results indicate that while TDP1 efficiently repairs TOP1-CCs at 3’ DNA break ends in an error-free manner, this process is detrimental to CSR efficiency. For TOP1-CCs to be utilized as substrates for DNA S-S synapsis and subsequent CSR, an alternative, TDP1-independent pathway is likely required (Fig. 5L). Other TDP1-independent TOP1-CC resolution pathways have been reported in independent studies, including pathways involving MRE11 (53), Structure-Specific Endonuclease Subunit MUS81 (MUS81) (54), ERCC4, and APEX2 (55). Since the importance of MRE11 (56), ERCC4 (57), and APEX2 (58) in CSR has already been established, we focused on MUS81 and generated MUS81 knockdown CH12 cells. Our results showed that MUS81 does not contribute to CSR toward IgA (Fig. 5 C and D).

Further TOP1 Depletion Reaches a Functional Threshold that Limits DNA Cleavage and CSR.

The evidence presented thus far supports the role of TOP1 as the primary enzyme mediating AID-dependent DNA cleavage. Paradoxically, we previously reported that miRNA-AGO2-mediated TOP1 reduction of TOP1 increases DNA cleavage frequency by inducing helical stress and non-B-DNA structures. Given that TOP1 is the enzyme responsible for cutting DNA, yet its reduction enhances cleavage, we sought to determine whether enhancement persists at extremely low levels of TOP1 protein.

To address this question, we performed sequential TOP1 knockdown (3xsiTOP1) in CH12-AIDER cells (Fig. 6). Western blot analysis confirmed that 3xsiTOP1 resulted in a more profound depletion of TOP1 protein compared to a single knockdown (1xsiTOP1) (Fig. 6B). Notably, this further reduction in TOP1 did not lead to a corresponding increase in CSR or DNA cleavage (Fig. 6 C–E). In CIT-stimulated samples, the IgA switching efficiency in 3xsiTOP1 cells even showed a relative decrease compared to 1xsiTOP1 (Fig. 6C). Consistent with these observations, the DNA cleavage assay revealed that while 1xsiTOP1 significantly increased DNA cleavage in the 5’ Sμ region, 3xsiTOP1 cells failed to sustain this increase, with cleavage levels returning toward the control baseline (Fig. 6E).

These results suggest a biphasic relationship: While a moderate reduction in TOP1 upregulates DNA cleavage by altering DNA topology, there exists a functional threshold beyond which further depletion no longer supports the catalytic requirements for DNA cleavage (Fig. 6F).

Discussion

The molecular mechanism underlying the formation of an irreversible TOP1-CCs following C-to-U editing by AID remains poorly understood. While we previously demonstrated that AID contributes to reducing TOP1 protein levels via an Ago2- and miRNA-mediated pathway to promote DNA cleavage, TOP1 knockdown alone is insufficient to initiate the level of cleavage required for efficient CSR (Fig. 6) (21–23). This observation suggests that, beyond its effect on TOP1 protein abundance, AID performs a distinct and essential function in triggering DNA cleavage that is depending on C-to-U editing activity.

In this study, we explored the molecular mechanism underlying DNA cleavage by ASOs targeting μ-GLT in CH12 cells. Our core finding is that ASO-mediated GLT degradation—which depletes R-loops at the IgH locus (Figs. 1 and 2)—does not primarily increase DNA single-strandedness (Fig. 2), but instead, induces positive DNA supercoiling and subsequent TOP1-CC formation (Fig. 3). This nonphysiological intervention implies a distinct mechanism in which the dynamic removal of R-loop RNA serves as a potent trigger for DNA cleavage, DNA S-S synapsis, and subsequent CSR (Fig. 4).

In ∆Sμ cells, spontaneous CSR to IgA was observed even in the absence of stimulation (Fig. 1 B and C). Our DNA cleavage assay further indicated that nonstimulated cells treated with Iμ-AS2 exhibited higher levels of DNA cleavage than control oligo-treated, nonstimulated cells. These observations suggest that ASO-mediated R-loop destabilization may trigger DNA cleavage through an AID-independent mechanism. Supporting this possibility, in vitro transcription studies (Fig. 3 A and C) demonstrated that RNA degradation by RNase H can induce DNA strand breaks even in the absence of AID. However, whether ASO-mediated TOP1-CC formation is entirely independent of AID will be definitively concluded after experimental validation using AID knockout ∆Sμ cells. Further evaluation is necessary to establish whether the DNA cleavage induced by ASO-mediated R-loop manipulation and TOP1-CC DNA cleavage occur fully independently of AID activity within a cellular environment.

A critical observation is that anti-GLT ASO-mediated TOP1-CC formation appeared to be restricted to the IgH locus, while knockdown of Malat1 using a corresponding ASO failed to induce TOP1-CC formation around the anti-Malat1 ASO targeting region (Fig. 3). This specificity also suggests that the R-loop disruption-induced DNA cleavage mechanism is not an off-target effect of ASO activity, but rather depends on the unique structural and transcriptional landscape of the IgH switch region.

We hypothesized that this locus specificity is driven by the synergistic effect of several factors. First, it may be partially related to the recruitment of AID. Although Malat1 is a target of AID-dependent mutation in human B lymphoma cells, the frequency of AID-dependent mutation in this gene is much lower than that in the IgH gene region (5). Second, to ensure antibodies are produced efficiently, the IgH locus is actively transcribed during B cell activation in a process regulated by the Eμ enhancer and the 3’RR (59). This regulatory network enables the specific positioning of IgH genes in the nucleus and high-order chromatin looping (60). This active transcription results in the accumulation of positive supercoils and subsequent RNAP2 complex collisions, which cooperatively promote the stable trapping of TOP1 as a TOP1-CC. This unique environment, with its high transcription rate and the open chromatin state of the rearranging IgH locus, likely explains why TOP1-CC was only induced at the Iμ-GLT region following ASO treatment.

While ASO treatment is an artificial intervention, the resultant TOP1-CC-mediated DNA cleavage we observed highlights the importance of RNA disruption in the molecular mechanism of AID-dependent events. This finding naturally raises the question of whether a similar R-loop destabilization mechanism occurs in vivo during physiological CSR. Based on these observed results, we propose a plausible model where AID itself initiates R-loop dissociation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). AID-mediated C-to-U editing in R-loops leads to a G:U mismatch (wobble base pair). Although both RNA and DNA strands are targeted by AID (8, 61), we currently lack direct evidence identifying which specific cytidine of DNA or RNA is edited. In both cases, this G:U mismatch would spontaneously destabilize the R-loop structure, rendering the GLT RNA more susceptible to degradation by nuclear RNases. This possible in vivo mechanism of R-loop disruption following AID-mediated editing would functionally mimic our artificial ASO/RNase H intervention, resulting in the accumulation of positive supercoiling and TOP1-CC formation we observed.

Determining which specific nuclear RNase mediates this cleavage step is crucial. RNase P is an endoribonuclease that recognizes tRNA 5’ ends (62), and RNase MRP is primarily involved in rRNA and mtRNA processing (63). Both are unlikely candidates due to their strict structural specificities. The G:U mismatch created by AID may be located at an internal point within the DNA/RNA hybrid. Therefore, for targeted cleavage of the destabilized RNA strand, an endoribonuclease is mechanistically more effective than an exoribonuclease. RNase H1 is one candidate thought to regulate R-loop overexpression; however, experiments in B cells from multiple groups have shown conflicting results (32–34), suggesting that regulation of the IgH gene’s R-loops may involve other enzymes. While the exosome complex (a 3’ to 5’ exo-RNase) is known to contribute to CSR by removing R-loops from the IgH gene, its exonuclease activity requires RNA ends and may not fully account for the specific internal processing required to generate a recombination-ready substrate. In this context, DROSHA, a member of the RNase III endonuclease family, is a plausible candidate. We have previously reported that the DROSHA and DGCR8 (DiGeorge syndrome critical region gene 8) complex—an RNase III family complex that acts on specific double-stranded RNA structures and is primarily known for its role in microRNA production—is required for CSR (23).

We hypothesize that RNA strands from AID-destabilized R-loops are processed into secondary structures, such as stem-loops, which render them susceptible to RNase III cleavage (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Although we do not have evidence that G:U mismatches create DROSHA-susceptible stem-loop structures, we know these factors are important in CSR. Several noncanonical functions of DROSHA/DGCR8 have been reported, including mRNA destabilization, splicing, response to DNA damage, and antiviral defense (64). Additionally, DROSHA’s association with chromatin has been shown by ChIP analysis. When DROSHA is recruited to chromatin, it promotes NHEJ and/or homologous recombination (65, 66), and this is often dependent on the DNA damage sensor MRN (MRE11-RAD50-NBS). Interestingly, DDX1, which promotes microRNA maturation by interacting with the DROSHA/DGCR8 complex (67), is also necessary for CSR as it supports R-loop formation (39). Collectively, these results suggest that DROSHA/DGCR8/DDX1 proteins may be recruited to the IgH gene region following AID-activation, indicating critical roles in RNA processing and preparing the locus for DNA cleavage. However, further studies are needed to clarify the exact CSR stage that would be affected by loss of DROSHA/DGCR8.

This R-loop disruption model may also partially explain why A/T-rich regions are preferentially targeted by AID, as is often noted in the context of SHM in the V region (68, 69) (SI Appendix, Table S3). The WRC motif (W = A or U, R = A or G) is the canonical preferred target of AID (70); however, this motif is not as frequent in the Iμ promoter region (0.083/bp) compared with the Sμ core region (0.252/bp). Instead, the 635 nt Iμ promoter region sequence in μ-GLT is notably A/U-rich, particularly in the first 210 nucleotides (A or U = 72.4%). This A/U-richness is a common feature of WA sequences (TA, AA, and TT), which are considered polymerase η hotspots that preferably contain and colocalize with AID hotspots within the V regions of human IGHV genes (68, 69). Furthermore, R-loop sequences rich in A, T, or U are associated with inherently less stable base pairing than G/C-rich sequences. This higher A/T/U percentage suggests that R-loops located within the Iμ promoter will be more prone to dissociation and degradation once AID-mediated G:U mismatches occur than R-loops in other regions.

While our study clarifies the use of TOP1-CC as a substrate for CSR, it also suggests that a previously unknown TOP1-CC processing pathway, other than TDP1, must be involved to promote DNA S-S synapsis, CSR, and SHM. TDP1 is the canonical enzyme responsible for faithfully removing TOP1-CCs from 3’-phosphate termini. This process is supported by SSB-repair proteins, such as PNKP, XRCC1, and LIG3 (71), and decreases the risk of genomic instability (72). In the brain tissue of TDP1 knockout mice, TOP1-CCs accumulated in the absence of DNA damage reagents (73), and primary cultured brain cells from the knockout mice were found to be more sensitive to DNA damage, such as that caused by CPT and ionizing radiation. These data indicate the specificity of TDP1 in resolving TOP1-CCs.

Crucially, Tdp1 knockout CH12 cells showed a tendency toward decreased CSR when rescued with WT TDP1 (Fig. 5). This result is highly significant, as it indicates that TDP1 does not contribute to CSR. Instead, it acts as a competitor by removing TOP1-CCs, which would otherwise serve as CSR substrates. TDP1 repairs TOP1-CCs in an error-free manner, using a process that is functionally opposite to the error-prone mechanisms used for CSR and SHM. Identifying the TDP1-independent pathway that converts the 3’ DNA break ends created by TOP1-CCs into suitable S-S synapsis substrates for CSR remains an unanswered and crucial question.

Collectively, our data reveal that the ASO-mediated destruction of IgH GLT R-loops is a potent trigger for TOP1-CC formation via topological stress. We propose that the physiological CSR pathway leverages AID-mediated R-loop destabilization and instability in a process that possibly involves Drosha/DGCR8. Furthermore, we provide evidence of an alternative TOP1-CC repair process that avoids the accurate TDP1-mediated repair pathway, thereby linking TOP1-CCs to CSR and SHM. Our study highlights R-loop stability as a crucial regulatory element in IgH gene diversification within the specialized nuclear environment of B lymphocytes.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture.

∆Sμ cells, generated from murine B lymphoma CH12 cells by deletion of the large region of Sμ using the CRISPR/Cas9 method, were kindly provided by Prof. Frederick W. Alt (Harvard Medical School). The sequences of the truncated Sμ regions are listed in Dataset S1. CH12 cells were cultured as described previously (21, 23). Stimulation of endogenous AID by a cytokine cocktail (CIT: CD40 ligand, IL-4, and TGFβ) and activation of the estrogen receptor ligand-binding domain (AID-ER) by 4-OHT were performed as previously described (21, 23).

Design of ASOs.

ASOs targeting the Iμ promoter region were designed by Exiqon (QIAGEN). Sense-strand oligos were designed and purchased from Exiqon and GeneDesign (Ajinomoto Bio-Pharma) to serve as the control oligos. The sequences of the ASOs are listed in Dataset S1.

Additional details of the other methods used in this study are reported in SI Appendix. The raw numerical data for all graphs and uncropped images of the immunoblots are available at Figshare (74).
