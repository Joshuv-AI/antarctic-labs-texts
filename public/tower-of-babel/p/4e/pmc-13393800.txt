Breaking bone-associated tumor vicious cycle through macrophage membrane-camouflaged nanodecoys for sonodynamic/epigenetic therapeutics of oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Mingxin Cao, Nan Zhou, Chuiyin Wang, Yang Xu, Xinming Li, Lei Sui, Yinsong Wang, Yue Wang, Xiaoying Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is an aggressive tumor that metastasizes frequently and invades the maxillary or mandibular bone easily when adjacent to the jaw bone. Caused by tumor-induced osteoclasts activation, bone invasion could release various cytokines from bone matrix that further promote cancer progression, leading to a bone-associated tumor vicious cycle and poorer prognosis. Herein, two clinical used small molecular drugs, verteporfin (Vp) and tazemetostat (Taz), are repurposed for off-label application and cleverly self-assembled into nanomedicine for dual eradicating cancer cells and inhibiting bone destruction. Specifically, Vp can be used for sonodynamic activation-mediated localized tumor ablation and generation of tumor antigens to activate antitumor immune response. EZH2 inhibitor Taz can amplify the antitumor immune response and inhibit bone invasion, when combining roles of Taz in epigenetically regulating tumor immunogenicity elevation and osteoclast inhibition. Moreover, macrophage membrane is utilized to encapsulate the nanomedicine to facilitate targeted drug delivery to OSCC and RANKL scavenging for osteoclast inhibition, aiding the microenvironment multi-mechanistic remodeling for synergistic therapy. Enhanced therapeutic outcomes have benefited from the nanodecoys in vivo via the animal model of OSCC with mandible invasion. Collectively, these results highlight the attractive functions of macrophage membrane-cloaked nanomedicine for effective and safe treatment against OSCC.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04573-1.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Bone invasion, Bone-associated tumor vicious cycle, Biomimetic nanomedicine, Sonodynamic therapy, Epigenetic regulation

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a common malignant tumor in the oral and maxillofacial region, with over 300,000 new cases annually worldwide [1]. Characterized by its high invasiveness, OSCC not only frequently metastasizes, but also invades the maxillary or mandibular bone easily based on its anatomically close proximity to the jaw bone [2]. Although various treatments are available for OSCC, mainly including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, their therapeutic potential has been limited especially for patients with advanced or recurrent conditions, leading to a five-year survival rate of only 40–60% [3, 4]. Currently, the targeted therapies (e.g., anti-EGFR cetuximab) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1 pembrolizumab) have been emerging and attracted extensive attention, while acquired resistance is increasingly recognized and the therapeutic benefits are largely limited to biomarker-selected subgroups [5, 6]. More critically, once OSCC invades the bone, the standard of surgery requires en bloc resection of the involved bone, resulting in more severe oral disabilities and malfunctions, which profoundly influence patients’ mental health and life quality [7]. The mechanism underlying the progression of bone invasion by OSCC involves the so-called “vicious cycle” between tumor cells and bone microenvironment. Specifically, OSCC cells could directly or indirectly promote the formation and activation of osteoclasts to induce bone resorption. Meanwhile, plenty of cytokines released from bone matrix during the process of osteolysis could further stimulate cancer proliferation and invasion [8, 9]. Therefore, an alternative treatment for OSCC with higher anticancer efficacy while synergistically suppressing osteoclasts to break the vicious cycle is greatly needed.

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is a noninvasive anticancer approach that utilizes ultrasound (US) to activate sonosensitizers for the generation of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in cancer cell apoptosis and necrosis while minimizing damage to adjacent normal tissues [10]. SDT is largely independent of specific genetic or protein targets, circumventing the classical pathways of acquired drug resistance observed in chemotherapy and targeted therapy [11]. Owing to its deep tissue penetration, high spatiotemporal precision, noninvasiveness, and low risk of developing resistance, SDT shows advantages that overcome the limited tissue penetration of photodynamic therapy (PDT) and is particularly suitable for OSCC with the potential to maximally preserve oral structures and functions [12, 13]. Apart from the role of tumor ablation in situ, SDT could induce the immunogenic cell death (ICD) of cancer cells, triggering the release of tumor-associated antigens for vaccine-like effect, meanwhile generating various damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that can activate a broad range of immune cells and initiate strong immune responses [14, 15]. However, tumors could escape immune elimination by poor immunogenicity such as defects in tumor antigen presentation and highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [16]. Consequently, SDT is often insufficient to overcome immunosuppressive barriers, and distinct combining therapeutic strategies are required depending on the mechanisms.

Defect in the surface expression of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules is one of the most important issues for tumor immune escape, owing to the decreased recognition by cytotoxic T lymphocytes [17]. Several cancers have been evidenced to express insufficient MHC-I, and this phenomenon occurs more common in OSCC, with over 50% exhibiting low expression of MHC-I which correlates with lymph node metastasis and poor prognosis [18]. This explains the fact that the majority of OSCC do not benefit from immunotherapy, although it has the high mutational burden and presence of immune infiltrates essential for eliciting immune responses [19, 20]. The mechanisms underlying the loss of MHC-I expression vary across different types of cancers, while are mainly attributed to reversible transcriptional or translational regulation [21]. In OSCC cells, transcriptional suppression of MHC-I genes is notably mediated by histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) on their promoters, which derives from the increased expression of a key methyltransferase enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) [22]. Recent studies have found that inhibiting EZH2 could activate anticancer immunity via multiple mechanisms, including the derepression of the MHC-I antigen presentation pathway, activation of the cGAS-STING signaling, and functional regulation of effector T cells [23–25]. Using EZH2 inhibitors in OSCC could effectively promote antigen presentation and cytotoxic T lymphocytes infiltration via inhibiting EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 modification [22, 26]. Thus, SDT combining EZH2 inhibition provides a promising strategy for achieving enhanced anticancer efficacy.

Inspiringly, EZH2 inhibitors have also been reported to exert significant efficacy in inhibiting osteoclasts formation and bone resorption [27, 28]. Osteoclasts, large multinucleated cells derived from the monocyte/macrophage lineage, are specialized for bone matrix resorption and play a critical role in OSCC-associated bone invasion [29]. Their differentiation and activation are primarily governed by receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL), a key cytokine that promotes the fusion and maturation of preosteoclasts into functional osteoclasts. Upon binding to its cognate receptor RANK expressed on the surface of preosteoclasts, RANKL initiates a cascade of intracellular signaling pathways, including MAPK, NF-κB, and AP-1. This signaling activation subsequently upregulates osteoclast-related genes, most notably the master transcription factor nuclear factor of activated T cells c1 (Nfatc1), while simultaneously suppressing the negative transcriptional factors [30]. EZH2 inhibitors are demonstrated to inhibit osteoclastogenesis by decreasing the H3K27me3 modification on the promoter of negative regulator interferon regulatory factor 8 (Irf8) and thus epigenetically promoting its expression [27]. Therefore, combining SDT and epigenetic therapeutics favors suppression of cancer cells and osteoclasts, which may be promising to break the vicious cycle and produce synergistic therapeutic outcomes. Consequently, two clinical used small molecular drugs, verteporfin (Vp) and EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat (Taz) were used in this study. Vp, a medication used for photodynamic therapy, has been regarded as an effective sonosensitizer for SDT [31], and Taz has been demonstrated to exert great potential for cancer treatment, eliciting anticancer immune responses and inhibiting bone resorption [32–34]. Inspiringly, Vp has the potential of self-assembly with the hydrophobic Taz to establish a nanomedicine via intermolecular interactions. This was partially derived from a reported study using self-assembly of chlorine e6 (Ce6) and Taz for colorectal cancer therapy, as Vp and Ce6 share the common molecular structures of a tetrapyrrole macrocyclic backbone for π-π stacking and a hydrophobic macrocyclic core for hydrophobic interactions with Taz [35]. Yet, the innovation of this work is the simultaneous delivery and synergistic action of Vp and Taz. Furthermore, the application challenges of naked nanoparticles in OSCC treatment needs to be solved due to their instability, poor pharmacokinetics, and tumor targeting concerns.

Herein, we have developed the macrophage membrane-camouflaged nanodecoys (MM-VT) encapsulating Vp and Taz-constructed nanomedicine (VpTaz) for not only enhancing selective drugs accumulation in tumor site but also improving therapeutic efficacy against OSCC (Scheme 1). Macrophage membrane coating could significantly increase biocompatibility, decrease immunogenicity, prolong circulation, and enhance tissue permeability [36]. Moreover, as these nanocarriers inherit the antigenic profile of macrophages, they could realize targeted delivery to tumor vasculature and RANKL scavenging to help resisting bone invasion, making them a powerful tool for OSCC treatment with high clinical translation potential [37, 38]. The MM-VT coated with macrophage membranes thus prepared was expected to achieve dual anticancer and bone destruction inhibition effects, to break vicious cycle for enhanced OSCC control. To be specific, after targeted delivery to OSCC site, MM-VT would be preferentially taken up by OSCC cells but not osteoclasts, as osteoclasts share a monocyte/macrophage lineage, and the distinct surface profiles on them would not be recognized by macrophage membrane-derived targeting moieties. Then, MM-VT could exert sonodynamic effects in OSCC cells that induce the generation of large amounts of ROS against cancer cells and trigger ICD upon US irradiation. Meanwhile, US-induced release of Taz would inhibit the epigenetic regulator of EZH2 to suppress H3K27me3 modification on promoter of MHC-I gene, reversing the gene silencing to increase MHC-I expression on cancer cells. Thus, the activated immunological cascade induced by ICD could recognize epigenetically reprogrammed cancer cells for further tumor elimination. Besides, MM-VT-mediated RANKL scavenging in OSCC microenvironment and Taz-mediated epigenetic regulation in osteoclasts work together to inhibit osteoclasts formation and prevent bone invasion. Collectively, this study provides a more effective and safe strategy for the combined SDT, immune activation, and osteoclasts inhibition against OSCC with the potential of bone invasion, and intervenes in the crosstalk between cancer cells and bone microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Materials, cell lines, and animals

Vp was purchased from Heowns Biochemical Technology (Tianjin, China). Taz was obtained from Sparkjade Biotech (Shandong, China). The SCC-7 mouse squamous carcinoma cell line, sourced from OTWO Biotech (Shenzhen, China), was maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Meilun Biotech, Dalian, China). Human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) line and mouse embryonic cell line NIH-3T3, both sourced from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Meilun Biotech, Dalian, China). The RAW 264.7 mouse macrophage cell line was supplied by BioVector National Type Culture Collection (NTCC, Beijing, China) and grown in DMEM. All culture media were supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For SDT treatment, cells underwent a 2-h incubation with therapeutic agents, followed by a 5-min exposure to US (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) delivered by an Intelect® ultrasound system (Chattanooga, USA). Comprehensive details of the cell experiments are provided in the Supporting Information.

11-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, with an average body weight of approximately 25 g, were supplied by SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China). To investigate the therapeutic efficacy of the MM-VT-mediated combination treatment against OSCC, a mandibular invasion tumor model was established. This was achieved by injecting 0.1 mL of a SCC-7 cell suspension (1.0 × 10⁶ cells/mL in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) into the right masseter muscle of the mice. Subsequently, the tumor-bearing mice received intravenous administrations of the therapeutic agents and housed under subdued lighting conditions, avoiding direct sunlight and strong light. For groups undergoing sonodynamic therapy, the tumor site was exposed to US irradiation (1 MHz, 2 W/cm2) for 5 min after 12 h of administration. All treatments were performed every 2 d for a total of 3 times. All animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yi Shengyuan Gene Technology (YSY-DWLL-2024409). A comprehensive description of the animal experiments is available in the Supporting Information.

Isolation of macrophage membranes

Macrophage membranes were isolated from RAW 264.7 cells following a previously established protocol [38]. In brief, the cells were detached using trypsin, harvested, and resuspended in an ice-cold homogenization buffer consisting of 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM KCl, 75 mM sucrose, 2 mM MgCl2, and a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (one tablet). This cell suspension was then subjected to lysis using an ultrasonic cell crusher (100 W, 3 min). The resulting lysate was initially centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 10 min to pellet and discard large intracellular components and debris. The membrane fraction was subsequently collected from the resulting supernatant by ultracentrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The purified membranes were then stored at −80 °C for further use. The protein concentration of the isolated macrophage membranes was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, USA), and 1.0 × 10⁶ RAW 264.7 cells were able to yield approximately 100 μg membrane protein.

Preparation and characterization of MM-VT

The VpTaz nanoparticles (NPs) were fabricated through a nanoprecipitation method driven by the self-assembly of Vp and Taz. Initially, each component was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to create separate solutions at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. These two solutions were then mixed in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. The resulting mixture was subsequently added dropwise into deionized water at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v). Following a 10-min stirring period, the mixed solution was transferred into a dialysis bag (1,000 kDa) and dialyzed to eliminate the DMSO solvent and any unincorporated free drugs, thereby yielding the final VpTaz. The payloads of Vp and Taz within these NPs were quantified using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260, USA), respectively.

MM-VT were constructed by fusing macrophage membrane vesicles with pre-formed VpTaz cores. To generate the vesicles, macrophage membranes harvested from 1.0 × 10⁶ cells were re-dispersed in deionized water and then subjected to sonication in an ice bath (100 W, 3 min). These membrane vesicles were subsequently fused with VpTaz NPs (containing 0.3 mg of Vp) by sequential extrusion through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane using a mini-extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA). The resulting suspension was then processed to purify the final product. An initial low-speed centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 30 min) was applied to remove excess, uncoated membrane fragments. The supernatant was subsequently collected and subjected to a higher-speed centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 5 min) to pellet the coated NPs, effectively discarding the supernatant containing soluble membrane proteins. The purified MM-VT were thus obtained, and quantitative analysis showed that 1.0 × 10⁶ cells-derived MM-VT contained about 85 μg membrane protein via BCA assay.

The morphology of the prepared VpTaz and MM-VT were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7700, Hitachi, Japan). Size and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zeta-Sizer instrument (Nano ZS90, Malvern, England). To confirm the presence of membrane proteins, sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis was performed. In brief, protein samples (20 μg each, quantified via BCA assay) were loaded onto the gel, electrophoretically separated, and subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. The resulting protein bands were documented photographically for comparative analysis. Additionally, the retention of specific membrane markers, namely lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) and receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK), on both the isolated macrophage membranes and the final MM-VT NPs was assessed via Western blot. For this, samples were seperated on 4–20% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then probed with primary antibodies against LFA-1 and RANK, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. Protein signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Syngene, England). A list of the antibodies used in this study is provided in the Supporting Information (Table S1). To evaluate the release of Taz in MM-VT, 1 mL MM-VT (containing 1 mg/mL of Vp) was transferred into a dialysis bag (MWCO of 3,500 Da), which was immerged in 50 mL PBS at 37 °C with stirring at 50 rpm. The dialysis bag was stimulated with or without US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) for 5 min. Then, 0.1 mL dialysis medium was collected at different time points (0, 1, 2, 6, 12, 24 h), followed by measuring the concentrations of Taz using HPLC.

Stability of MM-VT

MM-VT (1 mL, containing 1 mg/mL of Vp) were separately incubated at 37 °C with different media, an equal volume (1 mL) of 50% FBS diluted in PBS, an equal volume of deionized water, and an equal volume of PBS with the pH of 6.5. Over a 24-h period, the hydrodynamic size of the NPs in each medium was monitored at predetermined time intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h) using DLS.

Quantification of RANKL scavenging capability

The binding affinity of RANKL to the various formulations was assessed through an adsorption assay. Specifically, RANKL (100 pg/mL, PeproTech, USA) was incubated with VpTaz, MM-VT, or macrophage membrane vesicles at equivalent concentrations (300 μg Vp/mL or 1.0 × 10⁶ cells-derived membrane vesicles/mL). Following a 2-h incubation period at 37 °C, the mixtures were subjected to centrifugation at 16,100 g for 10 min to pellet particle-bound components. The residual amount of unbound RANKL remaining in the collected supernatant was subsequently quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Jianglaibio, Shanghai, China).

Sonodynamic performance of MM-VT

The generation of ROS was evaluated using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH) as a chemical probe. To prepare the probe, 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in a 10 mM NaOH solution containing the appropriate amount of DMSO. This mixture was allowed to react in the dark for 30 min, after which the reaction was quenched by the addition of PBS, yielding a 40 μM DCFH working solution. For sonodynamic activity assessment, the prepared DCFH was added to samples containing Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT, achieving final concentrations of 25 μg/mL for Vp and 20 μM for DCFH. These mixtures were then either exposed to US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) for 5 min or left untreated. The conversion of DCFH to fluorescent 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein (DCF), indicative of ROS generation, was subsequently measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7000, Hitachi, Japan) with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm.

The capacity of MM-VT to generate the single-linear state oxygen (1O2) under US stimulation was further investigated using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as a specific trapping agent. Briefly, Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT, each standardized to a Vp concentration of 25 μg/mL, were separately combined with 40 μL of DPBF solution (1 mg/mL in DMSO). Each resulting mixture was then either subjected to US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) for 5 min or maintained under identical conditions without sonication. 1O2 generation was evaluated by monitoring the decrease in DPBF absorbance at 425 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer.

TEMP was used as a trapping agent for further evaluating 1O2 levels. Briefly, Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT, each standardized to a Vp concentration of 25 μg/mL, were separately mixed with TEMP at a concentration of 20 mM. After being exposed to US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2, 5 min) or not, the sample solutions were detected using electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrometer (EMXplus, Bruker, Germany).

Hemolysis assay

The hemolytic potential was assessed using red blood cells isolated from healthy C57BL/6 mice. Following isolation, cells were resuspended in PBS. Aliquots of this cell suspension were then incubated with different samples, free Vp, free Taz, VpTaz, and MM-VT, each at final concentrations of 250 μg/mL for both Vp and Taz. PBS served as the negative control (0% lysis), while deionized water was used as the positive control (100% lysis). All mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with gentle shaken (100 rpm). Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 5 min to pellet intact cells and debris. The resulting supernatants were collected, and the amount of released hemoglobin, indicative of red blood cell lysis, was quantified by measuring absorbance at 541 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. The hemolysis rate for each sample was then calculated relative to the controls.

Statistical analysis

All the data were expressed as mean ± SD and graphed using Graphpad Prism 8.0 (http://www.graphpad.com). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with post hoc Dunnett’s multiple comparison test or Tukey’s multiple comparison test. p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MM-VT

The self-assembly of Vp and Taz, driven by intermolecular interactions, yielded uniform spherical NPs designated as VpTaz. VpTaz possessed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 111 ± 17 nm (PDI = 0.102 ± 0.032) and zeta potential of −33.0 ± 0.5 mV (Fig. 1a, b). The drug contents of Vp and Taz in NPs were determined to be 47.6% and 52.4% using UV–vis spectrum and HPLC, and their encapsulation efficiencies were calculated to be 81.2% for Vp and 89.3% for Taz. VpTaz was then mixed with macrophage membranes derived from murine macrophage cell line (RAW 264.7), and an extrusion method was utilized to coat macrophage membranes on the surface of VpTaz NPs to produce MM-VT. Post-coating characterization by DLS and TEM confirmed a spherical core–shell morphology, with the average particle size increasing to 138 ± 19 nm (PDI = 0.109 ± 0.023) (Fig. 1a, c). The zeta potential increasing to −26.9 ± 1.8 mV (Fig. 1b), in line with the zeta potential of pure macrophage membranes (−27.8 ± 0.6 mV). To further verify the successful coating, the preservation of membrane-associated proteins on the MM-VT surface was detected using SDS-PAGE assay. As presented in Fig. 1d, the protein profile of MM-VT closely resembled that of native macrophage membranes, confirming the retention of membrane proteins during the coating process. Given that macrophages express functional surface markers such as LFA-1, which facilitates tumor targeting via tumor vasculature-highly expressed ICAM-1 recognition, and RANK which can sequester RANKL to inhibit osteoclast formation [38, 39], we further detected the expressions of LFA-1 and RANK in MM-VT using Western blot. As shown in Fig. 1e, both LFA-1 and RANK were clearly detectable in MM-VT, whereas no signals were observed in uncoated VpTaz, demonstrating the effective transfer of macrophage membrane functionality to the NPs surface. As US has been widely used as an effective trigger for on-demand drug release [40], and the release of Taz is essential for determining its epigenetic regulation potential, the release of Taz upon US irradiation was evaluated and results showed that US could promote Taz release (Figure S1), verifying the US responsive properties of MM-VT. Importantly, MM-VT exhibited no changes in particle sizes during 24 h of incubation either in serum at 37 °C or in PBS with the pH of 6.5 mimicking tumor microenvironment, showing good stability essential for the in vivo application (Figs. 1f and S2). A hemolysis assay also revealed that MM-VT induced less than 1% hemolysis rate, well within the clinically accepted safety threshold (Figure S3).

RANKL sequestering and sonodynamic performances

Subsequently, RANKL sequestering capability mediated by the expressed RANK on MM-VT was evaluated by calculating the remaining RANKL concentration after coincubation of nanomedicine and RANKL solution. As displayed in Fig. 1g, incubation with MM-VT resulted in a substantial reduction in free RANKL levels, comparable to the positive control (MM vesicles). In contrast, uncoated VpTaz exhibited negligible RANKL depletion under identical conditions. This finding confirms that the RANKL-scavenging ability of MM-VT is specifically conferred by the transferred macrophage membrane, highlighting its potential to inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption.

Since MM-VT was mainly designed to improve OSCC immunosuppressive microenvironment after initiating SDT-mediated cancer therapy, sonodynamic activities of MM-VT in aqueous solution were then evaluated. The capacity for ROS generation upon US stimulation was first assessed using DCFH as a fluorescent probe [41, 42]. Upon reaction with ROS, non-fluorescent DCFH is rapidly converted to highly fluorescent DCF [43]. After US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) for 5 min, the fluorescence intensity of DCF for MM-VT significantly increased, indicating that MM-VT exhibits good sonodynamic performance. Meanwhile, comparable fluorescence signals were observed for MM-VT, VpTaz, and free Vp under the same sonication conditions, indicating that the nanoformulation and membrane coating do not compromise the intrinsic sonodynamic activity of Vp (Fig. 1h, i). Given that 1O2 is widely recognized as the primary cytotoxic species generated during SDT [44], its production was further examined using DPBF as a specific chemical trap. The reaction between 1O2 and DPBF leads to a characteristic decrease in absorbance at 425 nm, which can be monitored by UV–vis spectrophotometry [45]. As shown in Fig. 1j and 1k, the absorption curve of DPBF was consistently reduced in the group of Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT with US irradiation, demonstrating the sonosensitizer Vp-triggered 1O2 generation. Afterwards, TEMP was applied as the spin trap to further verify the Vp-triggered 1O2 production (Figure S4). Collectively, these results indicate that MM-VT retains potent sonodynamic activity, positioning it as a promising candidate for US-triggered cancer therapy.

Cellular uptake and SDT-mediated anticancer effect of MM-VT in vitro

The efficient targeted delivery of MM-VT to OSCC site is essential for exerting RANKL sequestering and US-induced therapeutic effects. Therefore, the interaction between MM-VT and ICAM-1 overexpressed on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) by inducing with TNF-α was investigated to assess the targeting ability in vitro, as LFA-1 on macrophage membranes could specifically bind to ICAM-1 [39]. After 10 min of incubation, flow cytometry was utilized to detect the MM-VT bound on HUVECs using the fluorescence signal of Vp. As shown in Figs. S5 and 2a, MM-VT incubation yielded detectable fluorescence even in unstimulated HUVECs compared to VpTaz, and this signal was markedly enhanced in TNF-α-activated cells overexpressing ICAM-1, confirming that LFA-1/ICAM-1 interaction confers specific targeting to tumor vasculature. Although macrophage membrane coating confers inherent affinity for not only tumor vasculature but also inflammatory sites, therapeutic specificity of our work is achieved by spatial control of US application, and thus off-target effects are minimized.

Then, the internalization of MM-VT by a mouse OSCC cell line SCC-7 was analyzed using flow cytometry and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Time-course analysis revealed that MM-VT uptake was time-dependent, reaching saturation at approximately 2 h. When SCC-7 cells were incubated with free Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT at equivalent Vp concentrations (2.5 μg/mL), all of them were efficiently internalized with no statistically significant differences observed (Figs. 2b and S6), indicating that the macrophage membrane coating does not impede NPs uptake. These flow cytometry findings were further corroborated by confocal imaging, which showed comparable intracellular fluorescence signals across all groups (Figure S7). Meanwhile, the SCC-7 cellular uptake efficiency of MM-VT was not affected when MM-VT binding with RANKL (Figure S8), indicating that the subsequent US-induced effects would not be influenced after RANKL scavenging. Additionally, the internalization of MM-VT by RAW 264.7 cell line, which has been often used for in vitro induction of osteoclasts formation, was evaluated using CLSM. Results showed that few MM-VT entered RAW 264.7 cells after 2 h of incubation when compared with SCC-7 cells (Figure S9), consistent with our previous study investigating the phagocytosis ability of RAW 264.7 in taking up macrophage membrane-camouflaged NPs [46]. This indicates that MM-VT in tumor microenvironment is preferentially internalized by cancer cells, but not osteoclasts, due to the monocyte/macrophage lineage shared by osteoclasts and the distinct surface profiles on them that would not be recognized by macrophage membranes-derived targeting moieties as the ability of macrophage membranes in escaping from the immune system.

Since ROS generation is critical for SDT cytotoxicity, we assessed the capability of MM-VT in elevating intracellular ROS levels via the ROS fluorescent probe of DCFH-DA. SCC-7 cells were pre-incubated with PBS, free Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT for 2 h, followed by exposure to US (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) for 5 min. Intracellular ROS production was then assessed by flow cytometry to quantify DCF fluorescence intensity. As shown in Fig. 2c and 2d, all Vp-containing formulations, free Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT, induced a marked increase in DCF fluorescence upon US irradiation, whereas no significant ROS generation was observed in the absence of US stimulation. Notably, the fluorescence intensities among the three Vp-based groups were comparable, with no statistically significant differences detected, a finding consistent with their similar cellular uptake profiles. We also observed the fluorescence of intracellular DCF using CLSM, and gained similar results as described above (Fig. 2e). These suggest that MM-VT could trigger the generation of large amounts of intracellular ROS based on the sonosensitizer Vp, achieving good sonodynamic performance.

The therapeutic efficacy of MM-VT-mediated SDT was next assessed in vitro using MTT assay. SCC-7 cells were exposed to free Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT for 2 h, with or without subsequent US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2, 5 min). As illustrated in Fig. 2f, in the absence of US, none of the Vp-containing formulations exhibited appreciable cytotoxicity, even at Vp concentrations up to 5 μg/mL. In contrast, upon US stimulation, all three treatments induced dose-dependent cytotoxicity, confirming their US-triggered anticancer activity (Fig. 2g). At equivalent Vp concentrations, no statistically significant differences in cell killing were observed among the groups under US exposure, and Taz alone showed no cytotoxic effect even at concentrations as high as 20 μg/mL (Figure S10). These findings collectively indicate that the observed cytotoxicity of MM-VT is attributable specifically to the sonosensitizer Vp, with no contribution from the carrier or Taz. To assess biosafety, MM-VT was tested on normal cell lines, including RAW 264.7 macrophages and NIH-3T3 fibroblasts. Even at the highest tested Vp concentration (5 μg/mL), MM-VT induced no significant cytotoxicity in these cells (Figure S11), underscoring its favorable safety profile. Meanwhile, live/dead cell staining assay was used to visually detect the cell killing effects of the above treatments, with live cells emitting green fluorescence and dead cells showing red fluorescence. Consistent with the MTT results, MM-VT combined with US irradiation led to extensive cancer cell death, as evidenced by predominant red fluorescence (Figure S12). Given that SDT mediates cancer cell death mainly by inducing cell apoptosis [12], the anticancer effect of MM-VT was subsequently evaluated by detecting the cell apoptosis via flow cytometry. As shown in Figs. 2h and S13, MM-VT treatment followed by US exposure resulted in a markedly high apoptotic rate of approximately 71% in SCC-7 cells, demonstrating the potent pro-apoptotic activity of this SDT strategy. These results collectively establish MM-VT as a highly effective and biosafe sonosensitizer for US-triggered cancer therapy.

MM-VT-mediated immune activation by ICD effect and epigenetic reprogramming of OSCC cells

It is well established that SDT can trigger ICD effect, a type of programmed cell death that induces adaptive immune responses. This process includes the release of tumor-associated antigens and DAMPs, which could promote the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) and the subsequent cross-priming of antigen-specific effector T cells, capable of specifically recognizing and eliminating tumor cells [47, 48]. To determine whether MM-VT-mediated SDT elicits such a response, we firstly examined canonical ICD markers: calreticulin (CRT) surface exposure, high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1) nuclear translocation, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) extracellular release [49]. The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that after US irradiation (1 MHz, 1 W/cm2) for 5 min, the surface of SCC-7 cells treated with Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT all evidently expressed CRT (Fig. 3a). Quantitative analysis was further conducted using flow cytometry. The results also indicated that Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT markedly induced the surface exposure of CRT after US treatment (Fig. 3b). Then, the translocation of HMGB1 from cell nucleus to cytoplasm was observed using CLSM after immunofluorescence staining. While PBS- and Taz-treated cells retained HMGB1 predominantly within the nucleus following US exposure, clear translocation of HMGB1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm was observed in cells treated with Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT (Fig. 3c). Finally, the extracellular release of ATP was assessed using ATP assay kit. Results showed that Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT all evidently promoted the release of ATP after US irradiation (Figure S14). These indicate a pronounced ICD response owing to the SDT of MM-VT.

Then, ICD-triggered anticancer immune response was evaluated via detecting the DCs maturation, as it is the initial and one of the most important steps in immune response cascades [50]. From the results of flow cytometry, the proportions of CD80+ CD86+ DCs in the group of Vp, VpTaz, and MM-VT with US irradiation were all over 30% without evident differences, and significantly higher than those in other groups (Fig. 3d, e), indicating that the activity of DCs was enhanced in vitro. Thus, we conclude that SDT-mediated by MM-VT has a killing effect on SCC-7 cells, and the generated DAMPs and cell fragments could elicit DCs maturation.

Mature DCs can present tumor antigens to T cells and promote them to differentiate into effector T cells for specific tumor killing. However, OSCC cells have been reported to be lack of MHC-I expression, leading to failure of specific recognition and killing via effector T cells [18]. Mechanistically, EZH2 inhibition by Taz could transcriptionally elevate MHC-I expression on OSCC cells by reducing its H3K27me3 modification [22]. Therefore, Taz was chosen to construct the nanoformulation and its epigenetic regulatory effect on SCC-7 cells was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3f, MM-VT could dramatically inhibit H3K27me3 expression but not EZH2 in SCC-7 cells by Western blot, and the inhibitory effects of pure Taz on H3K27me3 were concentration-dependent (Figure S15). Next, flow cytometry results revealed that MM-VT treatment could evidently enhance MHC-I protein levels on SCC-7 cells surface, contributing to about 60% of MHC-I+ cells compared with around 10% in the group of Control and Vp (Fig. 3g, h). In addition, the mRNA levels of H2-K1 and B2M, essential antigen presentation genes of MHC-I, were obviously increased by MM-VT (Fig. 3i, j). These reveal the epigenetic reprogramming roles of MM-VT on elevating MHC-I expression of OSCC cells, which has the advantage of promoting tumor cells recognition to further enhance ICD-induced anticancer immunity.

In vitro osteoclast inhibition by MM-VT

Meanwhile, EZH2 inhibitors have been reported to exhibit suppressive roles on osteoclasts by epigenetically elevating expressions of negative regulators of osteoclasts formation [27, 28]. Combining the roles of macrophage membrane on sequestering RANKL, MM-VT was assumed to play dual inhibitory effects on osteoclasts formation, thus preventing bone invasion of OSCC. In that case, we firstly examined whether US-pretreated MM-VT could inhibit osteoclasts formation using mouse bone marrow-derived monocytes (BMMs). As shown in Fig. 4a and b, following a 3-day expansion phase with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), preosteoclasts derived from BMMs readily differentiated into large, multinucleated, TRAP-positive mature osteoclasts upon 5-day exposure to RANKL and M-CSF. This differentiation process was notably attenuated by treatment with either Taz or MM vesicles alone, and was most profoundly inhibited by MM-VT. Quantitative analysis confirmed that both the number and size of mature osteoclasts were significantly reduced in the MM-VT-treated group (Fig. 4c, d). Consistent with decreased osteoclasts formation, qRT-PCR results showed that MM-VT reduced RANKL-mediated upregulation of key osteoclast factor Nfatc1 (Fig. 4e). Further, Taz and MM-VT evidently prevented the downregulation of the representative osteoclast inhibitory factor Irf8 in RANKL-treated BMMs (Fig. 4f), in line with previous studies reporting the epigenetically regulatory role of EZH2 inhibitors in suppressing osteoclasts formation. These certificate that US-pretreated MM-VT could inhibit osteoclasts formation and holds great potential in preventing bone invasion of OSCC.

In vivo tumor targeting and sonodynamic efficacy of MM-VT

Encouraged by the promising in vitro results, we proceeded to evaluate the tumor-targeting capability of MM-VT in vivo using a mouse model of OSCC with mandibular invasion, established by inoculating SCC-7 cells into the right masseter region of C57BL/6 mice [9]. Following intravenous administration of free Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT at an equivalent Vp dose of 7 mg/kg, the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution were monitored over 24 h via in vivo fluorescence imaging, leveraging the intrinsic fluorescence of Vp. As shown in Fig. 5a and b, MM-VT exhibited the slowest blood clearance among three groups, indicating that the macrophage membrane coating confers immune evasion properties and extends systemic circulation time. Regarding tumor accumulation, both free Vp and VpTaz showed declining fluorescence signals at the tumor site within 12 h post-injection, with free Vp demonstrating the weaker retention due to its rapid clearance. In contrast, MM-VT accumulated rapidly at the tumor region within 4 h and continued to exhibit progressively stronger fluorescence signals up to 12 h after administration (Fig. 5c), suggesting that the macrophage membrane coating substantially enhances tumor-targeting efficiency. Ex vivo imaging of excised tumors at 12 h post-injection further confirmed that MM-VT achieved significantly higher tumor accumulation compared to the other formulations (Figs. 5d, S16). Histological analysis of frozen tumor sections corroborated these findings, revealing intense Vp fluorescence within tumor tissues from the MM-VT-treated group (Fig. 5e). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MM-VT possesses superior tumor-targeting capability, attributable to the functional benefits conferred by the macrophage membrane coating.

Next, the in vivo sonodynamic performance was evaluated in the above constructed SCC-7 tumor-bearing mice. After 12 h of intravenous administration, these mice received US irradiation (1 MHz, 2 W/cm2) for 5 min at the tumor site. SOSG was used as a fluorescent probe to detect the generation of intratumoral ROS [51]. As presented in Fig. 5f, tumors from mice receiving MM-VT followed by US irradiation displayed the most pronounced green fluorescence signals among all treatment groups, indicating robust intratumoral ROS generation. This enhanced sonodynamic effect underscores the potential of MM-VT as an effective sonosensitizer for US-mediated cancer therapy.

Therapeutic efficacy of MM-VT in the mouse OSCC model with mandibular bone invasion

We next evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of MM-VT, focusing on its triple mechanisms of direct tumor cell killing, immune activation, and osteoclast inhibition. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to receive intravenous injections of PBS (Control), free Vp, VpTaz, or MM-VT (all at a Vp dose of 7 mg/kg) every two days for a total of three doses. For groups receiving SDT, the tumor region was exposed to mild US irradiation (1 MHz, 2 W/cm2, 5 min) at 12 h post-administration (Fig. 6a). As shown in the tumor growth curves (Fig. 6b), US alone had no impact on tumor progression. In contrast, all Vp-containing formulations combined with US significantly suppressed tumor growth, confirming the SDT-mediated antitumor activity of Vp-based sonosensitizers. Notably, even in the absence of US, both VpTaz and MM-VT exhibited moderate tumor inhibition, likely attributable to their ability to modulate the immunosuppressive microenvironment and disrupt the tumor-osteoclast vicious cycle. Upon US stimulation, MM-VT demonstrated the most pronounced tumor suppression, outperforming uncoated VpTaz. This enhanced efficacy supports the synergistic contribution of targeted delivery, SDT-mediated cytotoxicity, immune activation, and osteoclast inhibition. Images of mandibular tumors excised from each group on day 14 are presented in Fig. 6c. Consistent with the growth curve data, tumors from mice treated with MM-VT plus US were visibly the smallest, further substantiating its potent antitumor effect. Throughout the treatment period, no significant body weight fluctuations were observed in the MM-VT plus US group, whereas mice in other groups experienced mild weight loss to varying degrees (Figure S17). This variation may be attributed to tumor location affecting oral function and feeding behavior as the tumor progressed. Systemic biosafety was further assessed through routine blood analysis and histopathological examination. All hematological parameters measured in treated mice fell within normal reference ranges (Figure S18). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, revealed no observable morphological abnormalities or tissue damage across all groups (Figure S19), confirming the favorable biosafety profile of the treatment regimen.

To assess the degree of mandibular bone destruction by OSCC cells, micro-CT was utilized and revealed extensive bone destruction on the mandibular bone surface in the Control group. In contrast, bone destruction was significantly attenuated in mice treated with VpTaz, and this attenuation effect was more evident in the MM-VT group. These indicate that the inhibition of bone resorption was achieved by targeted delivery of MM-VT, and thus macrophage membrane-mediated RANKL sequestering and inhibitory role of Taz in osteoclasts formation. Moreover, in the group of MM-VT with US irradiation, nearly no destruction of the mandibular bone was observed when compared with the group of MM-VT (Figs. 6d, S20), suggesting that sonodynamic killing of tumor cells prevented bone invasion possibly by decreasing the levels of cancer-derived osteoclasts-promoting cytokines. Quantitative morphometric analysis further showed that the relative bone volume (BV/TV), trabecular bone number (Tb.N), and trabecular bone separation (Tb.Sp) were markedly improved in the MM-VT group with US irradiation (Fig. 6e–g).

Histological evaluation provided a further representation of the therapeutic efficacy of MM-VT against OSCC with the potential of bone invasion. Consistent with the micro-CT results, H&E staining showed that compared to all other treatment groups, mice receiving MM-VT combined with US irradiation exhibited markedly reduced osteolysis at the tumor-bone interface. Moreover, extensive tumor tissue damage, characterized by cellular shrinkage and necrosis, was evident within the tumor lesions of the MM-VT plus US group. TRAP staining further demonstrated a significant reduction in the number of TRAP-positive osteoclasts following MM-VT treatment with US stimulation, confirming its inhibitory effect on osteoclast formation. At the same time, immunohistochemistry staining of RANKL, Ki67, and TUNEL analysis were carried out, and results showed that MM-VT treatment evidently suppressed RANKL expression and tumor proliferation after US irradiation, and promoted tumor apoptosis, when compared with other groups (Fig. 6h, i). Collectively, these results indicate that MM-VT is a safe and efficient strategy, which could inhibit tumor growth via sonodynamic killing and immune activation effects, meanwhile preventing bone invasion of OSCC.

In vivo anticancer immune responses

Finally, the stimulation of anticancer immune responses was analyzed in vivo. At 48 h after the first treatment, 3 mice were randomly selected from each group, and their tumors and spleens were collected to evaluate the immune activation. Immunofluorescence staining of frozen tumor sections was first performed to assess ICD, a critical trigger for antitumor immunity. As shown in Fig. 7a, tumors from mice treated with MM-VT combined with US irradiation exhibited the most pronounced CRT exposure on the tumor cell surface and the most extensive HMGB1 translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, indicating robust ICD induction by sonodynamic killing. We next examined the activation status of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment using flow cytometry. Upon US stimulation, MM-VT significantly increased the proportions of CD80⁺ CD86⁺ DCs and CD3⁺ CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells within tumors, whereas no such changes were observed in groups without US exposure (Fig. 7b, c, e, f). These findings demonstrate that MM-VT-mediated SDT effectively promotes DC maturation and cytotoxic T lymphocyte activation. Similar trends were observed in splenic immune cell populations (Figure S21), suggesting systemic immune activation. At the end of treatment, spleens were collected to evaluate the generation of immunological memory via flow cytometric analysis. Compared to the control group, MM-VT with US irradiation evidently elevated proportions of CD44⁺ CD62L⁻ effector memory T cells, as well as CD44⁺ CD62L+ central memory T cells (Figs. 7d, g, S22), indicating the establishment of long-lasting antitumor immunological memory. All above indicate that MM-VT shows great superiority for enhanced immunotherapy via dual effects of ICD-induced immune-promoting cells increasing and Taz-mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment improving.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully developed a novel biomimetic nanomedicine composed of macrophage membrane-encapsulated sonosensitizer Vp and EZH2 inhibitor Taz, for breaking the bone-associated tumor vicious cycle by dual eradicating cancer cells and inhibiting bone destruction, and thus achieving improved therapeutic efficacy towards OSCC. On one hand, we identified that MM-VT can be delivered to tumor vasculature under the guidance of macrophage membranes, and then not only effectively stimulate the generation of intracellular ROS for cancer cell death, but also exert SDT-induced immunostimulatory effects to further eradicate epigenetically reprogrammed cancer cells. On the other hand, MM-VT had potent osteoclasts inhibition performance owing to the RANKL scavenging and epigenetic regulation effects, leading to reduced bone invasion by OSCC. Importantly, MM-VT exhibited minimal systemic side effects, and was promising in maintaining oral function without causing disfigurement. Nevertheless, several limitations exist in the current study. The functional status of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells requires further characterization to fully elucidate the mechanisms of T cell-mediated killing. In addition, the long-term durability of the antitumor immune response remains to be validated. These aspects will be addressed in our future investigations. Despite these limitations, the proposed nanomedicine provides an effective and safe strategy for OSCC treatment, holding broad biomedical application prospects.
