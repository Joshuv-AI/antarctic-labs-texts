A Meta-analysis of Randomized Placebo-controlled Trials on the Effects of Probiotics for Autism Spectrum Disorders
Authors: Ping-Wen Huang, Shun-Chin Liang, Cheuk-Kwan Sun, Yu-Shian Cheng, Kuo-Chuan Hung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe current meta-analysis aimed at updating evidence regarding the therapeutic potential of probiotics against autism spectrum disorder (ASD) symptoms and identifying outcome confounders.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, randomized placebo-controlled trials retrieved from PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ScienceDirect were analyzed for effect size of primary outcomes (i.e., overall behavioral symptom changes) expressed as standardized mean difference (SMD) and odds ratios (ORs) for continuous and categorical variables, respectively, with 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsMeta-analysis of eight studies (465 participants, mean age 6.03, range 1.5−45, female 17.4%) revealed total ASD symptom improvements in the probiotic group compared to placebos (SMD −0.19, 95% CI −0.38 to −0.01, p = 0.04, I2 = 0%, eight studies, 465 participants, certainty of evidence [COE] low). Subgroup analyses showed probiotics-associated improvements only in studies recruiting predominantly preschool children (SMD −0.30, 95% CI −0.59 to −0.01, p = 0.04, three studies, 191 participants) or those using probiotics for over three months (SMD −0.39, 95% CI −0.73 to −0.06, p = 0.04, three studies, 144 participants) without difference between multiple- and single-strain probiotics. No difference was noted in overall dropouts between individuals treated with probiotics and those taking placebos (OR 0.98, p = 0.94, eight studies, I2 = 0%, 464 participants, COE low). Despite a low risk of bias in most studies, COE was deemed low from limited trials and inconsistencies on sensitivity analysis.ConclusionThe current study showed an association between probiotics use and an improvement in ASD symptoms, mainly in those aged below six or over three-month treatments. More large-scale investigations are warranted to support our findings.Keywords: Probiotics, Complementary therapies, Gut-brain axis, Autism spectrum disorder

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorders (ASD), which are characterized by the core behavioral manifestations of significant social and communication difficulties, as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities, comprise a group of neurodevelopmental disorders with onset often identifiable at early developmental stages [1]. The prevalence of ASD was estimated to be as high as 2.3% in children and 2.2% in adults with a notable increase over the past few decades possibly due to a heightened public awareness of the behavioral symptoms of ASD and changes in diagnostic criteria [1].

Nevertheless, currently available pharmacological interventions mainly target the associated psychiatric symptoms of ASD including irritability, while the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has not approved any pharmacological treatments against the core symptoms of ASD [1]. As a result, intensive behavioral interventions from early childhood remain the mainstay of treatment [1], although their benefits are often challenged by their costliness and time-consuming nature [2]. Therefore, complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) has gained popularity among caregivers of patients with ASD despite a lack of strong evidence to support its efficacies against ASD symptoms [3]. The two main categories of CAM for ASD are biologically based treatments such as dietary interventions and non-biologically based approaches such as art therapy [3].

Dietary interventions or supplementations are among the most popular [3], not only because of the common gastrointestinal (GI) complaints in subjects with ASD [4] but also the possible interactions between enteric microbiome and ASD-associated behavioral symptoms via the gut-brain axis (GBA) reported in several experimental and human studies [5]. Therefore, modulation of gut microbiota through probiotic-related products has been proposed to be a potential treatment alternative against the symptoms of ASD in several clinical trials [6-14]. However, the results of previous clinical investigations were inconsistent. Although most studies showed results apparently in favor of probiotic interventions compared with placebos [6-14], only one reported statistically significant improvement in the behavioral symptoms of ASD after probiotic treatment [14]. The findings of previous meta-analyses or network meta-analytic studies focusing on the therapeutic impact of probiotics on the symptoms of ASD were also inconclusive [15-18]. While most meta-analytic studies showed no significant correlation between probiotic use and improvements in ASD symptoms [15-18], subgroup analysis targeting studies using probiotic blends in one meta-analysis demonstrated significant improvements in ASD-related behavioral symptoms [15]. These findings, therefore, still suggested a potential beneficial role of probiotics in ASD treatment and also indicated that modifications of treatment strategies (i.e., probiotic blends vs. single-strain regimens) may enhance the therapeutic effects of probiotics against the symptoms of ASD. Consistently, a prior in vitro study demonstrated that, compared with single-strain probiotics, multiple-strain regimens may offer additional benefits through enhancing the probability of favorable bacterial adhesion to the enteric mucosa [19].

In addition to the components of probiotics, several other factors including supplementation timing and treatment duration may influence their therapeutic effects [20]. A previous meta-analysis on preterm infants reported a reduced risk of auditory impairment only in those receiving early supplementation of multiple-strain probiotics (i.e., within postnatal seven days) compared to that in the placebo group [20]. The mechanisms supporting the therapeutic use of probiotics for ASD involve not only the crosstalk between the central and enteric nervous systems [21] but also the anti-inflammatory effects of some probiotics [22,23]. Given that the effects of inflammation in the central nervous system may vary with the stages of neurological development [24,25], he roles of probiotics may change during different neurodevelopmental periods (e.g. preschool vs. school-age children). Moreover, the duration of treatment may be an important contributor to the therapeutic benefits of probiotics as prior evidence showed an absence of long-lasting probiotic effects on gut colonization after cessation of supplementation for 1−4 weeks [26]. However, these issues were not addressed in a prior meta-analytic study [15], the quality of which was further hampered by the inclusion of several trials that did not use placebos as controls [15].

Therefore, the present meta-analysis aimed at updating evidence regarding the treatment effects of probiotics against the symptoms of ASD by including only randomized placebo-controlled clinical trials as well as identifying other potential factors, namely age, number of probiotic strains, and duration of treatment, that may influence the therapeutic effects.

METHODS

Protocol and Registration

This meta-analysis was performed in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews as well as the Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1; available online) [27]. It was registered in the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO CRD42023482049).

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that investigated probiotic use against ASD-associated behavioral symptoms were retrieved from electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ScienceDirect from inception to 13/12/2023 without linguistic and geographic restrictions. Information on the adopted keywords and search strategies for the databases is available in Supplementary Table 2 (available online). To ensure completeness of our search, reference lists of the retrieved literature were also screened for potentially eligible articles. The PICO (i.e., population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes) criteria for study eligibility were: (1) population: RCTs recruiting participants diagnosed with ASD; (2) intervention: studies that used probiotics as monotherapy or supplementation to existing standard treatments; (3) comparator: placebos; and (4) outcome: changes in the overall behavioral symptoms of ASD. We excluded studies (1) in which interventions also involved other dietary supplements such as prebiotics or omega-3-fatty acid; (2) with comparators comprising non-treatment controls or other potential therapeutic agents other than placebos; or (3) in which the outcome assessment did not include ASD-related behavioral symptoms.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Based on predetermined search strategies and keywords (Supplementary Table 2; available online), two independent authors examined the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles (PW Huang and CK Sun) and recorded data pertinent to the characteristics and outcomes of the studies. Discussion with a third author (KC Hung) was needed in case of disagreements on study eligibility or data to be included. Kappa coefficient was used for evaluating inter-rater reliability [28]. For data retrieval, electronic mails were forwarded to the corresponding authors of articles that did not provide the necessary information. The Cochrane’s “risk of bias” assessment tool was utilized to assess the risk of bias within studies [29], while we used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) for appraising the level of evidence of each outcome [30]. In case of the presence of potential bias (e.g., attrition bias), we conducted a subgroup analysis to elucidate its impact on our study outcomes. All disagreements on the certainty of evidence ratings and the risk of bias were resolved through discussion.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

The primary outcome of the current study was change in the overall ASD symptoms measured with verified assessment tools or rating scales including the aberrant behavior checklist (ABC), the autism diagnostic observation schedule (ADOS), the clinical global impression – severity (CGI-S), the social responsiveness scale (SRS), and the Achenbach system of empirically based assessment (ASEBA). The secondary outcomes included acceptability evaluated by the numbers of individuals in the probiotic and placebo groups who discontinued their participation in the study. Effect size (ES) was used to quantify the outcomes of interest. It was expressed as standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for continuous variables and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI for categorical variables. Data analysis was conducted with Review Manager 5 (RevMan 5.4; Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Center, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). The Mantel-Haenszel method and generic inverse-variance method were used for analyzing categorical and continuous data, respectively. We adopted a fixed effects model when heterogeneity (I2) was less than 50%, while a random effects model was used when heterogeneity (I2) was greater than 50%. Subgroup analyses were conducted to determine the effects of treatment duration, strain number in a probiotic regimen (i.e. single vs. multiple), and age of participants on the therapeutic outcomes. To identify the potential sources of between-study variability, we conducted a meta-regression analysis using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) software version 4.0 (Biostat) to assess the influence of specific study-level characteristics on the ES of probiotic treatment against ASD symptoms. The moderators included the mean age of participants (years), the proportion of female participants (percent), and the duration of treatment (weeks). To assess the robustness of study outcomes, we used sensitivity analysis with a leave-one-out approach for evaluating the impact of an individual study and I-squared test for assessing heterogeneity among the included studies. The probability of small study effects was evaluated by funnel plots, the results of which were considered informative only when more than 10 studies were included. For all outcomes, a pvalue of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Eligible Studies and Characteristics

The study selection process was in compliance with the PRISMA statement [27] (Fig. 1). Out of the 334 articles initially retrieved from the databases, 23 were deemed suitable for full-text review following exclusion of 311 trials after title and abstract screening (Supplementary Table 3; available online). Finally, eight studies including 465 participants (mean age: 6.03; range: 1.5−45; female proportion: 17.4%) meeting our inclusion criteria were considered eligible for the current investigation [6-13]. The kappa coefficient for study eligibility was 1. Data from the included studies were collected on 13/12/2023.

The characteristics of the eligible studies are summarized in Table 1. Three of the eight studies enrolled mainly preschool children [8,10,12] with one recruiting mostly young adults [13], while the remaining trials focused primarily on school-age children [6,7,9,11]. Regarding probiotic strain number, four studies adopted multiple-strain probiotics [6-8,12] and four used single-strain regimens [9-11,13]. The duration of treatment was less than 3 months in five studies [6,7,9,11,13], and over 3 months in the other three [8,10,12]. Most studies allowed concomitant use of single or multiple psychotropics regardless of their nature except one that excluded the use of psychotropics other than methylphenidate [10]. Focusing on study design, five studies adopted a parallel design [8-12] and the remaining three were cross-over studies [6,7,13]. On the other hand, most studies allowed the presence of GI symptoms in their participants except one study that excluded those experiencing such symptoms [13]. Despite the lack of difference in baseline severity of GI symptoms between the probiotic and placebo groups, such information was not available in two studies [6,11]. The eligible trials were from the USA [9,12,13], Taiwan [10,11], Italy [8,12], and the UK [6].

Risk of Bias Assessment

Analysis with the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for risk of bias assessment showed a low risk of bias in randomization sequence and allocation concealment in most studies. Besides, detection and performance biases were considered low in the majority of studies because of their adoption of a double-blind design. On the other hand, attrition bias was noted in three trials likely attributable to their relatively long follow-up periods (Fig. 2) [8,10,12]. The risk of reporting bias was deemed low in all trials due to their direct targeting ASD-related behavioral problems as their primary outcomes (Fig. 2). However, the risk of other bias was regarded as high in three studies on account of their sponsorship from private companies (Fig. 2) [10,11,13]. Our subgroup analysis to assess the effects of potential bias related to sponsorship by private companies on study outcomes showed no obvious differences between the subgroup of studies sponsored by private companies and those without sponsorship (Supplementary Fig. 1; available online). On the other hand, SMD was higher in the subgroup of studies with a high dropout rate (> 20%) than that in those with a relatively low number of dropouts (SMD = 0.39 vs. 0.11, respectively) despite a lack of statistical significance on subgroup comparison (Supplementary Fig. 2; available online).

Results of Syntheses

Primary outcome

The findings of the present investigation showed significant improvements in the total symptoms of ASD in the probiotic group relative to the placebo group (SMD = −0.19, 95% CI: −0.38 to −0.01, p = 0.04, eight studies, 465 participants) (Fig. 3) without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0% and p = 0.91). Nevertheless, our leave one-out sensitivity tests indicated a loss of statistical significance of the result if any of three studies was excluded [8,9,12]. Our subgroup analysis of age showed a significant improvement in the probiotic group relative to the placebo group only in studies recruiting predominantly preschool children (SMD = −0.30, 95% CI: −0.59 to −0.01, p = 0.04, I2 = 0%, three studies, 191 participants), but not in those focusing on school-age children or young adults (SMD = −0.12, 95% CI: −0.36 to 0.12, p = 0.32, I2 = 0%, five studies, 274 participants) (Fig. 3). On the other hand, subgroup comparison showed no significant difference between the two subgroups (p = 0.32). Likewise, notwithstanding the similar therapeutic outcomes between the subgroup of studies with probiotics use less than three months and those prescribing probiotics for longer than three months, only those using probiotics for over three months showed a significant improvement in the total symptoms of ASD in the probiotic group compared to the placebo group (SMD = −0.39, 95% CI: −0.72 to −0.06, p = 0.04, I2 = 0%, three studies, 144 participants), but not in those with probiotic use under three months (SMD = −0.11, 95% CI: −0.33 to 0.11, p = 0.34, I2 = 0%, five studies with 321 participants) (Fig. 4). Finally, both subgroups of studies adopting multiple-strain probiotics and those using single-strain regimens demonstrated no notable improvement in the total symptoms of ASD (SMD = −0.22, 95% CI: −0.47 to 0.03, p = 0.09, I2 = 0%, four studies, 247 participants; and SMD = −0.16, 95% CI: −0.43 to 0.10 p = 0.23, I2 = 0%, four studies, 218 participants, respectively) when compared with their respective placebo groups (Fig. 5).

Secondary outcomes

The overall number of dropouts showed no significant difference between the probiotic and placebo groups (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.57 to 1.69, p = 0.94, I2 = 0%, six studies, 436 participants) (Supplementary Fig. 1; available online). For our secondary outcome, no notable heterogeneity or inconsistency on sensitivity analysis was found (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4; available online).

Meta-regression Analysis

Meta-regression analysis conducted to explore the potential contributions of study-level covariates to differences in ES across the included trials showed no significant association of ES with age (coefficient = 0.013, p = 0.581) (Supplementary Fig. 4; available online), female proportion (coefficient = −0.008, p = 0.447) (Supplementary Fig. 5; available online), and treatment duration (coefficient = −0.015, p = 0.241) (Supplementary Fig. 6; available online), indicating no meaningful influence of the three moderators on the observed treatment outcomes.

Certainty of Evidence

The results of appraisal of the level of evidence for the outcomes of interest are summarized in Supplementary Table 4 (available online). The primary outcome was downgraded to low because of the small number of available studies and loss of significance if any of three studies [8,9,12] out of the eight trials was removed on leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. In addition, the secondary outcome of the overall number of dropouts was also downgraded to low as a result of an even more limited number of trials.

DISCUSSION

Our primary results based on eight randomized placebo-controlled trials including 465 participants demonstrated a positive correlation between probiotic use and improvements in behavioral symptoms regardless of the use of single- or multiple-strain regimens. Our subgroup analyses further revealed significant improvements in ASD-associated behavioral symptoms in the probiotics group relative to placebos only in the subgroup of participants receiving probiotics before the age of six or those being treated for more than three months. Our analysis also indicated a fair acceptability of probiotics as reflected by the comparable ORs of overall dropouts between the probiotics and placebo groups. Taken together, the current study showed a positive correlation between probiotic use and an improvement in ASD-associated behavioral symptoms only in those aged less than six or using probiotics for more than three months with a small ES.

The results from several clinical studies investigating the use of probiotics in patients with ASD remained inconsistent; while some open-label studies reported improvements in ASD symptoms [31,32], most RCTs showed no significantly greater improvement in ASD symptoms among subjects treated with probiotics than those receiving placebos [6-13], except one that combined probiotics with the prebiotic fructo-oligosaccharide [14]. Similarly, previous meta-analyses [15-17] and a network meta-analysis [18] also reported no significant correlation between probiotic use and improvements in ASD symptoms. However, two previous meta-analyses included no more than five trials in their analyses [17,18]. In addition, although another meta-analysis investigated up to 10 trials, it included studies that only used prebiotics [16]. In fact, prebiotics encompass a wide range of dietary supplements, some of which may not only stimulate growth of bacteria in the colon [33] but also have their own effects on improving neurocognitive function (i.e., omega-3-fatty-acid) [34]. Therefore, our meta-analysis included only probiotics to focus on their potential therapeutic benefits in the present clinical setting. In addition, the current study not only increased the number of RCTs but also focused exclusively on randomized placebo-controlled trials to reinforce the elucidation of this issue. On the other hand, notwithstanding the more strict selection of studies than that of previous meta-analyses, evidence from the present investigation was rated as low because of the small number of available studies as well as the small ES that lost significance if any of three studies [8,9,12] out of the eight trials was removed on leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. More large-scale clinical studies are required to support our results.

An interesting finding of our subgroup analysis was the significant behavioral improvement in participants aged below six years in the probiotic group compared to the placebo group but not in those older, notwithstanding an absence of statistical significance in subgroup comparison between the two age groups and non-significant finding in our meta-regression analysis for age. Provided the known implications of central nervous system inflammation in different diseases during specific neurodevelopmental periods [24,25], the anti-inflammatory effects of probiotics may vary at different developmental stages. Besides, intestinal microbiotas change throughout the lifetime during which the pattern of synaptogenesis also varies [35]. A recent study further underscored the correlation between enteral microbiota and neurodevelopment by showing an association of a reduced gut microbial diversity with an increased risk of delay in neurological development among small-for-gestational age infants relative to their appropriate-for-gestational age counterparts [36]. Therefore, in light of evidence underpinning a variation in the effects of probiotics at different stages of neurodevelopment, our findings supported the potential benefit of administering probiotics in early childhood.

Another intriguing outcome of our subgroup analysis was significant improvements in ASD-associated behavioral symptoms in subjects being treated with probiotics for more than three months compared with those taking placebos. However, such improvements were absent in the subgroup of studies with shorter treatment durations, notwithstanding the lack of significant difference between the two subgroups (i.e., below vs. over three months) and non-significant finding in our meta-regression analysis for treatment duration. Prior evidence showed a relatively short probiotic colonization of only 1−4 weeks following cessation of supplementation [26], suggesting a need for a prolonged treatment. In concert with this finding, a previous meta-analysis of patients with cardiovascular diseases reported a positive correlation between the treatment effects of probiotics and the duration of treatment [37]. Consistently, another meta-analysis further demonstrated a beneficial effect of probiotics on neurocognitive functions in infants and young children receiving a longer duration of intervention [38]. Our results further suggested the possibility of an insufficient impact of a short course probiotic supplementation on long-term neurodevelopment. On the other hand, a high risk of attrition bias in all three studies with a treatment duration of over three months [8,9,12] was possibly due to an increased dropout rate on prolonged follow-up instead of the occurrence of probiotics-related adverse side-effects as reflected by the comparable dropout rates between the placebo and probiotic groups [8,9,12]. Nevertheless, taking into account the fact that our finding was derived from only three RCTs with a high risk of attribution bias as well as the lack of significant association between the therapeutic effects of probiotics and treatment duration on our meta-regression analysis, further long-term clinical investigations with a low dropout rate are warranted to better address this issue.

Several limitations need to be addressed in the present study. First, despite our effort to include eight RCTs that involved 465 participants in total to update the evidence, the robustness of our findings requires verification from further large-scale clinical investigations. Second, although a number of confounding factors (e.g., dietary habits, use of other nutritional supplements) may affect our study outcomes, relevant information was not available for subgroup or meta-regression analysis. Third, because most of the included RCTs focused on preschool and school-age children with only one study targeting the adult population [13], our findings may not be extrapolated to adults diagnosed with ASD. Fourth, given that patients with ASD are known to have a higher prevalence of GI problems than that in the general population [3] and most of our included studies recruited participants showing GI symptoms except one [13], we cannot rule out the possibility that the improvement in behavioral symptoms may be secondary to mitigation of GI symptoms. However, the ES of the only study that excluded participants with GI symptoms [13] was similar to that of our meta-analysis (ES = −0.12 vs. −0.19, respectively) and the severity of GI symptoms was not significantly different between the probiotic and placebo groups in most studies [7-10,12], with the exception of two that did not provide such information [6,11]. Therefore, the current study demonstrated no evidence suggesting an association between an improvement in ASD symptoms and that in GI symptoms. On the other hand, the wide variation in rating scales for assessing GI symptoms in most of the included studies precluded our conduction of a meta-regression analysis to elucidate the impact of GI symptoms on the therapeutic outcomes of probiotics. Further studies are warranted to address this issue. Fifth, although three out of the eight studies were sponsored by private companies, our subgroup analysis showed no significant difference between the subgroup of studies with sponsorship from private companies and those without. Nevertheless, the finding needs to be interpreted with discretion. Sixth, despite a larger ES associated with a longer duration of probiotic use than that linked to a shorter duration (ES = 0.39 vs. 0.11), the availability of only three studies with a high risk of attribution bias for assessing the long-term therapeutic effects [8,9,12], may impair the robustness of our findings. Further studies are required for verification. Seventh, although the heterogeneity was low in our results, inclusion of only eight studies may not reflect a reliable estimation due to a low statistical power derived from a limited number of trials. Finally, the ES was small and became non-significant if any of three studies [8,9,12] was removed on sensitivity tests. Therefore, evidence from the current study remains weak and requires further large-scale investigations for verification.

CONCLUSIONS

The current study supported an association between probiotic use and an improvement in the behavioral symptoms of ASD. In addition, our subgroup analyses showed significant improvement in ASD-associated behavioral symptoms only in those treated with probiotics before the age of six or those undergoing treatment for over three months. We also found fair acceptability and tolerability regarding the use of probiotics compared with placebos. Nevertheless, because the robustness of evidence from the present study remains insufficient to guide clinical practice, more large-scale clinical investigations are warranted to support our results.
