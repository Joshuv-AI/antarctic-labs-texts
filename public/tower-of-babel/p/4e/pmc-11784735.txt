Effect of Preoperative Pulmonary Hypertension on the Course of Right Ventricular Function: A Three-dimensional Echocardiographic Study in Valvular Surgery Patients
Authors: Shinya Ito, Kenji Yoshitani, Satsuki Fukushima, Hitoshi Matsuda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Context:Postoperative right heart failure is a poor prognostic factor after cardiac surgery. However, the factors that influence perioperative right ventricular (RV) function remain unknown. This study investigated whether preoperative pulmonary hypertension (PH) affects changes in RV systolic function during the perioperative period.Aims:This study aimed to determine the effect of preoperative PH on changes in RV systolic function in patients undergoing aortic or mitral valve surgery.Settings and Design:This was a retrospective review of three-dimensional echocardiographic datasets of 157 patients undergoing aortic or mitral valve surgery.Materials and Methods:Patients were classified into the PH (n = 105) or non-PH (n = 51) group based on preoperative right heart catheterization findings. RV ejection fraction and RV longitudinal strain (RVLS) of the free wall and septum were calculated using semiautomated image analysis software at four time points: before surgery, end of surgery, 3 months after surgery, and 1 year after surgery.Statistical Analysis Used:A mixed-effects model was used to compare changes in RV function between groups.Results:Over 1 year, no significant differences in trends were observed between groups for any of the parameters. However, a significant deterioration in free wall RVLS was observed in the non-PH group when focusing on the change from baseline to 1 year (P value for interaction = 0.013).Conclusions:In patients undergoing valvular surgery, the presence of preoperative PH did not significantly influence changes in RV function throughout the perioperative period.

INTRODUCTION

Right heart failure (RHF) after cardiac surgery is an independent predictor of postoperative complications.[1] The existence of perioperative RHF is associated with prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay, prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation, increased readmission rates, and increased mortality, thereby worsening postoperative outcomes.[23]

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) increases right ventricular (RV) afterload. Typically, the right ventricle, with its comparatively thinner walls, ejects blood at a lower pressure than the left ventricle.[4] Therefore, the right ventricle is more sensitive to afterload than the left ventricle, leading to a close association between PH and the development of RHF.[5]

In patients with left-sided valvular heart disease, the coexistence of PH is common, especially in those with severe disease requiring valve surgery. The prevalence of PH has been reported to be 78% in patients requiring surgery for mitral valve disease, 50% in those with severe aortic stenosis, and 10–20% in those with severe aortic regurgitation.[6]

To date, there have been some studies of perioperative RV function after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass.[789] However, these studies have been conducted over a relatively short period, typically no longer than 1 year, and methods for assessing postoperative RV function have varied between studies.

In this study, we evaluated the impact of preoperative PH on perioperative RV function in a cohort of patients undergoing valve surgery using three-dimensional (3D) echocardiogram datasets from before surgery to 1 year after surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This study is a retrospective, observational, single-center cohort study that enrolled patients who underwent mitral, aortic, or combined valve surgery between February 2020 and March 2021 at a tertiary care hospital. Our hypothesis is that the preoperative status of PH may influence perioperative RV function.

The institutional ethics committee approved this study (registration number: R20014-2). Permission was granted to proceed with the opt-out consent process. Exclusion criteria were (1) age less than 20 years, (2) emergency surgery, (3) history of median sternotomy, (4) diagnosis of moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation, (5) contraindication to pulmonary artery catheter use or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), (6) lack of postoperative echocardiographic follow-up, and (7) refusal to participate by the patient. PH was defined as mean pulmonary arterial pressure of 20 mmHg or higher at rest according to the 6th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension.[10] Patients were classified into either the PH group or the non-PH group based on mean pulmonary artery pressure after induction of anesthesia.

Primary analysis

The primary analysis compared overall changes in 3D RV functional parameters (RV ejection fraction [RVEF], free wall RV longitudinal strain [RVLS], and septal RVLS) between the PH and non-PH groups across four time points: after induction of anesthesia, after sternal closure, 3 months after surgery, and 1 year after surgery. In addition, we compared changes from baseline (after induction of anesthesia) in the aforementioned 3D RV functional parameters at each subsequent time point.

Secondary analysis

For the secondary analysis, 2D echocardiographic indices (Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion [TAPSE], RV fractional area change [RVFAC], and left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]) were used to make the same comparisons as for the 3D parameters. Perioperative hemodynamic changes were also evaluated.

Perioperative management

General anesthesia consisted of midazolam (0.1 mg/kg), fentanyl (4–6 μg/kg), remifentanil (0.5–1.0 μg/kg/min), and rocuronium (0.6–1.0 mg/kg) for induction. The TEE probe (X8-2t transesophageal transducer [Philips Medical Systems, Andover, Massachusetts, USA]) was inserted after tracheal intubation. Maintenance of anesthesia was achieved through a combination of propofol (5 mg/kg/h), fentanyl (6–8 μg/kg in total), remifentanil (0.5 μg/kg/min), and sevoflurane (1%–3%) as needed.

A radial arterial line was inserted immediately after induction. A central venous catheter and a pulmonary artery catheter were placed through the right internal jugular vein. Arterial blood pressure (ABP), central venous pressure (CVP), pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), heart rate (HR), and cardiac index (CI) were continuously monitored throughout the operation. Inotropes and vasopressors such as dopamine (3–5 μg/kg/min) and norepinephrine (0.03–0.05 μg/kg/min) were used at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist based on findings from pressure monitoring, HR, cardiac output derived from the pulmonary artery catheter, and TEE images.

Aortic or mitral valve replacement, or both, was performed through full median sternotomy. Mitral valvuloplasty for mitral regurgitation was performed as robot-assisted minimally invasive cardiac surgery through a right mini-thoracotomy. The pericardium was routinely closed.

For postoperative management, patients were kept intubated under moderate sedation and transferred to the ICU. The decision to extubate was made by an ICU physician or cardiac surgeon within a few days after surgery based on institutional criteria such as PaO2/FiO2 ≥200, improvement in mental status, sufficient clearance of secretions, hemodynamic stability, complete rewarming, and reversal of muscle relaxant action.

Data collection

Most patients were regularly followed in an outpatient clinic for over 1 year unless they lived in a remote area. Baseline characteristics of individual patients at the time of admission were obtained from electronic medical records. Information on surgical procedures and physiological parameters was obtained from anesthesia records.

Perioperative datasets were acquired by two board-certified cardiac anesthesiologists at induction of anesthesia and sternal closure. A Philips EPIQ 7 ultrasound machine (Philips, Bothell, Washington, USA) and an X8-2t transesophageal transducer were used. The 3D datasets were acquired with a frame rate of at least 20 Hz and with the full-volume mode setting during a short pause of mechanical ventilation. Recording time was set to three heartbeats or 3 s in patients with atrial fibrillation and included at least one cardiac cycle. All datasets were saved in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format for the following offline analyses.

Postoperative datasets were acquired by a sonographer at 3 months and 1 year after surgery during outpatient follow-up visits. An EPIQ 7 ultrasound machine and X5-1 transthoracic transducer (Philips Medical Systems) were used with the same settings. TAPSE, RVFAC, and LVEF were measured with conventional 2D echocardiography at follow-up visits.

Processing and analysis of three-dimensional right ventricular datasets

TEE – and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) – derived 3D datasets were processed offline using TOMTEC 4D RV-Function and Image Arena software (TOMTEC Imaging Systems GmbH, Unterschleissheim, Germany). All analyses were performed by a single analyst. The frame with the largest ventricular area was selected as the end-diastolic frame. The frame with the smallest area was selected as the end-systolic frame. Each landmark was set manually according to the on-screen instructions. The software automatically traced the endocardium throughout the cardiac cycle to create a dynamic 3D RV model. RV end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), stroke volume (SV, calculated as EDV-ESV), ejection fraction (EF, calculated as SV/EDV), free wall RVLS, and septal RVLS were calculated from this 3D model [Figure 1].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics

Demographic variables, comorbidities, medications, surgery-related details, and echocardiographic findings were summarized with means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. The paired t-test was used for comparing the mean of parameters before and after surgery. Fisher‘s exact test was used for categorical and ordinal data. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Data handling for missing observations and confounders

Given that our datasets contained some missing values, primarily due to instances of low-quality data, we conducted our analyses using a mixed-effects model that accounted for these missing data points under the assumption that they were missing at random (MAR). In this mixed-effects model, patient identifiers (IDs) were treated as random intercepts to account for interpatient variability. In addition, we included patient characteristics as fixed effects to adjust for confounding. This approach allowed us to use all available data and provide unbiased estimates under the MAR assumption. Therefore, we did not exclude patients with missing data from the analysis.

Primary analysis

For the 3D RV functional parameters designated as dependent variables, both the presence of PH and time points were incorporated as fixed effects. Patient IDs were treated as random effects. The mixed-effects model was used to evaluate the interaction between PH status and all time points. We also applied the same model to compare the baseline with each of the subsequent time points, selectively extracting the corresponding data. These analyses provided P values for the interaction effect.

Secondary analysis

In the secondary analysis, the same modeling approach was applied to 2D echocardiographic parameters. Perioperative hemodynamic parameters were assessed using the same mixed-effects model structure.

Sample size calculation and analytical software

A 5% change in RV strain was considered clinically significant when calculating sample size.[11] With standard deviation = 8%, alpha = 0.05, beta = 0.20, and a two-sided analysis, a sample size of approximately 41 patients would be required for each group. We estimated that 80% of the patients would have data quality that is sufficient for analysis. Therefore, we decided on a sample size of at least 51 patients for each group. All data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.0; R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).[12]

RESULTS

Patient enrollment and characteristics

During the study period, 177 patients were enrolled. One patient was excluded due to difficulty in inserting a pulmonary artery catheter and 20 patients were excluded because they did not undergo TTE during outpatient follow-up. Therefore, the final analysis included 157 patients. None of the patients were readmitted or died during the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

Up to 1 year after surgery, there were no significant differences in RV functional changes between the PH and non-PH groups. However, when focusing on changes from baseline to 1 year after surgery, the PH group had significantly lower free wall RVLS levels than the non-PH group, indicating improved RV function.

On the other hand, the influence of preoperative PH on changes in the RV functional parameters of TAPSE and RVFAC derived from 2D echocardiography before and after surgery was not significant. TAPSE temporarily decreased in both groups at 3 months but returned to baseline at 1 year after surgery. PAWP, which reflects left atrial pressure, remained stable after surgery in the non-PH group. However, a decrease was observed in the PH group. One reason for the lack of significant differences in the analysis of all time points might be the crossover between the two groups in RV function from 3 months to 1 year. Unlike the non-PH group, the PH group had U-shaped changes.

The return to preoperative RV functional levels during the follow-up period in the PH group might be due to the reduction in left atrial pressure resulting from valve repair, leading to improvement in postcapillary PH. A decrease in PAWP was observed immediately after valve repair in the PH group. Both groups had nonstatistically significant lower RV function immediately after surgery. However, the various adverse effects of cardiopulmonary bypass and surgery on RV function might diminish over time,[13] revealing actual postoperative RV function in the PH group. In contrast, in the non-PH group, RV function did not recover 1 year postoperatively and even demonstrated a tendency to decline. One potential explanation for this observation is that a significant proportion of patients in the PH group had relatively mild PH, which may have resulted in adaptive RV remodeling before surgery. The absence of adaptive remodeling in the non-PH group may be a contributing factor to the observed differences. It has been discussed that early-stage adaptive remodeling of the RV exerts a protective effect on RV contractility.[14]

TAPSE was significantly lower in both groups at 3 months after surgery before recovering at 1 year. Previous research has found a direct correlation between pericardial incision during open heart surgery and a decrease in RV longitudinal contraction.[15] However, another study showed that longitudinal motion decreases after pericardial incision, but at the same time RV sphericity increases, which maintains RVEF. Therefore, parameters that assess longitudinal motion, such as TAPSE, might underestimate RV function at 8 to 10 months after surgery.[1617] The TAPSE results might not have shown a difference by PH status because they were affected by changes in the shape of the right ventricle.

Study strengths

The primary strength of this study is the long-term assessment of RV function using 3D echocardiography. Although cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains the gold standard for assessing RV function, its high cost and extensive time requirements make repeated, long-term use challenging.[18] It is particularly impractical for immediate postoperative use. Previous studies have shown that 3D echocardiography correlates better with MRI than 2D echocardiography.[192021]

In this study, we used a uniform, semiautomated data processing pipeline performed by a single analyst for both TEE and TTE datasets during postprocessing analysis. This method minimized both inter- and intraoperator variation in the measurement of RV functional parameters throughout the follow-up period.

Furthermore, the use of a mixed-effects model to account for missing RV functional parameter values due to poor image quality allowed these data to be included in our analysis without omission.

Study limitations

This study has a number of limitations. It was conducted at a single center, which might limit the generalizability of our findings. In addition, we began collecting 3D data relatively recently, resulting in a smaller patient cohort. Moreover, the number of study participants was further reduced because some patients did not undergo TTE during the follow-up period, although they were seen as outpatients.

In this study, PA pressure measurements for diagnosis of PH were performed after induction of anesthesia because preoperative PA catheterization was not routinely performed without specific indications. However, this method is not the standard approach for diagnosing PH, which may have influenced the accuracy of PH diagnosis. Another possible factor contributing to the lack of significant differences in our primary analysis may be that a large proportion of the PH group, nearly 60%, fell into the borderline mild PH category (mean PAP 21–25 mmHg).

Regarding the etiology of PH, we assume that most of our participants had Group 2 PH (PH due to left heart disease), either postcapillary or combined. We did not classify PH by etiology because wedge pressures were recorded in approximately 70% of cases. However, regardless of the type of PH, PH imposes afterload on the RV. Because our participants were elective surgical patients without acute comorbidity (i. e, acute PH), we believe that the effect of preoperative PH on the RV was equivalent across etiologies. However, from a pathological perspective, precapillary or combined PH is not predicted to improve after surgery, which, if included, may have negatively affected the statistical power.

We also need to be concerned about the potential impact of using TEE and TTE in a single analysis on data uniformity, there are studies showing high correlations between 3D TEE RVEF and cardiac MRI and between 3D TTE RV strain, EF, and cardiac MRI.[2223] However, there is still no direct evidence that 3D TEE and 3D TTE are interchangeable.

The lack of postoperative pulmonary artery catheterization in the distant postoperative period leaves postoperative PH status unknown; however, we do not believe that this undermines the value of the study because the goal of this research was to help other investigators understand whether preoperative PH is a potential factor that worsens postoperative RV function. Although there were no deaths within 1 year, we did not evaluate patient outcomes other than mortality.

Finally, this study was observational, and it is important to emphasize that even if differences in perioperative changes in RV parameters were observed, the clinical significance of these changes remains unclear. Our study was designed to investigate whether preoperative PH influences postoperative RV function; the observation of significant differences does not necessarily clarify causality or specific clinical implications. This highlights the need for further research to determine the real-world impact of perioperative changes in RV function in patients with preoperative PH.

CONCLUSION

Preoperative PH did not significantly influence perioperative RV function for 1 year in patients undergoing valve surgery. For patients with concomitant PH, surgical relief of postcapillary PH could potentially have a beneficial effect on RV function at 1 year.
