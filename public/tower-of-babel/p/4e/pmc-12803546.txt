Finerenone in patients with severe heart failure: The FINEARTS‐HF trial
Authors: Riccardo M. Inciardi, Henri Lu, Brian L. Claggett, Akshay S. Desai, Pardeep S. Jhund, Alasdair D. Henderson, Carolyn S.P. Lam, Bela Merkely, Michael Zi Miao, Michele Senni, Sanjiv J. ABSTRACT
AimsWhile patients with severe heart failure (HF) were historically considered to have reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), it is increasingly recognized that severe HF occurs across the full spectrum of LVEF. The aim of this study was to assess prevalence, cardiovascular (CV) outcome risk, and treatment response to the non‐steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone among patients with severe HF in FINEARTS‐HF.Methods and resultsTreatment effects of finerenone on the primary endpoint of total (first and recurrent) HF events and CV death were assessed by severe HF status, as defined by the adapted multiparametric ESC‐HFA criteria including New York heart Association functional class III/IV, hospitalization for HF within the previous 12 months, and impairment of health status measured by Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire total symptom score <75. Overall, 888 (14.8%) patients fulfilled the definition for severe HF. Patients with severe HF were older, with a higher comorbidity burden, and higher N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide levels. Over a median follow‐up of 2.7 years, total HF events and CV death occurred at a higher rate among those with severe HF (31.6 per 100 patient‐years [py]) as compared to those without severe HF (13.9 per 100py). Finerenone was beneficial in reducing the rate of the primary endpoint regardless of severe HF status (p interaction = 0.98), with a greater absolute rate reduction in those with severe HF (5.9 per 100py) compared with those without severe HF (2.2 per 100py) in light of higher baseline risk. The proportion of patients who discontinued study treatment for any reason or experienced adverse events according to treatment assignment was similar regardless of severe HF status.ConclusionsAmong patients with mildly reduced or preserved LVEF, severe HF was associated with a heightened risk of CV events. Treatment with finerenone appeared safe and effective, regardless of HF severity.

Introduction

Patients with heart failure (HF) with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) or preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) experience an excess risk of morbidity and mortality worldwide, despite therapeutic advances. 1 , 2 A distinct subgroup with severe HF features is increasingly recognized, characterized by progressive symptoms, reduced tolerance to therapy, and frequent decompensations despite optimized medical therapy. 3 Although severe HF was historically applied to individuals with reduced ejection fraction (EF), the Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC‐HFA) has more recently put forth a multiparametric definition of severe HF applicable across EF phenotypes defined by symptom burden, functional limitation, and clinical trajectory. 4 This evolving phenotype often includes patients with persistent congestion, recurrent hospitalizations, or intolerance to foundational therapies due to hypotension, renal dysfunction, or frailty. Recognizing and characterizing this subgroup is critical, particularly as novel pharmacologic options emerge and care pathways are being redefined to personalize management and guide timely referral to specialists.

Sodium–glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have shown consistent benefit in reducing risks of cardiovascular (CV) outcomes among patients with severe HF, regardless of EF. 5 More recently, the FINEARTS‐HF (Finerenone Trial to Investigate Efficacy and Safety Superior to Placebo in Patients With Heart Failure) trial demonstrated that the non‐steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) finerenone reduced the risks of CV death and total HF events in patients with HFmrEF/HFpEF, and specifically enrolled patients across the care spectrum including those who were actively or recently hospitalized. 6 Yet, there is an unmet need for effective and safe treatment with non‐steroidal MRA among patients with more severe stages of HF. In the FINEARTS‐HF trial, we examined the safety and efficacy of the non‐steroidal MRA finerenone in patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF, according to HF severity.

Methods

The FINEARTS‐HF trial

FINEARTS‐HF (NCT04435626) was a prospective, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, event‐driven trial that examined the efficacy and safety of finerenone compared with placebo in symptomatic patients with HFmrEF/HFpEF. The design, baseline characteristics, and primary results have been previously published. 6 , 7 , 8 The trial protocol was approved by the ethics committee at all participating institutions, and all patients provided written informed consent. Main inclusion criteria included age ≥40 years, symptomatic HF in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class II through IV, treatment with a diuretic for ≥30 days before randomization, and left ventricular EF (LVEF) ≥40% with evidence of structural heart disease (left atrial enlargement or left ventricular hypertrophy) measured within 12 months of screening. Patients were also required to have elevated natriuretic peptide levels (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide [NT‐proBNP] ≥300 pg/ml or B‐type natriuretic peptide [BNP] ≥100 pg/ml for patients in sinus rhythm, or NT‐proBNP ≥900 pg/ml or BNP ≥300 pg/ml for patients in atrial fibrillation) measured within 90 days in those with a recent HF event within 90 days of randomization or measured 30 days before randomization in those without a recent HF event. Both ambulatory and hospitalized patients were eligible for enrolment. Patients with previous LVEF <40% with subsequent improvement to ≥40% were also eligible for enrolment provided that ongoing HF symptoms were present and all other inclusion criteria were satisfied. Main exclusion criteria were estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <25 ml/min/1.73 m2, serum/plasma potassium >5.0 mmol/L at screening or randomization, or symptomatic hypotension with mean systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg at screening or randomization. A complete list of exclusion criteria is provided in the design article. Eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to finerenone or matching placebo. The starting dose was 10 mg once daily in participants with an eGFR ≤60 ml/min/1.73 m2 with a maximum maintenance dose of 20 mg once daily, whereas the starting dose was 20 mg once daily if the eGFR was >60 ml/min/1.73 m2 with a maximum maintenance dose of 40 mg once daily.

Trial outcomes

The primary outcome of the trial was the composite of total (first and recurrent) HF events (i.e. HF hospitalization or urgent HF visit) and CV death. We also assessed the components of the primary outcome, first HF event or CV death, first hospitalization for HF event or CV death, a composite kidney endpoint (defined as a sustained decline in eGFR ≥50% relative to baseline over at least 4 weeks, or sustained eGFR decline <15 ml/min/1.73 m2, or initiation of dialysis or renal transplantation) and all‐cause death. All deaths and potential primary outcome non‐fatal events were adjudicated by an independent blinded committee. Prespecified safety analyses included adverse events leading to discontinuation of trial treatment and adverse events of interest (i.e. elevations in serum creatinine, hyperkalaemia, hypokalaemia, and hypotension).

Definition of severe heart failure

Severe HF was defined according to previously published criteria in the 2018 ESC‐HFA advanced HF position statement. 4 We used the term ‘severe HF’ to distinguish the population of interest from patients in more advanced HF stages who may require heart transplant or mechanical circulatory support. Although numerous criteria for severe HF have been proposed in the past by scientific societies, we selected this definition as it uses simple and direct criteria to apply to a clinical trial population. Based on these criteria, patients were required to have all of the following: (i) NYHA functional class III to IV, (ii) evidence of HF with reduced ejection EF (HFrEF), HFmrEF or HFpEF according to ESC definitions, (iii) hospitalization for HF within the previous 12 months, and (iv) impairment of health status measured by the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire total symptoms score (KCCQ‐TSS, <75) (this criterion was adapted reporting evidence of impaired quality of life as a surrogate of impairment of exercise capacity 5 ).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics according to severe HF status were summarized as frequencies with percentages, means with standard deviation, or medians with interquartile range. Differences in baseline characteristics were tested using the chi‐square test for binary or categorical variables and the Wilcoxon test and two‐sample t‐test for non‐normally and normally distributed continuous variables, respectively.

We first analysed the prognostic impact of HF severity on clinical outcomes. Analyses were performed both unadjusted and adjusted for age, sex, geographic region, systolic blood pressure, body mass index, NYHA functional class, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, eGFR, LVEF, and use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, loop diuretics, and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, as well as treatment assignment (finerenone or placebo). In a third model, log‐transformed NT‐proBNP was additionally included. Selection of covariates for adjustment in model was guided by both clinical relevance and statistically significant differences detected when comparing baseline characteristics. Events rate were modeled using Poisson regression with patient follow‐up time as the exposure variable. Robust standard errors were used for recurrent event outcomes. To allow for potentially non‐linear associations, LVEF was expressed using restricted cubic spline terms, stratified by severe HF status. Models with 3 to 6 knots were tested, and the model with the lowest Akaike information criterion was selected. Next, we assessed the treatment effect of finerenone compared with placebo, with interaction terms for effect modification by severe HF status. The primary outcome and total HF events by severe HF status were compared using recurrent events analyses based on the Lin, Wei, Yang, and Ying model. Other outcomes were analysed using the stratified Cox proportional hazards model. The number of patient‐years (py) needed to treat to prevent one event was estimated as the inverse of the absolute risk reduction (expressed as patient‐years per event). All treatment effect analyses were stratified by geographic region and baseline LVEF (<60% or ≥60%), in accordance with the approach used in the FINEARTS‐HF protocol. 6 Finally, safety outcomes according to severe HF status were analysed using logistic regression models. Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 6001 patients enrolled in FINEARTS‐HF, 6000 patients had available data to assess severe HF status (one patient was excluded because of missing KCCQ‐TSS at baseline). Overall, 888 (14.8%) patients fulfilled the severe HF definition (362/2172 [16.7%] with EF 40% to ≤50%, and 526/3820 [13.8%] with EF ≥50%). Baseline characteristics stratified by severe HF status are shown in Table 1 . Patients with severe HF were older, more likely to be women and White, with a larger body mass index. They also had a higher burden of CV comorbidities including diabetes, atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease, and presented lower baseline LVEF, and higher plasma values of NT‐proBNP. Background medical therapy was balanced between groups except for diuretic use, which was more frequently used among patients with severe HF.

Outcomes according to severe heart failure status

Patients with severe HF had a higher risk for the primary endpoint of total HF events and CV death, compared to those without severe HF (unadjusted rate ratio [RR] 2.26, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.95–2.62) (Table 2 and online supplementary Figure Appendix S1 ), with an event rate of 31.6 (29.2–34.2) per 100py versus 13.9 (13.3–14.6) per 100py among patients without severe HF. Similar results were observed for all secondary clinical outcomes. The increased risk associated with severe HF status remained consistent after accounting for clinical covariates and baseline NT‐proBNP (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] for the primary endpoint 1.73; 95% CI 1.43–2.13). However, the associations with CV and all‐cause death were no longer nominally statistically significant after NT‐proBNP adjustment. The higher risk associated with severe HF was consistent across the whole spectrum of EF evaluated in the FINEARTS‐HF trial (Figure 1 ).

Efficacy of finerenone on clinical outcomes according to severe heart failure

Finerenone, compared with placebo, reduced the risk of the primary endpoint both in patients with severe HF (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.66–1.09) and without severe HF (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.73–0.98), with no evidence of an interaction between severe HF status and treatment effect (p interaction = 0.98) (Table 3 and Figure 2 ). The absolute treatment effect of finerenone was consistent for all the other outcomes examined regardless of severe HF status. The effect of finerenone on the primary outcome remained consistent across LVEF categories (40% to ≤50% and ≥50%) irrespective of severe HF status (online supplementary Table S1 ; 3‐way p interaction with continuous LVEF = 0.61).

Safety outcomes

Adverse events were slightly more common among patients with severe HF compared to those without. However, the proportions of patients who experienced any serious adverse event or discontinued study treatment for any reason according to treatment assignment were similar regardless of severe HF status (Table 4 ). There was no evidence of heterogeneity in treatment‐related rates of increase in serum creatinine, increases or decreases in serum potassium, and hypotension in patients with and without severe HF.

Discussion

In a large contemporary HFmrEF and HFpEF population enrolled in the FINEARTS‐HF, we found a significant proportion of patients with severe HF accounting for 15% of the study population. Severe HF patients experienced a higher risk of HF events or CV death, even after adjustment for known prognostic confounders. The non‐steroidal MRA finerenone reduced the risk of clinical outcomes regardless of severe HF status across the spectrum of mildly reduced or preserved LVEF (Graphical Abstract). While patients with severe HF experienced more adverse events, there was no observed heterogeneity in the safety profile of finerenone by severe HF status.

The prevalence of severe HF has traditionally been associated with patients with HFrEF, particularly those referred to surgical interventions such as left ventricular assist device or transplantation. 9 However, recent data have challenged this view. In DAPA‐HF and DELIVER, approximately 7% of trial participants met criteria for severe HF, with a relatively equal distribution across EF categories, including HFmrEF and HFpEF. 5 Similarly, population‐based data from the Olmsted County cohort reported that more than half of the population with severe HF had mid‐range or preserved EF, with a poor prognosis regardless of EF. 10 These results challenge the previous assumption that disease progression to a severe stage is confined to HFrEF. This shift in epidemiology is reinforced in global datasets such as REPORT‐HF, which underscore the growing burden of severe HF beyond classic HFrEF phenotypes. 11 While severe HF accounted for about one‐fifth of hospitalized HF patients, up to 20% fell in the spectrum of preserved EF. These findings emphasize the need for heightened clinical awareness and tailored therapeutic strategies for severe HF across the full spectrum of LVEF. Our results confirm and extend previous data in a well‐defined global HFmrEF/HFpEF population, with several implications given the rising prevalence worldwide and new available treatment options. Despite advances in HF treatment, 12 , 13 this condition continues to progress over time. As patients transition to more severe stages, they face an increasing symptom burden and recurrent hospitalizations, even with optimized medical therapy, accompanied by a persistently high risk of mortality. 14 , 15 This clinical trajectory imposes substantial economic strain, contributing to escalating healthcare costs.

While previous analyses from FINEARTS‐HF specifically focused on individual components of the HFA definition (such as NYHA functional class, 16 prior worsening HF events, 17 and baseline LVEF 18 ), each criterion captures only a single dimension of disease severity and may fail to identify patients progressing toward more severe stages of HF. Our study expands upon prior works by offering a broader evaluation of the safety and efficacy of non‐steroidal MRAs specifically in the context of severe HF. Given the higher baseline risk, we also found that the absolute rate reduction with finerenone appeared greater in those with severe HF (5.9 per 100py) compared with those without severe HF (2.2 per 100py). Importantly, our findings underscore the need for earlier recognition of patients with worsening disease trajectories, thereby enabling timely referral and the consideration of intensified or individualized therapeutic strategies beyond standard treatment. Although the identification of severe HF remains challenging and multiple classification systems have been proposed, the HFA's recent framework offers a pragmatic and clinically applicable approach that incorporates routinely available clinical data for HF patients in severe disease stages. Consistent with prior analyses, 5 , 19 we adopted the term ‘severe HF’ to describe this population, distinguishing it from advanced HF patients who might be eligible for durable HF therapies. Nevertheless, an important finding of our study was that the prognostic association of the HFA definition with mortality was attenuated after adjustment for NT‐proBNP. While this may in part reflect the lower number of fatal events, it also raises the question of whether natriuretic peptides should be formally incorporated into the multiparametric classification of severe HF. Future refinements of the definition may benefit from integrating biomarkers alongside clinical and functional parameters to more accurately capture disease severity.

Although international HF guidelines emphasize the need to implement foundational therapy in all patients with HF, those with severe HF may not tolerate recommended therapies. 20 Previous analysis showed that treatment with a SGLT2 inhibitor was safe and well tolerated, and study drug discontinuation and serious adverse events were not more frequently reported in the dapagliflozin group. 5 Our analysis further suggests that non‐steroidal MRA initiation was also well tolerated in patients with severe HF, without significant differences in adverse events compared to placebo. In line with recent suggestions advocating for an upfront, phenotype‐driven optimization of therapy in HFmrEF/HFpEF, 13 our findings support early initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors and non‐steroidal MRAs as core components of a pharmacological strategy even in the severe HF stages. The combined use of these agents may yield additive benefits by improving patient outcomes while reducing HF hospitalizations (and potentially attendant healthcare expenditures).

Taken together, these results highlight the need for early recognition of this severe HF phenotype who face high residual risks of mortality and morbidity and in whom the prompt initiation of foundational therapies and referral to a HF specialist may be beneficial.

Limitations

Our results are post hoc analyses of a clinical trial, and results should be considered hypothesis‐generating. The FINEARTS‐HF trial was not powered to evaluate efficacy and safety in patients with and without severe HF. An additional limitation is the potential for selection bias inherent to randomized trial populations. Inclusion and exclusion criteria in FINEARTS‐HF may have excluded patients with the most advanced or unstable clinical profiles, which could limit the generalizability of our findings to unselected real‐world populations. Of note, it should be noted that patients with NYHA class IV represented <5% of the overall study population. Therefore, conclusions regarding this subgroup must be interpreted with particular caution, as the trial was not powered to evaluate treatment effects specifically in this small subset. Future real‐world data will further strengthen the generalizability of our findings by including a broader HF population. Although there is no universally accepted definition for severe HF and the current HFA classification may misclassify some patients, it represents the most updated definition that has been adopted in previous analyses. 5 , 17 While alternative classification systems, such as the ‘I NEED HELP’ criteria, may provide additional granularity to differentiate patients with severe but stable HF from those with advanced or refractory disease, several key variables required for this framework were either not collected or constituted exclusion criteria in FINEARTS‐HF. A potential limitation of this framework is the lack of explicit consideration of natriuretic peptide levels. Our analysis indeed showed an attenuation of mortality associations of severe HF patients observed when NT‐proBNP was added to the models. Each criterion of the HFA definition relied upon documentation available in the trial record, potentially leading to misclassification. In our analysis, we substituted KCCQ‐TSS <75 as a patient‐reported measure of health status impairment in place of cardiopulmonary exercise testing or 6‐min walk distance, which were not systematically available in FINEARTS‐HF. This pragmatic adaptation is consistent with prior trial analyses, including a recent pooled analysis from DAPA‐HF and DELIVER, where KCCQ was similarly used to define severe HF. 5 While KCCQ‐TSS has been validated as a robust measure of functional limitation and prognosis in HF across EF phenotypes, we acknowledge that this substitution may not fully align with the original ESC‐HFA definition and carries a potential risk of misclassification. Nonetheless, the observed prevalence, clinical profile, and event rates of patients identified as having severe HF in our study closely mirror those reported in contemporary registries, supporting the external validity of this adapted approach.

Conclusions

In a large, global contemporary HFmrEF and HFpEF trial population from the FINEARTS‐HF trial, patients with severe HF experienced a higher risk of HF events and CV death. Finerenone consistently reduced the risk of clinical outcomes regardless of severe HF status, irrespective of LVEF category, and was safe and well tolerated. The data suggest that the non‐steroidal MRA finerenone may be effective and safe even among HFmrEF and HFpEF with severe HF stages for whom current treatment options might be limited or not well‐tolerated.
