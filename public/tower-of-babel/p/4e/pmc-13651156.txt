TREM2 in neurodegenerative diseases and acute neurological injuries: mechanisms to targeted therapies
Authors: Haixia Wang, Ruiming Wen, Emily Parker, Luodan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is a critical myeloid receptor expressed on the surface of central nervous system microglia, capable of integrating signals from lipids, damage-associated molecular patterns, and abnormal protein aggregates to regulate phagocytosis, metabolic adaptation, inflammatory remodeling, and pathology-associated responses. Accumulating evidence indicates that TREM2 is neither uniformly protective nor uniformly pathogenic; rather, its biological effects are highly context-dependent, governed collectively by disease stage, pathological substrates, cellular compartments, and the local microenvironment. By coupling with TYROBP/DAP12 or DAP10, TREM2 actively drives the state remodeling of pathology-associated microglia. It profoundly influences the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple sclerosis (MS), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), as well as acute central nervous system injuries, including ischemic stroke, spinal cord injury (SCI), and traumatic brain injury (TBI). Concurrently, soluble TREM2 (sTREM2) holds significant potential not only as a biomarker but also as a context-dependent effector molecule actively participating in pathological regulation. This review synthesizes current advancements by focusing on four core themes: the structural and signaling logic of the TREM2 axis; its regulation of disease-associated microglia (DAM) remodeling; the cross-disease significance of sTREM2; and the mechanistic basis for the divergent outcomes observed with TREM2-targeted therapies across different experimental models and disease stages. The objective is to elucidate the context-dependent roles of TREM2 by analyzing consensus mechanisms, sources of discrepancy, and translational implications, thereby providing a theoretical framework and strategic direction for more precise TREM2-targeted interventions.Keywords: TREM2, sTREM2, Central nervous system, Microglia, Neurodegenerative diseases, Acute nervous system injury

Background

Despite diverse clinical manifestations, neurological disorders share two fundamental hallmarks: sustained neuroinflammation and microglial dysfunction [1, 2]. As the primary innate immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), microglia maintain tissue homeostasis, yet their dysregulation drives neuronal damage and disease progression [3]. Consequently, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), a core regulator of microglial function, has emerged as a pivotal therapeutic target.

Expressed predominantly on microglia and peripheral myeloid cells, TREM2 extends beyond basic immune regulation to modulate neural metabolism, cell survival, and synaptic plasticity [4, 5]. Its deficiency impairs metabolic reprogramming, causing lipid accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction [6], whereas its activation attenuates neuroinflammation via NLRP3 inflammasome suppression [7]. Because neurological diseases involve complex, multifaceted pathologies refractory to single-target therapies, TREM2 represents a promising therapeutic nexus. Notably, TREM2 genetic variants strongly correlate with Alzheimer’s (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) risk, while its expression levels track with pathological severity and prognosis [8].

Crucially, TREM2 function is highly context- and stage-dependent. While its activation promotes myelin clearance and functional recovery in acute neural injury [9], TREM2 exhibits divergent roles in multiple sclerosis (MS), being protective in central microglia yet pathogenic in peripheral T cells. It also shows stage-specific effects in AD, transitioning from safeguarding neurons to exacerbating pathology [10]. Furthermore, soluble TREM2 (sTREM2) acts not only as a disease biomarker but also as a context-dependent extracellular effector that may regulate pathological processes through ligand binding, competitive modulation of membrane-bound TREM2 ligands, or non-canonical receptor-dependent mechanisms. Elucidating how TREM2 integrates spatiotemporal immune-metabolic signals is therefore vital for precision therapies.

Rather than cataloging isolated diseases, we organize the literature around four convergent themes: (1) the structural and signaling logic of TREM2; (2) its orchestration of disease-associated microglia (DAM) remodeling; (3) the cross-disease utility and mechanistic nuances of sTREM2; and (4) the mechanistic basis for the divergent outcomes of TREM2-targeted therapies. By addressing these aspects, we seek to clarify consensus mechanisms, explain discrepancies, and highlight priorities for translational development.

Overview of TREM2 biology

TREM2 gene and protein structure

The TREM2 gene is located at the 6p21.1 region of human chromosome 6 or the 17C3 region of mouse chromosome, with a total length of 4676 base pairs [11–13]. It contains five exons, with exon 1 encoding the signal peptide (SP), exon 2 encoding the immunoglobulin (Ig)-like V-domain (IgV) sequence, exon 3 encoding the short stem sequence and a partial transmembrane region, and exons 4 and 5 encoding the transmembrane helix and cytoplasmic tail [11–13]. TREM2 transcription generates four major transcripts [14–16], among which ENST00000373113 is the most highly expressed and functionally core full-length transcript, encoding a 230 amino acid transmembrane receptor protein, and serves as the primary executor of TREM2 function [17]. The other three transcripts all have exon deletions, such as ENST00000373122, with exon 5 missing and exon 4 with an alternative starting site, which encodes 222 amino acids. ENST00000338469 is absent exon 4, encoding the transmembrane region, which encodes 219 amino acids, and TREM2 Δe2 is absent from exon 2, encoding the immunoglobulin-like domain, ultimately producing non-functional receptors without ligand-binding ability. Currently, the in vivo translation status of these three transcripts remains unclear [16, 18] (Fig. 1).

The TREM2 protein, encoded by the full-length transcript, is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein within the immunoglobulin superfamily. Comprising 230 amino acids, it is predominantly expressed in myeloid cells and microglia of the CNS [17]. Its structure encompasses a SP, extracellular domain (ECD), transmembrane domain (TMD), and intracellular domain (ICD) [17] (Fig. 1). The N-terminal SP facilitates protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). The ECD features an IgV stabilized by disulfide bonds, essential for ligand binding, and a short peptide region (also known as the stem segment). The IgV domain binds diverse ligands, with the Asn79 site undergoing N-glycosylation, crucial for proper protein folding and ligand-binding efficiency [19]. The stem segment, though brief, connects ECD and TMD and can be cleaved by the A disintegrin and metalloproteases (ADAM) family ADAM10/17 to produce sTREM2, thereby terminating membrane-bound TREM2 (mTREM2) signaling [20]. The TMD is a single alpha-helical structure with conserved sequences, forming a heterodimer with adapter proteins DNAX activation protein 12 (DAP12, also known as TYROBP or KARAP)/DAP10 with negatively charged regions via positively charged lysine residues, enabling membrane anchoring. In addition, γ-secretase also cleaves this region for secondary processing [21]. In contrast to the complex ECD and TMD, the C-terminal cytoplasmic tail ICD lacks intrinsic signaling motifs and depends on DAP12 or DAP10 for downstream signal transduction. It can also be cleaved and released intramembranously by γ-secretase, and the biological functions of its released fragments are still under further investigation [22, 23].

Production of sTREM2

sTREM2 is an extracellular fragment shed from mTREM2 [22]. Its generation primarily relies on protease-mediated cleavage of the ECD, with alternative splicing contributing to aberrant transcript translation as a supplementary source [22]. In the cleavage pathway, three types of proteases play central roles with distinct cleavage sites. Firstly, the members of the α-secretase family, ADAM10 and ADAM17, act as the primary proteases for sTREM2 production, cleaving mTREM2 between histidine 157 and serine 158 (H157-S158) in the stem segment to release sTREM2 containing the complete IgV domain, which terminates mTREM2’s membrane signaling [24]. Secondly, meprin β protease functions as a supplementary cleaver, acting between arginine 136 and aspartic acid 137 (R136-D137) in the stem segment [25]. This site is closer to the IgV domain than that of ADAM10/17, resulting in a slightly shorter sTREM2 fragment, and the enzyme can further degrade the released sTREM2 to regulate its extracellular concentration. Thirdly, while γ-secretase does not directly generate sTREM2, it acts on the junction of the remaining TMD and cytoplasmic tail of mTREM2 after ADAM10/17 cleavage, mediating intramembrane cleavage to produce the TREM2 intracellular domain (TREM2-ICD). This clears residual mTREM2 fragments from the membrane and indirectly affects sTREM2 production efficiency [21]. The translation of aberrant transcripts is a secondary source of sTREM2. When TREM2 pre-mRNA undergoes post-transcriptional alternative splicing, a splicing variant is produced that translates into sTREM2, lacking the TMD and ICD [26]. Transcripts with missing transmembrane coding sequences due to exon skipping, like ENST00000373122 and ENST00000338469, may translate into sTREM2, with up to 25% of sTREM2 potentially generated by splicing variations in ENST00000338469 rather than TREM2 cleavage [18]. Moreover, TREM2 gene mutations may affect sTREM2 secretion [27], but uncertainty remains regarding whether the transcripts ENST00000373122, ENST00000338469, and TREM2Δe2 are translated in vivo (Fig. 2).

TREM2-related ligands and signal transduction

The ligands of TREM2 include both endogenous damage signals and exogenous pathogen components, which can be categorized into three main types. The first type consists of neuropathology-related ligands, such as β-amyloid (Aβ) oligomers, tau protein, apolipoprotein E (APOE), apolipoprotein J (ApoJ), and misfolded proteins such as TAR DNA-binding protein 43 kDa (TDP-43) [28, 29]. The second type comprises ligands associated with lipid metabolism and tissue damage, including high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), phosphatidylserine (PS), aminophospholipids, glycolipids, as well as lipid particles and dysfunctional mitochondria in adipose tissue [30, 31]. The third type consists of pathogen-related ligands, including anionic molecules such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), lipoteichoic acid (LTA), bacterial DNA, and fungal cell wall components on bacterial surfaces, which can be recognized by TREM2 on the surface of macrophages to initiate anti-infective immunity [32]. These ligands engage the IgV domain of TREM2 through partially overlapping hydrophobic and basic surfaces, with the hydrophobic interface contributing importantly to engagement of Aβ-related ligands and the basic surface participating in recognition of anionic phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine [33–35] (Fig. 3).

TREM2-associated ligands and signaling pathways. The TREM2 IgV domain recognizes neuropathology-related, lipid- or damage-associated, and pathogen-associated ligands. Canonical TREM2 signaling is primarily mediated by the TYROBP/DAP12 adaptor. Ligand binding induces DAP12 ITAM phosphorylation and SYK activation, subsequently engaging downstream signaling modules, including PLCγ-mediated calcium mobilization, PI3K/AKT cascades, MAPK pathways, and NF-κB-dependent inflammatory signaling. Meanwhile, DAP10 may facilitate PI3K/GRB2 recruitment or amplify TREM2/DAP12-dependent signaling rather than acting as an equivalent alternative adaptor. Collectively, these pathways regulate microglial phagocytosis, lipid metabolism, inflammation, survival, and responses to pathological deposits. Aberrant signaling leads to microglial dysfunction and impaired clearance of pathological aggregates

TREM2 signal transduction relies on complex formation with DAP12 and DAP10 [36]. When the ligand binds to TREM2, the transmembrane charge interaction between its TMD and DAP12 is activated, causing the immune receptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) in the intracellular segment of DAP12 to be phosphorylated by Src family kinases, which in turn recruits and activates spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) [36]. Subsequently, SYK further triggers downstream cascade reactions, including phospholipase Cγ (PLCγ)-mediated Ca²⁺ signaling. In specific experimental contexts, TREM2-SYK signaling has been reported to attenuate excessive mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappaB (NF-κB)-related inflammatory pathway activation [37, 38]. Moreover, activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway drives the conversion of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), as well as activation of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), Glycogen synthase kinase 3 Beta (GSK-3β)/mTOR, mTOR/hypoxia inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), and Forkhead box O1 (FOXO1), maintaining cell metabolism and survival [39–41]. Canonical TREM2 signaling relies on the TYROBP/DAP12 adaptor. Conversely, DAP10 can recruit the PI3K p85 subunit and adaptor protein growth factor receptor-bound 2 (GRB2) through its YINM motif to augment TREM2 activation responses [42]. Consequently, DAP10 functions as an auxiliary modulatory adaptor rather than a functional equivalent of DAP12. Additionally, DAP12 recruits docking protein 3 (DOK3), along with its associated GRB2 and son of sevenless 1 (SOS1), to inhibit the Ras-extracellular signal-regulated kinase (RAS-ERK) pathway [43]. The binding of the Src homology 2 (SH2) domain-containing inositol phosphatase-1 (SHIP1) to DAP12 can prevent PI3K from being recruited to DAP12, further regulating signal output [23]. Separately, sTREM2 can bind to transgelin-2 (TG2) on neurons, induce Ras homolog family member A (RhoA) phosphorylation, and inhibit the RhoA-ROCK-GSK3β pathway [44]. Collectively, these signals regulate the core functions of microglia, mediating their roles in maintaining CNS homeostasis and responding to pathological conditions. Specifically, TREM2 promotes microglial phagocytosis and plaque-associated clearance of pathological substrates such as Aβ, and it is also required for normal microglia-mediated synaptic pruning [35, 45, 46]. TREM2 further shapes inflammatory activation and maintains microglial metabolic fitness in response to tissue injury and protein aggregation [4, 37]. However, aberrant TREM2 signaling may cause microglial dysfunction, impairing the effective clearance of pathological deposits such as Aβ plaques or α-synuclein (α-syn) and ultimately leading to neuronal damage. Thus, a deeper understanding of the ligand recognition and signaling mechanisms of TREM2 is crucial for developing novel therapeutic strategies for CNS diseases.

TREM2-TYROBP/DAP12 signaling and complement

The critical function of the TREM2-TYROBP/DAP12 axis lies in translating pathological sensing into a coordinated microglial response. Under Aβ burden, impairment of this axis does not merely dampen reactivity; it severely limits peri-plaque microglial clustering, plaque compaction, and the transition to disease-associated states, highlighting its central role in pathology-driven microglial remodeling [47]. Current research further indicates that TREM2 signaling is not a uniform pathway. It encompasses a SYK-dependent component critical for plaque encapsulation and mature cellular responses, alongside a SYK-independent mechanism that partially sustains early APOE-associated pathological programs. This divergence explains why TREM2 activation yields inconsistent outcomes across different experimental models [47, 48]. Moreover, TYROBP/DAP12 is not an exclusive adapter for TREM2. It integrates into a broader myeloid receptor network and functionally couples with pathology-associated transcriptional programs such as APOE. Consequently, the biological effects of TREM2 are continuously shaped by parallel receptor signaling and the local tissue microenvironment [49, 50]. The complement cascade is best understood as an effector system that functionally intersects with the TREM2-TYROBP/DAP12 axis. While C1q and C3 tag vulnerable synapses and pathological debris for complement receptor 3 (CR3)-mediated microglial phagocytosis, TREM2 can directly bind C1q to restrain excessive complement-dependent synaptic pruning [51, 52]. Thus, the interplay between the TREM2-TYROBP/DAP12 axis and the complement system modulates not merely the magnitude of inflammation, but rather the precise microglial bias toward recognizing and clearing pathological substrates versus synaptic structures. This functional crosstalk likely provides the mechanistic foundation for the stage-dependent and bidirectional outcomes frequently observed following TREM2 manipulation.

TREM2 and the DAM program

The central role of TREM2 in the DAM program is not merely to enhance microglial activation, but rather to drive transcriptional reprogramming from a homeostatic signature to a pathology-associated state. Single-cell studies reveal that this process occurs in stages: an initial TREM2-independent transition followed by a TREM2-dependent DAM maturation phase. This stepwise progression indicates that TREM2 functions as a critical gatekeeper, dictating whether microglia successfully complete their programmed state remodeling [53, 54]. During this transition, the TREM2-APOE axis essentially mediates the exit from the homeostatic microglial program. It actively suppresses homeostatic gene expression while upregulating pathology-associated genes (e.g., Apoe, Lpl, and Clec7a). Consequently, microglia shift from maintaining neural homeostasis to mounting targeted responses against protein aggregates, lipid burden, and tissue damage [53, 54]. However, the DAM profile cannot be simply equated with a broadly protective phenotype, as its biological effects remain tightly constrained by disease stage, pathological context, and the local microenvironment. Transcriptomic studies of AD in both humans and mice demonstrate that TREM2 orchestrates the establishment of DAM-like programs while simultaneously modulating the response magnitude of distinct cellular subpopulations to local pathology [55, 56]. Therefore, TREM2 is more accurately defined as a fundamental regulator of state remodeling rather than a strictly pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory molecule. Consequently, emerging single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic evidence indicates that categorizing microglia using state-specific terminology, such as homeostatic, reparative, DAM, phagocytic, or inflammatory, captures TREM2-associated cellular heterogeneity and pathology-dependent dynamics far more accurately than the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy [57].

An explanatory framework for TREM2 situational dependence

The function of TREM2 cannot be binarily classified as purely protective or pathogenic. Instead, it must be evaluated through the framework of disease progression stage, primary pathological substrate, cellular and compartmental context, and the local signaling milieu. Disease stage serves as the primary determinant of its net biological effect. In early AD, for instance, TREM2 facilitates microglial clustering, plaque compaction, and metabolic adaptation. Conversely, during advanced stages characterized by self-sustaining chronic inflammation, continued enhancement of TREM2 signaling may amplify neuroinflammation and exacerbate pathology [58–60]. The dominant pathological substrate is equally critical, given that amyloid plaques, tau lesions, alpha synuclein aggregates, myelin debris, and ischemic necrotic tissue present distinct ligand landscapes and phagocytic demands. In PD, TREM2 mediated uptake of alpha synuclein may initially aid in clearance. However, under lysosomal stress, this same process can promote toxic processing and pathological spreading [61, 62].

Cellular and compartmental contexts further dictate TREM2 output. A prime example occurs in MS, where central microglial TREM2 predominantly drives myelin clearance and tissue repair. In stark contrast, elevated TREM2 within peripheral CD4 + T cells promotes pathogenic Th17 differentiation, thereby yielding an opposing and detrimental effect [63, 64]. Furthermore, the ultimate biological consequence of TREM2 activation is strictly regulated by the local signaling environment, encompassing the APOE program, TYROBP and DAP12 complement coupling, lysosomal pressure, and metabolic adaptation status [51, 53, 65]. Consequently, identical TREM2 activation can produce bidirectional outcomes, either driving repair or amplifying inflammation across different models and disease courses. Therefore, the key to understanding TREM2 lies not in defining it as universally beneficial or harmful, but in precisely identifying the specific stages, pathological contexts, and cellular compartments where it retains a net protective function [66].

To make this context-dependent framework operational in the disease-specific sections below, we interpret TREM2 function through five interrelated dimensions: disease stage, pathological substrate, cellular compartment, signaling bias, and therapeutic implication. Disease stage determines whether TREM2 activation occurs during an adaptive damage-clearing phase or within a chronically inflamed phase. The dominant pathological substrate defines the ligand landscape and the major microglial task required, such as plaque compaction, tau containment, α-synuclein processing, myelin debris clearance, or necrotic tissue removal. Cellular compartment further modifies the net outcome, because central microglial TREM2 and peripheral immune-cell TREM2 may produce opposing biological effects. Signaling bias determines whether TREM2 engagement preferentially drives DAP12/SYK-dependent lesion sensing and cellular clustering, PI3K/AKT/mTOR-dependent metabolic and lysosomal fitness, or inflammatory amplification. Finally, therapeutic implication emerges from the interaction of these dimensions, indicating that TREM2 enhancement, shedding modulation, or pathway inhibition may be beneficial only when matched to the appropriate disease stage, pathological substrate, and cellular compartment.

The role of TREM2 in neurodegenerative diseases

The role of TREM2 in neurodegenerative diseases has long been a key focus in studies of AD, PD, and related disorders (Fig. 4). Neurodegenerative diseases represent a significant and heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by progressive loss of neuronal structure and function, resulting in cognitive, motor, and other functional deficits. The subsequent sections discuss the mechanisms and implications of TREM2 across various diseases, aiming to provide novel perspectives for advancing our understanding of pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

TREM2 is closely associated with diverse neurological diseases. TREM2 is associated with neurodegenerative diseases and acute nervous system injuries such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple sclerosis (MS), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), epilepsy, Nasu Hakola disease (NHD), Huntington’s disease (HD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), ischemic stroke (IS), spinal cord injury (SCI), and traumatic brain injury (TBI). TREM2 can affect disease progression by regulating pathological changes in the disease

Alzheimer’s disease

The core pathological features of AD include senile plaques formed by the extracellular deposition of Aβ and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), resulting from the abnormal hyperphosphorylation and aggregation of tau protein. Microglia, as one of the innate immune cells in the CNS, play a key role in modulating AD pathological progression [67]. Mutations in exon 2 of TREM2 significantly increase the risk of late-onset AD [68], establishing TREM2 as one of the core targets for understanding AD pathogenesis. During early brain development, deficiency of TREM2 impairs microglial maturation and leads to altered synaptic connectivity [46]. Early upregulation of TREM2 has been shown to suppress pathological microglial activation and ameliorate disease phenotypes in AD [69]. Despite notable species differences, the activation of DAM and Aβ accumulation depend on TREM2 in both mouse and human AD, though its precise role remains under debate [54, 70].

TREM2 signaling in Aβ clearance and neuroinflammation

TREM2 participates in the recognition of amyloid-associated pathology through both direct binding to Aβ species and indirect sensing of plaque-associated lipids and lipoproteins [35, 45, 71]. Aβ oligomers have been reported to bind directly to TREM2, whereas plaque-associated anionic and zwitterionic lipids provide an additional route by which microglia detect and respond to amyloid lesions [28, 31, 35]. The extracellular IgV domain of TREM2 contains positively charged ligand-binding surfaces that can interact with anionic and zwitterionic lipids associated with fibrillar Aβ, thereby providing a lipid-dependent route for microglial recognition of amyloid plaques [28, 45, 72]. These findings indicate that TREM2 activation in AD is shaped not only by Aβ itself, but also by the lipid and damage-associated molecular environment surrounding amyloid plaques.

The composition of this ligand is particularly important because disease-associated TREM2 variants can weaken substrate recognition and alter downstream microglial responses. The salt bridge between residues R76 and D87 stabilizes TREM2 oligomers to preserve their function. However, AD-associated variants such as R47H, D87N, R98W, and R62H disrupt this salt bridge [73]. This disruption not only impairs TREM2’s recognition of lipid ligands and binding to several lipid-associated ligands, including phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylinositol (PI), and sulfatides [45], but also hinders the activation of microglial inflammatory responses triggered by sTREM2 [74], thereby exacerbating AD pathology. Beyond lipids, several additional molecules, including APOE, have been proposed as candidate endogenous TREM2-interacting ligands, although not all have been conclusively validated as bona fide physiological ligands [75]. The R47H variant has been reported to weaken APOE binding in experimental systems and is independently associated with increased AD risk, although a direct causal link between these observations remains unproven. Additionally, TREM2 can bind to other lipoproteins such as ApoA1, ApoA2, ApoB, and ApoJ [71, 76, 77]. Galectin-3 (Gal3) also acts as an endogenous ligand for TREM2, and it interacts with TREM2 via its carbohydrate domain, colocalizes tightly in microglial processes, and triggers TREM2-DAP12-dependent signaling to accelerate the clearance of Aβ-fibrils (fAβ) [78]. Beyond its reliance on TREM2 signaling, the effects of Gal3 may involve pathways such as Toll-like receptor (TLR) and Notch [79–82]. Therefore, these ligands should not be viewed as isolated TREM2 interactors; rather, they collectively define the pathological substrate environment that determines whether TREM2-mediated microglial activation is likely to be adaptive or maladaptive.

Activation of the APOE-TREM2 pathway induces microglia to transition from a homeostatic phenotype to DAM, resulting in the loss of their capacity to prevent neuronal loss and deliver tolerogenic signals to T cells [48]. In contrast, TREM2 genetic deficiency significantly inhibits the APOE pathway. This inhibition can restore microglial homeostasis in APP-PS1 and SOD1 mice, but blocks the microglia’s defensive functions during disease progression [83]. Impaired microglial function further hinders their clearance of Aβ. In human TREM2-knockout induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived microglia, reduced sensitivity to stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), decreased C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) expression, and increased caspase-3/7 activity impair migration toward amyloid-related cues [56]. Microglia from TREM2−/− 5XFAD mice exhibit abundant autophagic vesicles, elevated cleaved caspase-3 and LC3+ foci, and reduced autophagy substrate p62 [4]. This impairs mTOR activation, promotes AMPK activation, autophagy, and induces cell death, resulting in neuronal malnutrition near Aβ lesions [4]. Meanwhile, TREM2 deficiency downregulates genes linked to phagocytosis, lipid metabolism, IL1b, Il1rn, Gpnmb, and Vegfa, counteracting the protective effects of CD33 knockout [84, 85], indicating that inhibiting CD33 may also ameliorate AD. However, TREM2 deletion markedly reduces the lysosomal marker CD68 and the pro-phagocytic phenotype Itgax/CD11c, while downregulating Aβ response-related genes Itgax and Clec7a. This finding indicates that TREM2 deficiency impaired microglial immune responses and Aβ phagocytosis [86]. Therefore, membrane-bound TREM2 on plaque-associated microglia is important not only for Aβ uptake, but also for microglial migration, survival, lysosomal function, and plaque-associated state remodeling.

Moreover, TREM2 can promote microglial clearance of Aβ and preserve microglial competence through multiple downstream pathways, but these pathways are unlikely to contribute equally to AD pathology [87]. Mechanistically, the best-supported signaling hierarchy in AD centers on two principal axes. The first is the DAP12/SYK-centered pathway, which is most closely linked to plaque sensing, peri-plaque clustering, lesion engagement, plaque compaction, and progression into pathology-associated microglial states [35, 47, 88, 89]. As a key component of TREM2 signaling, SYK deficiency reduces the proportion of IFN-γ and DAM precursor TM2 clusters in 5×FAD mouse microglia. TREM2 supports microglial proliferation, Aβ plaque encapsulation, and DAM precursor progression via SYK-dependent and -independent pathways [23, 47]. The TREM2-DAP12 interaction is essential for triggering phagocytic mechanisms and is associated with activation of the SYK/PI3K/AKT/PLCγ pathway [90, 91]. The second is the PI3K/AKT/mTOR-centered pathway, which appears more directly related to metabolic fitness, lysosomal competence, survival, and sustained phagocytic function of plaque-associated microglia [23, 45]. Therefore, DAP12/SYK signaling primarily dictates whether microglia can recognize, cluster around, and engage amyloid pathology, whereas PI3K/AKT/mTOR-related signaling governs whether the engaged microglia can sustain the metabolic and lysosomal capacity required for prolonged plaque processing. Other pathways are best interpreted as modulators of this core response, rather than equivalent mechanistic drivers. By contrast, pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin and Mcl-1/Bim are more appropriately viewed as supportive or model-dependent survival modules rather than uniformly dominant signaling cores across all AD settings [92, 93]. Additional effects, including the attenuation of ferroptosis and the preservation of neuronal resilience, are biologically relevant but are better interpreted as downstream consequences of broader TREM2-dependent microglial adaptation rather than equivalent central axes of amyloid handling [45, 94].

This context dependence is reflected by apparently conflicting findings across AD models. In 5xFAD mice, TREM2 upregulation activates PI3K/AKT signaling, induces nuclear translocation of p-FOXO3a, reduces inflammatory cytokine expression, and decreases amyloid burden, whereas in APP/PS1 mice it also suppresses a pro-inflammatory microglial program, increases BDNF, and improves neuronal maturation and cognition [95, 96]. TREM2 further promotes metabolic adaptation by engaging PI3K-AKT-GSK-3β-mTOR signaling and DAP12/DAP10-dependent ERK regulation, thereby facilitating microglial aggregation around plaques and Aβ clearance [23, 47]. However, enhanced function is not uniformly beneficial, because the Trem2-IPD model, which reduces TREM2 shedding, accelerates early microglial maturation, drives multiple activated states, increases inflammatory gene expression, and ultimately worsens Aβ deposition and neuroinflammation [60]. These observations indicate that stronger TREM2 signaling may preserve lesion engagement while simultaneously intensifying inflammatory amplification, thereby creating the translational paradox that greater pathway activation does not inevitably produce better pathological outcomes.

Beyond these core pathways, several upstream regulators and metabolic modifiers further tune TREM2 output. MTOR activation upregulates TREM2, enhancing lysosomal activity and Aβ uptake [97]. Yin Yang 1 (YY1) and cGAMP-STING-IRF3 signaling have been demonstrated to support AD prevention and treatment by targeting TREM2 to reduce Aβ deposition and neuronal loss [98, 99]. Conversely, paraoxonase 1 (PON1) deficiency activates the TREM2 pathway, promoting microglial phagocytosis and exogenous Aβ clearance while reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [100]. In contrast, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and their metabolites, branched-chain keto acids (BCKA), compete with Aβ for TREM2 binding sites, leading to impaired response of TREM2 to Aβ and exacerbating high-fat diet (HFD)-induced AD pathology [101]. Similarly, microRNA-34a-driven TREM2 downregulation impairs amyloid clearance mechanisms [102], and reduced TREM2 also triggers neuroinflammatory responses via the Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated MAPK signaling pathway, exacerbating Aβ accumulation and periplaque microglial proliferation [103]. These findings suggest that inhibiting BCAA/BCKA or enhancing TREM2 and mTOR activity may represent potential therapeutic strategies for AD. Although metabolic and inflammatory contexts reshape TREM2 output, the core SYK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR programs remain essential for plaque-associated microglial adaptation.

sTREM2 itself regulates microglial function and influences AD, and it has been identified as a potential biomarker for mild cognitive impairment (MCI)-to-AD progression [104, 105]. These findings suggest that cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sTREM2 may reflect stage-related microglial activation and immune remodeling during the AD continuum. Lacking the requisite transmembrane and intracellular domains, sTREM2 cannot directly initiate canonical TREM2/DAP12 signaling. However, it still functions as an extracellular effector to mediate non-canonical biological responses. By competitively binding ligands, sTREM2 acts as a decoy receptor to modulate ligand availability for membrane-anchored TREM2 [106, 107]. Furthermore, sTREM2 suppresses microglial apoptosis by activating PI3K/AKT-driven AKT/GSK3β/β-catenin signaling, while simultaneously increasing NF-κB p65 serine 536 phosphorylation to dose-dependently upregulate proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) [74]. Consequently, the biological roles of sTREM2 must be distinctly differentiated from canonical membrane-bound TREM2/DAP12 signaling.

However, sTREM2 has little effect on anti-inflammatory microglial markers such as Arg-1 and Ym-1, precluding comprehensive regulation of microglial transcriptional programs [74, 108]. The TREM2 H157Y mutation increases TREM2 shedding, raising brain and serum sTREM2 levels. Functionally, this mutation accelerates Aβ clearance, reduces Aβ load, ameliorates malnourished neural processes and gliosis, and downregulates neuroinflammation-related genes and amyloid-associated immune modules [109]. Additionally, sTREM2 levels are positively regulated by MS4A4A [110], while the effects of DAP12, ADAM10, or ADAM17 on CSF sTREM2 remain unclear. Thus, targeting MS4A4A or other regulators of TREM2 shedding may be a promising therapeutic direction, but future strategies should focus on tuning the membrane-bound TREM2/sTREM2 balance according to disease stage, ligand environment, and desired microglial output, rather than simply increasing or decreasing sTREM2.

Accumulating evidence indicates a predominantly protective role for TREM2 during the early, plaque-associated phase of AD, wherein it drives microglial clustering, lipid sensing, metabolic fitness, and the local containment of amyloid pathology. However, chronic or excessive activation within an established inflammatory milieu may fail to confer sustained benefit, instead amplifying inflammatory cascades without commensurate gains in pathology clearance. Conversely, TREM2 deficiency compromises phagocytic competence, plaque compaction, and microglial adaptation. Thus, rather than acting as a uniformly beneficial or detrimental factor, TREM2 operates as a state-dependent regulator whose net impact is dictated by temporal dynamics, pathological substrate, cellular compartment, signaling bias, the ligand microenvironment, gene dosage, and microglial activation status. This distinction provides a critical foundation for understanding the subsequent tau-related findings, wherein the outcomes of TREM2 manipulation are highly dependent on amyloid copathology, disease stage, and the inflammatory microenvironment.

Effects of TREM2 changes on tau pathology

The role of TREM2 in tau pathology is best interpreted according to the pathological background in which tau lesions emerge. In amyloid-containing models, TREM2 may indirectly limit tau accumulation and spread by improving plaque containment and reducing amyloid-associated neuronal stress. In pure tauopathy models, however, TREM2 activation may mainly influence microglial proliferation and inflammatory tone rather than tau clearance itself. Thus, the apparent controversy in the tau literature largely reflects differences in amyloid co-pathology, disease stage, TREM2 gene dosage, sex, brain region, and the inflammatory state of microglia [111]. In most tau mouse models, including those with TREM2 silencing [112], haploinsufficiency [113, 114], and homozygous deletion [115, 116], TREM2 deficiency exacerbates tau pathology and its spread, whereas TREM2 overexpression attenuates these pathological changes [117]. Compared with complete deficiency (TREM2−/−), TREM2 haploinsufficiency (TREM2+/−) more significantly impairs microglial responses to damage in aged mice, exacerbates tau pathology and fibrillary tau formation [113], and disrupts microglial envelope around early amyloid fibrils [118]. In PS19 mouse models of tauopathy, complete TREM2 deficiency attenuates microglial activation and neuroinflammation, without significantly altering phosphorylated tau levels [114]. Notably, the TREM2’s real impact on tau lies in early stages, rather than solely late-stage tau levels. The “tau reduction” observed in late stages may be misleading, as it fails to account for tau spread caused by early TREM2 deficiency. The state of microglia in the early stages is a key determinant of late-stage tau pathology. Furthermore, TREM2 deficiency exerts a gene dose-dependent effect on AD, making it hard to predict whether increasing TREM2 gene dose alters microglial transcriptome responses broadly or subtly.

Similarly, the function of TREM2 is highly dependent on pathological context, with its effects varying across different disease backgrounds. In mouse models with Aβ pathology, TREM2 can promote Aβ clearance by binding to ligands such as APOE or CLU/APOJ, limit Aβ-driven tau accumulation and spread, and reduce brain atrophy [71, 119]. However, long-term TREM2 activation via antibodies exacerbates microglial activation around plaques. Notably, long-term TREM2 activation does not reduce Aβ plaque burden but instead worsens abnormal tau pathology and periplaque dystrophy [120]. These findings suggest that during Aβ deposition and the initiation of tau pathology, sustained TREM2 activation may induce the DAM phenotype. Nevertheless, it exacerbates Aβ-mediated tau pathology via mechanisms unrelated to tau phagocytic degradation. In pP5-183 mice without Aβ pathology or PS19 mice with pure tau lesions [119, 121], TREM2 deficiency does not affect tau accumulation, propagation, phosphorylated tau levels, or insoluble tau levels. It only significantly reduces microglial proliferation in the hippocampus and piriform cortex, and lowers inflammatory cytokine expression. This aligns with findings in APOE-deficient PS19 mice, where reduced inflammation and neurodegeneration were detected [122]. These results indicate that TREM2 dysfunction in pure tau lesions exerts protective effects solely by inhibiting neuroinflammation, and TREM2 may suppress tau pathology by regulating Aβ-related pathways.

Furthermore, pathogenic TREM2 mutations have been found to exert specific and gender-dependent effects on tau pathology. For example, in the P301S tauopathy model, human microglia carrying the TREM2 R47H mutation activate pro-inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB) and upregulate proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α), modulate the IL2-STAT5 pathway, enrich DAM, and exacerbate APOE upregulation [123]. Notably, this mutation only accelerates tau-related AD pathological processes in female mice, with no adverse effects on males, an outcome potentially mediated by sex differences in microglial transcriptomes [124–126]. When combined with APOE4, the TREM2 R47H variant further aggravates tau hyperphosphorylation in the frontal cortex of female tauopathy mice, activates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and downstream interferon responses triggered by tau lesions in microglia, and upregulates cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and B cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) associated X protein (Bax)-dependent senescence to drive neurodegeneration [127]. Interestingly, the TREM2R47H variant only increases AT8-positive tau phosphorylation in the mouse cortex [128], with no impact on MC1-positive tau in the hippocampus [123, 129]. In human AD, males and females exhibit distinct disease-induced pathways, reflecting gender-specific responses to similar pathological states. Nevertheless, male and female mice with tau lesions exhibit responses that deviate from the gender-specific patterns observed in humans. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differing degrees of cognitive impairment, and further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying association.

TREM2 exerts a brain region-specific effect on AD. In human postmortem AD brain tissue, TREM2 levels positively correlate with AD diagnosis, cognitive decline, and Aβ neuropathy. In contrast, its levels in the caudate nucleus show no correlation or even a negative correlation with AD pathology [130]. These differences may arise from variations in neuronal subtypes, microglial functions, AD pathological loads across brain regions (e.g., cortex, hippocampus and caudate nucleus), and differential transcriptional regulatory activity of TREM2, which modulates its expression and pathological associations. In addition, TREM2 regulates p-tau via sTREM2. sTREM2 has been reported to bind to the neuronal membrane receptor TG2, thereby inducing RhoA phosphorylation at Ser188. Inactivated RhoA fails to activate the downstream effector ROCK, ultimately reducing p-tau at AD-relevant sites, such as Ser202, Ser396, Thr181, and Ser404 in neurons, and thereby affecting microglia-neuron interactions [44]. Together, these findings indicate that TREM2 effects on tau pathology are substrate-dependent, involving amyloid-associated microglial remodeling, inflammatory regulation in pure tau settings, and sTREM2-mediated neuronal TG2-RhoA-ROCK signaling.

Collectively, the prevailing consensus establishes that TREM2 drives plaque-associated microglial adaptation by facilitating lesion sensing, metabolic resilience, cellular clustering, and the local containment of amyloid pathology (Fig. 5). The central debate no longer concerns the fundamental importance of TREM2, but rather focuses on delineating the precise contexts in which its activation remains protective versus when sustained signaling becomes maladaptive, particularly within tau-driven, late-stage, or chronically inflamed milieus. Apparent discrepancies across the literature likely stem from inherent variations in pathological background, intervention timing, receptor dosage, and the specific experimental models employed (spanning murine, human, and in vitro platforms). Therapeutically, TREM2 activation may be most beneficial during early amyloid-dominant stages with preserved lysosomal capacity, whereas caution is needed in tau-dominant, late-stage, or chronically inflamed settings. Future translational efforts must therefore transcend binary assessments of TREM2 function, prioritizing instead the identification of specific temporal and microenvironmental windows wherein TREM2-directed modulation can confer optimal therapeutic benefit.

The mechanism underlying TREM2-mediated regulation of PD and AD. In AD, TREM2 binds ligands like APOE to activate PI3K/AKT, SYK, Wnt/β-catenin, and ERK pathways, regulating microglial Aβ clearance and inhibiting tau phosphorylation. Its function, shaped by disease stage or microenvironment, shows a stage- and microenvironment-dependent effect. In PD, TREM2 mediates microglial α-syn phagocytosis, activates autophagy, and protects dopaminergic neurons by inhibiting NF-κB, NLRP3, TLR4, JNK, and MAPK pathways, while TREM2 loss exacerbates pathology and inflammation. Notably, depending on the disease stage, α-syn burden, lysosomal stress, and experimental model, its role may be either protective or maladaptive

Parkinson’s disease

PD, the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, has core pathological features, including the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and the formation of Lewy bodies, with abnormal α-syn aggregation being the main component of these Lewy bodies [131]. In recent years, TREM2 variants have shown a strong correlation with PD, have been established as PD risk factors [132, 133], and play complex yet critical roles in PD pathogenesis.

Effects of TREM2 changes on α-syn

In α-syn preformed fibrils (α-syn PFF)-based microglial and mouse PD models, TREM2 directly mediates the endocytosis of α-syn fibrils and can facilitate early-stage clearance of α-syn pathology. However, in the later stage, internalized α-syn PFF is cleaved by AEP in lysosomes to generate toxic N103 fragments after C-terminal truncation, thereby exacerbating α-syn pathological spread [134, 135]. Knocking out TREM2 inhibits this endocytic process and alleviates α-syn PFF-induced PD-like motor deficits [61]. While AEP knockout does not affect phagocytosis, it blocks N103 fragment production and reduces pathological α-syn spread. Collectively, TREM2 and AEP form a key link in promoting α-syn pathological progression [61]. In the context of PD, TREM2 defects exert adverse effects. For instance, inhibiting TREM2 expression exacerbates α-syn-induced microglial inflammation, upregulates TNF-α, IL-1β, iNOS, and COX2 expression, and drives microglia toward a pro-inflammatory state [136, 137]. TREM2 knockout mice also exhibit worsened α-syn diffusion pathology and increased loss of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons in the substantia nigra [138]. Besides, TREM2 deficiency indirectly impairs microglial proliferation and their phagocytic capacity for α-syn, disrupting interactions with α-syn monomers or PFF. Decreased TREM2 expression has been associated with altered AKT/mTOR/ULK1 phosphorylation and impaired autophagic responses [138]. Concurrently, TREM2 deficiency compromises lysosomal membrane integrity, induces hydrolase inactivation, and drives ERK1/2/TFEB-mediated lysosomal dysfunction, thereby impairing α-syn PFF degradation, accelerating α-syn phosphorylation, and promoting neuronal injury [62]. Conversely, TREM2 upregulation increases p-ULK1 levels, enhances microglial autophagic responses, and suppresses proinflammatory cytokine release. Collectively, these findings suggest that TREM2-mediated autophagy and mTOR-associated alterations should be interpreted as integrated components of a broader microglial metabolic, phagolysosomal, and inflammatory adaptation rather than a simple linear cascade. Nonetheless, miR-3473b directly targets the 3’-UTR of TREM2 to suppress its expression [139], indicating the miR-3473b/TREM2/ULK1 pathway may be a potential therapeutic target for PD. It is worth mentioning that knocking down discoidin domain receptor 1/2 (DDR1/2) reduces TREM2 levels, thereby lowering α-syn, tau, and Aβ levels to clear neurotoxic proteins [140]. Here, TREM2 appears to play a detrimental role, and these conflicting findings highlight the complexity of the TREM2-α-syn interaction, which requires further investigation. Collectively, these findings indicate that TREM2 should be viewed as an orchestrator of the microglial α-syn response rather than a singular autophagy-related regulator. In α-syn-based PD models, TREM2-dependent uptake is linked to microglial proliferation and phagocytic capacity, while PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling likely sustains microglial metabolic fitness, survival, and continuous phagosomal degradation. When autophagic and lysosomal capacities are preserved, this response facilitates the clearance of pathological α-syn. However, under severe lysosomal stress or advanced α-syn burden, TREM2-mediated uptake may paradoxically promote toxic α-syn processing and pathological propagation. Therefore, the net impact of TREM2 on α-syn pathology is contingent upon disease stage, cellular context, α-syn burden, and lysosomal competency.

Effects of TREM2 changes on dopaminergic neurons

TREM2 can reduce dopaminergic neuron loss by inhibiting inflammation-related pathways [7]. On the one hand, it first suppresses the expression of TLR4 and myeloid differentiation factor-88 (MyD88) to prevent the activation of the NF-κB pathway. Subsequently, TREM2 inhibits the activation of the NLR Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which reduces the expression of inflammatory factors (IL-1β and IL-18) and apoptosis factors (GSDMD and GSDMD-N). These effects alleviate excessive microglial activation and the consequent loss of dopaminergic neurons [7]. On the other hand, TREM2 can block the TLR4-dependent activation of Toll adaptor proteins (MyD88, IRAK-1, and TRAF-6) [141]. This further reduces the phosphorylation levels of NF-κB, p38, and JNK, thereby inhibiting 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced loss of TH-positive neurons in the substantia nigra and striatum and improving dopaminergic neurodegeneration [142]. In a neuronal repair context, TREM2 overexpression has been reported to activate the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway, promote iPSC differentiation toward dopaminergic neurons, and increase dopaminergic markers such as TH and DAT, suggesting a potential role in dopaminergic lineage repair [143]. This mechanism should be distinguished from microglia-centered inflammatory and autophagic responses. In LPS- or MPTP-related inflammatory PD models, TREM2 activation has been associated with increased LC3 expression, reduced p-p38 and p-mTOR levels, enhanced autophagic responses, and suppression of microglial inflammation, thereby indirectly protecting dopaminergic neurons [144]. Conversely, TREM2 deficiency causes detrimental effects. For example, it impairs the phosphorylation of STAT6, thereby downregulating anti-inflammatory marker expression, including ARG1 and YM1, in microglia. This dysregulation drives the phenotypic shift of microglia from an anti-inflammatory to a pro-inflammatory state, ultimately accelerating dopaminergic neurodegeneration in AAV-SYN PD mouse models [136]. Notably, a contradictory finding from another study shows that in MPTP-induced PD mouse models, TREM2 deficiency fails to alter dopaminergic neuron loss but instead reduces Iba-1, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels [145]. These discrepancies may arise from variations in pathological driving mechanisms, neurotoxic action modes, and microglial functional demands between the two PD models. Furthermore, sTREM2 expression exhibits differences across pathological subtypes. In human CSF cohorts, no significant difference in sTREM2 levels has been observed between healthy controls and patients with PD, indicating limited utility for primary diagnosis. However, sTREM2 decreases in α-syn pathology, increases in cases with CSF tau pathology, and remains unchanged in PD subgroups with positive CSF Aβ biomarkers [146]. This finding suggests that sTREM2 can be used as a predictive factor for detecting neuronal damage, microglial activity, and sleep disorders [147, 148], and such subtype-specific differences are closely associated with neuronal damage patterns and the status of the neuroimmune microenvironment.

Current evidence indicates that TREM2-mediated regulation of PD should be conceptualized as a context-dependent microglial response rather than a simplistic linear TREM2/autophagy/mTOR pathway. Depending on the experimental model, disease stage, cell type, and specific manipulation, TREM2 signaling may intersect with PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation, microglial metabolic fitness, survival, phagocytic capacity, or altered autophagic and lysosomal responses (Fig. 5). Recent discussions regarding the spatiotemporal regulation of TREM2-mediated microglial responses in PD further emphasize that the functional outcomes of TREM2 are dictated by α-syn pathology, neuroinflammatory status, disease stage, and the timing of therapeutic intervention [149]. Therefore, the central mechanistic debate surrounds α-syn handling, as TREM2-dependent uptake may confer early-stage benefits via enhanced clearance, yet paradoxically drive pathogenic processing under conditions of severe lysosomal stress. Such discrepancies largely stem from divergent experimental designs, including the choice of toxin-based versus α-syn seeding paradigms, the pathological stage analyzed, and the intrinsic lysosomal burden of the model. Future investigations must therefore deconvolute the distinct effects of TREM2 on α-syn uptake, lysosomal degradation, inflammatory signaling, and neuronal survival, rather than conceptualizing these processes as a monolithic axis. In this sense, PD exemplifies a substrate- and lysosome-dependent paradigm of TREM2 contextuality, wherein the very same TREM2-mediated uptake mechanism can be beneficial when degradation is efficient, but becomes detrimental when the α-syn burden exceeds lysosomal processing capacity.

Multiple sclerosis

MS is a chronic demyelinating disease driven by chronic inflammation of the CNS [150]. This inflammation causes myelin loss and impairs the ability of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) to differentiate into mature oligodendrocytes (OLs), ultimately leading to extensive accumulation of myelin debris and axonal damage [150]. Microglia play a key role in the clearing of myelin debris, a process that is critical for activating OPCs, recruiting them to demyelinated lesions, and supporting their differentiation into mature OLs [151]. As a core surface receptor on the microglia, TREM2 also contributes to the pathological regulation of MS.

TREM2 is widely expressed on myelin-rich macrophages within activated MS plaques, monocytes in human CSF, and microglia in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [152, 153]. It directly facilitates the clearance of myelin debris by enhancing phagocytosis and promoting a reparative, debris-clearing microglial state [152, 153]. Additionally, TREM2 acts synergistically with receptors such as CR3 and signal regulatory protein (SIRP) to sustain efficient phagocytosis [154]. In TREM2-knockout microglia, upregulated levels of p-JNK and p-eIF2α trigger ER stress and shut down protein translation [155, 156]. This not only prevents cholesterol ester production but also reduces triacylglycerol (TAG) generation. Ultimately, it impairs lipid droplet biosynthesis and myelin regeneration, leading to insufficient migration of microglia and inadequate myelin debris phagocytosis [155, 156].

During the myelin regeneration stage, acute blockade of TREM2 depletes microglia with high expression of the transcription factor Musculoaponeurotic Fibrosarcoma Oncogene Homolog B (MAFB) [157]. This exacerbates symptoms in experimental MS models (e.g., EAE) and causes defects in OL maturation [63]. In the Cuprizone (CPZ)-induced demyelination model, TREM2−/− mice show reduced numbers and proliferation of microglia, impaired myelin debris clearance, and marked accumulation of cholesterol esters (CE) and oxidized CE [158, 159]. While TREM2+/− mice can maintain microglial expansion, they fail to fully clear large amounts of myelin debris, leading to reduced OPC recruitment.

Notably, the TREM2 agonist AL002a activates TREM2 both in vitro and in vivo. This activation allows TREM2 to sense lipid components released upon myelin damage [160], upregulate microglial activation markers (Iba1 and CD80) and phagolysosome activity markers (CD68 and LAMP1), promote OLs generation from OPCs, and improve the efficiency of myelin phagocytosis and degradation [161]. Similarly, intravenous injection of TREM2-transduced myeloid precursor cells can reduce axonal injury and demyelination in EAE by promoting IL-10 production and p-ERK, inhibiting the transcription of TNFα, IFNγ, IL-1β, and NOS2, and clearing cell debris [162]. Furthermore, TREM2 accelerates the clearance of oxidised phosphatidylcholines (OxPCs), a therapy preventing MS-related neurodegeneration [163]. Together with chitotriosidase (CHIT1) and chitinase 3-like-1 (CHI3L1), it serves as a marker of microglial heterogeneity, and the phagocytosis it mediates is key to maintaining CNS homeostasis [164]. These studies are consistent in identifying TREM2 as a potential new target for promoting myelin regeneration. Nevertheless, further research is needed to clarify whether the benefit of TREM2-mediated myelin regeneration at different pathological stages stems from accelerated debris clearance, enhanced inflammation resolution, or increased OPC recruitment.

Loss-of-function mutations affecting the TREM2-TYROBP/DAP12 signaling axis in humans can result in severe neurodegenerative phenotypes characterized by microglial dysfunction, white matter abnormalities, and progressive neurological decline [165–167]. However, TREM2 also exerts pathogenic effects. Most research on TREM2 has focused on myeloid cells, especially microglia in the CNS. There is little research, though, on its role in T helper (Th) cells of the peripheral immune system. A recent study reported increased TREM2 expression in peripheral CD4+ T cells from patients with MS and in EAE mice [64]. Mechanistically, TREM2 enhances p-zeta-chain-associated protein 70 (ZAP70) and STAT3 phosphorylation, upregulates IL-6, IL-17, IL-22, and IL-23R, and thereby promotes pathogenic Th17 differentiation [64, 168]. These pathogenic Th17 cells further produce IL-17, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and GM-CSF, which contribute to blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption and aggravation of neuroinflammation in MS [169].

In addition, the level of sTREM2 in the CSF of MS patients is elevated and exhibits a significant positive correlation with T cell activation, axonal injury (NfL and pNfH), disability, disease severity, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and inflammatory cytokines (IL-8, G-CSF, and IL-5) [170–172]. This correlation reflects the central inflammatory status [170–172]. In contrast, no difference in serum sTREM2 levels has been observed among MS patients [152]. Currently, there is no direct correlation between sTREM2 and patient gender, MS progression risk, or MRI structural measurement indicators (e.g., cortical thickness, T2 lesion burden, and cerebellar volume). Overall, sTREM2 only reflects disease-related inflammatory characteristics. Although sTREM2 cannot be used for MS diagnosis, it appears to modulate MS pathogenesis by blocking the binding of membrane-bound TREM2 to myeloid cells, which in turn inhibits anti-inflammatory function [152].

In the context of MS, prevailing consensus establishes that TREM2 orchestrates protective CNS myeloid functions crucial for myelin debris clearance, lipid metabolism, and remyelination. However, a critical layer of complexity arises because TREM2 actions extend beyond microglia; peripheral immune compartments (particularly pathogenic T cells) may exert opposing, disease-promoting effects. Furthermore, divergent findings across EAE, Cuprizone, and human lesion studies highlight that the relative dominance of phagocytosis, inflammation resolution, and oligodendrocyte support fluctuates profoundly by model and disease stage. Consequently, delineating compartment- and cell-type-specific TREM2 signaling represents an urgent priority, essential for developing interventions that selectively harness central repair mechanisms without exacerbating peripheral immunopathology (Fig. 6). In short, MS provides a clear example of compartment-dependent TREM2 biology, wherein central nervous system myeloid TREM2 may support repair, whereas peripheral T-cell-associated TREM2 potentially exacerbates autoimmune inflammation. Therapeutic strategies for MS should aim to enhance central debris clearance and myelin-regeneration-associated TREM2 activity, while concurrently avoiding the activation of pathogenic peripheral immune cell programs.

The mechanism underlying TREM2-mediated regulation of MS and ALS. In MS, microglial TREM2 activates PI3K/ERK pathways to promote myelin debris phagocytosis and a reparative microglial state to support myelin regeneration, while CD4+T cell TREM2 drives Th17 differentiation via ZAP70/STAT3, exacerbating inflammation and blood-brain barrier damage. In ALS, TREM2 may regulate lipid metabolism in pathology. Its p.R47H mutation is a risk factor, and NLRP3 inhibitors reduce TREM2 cleavage to improve motor deficits

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a multigenic, multifactorial progressive neurological disorder with heterogeneous clinical manifestations. It arises from the degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons in the motor cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord [173]. Mutations in ALS-associated genes (e.g., SOD1, TARDBP, FUS, and C9orf72) can exacerbate neuroinflammation [174]. However, the role of TREM2 variants in ALS remains debated. Among these variants, the TREM2 rs75932628 T variant shows no significant association with ALS [175, 176]. In contrast, the TREM2 p.R47H variant is a well-recognized risk factor for sporadic ALS. This variant can interfere with the binding of TREM2 to unknown extracellular ligands or disrupt signal transduction via DAP12 [177].

At the pathophysiological level, progressive gray matter demyelination occurs in the SOD1-G93A ALS model [178]. TREM2 has been implicated in ALS-related microglial and disease-associated responses, but its specific role in demyelination and lipid metabolic remodeling remains insufficiently resolved [179]. Notably, anti-miR-124 treatment can enhance TREM2 gene expression and prevent neuronal dysregulation in ALS mice [180]. The Poly-Gly-Ala (poly-GA) protein promotes ADAM10-mediated cleavage of TREM2. Meanwhile, the NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor MCC950 can reduce this cleavage and alleviate motor deficits. This finding suggests that targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome to preserve TREM2 represents a potential strategy for ALS prevention [181].

Regarding the expression levels of TREM2 and sTREM2, TREM2 levels are increased and correlate with disease progression in human ALS cohorts [182]. Most studies have shown that sTREM2 levels in the CSF and serum of ALS patients are significantly higher than those in control groups [182]. Both CSF and serum sTREM2 levels are independently associated with ALS diagnosis [183]. The elevation of CSF sTREM2 is most notable in the early stages of ALS. Higher sTREM2 levels in the later stages are also linked to slower disease progression, implying potential beneficial effects of sTREM2 [184]. However, some studies have found no difference in CSF sTREM2 levels between control groups, mimic patients, and ALS patients [185]. This discrepancy may arise from differences in study design, including the subtypes of ALS (sporadic vs. Familial), disease subtypes, inclusion/exclusion criteria, disease stages, and detection methodologies. Given the high heterogeneity of ALS, these factors are likely to collectively influence study outcomes (Fig. 6).

For ALS, reproducible findings primarily center on altered TREM2 expression profiles and the utility of sTREM2 as a disease-tracking biomarker, while definitive mechanistic insights remain scarce. Although emerging data implicate TREM2 in microglial lipid handling and inflammatory adaptation, the ALS literature remains notably more heterogeneous and conceptually nascent than that of AD or MS. Inconsistencies across clinical cohorts likely reflect profound biological heterogeneity (spanning familial versus sporadic etiologies, diverse genetic architectures, and variable sampling windows). Advancing the field therefore requires moving beyond correlative observations to definitively establish whether TREM2 functions merely as an epiphenomenal marker of microglial activation, an active modifier of disease trajectory, or a viable therapeutic target within specific ALS subpopulations.

In conclusion, across AD, PD, MS, and ALS, the most robust conclusion is that sTREM2 should not be interpreted as an etiology-specific diagnostic marker, but rather as a context-sensitive readout of myeloid activation and tissue injury. In AD, the evidence is strongest for stage-sensitive dynamics, supporting its value as an indicator of evolving microglial responses during disease progression. In PD, by contrast, the signal appears less robust for primary diagnosis and may more often reflect concomitant tau-related pathology or general neuronal injury than pure α-synucleinopathy. In MS, the most reproducible finding is that CSF sTREM2 tracks central inflammatory activity and axonal injury, whereas serum findings and disease specificity remain less consistent. In ALS, altered sTREM2 levels also show disease-tracking potential, but their interpretation remains more equivocal because of marked cohort heterogeneity, including differences in genetic background, clinical subtype, disease stage, and sampling strategy. Collectively, these cross-disease patterns support a unified interpretation of sTREM2 as a dynamic barometer of neuroimmune activation, while also underscoring that its clinical utility depends strongly on pathological context, biospecimen source, and stage of disease.

Other neurological diseases

Epilepsy

Epilepsy is characterized by abnormal neuronal hyperexcitability and discharge, and TREM2 is deeply involved in its pathological process. Under both pathological and physiological conditions, partial or complete loss of TREM2 renders mice highly sensitive to spontaneous epileptic seizure activity, increases seizure frequency, and triggers associated pathological alterations [186, 187]. Furthermore, TREM2 knockout inhibits the morphological transformation of microglia from a ramified to an amoeboid-like state following epileptic seizures. It also reduces microglial proliferation and numbers, and impairs their phagocytic clearance of apoptotic neurons. These effects further promote epileptic seizures [188]. Additionally, in Tg2576 mice, TREM2 deficiency, particularly complete ablation, leads to excessive production of transgenic Aβ and a reduction in the hippocampal theta frequency [186]. However, TREM2 deficiency exerts no significant effect on the coupling of hippocampal θ-γ amplitude. From the perspective of anti-epileptic effects, the TREM2 and PI3K/AKT pathways are often inhibited in epilepsy models. In contrast, TREM2 overexpression activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, and therapy can increase the level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and decrease the levels of the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and caspase-3. These changes collectively inhibit hippocampal neuronal apoptosis, mitigate oxidative stress, and alleviate pilocarpine-induced seizures in mice [189]. Notably, inhibiting the interaction between methyl-CpG-binding domain protein 3 (MBD3) and DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) reduces the hypermethylation of TREM2 at the cg25748868 locus, upregulates TREM2 levels, and further activates the PI3K/AKT pathway. These actions alleviate hippocampal neuronal damage and suppress acute epileptic seizures [190]. This finding indicates that targeting the TREM2/PI3K/AKT pathway represents a novel approach for ameliorating epileptic seizures (Fig. 7).

The mechanism underlying TREM2-mediated regulation of epilepsy, NHD, HD, and FTD. In epilepsy, TREM2 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to inhibit neuronal apoptosis and reduce seizures. Its methylation is regulated by MBD3/DNMT1, while TREM2 deficiency exacerbates pathology by suppressing microglial proliferation and promoting Aβ plaques. NHD arises from TREM2 mutations (e.g., Q33X, W50C, Y38C, T66M, and V126G), causing protein dysfunction and microglial impairment. TREM2 expression in HD correlates with cognition, and TREM2 p.H157Y mutation in FTD worsens brain atrophy and cognitive damage

Nasu Hakola disease

Nasu Hakola disease (NHD), also known as polycystic lipomembranous osteodysplasia with sclerosing leukoencephalopathy (PLOSL), is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by progressive threatened dementia, bone cysts, myelin loss, and basal ganglia calcification. Its pathogenesis is strongly linked to homozygous pathogenic mutations in the TREM2 or DAP12 genes, accompanied by primary microglial dysfunction [191]. Studies have verified that NHD-associated mutations are heterogeneous and involve both TREM2 and TYROBP/DAP12, with multiple pathogenic variants reported across different families [165, 192, 193]. This mutation introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in non-functional proteins that trigger lysosomal dysfunction, Aβ deposition, and microglial activation dysregulation [194]. Additionally, a homozygous T-to-C substitution (c.482 + 2T > C) at the splicing donor site in intron 3 of the TREM2 gene causes exon 3 skipping and truncated protein expression [195]. A novel mutation in exon 2 c.244G > T (p.W50C) has also been identified as the cause of NHD phenotype [196]. Notably, four highly pathogenic TREM2 mutations (Y38C, W50C, T66M, and V126G) alter the ECD conformation by modifying hydrogen bonding within and between CDR1 and CDR2 loops, ultimately impairing TREM2 ligand binding [197]. Whether mutations occur in TREM2 or DAP12, NHD patients may experience impaired activation and dysregulated inhibition of microglia, contributing to pathological changes such as myelin sheath defects. This further supports that NHD is a cognitive impairment disorder caused by primary microglial dysfunction [191] (Fig. 7).

Huntington’s disease

Huntington’s disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a pathogenic CAG repeat expansion in the HTT gene [198]. Elevated levels of TREM2 and TLR4 have been detected in the brain tissue of HD patients, and the TREM2 R47H variant has been linked to cognitive decline in this population [199]. In contrast, another study has indicated that the concentration of TREM2 in the CSF of HD patients remains unchanged [200]. The previously observed increase in CSF TREM2 concentration in HD patients was a result of aging rather than the disease itself [200]. The specific mechanism underlying TREM2 function in HD has not yet been clarified [200]. Therefore, TREM2 is unlikely to act as a biomarker for HD progression. However, its potential as a pharmacodynamic target biomarker for ameliorating microglial dysfunction cannot be ruled out. This also does not deny its role in the pathogenesis of HD or the importance of immune dysfunction as a pathogenic pathway (Fig. 7).

Frontotemporal dementia

The pathogenesis of frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is associated with TREM2 mutations. Reports have shown that compound heterozygous mutations of TREM2 are linked to FTD [201, 202]. Compared with the healthy control group and the FTD group without the TREM2 mutation, FTD patients carrying the TREM2 heterozygous p.H157Y mutation exhibit earlier behavioral changes, more severe impairment of cognitive and executive functions, and increased brain atrophy in FTD-related brain regions, including the frontal lobe, temporal lobe, parietal lobe, basal ganglia, and hippocampus. Additionally, some of these patients present with reduced gray matter in the basal ganglia and thalamus, as well as extensive TDP-43 type B pathology [203]. These findings suggest that the TREM2 p.H157Y mutation exacerbates neurocognitive disorders and brain atrophy (Fig. 7).

sTREM2 as a biomarker across neurodegenerative diseases

sTREM2 is more accurately understood as a fluid readout of microglial activation and neuroimmune engagement rather than an etiology-specific diagnostic biomarker [204–206].

In AD, CSF sTREM2 exhibits stage-dependent elevations during the transition from the preclinical to the early symptomatic phases. For autosomal dominant AD, this increase occurs after the emergence of biomarkers indicating amyloid deposition and neuronal injury [207]. CSF sTREM2 typically correlates more strongly with total and phosphorylated tau than with CSF Aβ42 [206]. Higher baseline CSF sTREM2 is associated with attenuated cognitive and clinical decline, slower Aβ accumulation, and reduced future neurodegeneration risk linked to the APOE ε4 allele [208–210]. These findings argue against interpreting sTREM2 as a purely detrimental signal. Genetic evidence indicates that MS4A locus variants modulate CSF sTREM2 levels and correlate with altered AD risk and onset age [110]. Longitudinal analyses further reveal dynamic CSF sTREM2 trajectories across the AD continuum, reinforcing its utility as a stage-sensitive state marker rather than a static cross-sectional classifier [105, 211].

In PD, the biomarker utility of sTREM2 is highly conditional and subgroup-dependent. CSF sTREM2 does not consistently elevate in unstratified cohorts, limiting its frontline diagnostic value [146]. Conversely, elevations are more pronounced in biomarker-defined subgroups exhibiting abnormal tau profiles or subsequent cognitive decline [147, 212]. This suggests that in PD, sTREM2 likely reflects concomitant neuronal injury and cognitive vulnerability rather than pure α-synucleinopathy.

By contrast, evidence in MS is more robust. CSF sTREM2 is reproducibly elevated, decreases following natalizumab or mitoxantrone therapy, and correlates with markers of inflammation and axonal injury [171, 213]. These dynamics support its role as an indicator of active central neuroinflammation rather than a standalone diagnostic tool. Additional analyses substantiate the association between elevated CSF sTREM2 and MS risk or inflammatory activity, although its incremental value over existing biomarkers remains to be established [214].

In ALS, evidence supporting sTREM2 as a biomarker is accumulating but remains less mature than in AD and MS. Although TREM2 or sTREM2 levels can be elevated in CSF, blood, or spinal cord tissue, and higher CSF sTREM2 correlates with upper motor neuron burden and prognosis [182, 183], data interpretation is hindered by substantial cohort heterogeneity. Variations across sporadic versus familial cases, clinical subtypes, disease stages, and sampling strategies currently preclude its use as a broadly applicable independent diagnostic marker [182, 183].

For other neurological disorders, fluid biomarker evidence for sTREM2 is sparse and highly variable. In FTD, CSF sTREM2 is not consistently elevated in unstratified cohorts and is thus unsuitable as a universal diagnostic marker [215]. However, it can increase in GRN-related familial FTD and shows a faster age-dependent rise in CHMP2B-related cases [216, 217]. In HD, age-adjusted CSF sTREM2 lacks stable elevation and does not significantly correlate with motor, functional, cognitive, or brain volume metrics, precluding its utility for disease monitoring [200]. Although direct biomarker studies in NHD remain limited, individuals harboring TREM2 variants linked to NHD exhibit reduced CSF sTREM2 levels, as these mutations impair TREM2 maturation, membrane expression, and proteolytic cleavage [27, 204]. Therefore, within the context of this rare monogenic disorder, sTREM2 is most appropriately utilized as a mechanistic readout of functional deficits resulting from these genetic alterations. Furthermore, regarding epilepsy, current genetic epidemiological analyses do not support a robust association between genetically determined CSF sTREM2 levels and disease risk [214].

In essence, CSF sTREM2 currently demonstrates the strongest biomarker utility in AD and MS, where it functions as a stage and state-sensitive indicator. In PD, it serves primarily as a conditional marker of concomitant neuronal damage and cognitive vulnerability. It shows emerging potential for disease monitoring in ALS, while evidence in FTD, HD, NHD, and epilepsy remains sparse, highly subtype-dependent, or premature.

The role of TREM2 in acute nervous system injury

Acute nervous system injury is triggered by sudden factors that lead to impaired neurological function. It has a complex pathogenesis and causes serious consequences, and it has long been a challenge in the field of neuroscience research. As a key molecule with neuroprotective properties, TREM2 exerts an undeniable effect on the progression of acute nervous system injury. Across acute nervous system injuries, the best-supported functions of TREM2 converge on four processes: phagocytic clearance, inflammatory reshaping, lipid handling, and tissue repair.

Ischemic stroke

In experimental ischemic stroke models, intact TREM2 function is critical for post-stroke recovery [218]. TREM2 is highly expressed in activated microglia following stroke, where it plays a pivotal role in maintaining microglial phagocytic capacity [219]. Therefore, the intact function of TREM2 is essential in preventing the accumulation of cholesterol esters (CE) and the aberrant formation of lipid droplets. Conversely, TREM2 deficiency impairs microglial phagocytosis, lipid metabolism, and the clearance of ischemic tissue, thereby exacerbating brain injury [219]. Additionally, TREM2 deficiency downregulates the transforming growth factor beta1 (TGF-β1)/Smad2/3 signaling pathway, promoting a pro-inflammatory microglial state [10]. In terms of regulating microglial function, microglial subpopulations with increased abundance after IS upregulate TREM2 and its downstream signal insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) [220]. This upregulation enhances oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and phagocytic activity [220]. Concurrently, the lysed brain cells release nucleic acids into the extracellular region, which can act as risk factors and bind to TREM2 on the surface of adjacent microglia, thereby exerting a protective mechanism for nerve repair after ischemic injury [219]. However, the specific type and sequence of nucleic acids that bind to TREM2, as well as more detailed signaling processes involved, have not yet been confirmed.

Both the APOE mimetic peptide COG1410 and enriched environment activate TREM2, which subsequently promotes a reparative microglial state [221, 222]. This phenotypic transition inhibits neutrophil infiltration, thereby reducing neuronal apoptosis, brain edema, and neuroinflammation following IS [221, 222]. These effects are primarily mediated by activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, accompanied by upregulation of Bcl-2 and downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and the pro-apoptotic protein Bax [221, 222].

When TREM2 interacts with its ligands sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and FTY720 (Fingolimod), it promotes the translocation of ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) from lysosomes to the cell membrane in microglia [223, 224]. This translocation accelerates cholesterol efflux and reduces lipid droplet accumulation [223, 224]. It also provides cholesterol to OLs, supports myelin regeneration, and alleviates ischemia-induced neuronal and white matter damage. Notably, the absence of TREM2 abolishes these effects [223, 224]. Additionally, the METTL14-mediated YTHDF2/AX6 signaling axis inhibits TREM2, shifting microglia toward a pro-inflammatory state and exacerbating IS severity [225]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting TREM2-S1P/FTY720 interactions or silencing METTL14 hold promise for mitigating neuroinflammation in ischemic stroke.

Beyond microglia, TREM2 expression is significantly upregulated in astrocytes following acute ischemic stroke. In astrocytes, TREM2 reduces ischemia-induced neuronal apoptosis and facilitates astrocyte phenotypic switching from neurotoxic to neuroprotective states [226]. This protective effect is mediated by enhanced Kruppel-like transcription factor 4 (KLF4) and suppressed NF-κB phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, ultimately alleviating cerebral ischemic injury [226].

In the hyperacute phase of IS, elevated plasma sTREM2 levels may serve as a compensatory mechanism by promoting an inflammation-resolving microglial response, recruiting anti-inflammatory factors, and clearing apoptotic debris, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation [227]. However, sTREM2 concentrations decline sharply in the post-acute phase, making it difficult to counteract the sustained high inflammatory state, leading to chronic inflammation, and subsequent sustained decline may be associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, sTREM2 functions as a biomarker of microglial activation, with elevated levels reflecting aggravated neuroinflammation [228]. It may also act as a decoy receptor, competitively binding TREM2 ligands and inhibiting TREM2-mediated neuroprotection [228]. Nevertheless, further research is needed to elucidate sTREM2 dynamics and its role in subacute and chronic ischemic stroke phases.

In contrast, following intracerebral hemorrhage, overexpressed TREM2 suppresses TLR4-mediated activation of NF-κB and MAPK (ERK and p38) signaling pathways, drives microglial polarization toward an anti-inflammatory state, and reduces acute-phase injury [38] (Fig. 8). A limitation here is the lack of research on the impact of TREM2 on long-term disease outcomes.

The mechanism underlying TREM2-mediated regulation of IS, SCI, and TBI. In IS, TREM2 activates PI3K/AKT and inhibits TLR4 and NF-κB to promote a reparative microglial state, and binds FTY720 to support OLs maturation and myelin regeneration. TREM2 can also boost KLF4 in astrocytes to reduce damage. In SCI, TREM2 inhibits apoptosis and neuroinflammation via PI3K/AKT/FOXO1, while its silencing enhances lysosomal function to facilitate recovery. In TBI, TREM2 activates AKT/CREB, LXR, and mTOR pathways to suppress inflammation and neuronal apoptosis and alleviate BBB damage

Spinal cord injury

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe acute neurological condition. While research on TREM2 in SCI remains limited, TREM2 exerts effects via multiple pathways, including regulation of inflammatory responses, cell pyroptosis, myeloid cell distribution, and lysosomal function. TREM2 is upregulated in BV2 cells treated with serum, CSF, and LPS from SCI patients [229, 230]. It reduces the release of inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby mitigating microglial inflammatory responses post-SCI. Silencing miR-665 relieves the targeted inhibition of TREM2 to further enhance this protective effect [230].

In the context of spinal cord ischemia-reperfusion injury, TREM2 overexpression promotes p- FOXO1 by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [41]. This reduces the levels of Bax, GSDMD, IL-1β, and cleaved caspase-1, increases Bcl2 expression, inhibits cell pyroptosis, attenuates spinal cord ischemia-reperfusion injury-induced microglial overactivation, and improves neurological deficits [41]. Therefore, the TREM2/PI3K/AKT and miR-665/TREM2 axes may serve as potential targets to alleviate cell pyroptosis and inflammation.

Regarding the regulation of myeloid cell distribution and function, ablation of TREM2 can reduce the aggregation of P2RY12⁺ purine-sensitive microglia at the injury edge to promote ATP metabolism and protect neurons [229]. In addition, TREM2 deficiency decreases the phagocytic phenotype of infiltrating macrophages (MF-Infls), which is beneficial for SCI [229]. This finding suggests that TREM2 blockade therapy targeting MF-Infls may have benefits in acute nerve injuries like SCI. However, the role of TREM2 in the presence and phenotypic transformation of microglia and MF-Infls during chronic nerve injury remains unclear.

In terms of lysosomal function, after SCI onset, the expression of TREM2 and lysosomal membrane permeability-related genes (Cd68, Ctsd, and Ctsb) increases, and TREM2 and the lysosomal markers LAMP1 are highly expressed at the injury site boundary [231]. Trem2−/− mice show enhanced SCI recovery by activating autophagy and lysosome-related pathways, accompanied by better motor function [231]. Silencing Trem2 enhances TFEB nuclear translocation by reducing SYK phosphorylation, which restores lysosome membrane permeability [231] (Fig. 8). Overall, further research is needed to clarify TREM2’s differential effects in acute and chronic SCI phases, considering variations in the injury microenvironment and cellular targets. The regulatory mechanism of TREM2 on myeloid cells in the chronic phase should not be ignored to facilitate TREM2-related therapies in SCI.

Traumatic brain injury

In traumatic brain injury (TBI), TREM2 operates through multiple pathways, with its expression and function showing phased patterns [232]. Fourteen days post-TBI, TREM2 levels rise notably, aligning with the upregulation of microglial marker IBA-1 and astrocyte marker GFAP. Alongside TYROBP, Clec7a, and Cd68, TREM2 also serves as a core hub in the TBI-driven immune response gene network [232].

Mechanistically, TREM2 binds and activates the APOE mimetic peptide COG1410 or APOE, boosting p-Akt, p-CREB, BDNF, and Bcl-2 expression, while suppressing TNF-α, IL-1β, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 levels [233]. These changes reduce BBB damage, facilitate cerebral blood flow recovery, inhibit microglial activation and neuronal apoptosis, and improve cortical impact-induced TBI [233]. Conversely, TREM2 inhibits delta24-dehydrocholesterol reductase activation of the liver X receptor (LXR) signaling pathway. This inhibition promotes cholesterol ester efflux from microglia, enhances their phagocytic activity, reduces OLs apoptosis, and mitigates white matter damage post-TBI [234]. Additionally, during the acute phase of TBI, TREM2 maintains microglial energy metabolism via the AKT/mTOR/HIF1α pathway, regulates glycolysis balance, and drives homeostatic microglia to mature DAM-like cells. These DAM-like cells exhibit stronger phagocytic ability and secrete BDNF and VEGFA to foster vascular repair [40]. However, it should be noted that administering TREM2 agonists in TREM2−/− TBI model mice yields no positive effect, likely due to the TREM2 gene knockout and the weak therapeutic impact of simple agonists. Contradictory findings also exist. For example, TREM2 knockout mice showing strong macrophage activation in the lesion area and reduced hippocampal atrophy, improving TBI pathological outcomes in both acute and chronic phases [235] (Fig. 8). TREM2’s effects may vary due to differences in TBI model injury type and degree, pathological stage, microglial activation, functional regulation, and downstream signaling pathway activation influenced by factors like APOE subtypes.

Therapeutic strategies involving the TREM2 axis

Given the critical role of TREM2 in neurological diseases, therapeutic strategies involving the TREM2 axis should be classified according to their degree of direct TREM2 engagement and level of evidence. Direct TREM2-targeting approaches include TREM2 agonist antibodies, engineered TREM2-activating antibodies with enhanced CNS delivery, shedding-modulating or receptor-stabilizing antibodies, and TREM2 gene delivery or functional restoration. In contrast, small molecules, natural compounds, exercise, dietary modulation, and environmental interventions are better regarded as indirect TREM2-associated strategies unless direct target engagement or TREM2-dependent causality has been demonstrated. In these indirect interventions, changes in TREM2 expression or signaling may function as mechanistic contributors, downstream pathway changes, or pharmacodynamic readouts rather than definitive evidence of direct TREM2 targeting (Table 1). Therefore, the key translational challenge is not simply whether TREM2 should be increased or decreased, but rather which type of TREM2-related intervention is appropriate for a specific disease stage, pathological context, cell type, and level of clinical evidence.

Direct TREM2 activation

Direct TREM2 activation primarily encompasses TREM2 agonist antibodies and engineered TREM2-activating antibodies with enhanced CNS delivery, which bind to TREM2 and amplify receptor signaling.

AL002

AL002 is an excitatory anti-human TREM2 monoclonal antibody. The excitatory anti-human TREM2 monoclonal antibody binds to the ECD activation signal of TREM2 spanning amino acids 112–174 to promote microglial proliferation, reduce amyloid deposition, and improve microglial inflammatory response [236]. It has shown good safety and tolerability in human phase I clinical trials without serious adverse events or drug toxicity [237]. Mechanistically, following TREM2 activation, AL002 decreases the level of CSF sTREM2, upregulates the expression of its downstream signal SPP1, and increases the levels of colony-stimulating factor-1 receptor (CSF1R) and interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL1RN) in CSF [238]. However, in the Phase 2 INVOKE-2 trial for early-stage AD although AL002 maintained sustained pharmacodynamic activity, it failed to achieve its primary endpoint of slowing clinical decline [239]. These results reveal a critical disconnect between TREM2 target engagement and tangible clinical efficacy. Consequently, there is an urgent mandate to improve patient stratification, clarify disease-stage intervention windows, and identify predictive downstream biomarkers for future TREM2-directed therapies.

Ab-T1

The Ab-T1 monoclonal antibody binds with high affinity to the ECD domain of TREM2 and accelerates the clearance of Aβ and apoptotic neurons by enhancing SYK and Akt phosphorylation. Importantly, Ab-T1 can also improve cognitive abilities in female 5xFAD mice [241].

ATV: TREM2

ATV: TREM2 is an engineered TREM2-activating antibody with enhanced CNS delivery. It actively translocates into the CNS through transferrin receptor (TfR)-mediated transport and enhances p-SYK activity, thereby improving microglial metabolism and phagocytosis in a TREM2- and PLCG2-dependent manner. These effects further promote brain glucose metabolism and improve AD-related outcomes [242].

Engineered bispecific antibodies

Antibody engineering optimization can enhance TREM2 activation efficiency. For example, increasing the valency of Ab18 and Ab2 using tetra-variable domain immunoglobulin (TVD-Ig) promotes TREM2 aggregation on the microglial surface without interfering with TREM2 ligand interactions, thus improving their phagocytic efficiency towards oligomeric amyloid-β (oAβ). To address central delivery issues, bispecific antibodies targeting TREM2 and TfR (e.g., Ab18-TVD-Ig/αTfR and Ab2-TVD-Ig/αTfR) have been developed. These antibodies increase brain antibody levels via TfR transport, reduce plaque burden and tau hyperphosphorylation in 5XFAD mice, and alleviate cognitive impairment [243, 244].

Species-specific antibodies

In the TREM2R47H-5XFAD mouse model, systemic administration of the anti-human TREM2 mAb crosses the BBB, promotes cell cycle re-entry and Cyc-M amplification, induces terminal IFN-R populations, but does not alter the transcriptional profiles of terminal cell types. Furthermore, treatment with its murine version (mT2AB) drives microglial production of large amounts of chemokines (CCL4 and CXCL10) and IL-1β, leading to enhanced microglial activation. Notably, anti-human TREM2 mAb induces more pronounced effects in male than female mice [245].

Although agonist antibodies exhibit promising beneficial effects in neurodegenerative diseases and brain injury, certain limitations persist in specific types of these antibodies. For instance, particular TREM2 agonistic antibodies (Para. 09, mAb5 and mAb7) only promote microglial proliferation in AD models. They have no beneficial effects on microglial pathological response ability, pathological burden, or neurodegenerative indicators, nor do they activate DAM features or alter amyloid and tau accumulation. In the MS demyelination model, TREM2-activating antibodies even impair myelin regeneration [266]. These antibodies face challenges, including easy inactivation by repeated in vitro stimuli, incomplete engagement of central targets, and background-dependent effects, making it hard to achieve effective therapeutic effects by activating the TREM2 pathway. Although some differences can be attributed to antibody design, the deeper reason for these variations may lie in the phenotypic diversity and complex activation characteristics of microglia themselves.

The heterogeneous efficacy of TREM2 agonist antibodies indicates that receptor engagement alone is insufficient to guarantee therapeutic benefit. Rather, the outcome of TREM2 agonism is also shaped by the pre-existing TYROBP/DAP12-complement signaling milieu and the stage of microglial state remodeling. At least five non-mutually exclusive factors likely underlie these divergent outcomes. First, epitope selection and antibody valency influence whether TREM2 is productively clustered, whether receptor shedding is reduced, and whether downstream signaling is biased toward phagocytic adaptation or inflammatory amplification. Second, CNS delivery and target occupancy are critical, as antibodies incorporating blood-brain barrier transport strategies show stronger central pharmacodynamic effects than antibodies with limited brain exposure. Third, disease stage and the pre-existing microglial state are likely decisive, because metabolically adaptable, plaque-responsive microglia may respond fundamentally differently from chronically inflamed, transcriptionally exhausted, or demyelination-associated myeloid populations. Fourth, pathological context matters: an amyloid-rich environment, a tau-dominant milieu, and a demyelinating lesion provide distinct ligand landscapes, metabolic constraints, and effector demands, thereby altering the consequences of TREM2 activation. Fifth, species background, disease genotype, and biological variables such as sex may further shape treatment responses, as suggested by model-specific and sex-biased effects observed in some studies. Collectively, these findings suggest that the central translational challenge is no longer whether TREM2 can be activated, but rather when, where, and in which microglial state such activation remains beneficial rather than maladaptive.

TREM2 shedding modulation

TREM2 shedding modulation aims to preserve membrane-retained TREM2 signaling by limiting proteolytic release of sTREM2. This strategy should be distinguished from conventional receptor agonism because it does not simply activate TREM2, but instead stabilizes the membrane receptor pool and alters the balance between membrane-bound TREM2 and sTREM2. Monoclonal antibody 4D9 is better classified as a shedding-modulating or receptor-stabilizing antibody rather than a conventional agonist antibody. It blocks α-secretase-mediated shedding of the TREM2 stalk region, thereby enhancing membrane-retained TREM2 signaling. In APP-NL-G-F mice and primary mouse microglia, 4D9 enhances p-SYK signaling, promotes microglial uptake of myelin debris and Aβ peptide, reduces cortical amyloid plaque area, and facilitates microglial transition toward disease-associated states [240]. Thus, 4D9 represents a strategy that modulates TREM2 processing and receptor stabilization rather than direct agonistic activation alone.

TREM2 gene restoration

TREM2 gene restoration strategies aim to restore or enhance TREM2 expression directly. These approaches are more direct than small molecules or lifestyle interventions because they deliver TREM2 cDNA, TREM2 plasmids, or TREM2-modified cells. However, their current evidence remains largely preclinical, and long-term safety, cell-type specificity, delivery efficiency, dose control, and sustained expression require further validation.

Whole-body hematopoietic cell transplantation can boost microglial replacement and restore TREM2 function [267]. Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and progenitor cells (HSPCs) are delivered via the CNS, thereby allowing HSPCs transduced with human TREM2 cDNA to preferentially colonize, expand, and differentiate into microglial-like cells in the CNS [257]. These cells target AD-related Aβ deposition areas like the cortex and hippocampus, enhancing Aβ phagocytic degradation to reduce brain Aβ deposition, ultimately improving mouse behavioral and histopathological damage with good safety [257]. Biomimetic exosome-liposome hybrid nanovesicles, called TSEL, exploit the targeted homing properties of exosomes and angiopep-2 peptides to penetrate the BBB. Once across, they aggregate and release TREM2 overexpression plasmid (pTREM2) and siBACE1 in AD lesion areas, transforming microglia from the pro-inflammatory to the anti-inflammatory phenotype for synergistic AD gene therapy [258]. Besides, the gene delivery system PHSA@PF/PTREM2 has a core of fluorinated polyethyleneimine with TREM2 plasmid and an outer layer of human serum albumin with specific molecules. Through the albumin shedding effect, it achieves long circulation and low toxicity. After crossing the BBB and entering the lysosome, the outer shell detaches for PF/pTREM2 gene transfection, upregulating TREM2 in microglia and promoting a reparative microglial program. It reshapes the inflammatory microenvironment, enhances Aβ clearance, and improves cognitive function in APP/PS1 mice [259]. Although these approaches directly restore or enhance TREM2 expression, their current evidence remains largely preclinical. Long-term safety, cell-type specificity, delivery efficiency, dose control, and sustained expression require further validation before clinical translation.

Indirect pharmacological modulation

Small molecules and natural compounds should generally be considered indirect TREM2-associated modulators. While these interventions may regulate TREM2 expression, downstream signaling, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, pyroptosis, or microglial state transitions, their actions should not be automatically interpreted as direct TREM2 targeting.

Small molecule compounds

Small-molecule compounds modulate TREM2 either by upregulating its expression or inhibiting its activity, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects [41, 246, 247]. Dexmedetomidine (DEX), edaravone dexborneol (EDB), diosmetin, and nanomedicine all exert neuroprotective effects by upregulating TREM2 [246]. TREM2 expression decreases after heatstroke, while DEX intervention upregulates TREM2 through increasing p-PI3K, p-AKT, TGF-β, and IL-10 to alleviate heatstroke-induced neuroinflammation [246]. EDB upregulates TREM2 and inhibits the TLR4/MAPK pathway, which reduces levels of neuroinflammatory factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, COX-2, and Aβ deposition, improving cognitive function in APP-PS1 mice [247]. Diosmetin binds TREM2 with high affinity, not only reducing neuroinflammation by enhancing the TREM2/PI3K/AKT/FOXO1 pathway but also decreasing microglial pyroptosis after spinal cord ischemia-reperfusion injury [41]. In addition, combining nanomedicine (NMeds) with systemic ibuprofen and mouse nerve growth factor (mNGF) increased TREM2 expression and improved SCI outcomes [248]. On the other hand, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), 4,4’-dihydroxychalcone (DMC), and deferoxamine (DFO) exhibit inhibitory effects on TREM2. DHA reduces TREM2 expression in the ischemic penumbra after middle cerebral artery occlusion and increases mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF) abundance to reduce infarct size and aid post-stroke nerve repair [249]. DMC and DFO, respectively, reduce TREM2 upregulation after TBI by inhibiting PI3K/AKT/NF-κB and promoting mTOR signaling cascades, which decreases cell apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and maintains iron homeostasis [250, 251]. These small-molecule compounds hold potential as candidate drugs for the treatment of nerve damage.

Natural compounds

Many natural compounds protect the CNS from damage by targeting TREM2 signaling in microglia, with some sharing common mechanisms [268]. For instance, dihydroquercetin (DHQ) and hydroxysafflor yellow A (HSYA) both activate microglial TREM2 to alleviate neuroinflammation [252, 253]. DHQ shields substantia nigra dopamine neurons from neurotoxic damage by reducing IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS release [252]. HSYA inhibits Aβ1-42-induced TLR4/NF-κB p65 and IκB-α pathway activation via TREM2, suppressing iNOS, IL-1β, and IL-6 transcription and promoting an anti-inflammatory microglial state [253]. Celastrol boosts TREM2 and T-box transcription factor 21 (TBX21) levels, reduces p-tau and DAP12 expression, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and promoting neuronal survival. Unsurprisingly, the benefits of DHQ, HSYA, and Celastrol vanish when TREM2 is silenced or knocked out [254]. Although cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G) upregulates microglial TREM2 to clear Aβ, it’s unclear if it directly targets TREM2 [255]. Unlike the above compounds, Huanglian Jiedu Decoction (HLJD) reduces amyloid plaque deposition in 5xFAD mice by inhibiting TREM2/DAP12 signaling, thus suppressing neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment, with higher doses showing stronger TREM2 inhibition [256].

Lifestyle and environmental modulation

Lifestyle, dietary, and environmental interventions should not be interpreted as direct TREM2-targeted therapies. Instead, TREM2 changes in these settings may represent mechanistic contributors or pharmacodynamic readouts within broader neuroimmune, metabolic, and repair-related adaptations.

Physical exercise can promote the long-term expression of TREM2 in post-stroke rats. TREM2 not only enhances the phagocytic ability of microglia to clear myelin debris but also activates the activin-A/Acvr signaling axis, which in turn promotes the differentiation of OPCs into mature OLs, thereby achieving myelin regeneration [260]. In the meantime, physical exercise may assist white matter repair by polarizing astrocytes to the A2 phenotype, enhancing neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, and ultimately reducing neurological deficits [261]. Running exercise inhibits TREM2 loss, promotes brain glucose metabolism in AD mice, and blocks cognitive impairment in APP/PS1 mice [262, 263]. Besides the above, intermittent hypoxia training can activate the nuclear translocation of TFEB in DAM. It also upregulates vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 35 (VPS35) transcription to enhance TREM2 expression, which in turn increases Aβ endocytosis, reduces brain Aβ load, and improves AD cognitive impairment [264]. Although the protective effect of exercise training through TREM2 activation has been confirmed, the relationship between different exercise types, intensities, time windows, and TREM2 level fluctuations remains unresolved. Further research is needed to explore the optimal exercise regimen for clinical rehabilitation of patients.

It is noteworthy that dietary and environmental factors can also modulate TREM2 [269]. For instance, a high-salt diet exposes macrophages in ischemic stroke patients and mice to a high-salt microenvironment. Consequently, these changes downregulate TREM2 expression, undermine the capacity of macrophages to engulf dead cells, promote a pro-inflammatory macrophage state, worsen BBB damage, and ultimately delay recovery from moderate stroke lesions [265]. Limiting salt intake or overexpressing TREM2 can enhance IS prognosis. Furthermore, an enriched environment can also improve IS by targeting the TREM2/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [222] (Fig. 9).

Therapeutic strategies involving the TREM2 axis in central nervous system diseases. Direct TREM2-targeting approaches include TREM2 agonist antibodies, engineered TREM2-activating antibodies with enhanced CNS delivery, shedding-modulating or receptor-stabilizing antibodies, and TREM2 gene restoration strategies. Notably, 4D9 is more precisely classified as a shedding-modulating or receptor-stabilizing antibody rather than a traditional agonistic antibody. Indirect methods encompass small molecules, natural compounds, exercise, dietary modifications, and environmental interventions, which may modulate TREM2 expression, downstream signaling, or associated neuroinflammatory states. Consequently, TREM2 alterations observed in these indirect interventions should be interpreted as potential mechanistic contributors or pharmacodynamic readouts, rather than definitive evidence of direct TREM2 targeting

Unresolved challenges in precision TREM2-based therapies

Despite encouraging preclinical evidence, several major obstacles continue to hinder the clinical translation of TREM2-based therapies. First, the optimal mode of intervention, whether through agonism, stabilization, or context-dependent inhibition, likely varies across different diseases and their respective stages. Second, beneficial effects on central microglia may not accurately predict the impact of TREM2 modulation on peripheral immune compartments. Third, biomarker-guided patient stratification remains underdeveloped. Specifically, the use of CSF sTREM2, neuroimaging, or transcriptomic markers to identify the most suitable candidates for treatment requires further refinement. Fourth, robust target engagement in animal models does not guarantee meaningful clinical efficacy in humans, as demonstrated by the disappointing outcomes of recent anti-TREM2 antibody trials in AD. Collectively, these challenges suggest that future TREM2-directed regimens will demand a highly precise integration of disease stage, pathological context, and patient selection, rather than relying on the uniform amplification of TREM2 signaling.

Conclusions and perspectives

As a critical node within the neuroimmune-metabolic network, TREM2 plays an essential regulatory role in the pathogenesis and progression of both neurodegenerative diseases and acute neurological injuries [106, 270, 271]. At the molecular level, TREM2 establishes a multi-tiered regulatory network governed by differential transcription, distinct structural domain functions, and the generation of soluble fragments. Together, its ligand-binding capacity, signal transduction efficiency, and sTREM2 levels shape microglial responses to pathological stimuli [272–274]. Pathologically, TREM2 exhibits distinct bidirectional and stage-dependent effects. In early-stage AD, it promotes amyloid-beta clearance and plaque encapsulation; however, under conditions of sustained inflammation and complex pathology, it may inadvertently amplify detrimental inflammatory cascades [4, 275]. In PD, TREM2 can protect dopaminergic neurons by suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome, yet in specific advanced pathological contexts, it may also contribute to imbalanced toxic protein processing and pathological spread [7, 276]. In acute injury models, TREM2 generally facilitates remyelination and tissue repair, but its true efficacy in scenarios like chronic traumatic brain injury remains constrained by disease stage, cell type, and the local microenvironment [235, 277, 278].

Concurrently, while sTREM2 holds significant research value as both a potential biomarker and an active effector molecule, its clinical translation is hindered by inconsistent detection standards, poorly defined dynamic trajectories, and a lack of disease specificity [279]. Therapeutic strategies targeting TREM2 have made promising advancements, with agonistic antibodies, small-molecule compounds, natural products, gene therapies, and even exercise or environmental interventions demonstrating translational potential [222, 280–284]. Nevertheless, these strategies face several prominent challenges, including inefficient central nervous system delivery of antibodies, poor tissue specificity of certain pharmacological agents, unverified long-term safety and stability of gene therapies, and the limitation that most evidence derives from animal models with restricted clinical extrapolability [280, 281].

Future research must prioritize several critical areas. First, researchers should utilize single-cell transcriptomics, spatial omics, and multi-omics technologies to systematically map the dynamic expression and functional divergence of TREM2 across varying disease stages, cellular subpopulations, and microenvironments, thereby precisely delineating its protective versus pathogenic boundaries. Second, advanced engineering is required to develop highly efficient, brain-targeted delivery systems that enhance the blood-brain barrier penetrance and cerebral enrichment of TREM2-targeted therapeutics. Third, further studies must elucidate the functional crosstalk between TREM2 and key pathways, such as PD-1, TLR4, NLRP3, and metabolic reprogramming, to identify novel targets for combinatorial interventions. Fourth, investigators must design rigorous prospective clinical trials to validate the efficacy, safety, and optimal therapeutic windows of TREM2-targeted treatments in human populations. Ultimately, TREM2 should not be categorized simplistically as a unidirectional protective or deleterious molecule. Instead, it must be recognized as a highly context-dependent, pathology-associated regulatory node. As mechanistic insights deepen and translational technologies advance, TREM2 holds substantial promise for providing novel theoretical foundations and interventional pathways for the precision stratification and targeted therapy of neurological disorders.
