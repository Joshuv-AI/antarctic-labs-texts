Age-related patterns of resting EEG power in infancy: Associations with prenatal socioeconomic disadvantage
Authors: Aislinn Sandre, Sonya V Troller-Renfree, Melina Amarante, Amanda M Dettmer, Jerrold S Meyer, Kimberly G Noble
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe brain develops rapidly during the prenatal period and first two years of life, making it particularly sensitive to environmental influences. Family socioeconomic disadvantage is one environmental factor that may shape the development of brain function in infancy. However, it is unclear how brain function changes across infancy or whether prenatal family socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with age-related differences in brain function during this period. Here, we examine whether resting electroencephalography (EEG) power (theta, alpha, beta, and gamma) shows linear and/or non-linear age-related patterns across four assessments from 1 to 18 months of age (N = 165), and whether these patterns are moderated by prenatal family socioeconomic disadvantage. We find that lower-frequency (relative theta) and higher-frequency (relative alpha, beta, and gamma) power show non-linear age-related patterns during the first 18 months of life. Prenatal family socioeconomic disadvantage moderates these patterns, such that infants from lower-income families show less steep age-related decreases in lower-frequency (relative theta) power and less steep increases in higher-frequency (relative beta) power. These associations hold when adjusting for other prenatal and postnatal experiences, as well as infant demographic and health-related factors. These data suggest that lower prenatal family income is associated with age-related differences in brain function during infancy.Keywords: Socioeconomic status, Brain development, Electroencephalography (EEG), Resting brain activity, Infancy

Introduction

The brain develops rapidly during the prenatal period and first two years of life, undergoing substantial structural and functional changes that lay the foundation for subsequent brain development (Gilmore et al., 2018). The tremendous plasticity of the developing brain during this time makes it especially sensitive to environmental influences, with lasting effects on children's cognitive, socio-emotional, and language development, as well as their lifelong health (Dawson et al., 2000, Monk et al., 2019; Van den Bergh et al., 2020). Family socioeconomic status (SES)—commonly defined as parental educational attainment, occupational prestige, and/or family income—during the prenatal period is one environmental factor that may shape the development of early brain function (Nielsen et al., 2024), likely through a variety of experiences (e.g., maternal stress, toxin exposure, nutrition, and access to prenatal care; Hackman et al., 2010; Herzberg and Smyser, 2024). However, it is unclear how brain function changes across infancy or whether prenatal family SES is associated with age-related differences in brain function during this period. A better understanding of normative age-related patterns in early brain function, and how prenatal SES influences these patterns, is essential for informing preventive interventions and policies that support infants’ long-term development and health.

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a useful tool for understanding age-related patterns in early brain function (Anderson and Perone, 2018). EEG provides a direct, non-invasive measure of electrical brain activity, and is commonly recorded at rest in infants. At rest, EEG oscillations reflect network-level rhythms that coordinate neural activity across brain regions, supporting cognitive, social, and affective processes (Cuevas and Bell, 2022). Resting EEG can be characterized along two key dimensions: frequency and power. “Frequency” refers to oscillatory brain activity that occurs at different rates across the brain and is categorized into different frequency bands (Saby and Marshall, 2012). Some bands correspond to slower oscillations (e.g., theta), while others reflect faster oscillations (e.g., alpha, beta, and gamma). “Power” refers to the amount of activity within a given frequency band, with higher power indicating greater synchronized electrical activity generated by the brain (Saby and Marshall, 2012, Tan et al., 2024).

Studies examining resting EEG in early childhood typically quantify power using two metrics: absolute power and relative power. Absolute power refers to the raw amount of electrical activity within a specific frequency band, while relative power represents the proportion of power in a specific frequency band relative to the total power across all frequency bands. Both measures have advantages and limitations. For instance, absolute power is highly sensitive to non-neural anatomical factors such as skull thickness, scalp impedance, and hair density (Benninger et al., 1984, Cuffin, 1993, Hagemann et al., 2008), which can introduce noise and may obscure developmental differences. In contrast, relative power normalizes across the EEG spectrum, making it less sensitive to these anatomical factors (Clarke et al., 2001, Marshall et al., 2004). However, because relative power is calculated as a proportion, changes in one frequency band can influence estimates in others, complicating the interpretation of developmental changes. Despite these limitations, we focus on relative power as it may more robustly capture developmental differences by minimizing inter-individual variability.

Because variation in lower- and higher-frequency brain activity is linked to children’s cognitive (Brito et al., 2016, Marshall et al., 2008, Williams et al., 2012), language (Gou et al., 2011) and socio-emotional development (Brito et al., 2019), a growing body of research has sought to chart normative age-related patterns in resting brain activity across early childhood to better understand how it supports these skills. This work suggests that, among typically developing children, relative power in lower-frequency bands (i.e., theta) tends to decrease, while relative power in higher-frequency bands (i.e., alpha, beta, gamma) tends to increase from infancy to middle childhood (Marshall et al., 2002, Perone et al., 2018, Takano and Ogawa, 1998). However, much of this research has relied on cross-sectional designs (Anderson and Perone, 2018, Perone et al., 2018, Takano and Ogawa, 1998) or has assumed linear age-related trends in brain activity (Hofstee et al., 2024, Marshall et al., 2002). Yet, early brain development is a non-linear process (Chen et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2023), necessitating longitudinal investigations that consider non-linear age-related variation.

To date, there is limited longitudinal research examining whether lower- and higher-frequency EEG power show non-linear age-related patterns during the first two years of life—and most existing work has focused on absolute power (e.g., Tierney et al., 2012; Wilkinson et al., 2023). Emerging evidence suggests that relative power may also change non-linearly across infancy. For example, Rico-Picó et al. (2023) found that lower-frequency (relative theta) power rapidly decreased from 6 to 9 months, followed by a slower decline or plateau from 9 to 16 months. In contrast, higher-frequency (relative alpha and beta) power rapidly increased from 6 to 9 months, then showed a slower increase or plateau through 16 months. This suggests that lower- and higher-frequency relative power undergo rapid changes during the first year of life, followed by slower and more stable decreases or increases, respectively, in the second year. Nonetheless, further longitudinal research—especially focused on relative power—is needed to determine whether these patterns follow linear or non-linear trends across the first two years of life, as this may help identify early biomarkers of children’s future developmental outcomes.

Beyond evidence of developmental change, emerging evidence suggests that family SES is associated with differences in resting brain activity during infancy. For instance, infants exposed to socioeconomic disadvantage tend to show more lower-frequency (theta) power and less higher-frequency (alpha, beta, and gamma) power, compared to infants from more advantaged circumstances (Brito et al., 2020, Cantiani et al., 2019, Otero, 1997, Otero et al., 2003, Pierce et al., 2019, Sandre et al., 2024, Tomalski et al., 2013). Such patterns of brain activity emerge as early as one month of age (Sandre et al., 2024) and persist through childhood and early adolescence (Harmony et al., 1988, Maguire and Schneider, 2019, Otero, 1997). Moreover, boosting family income through unconditional cash transfers to families residing in poverty may cause increased higher-frequency power in infants (Troller-Renfree et al., 2022). However, much of this research has relied on cross-sectional designs and focused primarily on postnatal disadvantage (Brito et al., 2020, Otero, 1997, Pierce et al., 2019, Tomalski et al., 2013). Thus, it remains unclear whether prenatal family SES is associated with differences in age-related patterns of brain activity during infancy, which is crucial for understanding when and how strongly these differences emerge and persist across early development.

Additionally, family SES is a multi-dimensional construct encompassing both correlated and independent economic (e.g., family income) and social (e.g., parental education) factors (Duncan and Magnuson, 2012), each of which may show distinct associations with age-related patterns of brain activity during infancy. Consistent with this, a recent longitudinal study found that lower family income—but not maternal education—was associated with less absolute power in both lower-frequency (theta) and higher-frequency (alpha, beta, gamma) power at 2–3 months, as well as steeper increases in lower-frequency (theta) and higher-frequency (alpha) power from 3 to 9 months (Wilkinson et al., 2023). These findings suggests that income may have particularly strong effects on early brain development. However, this work focused on postnatal SES and absolute EEG power only. Given that few longitudinal studies have simultaneously considered both prenatal family income and parental education, there is a need to examine whether these SES factors show similar or distinct associations with age-related patterns of brain activity during the first two years of life.

In this prospective longitudinal study, we aimed to 1) examine whether resting brain activity shows linear or non-linear age-related patterns during the first 18 months of life, and 2) assess whether prenatal family socioeconomic factors—specifically income-to-needs and parental education—moderate these patterns. We hypothesized that resting brain activity would show non-linear age-related changes, characterized by rapid decreases in lower-frequency (relative theta) power and rapid increases in higher-frequency (relative alpha, beta, and gamma) power during the first year of life, followed by slower decreases and increases in the second year, respectively. Additionally, we predicted that prenatal family SES would moderate associations between age and resting brain activity, such that infants from families with higher income-to-needs ratios and more years of parental education would show more rapid decreases in lower-frequency (relative theta) power and more rapid increases in higher-frequency (relative alpha, beta, and gamma) power across the first 18 months of life compared to infants from more disadvantaged families. Exploratory analyses were conducted to examine whether associations between prenatal family SES and patterns of resting brain activity were stronger over certain regions of the scalp (i.e., frontal, central, parietal, temporal, occipital). Finally, we tested whether any significant associations between prenatal family SES and age-related changes in brain activity held when adjusting for various prenatal and postnatal experiences, infant demographics, and health-related factors that tend to co-vary with SES (Hackman et al., 2010).

Methods

Participants

Participants were 209 mother-infant dyads from the New York City metropolitan area that were part of a larger longitudinal study examining associations between early experiences and child development (Sandre et al., 2024, Sperber et al., 2023, Troller-Renfree et al., 2025). Mothers were recruited during their third trimester of pregnancy from local prenatal clinics, community events, and social media, and were intentionally recruited to span a range of educational attainment, from having less than a high school education to holding an advanced degree. Recruitment occurred over two time periods because of a temporary interruption in data collection due to the COVID-19 pandemic: the first cohort of mothers (Cohort 1) was recruited from June 2019 through March 2020 (N = 93) and the second cohort of mothers (Cohort 2) was recruited from August 2021 to September 2022 (N = 116). Inclusion criteria for mothers included: 18 years of age or older, at least 35 weeks pregnant, carrying a singleton fetus with no known neurological or developmental issues, and ability to speak either English or Spanish. Eligible mothers were invited to participate in a prenatal visit in our lab or their home.

After birth, mother-infant dyads were invited to participate in follow-up home or lab visits at four time points: approximately 1, 6, and 12 months of age, as well as at either 15 or 18 months of age (families were invited to complete an 18-month visit only if they did not participate in the 15-month visit). Inclusion criteria for infants included a gestational age of at least 37 weeks and no known neurological or developmental issues at birth. Families in Cohort 2 were invited to all four time points to complete EEG collection. In contrast, families in Cohort 1 were invited to complete EEG collection only at the 1-month visit; all subsequent visits were conducted remotely due to the pandemic. Across all time points, EEG data from eight infants were excluded from analyses due to either poor-quality recordings or an insufficient number of artifact-free epochs (see details below). Additionally, one infant was excluded due to a technical error during EEG recording (i.e., a corrupted file). Of the recruited sample of 209 mother-infant dyads, usable EEG data was collected from 160 infants at the 1-month assessment, 81 infants at the 6-month assessment, 80 infants at the 12-month assessment, and 65 infants at the 15- or 18-month assessments. The present study included infants for whom we had usable EEG data for at least one time point. Therefore, the final sample consisted of 386 resting EEG recordings collected from 165 infants (64 from Cohort 1 and 101 from Cohort 2; see Supplementary Fig. S1). Measures

Supplementary Fig. S3 presents an overview of the measures collected at the prenatal, 1-month, 6-month, 12-month, and 15/18-month time points. Supplementary Table S2 presents descriptive statistics of study variables. For additional details about study measures, see the Supplementary Results.

Prenatal family socioeconomic status

We measured two indices of SES prenatally via questionnaire: average parental education and family income-to-needs (ITN) ratio. Average parental education was calculated by averaging the number of years of education attained by the mother and the second parent. Response options for the parental education measure ranged from 0 to 22 years, with anchors at specific milestones (e.g., 0 = no formal schooling, 12 = high school graduate/GED, 14 = associates degree or trade school, 16 = bachelor’s degree, 18 = master’s degree, 22 + = PhD, MD, JD, or other professional degrees). ITN was calculated by dividing total household income by the U.S. poverty threshold for the respective family size for the year of data collection. An ITN below 1 indicates that a family is living below the federal poverty threshold, whereas an ITN above 1 indicates that a family is living above the federal poverty threshold. Three mothers had outlying (>3 SDs) ITN values, and so these values were winsorized prior to analyses to preserve power for analyses. The ITN for the sample ranged from 0 to 43.74, with a median ITN value of 2.81 (IQR=9.28). Twenty-seven percent (N = 44) of the sample reported an ITN below the poverty line, and eight percent (N = 14) of the sample reported ITN values between 1 and 2, considered to be within the “near-poor” range. Additionally, ten mothers reported a family income of zero dollars. ITN values were positively skewed, and so ITN values were natural log-transformed (ln) for all analyses. To enable log transformation, one dollar was added to all income values prior to calculating ITN. Supplementary Fig. S4 presents histograms of family income and ITN, and Supplementary Fig. S5 presents a histogram of parental education. Family ITN was also measured at 12-months. Supplemental Fig. S.6 presents a spaghetti plot depicting changes in family ITN from the prenatal to 12-month time points.

Maternal perceived stress

Maternal perceived stress was assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; Cohen et al., 1983) at the prenatal, 6-month, 12-month, and 15/18-month time points, and the Life Events Scale (LES; Sarason et al., 1978) at the prenatal and 12-month time points. Consistent with prior research from our lab (Merz et al., 2019, Simon et al., 2021), we created a “Prenatal Perceived Stress Composite” and a “Postnatal Perceived Stress Composite” by conducting separate exploratory factor analyses for the prenatal and postnatal stress measures, using principal factor extraction and Varimax rotation. Higher scores on the Prenatal and Postnatal Composites indicate greater maternal perceived stress.

Maternal physiological stress

Maternal physiological stress was assessed prenatally via hair cortisol. A small hair sample was collected from a standardized area of the posterior vertex of the mother’s scalp, with each sample weighing at least 15 mg. Each hair sample was trimmed to be approximately 3 cm long (measured from the end closest to the root), thereby containing cortisol deposited during roughly the past three months (based on an estimate of 1 cm hair growth per month; Greff et al., 2019). Three mothers were excluded from analyses because they were currently using steroid medications. Additionally, two mothers had outlying (>3 SDs) hair cortisol values, and so these values were winsorized prior to analyses. Hair cortisol values were natural log-transformed (ln) to correct for skew. There were no significant associations between hair cortisol and hair washing frequency, use of oral contraceptives, or use of hair dye (ps > .65).

Maternal vocabulary skills

Maternal vocabulary skills were measured prenatally using the Picture Vocabulary Test (PVT) of the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery (Gershon et al., 2014). The PVT assesses receptive vocabulary via auditory comprehension of single words that are graded in difficulty using an auditory word-picture matching paradigm. Mothers’ performance on the PVT was converted to a theta score (ranging from 4 to −4), based on item response theory.

Parent-child activities

Maternal engagement in infant learning activities was assessed at 6 months using the Parent-Child Activities Index (Rodriguez and Tamis-LeMonda, 2011). Mothers were asked about the frequency of which they (and the second parent) engaged in reading, telling stories, building things, and participating in playgroups with their infants. Scores for maternal and second parent activities were summed separately and averaged to compute an overall parent-child activities score. Higher scores on the parent-child activities index indicate more engagement in learning activities.

Home language input

The home language environment was assessed at 6-months using the Language Environment Analysis (LENA) system, an automated device that can record a child's language environment for up to 16 h. We used LENA's validated speech-identification algorithms to segment the audio and estimate the number of adult words spoken to the child. Mean hourly adult word counts were calculated by dividing the adult word count by the total recording duration (in hours) for each participant.

Feeding style

At one-, six-, and twelve-months, mothers were asked how they were currently feeding their infant (e.g., “What do you currently feed your baby?"). At one- and six-months, response options included “Only breast milk”, “Only baby formula”, and “Both breast milk and baby formula”. At twelve months, an additional option was provided: “Other milks (e.g., cow’s milk)”.

Nutrition

At 12 months, mothers reported their child’s daily intake of fruits, vegetables, sweets (e.g., sweetened cereals, fruit bars), and sweetened drinks (e.g., juice, chocolate milk) using an adapted version of the Los Angeles County WIC Survey (Davis Research LLC, 2017). Frequency was assessed via a Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘never’) to 5 (‘5 times per day’). Responses were summed to create two daily food intake indices: a 'healthy foods' index (fruits and vegetables) and an 'unhealthy foods' index (sweets and sweetened drinks), representing total daily frequency for each food type.

Infant demographic and health-related factors

Mothers reported infants' gestational age at birth, birth weight, sex, race, ethnicity, and age at the 1-month visit. Infant ages at the 6-, 12-, and 15/18-month visits were also collected via questionnaires.

Electroencephalography data acquisition and processing

Resting EEG was recorded at 1, 6, 12, and either 15 or 18 months using a 128-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net (Magstim Electrical Geodesic Inc., Eugene, OR, USA) with a Net Amps 400 amplifier. Electrode impedances were kept below 50 kΩ whenever possible. Data were sampled at 1000 Hz and referenced online to Cz. The EEG collection procedure is detailed in the Supplementary Results. EEG data were analyzed offline using the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme and Makeig, 2004; Gershon 2022.1), MATLAB (Version R2021b; The MathWorks, Natick, MA), and the MADE pipeline (Debnath et al., 2020). The outermost ring of electrodes (i.e., electrodes located near the base of the skull) were removed since they are heavily susceptible to eye, face and head movements. Data were then high-pass filtered at 0.30 Hz and low-pass filtered at 50 Hz. Bad channels were identified and removed using the EEGLAB plug-in FASTER (Nolan et al., 2010). Infants with globally bad channels across all epochs were excluded from further processing (removed N = 3). Ocular artifacts and generic noise were removed using the standard MADE ICA removal procedure (Debnath et al., 2020). Artifactual ICs were removed from the original dataset by using the Adjusted-ADJUST algorithm (Leach et al., 2020).

Subsequently, data were epoched in one-second segments with 50 % (500 ms) overlap. Epochs where any of the frontal electrodes exceeded a voltage threshold of ±150 μV were rejected to ensure the removal of ocular artifacts. For the remaining epochs, bad channels were identified if the electrode exceeded the voltage threshold, and epochs were rejected when more than 20 % of channels were interpolated. Infants with artifactual epochs across all segments (i.e., more than 20 % of channels interpolated on every epoch) were excluded from further processing (removed N = 4). All rejected channels were interpolated. Data were then re-referenced to an average reference.

Following pre-processing, a Fast Fourier Transform with a Hanning Window was applied to the epoched data. Power spectral density was first calculated in linear units (μV²) for each electrode and epoch. Power spectra were then averaged across usable epochs for each electrode. Subsequently, a log10 transformation (specifically, log10[1 + μV²]) was applied to the averaged power spectra to reduce skew and improve distributional properties (the +1 ensures all values remain positive). Absolute power for each frequency band—theta (3–5 Hz), alpha (6–9 Hz), beta (13–19 Hz), and gamma (21–45 Hz)—was computed by averaging the log-transformed power values within the corresponding frequency ranges. These bands were selected to align with prior infant EEG research (Rico-Picó et al., 2023, Sandre et al., 2024, Tomalski et al., 2013). Although there is some variability in the exact frequency ranges used to define gamma power across studies—for example, some have labeled 20–30 Hz as high beta (Arnett et al., 2024, Wilkinson et al., 2024)—we selected this range for gamma power to align with prior studies in infants of a similar age to our sample (Brito et al., 2020, Jensen et al., 2021, Tomalski et al., 2013, Troller-Renfree et al., 2023).

Relative power for each frequency band was computed by dividing the band-averaged power by the sum of the band-averaged powers across all four bands. For example, relative alpha power was calculated as: Relative alpha = mean(log10(1 + AlphaFreqs)) / (mean(log10(1 + ThetaFreqs)) + mean(log10(1 + AlphaFreqs)) + mean(log10(1 + BetaFreqs)) + mean(log10(1 + GammaFreqs))). Absolute and relative power values were first computed per electrode and then averaged across all electrodes to obtain whole-brain estimates. We also calculated both absolute and relative EEG power for each frequency band within five regions of interest (ROIs): frontal, central, parietal, temporal, and occipital (see Sandre et al., 2024 for electrodes included in our regional scorings). Absolute power for each region was computed by averaging the power across all electrodes within that ROI for each frequency band. To calculate relative power, we then divided the absolute power in each frequency band by the total power summed across all frequency bands within the same ROI. This process was repeated for each of the five ROIs. Given that relative power may be a more sensitive measure of developmental change in EEG power as it minimizes individual differences in skull thickness, resistance and impedances that impact absolute power (Clarke et al., 2001, Marshall et al., 2004), our primary analyses focused on relative power.

To determine the minimum number of epochs needed for reliable power estimates at each time point, we used the Spearman-Brown split-half correlation procedure with multiple iterations (Leach et al., 2020, Troller-Renfree et al., 2021). The results of these analyses are reported in Supplementary Table S3. One infant had too few artifact-free epochs at 12-months to provide reliable EEG power estimates and was therefore excluded from our analyses. Descriptive statistics of the number of artifact-free epochs at each EEG assessment are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

Missing data

Supplementary Table S2 presents the number and percentage of mother–infant dyads with available data for each study measure relative to the final sample (N = 165). To assess whether missing data in our independent variables and covariates were missing completely at random (MCAR), we conducted Little’s MCAR test (Little, 1988), which indicated that the data were indeed MCAR, χ²(1689)= 1567.30, p > .05. To address this missingness, we conducted multilevel imputation for independent variables and covariates; EEG data were not imputed. Additional details on this procedure are provided in the Supplementary Results.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS (Version 29) or R (Version 4.4.1). To examine age-related patterns in infant resting brain activity, we used Generalized Additive Mixed-Effects Models (GAMMs; Wood, 2004), which flexibly model both linear and nonlinear relationships by fitting smooth functions to predictors. Smooth functions are constructed from a linear combination of weighted basis functions and are penalized for increasing complexity (Wood, 2004). Instead of beta coefficients, GAMMs compute effective degrees of freedom (EDF) for smooth terms, which specify the complexity of the relationship between predictor and outcome variables. An EDF of 1 is linear and higher EDFs indicate more nonlinearity.

All GAMMs were structured so that EEG power, infant age, and the number of artifact-free epochs at each time point were nested within participants. In our first set of models, we examined age-related differences in resting brain activity by fitting separate GAMMs for whole-brain relative theta, alpha, beta, and gamma power as the dependent variable. Infant age (in months) was included as a smooth term, with cohort number (0 =Cohort 1, 1 =Cohort 2), and number of artifact-free epochs as linear covariates. To identify periods of significant change in associations between age and whole-brain relative power, we calculated the first derivative of the smooth age term using the gratia package (Simpson, 2024). Intervals of significant age-related change were identified when the simultaneous confidence interval of the derivative did not include zero. Following this, we conducted follow-up regional analyses by fitting separate GAMMs for each of our five whole-brain ROIs, with each ROI entered as the dependent variable. We defined our GAMMs as follows:

Whole-brain: Relative Power ∼ s(Age, k = 4) + Covariates

Regional: Relative Power ROI ∼ s(Age, k = 4) + Covariates

In our second set of models, we examined whether prenatal family SES moderated age-related differences in resting brain activity by fitting separate GAMMs for whole-brain relative theta, alpha, beta, and gamma power as the dependent variables. Infant age and prenatal SES (family ITN or parental education) were included as smooth terms, along with a tensor product interaction term for age and prenatal SES. This interaction term allows the smooth term for age to vary as a fully non-linear function of prenatal SES. Cohort number and the number of artifact-free epochs were included as linear covariates. Because nonlinear moderations preclude follow-up with simple slopes (Wood, 2017), significant interactions were probed by examining associations between prenatal SES and age-related differences in whole-brain relative power separately in infants from families with higher and lower SES, as defined by a median split. To determine whether associations between family SES and age-related differences in infant brain activity were stronger over certain regions of the scalp (i.e., frontal, central, parietal, temporal, and occipital), we conducted separate GAMMs for each of our five ROIs, using the same predictors and covariates as in our whole-brain models. We defined our GAMMs as follows:

Whole-brain: Relative Power ∼ s(Age, k = 4) + s(Prenatal Family SES, k = 4) + ti(Age, Prenatal Family SES, k = 4) + Covariates

Regional: Relative Power ROI ∼ s(Age, k = 4) + s(Prenatal Family SES, k = 4) + ti(Age, Prenatal Family SES, k = 4) + Covariates

All GAMMs included a random intercept for participant with age splines restricted to four basis functions (K=4) for flexibility. Given that GAMMs can model both linear and non-linear associations, in instances where there is no non-linear association between age and resting brain activity, the smooth term for age was penalized down to a linear term. Models used thin plate regression splines for smooth terms, with smoothing parameters estimated via restricted maximum likelihood (REML). GAMMs were computed using the mgcv package.

To account for multiple comparisons, P-values from all related GAMMs were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method with the p.adjust package. For whole-brain analyses, FDR corrections were applied across the four frequency bands (relative theta, alpha, beta, and gamma), resulting in 4 corrections for whole-brain relative power. For regional analyses, FDR corrections were applied across 5 ROIs—frontal, central, parietal, temporal, and occipital—within each frequency band, resulting in 5 comparisons per frequency band. This yielded a total of 20 comparisons across all regions and frequency bands. The same FDR correction approach was applied to whole-brain and regional absolute power.

Sensitivity analyses

Given that family SES often covaries with various early experiences and family characteristics (Bradley and Corwyn, 2002, Farah, 2017, Hackman et al., 2010, Hoff and Laursen, 2019, Noble et al., 2021, Ursache et al., 2017), we tested the robustness of associations between prenatal SES and age-related patterns in infant resting brain activity by re-running our whole-brain relative power models with a set of covariates that have established links to SES (Hackman et al., 2010). Specifically, we included maternal prenatal perceived stress and physiological stress (i.e., hair cortisol), receptive vocabulary, maternal postnatal perceived stress, postpartum exclusive breastfeeding at one, six and 12- months (1 =exclusively breastmilk fed, 0 =exclusively formula fed, 0 =mixed breastmilk and formula fed), exclusive formula feeding at one, six and 12- months (1 =exclusively formula fed, 0 =exclusively breastmilk fed, 0 =mixed breastmilk and formula fed), exclusive other milk fed at 12months (1 =exclusively other milk fed, 0 =exclusively breastmilk fed, 0 =exclusively formula fed, 0 =mixed breast milk, formula or other-milk fed), parent-child activities, adult word count, healthy and unhealthy foods consumed per day, postnatal ITN, infant sex (0 =male, 1 = female), race (0 =White, 1 =Non-white), ethnicity (0 =Non-Hispanic/Latino, 1 =Hispanic/Latino), weight at birth (in lbs), and gestational age at birth (in weeks). All covariates were entered simultaneously into each model. We modeled all covariates as linear terms to prioritize parsimony and minimize the risk of model overfitting, given the absence of strong a priori hypotheses about the functional form of these associations.

Smooth terms for both prenatal family ITN and parental education were included in our models to examine their unique associations with infant whole-brain relative power. Although prenatal and postnatal family ITN were significantly correlated (r = .65, p < .01), as were prenatal ITN and parent education (r = .53, p < .01) they did not violate assumptions of multicollinearity (VIF<7; Kim, 2019).

Results

Age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative power

We first examined whether infant whole-brain relative power showed linear or non-linear age-related patterns during the first 18 months after birth. Results indicated that whole-brain relative theta (Fs(Age)= 215.30, EDF= 2.84, R2partial = 0.70, pFDR< .001), alpha (Fs(Age)= 215.20, EDF= 2.77, R2partial= 0.70, pFDR< .001), beta (Fs(Age)= 35.05, EDF= 2.80, R2partial = 0.29, pFDR< .001), and gamma power (Fs(Age)= 41.07, EDF= 2.95, R2partial= 0.35, pFDR< .001) showed non-linear age-related trends during the first 18-months after birth, after controlling for covariates. Relative theta power significantly decreased over the first 15.0 months, then plateaued with no significant change between 15.1 and 20.4 months of age (Fig. 1a). Relative alpha power increased during the first 15.3 months and subsequently plateaued with no significant change between 15.5 and 20.4 months (Fig. 1b). Relative beta power increased during the first 8.1 months, then plateaued with no significant change between 8.3 and 20.4 months (Fig. 1c). Relative gamma power increased over the first 7.1 months, decreased after 8.5 months, and then plateaued with no significant change between 13.9 and 20.4 months of age (Fig. 1d). Topographic heat maps showing the scalp distribution of relative power for each of the four frequency bands at each time point are presented in Fig. 2. Additional results are provided in the Supplementary Results, including age-related patterns of regional relative power (Table S5, Fig. S7), whole-brain and regional absolute power (Table S6, Figs. S8 & S9), topographic heat maps of absolute power (Fig. S10), and power spectra for each time point (Figs. S11 & S12).

Associations between prenatal family ITN and age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative power

We next examined whether prenatal family ITN moderates age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative power (results for absolute power are presented in Supplementary Table S7 and Fig. S13 & Fig. S14). As indicated in Table 2, higher prenatal family ITN was marginally associated with less relative theta power (Fs(ITN)= 3.52, EDF= 2.03, p = 0.03, pFDR= .06) and significantly associated with more relative beta power (Fs(ITN)= 4.69, EDF= 2.19, pFDR= .03) in infants during the first 18-months after birth, after controlling for covariates (Supplementary Fig. S15). Moreover, prenatal family ITN significantly moderated age-related patterns of relative theta power (Fti(AgexITN)= 3.02, EDF= 3.80, R2partial= 0.06, pFDR< .05) and relative beta power (Fti(AgexITN)= 4.06, EDF= 4.02, R2partial= 0.07, pFDR< .05) in infants after controlling for covariates (Table 2). Infants from families with higher prenatal ITN showed a qualitatively more rapid decrease in relative theta power compared to infants from families with lower prenatal ITN (Fig. 3a). This difference emerged around 2 months of age, widened over time, and plateaued by 11 months. In contrast, infants from families with higher prenatal ITN showed a qualitatively more rapid increase in relative beta power compared to infants from families with lower prenatal ITN (Fig. 3c). This difference emerged around 1 month of age, widened over time, and plateaued by 12 months. We did not observe any significant main effects of prenatal family ITN, nor interactions between age and prenatal family ITN, on relative alpha or gamma power in infants (Fig. 3b & d).

Associations between prenatal family ITN and age-related patterns of infant regional relative power

To better understand whether associations between prenatal family ITN and age-related patterns of relative theta and beta power were stronger over certain regions of the scalp, we conducted follow-up GAMMs for relative power over frontal, central, parietal, temporal and occipital regions (Supplementary Table S11). Results of these analyses indicated that higher prenatal family ITN was associated with a qualitatively more rapid decrease in relative theta power in frontal (Fti(AgexITN)= 3.04, EDF= 4.27, R2partial= 0.07, p = .02, pFDR= .08), temporal (Fti(AgexITN)= 2.38, EDF= 4.07, R2partial= 0.05, p = .05, pFDR= .08) and occipital regions (Fti(AgexITN)= 2.78, EDF= 2.47, R2partial= 0.04, p = .03, pFDR= .08), after controlling for covariates (Supplementary Fig. S19). However, these associations were not significant after FDR correction. In contrast, higher prenatal family ITN was associated with a qualitatively more rapid increase in relative beta power in frontal (Fti(AgexITN)= 3.68, EDF= 4.70, R2partial= 0.09, pFDR< .01), central (Fti(AgexITN)= 4.68, EDF= 3.48, R2partial= 0.08, pFDR< .01), parietal (Fti(AgexITN)= 4.47, EDF= 2.82, R2partial= 0.06, pFDR< .01), temporal (Fti(AgexITN)= 2.56, EDF= 4.19, R2partial= 0.05, pFDR< .05), and occipital regions (Fti(AgexITN)= 5.33, EDF= 1.93, R2partial= 0.03, pFDR< .05) after controlling for covariates (Supplementary Fig. S20).

No association between prenatal parental education and age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative power

We next examined whether prenatal parental education moderates age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative power (results for absolute power are presented in the Supplementary Table S7 and Fig. S21). We did not observe any significant main effects of parental education, nor interactions between age and parental education, in any frequency band in infants after controlling for covariates (Table 2). See Fig. S22 in the Supplementary Results for age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative power, plotted separately by families' highest level of parental educational attainment.

Sensitivity analyses

Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings by examining whether prenatal family ITN moderates age-related patterns of infant whole-brain relative theta and beta power when adjusting for the influence of other measured prenatal and postnatal experiences; infant demographic and health-related factors; postnatal family ITN; and parental education (Table 3; results for whole-brain absolute power are presented in Supplementary Table S12 and Fig. S23). We again found that prenatal family ITN significantly moderated age-related patterns of whole-brain relative theta (Fti(AgexITN)= 3.38, EDF= 4.17, R2partial= 0.06, pFDR< .01) and relative beta power (Fti(AgexITN)= 4.27, EDF= 4.35, R2partial= 0.07, pFDR< .01) in infants after controlling for these covariates. Infants from families with higher prenatal ITN showed a qualitatively more rapid decrease in relative theta power as well as a qualitatively more rapid increase in relative beta power, compared to infants from families with lower prenatal ITN (Supplementary Fig. S24). Moreover, prenatal parental education did not significantly moderate age-related patterns of whole-brain relative theta and beta power when controlling for covariates, indicating that socioeconomic-related differences in infant EEG power were specific to family ITN and not parental education.

Discussion

Here we examined whether resting brain activity shows linear or non-linear age-related patterns during the first 18 months of life, and whether prenatal family SES moderates these patterns. Consistent with our hypotheses, both lower-frequency (relative theta) and higher-frequency (relative alpha, beta, and gamma) power showed non-linear age-related changes. Specifically, relative theta power decreased most rapidly during the first 15 months. Relative alpha power increased most rapidly during the first 15 months, while relative beta power increased most rapidly during the first 8 months before plateauing. In contrast, relative gamma power followed a more complex pattern, with a rapid increase during the first 7 months, a decline between 8 and 14 months, and a plateau thereafter. Additionally, prenatal family ITN moderated these non-linear associations between age and resting brain activity, such that infants from families with higher ITN showed a more rapid decrease in lower-frequency (relative theta) power and a more rapid increase in higher-frequency (relative beta) power over time, compared to infants from families with lower ITN.

These results are consistent with prior research indicating age-related changes in relative EEG power during the first two years of life (Hofstee et al., 2024, Marshall et al., 2002) and further clarify this work by showing that these changes in may be non-linear. EEG power reflects network-level oscillations, with lower-frequency (theta) oscillations supporting long-range communication between networks and higher-frequency (alpha, beta, gamma) oscillations facilitating short-range, localized communication within networks (Von Stein and Sarnthein, 2000). The non-linear patterns of brain activity observed likely reflect multiple dynamic neurodevelopmental processes, which contribute to the integration of more efficient local and large-scale neural networks (Hu et al., 2022, Von Stein and Sarnthein, 2000). For example, increases in relative alpha, beta, and gamma power may reflect increases in myelination, which peaks during the first year and slows thereafter (Dubois et al., 2014), enhancing the speed and synchronization of neuronal signaling. It is important to note, however, that because relative power is a proportional measure—with total power across all frequency bands in the denominator—observed decreases in relative theta power may reflect increases in alpha, beta, and/or gamma power. Indeed, we observed an age-related shift in peak frequencies from the theta to the alpha range, which may also contribute to the decline in theta power (see Supplementary Figs. S11 and S12). This is a key limitation when interpreting age-related changes in relative power and underscores the importance of examining both absolute and relative metrics within the same study. At the regional level, we also observed both linear and non-linear age-related patterns of relative power (see Table S5 and Fig. S7), consistent with evidence that myelination progresses at different rates across brain regions (Dubois et al., 2014, Huttenlocher and Dabholkar, 1997). Future work employing both EEG and MRI will be helpful in elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying changes in EEG power during infancy.

Another important consideration is that the observed age-related patterns in relative power may be driven, in part, by changes in the aperiodic components of the EEG signal (Ostlund et al., 2022). EEG power reflects a combination of aperiodic activity (a 1/f-like background spectrum) and periodic or oscillatory activity (peaks superimposed on this background; Donoghue et al., 2020). Recent work has shown that periodic and relative power trajectories may diverge, and that developmental changes in relative theta and beta power may be more likely to be influenced by age-related variation in the aperiodic signal (e.g., Rico-Picó et al., 2023). As such, the age-related patterns observed in our study could be driven by periodic activity, aperiodic activity, or both. Future studies using spectral parameterization methods (e.g., Donoghue et al., 2020; Hill et al., 2022; Wilkinson et al., 2024) will be important for clarifying the underlying components that contribute to developmental changes in EEG power during infancy.

Findings from the current study also align with previous research linking postnatal family SES to developmental differences in lower- and higher-frequency brain activity in infants (Brito et al., 2020, Otero, 1997, Otero et al., 2003, Pierce et al., 2019), and extends this work by showing that prenatal family income may shape age-related patterns of brain activity during the first 18 months of life. Although resting EEG power in infants is partially heritable (Orekhova et al., 2003), it is also subject to substantial environmental influence (Saby and Marshall, 2012, Troller-Renfree et al., 2020). The patterns of brain activity we observed may therefore reflect genetic predispositions and/or more proximal prenatal or early postnatal experiences that covary with SES (Hackman et al., 2010). Our sensitivity analyses, however, indicated that associations between prenatal family ITN and age-related patterns of brain activity were independent of various measured prenatal factors (maternal stress, maternal vocabulary), postnatal factors (maternal stress, feeding style, engagement in learning activities, home language input, nutrition), and infant characteristics (demographic factors, gestational age, birth weight). These associations were also specific to prenatal, and not postnatal ITN. Nonetheless, because we did not directly assess genetic factors or measure other important prenatal (e.g., maternal nutrition, exercise, sleep quality, toxin exposure) and postnatal (e.g., parenting, childcare) experiences, their potential influence on the development of infant brain activity cannot be ruled out.

The patterns of brain activity observed among infants from lower-income families may have important implications for their future developmental outcomes. In infancy, both a greater amount of lower-frequency (theta) power, as well as less higher-frequency (beta) power, have been linked to lower scores on subsequent cognitive (Scher et al., 1996, Williams et al., 2012) and socio-emotional skills (Brito et al., 2019), as well as with the development of psychopathology (McLaughlin et al., 2010). Such patterns of brain activity in infancy also mediate associations between environmental deprivation and later outcomes, such as lower IQ (Tan et al., 2023) and ADHD symptoms (McLaughlin et al., 2010), suggesting a potential pathway linking prenatal income disparities to increased risk for adverse developmental and mental health outcomes. We note here, however, that children’s brain development reflects an adaptation to their lived experiences (Noble et al., 2021), and not all children from families facing socioeconomic disadvantage show the brain activity patterns we observed. Moreover, such patterns of brain activity may support the development of skills that are advantageous in the context of limited material resources (Ellis et al., 2022). Thus, adaptation does not necessarily indicate dysfunction or deficit but rather an expected and appropriate response that helps children function within their environment.

Interestingly, prenatal family income—but not parental education—was associated with age-related patterns of resting brain activity in infants. These data are consistent with the results of some previous studies (Cantiani et al., 2019, Tomalski et al., 2013, Wilkinson et al., 2023) and underscore the fact that family income and parental education represent distinct aspects of SES, each tied to different early experiences (Duncan and Magnuson, 2012). It is possible that experiences associated with income (e.g., crowding, neighborhood poverty, exposure to stress and toxins, nutrition, access to medical care) may have a stronger impact on age-related patterns of brain activity in early childhood (Troller-Renfree et al., 2022). In contrast, parental education might more directly shape the parenting environment, potentially influencing these patterns later in childhood.

Our follow-up exploratory analyses indicated some regional specificity. For instance, higher prenatal family ITN was associated with a more rapid decrease in lower-frequency (relative theta) power over time, particularly in the frontal, temporal, and occipital regions; however, these associations were not statistically significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons. In contrast, higher prenatal family ITN was linked to a more rapid increase in higher-frequency (relative beta) power across multiple regions, including the frontal, central, parietal, temporal, and occipital areas. These results align with prior research indicating socioeconomic-related differences in regional theta (Otero, 1997, Otero et al., 2003, Sandre et al., 2024) and beta (Brito et al., 2020, Jensen et al., 2021, Otero, 1994) power in young children. Taken together, these findings suggest that family income may influence functional brain development in a region-specific manner, with certain regions showing greater sensitivity to income-related differences in specific frequency bands.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first longitudinal studies to examine both prenatal family ITN and parental education and their associations with age-related patterns of resting brain activity in a large, socioeconomically and racially/ethnically diverse sample of infants. However, there are important limitations to consider. First, this study is correlational, and we cannot establish that observed differences in infant brain activity are caused by prenatal family SES. Second, the effect sizes observed in this study are small in magnitude, highlighting that prenatal family income is likely only one of many factors associated with differences in the development of infant brain activity. Third, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, EEG data could not be collected from the full sample at the 6-, 12-, and 15/18-month assessments, limiting statistical power and the generalizability of our findings. We therefore conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to Cohort 2, as this subset includes participants for whom EEG data collection resumed post-pandemic and comprises a higher proportion of infants with repeated measures. Results from this analysis were consistent with the full-sample findings (see Supplementary Table S10 and Fig. S18). Although the interaction between age and prenatal ITN on relative theta power was marginally significant in this subset, the overall pattern of associations supports the robustness of our main findings. Fourth, we observed differences between families recruited before and after the pandemic on several study variables (e.g., maternal prenatal perceived stress, physiological stress, receptive vocabulary scores, parental education, infant ethnicity, and age), making it unclear whether these differences influenced the observed results. Although we controlled for these variables in our sensitivity analyses, future studies are needed to determine whether our results replicate in samples with more complete data and to better understand the potential influence of the pandemic on age-related patterns of resting brain activity in infancy. Finally, the frequency bands used in this study were selected based on prior work in infants (Tomalski et al., 2013) and to maintain consistency across analyses. However, we acknowledge that definitions of frequency bands—particularly in the upper beta and gamma ranges—vary considerably across the infant resting EEG literature (e.g., Anderson and Perone, 2018; Troller-Renfree et al., 2025). These definitional inconsistencies may contribute to differences in findings across studies and should be considered when interpreting our results.

In conclusion, this study suggests that resting brain activity follows non-linear age-related patterns during the first 18 months of life. Moreover, prenatal ITN is associated with differences in these patterns, suggesting that prenatal environments may shape early functional brain development. Notably, SES-related differences in patterns of infant brain activity are specific to family income, rather than parental education, and are independent of various measured prenatal and postnatal experiences, as well as infant demographic and health-related factors. While additional research is needed, our results suggest the possibility that investment in income-support programs and policies during pregnancy may be an important target for supporting infants’ brain development.
