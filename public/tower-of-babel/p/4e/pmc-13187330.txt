Body fat percentage and sacral–abdominal wall distance are associated with obesity in patients with osteoporosis: a retrospective study
Authors: Takashi Nagai, Fumihito Kasai, Mizuki Sugiyama, Ichiro Okano, Soji Tani, Koki Tsuchiya, Koji Ishikawa, Katsunori Inagaki, Keizo Sakamoto, Yoshifumi Kudo, Nobuyuki Kawate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity assessment in patients with osteoporosis is challenging because spinal deformities and height loss may distort body mass index (BMI)-based classifications. This retrospective cross-sectional study investigated the associations between obesity and two body composition-related indices: the android/gynoid fat ratio (A/G ratio) derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and the sacral–abdominal wall distance (SAD) measured on standing lateral spine radiographs. A total of 385 women with osteoporosis were included. Obesity was defined using two criteria: BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or body fat percentage ≥ 35%. Higher A/G ratio and greater SAD were independently associated with obesity under both definitions after adjustment for potential confounders. When a combined criterion incorporating both A/G ratio and SAD was applied, the strength of the association with obesity was greater than that observed when either parameter was considered alone in multivariable models. These findings were consistent across the two obesity definitions. The combined use of A/G ratio and SAD may be useful for characterizing obesity-related body composition in women with osteoporosis, particularly in clinical settings where BMI-based assessment is limited by spinal deformity. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal, and further longitudinal studies are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43802-3.

Background

Japan is experiencing a demographic shift, with a total population of 124 million in 2023, of which individuals aged 65 years and over accounting for 29.1% of the total population in 2023. By 2030, the percentage of individuals aged 65 years and over is expected to exceed 30% of the total population1. In 2022, Yoshimura et al. conducted an epidemiological survey of Japanese patients with osteoporosis (mean age 65.2 years) and reported that the prevalence of osteoporosis, as determined by bone mineral density measurements at the lumbar spine or femur, was 4.7% in men and 24.4% in women2. Osteoporosis causes compression fractures of the vertebrae, fractures of the proximal femur, and wrist fractures owing to deterioration in bone density and bone quality3. Fractures can lead to reduced mobility and an increased need for assistance; thus, it is important for older people to prevent and treat osteoporosis.

Weight and body mass index (BMI) are closely related to nutritional status and disease risk4. Weight loss may increase the risk of malnutrition, sarcopenia, and disease progression (diabetes and heart disease)5. Conversely, being overweight can lead to obesity, which increases the risk of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and myocardial infarction6. Excessive weight increases the risk of death, and among women, the 65–79 age group is associated with the highest number of deaths related to high BMI. This places a significant economic burden on society6. Moreover, by 2030, an estimated 60% of the world’s population (3.3 billion people) will have obesity (2.2 billion people) or overweight (1.1 billion people)7. Therefore, proper weight management is crucial.

BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 is defined as overweight, and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 is defined as obesity8. In Japan, if a person is classified as being overweight according to the World Health Organization diagnostic criteria, they are more likely to develop health problems. Therefore, the Japanese Society for the Study of Obesity established BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 to indicate obesity9. Excess body fat is also a contributing factor in metabolic syndrome10. Huang et al. emphasized the importance of using the body fat percentage in addition to BMI11. They categorized patients based on BMI and body fat percentage (normal weight [women: BMI < 25 kg/m2, body fat percentage < 35%; men: BMI < 25 kg/m2], obese [BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2], and normal weight–obese [BMI < 25 kg/m2 and body fat percentage ≥ 35%]) and investigated the prognosis of primary aldosteronism. They found that normal-weight obesity was associated with a higher risk of residual hypertension after adrenalectomy11.

Adipose tissue is broadly classified as either visceral or subcutaneous depending on the site of accumulation12. Visceral fat tissue generally refers to the intra-abdominal fat tissue accumulated in the mesentery and omentum, while subcutaneous fat refers to the fat tissue accumulated mainly in the lower body, such as the lower abdomen and thighs13. Additionally, sex differences in body fat distribution are observed, with visceral fat accumulation more prevalent in men and subcutaneous fat accumulation more prevalent in women12. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) testing not only measures bone density but also muscle mass and fat percentage. Android soft tissue fat percentage measures fat in the abdominal area, while gynoid soft tissue fat percentage measures fat in the buttocks area14. The android soft tissue fat percentage/gynoid soft tissue fat percentage (A/G) ratio can be used as an indicator of fat distribution15. If the A/G ratio is small, it indicates a lower body-dominant subcutaneous fat distribution (subcutaneous fat-type obesity), and if the A/G ratio is large, it indicates an upper body-dominant visceral fat distribution (visceral fat-type obesity)16. The A/G ratio reportedly demonstrates a higher association with cardiovascular metabolic abnormalities than does android-type body fat percentage, gynoid-type body fat percentage, or BMI17. Furthermore, the A/G ratio has demonstrated significant results in complex conditions and cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and metabolic diseases18.

Nagai et al. reported the sacral-abdominal wall distance (SAD) during standing as a new indicator19. SAD can be measured using X-ray images taken during a full spinal or lumbar spine examination. Anatomically, the rectus abdominis muscle originates from the fifth to seventh ribs and the xiphoid process and inserts into the pubic bone to form a skeletal muscle20; it works in conjunction with the internal and external oblique muscles to assist in lumbar flexion, lateral rotation, protection of abdominal organs, regulation of intra-abdominal pressure, and anterior pelvic tilt. Since the abdominal muscles attach from the sternum to the pelvis, spinal flexion reduces abdominal muscle tension and increases SAD19. An increase in kyphotic deformity due to vertebral fractures also increases SAD21. Spinal deformities caused by osteoporosis may also influence obesity22. In patients with osteoporosis aged 65 years or older, when the SAD exceeds 160 mm, adverse effects on physical function, such as prolonged time on the 3-m timed up and go test, reduced grip strength, and shorter time on the one-leg stance test (with eyes open), as well as difficulty standing up from a chair, have been observed21. Furthermore, in patients with osteoporosis aged 65 years or older, an SAD exceeding 160 mm is associated with physical characteristics such as increased waist circumference, increased sagittal vertical axis, and increased pelvic tilt22.

The android soft tissue fat percentage can measure the percentage of fat around the waist, which includes visceral fat and subcutaneous fat. However, Android soft tissue fat percentage reflects central fat distribution, whereas SAD reflects abdominal morphology in the context of spinal alignment and posture. Therefore, combining A/G ratio and SAD may provide complementary information for characterizing obesity-related body composition in patients with osteoporosis. Participants with osteoporosis often develop kyphosis due to vertebral fractures, resulting in a decrease in height. We hypothesized that the BMI at the height when the vertebral fractures were absent would differ from the BMI at the current height with the vertebral fractures. Therefore, we aimed to examine the relationships between obesity and A/G and SAD using a combination of BMI and body fat percentage, rather than the BMI alone.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects at Showa Medical University (approval number: 2024-067-A, registration date: May 21, 2024). Given the retrospective nature of the study, an opt-out consent procedure was adopted, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Patient anonymity was fully preserved. Clinical trial registration was not applicable.

Study design and participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study included women aged 65 years or older who attended outpatient osteoporosis clinics at three medical institutions. Osteoporosis was diagnosed based on established criteria. Patients with implanted cardiac pacemakers were excluded because DXA measurements could not be performed (Fig. 1).

The study aimed to examine associations between obesity and body composition–related indices. Participants were classified into obese and non-obese groups using predefined obesity criteria, and their characteristics were compared. In addition, the combined associations of the android/gynoid fat ratio (A/G ratio) and sacral–abdominal wall distance (SAD) with obesity were evaluated. Although the A/G ratio was available for all participants, SAD could not be measured in some participants because standing lateral spine radiographs were not routinely obtained at two of the three participating institutions.

Bone mineral density and body composition

Body composition was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare). Bone mineral density was measured at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and distal radius. DXA-derived lean mass was used as an estimate of skeletal muscle mass, acknowledging that lean mass represents an approximation rather than a direct measurement of muscle tissue.

Obesity was defined using two criteria. The first definition was based on body mass index (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), which is the standard cutoff for obesity in Japan9. The second definition incorporated body fat percentage to identify normal-weight obesity and was defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or body fat percentage ≥ 35%, based on population-specific criteria11.

Measurement of sacral–abdominal wall distance

The sacral–abdominal wall distance (SAD) was measured on standing lateral spine radiographs as the horizontal distance between the anterior surface of the sacrum and the anterior abdominal wall (Fig. 2). SAD was interpreted as a structural–anthropometric marker reflecting abdominal morphology in the context of spinal alignment and posture, rather than as a direct surrogate for visceral fat mass.

Physical function

Grip strength was measured using a handheld dynamometer (MY-2080; Matsumiya Medical Seiki Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) while participants stood with arms extended at their sides, based on previous studies23,24. Three measurements were taken for each hand, and the maximum value was recorded. Grip strength was included as a descriptive variable but was not incorporated into the primary analysis.

Muscle mass and sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was assessed according to the diagnostic criteria proposed by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) in 201925. In women, low muscle mass was defined as a skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), calculated as appendicular lean mass divided by height squared, of < 5.4 kg/m225.

Nutritional status

Blood samples were obtained as part of routine outpatient clinical care. Fasting status was not standardized. Nutritional status was evaluated using the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score, which incorporates serum albumin concentration, total cholesterol level, and lymphocyte count26,27. Participants with acute inflammatory conditions were not included in the analysis.

Data collection

The following data were collected: age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, fracture history, comorbidities, current osteoporosis medication use, and frailty status. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was used to assess bone mineral density, skeletal muscle mass index, body fat percentage, and regional fat distribution, including android- and gynoid-type soft tissue fat percentages. Muscle strength was assessed using grip strength measurements. Laboratory data included serum calcium, albumin, lipid profiles, intact parathyroid hormone, hemoglobin A1c, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D. The sacral–abdominal wall distance (SAD) was measured using standing lateral spine radiographs.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Comparisons between obese and non-obese groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between obesity and body composition–related indices. To evaluate the combined effect of the A/G ratio and SAD, a binary composite variable was created (A/G ratio ≥ 1.0 and SAD ≥ 160 mm). This composite variable was entered into multivariable logistic regression models with obesity as the dependent variable, adjusting for age, height, and CONUT score.

Sample size considerations were based on the number of outcome events per variable included in multivariable models to ensure adequate statistical robustness. All statistical analyses were performed using Stat Flex software (version 7.0.8; Igaku Tokei Kenkyujo Co., Ltd., Ube, Japan). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

At the osteoporosis outpatient clinic, whole-body bone mineral density was routinely measured except in cases where a pacemaker was implanted in the heart. During the study period, whole-body bone mineral density was measured in 513 patients. A total of 385 patients were analyzed, excluding 37 men, 34 patients aged 50 years or younger, and 57 patients in whom the abdominal wall-sacrum distance could not be measured (Fig. 1). The median age was 74.0 years [66.0–81.0 (first quartile–third quartile)].

Baseline patient characteristics and group allocation

Table 1 shows the participants’ backgrounds. The mean body weight was 48.5 (standard deviation 8.4) kg. The average BMI was 21.1 kg/m2, with 24.7% having BMI < 18.5 kg/m2, 61.8% between 18 and 25 kg/m2, 11.4% between 25 and 30 kg/m2, and 2.1% at or above 30 kg/m2. Fifty-two patients (13.5%) had BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. Osteoporosis medications were used in 93% of cases (Table 1). Pre-existing vertebral fractures were present in 196 patients (50.9%), and a history of femoral neck fractures was present in seven patients (1.8%).

Obesity defined by BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2

When obesity was defined by BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, 52 patients had obesity and 333 had normal weight. When comparing the obese and normal-weight groups, the obese group had significantly lower age (p < 0.001), height (p < 0.001), estimated glomerular filtration rate (p < 0.05), total cholesterol (p < 0.05), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (p < 0.001), and significantly higher BMI (p < 0.001), SMI (p < 0.001), HbA1c (p < 0.01), android soft tissue fat (p < 0.001), gynoid soft tissue fat (p < 0.001), and SAD (p < 0.001). There were no differences in albumin, serum calcium, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (Table 2). We conducted a multivariable logistic regression analysis with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 as the dependent variable and age, SAD, A/G ratio, height, and CONUT as independent variables. Higher SAD (per 10 mm increase) (odds ratio [OR] = 2.13, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.71–2.64), as defined in Table 3, and a higher A/G ratio were significantly associated with obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2). In addition, lower age (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.96) and shorter height (OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.85–0.98) were also associated with a higher likelihood of obesity. (Table 3).

Obesity defined by BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or body fat percentage ≥ 35%

When obesity was defined by BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or body fat percentage of ≥ 35%, 160 patients had obesity and 225 had normal weight. In the group with obesity, age (p < 0.05), weight (p < 0.001) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (p < 0.001) were significantly lower, while BMI (p < 0.001), SMI (p < 0.001), HbA1c (p < 0.05), android soft tissue fat (p < 0.001), gynoid soft tissue fat (p < 0.001), A/G (p < 0.001), and SAD (p < 0.001) were significantly higher. There were no significant differences in height, albumin and calcium levels, estimated glomerular filtration rate, total cholesterol, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (Table 2).

We performed multivariable logistic regression with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or body fat percentage ≥ 35% as the dependent variable and age, SAD, A/G, height, and CONUT as independent variables. A higher SAD (per 10 mm increase) (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 1.31–1.74), a higher A/G ratio (OR = 449.7, 95% CI 100.68–2008.76), lower age (OR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.90–0.97]) and a lower CONUT score (OR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.56–0.93]) were associated with a higher likelihood of obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or body fat percentage ≥ 35%) (Table 3).

Associations of the combined A/G–SAD criterion with obesity

When the combined criterion (A/G ratio ≥ 1.0 and SAD ≥ 160 mm) was entered into a multivariable logistic regression model, meeting both criteria was significantly associated with obesity, independent of age, height, and nutritional status (Supplementary Table S4). The magnitude of the association for the combined criterion was greater than that observed for models including either the A/G ratio or SAD alone, suggesting that the combined use of these parameters provides complementary information for characterizing obesity-related body composition.

Discussion

Main findings

In this study, we examined the associations between obesity and two body composition–related indices, the android/gynoid fat ratio (A/G ratio) derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and the sacral–abdominal wall distance (SAD) measured on standing lateral spine radiographs, in women with osteoporosis. The principal finding was that both higher A/G ratio and greater SAD were independently associated with obesity, regardless of whether obesity was defined using BMI alone or a combined definition incorporating body fat percentage. Furthermore, the combined use of A/G ratio and SAD showed a stronger association with obesity than either parameter alone.

Interpretation of SAD and A/G ratio

The A/G ratio reflects regional fat distribution and has been reported to be more strongly associated with metabolic abnormalities than BMI or absolute fat mass28. However, DXA-based indices do not capture postural changes or spinal deformities, which are common in older adults with osteoporosis. In contrast, SAD, originally proposed as a radiographic indicator of abdominal morphology11, reflects abdominal protrusion in the context of spinal alignment and posture rather than visceral fat mass per se.

Accordingly, SAD should be interpreted as a structural–anthropometric marker that integrates abdominal shape and spinal alignment, rather than as a direct surrogate for visceral adiposity. Prior studies have shown that increased SAD is associated with reduced physical function and altered trunk alignment in patients with osteoporosis11. The present findings extend these observations by demonstrating an association between SAD and obesity-related body composition when considered alongside DXA-derived fat distribution.

Complementary roles of SAD and A/G ratio

Our results suggest that SAD and the A/G ratio provide complementary information. While the A/G ratio characterizes fat distribution, SAD captures structural and postural aspects of abdominal morphology that are not reflected by DXA measurements alone. When these two parameters were considered together, their combined use showed a stronger association with obesity than either parameter alone, supporting the concept that integrating regional fat distribution with structural body morphology may enhance characterization of obesity-related body composition in this population.

Clinical relevance in osteoporosis

Accurate assessment of obesity in patients with osteoporosis presents specific challenges. Vertebral fractures and kyphotic deformities often lead to height loss, which can distort BMI-based classifications. As a result, individuals with preserved fat mass may be misclassified as non-obese when BMI alone is used. To address this limitation, we employed an alternative obesity definition incorporating body fat percentage, which has been proposed to identify normal-weight obesity28.

In this context, the combined use of A/G ratio and SAD should not be interpreted as a predictive or causal model, but rather as a pragmatic phenotypic stratification tool. This approach may be particularly useful in clinical settings where CT-based assessment of visceral fat is impractical or contraindicated.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as risk relationships. Second, SAD is not a specific surrogate for visceral fat and may be influenced by non-adiposity-related factors such as posture or abdominal contents. Although pathological intra-abdominal conditions were unlikely in this outpatient population, direct validation using CT was not available.

Third, a proportion of potentially eligible participants were excluded because SAD could not be measured due to institutional imaging protocols or technical limitations. However, baseline characteristics of excluded participants were comparable to those of included participants, suggesting that major selection bias is unlikely. Fourth, kyphotic angle was not quantitatively assessed; nevertheless, SAD partially reflects postural and alignment-related changes, which may mitigate this limitation. Finally, the study population consisted exclusively of women with osteoporosis, which may limit generalizability.

Baseline characteristics of participants excluded due to unavailable SAD measurements are provided in the Supplementary Material, and no major descriptive differences were observed between excluded and included participants.

Conclusions and future directions

In conclusion, higher A/G ratio and greater SAD were independently associated with obesity in women with osteoporosis. The combined use of these parameters showed a stronger association when both parameters were considered together, suggesting complementary information compared with either measure alone. This combined approach may be useful for characterizing obesity-related body composition in populations where conventional BMI-based assessment is limited. Future longitudinal studies incorporating direct measures of visceral fat and spinal alignment are warranted to further clarify the clinical implications of these findings. The large odds ratios observed for the A/G ratio likely reflect the scale of this continuous variable rather than a direct causal magnitude.
