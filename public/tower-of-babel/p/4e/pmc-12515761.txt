State‐level prevalence of chronic conditions co‐occurring with obesity among US adults
Authors: Lixia Zhao, Alyson B Goodman, Xin Tong, Stephen Onufrak, Samantha L Pierce, Reena B Oza‐Frank, Carlen Stadnik, Brook Belay, Heidi M Blanck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsRecent estimates of chronic conditions (CCs) among adults with obesity are lacking. This study analysed state‐representative data to assess the prevalence of CCs that co‐occur with obesity.Materials and MethodsWe used 2021–2022 Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System data to assess the age‐standardised prevalence of nine CCs among adults with obesity (body mass index ≥30 kg/m2) based on self‐reported weight and height. We examined prevalence overall and by key sociodemographic factors, obesity class, and states and territories, using t‐tests for subgroup comparisons.ResultsThe most prevalent CCs with obesity were hypertension (42.2%), high cholesterol (37.4%), arthritis (30.2%), and depression (26.4%), followed by diabetes (16.7%), asthma (13.4%), heart disease/stroke (9.2%), cancer (6.9%), and kidney disease (3.9%). Prevalence generally increased with age except for depression and asthma, which were lower among those 65 and older compared to those aged 18–44 (p < 0.001). There was an inverse relationship between CCs prevalence and household income. Significant differences were observed across sex, race/ethnicity, education, employment status, healthcare access, and obesity class. Across states and territories, hypertension was the most prevalent CC, ranging from 31.8% in the Virgin Islands (VI) to 51.6% in Alabama, followed by high cholesterol (28.1% in VI to 44.9% in Guam). Other CCs also showed significant variation by state and territory.ConclusionsAmong US adults with obesity, the age‐standardised prevalence of CCs varies significantly across population subgroups and states. Awareness of these differences can help clinicians, public health officials, and decision‐makers address chronic disease prevention and obesity management for populations at higher risk.Keywords: body composition, cardiovascular disease, obesity therapy, population study, type 2 diabetes, weight management

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a complex chronic disease and a major public health concern in the United States, affecting approximately 40% of adults and contributing to increased risk of morbidity and mortality. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Excess adipose tissue (fat cells) leads to the release of inflammatory cytokines and spillage of lipids, which cause both short‐ and long‐term effects on multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and nervous systems. 5 This contributes to the association between obesity and a wide range of other chronic conditions (CCs) such as hypertension (high blood pressure), type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol, heart disease, depression, kidney disease, and some cancers. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 The global prevalence of obesity increased by 130% from 1990 to 2021, with over half of adults worldwide projected to have overweight or obesity and face a growing obesity‐related disease burden by 2050. 12 In the United States, nearly half of adults are expected to have obesity by 2030, with severe obesity becoming the most common body mass index (BMI) category among certain demographic subgroups. 13 By 2050, two in three adults aged 25 and older are projected to have obesity in most states. 14

Although obesity's health effects often involve diabetes, dyslipidaemia and hypertension, it is also an independent risk factor for cardiovascular and kidney diseases. 15 , 16 Obesity, along with its associated conditions, can profoundly affect quality of life and is linked to increased mortality and healthcare costs. 17 , 18 The prevalence and healthcare costs of single or multiple obesity‐related CCs (ORCCs) in the United States are well documented, with data derived from sources such as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) or electronic health records (EHR). 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 A recent EHR study showed an increase in obesity‐related healthcare costs and CCs over time among privately insured US adults. 19 Some studies have used the Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) and the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) to examine the prevalence of diagnosed single and multiple CCs (≥2). 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 However, these studies have primarily focused on either the general population or specific CCs, often overlooking the comprehensive impact of individual CCs among adults with obesity, particularly at the state level.

Despite extensive research on obesity and its associated health risks, there is a notable gap in understanding the current prevalence of CCs that co‐occur with obesity among adults across US states and territories. This study aims to address this gap by analysing state‐representative data from the BRFSS to estimate the prevalence of nine CCs among US adults with obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 based on self‐reported height and weight. Specifically, we aim to provide detailed prevalence estimates of these conditions and assess variations by key sociodemographic and economic characteristics, obesity classes, and across states and territories for the years 2021–2022. Notably, this study provides new insights by presenting comprehensive, age‐adjusted prevalence estimates of CCs co‐occurring with obesity across diverse populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

The data were from the 2021 and 2022 Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), which is an ongoing, cross‐sectional, state‐based random‐digit‐dialled telephone survey of non‐institutionalised US adults aged 18 years or older. BRFSS collects state‐level data from more than 400 000 adults each year to monitor chronic health conditions and health‐related risk behaviours in 50 states, the District of Columbia, and 3 territories. 30 In 2021, Florida did not collect sufficient data to meet the minimum requirement for inclusion in the public‐use data set. The study was exempted from CDC's Institutional Review Board approval because BRFSS is a publicly accessible database.

We excluded adults with missing data on weight and height and those with biologically implausible values (i.e., height <50 cm or >300 cm and weight <20 kg) (n = 94 714). This yielded 772 753 participants in BRFSS 2021–2022. Among them, 33.1% had obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) (n = 258 612). Among adults with obesity, an additional 539 adults with missing education level, 37 395 adults with missing household income, 1587 adults missing employment status, 1666 adults missing healthcare access, and 514 adults who did not report physical activity were excluded only from the analyses that included these variables. Additionally, three territories (n = 16 360) were included when computing prevalence estimates by state and territory, but were excluded from the overall and sociodemographic analyses.

Measures

We defined a person as having a CC if they reported “yes” to the questions on the BRFSS surveys asking: “Have you ever been told by a doctor, nurse or other health professional that you have any of the following: arthritis; a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, or stroke (heart disease/stroke); skin or other types of cancer (cancer); current asthma; depression; diabetes; high cholesterol; high blood pressure; or kidney disease?” Respondents who reported having asthma only as a child and not as an adult were classified as not having asthma. Women who reported having diabetes or high blood pressure only while pregnant were classified as not having those conditions.

We used self‐reported height and weight data to calculate BMI (weight [kg]/height [m] 2 ). Obesity was defined as a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or higher. 31 Obesity was further classified into three classes based on severity (Class I: BMI of 30–34.9 kg/m2, Class II: BMI of 35–39.9 kg/m2, Class III: BMI of 40 kg/m2 or more). 31 Sociodemographic and economic variables included sex (men, women); age group (18–44 years [young adults], 45–64 years [middle‐aged adults], ≥65 years [older adults]); race/ethnicity (Hispanic, non‐Hispanic Asian, non‐Hispanic Black, non‐Hispanic White, and non‐Hispanic Others including other race/ethnicity designation and multiracial); education (high school graduate or General Educational Development [GED] certificate or less, some college, college graduate or above); household income (<$25 000, $25 000–<$50 000, $50 000 or more); employment status (employed, not employed, retired); healthcare provider was defined based on the question: “Do you have one person or a group of doctors that you think of as your personal healthcare provider?” (yes, no); physical activity was defined based on the question: “Adults who reported doing physical activity or exercise during the past 30 days other than their regular job” (yes, no).

Statistical methods

We estimated the weighted prevalence and 95% confidence interval (CI) of obesity as well as self‐reported CCs among US adults with obesity for the overall population, by obesity class, sociodemographic characteristics, and state in 2021–2022. However, data for high cholesterol and high blood pressure were only available for 2021. Age‐standardised prevalence estimates were calculated using the direct method, adjusting to the 2000 US Census population as a standard, with the age groups 18–44, 45–64 and 65 and over. T‐tests were used for comparing prevalence estimates across sociodemographic subgroups. All tests were two‐sided, and p‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. 32 The analyses were conducted in SAS‐callable SUDAAN version 11.0 (RTI international, Research Triangle Park, NC) with survey sample weights to account for the complex sampling design.

RESULTS

Study population characteristics and prevalence of obesity

In 2021–2022, 258 612 survey participants had obesity; the age‐standardised prevalence of self‐reported obesity was 33.1% among US noninstitutionalized adults. The prevalence of obesity was significantly higher among women (34.0%), adults aged 45–64 (38.6%), non‐Hispanic Black adults (42.6%), those with less than a high school education (36.2%), those with less than $25 000 household income (38.2%), the unemployed (34.8%), those with healthcare providers (34.0%) and physically inactive adults (42.7%) compared to their reference group (p < 0.05; Table 1).

Chronic conditions co‐occurring with obesity

Among adults with obesity, the most prevalent CCs were high blood pressure (42.2%), high cholesterol (37.4%), arthritis (30.2%) and depression (26.4%), followed by diabetes (16.7%), asthma (13.4%), heart disease/stroke (9.2%), cancer (6.9%) and kidney disease (3.9%) (Table 2). Prevalence of CCs differed by population subgroups. Age‐standardised prevalence of arthritis, asthma, cancer, depression and kidney disease was significantly higher among women than men (p < 0.001), whereas age‐standardised prevalence of heart disease/stroke, high blood pressure and high cholesterol was significantly higher among men than women (p < 0.001). Prevalence of CCs increased significantly with age for most conditions. For instance, the prevalence of high blood pressure rose from 25.1% in young adults aged 18–44 years to 54.6% in those aged 45–64 years, and further increased to 73.8% in those aged 65 and older (p < 0.001). The rate of diabetes increased from 6.9% (18–44 years) to 24.0% (45–64 years) to 34.1% (65 years and older) (p < 0.001). Exceptions by age were for depression and asthma, with lower prevalence among adults aged 65 years and older compared to those aged 18–44 years (p < 0.001). Prevalence of all CCs was inversely associated with household income (Table 2). Additionally, the prevalence of several CCs varied significantly by race/ethnicity, education, employment status, having a healthcare provider and obesity class (p < 0.05; Table 2); the presence of most CCs was significantly greater in the higher obesity classes. For example, the age‐standardised prevalence of high blood pressure increased from 36.7% (95% CI: [35.8, 37.5]) in class I obesity to 45.6% (44.4, 46.7) in class II obesity, and further to 51.2% (49.9, 52.6) in class III obesity. Similarly, the prevalence of diabetes rose from 13.4% (13.0, 13.7) (class I obesity) to 19.0% (18.4, 19.6) (class II), and then to 24.4% (23.6, 25.2) (class III) (Table 2, Figure 1).

State‐level CCs co‐occurring with obesity

Across states and territories, age‐standardised high blood pressure was the most prevalent CC, ranging from 31.8% in the Virgin Islands (VI) to 51.6% in Alabama, followed by high cholesterol (from 28.1% in VI to 44.9% in Guam), arthritis (from 19% in VI to 39.1% in West Virginia), depression (from 13.2% in Hawaii to 36.8% in Vermont), diabetes (from 12.9% in Montana to 24.4% in Guam), asthma (from 8.5% in Guam to 19.1% in Vermont) and heart disease/stroke (from 6.9% in Colorado to 12.3% in Tennessee and West Virginia). Additionally, we found prevalences of cancer (from 3.6% in Guam to 8.7% in Pennsylvania) and kidney disease (from 2.3% in South Dakota to 5.4% in Georgia) (Table A1, Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the prevalence and distribution of nine diagnosed CCs among adults with obesity in the United States, highlighting significant variations across sociodemographic subgroups as well as states and territories. These data can help inform healthcare and public health practitioners and other decision‐makers regarding resource allocation for the prevention and treatment of obesity and co‐occurring CCs.

The observed high prevalence of high blood pressure (42.2%), high cholesterol (37.4%) and diabetes (16.7%) among adults with obesity underscores the significant burden of these comorbidities in this population. A recent study found high obesity prevalence among adults with hypertension and emphasised the need for obesity control to reduce the overall burden of hypertension and its complications. 33 The substantial prevalence of arthritis (30.2%) and depression (26.4%) further highlight the burden of musculoskeletal and mental health among those with obesity. 8 , 34 Additionally, many CCs, such as heart disease/stroke, cancer, diabetes, and kidney disease, are often associated with high morbidity and mortality. 35 , 36 These conditions collectively contribute to impaired quality of life and increased healthcare utilisation. 37 Given the etiological association between obesity and many CCs, studies have shown that modest weight loss (as low as 5%) can prevent or improve some of these comorbidities, including high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, and high cholesterol. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 Therefore, the prevention of and early intervention for obesity can serve as one avenue to mitigate the progression to CCs.

Age‐related trends revealed an increase in CC prevalence with advancing age, except for depression and asthma, which were more common among younger adults with obesity. Similarly, an NHANES study of adults with hypertension found that depression and asthma were the most common co‐occurring conditions among young adults with obesity, while hypertension and high cholesterol were most prevalent among those aged 65 and older. 29 This underscores the need for life stage‐specific chronic disease preventive measures and early interventions. The inverse relationship between household income and CC prevalence indicates that socioeconomic factors may play a role in the burden of CCs among individuals with obesity in the United States.

Furthermore, the prevalence of CCs varied significantly by race/ethnicity, education, employment status and healthcare access, suggesting the influence of social drivers of health. The rising CC prevalence with higher obesity class highlights the disease burden in those with severe obesity, and the need to prevent its progression. A longitudinal EHR study found rising CKD prevalence across obesity classes (class I: 2.8%, II: 3.5%, III: 4.4%) and a doubling of T2D from 13.1% (class I) to 31.1% (class III), consistent with our findings. Our slightly higher CKD prevalence may reflect broader population coverage and different data years. The EHR study also showed that medical costs of obesity‐related CCs increase with obesity severity. 19 Individuals with class III obesity had 27% higher costs than those with class I obesity in year 1, rising to 41% by year 8. 19 This indicates the potential economic benefit of prevention and early treatment of obesity as a chronic disease.

Geographic disparities were also notable, with states and territories showing a wide range of differences in the age‐standardised prevalence of CCs with obesity. For example, high blood pressure prevalence ranged from 31.8% in the Virgin Islands to 51.6% in Alabama. Such regional variations point to the need for localised strategies across sectors (e.g., healthcare, public health, social services, business and education) that consider the unique demographic, socioeconomic, and cultural contexts of each state or territory. 42

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. BRFSS data are self‐reported, subject to recall and social‐desirability biases. Consequently, self‐reported height and weight may underestimate obesity prevalence due to people's tendency to over‐report their height and under‐report their weight. 43 Additionally, the prevalence of diagnosed CCs reported by participants may be underestimated due to socioeconomic disadvantages, such as lower household income, lower educational status, and barriers to health care access. 44 The use of BMI ≥30 across racial/ethnic subgroups could also impact the findings; for example, Americans of Asian ancestry may have CCs at a lower BMI. 45 Moreover, diagnosed CCs are based on self‐report rather than clinical measures, leading to the omission of undiagnosed conditions, which may vary by condition and according to sociodemographic characteristics or geographically. Furthermore, BRFSS excludes institutionalised adults, particularly those living in nursing homes and long‐term care facilities. Given the relatively higher burden of CCs among older adults residing in these facilities, the prevalence may be underestimated. Nevertheless, BRFSS is the largest health survey system providing state‐representative data to identify groups with or at high risk for CCs.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings from this study evaluated the sociodemographic and diverse geographic distribution of CCs among US adults with obesity. Awareness and better understanding of the varying prevalence of CCs co‐occurring with obesity can help healthcare providers, public health officials, and decision‐makers prioritise resources and interventions to reduce disease burden and improve the health and well‐being of affected communities across the United States.
