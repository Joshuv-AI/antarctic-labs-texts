Evaluating preoperative imaging in patients with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension: Is computed tomography pulmonary angiography a reliable alternative to pulmonary angiography?
Authors: Rawan Alamri, Hamnah Majeed, Fayeza Ahmad, Ziyab Sarfaraz, Andrew Hirsch, Jean-François Morin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension is a potentially curable form of pulmonary hypertension, with pulmonary endarterectomy serving as a key surgical treatment option. Accurate preoperative imaging is critical for determining surgical candidacy and optimizing operative outcomes. Although digital subtraction pulmonary angiography remains the gold standard, computed tomography pulmonary angiography has emerged as a noninvasive alternative. Functional and hemodynamic outcomes were also assessed before and after surgery.ResultsSeventeen patients (mean age 52.8 years [±3.7]; 53% female) were included. Patients experienced postoperative improvements in New York Heart Association functional class, pulmonary artery pressures, and cardiac index. Central Message

CTPA shows high concordance with surgical findings in central and lobar CTEPH, supporting its frontline role, whereas DSPA and intraoperative assessment remain important for complete disease assessment.

Perspective

These findings support CTPA as a frontline modality for central and lobar CTEPH, showing strong concordance with surgical pathology. Persistent segmental discrepancies highlight the limitations of single-modality assessment and reinforce the value of a multimodal imaging approach integrating CTPA and DSPA to optimize preoperative evaluation and surgical planning.

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a rare but potentially curable form of pulmonary hypertension, resulting from incomplete resolution of pulmonary thromboembolic material within the pulmonary arterial tree. Progressive vascular obstruction leads to increased pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular dysfunction, and ultimately right heart failure if left untreated.1 Unlike other forms of pulmonary hypertension, CTEPH offers the possibility of definitive treatment through pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA), which remains the treatment of choice for appropriately selected patients.2, 3, 4

The diagnosis and management of CTEPH requires a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach, integrating clinical evaluation, hemodynamic assessment, and advanced imaging.4,5 Accurate characterization of the anatomic distribution of disease is particularly important because operability is largely determined by the location and extent of thromboembolic obstruction. Early and accurate imaging is essential, because disease confined to surgically accessible vessels (disease involving the main, lobar, and segmental pulmonary arteries) is generally amenable to surgical endarterectomy, whereas more distal disease may be better managed with balloon pulmonary angioplasty.6 Consequently, precise preoperative imaging is critical for treatment selection and operative planning.

The current imaging pathway for CTEPH typically begins with ventilation-perfusion scanning, which remains the preferred screening modality because of its high sensitivity for chronic thromboembolic disease.5,7 Patients with abnormal findings undergo further anatomic assessment using computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), pulmonary angiography, and right heart catheterization. Historically, digital subtraction pulmonary angiography (DSPA) has been considered the reference imaging modality for defining disease extent and evaluating surgical operability because of its ability to directly visualize vascular occlusions, webs, bands, and stenotic lesions.8 However, DSPA is invasive, requires cardiac catheterization, and exposes patients to both ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast.

Advances in multidetector computed tomography have substantially expanded the role of CTPA in the evaluation of CTEPH. Contemporary CTPA provides detailed visualization of the pulmonary arterial tree and can identify characteristic features of chronic thromboembolic disease, including webs, bands, eccentric wall thickening, abrupt vessel cutoffs, and mosaic perfusion abnormalities.9, 10, 11, 12 Several studies have demonstrated excellent performance of CTPA in detecting central and lobar disease; however, challenges remain in the assessment of more distal pulmonary arterial territories.7 As a result, the relative role of CTPA and invasive pulmonary angiography in defining operability and guiding treatment decisions remains an area of ongoing clinical interest.

Importantly, most comparative studies have evaluated imaging modalities against one another or against clinical diagnosis, whereas relatively few studies have examined how well preoperative imaging reflects operative findings at the time of PEA.13,14 Because surgical assessment remains the definitive evaluation of disease distribution in operable CTEPH, understanding the degree of agreement between imaging and operative findings may provide valuable insight into the strengths and limitations of current preoperative imaging strategies.

Accordingly, this retrospective cohort study aimed to compare the agreement of preoperative CTPA and DSPA with operative findings in patients undergoing PEA for CTEPH at a specialized Canadian center. The primary objective was to evaluate which imaging modality most closely reflected the operative distribution of disease using a territory-based anatomic assessment. Secondary outcomes included postoperative functional and hemodynamic measures to provide clinical context for the study cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients (≥18 years), diagnosed with CTEPH at the Jewish General Hospital (Montreal, QC, Canada), who subsequently underwent PEA between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2017.

The Jewish General Hospital is one of few specialized centers in Canada that performs PEA and serves as a major referral center for patients with CTEPH, particularly in Quebec. The study was approved by the Jewish General Hospital Research Ethics Board (No. 2024-3900), which determined that it met institutional scientific and ethical standards. The study was reported in accordance with the STARD 2015 reporting recommendations for diagnostic agreement studies.15

All included patients underwent both CTPA and DSPA as part of their routine preoperative evaluation. Patients with incomplete or unavailable imaging data were excluded.

Imaging Protocols

CTPA was performed using contrast-enhanced multidetector computed tomography with dedicated pulmonary angiographic acquisition to evaluate the pulmonary arterial tree. DSPA was performed during right heart catheterization using selective contrast injection into the right and left pulmonary arteries via 7F high-flow pigtail catheters under fluoroscopic guidance. Digital subtraction angiography acquisitions were obtained in standard anteroposterior and lateral projections.

Study Variables and Concordance Assessment

Demographic and clinical variables were collected from the electronical medical record, including age, sex, prior cardiac surgery, coronary artery disease, and other relevant comorbidities. Preoperative and postoperative variables included New York Heart Association functional class, left ventricular ejection fraction, cardiac index, pulmonary artery pressures, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, and pulmonary vascular resistance.

The operative reference standard was defined as the surgeon's intraoperative assessment as documented in the operative report. Although surgical specimen and intraoperative photographs were available for many patients, these were not used as the primary source for data extraction because they did not consistently capture distal disease. Operative reports were considered the most comprehensive assessment of disease distribution across both proximal and distal pulmonary artery territories.

Preoperative imaging findings from CTPA and DSPA were compared with operative findings using a territory-based anatomic approach. Predefined pulmonary arterial territories included right and left pulmonary arteries, right upper, middle, and lower lobes, left upper and lower lobes, and lingula.

Within each territory, disease was assessed in a binary manner (presence or absence of chronic thromboembolic disease). A territory was considered positive if imaging or operative assessment described chronic occlusion, luminal stenosis, webs or bands, pouch lesions, abrupt vessel cutoff, or other chronic thromboembolic abnormalities.

Concordance was defined as agreement between the imaging modality and the operative reference standard regarding the presence or absence of disease within the same anatomic territory. Discordance was defined as disagreement between imaging and operative findings.

Imaging findings were extracted from finalized radiology reports and documented multidisciplinary clinical assessments. A centralized blinded re-review of imaging was not performed. Operative findings were abstracted retrospectively from operative reports by study investigators. The mean interval between preoperative imaging and PEA was approximately 86 days (2.9 months).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics and 95% CIs were computed using STATA (StataCorp). Agreement between imaging modalities and operative findings was reported descriptively as territory-level concordance. Given the small sample size, incomplete territory-level data, and clustering of multiple vascular territories within individual patients, formal comparative statistical testing was not performed. Only territories with clearly documented findings in both imaging and operative reports were included in the analysis. No imputation of missing data was performed.

Results

Operative Characteristics

Seventeen patients (53% female) with CTEPH who underwent PEA were included. The mean age was 52.8 ± 3.7 years. The mean cardiopulmonary bypass time was 162.9 ± 6.6 minutes, mean cross-clamp time was 68.6 ± 3.8 minutes, and mean total circulatory arrest time was 47.2 ± 3.3 minutes. Median intensive care unit length of stay was 4 days, and the median length of hospital stay was 12 days (Table 1).

Agreement Between Imaging Modalities and Operative Findings

When compared with operative findings, CTPA demonstrated greater concordance in the right main pulmonary artery (76.5%), right middle lobe (21.4%), right lower lobe (13.3%), left upper lobe (70.6%), and lingula (64.7%). DSPA demonstrated greater concordance in the right upper lobe (31.3%), whereas both demonstrated equivalent in the left main pulmonary artery (76.5%) and left lower lobe (17.6%) (Table 2).

Clinical and Hemodynamic Outcomes

The mean follow-up duration after PEA was 1983 days (∼5.4 years), excluding 1 patient who died during the index hospitalization and 1 patient who was lost to follow-up.

Postoperative values represent the most recent available follow-up measurements documented in the medical record rather than a standardized postoperative assessment time point. Follow-up right heart catheterization was available in only 4 patients; therefore, postoperative pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance data were not reported because invasive hemodynamic reassessment was not routinely performed across the cohort.

Patients demonstrated improvement in their New York Heart Association functional class (2.9 ± 0.1 to 2.1 ± 0.2), cardiac index (2.4 ± 0.2 to 2.5 ± 0.1 L/min/m2), and pulmonary artery pressures after surgery (Table 3).

Discussion

CTEPH remains a complex and potentially curable form of pulmonary hypertension when appropriately treated with PEA. Accurate preoperative imaging is essential in determining operability, characterizing disease distribution, and guiding treatment strategy. In this retrospective cohort study, we compared CTPA and DSPA with operative findings in patients undergoing PEA for CTEPH. Our analysis demonstrated that CTPA showed greater agreement with operative findings in several pulmonary arterial territories, particularly the right main pulmonary artery, left upper lobe, and lingula. DSPA demonstrated greater agreement in the right upper lobe, whereas both modalities performed similarly in the left main pulmonary artery and left lower lobes.

These findings support the growing role of CTPA as an important, noninvasive imaging modality in the evaluation of CTEPH, particularly for central and lobar disease. However, persistent discrepancies between imaging and operative findings across multiple territories highlight an important challenge in CTEPH: no currently available imaging modality can completely characterize the full extent of disease encountered at surgery. Accordingly, operative assessment remains an important reference for defining disease distribution in patients undergoing PEA.

The contemporary management of CTEPH has evolved beyond a purely surgical paradigm.4 Although PEA remains the treatment of choice for patients with proximal operable disease, balloon pulmonary angioplasty and targeted medical therapy have expanded treatment options for patients with distal or inoperable disease.16 Within this framework, accurate characterization of both proximal and distal disease is increasingly important, because treatment selection depends on the anatomic distribution of thromboembolic burden. Consequently, the role of preoperative imaging extends beyond diagnosis alone and includes assessment of operability and identification of patients who may benefit from alternative or complementary therapies.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating good agreement between CTPA and operative findings in surgically accessible pulmonary arterial territories.13,14 McInnis and colleagues2 demonstrated meaningful correlation between preoperative CTPA findings and disease distribution encountered at PEA using the Jamieson classification, whereas Grafham and colleagues5 reported overall agreement between CTPA and operative findings with variability across specific pulmonary arterial territories. Consistent with these observations, our study demonstrated strong agreement between CTPA and operative findings within several proximal and lobar territories while also identifying areas where DSPA provided additional information. Notably, DSPA demonstrated greater agreement in the right upper lobe, supporting its continued value in selected anatomic regions and complex cases.

Taken together, these findings support a complementary multimodal imaging strategy rather than reliance on a single imaging modality. CTPA offers several advantages, including widespread availability, noninvasive acquisition, and detailed anatomic assessment. However, pulmonary angiography and right heart catheterization continue to provide important information regarding vascular anatomy and pulmonary hemodynamics that may influence treatment decisions. Therefore, our results support the continued integration of CTPA, DSPA, hemodynamic assessment, and multidisciplinary review when evaluating patients with CTEPH.

In addition to imaging findings, our cohort demonstrated meaningful postoperative improvements in functional status and hemodynamic parameters after PEA, including improvements in New York Heart Association functional class, pulmonary artery pressures, and cardiac index. These findings are consistent with the established benefits of PEA in appropriately selected patients. However, postoperative outcomes were not stratified according to imaging concordance, and our study was not designed to evaluate whether greater agreement between imaging and operative findings translates into improved clinical outcomes. Accordingly, these results should be interpreted as descriptive clinical context rather than evidence of a causal relationship between imaging performance and postoperative recovery.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings in this study. First, this was a single-center nature retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Second, the study cohort was restricted to patients who ultimately underwent PEA and therefore represents a surgically selected population. As a result, our findings may not be generalizable to the broader spectrum of CTEPH, particularly patients with predominantly distal or inoperable disease who may be managed with balloon pulmonary angioplasty or medical therapy. Third, imaging findings and operative assessment were derived from routine clinical reports and multidisciplinary evaluations rather than from a centralized blinded re-review of imaging and operative findings. Although this approach reflects real-world clinical practice, it introduces the potential for interpretation variability and abstraction bias. Fourth, the interval between imaging studies and surgery varied among patients, which may have influenced observed concordance because of interval changes in disease distribution. Finally, incomplete or nonspecific documentation of certain vascular territories resulted in missing territory-level data and variable denominators across analyses, limiting the completeness of territory-specific comparisons.

Despite these limitations, the study provides real-world insight into the agreement between commonly used imaging modalities and operative findings in patients undergoing PEA for CTEPH. Future multicenter studies incorporating larger patient populations, standardized imaging interpretation, formal agreement metrics, and contemporary disease classification systems may further define the relative strengths of individual imaging modalities and their role in guiding treatment selection across the full spectrum of CTEPH.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective study, CTPA demonstrated substantial agreement with operative findings in several central and lobar pulmonary arterial territories among patients undergoing PEA for CTEPH. Although these findings support the role of CTPA as an important frontline imaging modality, discrepancies persisted between imaging and operative assessment, particularly across certain vascular territories. Accordingly, our results support the continued use of a multimodality approach integrating CTPA, DSPA, hemodynamic assessment, and multidisciplinary evaluation when determining operability and treatment strategy in CTEPH. Larger prospective studies are needed to further define the optimal role of each imaging modality across the spectrum of CTEPH disease.
