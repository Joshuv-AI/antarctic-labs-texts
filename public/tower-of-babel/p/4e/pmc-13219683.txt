Updating radiographic parameters for the healthy growing hip: are current parameters still valid?
Authors: Konstanze Hütter, Maria Anna Smolle, Sophie Butter, René Schroedter, Sebastian Tschauner, Tanja Kraus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate whether the acetabular angle (AA) by Tönnis and lateral center–edge angle (LCEA) by Wiberg—key radiographic parameters in pediatric hip assessment—have changed in a contemporary pediatric population compared with historical reference values, considering trends in earlier skeletal maturation and body composition.Materials and methodsThis retrospective diagnostic accuracy study assessed changes in AA and center–edge angle (CE) angles in a contemporary cohort. A total of 1774 anteroposterior pelvic radiographs (3548 hips) from patients aged 0–18 years without hip pathology were analyzed. Radiographs were obtained between 2006 and 2018 and measured using the Supervisely digital platform. Standardized anatomical landmarks were applied. Data were stratified by age and sex and compared with historical values using two-sample t-tests (p < 0.05). To minimize measurement bias, two independent observers conducted all assessments using standardized digital protocols.ResultsA total of 1774 patients aged 0–18 years (mean age, 8.30 ± 5.20 years; 666 females, 1108 males) were evaluated. AA values did not show statistically significant differences in 11 of 16 age groups compared with historical data (p > 0.05). In contrast, LCEAs were significantly higher in the contemporary cohort, especially in the 5–8, 9–12, and 12–16 age groups (all p < 0.01), indicating a trend toward earlier or increased acetabular ossification and femoral head coverage.ConclusionsLCEA values were significantly higher in the contemporary pediatric cohort compared with historical data, while AA values remained stable across most age groups. These findings suggest that current radiographic reference standards for LCEA may warrant reassessment to ensure accurate dysplasia assessment.Level of evidence: level 3 (diagnosis).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10195-026-00917-7.

Introduction

Accurate evaluation of pediatric hip development relies on key radiographic parameters, particularly the acetabular angle (AA) by Tönnis and the lateral center–edge angle (LCEA) by Wiberg [1]. These parameters support diagnosis of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) by quantifying acetabular orientation and femoral head coverage throughout growth [2, 3].

In early childhood, much of the hip joint is cartilaginous and invisible on conventional radiographs. As a result, age-specific normative values are essential for accurate radiographic assessment. These reference values are particularly important during early childhood—a period marked by rapid skeletal growth and ossification [4].

While these parameters remain widely used, contemporary shifts in pediatric health—such as rising obesity rates and decreased physical activity—may influence hip biomechanics and skeletal maturation. A sedentary lifestyle has been associated with declining cardiovascular fitness, altered musculoskeletal development, and increasing rates of obesity. Evidence suggests that reduced mechanical loading from inactivity contributes to decreased muscle mass, lower bone mineral density, and postural imbalances, all of which may impact skeletal development with potential long-term orthopedic consequences [5]. Conversely, accelerated skeletal maturation has been observed in children with overweight and obesity [6, 7]. Altered body composition, potentially leading to earlier physeal closure, may influence hip morphology [8, 9] and modify baseline radiographic parameters such as the AA and LCEA.

In addition to changes in body composition, other factors may limit the applicability of historical reference values to contemporary pediatric populations. Secular trends toward earlier skeletal maturation have been reported, with physeal fusion occurring at younger ages in recent birth cohorts compared with those used to establish traditional standards [10]. Furthermore, advances in digital imaging and measurement techniques may influence the delineation of anatomical landmarks on radiographs [11].

Despite the widespread clinical use of AA and LCEA, the validity of their original reference values in today’s children remains largely untested. This gap is clinically relevant: Outdated reference ranges may lead to misclassification of normal or borderline findings [12], potentially affecting diagnosis and management. Considering contemporary changes in body composition, skeletal maturation, imaging techniques, and population characteristics, reassessment of these parameters in modern pediatric cohorts appears warranted. The aim of this study was to compare AA and LCEA values in a contemporary pediatric cohort with historical reference standards.

Methods

This was a retrospective, single-center study based on radiographic data from a contemporary cohort from 2006 to 2018. This study period ensured a complete dataset with consistent radiographic protocols. The study was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB, ethics committee no. 32-116 ex 19/20), with informed consent waived owing to the retrospective design.

Pelvic radiographs of growing pediatric patients—predominantly of Caucasian descent—who received care at our institution between 2006 and 2018 were analyzed. Radiographs were obtained as part of routine clinical evaluation for hip pain or to rule out trauma. All imaging data were retrieved from the hospital’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), ensuring standardized and consistent radiograph quality. All pediatric patients aged 0–18 years with at least one anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiograph and no history of hip-altering conditions, such as previous hip or near-hip fractures, developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), or Perthes disease, were potentially eligible. Excluded were patients older than 18 years, as well as those with traumatic or pathological hip conditions (e.g., congenital hip dislocation, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, Perthes disease, or cerebral palsy), systemic disorders affecting skeletal development (e.g., achondroplasia), follow-up radiographs of the same patient taken less than 6 months apart, and radiographs with improper positioning.

The acetabular angle (AA) was measured on standardized anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiographs as the angle between Hilgenreiner’s line and the acetabular roof [13, 14]. The lateral center–edge angle (LCEA) was measured according to the original method described by Wiberg, using the lateral osseous acetabular rim as the reference point on AP pelvic radiographs [15]. Alternative definitions based on the lateral sourcil have been reported to produce systematically different measurements [16]. The original Wiberg definition was chosen to maintain consistency with the historical reference values used for comparison. Because accurate identification of the femoral head center is not feasible in early childhood owing to incomplete ossification, LCEA measurements were not performed in children aged 0–5 years [17].

All pelvic radiographs were performed using a standardized positioning protocol according to Zimmer-Brossy [18]. Patients were placed supine with legs parallel and knees extended. The feet were internally rotated until the halluces touched, maintaining a heel-to-heel distance of approximately 3–4 cm to ensure proper alignment of the femoral necks. A cushion was placed beneath the knees to reduce lumbar lordosis, and abdominal compression was applied in obese patients to minimize image distortion. This standardized setup ensured symmetrical pelvic alignment and optimal visualization of the triradiate cartilages. Only radiographs meeting these criteria were included in the analysis.

The radiographic measurements were performed using the radiological Supervisely platform (Supervisely OÜ, Tallinn, Estonia), a web-based annotation and image analysis tool designed for medical imaging and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted workflows. Two independent investigators (S.B. and S.T.) manually positioned the anatomical landmarks (Fig. 1), and measurements were conducted using calibrated tools within the platform to ensure anatomical accuracy. All landmarks were defined according to established radiographic conventions to ensure consistency and comparability with prior studies. Statistical analysis

Age group- and sex-stratified comparisons of contemporary data with historical reference values from the book by Tönnis [14] were conducted using two-sample t-tests (equal or unequal variance, as appropriate) to assess statistical differences. Because historical reference data were available only as aggregate summary statistics, comparisons were limited to group-level analyses rather than individual-level modeling. Multiplicity was accounted for using the Bonferroni method. Significance was set at p < 0.05. To assess the robustness of the main findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a refined version of the dataset in which repeated patient entries were excluded (47 patients with two and 6 patients with three radiographs). In addition, all radiographic data were reanalyzed by age in single-year increments (e.g., 5, 6, and 7 years) instead of grouped categories. The same statistical approach (two-sample t-tests) was applied. Age groups were stratified according to the original categorization in the historical reference by Tönnis to allow direct comparison.

Results

A total of 3786 radiographs were reviewed. Of these, 1774 radiographs (3548 hips) met the inclusion criteria, while the remaining were excluded owing to poor positioning, age > 18 years, or known musculoskeletal pathology. The final study population consisted of 666 female patients (1332 hips) and 1108 male patients (2216 hips) (Fig. 2). Right and left hip measurements were analyzed separately. No consistent directional trends between sides were observed in group means. A sensitivity analysis excluding images of patients with double or triple entries yielded similar results to the initial analysis. Discussion

This study showed that LCEA values were significantly higher in a contemporary pediatric cohort compared with historical data, while AA values remained stable across most age groups. Several factors call for a critical re-evaluation of these historical norms. First, technological advances—particularly the widespread use of digital radiography—have improved image quality and measurement standardization, potentially influencing radiographic parameters [19]. Second, demographic, lifestyle, and biological changes over recent decades may have altered skeletal development patterns, raising questions about the continued validity of reference data established in earlier populations [6–8, 20, 21].

The AA values in our contemporary cohort showed no significant deviation from the historical reference data across all age groups. The age-dependent decline in the AA followed the expected developmental trajectory described by Tönnis in the historic cohort, indicating that acetabular roof orientation has remained stable over time [14, 22]. This stability may be attributed to the nature of the AA itself: as it is derived solely from acetabular bony landmarks, it might be less susceptible to variation caused by pelvic tilt or positioning errors [23]. Previous studies have also reported high inter- and intra-observer reliability for this parameter, reinforcing its value as a robust and consistent index for evaluating acetabular morphology in growing children [24–27]. Furthermore, acetabular ossification may progress more slowly than femoral head ossification, rendering acetabular-based parameters like the AA less affected by individual differences in skeletal maturation and early developmental shifts—changes that may already become apparent in LCEA measurements [28].

The LCEAs in this study were elevated from age 5 years onward and remained consistently higher throughout adolescence. This pattern may suggest that hip development in today’s pediatric population may occur earlier than in previous generations. Clinically, higher LCEA values may influence the interpretation of femoral head coverage and borderline dysplasia in growing children. If contemporary normative values are shifted upward, reliance on historical thresholds may lead to misclassification of hip morphology and potentially affect treatment decisions. However, the present study did not assess diagnostic classification or clinical outcomes, and therefore the direct clinical impact of these differences cannot be determined. LCEA measurements were not conducted in children under 5 years owing to insufficient and asymmetric ossification of the femoral head, which precludes reliable determination of its center. Since the LCEA reflects both lateral acetabular morphology and femoral head positioning, the observed increase likely results from a combination of three factors. First, as a morphological factor, accelerated ossification and age-related growth of the femoral head—particularly through symmetric epiphyseal extension—may lead to a medial shift of its geometric center, thereby increasing apparent lateral coverage and elevating LCEAs, even without changes in acetabular contour [8, 29, 30]. Second, biomechanical loading during development—amplified by higher body mass index (BMI) or greater body mass—may stimulate earlier or more pronounced structural adaptation [31, 32]. Third, technical factors may also contribute: Enhanced contrast and resolution in modern digital radiographs improve delineation of the lateral acetabular margin, potentially yielding systematically higher LCEA values compared with earlier analog imaging [23]. Similar increases in LCEAs have been observed in other populations: Tuğrul et al. reported a steady age-related rise in Turkish children [33], while a Saudi Arabian cohort study linked higher center–edge angle (CE) values to elevated BMI, suggesting that altered body composition may promote earlier skeletal maturation [32].

The divergent trends between LCEA and AA values can be explained by their distinct anatomical definitions. The AA is derived solely from acetabular landmarks and remains unaffected by femoral development or spatial shifts, which likely contributes to its temporal stability. In contrast, LCEA measurements incorporate the femoral head center, making them sensitive to growth-related positional changes. Advances in imaging may further amplify this effect by improving visualization of the lateral acetabular edge, particularly in early ossification stages [23].

This study has several limitations. First, historical data were limited to aggregate means and standard deviations, precluding individual-level analyses and multivariable modeling. Second, the retrospective design prevents assessment of longitudinal acetabular development. Third, the use of single-center recruitment introduces potential selection bias. Fourth, variability in imaging technique, positioning, and beam angulation between historical and contemporary radiographs may affect measurement comparability. Fifth, although changes in body composition and BMI were considered as potential explanatory factors, no BMI data were available in the current cohort, and no associations between anthropometric parameters and angle measurements could be assessed. Ethnicity was not available in the dataset, and the single-center cohort likely reflects a relatively homogeneous Central European population, which may limit generalizability to more diverse populations. Formal inter- and intra-observer reliability testing was not performed. However, previous studies have demonstrated good inter- and intra-observer agreement for commonly used pediatric hip radiographic parameters, including the acetabular angle and LCEA, on standardized AP pelvic radiographs [34, 35]. Although the cohort represents the most recent dataset available at our institution and is contemporary relative to historical reference populations, it does not reflect the most current pediatric population, and secular trends in growth and maturation may have continued since the end of the study period. Because this study was not designed to identify causal mechanisms, the observed differences should be interpreted as descriptive rather than explanatory and should primarily be viewed as hypothesis generating for future prospective studies. Future research should employ prospective, longitudinal, multicenter cohorts with standardized imaging protocols and integrated clinical data to refine diagnostic thresholds.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that LCEA values in contemporary pediatric populations are significantly higher than historical reference values, while AAs have remained stable. This divergence may reflect a combination of accelerated skeletal maturation, biomechanical loading factors such as increased body mass and improved imaging resolution. As LCEA measurements incorporate both acetabular and femoral components, their sensitivity to developmental and technical influences may warrant re-evaluation. In contrast, the anatomical stability and measurement robustness of the AA support its continued use as a reliable reference parameter. Future diagnostic frameworks should account for these shifts by integrating longitudinal data, refined imaging protocols, and contemporary population characteristics.
