The association between AST/ALT (De Ritis) ratio and osteoporosis or bone mineral density in middle-aged and older adults: A cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data
Authors: Hongling Zhao, Shiyu Peng, Shuwen Zheng, Qiurui Li, Dong Zhao, Jing Ke, Longyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis (OP), a metabolic bone disease characterized by low bone mineral density (BMD), has an underexplored link with the De Ritis ratio (aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase) – a marker of metabolic and inflammatory status. This study investigated the association between the De Ritis ratio and both OP and BMD in middle-aged and older adults. BMD was assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and OP was diagnosed based on a T-score ≤−2.5. First, multivariate logistic regression was employed to estimate the adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) for OP in relation to the De Ritis ratio, with covariates including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, parental OP history, and key liver markers. Second, multivariable linear regression analyzed the association between the De Ritis ratio and BMD, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. Finally, the predictive ability of the De Ritis ratio was assessed via receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, with results expressed as the area under the curve. In 2483 subjects (233 OP cases, 9.39%), a higher De Ritis ratio was closely associated with a greater risk of OP after full adjustment. Subgroup analyses asserted this positive association in females, individuals with a BMI < 30 kg/m2, individuals without hypertension/diabetes/parental OP, and individuals across liver marker subgroups. The multivariable linear regression suggested that participants in the highest De Ritis ratio tertile (T3) had significantly lower BMD at the total femur (β = −0.062, P = .002; P-trend = .002) and femoral neck (β = −0.049, P = .025; P-trend = .018) than those in the lowest tertile (T1). Stratified analyses consistently revealed negative correlations between the De Ritis ratio and BMD at both sites. The De Ritis ratio provided an area under the curve value of 0.644 (95% confidence interval = 0.606–0.681) for OP in middle-aged and older adults. Our findings indicate that a higher De Ritis ratio is significantly associated with an increased risk of OP and lower BMD in middle-aged and older adults, suggesting its potential utility as a useful adjunct clinical tool for OP risk stratification. However, the complex relationship underlying this association warrants further validation in large-scale prospective studies.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a whole-body skeletal condition with typical features, including deteriorated bone microarchitecture and significantly decreased bone mineral density (BMD), which increases the risk of bone fragility and fracture.[1–3] Globally, OP affects more than 19.7% of adults, with a significantly greater prevalence in older populations.[4,5] OP-associated fragility fractures contribute to severe morbidity, chronic disability, and substantial healthcare expenditures, making them a pressing public health challenge.[6–9] Early-stage OP generally progresses asymptomatically and remains undiagnosed until fractures occur. Therefore, it is critical to explore possible risk factors or biomarkers and intervene timely for OP.

Metabolic diseases are recognized as significant risk factors for OP, with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) serving as a key hepatic manifestation. The pathological states associated with MASLD, such as chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance, collectively create a systemic environment detrimental to both bone and muscle homeostasis. This not only disrupts the balance of bone remodeling mediated by osteoblasts and osteoclasts but may also lead to the development of sarcopenia. Notably, sarcopenia and OP often coexist, forming “osteosarcopenia,” where muscle loss further exacerbates bone fragility due to diminished mechanical stimulation. For example, Wang et al indicated that MASLD may aggravate the inflammatory state in elderly OP patients by disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby accelerating functional decline.[10] Furthermore, bidirectional signaling between the liver and bone tissues through endocrine and metabolic factors jointly regulates metabolic functions, suggesting a potential causal relationship between OP and MASLD.[11,12] Thus, exploring accessible MASLD-related biomarkers is crucial for evaluating OP risk in this shared metabolic setting.

Liver enzymes, especially alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), are essential indicators of liver function. ALT is a transaminase that is primarily localized in hepatocytes and skeletal muscle, whereas AST can be detected in organs such as the liver, cardiac tissue, and skeletal muscle.[13,14] The De Ritis ratio (AST/ALT), originally proposed by De Ritis as a prognostic indicator for viral hepatitis severity in 1957,[15] is a widely recognized biomarker of hepatic injury.[16] The De Ritis ratio is related to insulin resistance and metabolic disorders; therefore, it can be used to assess various pathologies, including malignancies, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular conditions.[17–20] Metabolic dysregulation adversely affects skeletal homeostasis, potentially accelerating the pathogenesis of OP.[21,22] A large epidemiological study in Western China reported that higher serum AST/ALT levels are linked with higher sarcopenia incidence in adults aged ≥50 years.[23] Sarcopenia and OP share convergent pathophysiological mechanisms, including dysregulated inflammatory pathways, genetic susceptibility, ectopic lipid accumulation in the bone marrow and muscle, and endocrine factors.[24] Furthermore, a Korean cross-sectional study revealed that the correlation of liver enzyme levels (including AST and ALT) and BMD of femoral neck,[25] and a cross-sectional study in China elevated AST/ALT ratio is associated with the evaluated risk of OP in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).[26] However, direct epidemiological evidence establishing the association between the De Ritis ratio and OP risk or BMD remains limited, particularly in middle-aged and older adults, and existing studies are largely confined to Asian cohorts. To address this gap and examine the generalizability of these associations across diverse populations and healthcare settings, future studies are warranted in multiethnic and multiregional cohorts.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) constitutes a nationally representative sample of the US population. To date, studies on the correlation between the AST/ALT ratio and OP in middle-aged and older adults in the United States are limited. The aim of this study was to determine the association between the AST/ALT ratio and OP or BMD in middle-aged and older adults in the United States.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources and study population

The NHANES, which was established in 1959, performs annual cross-sectional assessments of health and nutritional status in the United States. Data encompassing demographics, dietary intake, laboratory measurements, and questionnaire responses are released biennially. The data supporting this study’s findings are available at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx. The National Center approved the protocols for NHANES for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Institutional Review Board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The NHANES study was approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board in the United States. The current study only involved secondary data analysis of the existing NHANES database, which is publicly available and has been de-identified, thus there is no need to apply for approval from our institution. Considering that the recent data are representative and include complete diagnostic data on OP, we enrolled participants ≥40 years old who had complete laboratory data and BMD measurements from NHANES cycles 2013 to 2014 and 2017 to 2020 (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were the following: age ≥ 40 years; participants had dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan of femur and spine and full data available for this scan; and had complete data for ALT, AST, sex, race, body weight, body height, birthplace, education, marital status, history of smoking, history of drinking, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, history of OP in parents, history of previous fractures, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, total bilirubin, uric acid, cholesterol, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and albumin. The exclusion criteria were the following: age < 40 years; participants had incomplete data for femoral/spine DXA scans, ALT, AST, or any covariate. We used listwise deletion without imputation; participants with any missing value were excluded.

Flowchart of participant selection. NHANES = The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. BMD measurement and OP diagnosis

By employing Hologic QDR-4500A fan-beam densitometers (Hologic, Inc.), BMD was determined at the total femur, total spine, and femoral neck based on NHANES using DXA. In general, left hip examinations were performed, whereas right hip examinations were performed only for self-reported left hip replacements, pins, and fractures. Total spine BMD represented the average BMD of the 1st to 4th lumbar vertebrae. Pregnant patients, those with bilateral hip pin/fracture/replacement history, those with radiographic contrast agent application history, and those with >450 lbs did not undergo DXA examinations. OP was defined based on the mean peak BMD and corresponding standard deviation for total hip, femoral neck, and total spine in young white women (aged 20–29 years) from the US NHANES III database. BMD values measured at these sites were converted to T‑scores using established methods.[27–29] T scores ≤−2.5, between −2.5 and −1, and >−1 represent OP, osteopenia, and normality, respectively. The healthy and osteopenic participants were classified as non-OP.

2.3. Variables

Certified personnel implemented standardized data collection protocols across all NHANES study sites. BMD at the total spine, total femur, and femoral neck and the incidence of OP served as dependent variables, and the De Ritis ratio was the primary independent variable. The covariates included race (Mexican American; other Hispanic; non-Hispanic White; non-Hispanic Black; other race), sex (male and female), education (<9th grade; 9–11th grade; high school; some college; college graduate), age, marital status (married or living with partner; widowed, divorced or separated; never married), birthplace (the 50 US states or Washington D.C and other place), body mass index (BMI, i.e., body weight [kg] divided by body height squared [m2]), history of smoking (smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life?), history of drinking (had at least 12 alcohol drinks/1 year?), history of hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg), history of diabetes (glycohemoglobin [%] ≥6.5% and/or fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L), history of OP in parents (parents ever told had OP?), history of previous fractures (self-reported information regarding fractured hip, wrist, or spine), alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, total bilirubin, uric acid, cholesterol, GGT, and albumin. Patient body weights (kg) were measured with a digital weight scale. Patient standing heights were measured with a stadiometer that contained 1 fixed vertical backboard along with 1 adjustable headpiece. Among these covariates, we selected confounding factors that might influence OP and liver function indicators to conduct subgroup analysis and interaction analysis.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Nationally representative estimates were generated using NHANES sampling weights with stratification and clustering adjustments. The normally distributed continuous variables were represented as the mean ± standard deviation in the descriptive analysis and analyzed by Student t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables, represented as medians and quartile ranges, were analyzed by Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables, represented by frequency and frequency percentage, were analyzed by conducting the chi-square test. Furthermore, logistic regression models were constructed after categorizing the De Ritis ratio into tertiles to examine its associations with OP. Model 1 was adjusted for sex, age, race, birthplace, education level, marital status, history of hypertension, and history of diabetes. Model 2 was further adjusted for history of OP in parents, previous fractures, history of smoking, history of drinking, and creatinine. Model 3 additionally adjusted for GGT, total bilirubin, cholesterol, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, uric acid, and BMI. In addition, multicollinearity assessment showed all VIF values <5. Multivariate linear regression models were then constructed to analyze the relationships between the De Ritis ratio and BMD at the total femur, femoral neck, and total spine. Stratified analysis was performed to examine key covariate-based effect modifications. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were performed, and the area under the curve values were estimated. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSSAU (Beijing Qingsi Technology Co., Ltd.) and R (version 3.5.3; R Core Team). All differences were deemed statistically significant at P < .05 (two-tailed).

3. Results

3.1. Basic features of the study cohorts stratified by OP status

In this study, 2482 participants were enrolled following the exclusion of nonqualified individuals (Fig. 1). The characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. The cohort comprised 52.3% females and 47.7% males. Weighted distributions of characteristics were analyzed according to the status of OP. Compared to the non-OP group, the osteoporotic group presented significant differences in several characteristics. Individuals with OP were predominantly female, older, and had lower body weights. Their education level was lower and they were mostly widowed, divorced, or separated. In addition, the OP group reported a greater prevalence of parental OP history and previous fractures and included a lower proportion of non-Hispanic Black participants. Biochemically, the osteoporotic group presented a higher De Ritis ratio, increased cholesterol levels, and increased alkaline phosphatase. In contrast, they presented lower AST, ALT, creatinine, GGT, total bilirubin, albumin, and uric acid levels. The BMD of the OP group was considerably lower at the total spine, total femur, and femoral neck sites.

3.2. Relationship of the De Ritis ratio with the OP

The relationship between the De Ritis ratio and OP risk was estimated through multivariable logistic regression (Table 2). According to all 3 progressively adjusted models, higher De Ritis ratios were significantly and positively associated with increased OP risk when analyzed as a continuous variable: Model 1 (odds ratio [OR] = 1.986, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.377–2.865), Model 2 (OR = 2.060, 95% CI = 1.425–2.978), and Model 3 (OR = 1.573, 95% CI = 1.056–2.342). When the De Ritis ratio was analyzed categorically by tertiles (T1–T3, with T1 as a reference), a dose–response relationship could be observed: Model 1 (OR = 1.007, 95% CI = 0.658–1.540; OR = 1.863, 95% CI = 1.251–2.776; P for trend < .001), Model 2 (OR = 1.007, 95% CI = 0.657–1.543; OR = 1.903, 95% CI = 1.272–2.846; P for trend < .001), and Model 3 (OR = 0.856, 95% CI = 0.547–1.137; OR = 1.350, 95% CI = 0.879–2.072; P for trend = .051). These analyses suggest an initial dose–response trend between an elevated De Ritis ratio and a higher incidence of OP. However, this association was attenuated and lost statistical significance in Model 3, indicating that the relationship may be partially mediated or confounded by the covariates included in this model.

3.3. Relationship between the De Ritis ratio and BMD

The outcomes of multivariate linear regression performed to establish the relationship between the De Ritis ratio and BMD at different sites are presented in Table 3. All multivariable linear regression models revealed negative correlations between the De Ritis ratio and the BMD at the total femur (β = −0.114, P < .001; β = −0.060, P = .001), femur neck (β = −0.083, P < .001; β = −0.038, P = .041), and total spine (β = −0.046, P = .019; β = −0.001, P = .966). After the De Ritis ratio was converted into a categorical variable (tertiles) from a continuous variable, the De Ritis ratio exhibited an apparent negative relationship with the BMD of the total femur in Model 1 (β = −0.032, P = .109; β = −0.129, P < .001; P for trend < .001) and Model 2 (β = −0.006, P = .766; β = −0.062, P = .002; P for trend = .002). Similarly, the De Ritis ratio was associated with femur neck BMD in Model 1 (β = −0.024, P = .256; β = −0.104, P < .001; P for trend < .001) and Model 2 (β = −0.002, P = .912; β = −0.049, P = .025; P for trend = .018). These analyses demonstrated that an elevated De Ritis ratio is inversely associated with BMD at the total femur and femur neck. However, this association was not observed for the total spine in the fully adjusted model, indicating a site-specific relationship.

3.4. Relationships of the De Ritis ratio with OP or BMD in subgroup analyses

To determine variations in the relationship between the De Ritis ratio and the incidence of OP, this study performed subgroup analyses, stratifying participants based on their age, sex, BMI, hypertension status, diabetes status, history of OP in parents, GGT levels, total bilirubin levels, and alkaline phosphatase levels (Fig. 2). While point estimates suggested trends, most interaction P-values were not statistically significant, indicating limited evidence for effect modification across the subgroups analyzed. Notably, sex-stratified analysis revealed a strong, positive relationship between a higher De Ritis ratio and increased risk of OP among females (OR = 1.836, 95% CI = 1.154–2.919; P for interaction = .383). Individuals aged >60 years showed a stronger positive association (OR = 1.836, 95% CI = 1.154–2.919; P for interaction = .383) than younger participants. It should be noted that some subgroup estimates were imprecise, as indicated by wide confidence intervals. Similarly, subgroup analyses for BMD outcomes are presented in Table 4. Although not all interaction terms were significant, the negative association between a higher De Ritis ratio and BMD appeared more pronounced in the point estimates for females, participants >60 years old, and those with BMI ≤ 30 kg/m2. These observations should be interpreted with caution due to the nonsignificant interactions and, in some cases, limited precision of the estimates.

3.5. Predictive ability of the De Ritis ratio for OP

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive ability of the De Ritis ratio for OP. It demonstrated limited discriminative ability, with an area under the curve of 0.644 (95% CI = 0.606–0.681). The optimal cutoff value was identified as 1.2367, suggesting that a De Ritis ratio higher than this threshold is associated with a higher risk of OP in middle-aged and older adults (Fig. 3).

4. Discussion

This large cross-sectional study comprehensively examines the correlation between the De Ritis ratio (AST/ALT) and the incidence of OP coupled with BMD measurements via the NHANES database. Our findings showed that the De Ritis ratio was associated with the incidence of OP, and that higher De Ritis ratios were associated with a decrease in total femur/femoral neck BMD. These associations remained consistent across stratified analyses. The ROC curve analysis showed a significant association between the De Ritis ratio and OP presence. While this suggests potential discriminatory capacity, its standalone predictive utility requires further validation in prospective studies and comparison with established risk factors. The results could provide new insights into bone metabolism and contribute to the early detection and risk stratification to improve adults’ bone health.

Previous studies on the De Ritis ratio focused on its associations with muscle mass alterations in T2DM, cardiometabolic diseases, and gastrointestinal malignancies.[19,30–32] Some studies have shown that this ratio is a biomarker of oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, with elevated values reflecting hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome, which collectively indicate hepatic injury and progressive inflammatory states.[16,33,34] Despite these established associations, the correlation of the De Ritis ratio with OP has been reported previously. The clinical importance of this ratio stems from its advantages as an accessible biomarker: ALT and AST are routinely measured worldwide because of their low cost, rapid detection, and suitability for serial monitoring.[30,35] While ALT is predominantly hepatic, AST has a broader tissue distribution (heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, erythrocytes, and brain), making it a recognized marker of multiorgan damage.[30] Notably, emerging links to musculoskeletal health provide biological plausibility for bone metabolism associations. For example, a higher AST/ALT ratio is related to a higher incidence of sarcopenia in adults >50 years.[23] The ALT/AST ratio is negatively related to the muscle mass of T2DM patients,[30] and the serum AST/ALT ratio contributes to the diagnosis of sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults in China.[23] Sarcopenia and OP are age-related degenerative diseases with similar pathophysiological foundations, including mechanistic, genetic, fatty infiltration, and endocrine factors.[24,36] These connections provide a mechanistic context for our key findings. We found similar results in this study, with a prominent independent relationship between a higher De Ritis ratio and a higher incidence of OP. Moreover, the De Ritis ratio was strongly and negatively correlated with BMD at weight-bearing sites after full covariate adjustment. Therefore, the De Ritis ratio may serve as a potential biomarker indicative of osteoinflammatory states.

In this study, subgroup analyses revealed significant effect modification by demographic factors. Females exhibited considerably greater susceptibility to OP associated with elevated De Ritis ratios than males. Similarly, participants aged >60 years showed a substantially greater risk of OP than younger participants. Consistent inverse associations were observed between the De Ritis ratio and BMD at weight-bearing sites, including the total femur and femoral neck. These stratification patterns were similar to established sarcopenia relationships, suggesting that the ratio may function as a composite biomarker for musculoskeletal deterioration.[23,30] While the underlying mechanistic pathways require further elucidation, we propose an integrative model to explain this association. First, the release of skeletal muscle-derived AST isozymes during myonecrosis may signal concurrent musculoskeletal decline; second, an elevated ratio, sensitive to hepatic steatosis, likely reflects systemic metabolic dysfunction that adversely affects bone; third, chronic inflammation may promote osteoclastogenesis via cytokine cascades such as receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and interleukin-6. Therefore, further studies are warranted to comprehensively elucidate the biological mechanisms linking the De Ritis ratio to bone metabolism.

This study had several advantages. First, the relationship between the De Ritis ratio and the risk of OP among representative American adults was evaluated. Its relatively large sample size ensured the high statistical precision and robustness of our estimates. Second, subgroup analyses were conducted to further examine the relationship between the De Ritis ratio and OP, providing information that may aid the formulation of prevention strategies that are more targeted.

However, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the cross-sectional design prevented the inference of a causal relationship between the De Ritis ratio and OP. Prospective studies are needed to establish temporal relationships. Second, this study used listwise deletion, which relies on the missing completely at random assumption. Violation could introduce selection bias. Third, although NHANES DXA scans adhered to rigorous quality control protocols – including daily phantom scans and cross-calibration – with no significant inter-machine drift reported during the study cycles, the exclusion of individuals weighing over 450 lbs may still introduce selection bias by omitting cases of severe obesity. Consequently, our findings may not generalize to extremely obese populations. Furthermore, multiple statistical analyses – including logistic and linear regression, subgroup comparisons, and ROC curve analysis – were performed without correction for multiple testing, thereby increasing the risk of type I error. As such, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and require validation in independent cohorts. In addition, a directed acyclic graph was not employed to guide variable selection; instead, we relied on existing literature and clinical expertise, which may have resulted in inadequate control or overadjustment of covariates. Despite adjustment for numerous potential confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors – such as physical activity levels, vitamin D status, calcium intake, medication use (e.g., steroids, bisphosphonates), and menopausal status – could still influence the observed associations.

An additional limitation involves the reliance on a single measurement of the AST/ALT ratio, which may fail to capture long-term or biological variability and could contribute to non‑differential misclassification. Moreover, we were unable to exclude participants with acute liver injury, alcohol‑related liver disease, or drug‑induced liver injury – conditions known to transiently elevate the AST/ALT ratio. Future investigations should incorporate repeated measurements, exclude individuals with acute hepatic conditions, and collect detailed medication histories to further substantiate our findings. Regarding the definition of OP, this study utilized reference data derived from young white females in NHANES III. Although we adjusted for race/ethnicity and sex in multivariable models to mitigate potential bias, this approach may still introduce ethnic and sex-based biases in our multi‑ethnic cohort. Furthermore, combining individuals with normal BMD and those with osteopenia into a single non‑OP group reduces clinical granularity and carries a risk of misclassification bias. While a more refined 3‑category classification – normal, osteopenia, and OP – would be preferable, our dichotomous approach aligns with much of the prior literature. Future research should aim to address these methodological nuances. Finally, postmenopausal estrogen deficiency is a well‑established risk factor for OP. Estrogen helps maintain bone mass, and its sharp decline after menopause accelerates bone loss. Therefore, menopausal status should be considered an important confounder in studies of bone metabolism. The De Ritis ratio (AST/ALT) is not only a traditional marker of liver injury but has also been linked to inflammation and oxidative stress. In this study, we observed an interaction between the De Ritis ratio and OP. Given the hepatoprotective effect of estrogen, postmenopausal estrogen withdrawal may alter AST/ALT levels. Consequently, menopausal status could influence both the De Ritis ratio and bone health through a common biological pathway. Future studies should therefore include menopausal status as a covariate and formally test for interaction.

5. Conclusion

To summarize, this study demonstrates a significant association between an elevated De Ritis ratio and OP prevalence in a representative US middle-aged and older adult population. This association was robust in females, older adults, and individuals with lower body mass indices. These findings suggest that the De Ritis ratio could serve as a useful adjunct clinical tool to help identify individuals who may be at elevated risk for OP and who might benefit from further diagnostic evaluation. However, further prospective cohort studies and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm these findings.
