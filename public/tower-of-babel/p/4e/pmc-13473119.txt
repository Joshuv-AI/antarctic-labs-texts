Exploring multiple minimal clinically important differences for the esophageal cancer scale QLICP-ES (V2.0) based on anchor-based and distribution-based methods
Authors: Jianfeng Tan, Ting Wu, Fengbo Guo, Gaofeng Li, Jiudi Zhong, Yujing Fang, Chonghua Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is an important phrase with big appeal in the interpretation of quality of life (QOL), and a bridge between statistical inference and clinical implication. This study aimed to explore multiple MCIDs of the QLICP-ES(V2.0), an esophageal cancer scale in Quality of Life Instruments system for Cancer Patients. The MCID were established by anchor-based and distribution-based methods. The item Q29 in EORTC QLQ-C30 was selected as an anchor, and Standard A (improves one level after treatment) and standard B (improves at least one level after treatment) were used to define effects in the anchor-based method, whereas methods of effect size (ES), standard error of measurement (SEM), and reliable change index (RCI) were used in the distribution-based method. A total of 232 esophageal cancer patients completed the QLICP-ES (V2.0) assessment. Under criterion A, 102 patients were included and the MCID values for the physical, psychological, social, common symptoms and side effects domains, the general module, the specific module, and the overall score were 15.1, 4.4, 3.1, 6.7, 4.8, 8.5, and 6.0, respectively. Under criterion B, 165 patients were included and the MCID values for the aforementioned domains and overall score were 19.3, 4.2, 4.8, 7.7, 6.5, 9.5, and 7.5, respectively. The MCID values using three approaches of effect size, standard error of measurement and reliable change index were ranged from 1.1 to 13.3. Although the threshold values of anchor-based method and 1.96SEM were recommended to establish the MCID of QLICP-ES(V2.0) in patients with ES, several MCID values have been presented by different methods and standards so that users may select according to different situations.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (ESC) is the sixth most common cancer in the world and the fourth most common cause of cancer deaths in China. Nearly half a million new ESC cases are diagnosed each year1,2.In China, the incidence of ESC ranks sixth among malignant tumors, and the incidence is higher in males and rural areas than in females and urban areas3. Early ESC often presents with subtle symptoms, with dysphagia being the most common4. This can be accompanied by gastrointestinal and other system-related issues, affecting patients’ physical and mental health and reducing their quality of life (QOL). With increasing adoption of the bio-psycho-social medical model (modern medical model), improvement in QOL is emphasized upon while paying more attention to the prolonging of life.

Therefore, QOL have been applied as significant outcome indicators for patients with ESC5,6, given the time course of the disease, its treatment, and the burden of treatment. Consequently, several specific instruments for patients with ESC have been developed and are used in cancer clinical research, including European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Group questionnaire, the QLQ-OES24 and the QLQ-OES187,8, and the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Esophageal cancer questionnaire (FACT-E)9. Considering cultural dependence of QOL, our QOL team developed a QOL scale system called QLICP (Quality of Life Instruments for Cancer Patients) for Chinese patients by module approach with a general module (QLICP-GM) being used with all types of cancer and some specific modules for different cancers10. The updated second version of QLICP (V2.0) includes the general module QLICP-GM(V2.0) and 22 cancer-specific scales11 such as breast cancer12, cervical cancer13, etc., in which, most scales of the QLICP (V2.0) have been developed and put into use. The QLICP-ES (V2.0) is one instrument of this system for specifically QOL measurement of ESC[14].

For a reasonable explanation of the actual clinical significance of the questionnaire survey and the scale score, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) as an important tool to assess change or scale responsiveness of appropriate benchmarks was proposed, with defining MCID as the minimum variation in questionnaire dimension score acceptable to patients without considering side effects and costs15,16.

The MCID has gained importance in the interpretation of QOL and other patient-reported outcomes17,18. It also bridges statistical inferences and clinical implications. Development of reasonable and reliable MCID values has become a hot topic in this field. Two main methods have been employed: anchor-based method and distribution-based method.

At present, although QLICP-ES (V2.0) has been developed, MCID has not been developed. Therefore, in this study, the MCID of QLICP-ES (V2.0) was established by using the commonly used anchor-based method and distribution-based method to present multiple results from both types of methods, and the advantages and disadvantages of the two methods were compared to evaluate their feasibility and selectivity for clinical application. This study not only provides the first estimates of MCID for the QLICP-ES(V2.0), but also several MCID values have been presented by different methods and standards so that users may select according to different situations.

Methods

Data sources and study participants

This study was designed as a prospective longitudinal observational study to determine the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of the QLICP-ES (V2.0) by comparing quality of life before and after treatments.

A total of 232 inpatients with ESC, pathologically confirmed and treated at Yunnan Tumor Hospital and Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Prevention Center during January 2012 and December 2013 were consecutively recruited. All 232 participants completed the QLICP-ES (V2.0) and Chinese version of EORTC QLQ-C3019 at both time points and were included in the final analysis.

The questionnaires were administered during hospitalization, with assessments conducted at two predefined time points: (1) baseline, prior to the initiation of treatment; (2) post-treatment, at the end of the treatment course. The treatment duration ranged from 5 days to 1 month, depending on the therapeutic regimen. The main treatment modalities included surgery, chemotherapy, or combined chemo radio therapy.

Patients were included if they were diagnosed with ESC by X-ray examination and fiberoptic esophagoscopy or gastroscopy biopsy, were receiving treatment for ESC, had at least a primary school education, were able to complete the questionnaires independently, and provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Patients were excluded if they were illiterate or lacked basic reading and writing skills, were unable to clearly express their true feelings due to impaired consciousness during hospitalization, or were unable to participate in the assessment because of other serious comorbid conditions.

Outcome measures

Two QOL instruments have been used in this study, including the QLICP-ES (V2.0) and Chinese version of QLQ-C3019.

The QLICP-ES (V2.0) includes a generic module QLICP-GM (V2.0) and an ESC-specific module, with the QLICP-GM (V2.0) including physical (8 items), psychological (9 items), social functions (8 items), and common symptoms and side effects (7 items), summing up to 10 facets and 32 items in 4 domains. The ESC-specific module includes 4 facets and 16 items. The whole scale consists of 14 facets and 48 items in 5 domains (dimensions), which can be used for patients with various types of ESC for the measurement of QOL during the periods of onset, treatment, and rehabilitation.

Previous studies have demonstrated the QLICP-ES exhibits good reliability, validity, and responsiveness and can be used as a tool for evaluating the QOL of patients with ESC in China.

The Chinese version of QLQ-C30 is a 30-item cancer-specific scale addressing various aspects of QoL, which comprises of fifteen domains, including five functional subscales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social), three multi-item symptom subscales (fatigue, pain and nausea/vomiting), a global health/quality of life subscales, and six single items addressing various symptoms and perceived financial impact. All items use a 4-point scale, namely, not at all, a little, quite a bit, and very much, except the global health status/QOL (Q29 and Q30) of which a 7-point scale is used19. Item Q29 (“How would you evaluate your overall health in the past week?”) was used as the anchor for anchor-based MCID estimation.

Item Q29 is scored on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Very poor”) to 7 (“Excellent”), with higher scores indicating better self-perceived overall health status. A one-grade change represents a minimal but perceptible change in patients’ global health perception and has been widely used as an anchor for determining clinically meaningful change in QOL.

Scoring procedure

Raw scores (RS) for each domain were calculated according to the QLICP-ES (V2.0) scoring manual. Scores were linearly transformed to standardized scores (SS) ranging from 0 to 100 using the range method:

SS = (RS - Min) × 100/R

where Min is the minimum score of the domain/side/total scale, Max is the maximum score of the domain/side/total scale, and R is the range of scores.

Anchor-based method

One method of determining clinically meaningful changes in QOL is by comparing measures of QOL to other measures or phenomena that have clinical relevance. Lydick and Epstein18 have called this method “anchor-based” interpretation. Anchor-based approaches interpret changes in scale scores by examining their association with an external measure that has clear clinical meaning. Because global ratings may explain only part of the variance in QOL scores, methodological guidelines recommend that an anchor should demonstrate an adequate association with the target outcome. Accordingly, before estimating the MCID, the relationship between the selected anchor and the QLICP-ES (V2.0) scores was examined. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between Item 29 of the EORTC QLQ-C30 and the total score as well as each domain score of the QLICP-ES (V2.0). An anchor was considered appropriate if the correlation coefficient between the anchor and the QOL scores met or exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.5020.

Based on changes in Item Q29 responses between baseline and post-treatment assessments, patients were classified into two groups: standard A, including patients with exactly one-grade improvement in Q29 (n = 102), and standard B, including patients with at least one-grade improvement in Q29 (n = 165), the mean differences in QLICP-ES (V2.0) scores before and after treatment were calculated and used to estimate the MCID.

For each standard, the differences in QLICP-ES (V2.0) scores across all domains and the total scale before and after treatment were calculated, and the mean differences were defined as the MCID.

Distribution-based method

The distribution method is used to determine the MCID from a statistical point of view by using the distribution(variation) of the sample data points of the evaluation tool. The commonly used indexes to calculate variation include effect size (ES), standard error of measurement (SEM), and reliable change index (RCI).

An ES representing a standardized measure of change over time is calculated by dividing the difference between pre-test and post-test scores by the SD at pre-test. The ES thus represents an individual change in terms of the number of pre-test SD(SDbaseline)21. The formula and the corresponding MCID are as follows:

ES is often used to compare two or more groups to measure the size of the difference between groups. In health-related QOL assessments, ES is currently the most recognized parameter in determining the importance of group or individual changes. In the literature, different benchmarks have been proposed for distribution-based MCID estimation. Samsa et al22. advocate that an effect size of 0.2 serves as an appropriate definition of an MCID. In contrast, Norman et al.23 argue that an effect size (ES) of 0.5 provides a more robust benchmark, as it represents a moderate and perceptible change that aligns well with patient-reported important differences. In this study, we calculated the MCID estimates corresponding to ES values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 per Cohen’s convention in order to compare them with anchor-based results and to determine which threshold provides the most appropriate clinical reference for the QLICP-ES (V2.0).

SEM is a measure of the precision of a test instrument, defined as the baseline SD multiplied by the square root of one minus sample test–retest reliability coefficient[24]. This was also calculated for comparison purpose25. The reliability is usually estimated using a test–retest reliability estimate, if test-retest reliability is not available, internal consistency reliability (for example Cronbach’s alpha) can be used as an alternative26. For this study, the test-retest reliability estimate was used to calculate the SEM, where the same participants completed the QLICP-ES (V2.0) questionnaires at two separate time points in order to calculate test-retest reliability coefficient r. The formula is as follows:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SEM = \sqrt {1 - r} \times \sqrt {\sum {(x_{0} - \bar{x}_{0} )^{2} } /n - 1}$$\end{document}

SEM is assumed to be fairly sample independent27, which is its best advantage: a growing SD is balanced by a higher reliability. Some authors like Wyrwich et al. consider 1 SEM as an approximation of the MCID28,29. Among the various SEM thresholds suggested in the literature for defining clinically meaningful differences (e.g., 1 SEM30, 1.96 SEM31, and 2.77 SEM28, this study calculated and reports the MCID values corresponding to 1 SEM and 1.96 SEM.

RCI is the change value of the questionnaire score divided by the square root of SEM. Consequently, RCI-based values are smaller than SEM-based values for a given change. The RCI approach is therefore more conservative for a given cutoff value than the SEM approach, classifying fewer individuals as improved or deteriorated. The formula is as follows:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RCI = \frac{{\bar{x}_{1} - \bar{x}_{0} }}{{\sqrt {2(SEM)^{2} } }}$$\end{document}

Threshold values for 1RCI, 1.96RCI, and 2.77RCI can be used to define clinically meaningful differences32. However, studies have suggested that a cutoff value of 1.96 is recommended to determine whether observed changes in scores over time should be classified as constant, improved, or worsened32. If RCI is greater than 1.96, then the change value has a 95% chance of being a meaningful change33.

Discussion

This study first established Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) estimates for the QLICP-ES (V2.0) in patients with esophageal cancer. As an exploration, it presented multiple results of two classic methods, which brings many benefits: first, they can be compared with each other; second, providing multiple results increases selectivity.

Using an anchor-based method, the MCID for the total score was estimated to be 6.0 (for an exact one-grade improvement) and 7.5 (for at least one-grade improvement). Based on the distribution method, a conservative and statistically reliable MCID of 2.2 was recommended using the 1.96SEM criterion. These MCID values can be used to determine whether changes in the score of the scale brought by clinical interventions have “clinical significance”34. For clinicians and researchers, understanding the implications of these thresholds is crucial. For instance, lower MCID values may lead to overestimation of the positive effects of treatment, while higher MCID values may incorrectly classify patients as ineffective when the treatment is actually beneficial35. The range of estimates provided here allows for context-specific application.

On methods of calculating MCID

At present, there is no gold standard for the formulation of MCID, and anchor-based and distribution-based methods are often used for calculating MCID. In this paper, two methods were used to calculate the MCID of the Quality of Life Measurement Scale QLICP-ES (V2.0) in patients with esophageal cancer, and the advantages and disadvantages of each method were discussed. It can not only play a role in complementing each other’s strengths, but also provide a variety of results for selection.

Among the many methods for calculating MCID, the anchor-based method is widely recognized for its ability to provide parameters that are directly related to clinical outcomes36. At its core, the anchor-based method consists in using one or more “anchors” that are closely related to the patient’s health status to assess whether the treatment effect is clinically significant. These anchors can be subjective feelings in patient-reported quality of life scales, or objective physiological measures such as 6-minute walking distance, etc. In this paper, the Q29 item in the EORTC QLQ-C30 scale was selected as an anchor, the correlation coefficient r between the Q29 and the score of the total scale was 0.72, but the correlation coefficient between the anchor Q29 and some domains, such as psychological domain and social domain, was 0.17 and 0.32, which were not too high. Therefore, the distribution-based method was further adopted, and the MCID values under ES, SEM, and RCI were calculated.

Studies have shown that when calculating MCID values by the ES method, 0.5 medium effect was the most appropriate, while 0.2 and 0.8 were too large or too small in calculation37. The MCID values calculated by the distribution-based method were similar to those calculated by the anchor-based method. Several recent studies compare anchor-based and distribution-based methods28,38–41. Norman et al.38 conducted a simulation comparing the minimally important difference based on patient global ratings of change with effect size values ranging from 0 to 0.75. They conclude that an effect size of 0.50 corresponds closely to values obtained from global ratings and that the two methods provide equivalent information in this situation. SEM is not completely dependent on the sample information and is slightly affected by the sample size. Therefore, scholars recommend 1SEM, 1.96 SEM, or 2.77SEM as MCID values28,39. Wyrwich et al.28considered that 1SEM could be regarded as the minimum change value of clinical significance, whereas others suggest using a conservative 1.96SEM as the minimum change value of clinical significance40,41.

The advantage of the anchor-based methods is that the change in the outcome measure score is associated with a meaningful external anchor that reflects the patient’s point of view and provides a professional explanation for the minimum change in clinical significance determined39. However, the disadvantage is that the measurement error is not considered, different criteria will produce different changes in the minimum clinical significance, and it is often difficult to find a suitable anchor. Moreover, in our study, the sample size was not too large and only a subjective anchor was considered. Therefore, subsequent studies with larger samples and objective anchors should be conducted in future.

The distribution-based method considers measurement error to determine the MCID values, which is relatively easy to implement, but the results obtained from different samples may be different. Therefore, the observed minimum measurable change value cannot be adequately explained in clinical terms, and recognized judgment criteria for the obtained minimum measurable change values are lacking. In developing MCID, the anchor-based method is generally preferred. When there are no good criteria or a small sample size, the distribution-based method can be considered.

The findings of this study can be directly contextualized within the seminal work by our team Ren et al.42, which pioneered the establishment of MCID for the QLICP-ES (V2.0) using anchor-based, ROC curve, and multiple linear regression methods. That study, utilizing the same anchor (Q29) and patient cohort, reported a set of recommended MCID values and concluded that the anchor-based method yielded the largest estimates. Our study here serves as a methodological extension and validation of that foundational work. Crucially, we introduced and triangulated classical distribution-based methods (ES, SEM, RCI). This addition provides a critical, statistically-grounded perspective to complement the clinically-anchored estimates. Reassuringly, our anchor-based MCID estimates for the total score under both Criterion A (6.0) and B (7.5) are highly consistent with those reported by Ren et al. (6.0 and 7.5, respectively)42, confirming the stability of patient-perceived meaningful change captured by this scale across independent analyses. Similarly, Crump et al43., using two distribution-based approaches (standard deviation and standard error of measurement), calculated the MCID for the five FACT-E domains and total score at multiple time points, with results being ranged from 1 to 3 points for most domains, 2 to 5 points for the esophagus cancer subscale, and 4 to 9 points for the FACT-E total score.

A key point of divergence lies in the final recommendation. While Ren et al.42 recommended values derived from the ROC curve method (prioritizing a balance of sensitivity and specificity), our multi-method triangulation leads us to advocate for the 1.96SEM-based value (2.2 for the total score) as a conservative benchmark for individual patient decision-making. This recommendation stems from our focus on maximizing specificity—minimizing the risk of false-positive conclusions—which is paramount when applying MCID to judge an individual’s treatment response. Thus, our study builds upon the earlier work by providing a broader methodological repertoire and a context-specific framework for MCID selection, enhancing the tool’s practical utility in diverse clinical and research scenarios.

Considering the estimates from both methodological families, this study compares and synthesizes a spectrum of MCID values to inform context-specific recommendations. A key observation is the variation in point estimates across methods (e.g., 15.1 for the physical domain using anchor-based Criterion A vs. 4.2 using 1.96SEM). This divergence is methodological in origin and informative: anchor-based values reflect a patient-perceived threshold for meaningful improvement, whereas 1.96SEM provides a statistically reliable threshold for change that is highly likely to exceed measurement error (95% confidence).

Among the distribution-based estimates, the value derived from 1.96SEM is recommended as the primary, conservative reference for individual patient decision-making. This threshold prioritizes specificity—minimizing the risk of a false positive conclusion (i.e., mistaking random variation for true improvement)—which is paramount in clinical contexts such as assessing whether a treatment is effective for a single patient. For group-level comparisons in research, where sensitivity to detect potential effects is valued, the anchor-based estimates may serve as a useful benchmark.

The MCID is not a fixed value, and different determination methods can produce different MCID values. Although MCID as a minimum threshold plays an important role in determining the scale score changes, its stability and variability may be affected by many factors, such as defects in the measurement method, demographic characteristics, patient comprehension, and study cycles42. The interpretation and application of MCID values should be carefully considered in combination with the actual clinical situation, and some methods such as Roc curve method, the multiple regression model method can be used to establish the MCID of QLICP-ES (V2.0) for Esophageal Cancer42,].

Clinical implications

This study provides the first estimates of MCID for the QLICP-ES(V2.0), offering clinicians and researchers guidance for interpreting meaningful changes in scores. When the change in quality of life score before and after treatment is greater than that of MCID, it is considered clinically significant.

At present, a large number of scales are used in clinical practice, and the statistical significance of the difference in scale measurement does not mean that it also has practical significance in clinical practice. More and more scholars recognize that it is unscientific to judge efficacy based only on the hypothesis test P value of the difference in scale scores before and after treatment, and that as long as the sample size is large enough, the P value will generally show statistical significance, but it does not indicate clinical significance. Therefore, a key question in the application of the scale is how much the score needs to change to be clinically meaningful, which is the MCID.

Different definitions and methods will obtain different MCIDs, and this paper gives the MCID results under two definitions of two types of methods, and provides a variety of options for users to choose. A notable finding in our anchor-based analysis is the convergence of MCID estimates derived from Criterion A (“exactly one-grade improvement”) and Criterion B (“at least one-grade improvement”), particularly for the total score (6.0 vs. 7.5) and several key domains. This pattern carries significant clinical interpretation.

Clinically, this convergence strongly suggests that the patient-perceived threshold for a minimally important improvement is predominantly captured by the first perceptible step of change (“exactly one-grade”). The fact that requiring a larger improvement (“at least one-grade”) yields only a marginally higher MCID value implies that the incremental clinical importance attributed to each additional grade of improvement diminishes substantially beyond the first step. In practical terms, this supports the use of a single-grade improvement on a global rating scale as a sufficient and highly efficient anchor for defining the MCID, as it robustly identifies the point where change becomes meaningful to patients.

Methodologically, the stability of the MCID estimate across these two related but distinct criteria reinforces the robustness of our anchor-based approach. It indicates that the identified threshold is not an artifact of a narrowly defined response but represents a consistent signal of clinically meaningful change within the patient cohort.

Study limitations and future research recommendations

It is important to note that this article only used data from inpatients with esophageal cancer, and did not include outpatients who received outpatient care. Outpatients may have different MCIDs depending on the intensity of treatment and the stage of the disease. Secondly, this paper only adopts the traditional methods of anchor-based and distribution-based to formulate MCID. Some new or improved MCID formulation methods42,44 have been proposed, such as ROC curve method, the multiple regression model, response cumulative distribution function, etc., and these methods will be introduced in subsequent research to try to formulate MCID. Finally, owing to the self-report nature of the QLICP-ES (V2.0) scale, this study was unable to include illiterate patients. This limitation may to some extent restrict the universality of the findings to this specific population.

Given these limitations, future research may be deepened in several directions. Future research should strive to include broader and more representative populations. Secondly, to ensure that the quality of life for all patients—particularly those with special needs such as illiteracy or cognitive impairment—can be effectively assessed, a key focus for future research will be the development or validation of appropriate scale versions. These may include interview versions that can be completed with interviewer assistance, or simplified self-report/other-report scales. Crucially, establishing a symmetrical threshold for the “Minimal Clinically Important Deterioration” in quality of life is paramount. Future research may symmetrically analyse MCID values anchored to Q29 item deterioration and compare whether thresholds for ‘improvement’ and ‘deterioration’ exhibit symmetry, thereby providing more refined guidance for clinical practice. Concurrently, investigations may explore multi-anchor calibration incorporating objective clinical indicators (such as tumor response rates) and attempt to apply the aforementioned novel statistical methods for establishing and validating MCID.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive, multi-method set of Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) estimates for the QLICP-ES (V2.0) in esophageal cancer. By triangulating anchor-based and distribution-based methods, we demonstrate that patient-perceived meaningful improvement is robustly captured by a single-grade change, while statistical methods offer a more conservative threshold. For practical application, we recommend the 1.96SEM-based value as a stringent benchmark for individual patient assessment, with the full spectrum of estimates available to inform different clinical and research contexts. Future work should focus on validating these thresholds in broader populations and exploring the MCID for quality of life deterioration.
