Bovine tuberculosis history as a risk factor for recurrence in English cattle herds experiencing a breakdown
Authors: Daisy Duncan, Adam Brouwer, Ricardo de la Rua‐Domenech, Rachelle Avigad, Jessica Parry, Lucy Brunton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBovine tuberculosis (TB) threatens cattle farming in England. Supplementing tuberculin skin testing with the interferon‐gamma blood test during TB incident (breakdown) management can enhance diagnostic sensitivity. We aimed to identify herds at greater risk of recurrence depending on their recent TB history to inform targeted interferon‐gamma test deployment.MethodsUsing observational data, we analysed TB breakdowns in herds with a lesion‐positive or bacteriologically positive (officially tuberculosis free‐withdrawn [OTF‐W]) breakdown in the High‐Risk Area or six‐monthly surveillance section of the Edge Area of England between 2018 and 2020. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the odds of recurrence 18 months post‐breakdown, based on TB status in the 18 months prior (OTF‐W, OTF‐suspended [OTF‐S], none). Covariates previously identified as recurrence risk factors were included.ResultsHerds with an OTF‐W breakdown history had significantly higher odds of recurrence than those with no history: odds ratio [OR] 1.3 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.1‒1.6). OTF‐S history did not confer increased risk: OR 0.9 (95% CI: 0.8‒1.1). A large herd size was another significant risk factor for recurrence.LimitationsLimited covariates were included in the analysis, which means that other factors potentially driving the recurrence of disease within herds may have been overlooked.ConclusionThis analysis supports prioritising interferon‐gamma testing of OTF‐W breakdown herds with an OTF‐W history but not an OTF‐S history. Large herds, irrespective of breakdown history, may also benefit. Effective interventions against TB recurrence require further investigation.

INTRODUCTION

Bovine tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium bovis (M. bovis), results in substantial losses for the cattle industry in England. Despite a national compulsory TB eradication strategy based on regular testing of herds and rapid removal of test‐positive cattle (reactors) and recent reductions in TB herd incidence and prevalence, the disease remains endemic in many areas and a serious threat to the cattle industry. 1

For a herd that is officially tuberculosis free (OTF), having a history of TB is known to be a major risk factor for the detection of reactors in a future round of testing. 2 In 2023, 54% of English herds with a new TB breakdown in the year had experienced another breakdown within the previous 3 years. 1 This highlights the important role that repeat infections within herds can have on annual TB incidence in England. Measures to reduce the risk of recurrent herd infections have the potential to make a significant impact on the progress towards disease eradication in England.

There are several tests approved for the detection of TB within the cattle population; however, the primary screening test used in the statutory routine surveillance programme in England is the single intradermal comparative cervical tuberculin test (SICCT or ‘skin’ test). The SICCT test compares reactions to bovine and avian tuberculin, allowing for differentiation between cattle infected with M. bovis and other mycobacteria (e.g., Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis, the causative agent of Johne's disease).

Where TB is detected by a positive SICCT test result, M. bovis infection may be confirmed by visible lesions at slaughter, detection of the organism on culture media or PCR testing. This results in a herd status of OTF‐withdrawn (OTF‐W). In the absence of visible lesions and positive culture (or PCR test) results, the OTF status of the skin test reactor herd is suspended (OTF‐S). OTF‐S status may also temporarily apply to herds with an overdue skin test; however, these OTF‐S cases are not included in the present analysis.

The SICCT test is estimated to have a specificity of 99.98%, 3 allowing for high confidence in the results when identifying and removing truly infected animals. However, the test has a less than perfect sensitivity, with a 2018 meta‐analysis based on cattle in the UK and Ireland providing a median estimate of 50%, with a credible interval of 26%‒78%. 4 Relying on the SICCT test alone to detect infected animals in a TB breakdown herd increases the risk of leaving undetected infection in the herd.

Since July 2021, the policy for the application of supplementary interferon‐gamma blood testing (gamma test) has been harmonised in the High‐Risk Area (HRA) and the six‐monthly surveillance section of the Edge Area (6m Edge) of England. Herds experiencing recurrent OTF‐W breakdowns, within 18 months of a past OTF‐W breakdown, received gamma testing in parallel with the SICCT test during breakdown management. When the gamma and SICCT tests are interpreted in parallel, animals with a positive result for either test are considered positive for M. bovis and are removed from the herd.

The gamma test can detect TB at an earlier stage of infection and has a greater sensitivity than the SICCT test 5 . The 2018 meta‐analysis estimated the median sensitivity of the gamma test to be 67%, with a credible interval of 49%‒82%. 4 This allows for the identification and removal of more truly infected animals when used alongside the SICCT test. 6 However, in the 2018 meta‐analysis, the gamma test had a lower specificity (median 98%, credible interval 96%‒99%) than the SICCT test, thus increasing the risk of false‐positive results. 4 This limitation leads to the need for careful risk‐based targeting of herds most likely to benefit from the enhanced sensitivity that parallel gamma testing affords.

The aim of this study was to establish whether OTF‐W breakdown herds with an OTF‐S TB history would benefit from supplementary gamma testing, by comparing the odds of future TB breakdowns for herds with a current OTF‐W breakdown, according to their prior TB history.

METHODS

Sample population and study design

The sampling frame consisted of OTF‐W TB breakdowns (‘study’ breakdowns) that occurred in the HRA or 6m Edge with a breakdown start date between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2020.

The hypothesis for this analysis was that the odds of an OTF‐W herd experiencing a future TB breakdown would be associated with the TB history of that herd, with the null hypothesis predicting no association.

The outcome was having a future TB breakdown (the ‘recurrent’ breakdown) within 18 months of the conclusion of the study breakdown.

An 18‐month definition of recurrence was selected for the outcome for two reasons. First, study breakdown herds were required to have the full follow‐up time available after the study breakdown ended. Any extension to this period would further reduce the number of herds eligible for the study. Second, the 18‐month definition of recurrence aligned with the existing gamma TB policy in the HRA and 6m Edge, which targeted OTF‐W herds with another OTF‐W breakdown within the past 18 months.

The primary exposure was herd TB history. Study herds were assigned to one of three TB history categories. A directed acyclic graph was used to visualise the assumed structure of the data generating model using DAGitty version 3.1 (Figure 2). The aim of the study was to identify the odds of a recurrent breakdown given an OTF‐W herd's TB history. This study did not seek to identify the total or direct causal effects of TB history on recurrence.

Eligibility

To ensure an independent study design, herds with more than one OTF‐W breakdown between 2018 and 2020 were restricted to include only the first breakdown for each herd. Herds were also omitted where less than 18‐month follow‐up time was available. Finally, herds missing data for any variable were also omitted from the analysis.

This left a total of 5020 OTF‐W TB study breakdowns starting in unique herds in the HRA or 6m Edge between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2020, which were followed up for a recurrent breakdown in the 18 months after the conclusion of the study breakdown.

Statistical analysis

All data handling and statistical analysis were carried out using STATA 15. Univariate logistic regression was conducted initially. Assessment of confounding and effect modification between TB history and the additional variables was carried out using the test of homogeneity of odds ratios (ORs) in the Mantel‒Haenszel estimate of the trend of odds. These were followed by multivariable logistic regression. A backward stepwise approach was taken to select the most parsimonious model, sequentially removing those with the highest p‐values from the univariable logistic regression. Covariate inclusion in the final model was determined using the likelihood ratio test. Akaike's information criterion was used to assess the use of continuous or categorical versions of the duration and TB‐positive variables in the final models. The Hosmer‒Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test was used to validate the models. Correlation coefficients were assessed to check for correlation between covariates in the final models.

Alternative models

Model 1 examined the likelihood of experiencing any recurrent TB breakdown (OTF‐W or OTF‐S) in the 18 months after the conclusion of the OTF‐W study breakdown.

Model 2 examined the same population, but the outcome was restricted to a recurrent OTF‐W breakdown, compared to a recurrent OTF‐S breakdown or no recurrent breakdown within 18 months.

Model 3 restricted the study population to eligible breakdowns that started in 2020 (n = 1707), with an outcome of any recurrent TB breakdown (OTF‐W or OTF‐S) in the 18 months after the conclusion of the OTF‐W study breakdown.

RESULTS

Multivariable model evaluation

The covariates included in final multivariable models 1 and 2 can be seen in Tables 2 and 3, and for model 3 in Table S2. The Hosmer‒Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test did not identify a significant lack of fit for any of the three models (model 1: p = 0.4083, model 2: p = 0.0921, model 3: p = 0.2810). No significant multicollinearity issues were detected between covariates included in the final models. The strongest associations were found between duration and log TB positives (model 1 = ‒0.51), gamma and risk area (model 1 = ‒0.37) and gamma and log TB positives (model 1 = ‒0.32).

TB history

Consistent associations between OTF‐W and OTF‐S TB history, and future recurrence were seen for all three models (Tables 1 and S1). Considering model 1, almost half (44%) of the study herds with an OTF‐W TB history went on to experience a third TB breakdown of any kind (OTF‐W or OTF‐S) in the 18 months after the conclusion of the study breakdown. In comparison, 30% of study herds with no TB history in 18 months prior to the study breakdown start date, and 26% of study herds with an OTF‐S TB history had any recurrent breakdown (Table 1).

Herd size

As study herd size increased, so did the percentage of herds that went on to have a recurrent breakdown in the 18 months after the conclusion of the OTF‐W study breakdown, from just 20% among herds with up to 100 animals, to 49% for herds with over 500 animals (Table 1). The adjusted OR for a recurrent breakdown in the largest herd size category was 2.23 (95% CI: 1.75‒2.83, p < 0.001), compared to the smallest (Table 2 and Figure 3). Increased herd size was also significantly associated with the odds of recurrent OTF‐W TB incidents (Table 3 and Figure 4) and for any future TB among herds that had an OTF‐W breakdown in 2020 (Table S2).

Herd type

Dairy herds were more likely to have a recurrent breakdown (41%) than beef herds (26%) (Table 1), with an OR of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.09‒1.46, p = 0.002) after controlling for other variables in model 1 (Table 2). Herd type did not improve the fit of models 2 or 3, as assessed by the likelihood ratio test statistic (p > 0.05).

Year of study breakdown

The odds of recurrent breakdowns and recurrent OTF‐W breakdowns decreased in each successive year between 2018 and 2020 (Tables 2 and 3). However, when study breakdown year was assessed for inclusion in final models 1 and 2, it did not improve the overall fit and was omitted.

Gamma testing

For the whole study period, from 2018 to 2020, 27% of study herds underwent supplementary interferon‐gamma blood testing during the management of the OTF‐W study breakdown (gamma herds). Of these, 31% went on to have a recurrent breakdown in the following 18 months, compared to 33% of herds without gamma testing (Table 1).

After controlling for the other factors in model 1, there was evidence for a 32% reduction in the odds of having a recurrent TB breakdown in those herds that underwent gamma testing during the study breakdown (OR 0.68, 95% CI: 0.57‒0.81, p < 0.001) (Table 2 and Figure 3). It must be noted however that the inclusion of study breakdown duration and number of TB‐positive animals within the model likely introduces overcontrol bias to this estimate and these results should be interpreted with caution (Figure 2).

Number of TB‐positive animals detected during the study breakdown

Herds with more animals removed for TB control reasons (TB positives) during the study breakdown were more likely to go on to have a recurrent TB breakdown within 18 months of restrictions being lifted.

Recurrent herds had a median of six TB positives detected during the study breakdown (range 1‒228, interquartile range [IQR] 3‒15), compared to four for herds that did not go on to have a recurrent breakdown within 18 months (range 1‒291, IQR 2‒9) (Table 1). Only 22% of herds with one TB positive detected during the study breakdown went on to have a recurrent breakdown in the next 18 months. This percentage increased with the number of TB positives, up to 46% for herds where 15 or more TB positives were detected (Table S4).

The inclusion of TB positives as a log‐transformed continuous variable significantly improved the fit of all three models.

Study breakdown duration

As study breakdown duration increased, so did the odds that the herd went on to have a recurrent breakdown within 18 months of restrictions being lifted. Study breakdowns of over 550 days’ duration were most likely to be followed by another breakdown within 18 months (53%) (Table S4). Similarly, those herds had the greatest percentage of recurrent OTF‐W breakdowns (34%) compared to herds that resolved more quickly (Table S5).

Study breakdown duration was treated as a continuous variable in each final model, as it provided a better fit than the categorised version of the variable.

TB risk area

The percentages of OTF‐W study herds with a recurrent breakdown in the HRA and 6m Edge were very similar (32% and 33%, respectively) (Table 1). For all three models, herds located in the 6m Edge had a higher adjusted odds for a recurrent breakdown compared to herds located in the HRA (Tables 2, 3 and S2). These adjusted estimates, however, are likely to be influenced by overcontrol bias due to the structure of the data generating model, specifically the inclusion of the downstream variables, gamma testing, TB positives and duration (Figure 2).

County prevalence

Differences in mean county prevalence between study herds with and without a future breakdown were modest (10.3% and 10.0%, respectively) (Table 1). However, for every percentage increase in county prevalence, there was a 5% increase in the odds of a recurrent TB breakdown within 18 months (OR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02‒1.09, p = 0.002) (Table 2), with similar findings for models 2 and 3 (Tables 3 and S2).

Cattle movements

For recurrent herds, a median of six cattle were moved into the herd in the 12 months prior to the study breakdown (range 0‒2254, IQR 0‒43), compared to a median of eight movements for herds that were not recurrent (range 0‒2956, IQR 0‒47) (Table 1). For the 2018‒2020 study period, log cattle movements did not significantly improve the fit of final multivariable models 1 or 2 and were omitted (Tables 2 and 3, respectively).

Log‐transformed cattle movements were found to improve the fit of model 3, with the odds of future recurrence decreasing as log cattle movements increased (Table S2).

Breakdowns omitted from the analysis

In total, 845 OTF‐W breakdowns were omitted from the 2018‐.2020 study population used to construct models 1 and 2. This was due to insufficient follow‐up time (n = 42), not being the first qualifying breakdown detected within a herd over the period of interest (n = 784), or due to missing herd size information (n = 19). For model 3, a total of 43 herds were omitted from the dataset: 31 due to insufficient follow‐up time, four due to the herd already being included with an earlier breakdown, and eight due to missing herd size.

The distributions of the outcome and explanatory variables within breakdowns omitted from the 2018 to 2020 study population are presented in Table S3. A higher percentage of breakdowns were omitted from later years in the study, primarily due to the retention of only the first breakdown, where a herd had multiple OTF‐W breakdowns between 2018 and 2020, but also due to the requirement for 18 months of follow‐up time after the conclusion of the OTF‐W study breakdown.

The breakdowns omitted from the study were more likely to have gamma testing (41% compared to 27%) and to have a lower county TB prevalence (9.3% compared to 10.1%).

DISCUSSION

In this analysis, the future risk for a recurrent TB breakdown was compared in OTF‐W breakdown herds that had experienced either an OTF‐W breakdown, an OTF‐S breakdown, or had no history of breakdowns in the previous 18 months.

All three models used to explore this relationship showed that OTF‐W breakdown herds in the HRA and 6m Edge with an OTF‐S TB history had no greater risk of recurrent TB within 18 months, compared to similar breakdown herds with no prior history of TB.

In contrast, herds with an OTF‐W history did have significantly increased odds of a recurrent TB breakdown compared to herds with no history of TB.

The current analysis was not designed to provide evidence of the benefits of gamma testing. The aim was to understand the risk of recurrent breakdowns within the 18‐month follow‐up period for herds with each TB history. However, it does demonstrate a rationale for targeting the use of the gamma test in OTF‐W breakdowns with an OTF‐W TB history, as these herds were shown to have a greater risk of going on to have a future breakdown.

Although there is extensive literature to support TB history as a risk factor for future recurrence, 2 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 several studies have not found the detection of lesions in TB test reactors to be associated with the risk of a recurrent breakdown. 2 , 9 , 11 , 12 The present study design was restricted to herds with an OTF‐W breakdown and explored TB history as a risk factor within that population. It may be that there is a cumulative risk from having animals with visible lesions and/or laboratory‐confirmed M. bovis infection in successive breakdowns over a short time, below 18 months.

Of the risk factors investigated, herd size was most strongly associated with a subsequent TB breakdown. This risk was lower after controlling for other variables in the models. However, increasing herd size was still significantly associated with future TB in all final models. Multiple factors are likely to contribute to the association between herd size and TB recurrence. There is evidence that the internal force of infection pressure is greater within larger herds due to increased contacts between animals. 13 The imperfect sensitivity of skin testing may also have a greater impact on larger herds. 14 However, there are many other possible factors influencing this relationship, such as cattle management practices, the size of the farm premises and contact with neighbouring herds. 15

These findings are consistent with other research 9 , 10 , 12 ; however, at least one study has found that herd size was not significant after controlling for herd type. 2 The association between herd size and type was recognised in this study. Even so, it was herd type that was dropped from models 2 and 3 when considered alongside herd size, based on the likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without herd type.

In 2016, Downs et al. provided evidence that dairy cattle were less likely to have visible lesions of TB detected after slaughter. 16 This aligns with the results of the present analysis, where being a dairy herd was no longer a significant risk factor when the future TB outcome was OTF‐W only (model 2). Regardless of the herd type, further work is required to understand effective interventions for reducing the recurrence of TB within large herds.

For an OTF‐W breakdown herd to regain OTF status, the policy in England requires the completion of two consecutive whole herd SICCT tests at least 60 days apart with negative results read at a more sensitive ‘severe’ interpretation. By supplementing this regime with a round of whole herd parallel gamma testing, applicable to all animals over 6 months of age, the overall sensitivity of the herds’ testing regime is enhanced. 6 The additional gamma tests potentially detect and lead to the removal of infected animals that may have been missed by the SICCT test. 5

Infected but undetected animals remaining in a herd pose several risks. First, they risk transmitting infection to other cattle, increasing total TB positives. They also risk being detected during a later round of testing during the same breakdown, thereby prolonging breakdown duration. Additionally, if the breakdown is officially resolved they risk triggering a recurrent breakdown. The longer infected animals persist within a herd, the greater the risk of environmental contamination.

The number of herds that received gamma testing increased over the study period. Prior to 2021, the policy in the HRA was to apply this test to all OTF‐W herds in areas that had completed at least 2 years of licenced badger culling. 17 The number of eligible cattle herds in the HRA grew rapidly between 2018 and 2020, reflecting the expansion of licensed badger culling areas, resulting in 38% of new OTF‐W breakdowns in 2020 receiving gamma tests, compared to 6% in 2018. The issue of licences was not random but determined by industry‐led applications that met licencing criteria. 17

The non‐random application of gamma testing and potential associations between gamma test use and the number of TB positives and breakdown duration may explain why the benefits of gamma testing were only apparent after controlling for confounding variables in this analysis. As a result, the ORs for herds that received gamma compared to no gamma testing should not be considered a reliable estimate of the effect of gamma testing on recurrence.

With these cautions in mind, it is interesting to note there was a significant 32% reduction in the odds of another TB breakdown in the 18 months following restrictions being lifted for herds with gamma testing (model 1). Furthermore, gamma testing was significantly associated with reduced odds of a future OTF‐W breakdown (48% reduction, model 2).

The number of SICCT test reactors detected during a breakdown has previously been shown to be a significant risk factor for the recurrence of TB within a herd. 2 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 A broader definition of the number of TB positives was developed for this analysis, due to the increase in gamma testing as a breakdown management strategy, and the greater understanding of the risk posed by two‐times inconclusive reactors, 18 , 19 , 20 which are now removed from herds under restrictions. The current analysis supports previous findings linking a higher number of reactors with increased future recurrence.

Herd breakdowns with a longer duration were more likely to be omitted from the study population than those with a short duration due to the follow‐up time required to determine the future TB outcome. As a result, a greater percentage of breakdowns with a long duration started earlier in the study window. To look at duration as a risk factor for future TB, a different study design would be preferable, taking breakdowns with restrictions that lifted in the period rather than those with a start date in the time window as the study population. Nevertheless, duration was included in this analysis to control for the confounding effects introduced by this variable on other variables within the models, as seen in previous research. 2

Herds that repeatedly fail subsequent rounds of TB testing during breakdown management may be more likely to have strong internal or external infection pressures. 21 These sources may in turn increase the chance of reinfection within the 18‐month time window following the end of the breakdown. 13 Despite improvements in the enhanced combined testing sensitivity where gamma testing is applied in parallel to the SICCT test, sensitivity is still not perfect and infected animals may remain in the herd. Gamma testing is also unable to prevent the recurrence of TB by reinfection where M. bovis persists in the farm environment, in local wildlife reservoirs, or is reintroduced into the herd via inward movements of undetected infected cattle.

Cattle movements were associated with a reduced risk of recurrence within the 2020 study population (model 3). The apparently protective effect of cattle movements against recurrence may be related to reduced disease maintenance within the herd for movement acquired infections, as postulated by Clegg et al. in 2018. 14

The complex transmission dynamics circulating M. bovis within and to a herd are not well understood and further work is required to identify and control for them in the future.

Limitations of the study

The present study was designed to be a practical and simple analysis to explore the risk of future recurrence in OTF‐W breakdowns with a recent history of a previous breakdown, and as such these results should not be extrapolated outside of this context.

The study of disease recurrence necessitates that the population selected for the analysis are themselves cases of the disease of interest (OTF‐W breakdowns). Limiting the study population to cases can introduce collider‐conditioning bias into an analysis, as has been demonstrated extensively. 22 , 23 Within the present analysis, attempts have been made to minimise this bias by controlling for known shared determinants of TB that were recorded in national animal health databases (e.g., herd size and type).

It is recognised, however, that the epidemiology of bovine TB is complex, and additional risk factors, both recognised but unavailable and wholly unknown, were not included in these models. Known examples include on‐farm biosecurity measures and stocking densities. The extent to which these factors might drive the recurrence of disease within herds is uncertain.

The exclusion of breakdowns for the reasons previously outlined ensured an independent study design. However, this potentially introduced further selection bias within the remaining study population by omitting a higher proportion of breakdowns that occurred later in time. More of the omitted breakdowns received supplementary gamma testing and were in counties with a lower prevalence of bovine TB.

To explore potential bias in the study due to the removal of subsequent breakdowns from model 1, the analysis was repeated for herds with a breakdown in 2020 only. The results of model 3 were consistent with model 1 and can be found in Table S2. This provides confidence that the omitted breakdowns did not unduly influence the findings of this analysis.

An inherent limitation in the study of recurrence is that key drivers for recurrent incidents may have changed since the study period due to the required follow‐up time. In the case of bovine TB, this may be exacerbated by changes in surveillance and control policies over time.

Furthermore, this study was not designed to look at scenarios in which gamma application had the greatest benefit, only the risks for future recurrence on the assumption that gamma application in those herds would be a worthwhile intervention.

CONCLUSIONS

The results of this analysis provide evidence in support of the 2021 gamma testing policy in the HRA and 6m Edge, which targets OTF‐W TB breakdowns with another OTF‐W episode within the previous 18 months. However, the results do not support the prioritisation of gamma testing in OTF‐W TB breakdown herds with a history of OTF‐S breakdowns to reduce recurrence. Even so, there may be other sound TB control reasons why risk managers may wish to deploy the supplementary gamma test to enhance diagnostic sensitivity in most OTF‐W breakdown herds, irrespective of their future risk of recurrence.

Herd size was identified as a variable that may have merit in the future targeting of gamma tests, but further work is required to understand the drivers for recurrent TB within large herds and to identify effective interventions to tackle these.

Gamma herd testing during the study breakdown was found to reduce the risk of recurrence in all three adjusted models. Characterising the types of TB‐infected herds in which supplementary gamma testing could have the greatest disease control impact could lead to enhanced test deployment strategies in the future and is a priority for further investigation.
