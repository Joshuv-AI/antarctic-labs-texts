Abortion stigma in healthcare: physicians’ perspectives
Authors: Leyla Tekdemi̇r, Semiha Zeynep Özsaydi, Zeynep Aydin, Beyza Nur Gürbüz, Hasan Durmuş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines the stigmatizing attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors of physicians and senior medical students in Kayseri, a mid-sized city located in Central Anatolia, Türkiye, toward abortion, exploring the factors influencing these views. Given the limited research on abortion stigma among Turkish physicians, the study provides valuable insights into their role in shaping abortion access and perceptions. A cross-sectional study was conducted in December 2024 with 585 participants through face-to-face questionnaire administration during their workplace rest periods, including assistant and specialist physicians, general practitioners, and senior medical students. Data were collected using a questionnaire comprising sociodemographic questions and the Stigmatizing Attitudes, Beliefs, and Behaviors Scale Towards Abortion (STAS-SABAS). Statistical analysis included Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman’s correlation tests. Results showed a mean stigma score of 26.48 ± 9.06, indicating low stigmatization levels. Male participants and those who supported abortion only for medical reasons had significantly higher stigma scores. Additionally, 18% of participants were unaware of Turkey’s legal abortion framework. Physicians unwilling to perform abortions despite training exhibited higher stigma levels. While 85.3% supported conscientious objection, those who did had higher stigma scores. The study highlights the need for enhanced reproductive health education to reduce stigma and improve abortion-related care. Raising awareness of legal rights and promoting nonjudgmental care can help ensure equitable abortion access.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01318-x.

Introduction

According to Colgrove (2025), abortion should be defined as an act that results in the death of an embryo or fetus, rather than merely the termination of pregnancy. This distinction ensures that procedures like elective cesarean sections or preterm deliveries are not mistakenly categorized as abortions [1]. Globally, 29% of all pregnancies annually end in abortion, with 61% of unintended pregnancies resulting in termination [2]. Despite its prevalence, the legality and permissibility of abortion remain subjects of ongoing debate, influenced by medical, religious, moral, and sociocultural considerations [3].

Abortion is a safe medical procedure when conducted in a setting that adheres to established medical standards, performed by adequately trained and authorized healthcare professionals, and utilizing appropriate techniques. The incidence of severe, life-threatening complications, such as sepsis and uterine perforation, is less than 1% [4]. In countries where abortion is legal and safely accessible through regulated healthcare systems, maternal deaths related to abortion are significantly reduced. However, legal status alone is not sufficient; healthcare quality, equity, and access remain critical determinants of abortion safety [5]. However, on a global scale, nearly half of the estimated 55 million abortions performed annually take place under unsafe conditions [6].

Poor socioeconomic conditions, low levels of education, legal restrictions, inadequate abortion services within healthcare institutions, and limited access to existing services due to a lack of information are among the primary factors contributing to unsafe abortion [7–9]. Additionally, the moral stigma surrounding both individuals seeking abortion and healthcare providers offering the procedure further deters women from accessing safe abortion services [6]. Complications associated with unsafe and illegal abortions include hemorrhage, endometritis, sepsis, uterine perforation, drug toxicity, amniotic fluid embolism, intrauterine adhesions, infertility, retention of pregnancy tissue, and maternal mortality. The risk of these adverse outcomes significantly increases when abortion is performed in unsafe and unregulated conditions [8].

Goffman first defined the concept of stigmatization as the process of “deeply discrediting people.” Stigmatized individuals are often perceived as deviating from societal norms and are consequently subjected to social exclusion, negative attitudes, and discriminatory behaviors [10]. Kumar et al. define abortion stigma as “a negative trait attributed to women who attempt to terminate a pregnancy, rendering them internally or externally inferior to the ideal of ‘femininity.’” This stigma reinforces societal expectations surrounding gender roles and contributes to the marginalization of individuals seeking abortion [11–13].

Abortion stigma is influenced by the sociocultural, moral, and religious values of society and occurs in three forms: perceived stigma, which refers to the fear or expectation of stigmatization; experienced stigma, which involves adverse treatment for having an abortion; and internalized stigma, which manifests as self-judgment or negative feelings about abortion [14].

Stigmatization has various consequences for both women seeking abortion and healthcare professionals [15]. Concerns about stigmatization, mistreatment, and disclosure may compel women to seek unsafe abortions or attempt to self-induce abortions in secrecy [15, 16]. Stigmatization and the need for confidentiality can result in social isolation and psychological distress [14]. Healthcare professionals providing abortion services may be reluctant or refuse to perform abortions due to fears of professional stigmatization, thereby contributing to the rise in unsafe abortions and maternal mortality [17, 18]. In some studies, stigmatized abortion providers reported feeling excluded by society and other healthcare professionals, leading to an increase in their negative perceptions of their profession [14].

According to the Population Planning Law No. 2827, abortion is legal in Türkiye up to the 10th week of gestation without the need for any justification or medical indication. Women may choose to undergo elective abortion within this time frame solely based on their own request. Furthermore, the law permits abortions up to the 20th week of pregnancy in cases where there is a risk to the woman’s life, fetal anomaly, or pregnancy resulting from rape or incest [19]. Despite the legal framework, abortion in Türkiye remains a subject of sociocultural, religious, and moral debates, which can influence public attitudes and contribute to stigma. Although abortion is legally permitted, recent pronatalist policies and governmental strategies aimed at increasing birth rates have raised concerns regarding the actual accessibility of abortion services. These policy directions, while not explicitly banning abortion, may create structural and institutional barriers—such as reduced service availability, lack of provider willingness, and administrative obstacles—that effectively hinder individuals’ ability to obtain safe and legal abortion care. This situation highlights a discrepancy between the legal framework and the practical realities faced by those seeking abortion services [20].

Negative attitudes and behaviors toward abortion can also be observed among healthcare professionals. Women seeking abortion have reported experiencing judgment, mistreatment, and deliberate attempts by healthcare providers to delay or even deny the procedure [15]. In a study conducted among gynecologists in Ireland, 51% stated that abortion on demand should not be legal in the absence of a medical condition, while 84% indicated that they would not provide abortion services under such circumstances [21]. In a survey conducted among medical and health sciences students in Jordan, 53% of respondents regarded abortion as murder, and the majority expressed the view that it should not be used as a means to prevent unwanted pregnancies [22]. Measuring the level of stigmatization among physicians and physician candidates toward abortion is crucial, as it can inform studies aimed at identifying the underlying causes and potential solutions to this issue. Additionally, there is currently no existing research on this topic in our country. This study aims to assess the stigmatizing attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors of physicians and sixth-year medical students working in Kayseri a mid-sized city in Central Anatolia, Türkiye, toward abortion and to examine the factors influencing these perspectives.

Materials and methods

Sample

This study is descriptive and cross-sectional. The sample size was calculated using the G*power program. To compare the mean scores of multiple groups with a medium effect size, a minimum sample size of 252 was calculated, assuming an α of 0.05 and a power of 95%. All participants who agreed to participate in the study were surveyed, and no one was excluded. Participants were selected via convenience sampling from physicians and final-year medical students in Kayseri, a mid-sized city in Central Anatolia, Türkiye. Inclusion criteria were being a practicing physician or a sixth-year medical student and providing verbal informed consent. Participants were recruited by the researchers during their workplace rest periods and break times. The data were collected face-to-face by the researchers in person at the participants’ workplaces, such as hospitals or medical school departments. The questionnaires were administered in a quiet and private area during these rest periods to ensure participant comfort and voluntary engagement. It was conducted in December 2024 with the participation of 314 assistant and specialist physicians, 79 general practitioners, and 192 senior medical students in Erciyes University totaling 585 participants. Gynecologists and their assistants were excluded to avoid bias, as their frequent direct involvement in abortion procedures may lead to significantly different experiences and attitudes compared to other physicians. Including them could have skewed the general physician population’s attitudes we aimed to capture.

Data collection tools

The questionnaire form was used as a data collection tool. The questionnaire consists of two parts and 39 questions. The first part includes questions related to sociodemographic characteristics such as gender, age, marital status, and perspective on abortion, while the second part consists of the Stigmatizing Attitudes, Beliefs, and Behaviors Scale Towards Abortion, which was developed by Schellenberg et al. in 2014 [23]. In 2021, a Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Güner and Öztürk [3]. Factor analysis revealed a three-factor model for the Stigmatizing Attitudes, Beliefs, and Behaviors Towards Abortion Scale (STAS-SABAS): negative stereotypes (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.85), discrimination and exclusion (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.80), and fear of contamination (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.80). The scale was finalized with 18 items, and the overall Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.90, indicating high reliability. The scale items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Only the 15th item of the scale is reverse-coded. The scale has no cut-off point; higher scores approaching 90 indicate stronger stigmatizing attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors toward abortion, while lower scores approaching 0 indicate lower levels of stigmatization.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the ………. University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Approval No: 497, Date: 26.11.2024). The questionnaire used in the study was administered to the participants directly and face-to-face on a voluntary basis after they were informed about the study. The research was conducted in accordance with the Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 25 package program was used for data analysis. The suitability of the data for normal distribution was examined using kurtosis-skewness values, histogram analysis, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and it was observed that the data were not normally distributed. For this reason, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare paired groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare more than two groups. In the relationships between dependent and independent variables, Spearman’s correlation analysis was used for ordinal data (age, number of living children, number of abortions) in groups that did not comply with normal distribution. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in the analysis.

Results

A total of 585 participants took part in the study, including 314 specialist physicians, 79 general practitioners, and 192 senior medical students. Among the participants, 47.4% were male, and 52.6% were female, with a mean age of 29.15 ± 8.01 years. The duration of employment was five years or more for 58.8% of the physicians. 69.4% of the participants were single, 92.5% had a nuclear family structure, 21.0% had children, and 51.8% described their economic status as good. Among female participants, 3.9% had a history of abortion. The mean scale score of the participants was 26.48 ± 9.06, indicating a low level of stigmatization. The stigmatization level among men was significantly higher than that of women (p < 0.001). However, scale scores showed no significant differences based on years of employment, marital status, family type, economic status, having children, or history of miscarriage (Table 1).

Discussion

In our study, the mean score on the Stigmatizing Attitudes, Beliefs, and Behaviors Scale Towards Abortion (STAS-SABAS) was 26.48 ± 9.06, indicating a low level of stigmatization. One possible explanation for the low scale score is the composition of the study group, which included only physicians. However, this may also be related to the fact that the participants were young, well-educated, and actively employed, which is consistent with findings in the existing literature [24]. Although a meta-analysis in Ethiopia found more positive attitudes among healthcare professionals trained in abortion care, the contexts are not directly comparable, as our study did not specifically examine participants with such training [25]. These studies in the literature attributed to physicians’ more profound understanding of the medical aspects of abortion, their recognition of abortion as a healthcare service, and the influence of medical ethics education, which emphasizes providing medical support without judgment in sensitive situations such as abortion. Additionally, their training fosters awareness of patient rights. The fact that none of the physicians agree with the statement “abortion should not be permitted under any circumstances” supporting the perspective that medical training and professional ethics may reduce stigmatizing attitudes towards abortion, as evidenced by the lack of absolute rejection of abortion among participants. It is noteworthy that while the average stigma scores were low, only 31.8% of participants indicated willingness to perform an abortion, and more than half believed it should be legally restricted to medical reasons. This contrast suggests that professional attitudes toward abortion may be shaped by a combination of formal knowledge and personal beliefs. This discrepancy suggests that while participants may theoretically recognize abortion as part of medical care, personal, cultural, or ethical reservations still influence their willingness to perform the procedure. Thus, low stigma scores may reflect cognitive acceptance, but not necessarily behavioral intent.

Although the median scale score was low overall, it was found to be significantly higher in males than in females. The literature does not present a clear consensus on the relationship between gender and the stigmatization of abortion. However, studies by Patel and Johns and Hatipoğlu Sümer have similarly found that men tend to hold more anti-abortion views [26, 27]. In addition to studies that found no gender-based difference [28, 29]; a meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia reported that the likelihood of having a positive attitude towards safe abortion was twice as high in men compared to women in healthcare providers [25]. Some studies suggest that physicians’ attitudes may differ from general societal views due to medical training and ethical principles [30]. However, physicians are also members of society and may still be influenced by prevailing norms and prejudices. Studies have shown that women tend to have higher levels of empathy than men and are more likely to adopt a patient-centered approach [31, 32]. Female physicians, due to their own reproductive health experiences and gender-related perspectives, may approach the issue of abortion with greater personal insight and empathy, allowing them to understand better the decisions that other women may have to make. In contrast, male physicians, lacking direct biological experience, may find it more challenging to comprehend the physical and emotional dimensions of abortion fully.

The literature indicates that individuals with more stigmatizing attitudes tend to hold more negative views regarding the legality of abortion [33, 34]. In our study, the stigmatizing attitudes of those who stated that abortion on demand should be legally possible were found to be significantly lower than those who believed it should be permitted only for medical reasons (Table 3). Additionally, men who exhibited higher levels of stigmatization were more likely to respond, “It should be legal under certain conditions for medical reasons” rather than “Abortion on demand should be legally possible” (Table 4). Among male participants, those who supported abortion only under medical conditions had significantly higher median stigma scores (28.5, 25th–75th percentile: 22–37) compared to those who supported abortion on demand (22, 25th–75th percentile: 18–28.5; p < 0.001). While conditional supporters perceive abortion as acceptable only in cases of medical necessity, those who advocate for abortion on demand may have lower stigmatization levels as they regard abortion as an individual right rather than a condition-based exception. Studies also indicate that individuals in countries with more restrictive abortion laws tend to have less supportive attitudes toward abortion [24]. While legal regulations may influence public attitudes, it is also possible that prevailing societal norms shape abortion legislation. Future research should explore this bidirectional relationship.

Another important finding of our study was that, despite abortion on demand being legal in Türkiye for pregnancies under 10 weeks since 1983, approximately 18% of the study group was unaware of this legal provision. To address this issue, medical school curricula should place greater emphasis on reproductive health and the legal framework surrounding abortion. Studies have shown that education on abortion during medical training enhances students’ understanding of abortion as a fundamental aspect of women’s healthcare and may contribute to reducing stigma associated with the procedure [35].

In our study, 85.3% of physicians believed that there should be a right to conscientious objection regarding abortion, and the scale scores of these individuals were significantly higher. Many authors suggest that the most significant conscientious objection issues for healthcare professionals are related to performing or supporting abortion, euthanasia, assisted reproductive techniques, and prescribing or distributing the morning-after pill [36, 37]. In a study conducted in Madrid, one of the most significant situations in which nurses can declare conscientious objection was identified as the request for voluntary abortion within the first 14 weeks of pregnancy [38]. A study investigating the mechanisms that contribute to abortion stigma within health systems found that structural abortion stigma is perpetuated by decision-makers through the avoidance or deprioritization of abortion and the recognition of the right to conscientious objection, which allows abortion to be excluded from mainstream healthcare services. The resulting fragility of abortion services limits access to person-centered care, including method choice, thereby reinforcing stigma at the individual level [39]. In a study on conscientious objection among medical students in Türkiye, approximately half of the students believed that conscientious objection should be recognized as a right. When faced with a medical intervention conflicting with their personal values, two-thirds of the students stated that they would seek a mandate to take an alternative action if possible, while 8.2% indicated that they would refuse to participate under any circumstances. Additionally, 14% of the students expressed opposition to voluntary termination of pregnancy [40]. This finding suggests that while the exercise of conscientious objection may create barriers to accessing safe abortion services, it must be acknowledged that the right to freedom of conscience is protected under international human rights law, particularly Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR). As Colgrove (2025) emphasizes, any legal accommodation of conscientious objection in medicine must be carefully delimited so that it does not undermine patients’ access to lawful medical services or jeopardize their health. While conscientious objection reflects important ethical and moral considerations for healthcare providers, it can, in some contexts, come into tension with the obligation to provide equitable and non-discriminatory care. In jurisdictions where abortion is recognized as a component of reproductive healthcare, this tension calls for a balanced approach that respects both the rights of providers and the health rights of patients.

In our study, higher levels of abortion-related stigma were observed among participants who indicated they would refuse to perform an abortion, even if they possessed the necessary competence and training (Table 3). One possible explanation is that some physicians view abortion not merely as a medical procedure, but as a morally complex issue that raises profound ethical concerns. These concerns may lead to greater hesitation or moral discomfort, potentially contributing to more stigmatizing attitudes toward both patients seeking abortion and colleagues providing such services. We acknowledge that abortion remains a deeply contested ethical issue, with sincere and divergent views held by professionals across the spectrum. While our study examines this phenomenon from a healthcare perspective, we recognize the importance of presenting these findings in a way that respects the moral pluralism inherent to this debate.

A study conducted among medical students in Türkiye found that as the complexity of abortion cases increased—requiring consideration of multiple ethical principles—the level of ethical dilemma experienced by students in the context of refusing to perform an abortion also increased [41]. Another study reported that while 42.7% of students supported physicians’ right to conscientious objection in general [40]. Physicians with experience in abortion education and practice tend to perceive the issue as a matter of supporting women’s choices and rights while ensuring the provision of professional healthcare services [42]. In a study conducted in Ghana, obstetricians providing abortion services had greater exposure to international debates, treaties, and safe abortion practices, and greater awareness of national research on the public health impacts of unsafe abortions; these factors tempered their religious views [43]. In a study conducted in Uruguay, many health professionals viewed abortion primarily as a sexual and reproductive health service and regarded it as a routine procedure in their daily professional practice [44]. Physicians who do not participate in abortion practice or lack training in this field may be more prone to develop stigmatizing attitudes, as they do not have the opportunity to observe the effects of abortion on patient health directly. Additionally, as members of society, physicians may also be influenced by prevailing social norms. A study conducted among medical students in Jamaica identified lack of experience with abortion and religiosity as the strongest predictors of negative attitudes toward abortion [45]. This aligns with our finding that participants who stated they would not be willing to perform an abortion, despite having adequate training and authorization, exhibited significantly higher levels of abortion stigma.

While the study provides valuable insight into abortion stigma among physicians and medical students, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretations, and the use of self-reported measures may introduce social desirability bias, particularly on a sensitive topic like abortion. Participants might have underreported stigmatizing views due to the connotation of the term ‘stigma’. Future studies may consider using neutral phrasing in questionnaire design. Despite these limitations, the study’s large sample size and use of a culturally validated stigma scale (STAS-SABAS) strengthen the reliability and relevance of its findings within the Turkish healthcare context.

Conclusions

In the study, although the level of stigmatization among physicians towards abortion was found to be low, it was determined that men exhibited higher levels of stigmatization compared to women. This finding highlights the need for greater emphasis on gender equality and reproductive health in medical education.

The association between views on the legality of abortion and stigmatization underscores the connection between the legal framework and social attitudes. To address this, training programs should be implemented to raise awareness that abortion is a legal and safe healthcare service.

The misconceptions among some physicians regarding the legal status of abortion in Türkiye highlight the need for more comprehensive education on reproductive rights in medical curricula. Strengthening abortion education and awareness-raising activities in both medical faculties and postgraduate training is essential to ensure that future healthcare professionals are well-informed and equipped to provide unbiased, patient-centered care.

Finally, physicians who were reluctant to perform abortion had higher stigmatization scores, suggesting that personal attitudes may influence clinical practices. Therefore, training and counseling services should be provided to support physicians in delivering an objective healthcare service that respects patient rights while adhering to their legal and ethical responsibilities.
