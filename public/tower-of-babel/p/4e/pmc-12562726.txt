Unveiling the dual role of Priestia megaterium VIT-2021 membrane vesicles as an antimicrobial and immunomodulator in supporting the growth ofOryza sativa ADT38
Authors: Huldah Pearlin Sarah Lazarus, Nalini Easwaran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo increase yield and productivity, plants are treated with chemicals; however, these interventions have inadvertently contributed to a decline in food security. To mitigate these challenges, plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPRs), which increase yield, have been widely used to modulate plant immunity. Some PGPRs have recently been reported to exhibit opportunistic pathogenicity in humans, raising safety concerns regarding their widespread application. Membrane vesicles (MVs) derived from PGPR may retain similar plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits and thus could aid in plant protection. This research tests the hypothesis that MVs derived from the PGPR Priestia megaterium VIT-2021 as an effective and sustainable alternative for combating bacterial infection. MVs were successfully isolated and characterized, and the results ensured that these MVs are efficient antimicrobial agents against Pseudomonas syringae. Proteomic analysis of the MVs revealed the presence of four antimicrobial peptides AMP underlying their antimicrobial effects. Furthermore, in vitro treatment with these MVs downregulated P. syringae virulence genes, including avrE, hrpS, and mgrA. In planta experiments conducted on Oryza sativa demonstrated that P. megaterium MVs effectively reduced P. syringae pathogenicity through immune modulation, as evidenced by the upregulation of WRKY13, IPA1, WKRY45, NPR1 and PR1, with fold increase of approximately 2.98, 3.55, 1.73, 2.44, and 5.51, respectively, at 4.2 × 10⁸ particle concentration. WRKY13 is involved in SA-mediated secondary cell wall synthesis, which resists pathogen ingress. Increased IPA1 promoted the upregulation of WRKY45, which could contribute to SA-mediated antimicrobial secondary metabolite synthesis. Increased expression of NPR1 and PR1 mediate cell wall rigidification and antimicrobial activity, ultimately contribute to robust immunity. Additionally, increased IPA1 is associated with multiple tiller outgrowths, panicle branching, and grain size development. Thus, P. megaterium MVs demonstrated dual functionality of immunomodulation and growth promotion in O. sativa. Therefore, the current research highlights a pioneering strategy for sustainable crop protection and yield enhancement.Keywords: P. megaterium VIT-2021, membrane vesicles (MVs), antimicrobial, immunomodulator, crop protection

Introduction

With the increase in the global population, the demand for food productivity is also high. To increase yield and productivity, plants have endured various chemical treatments, ultimately led to a significant reduction in food security.1,2 Plant health is safeguarded through plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) which increase plant growth and defense responses.3 However, recent studies have reported concerns regarding the opportunistic pathogenicity of PGPR, highlighting the elevated risks associated with their widespread field application.4,5 In the search for an alternative to PGPR but with similar characteristics, we have proposed membrane vesicles (MVs) derived from PGPRs as a promising solution. MVs are nanosized vesicles (ranging from 20 to 500 nm) released by bacteria with biomolecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and other small molecules.6,7 By acting as biological vehicles, MVs interact with host cells and function as immunomodulators. MVs have broad applications in human health, including drug delivery, vaccines, adjuvants, antibacterial adhesion agents, and immunotherapies.8 In a medical context, they are recognized as excellent immunomodulators and have been explored for therapeutic use.9 However, their application as plant immunomodulators remains largely unexplored. This research, therefore, focuses on the potential use of MVs in agriculture.

Rice is a staple food consumed by more than half of the world’s population.10 According to the OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook, the demand for rice is expected to increase by 11.4% globally by 2030.11 This research aims to increase plant health and human safety by utilizing PGPR-derived MVs as an alternative to whole bacterial cells. Pseudomonas syringae is an important phytopathogen that affects nearly all economically important crop species.12,13 In rice, it causes bacterial brown stripe, halo blight, and sheath rot diseases.13-15 Notably, a study by Peng et al. confirmed that multiple P. syringae. pv. syringae field isolates as the causative agent of bacterial leaf spot from symptomatic rice leaves in Heilongjiang, China.16 This study aims to apply the plant-beneficial bacterium Priestia megaterium--derived MVs against P. syringae infection in Oryza sativa. Although P. megaterium has been reported in recent decades as an opportunistic human pathogen, it also possesses significant PGP traits.17-19 One of our studies revealed that PGP traits are inherent in MVs of P. megaterium, just as they are found in whole cells, and have been validated as non-toxic through in vitro toxicity assays (Unpublished). Research on the application of MVs as potent plant immunomodulators and robust biocontrol agents remains highly limited. Therefore, this study aims to further explore the agricultural applications of MVs. Currently, three potential mechanisms by which plants recognize bacterial vesicles have been hypothesized, though they remain to be validated.20 First, MVs may be recognized through microbe- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs/PAMPs), which are detected by plant pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs), triggering immune responses such as callose deposition, defense hormone production, and phytoalexin synthesis. Second, in the acidic environment of the apoplast, vesicles may undergo lysis, releasing cargo molecules such as proteases, lipases, cellulases, and xylanases. These molecules disrupt cell wall integrity, leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, cell wall strengthening, and alterations in the cytosolic pH and Ca²⁺ concentration. Third, MVs may be internalized by the plant through clathrin-dependent or clathrin-independent pathways, facilitating intracellular interactions.21 This research aims to provide deeper insights into the role of MVs in plant immunity and their potential as sustainable alternatives to PGPR.

In general, MVs from beneficial bacteria have been proven to be non-toxic, and their non-replicable nature further enhances their appeal to researchers. Previous studies have shown that vesicles effectively carry highly persistent cargo, protecting their contents from various environmental stresses and degrading extracellular proteases.22 Vesicles are also known to fuse with specific targets, facilitating precise delivery to the host.23 This research highlights the diverse properties of MVs regarding their PGP activities, such as antimicrobial, anti-biofilm, plant growth promoters, and immune modulators, which elicit immune-related gene expression.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture conditions

P. megaterium VIT-2021 was isolated from a biofertilizer obtained from Gobichettipalayam (11.4549° N, 77.4365° E), Tamil Nadu, India. The whole genome sequence of the isolate in NCBI can be accessed: SAMN38582715. The isolate was maintained in Luria Bertani (LB) media at 30 °C at 100 rpm. Pseudomonas syringae MTCC11950 was procured from the Microbial Type Culture Collection (MTCC, Chandigarh, India). P. syringae was maintained in King’s B media at 30 °C.

Isolation of P. megaterium MVs

MVs from P. megaterium were isolated using the ultracentrifugation method. In detail, 30 mL of 1.5 OD (late log phase) P. megaterium culture was utilized for MV isolation. The bacterial culture was centrifuged at 5000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain a clear supernatant, which was then subjected to another round of centrifugation under the same conditions to remove cell debris. The pellet was discarded, and the resulting supernatants were sequentially filtered through 0.45 and 0.22 μm syringe filters. These filtered supernatants were then introduced into a 5 mL ultracentrifuge tubes (Beckman Coulter) and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 150,000×g for 1 h and 30 min at the Beckman Coulter Optima Max XP instrument. After centrifugation, the MV pellet was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4) and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.24

Characterization of P. megaterium membrane vesicles

Morphological analysis of membrane vesicles of P. megaterium

Field emission scanning electron microscopy.

Isolated MVs were subjected to visualization using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM). In a concise procedure, 10 µL of P. megaterium MVs were added to sterile foil before being air-dried. The sample was subsequently fixed with 100 µL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 5 min. This was succeeded by stepwise dehydration of the MVs using ethanol concentrations ranging from 70% to 100%. Once dehydrated, the foils with MVs were left to air-dry thoroughly overnight in preparation for microscopic examination. The samples were subsequently coated with gold for 30 s to increase the electron conductivity. The MVs were visualized using Thermo Fisher FEI QUANTA 250 FEG FESEM instrument equipped with a Schottky field emission electron gun operating at various voltages from 5 to 30 kV.25,26

Transmission electron microscopy.

The MVs were subjected to visualization under transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In a concise procedure, 10 µL of P. megaterium MVs were applied onto a copper grid, followed by negative staining with a 1% solution of uranyl acetate for 1 min. After removing the surplus stain, the preparation was air-dried and observed through TEM utilizing the FEI-TECNAI G2-20 121 Twin model at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.27

Size and concentration analysis of P. megaterium membrane vesicles

Size and concentration of P. megaterium MVs were evaluated through nano particle tracking analyser (NTA). Initially, MVs were stained with DiO (lipophilic dye) for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. To remove the excess stain, MVs were ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g for 1 h and 30 min at 4 °C using the Beckman Coulter Optima Max XP instrument. Using Particle metrix zetaview-PMX 130-mono laser, NTA, the size and concentration of the isolated MVs were analyzed. Using Horiba Scientific SZ-100 the integrity (zeta potential) of the MVs was determined.25,28,29

Antibacterial activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae

The antibacterial activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae by a spot-on-lawn (qualitative) assay. The LB plate was spread with 100 µL of 0.1 OD P. syringae and subsequently 100 µL of P. megaterium MVs, 100 µL of P. megaterium MVs treated with proteinase K (100 µg/mL) for 1 h and 100 µL of PBS buffer as a negative control were spotted individually. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. The zone of inhibition around the MV spot indicated the antibacterial activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae. Further quantitative analysis was performed using two-fold broth dilution in LB. Briefly, P. megaterium MVs of 4.2 × 10⁸, 2.1 × 10⁸, 1.05 × 10⁸, 5.25 × 107, 2.63 × 107 and 1.32 × 107 particle concentrations were added into 0.1 OD adjusted P. syringae culture. The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, after which the cell density was quantified at 600 nm, and the CFU count was subsequently calculated.24,30

Protein profile analysis of P. megaterium MVs

Protein profile analysis of P. megaterium MVs was done by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Briefly, 2.1 × 10⁸ particles of P. megaterium MVs were lysed with 1-min sonication and treated with proteinase K (100 µg/mL) for 1 h. The control sample without proteinase K treatment was also processed in the same way. MVs were subsequently mixed with Laemmli buffer containing 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 1% β-mercaptoethanol, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 12.5 mM EDTA, and 0.02% bromophenol blue. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and incubated at 95 °C for 10 mins. Following incubation, the samples were loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel comprising a 5% stacking gel and a 12% resolving gel. Electrophoresis was performed at 50 V until the samples entered the resolving gel, after which the voltage was increased to 100 V, and the process was continued for 60 min. The gel was subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining solution (0.1% Coomassie R-250, 50% methanol, and 10% glacial acetic acid).24

Proteomic profiling of P. megaterium MVs

The proteomic profiling of MVs was done using the LC MS/MS protocol with necessary modifications. The MVs were subjected to sonication for 1 min to release proteins, and approximately 50 µg of the released protein was processed in 100 µL of 7 M urea. To break disulfide bonds, 50 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Next, 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) was introduced, the mixture was vortexed, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to achieve alkylation. To reduce the urea concentration in preparation for trypsin digestion, 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH₄HCO₃) was added. Trypsin was then introduced at a 1:30 enzyme-to-substrate ratio, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. After digestion, the samples were dried under a speed vacuum and reconstituted in 20 µL of Milli-Q water containing 0.1% formic acid. Desalting was performed using a Thermo Scientific C18 Pierce column, where the resin was activated and equilibrated first in a 50% methanol/50% acetonitrile (ACN) solution, followed by equilibration in 5% ACN/0.5% TFA for 3–5 min. The reconstituted protein sample was loaded onto the resin, centrifuged for 3 min, and washed with 0.5% TFA in 5% ACN. Elution was carried out with 20 µL of 70% ACN, after which the samples were dried under a speed vacuum and stored at −80 °C for subsequent LC‒MS/MS analysis. LC‒MS/MS analysis employed mobile phases that consisted of 0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water (A) and 85:15 acetonitrile in Milli-Q with 0.1% formic acid (B). Separation was achieved using a PepMap RSLC C18 analytical column (2 µm, 100 Å, 50 cm) and an Acclaim PepMap 100 precolumn (100 µm × 2 cm, nanoViper). The analysis was conducted on a Q Exactive Plus Biopharma mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The Thermo raw files were converted to the open mzML format using ThermoRawFileParser (1.4.5). Peptides were identified by matching them to host protein sequences using MSGF + (2024.03.26) software. High-confidence peptides were filtered using a posterior error probability threshold of 0.01 in openMS (3.3.0). The filtered peptides were mapped to a target-decoy database for validation, and the peptides were extracted. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) were predicted using the DBAASP and CAMP servers, and the common AMPs identified by both servers were compiled and chosen for further analysis.31

Qualitative analysis of anti-biofilm activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae

The biofilm inhibition assay was carried out as previously described with slight modifications. Sterile glass cover slips and foils were placed in both the treated and untreated wells of P. syringae cultures for subsequent light microscopy and FESEM analysis. Briefly, P. megaterium MVs of 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations were added into the well along with LB, followed by 0.1 OD adjusted P. syringae culture. After inoculation, the plates were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the coverslips and foils were carefully displaced from the wells and rinsed with water to remove the unbound sessile cells. The coverslips were stained with 0.1% crystal violet and visualized under a light microscope. The foils were subsequently fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde followed by serial dehydration with 70%–100% ethanol. Finally, the samples were sputtered with gold for FESEM. FESEM was performed using Thermo Fisher FEI QUANTA 250 FEG FESEM, which is equipped with a Schottky field emission electron gun operating at voltages between 5 and 30 kV.32

Quantitative analysis of anti-biofilm activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae

Biofilm formation was assayed by the ability of cells to adhere to the wells of 96-well microtiter plates as previously mentioned. The non-adherent cells were carefully removed with a micropipette, and then the wells were gently rinsed with sterile water. Subsequently, 125 µL of 0.1% crystal violet (CV) was added to each well and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 15 min and the unbound strains were rinsed thoroughly with water. The biofilm was quantified after the addition of 30% acetic acid to each CV-stained well by determining the absorbance at 595 nm using a BioTech Epoch spectrophotometer. Further, biofilm associated eDNA was quantified for P. syringae treated with P. megaterium MVs of 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations. After 48 h of incubation, the biofilms were treated with 1 mL of Tris-EDTA NaCl buffer for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatants were subsequently discarded, and 700 µL of TE buffer was added to remove attached biofilm cells. The cells were subsequently pelleted, and the supernatant containing eDNA was extracted using phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol. The obtained eDNA was resuspended in TE buffer and quantified using a spectrophotometer.33

In vitro relative gene expression analysis of P. syringae treated with P. megaterium MVs

P. syringae was treated with 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations of P. megaterium MVs and incubated at 30 °C overnight. After incubation, RNA was extracted from both MV-treated and untreated cells of P. syringae using a Qiagen AllPrep RNA extraction kit (Germany) according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The quality of the extracted RNA was verified using NanoDrop spectrophotometer, after which it was reverse transcribed into cDNA using Prime Script RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed via TB green Premix ex taq II (Takara, Japan). cDNA served as the template, and primers were procured from Integrated DNA Technology, India. RT-PCR was performed using BIO-RAD CFX96 Real-time PCR system. The GAPDH gene was used as the housekeeping gene. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing and a final extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The relative mRNA expression levels were quantified using 2-ΔΔct method.34

In planta trials

In planta anti-bacterial activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae

The seeds of Oryza sativa Aduthurai 38 (ADT 38) were obtained from Tamil Nadu Agriculture University (TNAU), Aduthurai, Tamil Nadu, India. Seeds were surface sterilized, germinated and four-week-old seedlings were treated with 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations of MVs; 1 mM salicylic acid (SA) served as a positive control, and water served as a negative control. After 24 h, the seedlings were challenged with 0.02 OD P. syringae cells dispersed in 10 mM magnesium sulfate. Following the appearance of leaf yellowing, the leaves were photographed, excised, and plated on KB agar plates. The CFU/cm2 values were calculated and compared with those of the control groups. Furthermore, defense gene expression analysis was carried out as described below.24,35,36 The percentage of infected area was calculated using the formula, percentage of infected area = total number of infected area/total number of entire study area × 100.37,38

In planta relative gene expression analysis of rice seedlings

The seedlings were removed and snap-frozen using liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. RNA was extracted using the Macherey-Nagel NucleoSpin TriPrep kit, Germany, according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, and qRT-PCR was carried out. The GAPDH gene was used as a housekeeping gene, and the relative mRNA expression levels of important genes were quantified using the 2-ΔΔct method.39

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and statistical significance was determined for growth rate measurement, biofilm formation, eDNA quantification, gene expression analysis, and colony-forming units using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test with GraphPad Prism 8 software.

Results and discussion

P. megaterium MVs were successfully isolated using ultracentrifugation and subjected to sequential characterization. Our FESEM analysis facilitated the visualization of P. megaterium vesiculation, showing MVs close to bacterial cells, as in Figure 1A. Morphological analysis with FESEM and TEM revealed MVs with intact spherical structures and sizes ranging from 85 to 450 nm (Figure 1B–D). NTA revealed a MV concentration of 2.1 × 109 particles/mL (Figure 1E). Additionally, NTA confirmed an average MV size of 277.5 nm, which is consistent with the FESEM and TEM findings. MVs zeta potential indicated a value of −3.6 mV (Figure 1F), which confirmed structural integrity and supported successful isolation of MVs using ultracentrifuge.40

The isolated and characterized MVs were tested in vitro against P. syringae to validate our hypothesis. MVs exhibited antibacterial activity, as indicated by a spot assay, which showed significant growth inhibition against P. syringae (Figure 2A). Similar findings were reported by Wang et al., where Streptococcus mutans lawn spotted with Burkholderia thailandensis OMVs exhibited substantial growth inhibition.30 Quantitative analysis of the antimicrobial activity of MVs revealed significant inhibition of P. syringae growth at relatively high concentrations, with a decrease in inhibition observed at relatively low concentrations (Figure 2B). These findings indicated a dose-dependent inhibitory effect of MVs against P. syringae, aligns with the log CFU, which demonstrated inhibition of up to 14.87% and 12.66% at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations, respectively (Figure 2C). The pronounced growth inhibition upon MV treatment suggests that MVs retain key antimicrobial properties. This further implies that bioactive compounds such as antimicrobial peptides, enzymes, or secondary metabolites responsible for pathogen suppression are effectively packaged within the MVs. Our focus was then on identifying the active agent responsible for the inhibitory effect of MVs. To achieve this goal, MVs were treated with proteinase K and PBS spotted against P. syringae (Figure 2D and E). The absence of significant inhibition in proteinase K-treated MVs indicated that proteins or peptides in the MVs may be the possible reason for their bactericidal activity. Subsequently, proteinase K-treated MVs and untreated MVs were assessed using SDS-PAGE.24 The analysis revealed distinct protein bands in untreated MVs, whereas proteinase K-treated MVs presented diminished bands, confirming protein degradation. The prominent band observed in the proteinase K-treated MVs corresponded to the molecular weight of proteinase K (28.9 kDa) (Figure 3). The bands observed in untreated MVs, with approximately 40 and 50 kDa, might correspond to class III bacteriocin. Bacteriocins are well-known antimicrobial peptides that contribute to the antimicrobial activity of MVs. This hypothesis was further validated by proteomic profiling of P. megaterium MVs to identify specific proteins and antimicrobial peptides.

Through proteomic analysis, 128 peptides were obtained from P. megaterium MVs (Table S1), among which four were predicted to have antimicrobial activity using both the DBAASP and CAMP servers. The AMPs consistently identified by both the servers were designated as P1, P2, P3, and P4 (Table 1). The physicochemical properties of these peptides indicate their antimicrobial potential through their interaction with microbial membranes. Key factors such as charge, hydrophobicity, amphiphilicity, and structural propensities play a crucial role in their antimicrobial activity. All peptides exhibited a net positive charge of +1, with isoelectric points above 9.5, aligning with known AMPs. This suggests their ability to interact with anionic bacterial membranes via electrostatic attraction.41

A higher hydrophobic value is recommended for AMPs, as it enhances membrane disruption by destabilizing the bilayer and promoting cell depolarization. Notably, P1 and P3 displayed greater hydrophobic moments, indicative of a strong amphipathic character, with enhanced membrane disruption potential.42 Peptide penetration depth and tilt angles are also critical for membrane insertion. In agreement with previous studies, all four peptides exhibited high penetration depths and tilt angles, with P3 showing the highest penetration depth of 30 Å, suggesting deeper membrane insertion.43 Amphiphilicity plays a role in direct membrane lysis, facilitating peptide interactions with the membrane surface and penetration. P1 and P3 demonstrated relatively high amphiphilicity indices of 1.37 and 1.23, respectively, indicating potent antimicrobial activity.44 The propensity for polyproline II (PPII) coil structures contributes to peptide flexibility and penetration, with P3 and P4 exhibiting higher values of 1.13 and 1.04, respectively.45 Importantly, none of the peptides showed a propensity for in vitro aggregation, which is advantageous for maintaining bioactivity in solution.41 Thus, the overall characteristics of the P1 and P3 AMPs demonstrated their promising antimicrobial activity with balanced amphiphilicity, membrane-penetrating potential, and structural properties. However, further experimental validation is needed to confirm their antimicrobial efficacy and mechanism of action.

Given the antibacterial activity of MVs, we further investigated their antibiofilm properties. Biofilm formation is a major virulence strategy employed by pathogenic bacteria such as P. syringae to increase survival, making it critical to evaluate the anti-biofilm potential of P. megaterium MVs. Differentmicroscopic methods, such as light microscopy and FESEM imaging, have validated the anti-biofilm activity of P. megaterium MVs against P. syringae. Initial observations using light microscopy revealed a substantial reduction in P. syringae biofilm. Further these findings were supported by FESEM analysis, which revealed a significant decrease in biofilm density (Figure 4A–F). Quantitative analysis revealed biofilm inhibition of up to 52.44% and 32.57% and eDNA inhibition of up to 60.5% and 48.83% at particle concentrations of 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸, respectively (Figure 5A and B). eDNA is a key factor in bacterial adhesion and structural integrity of biofilms, primarily by enhancing the hydrophobicity of the cell envelope, thereby promoting robust cell-to-surface attachment.46 Consequently, through the inhibition of eDNA in P. syringae, MVs contributed to antibiofilm efficacy.

Additionally, molecular-level confirmation of the inhibitory activity of MVs against P. syringae was performed. Our findings demonstrated that MVs function as effective biocontrol agents by downregulating the expression of P. syringae virulence genes such as Avirulence protein E (avrE), Hypersensitive Response and Pathogenicity protein (hrpS), and Multiple Gene Regulator protein (mgrA). avrE was downregulated 3.57- and 2.17-fold at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations, respectively. The avrE reduction may inhibit the avrE effector protein from triggering stomatal closure, pathogen ingress, and water-soaked lesions and inhibit callose deposition, substantially attenuating the virulence of the pathogen.13 Furthermore, two important T3SS proteins, hrpS and mgrA showed 4 and 1.67-fold and 5.88- and 1.92-fold reductions at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations, respectively, which would have decreased P. syringae virulence, likely due to impaired translocation of effector proteins into the host cell. As these T3SS effectors typically disrupt the recognition of MAMPs by the host, their inhibition improves plant pathogen recognition and responses (Figure 5C).47,48 These findings support the potential application of MVs for field-level management of phytopathogenic infections.

Following these validations, MVs were evaluated for their in planta antibacterial activity against P. syringae in O. sativa seedlings. The in planta results demonstrated a significant reduction in infection in the seedlings primed with MVs, which was in line with the SA (positive control)-treated groups. SA is a well-known plant immune modulator with significant SAR-mediated antimicrobial activity against P. syringae.49 Seedlings primed with P. megaterium MVs exhibited resistance to P. syringae, with complete reduction or insignificant chlorosis at 4.2 × 10⁸ particle concentration and very moderate chlorosis at 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentration (Figure 6A–E). The percentage measurement of the infected area confirmed that MVs provided substantial protection against infection, with 40% in the infection control group, compared to 5%, 10% and 17.5% infection in SA-treatment, 4.2 × 10⁸, and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentration of MVs treatment groups, respectively. This resistance pattern corresponded with the CFU/cm2 of P. syringae, with 92.7%, 92% and 78.8% inhibition, respectively, relative to the infection control (Figure 6F), indicating that the 4.2 × 10⁸ particle concentration was more effective in the in planta trials. These findings directed the research towards molecular confirmation through gene expression analysis to investigate immune modulation and priming effects in plants mediated by the recognition of MVs.

(G) Growth and immune-related gene expression in O. sativa.

Our in planta, gene expression analysis revealed altered expression of 5 important genes: (i) WRKY13, originating from the conserved amino acid sequence WRKYGQK, located at the N-terminal end of the DNA-binding WRKY domain, which is associated with induced systemic resistance (ISR) and systemic acquired resistance (SAR); (ii) ideal plant architecture 1 (IPA1), a growth- and immunity-related gene; (iii) WRKY45, which is associated with SAR; and (iv) nonexpressor of pathogenesis-related gene 1 (NPR1), a common transcriptional regulator of ISR and SAR; and (v) pathogenesis-related gene (PR1), an SAR-marker gene. GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization.50 Analogous to human cells, plants can be immunomodulated by MVs.35 Previously proposed models of vesicle‒plant interactions suggest that MAMPs present in vesicles are recognized by plant pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), triggering PRR dimerization. This recognition activates a signaling cascade involving the phosphorylation of respiratory burst oxidase homolog protein D (RBOHD), leading to an elevated Ca²⁺ concentration and rapid production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Subsequently, mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) are activated, which in turn stimulate WRKY transcription factors to regulate the defense response.21 This cascade results in activation of the jasmonic acid and ethylene signaling pathways, phytoalexin production, and increased callose deposition.

WRKY TFs are recognized as key nodes at the convergence of the SA and JA signaling pathways. It acts as a regulator of SA-dependent gene expression while simultaneously functioning as a suppressor of JA-mediated responses, thereby increasing plant resistance against hemi biotrophic infections.51 In the context of our pathogen of interest, P. syringae, a hemi biotroph, plants typically respond by activating SA-induced immune pathways, as JA-mediated responses are generally less effective against hemi biotrophs.21,52 Furthermore, WRKY13 activates expression of the NST2 gene, a key regulator of secondary cell wall biosynthesis in sclerenchyma providing a physical barrier for pathogen ingress, likely accounting for the observed inhibition of P. syringae in our study.53,54 WRKY13 was upregulated, with fold changes of approximately 1.08, 2.82, 2.98, and 1.10 in the infection control, SA-treatment, MV treatment at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentration groups, respectively (Figure 6G). In case of IPA1 expression, the fold increase was 0.84, 3.76, 3.55, and 2.05 for the infection control, SA treatment, MVs at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle-treated seedlings, respectively. IPA1 subsequently activates DEP1, which is involved in the regulation of multiple tiller outgrowths, panicle branching, and grain size development in O. sativa.55 On the other hand, IPA1 expression induced by MVs, suggests MVs' involvement in the defense response through WRKY45 expression, which is consistent with the known dual functions of IPA1 in the regulation of plant growth and immunity.56 Our study findings also revealed the upregulation of WRKY45, with fold changes of approximately 1.30, 1.39, 1.73, and 1.96 in the infection control, SA-treatment, MV treatment at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations, respectively. WRKY45 elicits an SA-mediated defense response by regulating the expression of cytochrome P450s and Glutathione S-transferase (GTS), which together contribute to the synthesis of phytoalexin, an antimicrobial secondary metabolites.57 Previous reports have shown that vesicles of P. fluorescens Migula ATCC 13525 can trigger a wide range of immune responses in plants.24 In the present study, NPR1 expression showed fold increase of 0.55, 1.36, 2.44, and 1.67 for infection control, SA treatment, MV at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particle concentrations treated seedlings, respectively. This indicate that SA-mediated defense was induced upon MV application, resembling the response observed in the SA-treated positive control group. NPR1, a key co-activator of both ISR and SAR, promotes SA-related PR1 expression while subsequently suppressing JA-related defense responses against hemi biotrophic P. syringae infections in plants.58 Both MV and SA treatments in O. sativa resulted in significant resistance against P. syringae, with MV-treated plants showing a relatively high expression profile of NPR1. Moreover, NPR1 activation increased the expression of the SAR marker gene PR1, with increase of 1.23, 6.04, 5.51, and 1.44 for the infection control, SA treatment, MV at 4.2 × 10⁸ and 2.1 × 10⁸ particles treated seedlings, respectively. PR1 is associated with cell wall rigidification and antimicrobial activity, ultimately contributing to robust immunity in O. sativa.21

In summary, the infection control groups showed a basal immune response relative to the untreated control groups, indicating that a natural immune response was triggered upon pathogen recognition. However, P. syringae appears to suppress this response, as evidenced by relatively low induction of all the studied defense- and growth-related genes. On the other hand, SA treated plants exhibited moderate increased expression of NPR1 but demonstrated substantial upregulation of PR1. In contrast, MV treatment showed significant increased expression of NPR1 than SA treated plants. However, the corresponding increase in PR1 was slightly lower than that in SA-treated plants. Our findings indicate that although MVs can increase the expression of NPR1, the effective activation of PR1 depends on post-translational modification and activation of NPR1. Specifically, SA promotes NPR1 monomerization and nuclear translocation and enables interaction with the TGA transcription factor to drive downstream gene expression of PR1.21 Based on the collective observations of our study regarding the activation of defense-related genes, MVs appear to elicit an SA-related immune response in O. sativa similar to that induced by SA (positive control) treatment up on P. syringae infection.

Collectively, our findings suggest that MV application efficiently modulates key immune regulatory genes, notably WRKY13, IPA1, WRKY45, NPR1 and PR1, thereby promoting a synchronized augmentation of immune response and growth potential in O. sativa. This mechanism offers a promising and sustainable strategy for crop improvement by concurrently activating both growth- and defense-related genes.

Current research introduces a novel approach involving the exploration and application of MVs derived from P. megaterium VIT−2021 as a safe alternative to whole PGPR cells, addressing the growing concern of opportunistic pathogenicity associated with live microbial inoculants. Unlike conventional PGPR applications, this approach leverages the bioactive potential of cell-free MVs, which retain PGP and antimicrobial traits. The ability of these MVs to inhibit P. syringae by downregulating virulence gene expression while enhancing systemic immune responses in O. sativa, reinforces our underlying hypothesis. These findings represent a significant advancement toward the development of next-generation, sustainable biocontrol strategies for the enhancement of crop protection and productivity.

Conclusion

We successfully elucidated the antimicrobial activity of the PGPR P. megaterium-derived MVs against the plant pathogen P. syringae and evaluated their PGP potential in O. sativa under in vivo conditions. Our results suggested that MVs induce an SA-mediated defense response in O. sativa against hemi biotrophic pathogen P. syringae. These positive outcomes support the claim that MVs can serve as an alternative strategy to increase agricultural productivity. In regard to the commercialization of MV application, the following aspects, such as economic feasibility, stability, and long-term storage, need to be well standardized. Future possibilities to overcome some of these limitations include the following: PGPR strains can be engineered to increase MV production, and the encapsulation of MVs with possible carriers to improve stability and long-term storage. MVs derived from P. megaterium could be screened against a wide range of other pathogens, indicating its potential as a multiple disease control agent. Furthermore, comparing MVs from different PGPR strains may help in finding the most efficient candidates. Future research should focus on field trials and the development of bioactive formulations utilizing P. megaterium MVs for sustainable crop management.
