The pedagogical circle: an innovative approach to enhance learning and reduce stress in undergraduate dental education – a pilot study
Authors: Guillaume Savard, Hervé Becquet, Alice Bodin, Antony Chandonnay, Gael Y. Rochefort, Frédéric Denis, Matthieu Renaud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe initial cycle of dental training in France is highly stressful. The pedagogical circle is an innovative educational tool designed to cultivate a collaborative and psychologically secure learning environment, wherein students and teachers, seated in a circle, take turns articulating their emotional state, perceived challenges and learning requirements. The study aims to investigate the impact of the pedagogical circle on mitigating students’ perceived stress and enhancing their satisfaction with practical instruction, drawing upon non-violent communication and self-determination theory.Materials and methodsWe conducted a cross-sectional observational pilot study wherein students (n = 56) completed an anonymous questionnaire assessing stress and satisfaction with teaching strategies. A composite stress score was derived. Correlation analysis and ANOVA (employing Fisher’s F test, with significance set at p < 0.01) were conducted to evaluate the association between participation in pedagogical circles and stress levels.ResultsThe response rate was 98.21% (55/56 participants). The mean stress score was 10.3 (median: 11; standard deviation: 6.4). The stress score exhibited a statistically significant correlation (p < 0.01) with pedagogical circle items, as ascertained via Fisher’s correlation test, which compared stress levels between participants reporting positive experiences with the circles and those who did not. Cross-analysis of the stress score against items related to the pedagogical circle revealed a positive impact of this methodology on students’ perceived stress, with statistically significant effects (p < 0.01) on ‘feeling at ease within the group and being able to express oneself’ and ‘feeling supported in one’s learning’.ConclusionThe pedagogical circle methodology appears to exert a positive influence on students’ stress levels and learning outcomes. Nonetheless, owing to the brief timeframe and limited sample size, additional longitudinal studies are required to corroborate these findings. This innovative approach cultivates empathy and resilience, thereby paving the way for scalable interventions in health professions education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08156-w.

Background

Dental surgery studies in France are structured into three cycles, spanning six to nine years in total. The initial cycle encompasses two years, focusing on fundamental instruction and simulation-based procedural learning (approximately 500 h of practical work over two years), primarily in conservative dentistry and dental prosthetics. This pre-clinical training proves markedly stressful for novice dental students possessing varying technical aptitudes [1–3].

In 2022, a new Faculty of Odontology was established the University of Tours in France. The creation of this new faculty facilitated the implementation of an alternative pedagogical framework, whilst adhering to the practical and theoretical guidelines promulgated by the Ministry of Higher Education [4]. Furthermore, the objectives included mitigating stress arising from undergraduate education, thereby constructively channelling it towards enhanced learning efficiency and improved student well-being.

Debriefing is regarded as a crucial educational phase in health simulation-based learning [5–7]. Debriefing approaches, when integrated with care simulations, seek to enhance learning, future performance, and ultimately patient outcomes. They also enable management of the learner’s emotional state (as experienced in relation to the scenario, disclosure framework, etc.). This is accomplished, in part, by affording learners the opportunity to elucidate their knowledge and rationale for actions undertaken during the simulation [8–11]. According to a meta-analysis by Darling-Hammond et al., in-service training should be linked to practice, emphasise students’ learning of specific curricular elements, and foster robust relationships among teachers [12]. Furthermore, a systematic review has shown that debriefing constitutes an integral component of all healthcare simulation-based learning experiences [9].

Drawing upon the debriefing model, the pedagogical circle aims to enable each student and teacher, in turn, to articulate their current experiences within the procedural teaching session and, by extension, across faculty instruction generally. Students are also encouraged, at their discretion, to articulate their feelings, emotions, and immediate needs, thereby illuminating potential barriers to their learning process.

Grounded in the principle of non-violent communication, the pedagogical circle seeks to teach participants how to listen to themselves and others, and to express themselves authentically and empathetically within a secure environment (facilitated by pedagogical objectives and the teacher), emphasising respectful communication and mutual listening. It affords each individual (student, teacher, participant) an equal place, characterised by humanity and empathy. Furthermore, it ameliorates interpersonal relationships (among students and teachers), thereby fostering a more confident learning environment aligned with academic success and stress reduction during teaching sessions (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Concept of the pedagogical circle: all participants (students and teachers alike) are seated in a circle, with each speaking in turn

Empathy is a potent tool acknowledged in patient-caregiver relationships and serves as a universal instrument in interpersonal interactions. It encompasses concern for others, the capacity to contemplate their physical, emotional and cognitive states in the present moment, and the ability to maintain one’s identity (differentiating self-generated perceptions from those induced by others) [13, 14]. Thus, empathy represents one of the essential qualities for a mindful teacher to forge a high-quality, equitable, and balanced teacher-student relationship. Cultivating empathy towards oneself and others (students, caregivers, patients) is pivotal in improving the student-teacher relationship and, consequently, elevating the quality of teaching. Such empathetic communication enhances the learning environment, promotes adherence to the training curriculum, and facilitates learning [15, 16]. Non-violent communication constitutes one methodology employed to cultivate empathy. The concept of non-violent communication was initially formalised by clinical psychologist Marshall Rosenberg (1934–2015) to enhance communication and foster more peaceful dialogue among conflicting parties during the Civil Rights Movement of the 1960 s in the United States. Rosenberg’s communication tool and model emphasise empathic listening, sincere expression, and the cultivation of attention to one’s deepest needs and those of others, thereby developing compassion. The primary objective of non-violent communication is to cultivate an empathic bond between interlocutors [14]. Studies have demonstrated the positive impact of learning non-violent communication on self-esteem acquisition, empathy capacity, interpersonal relations, and students’ communication skills – particularly in preventing difficulties, facilitating dialogue, and comprehending and resolving conflicts [14],].

Following two years of trialling this pedagogical innovation, this study aimed to ascertain the impact of the pedagogical circle on perceived stress during practical teaching sessions and on learning enhancement, employing a questionnaire to evaluate the programme and pedagogy among undergraduate dental students at the Faculty of Odontology in Tours, France.

Materials and methods

At the initiative of a teacher or upon a student’s request when deemed necessary, a pedagogical circle is convened (typically once a month or every two months). The classroom is subsequently reorganised: the centre of the room is cleared; chairs are arranged in a circle; and each participant is invited to speak in sequence.

Following two years of implementing this educational innovation, an observational cross-sectional study was undertaken in June 2024 at the newly established Faculty of Odontology, University of Tours, France. It involved all 28 students per graduating class who had completed the initial cycle of dental education during the 2023 and 2024 academic years. All students (n = 56) were invited to complete an anonymous online questionnaire during the final practical session of the academic year.

The Ethics Committee of the University of Tours was consulted and determined that no specific authorisation was necessary, given the anonymous nature of the questionnaire. Moreover, students supported this evaluation of their instruction, which could yield added value and foster a positive learning environment.

The questionnaire comprised of two complementary sections [Appendix 1], adapted from validated instruments [2, 17] with local modifications for cultural pertinence: one section specifically evaluating the level of tension or psychological pressure perceived by individuals in stressful situations, thereby identifying potential discomfort; and another assessing the degree of well-being or contentment in particular domain, thus identifying positive states or alignment with expectations. The first section included 12 questions utilising a Likert scale from 0 to 4 to gauge stress pertaining to practical and theoretical teaching. The second section employed a satisfaction questionnaire examining diverse teaching methodologies (e.g., lectures, inverted classrooms, practical work), comprising 14 items on a Likert scale from 0 to 4 to evaluate student approval. Among these 14 items, four specifically addressed the pedagogical circle methodology. The questionnaire was developed and adapted by the Faculty of Odontology at the University of Tours, drawing upon the ‘Dental Environmental Stress’ questionnaire [3] and that employed by the Montpellier faculty to evaluate the student stress [2].

The questionnaire’s reliability (internal consistency) was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (yielding values between 0 and 1, with α > 0.7 generally deemed acceptable). Data analysis involved multidimensional evaluation of the 26 items and their intercorrelations. This approach enabled simultaneous comprehension of the questionnaire’s two sections (stress assessment and teaching evaluation). A Chi-square test, incorporating degrees of freedom, was utilised to ascertain statistically significant differences between expected (theorical) and observed data frequencies in contingency tables. Analysis of variance was performed using Fisher’s F test, accounting for between – and within – group variances. All tests employed a significance threshold of p < 0.01. Data were processed using Le Sphinx software (Chavanod, France).

A stress score was additionally computed by summing the five Likert scale response modalities across the 12 stress assessment items, yielding a range from 0 (0 × 12; no stress) to 48 (4 × 12; maximum stress). Subsequently, this stress score facilitated cross-analysis to quantify the pedagogical circle methodology’s impact on stress via Fisher’s correlation test applied to the four specific related items.

Results

The questionnaire was completed anonymously by 55 of the 56 targeted students (response rate: 98.21%; 55/56 participants), evenly distributed across the two years, comprising 44% males and 56% females. The questionnaire completion time, approximately 7 min, was pre-tested with five voluntary students. These pre-test results were subsequently incorporated into the total sample.

Stress questionnaire (12 items)

The stress questionnaire items yielded a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.90. The items most strongly associated with stress, in descending order, were: ‘End-of-semester examinations’, ‘Manual dexterity and acquisition of manual skills’, ‘Fear of making mistakes during practical work’, ‘The volume of coursework to assimilate’, ‘Tasks required during practical work’, and ‘Continuous assessment’ (Table 1). The overall mean across all items was 0.9 (standard deviation: 0.9) on the 0–4 Likert scale. Questionnaire evaluating the pedagogical circle (4 items)

Discussion

This study sought to elucidate the impact of the pedagogical circle on students’ perceived stress during practical learning sessions in the pre-clinical curriculum, alongside its effects on learning enhancement. Consistent with the literature, stressors including ‘end-of-semester examinations’, ‘manual dexterity and acquisition of practical skills’, ‘fear of making mistakes during practical sessions’, ‘volume of material to assimilate’, and ‘required tasks during practical sessions’ were identified as pivotal factors directly influencing instruction and students’ learning experiences [18, 19].

Furthermore, students appreciated elements such as ‘the overall atmosphere supports success during practical sessions’ and ‘interactions with instructors during lectures and practical sessions are satisfactory’ as factors positively influencing their learning. The assertion that ‘the environment during practical sessions contributes to progression and learning’ also surfaced as a notable satisfaction factor relative to baseline. Collectively, these elements contribute to a supportive environment for students’ development in hands-on instruction. The learning environment constitutes a central pillar of modern pedagogy, serving as a substantial asset for students by enhancing their well-being and academic performance [20, 21].

Stress management and the creation of a positive, high-quality educational environment are indispensable in teaching, especially in the initial years of training. Whilst positive stress can be challenging yet rewarding, ‘negative’ stress – commonly termed simply ‘stress’ – is characterised by adverse emotional states and frequently perceived as overwhelming. Stress is academically defined as a physiological, emotional, cognitive, and behavioural response to stimuli that may impair students’ capacity to adapt to academic demands [22].

Moreover, emotional stress levels in the initial years of dental training in France have been documented as exceeding those in other European student populations [23, 24]. Numerous studies have illustrated that burnout and elevated stress levels among students can result in diminished concentration, reduced professional satisfaction, and heightened risk of future medical errors [25]. Additional studies have underscored the prevalence of stress, anxiety, and depression among undergraduate dental students [24–27]. Hence, it is imperative to accord close attention to students in order to ameliorate interpersonal relationships (student-teacher, student-student, student-patient), elevate satisfaction levels, and optimise learning outcomes [26].

In recent decades, student perceptions of their educational environment have garnered increasing attention. Positive correlations have been documented between the learning environment and satisfaction with one’s field of study [20, 21, 28]. These studies also emphasise the pertinence of mindfulness-based stress reduction programmes, which are associated with high satisfaction levels, particularly when provided voluntarily. Satisfaction levels tend to increase over time, concomitant with greater comprehension of course relevance, as students advance through their medical education [25].

The pedagogical circle seems to address the fundamental psychological needs outlined in self-determination theory by empowering students with a voice, promoting horizontal communication with instructors, and engendering a shared sense of belonging. These factors not only facilitate stress reduction but also augment intrinsic motivation and profound learning. Moreover, by advancing mutual empathy and emotional regulation, the circle bolsters the development of emotional intelligence – an indispensable skill in healthcare professions. Pedagogical circles could likewise be recommended for inclusion in national curricula to mitigate burnout.

Our findings suggest that students perceived the pedagogical circle methodology as conferring added value. Elements including ‘Do you feel supported by this methodology?‘, ‘Does it contribute to creating a respectful group dynamic that allows you to develop in a serene environment?‘, ‘Does it help you feel comfortable in the group and able to express yourself?‘, and ‘Does the pedagogical circle facilitate the smooth progression of practical sessions?’ emerged as direct outcomes of this approach on the learning environment.

These items appear closely aligned with non-violent communication techniques, which have previously demonstrated their relevance in health education contexts [15, 29, 30]. The empathic approach thus appears to exert a comparably positive impact on both student learning and well-being. Moreover, stress scores indicated that ‘feeling comfortable within the group and being able to express oneself’ and ‘feeling supported in one’s learning’ exerted direct and beneficial effects on student stress levels [31–33].

Nevertheless, our analysis of student stress disclosed disparities relative to literature findings [2, 26]. Students in our faculty appeared generally less stressed. This may be partly attributable to the early integration of the pedagogical circle at the curriculum’s outset. Small class sizes may also have contributed to these results, alongside potential bias stemming from the ‘new faculty’ effect. Furthermore, practical sessions were delivered in small groups (two groups of 14 students), with especial emphasis on student well-being, potentially introducing further bias. Whilst the pedagogical circle approach appears to offer tangible benefits, prior studies indicate that its optimal group size for efficacy is approximately 12 participants [7]. Indeed, learning in larger pre-clinical groups may constitute a substantial disadvantage for students.

Study limitation

Sample size

The sample was relatively small (n = 56), encompassing all undergraduate dental students at the University of Tours engaged in pre-clinical teaching, thereby obviating selection bias. Nonetheless, this constrains the study’s statistical power. Future research should replicate the study annually to enlarge the sample and facilitate longitudinal analysis of stress evolution and the pedagogical circle’s impact. Supplementary studies on metacognition and metamemory could further assess effects on learning processes.

Temporal representativeness

The study was performed during the final practical session, potentially introducing a ‘last-session relief’ bias that might affect responses. Whilst questionnaire anonymity probably mitigated this, future designs should include multiple time points to bolster temporal validity.

Generalizability

Findings are specific to the Faculty of Odontology at the University of Tours and may be affected by the ‘new faculty’ effect, potentially engendering enthusiasm and contributing to reduced stress levels. External validity is yet to be established owing to the localised context. Replication across diverse settings over time is recommended to evaluate broader applicability.

Instrument validity

As a pilot study, the questionnaire was locally adapted from existing instruments without formal validation. Developing and validating a bespoke instrument centred on stress and metacognition would fortify future investigations.

Absence of control group

The absence of a control group impedes causal inferences. Comparative studies involving matched cohorts from other institutions or alternative pedagogical approaches would yield valuable insights.

Conclusion

The pedagogical circle appears to positively influence student stress and learning by furnishing a supportive and secure environment for development in procedural dental education. Notwithstanding the limitations of this pilot study (small sample size and localised context), the findings intimate that the pedagogical circle can confer substantial educational benefits when incorporated into undergraduate dental curricula. Future studies should corroborate these results and scrutinise its long-term impact, particularly in larger cohorts. Moreover, multicentre trials integrating digital tools could validate this innovative method on a global scale.
