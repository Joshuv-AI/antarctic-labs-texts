Comparative efficacy and safety of PSCA CAR-engineered Vδ1 γδ T cells for immunotherapy of pancreatic cancer
Authors: Zhixin Li, Ningyuan Liu, Zahir Shah, Lewei Jin, Lei Tian, Xiaolei Sun, Jianying Zhang, Zhiyao Li, Michael A Caligiuri, Jianhua Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundγδ T cells possess unique immunological features including tissue tropism, major histocompatibility complex-independent antigen recognition, and hybrid T/natural killer cell properties that make them promising candidates for cancer immunotherapy. However, the therapeutic potential of Vδ1 γδ T cells, particularly when engineered with chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), remains underexplored in solid tumors such as pancreatic cancer (PC), largely due to their low abundance in peripheral blood and challenges in ex vivo expansion. This study aims to directly compare the preclinical safety and efficacy among CAR-engineered Vδ1 γδ T cells, Vδ2 γδ T cells, and conventional αβ T cells.MethodsWe developed a CAR targeting prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) and engineered it into human blood-derived Vδ1 γδ T cells. These PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells were evaluated for antitumor activity against PSCA-expressing pancreatic tumor cells using both in vitro cytotoxicity assays and in vivo xenograft mouse models. Comparative analyses were conducted using PSCA CAR-Vδ1 γδ T cells, PSCA CAR-Vδ2 γδ T cells, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cells. Safety assessments of these CAR-engineered T cell subsets were conducted to evaluate graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) and cytokine release syndrome (CRS), while exhaustion profiles were assessed using single-cell RNA sequencing.ResultsOur study demonstrated that PSCA CAR-Vδ1 γδ T cells, PSCA CAR-Vδ2 γδ T cells, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cells exhibited similar antitumor efficacy. However, PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells did not exhibit GvHD or CRS compared with CAR-αβ T cells. PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells also had lower scores for exhaustion when compared with PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cells by single-cell transcriptomic analysis.ConclusionPSCA CAR-Vδ1 γδ T cells represent a potent and safe therapeutic modality for PSCA+ PC, with efficacy comparable to other CAR T cell types and potential advantages in safety and persistence. These findings support further development of CAR-Vδ1 T cell immunotherapy for solid tumors.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) currently ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the Western world, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 10%.1 By 2030, PC is projected to surpass breast, prostate, and colorectal cancers to become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths after lung cancer.2 Nearly 90% of pancreatic malignancies are classified as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas, which are typically diagnosed at an advanced, incurable stage due to the absence of effective screening methods and the scarcity of specific early symptoms. Since 1997, gemcitabine has been an option for treatment in locally advanced and metastatic PC.3 However, it has only a modest effect on survival, as gemcitabine chemoresistance can develop within a few weeks of treatment in initially chemosensitive tumors.4 Moreover, among other treatment modalities, PC has been largely unresponsive to immune checkpoint blockade therapy, unless it carries the rare alterations in mismatch repair.5 Effective treatment options are thus still limited for PC, and as such, there is an urgent need for innovative and safe treatment modalities for advanced PC.

γδ T cells constitute a minor subset of T lymphocytes that exhibit characteristics of both adaptive and innate immunity. Their subsets are distinguished by the δ chain of their T cell receptor (TCR), with Vδ1-expressing γδ T cells predominantly residing in mucosal tissues, while Vδ2-expressing cells are mainly found in the peripheral blood.6 7 Unlike conventional αβ T cells, which recognize peptide antigens presented by polymorphic major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II molecules, γδ T cells recognize invariant antigens in an MHC-independent manner. This unique recognition mechanism positions γδ T cells as promising candidates for the development of allogeneic, off-the-shelf cell-based immunotherapies for cancer. Furthermore, tumor-infiltrating γδ T cells are positively associated with patient survival across diverse cancers.8 9 Early clinical investigations of γδ T cells have shown antitumor effects on hematologic cancers as well as solid tumors with limited adverse effects in vivo.1012 In 2007, Kobayashi et al, reported the first pilot clinical study of in vitro expanded autologous γδ T cell therapy for advanced renal cell carcinoma; however, all patients (7 out of 7) eventually showed progressive disease.13 Other early clinical studies experienced a high rate of progressive disease and inconsistent results, demonstrating a need to increase γδ T cell antitumor activity.1417 Given the success of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy using conventional αβ T cell therapy in the clinic for hematologic malignancies,18 19 investigators have co-opted this strategy to engineer and test CAR γδ T cell therapies in both liquid and solid tumors.2024 While a few γδ T cell-related immunotherapies without a CAR have been reported as effective for PC treatment,25 26 the use and therapeutic effects of CAR γδ T cells especially Vδ1 γδ T cells (hereafter referred to as Vδ1 T cells) for PC have not been investigated.

The clinical application of γδ T cells has been somewhat limited to the predominant circulating Vδ2 γδ T subset (hereafter referred to as Vδ2 T cells). This is in part due to the discovery that soluble phosphoantigens (eg, isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP), (E)-4-hydroxy-3-methyl-but-2-enyl pyrophosphate (HMBPP,) or bromohydrin pyrophosphate (BrHPP))2729 or phosphoantigen-inducing aminobisphosphonates (eg, zoledronic acid) can be used to expand Vδ2 T cells to high numbers with excellent purity in vitro,30 31 which accelerated the clinical translation of Vδ2 T cells. Although no Vδ1 TCR cognate ligand has been reported for Vδ1 T cell specific expansion, two clinical-grade protocols for Vδ1 cell in vitro expansion reported by Almeida32 and Ferry33 may provide the opportunity to accelerate clinical development of Vδ1 T cell-based immunotherapy. Studies have shown that Vδ1 T cells are less prone to exhaustion compared with their Vδ2 T counterparts, which might provide a therapy with more persistence.34 35 Furthermore, even within the milieu of exhaustion, intratumoral Vδ2− γδ T cells (ie, mainly Vδ1 T cells) in human kidney cancer that exhibit markers of terminal T cell exhaustion have been found to still maintain cytotoxic function.36 37 This suggests that such cells may provide a more effective cancer immunotherapeutic option compared with their Vδ2 T counterparts.

Prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored cell surface protein that plays a role in intracellular signaling and the proliferation of prostate tumors.38 Aberrant overexpression of PSCA has been observed in 60-80% of PC tumors,39 40 while its expression remains limited in normal tissues.41 Thus, PSCA is a potential target for PSCA CAR immunotherapy, and its safety and efficacy have been evaluated recently with CAR-T cells, CAR-natural killer (NK) cells, and CAR-macrophages.4244 To explore the tumor reactivity and safety profile of Vδ1 T cells, we developed human PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells harboring a truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (tEGFR) as a safety switch43 and evaluated the product in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we have conducted a parallel comparison of Vδ1, Vδ2, and conventional αβ T cells enigneered with PSCA CAR against PC. Our PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells show potent CAR-specific antitumor activity against PC both in vitro and in vivo, leading to prominent tumor regression and prolonged survival without obvious graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) or systemic toxicity. Our preclinical results suggest that PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell-based cellular therapy may present an attractive option for clinical development against PC.

Results

Generation and immunophenotype of peripheral blood-derived Vδ1 T cells

Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells and αβ T cells were isolated, activated and expanded from healthy donors’ peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as described in Methods (figure 1A). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that during 14 days of in vitro culture, both Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells were successfully enriched (online supplemental figure 1A). Our Vδ1 T cell enrichment and expansion protocol resulted in a significant increase in Vδ1 T cells, from 0.4% to 9.1% of PBMCs after 21 days of enrichment and expansion (online supplemental figure 1B), representing a more than 1500-fold increase in the absolute number of Vδ1 T cells (figure 1B and C). Compared with pre-expanded Vδ2 and αβ T cells, pre-expanded Vδ1 T cells exhibited lower activation and effector memory profiles but a higher proportion of effector T cell phenotypes (figure 1D). On expansion, Vδ1 T cells demonstrated markedly elevated levels of NK cell-activating receptors such as NKG2D45 and CD25,45 the checkpoint receptor programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) with effector response,37 and a pronounced effector memory phenotype surpassing those of expanded Vδ2 and αβ T cells (figure 1D and E, online supplemental figure 1C). These findings indicate a robust activation and memory profile of Vδ1 T cells following in vitro expansion.

Next, we compared the phenotype of expanded Vδ1 T cells to expanded Vδ2 T cells and expanded conventional αβ T cells. Although expanded Vδ2 T cells were also activated, they expressed relatively higher levels of inhibitory receptors LAG-3 and NKG2A, suggesting a more suppressive or exhausted phenotype. αβ T cells, by comparison, generally exhibited lower expression of NK cell-associated markers and distinct memory subset distributions, highlighting the functional plasticity of γδ T cells (figure 1D and E). The surface density of additional markers including CD16, NKp44, DNAM-1, NKG2A, CTLA-4, TIM-3, TIGIT, and CD69 was broadly comparable between Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells, though subject to interdonor variability (figure 1E).

Collectively, these results support a more balanced immunophenotype for Vδ1 T cells, characterized by strong expression of antigens signifying cytotoxic potential (eg, NKG2D), antigens regulating immune checkpoint (eg, PD-1), and retention of memory subsets; features consistent with a versatile, tumor-reactive, and potentially checkpoint-responsive profile. Notably, expression patterns of such antigens remain consistent across donors, underscoring a reproducible trend despite inter-individual variation.

PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells exhibit CAR-specific serial lysis of pancreatic cancer cells in vitro

To generate CAR-Vδ1 T cells, enriched Vδ1 T cells (≥95% purity) were transduced with a retrovirus-based PSCA CAR construct (figure 2A), previously validated for its PSCA-specific antitumor efficacy in CAR-NK cells and CAR-macrophages.43 46 To assess the PSCA CAR-dependent cytotoxicity against pancreatic cancer (PC) cells in vitro, we selected PSCA-high expressing (PSCAhigh) tumor cells (Capan-1 and MIA PaCa-2) and PSCA-low expressing (PSCAlow) tumor cells (PANC-1) for functional validation (figure 2B). Prior to co-culture with PC cells, the purity of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell exceeded 95% and CAR expression. as determined by expression of the tEGFR marker, ranged from 40-60% across all donors (figure 2C and D, online supplemental figure 2A). Subsequently, both PSCAhigh and PSCAlow PC cells were cocultured for 24 hours with PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells or mock-Vδ1 T cells (mock consisted of the transduced tEGFR without the PSCA CAR) (figure 2E). The PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity compared with mock-transduced Vδ1 T cells against PSCAhigh Capan-1 and PSCAhigh MIA PaCa-2 tumor cells, but not against PSCAlow PANC-1 tumor cells (figure 2F), demonstrating PSCA CAR-dependent killing of PSCA-positive tumor cells. Of note, both fresh and cryopreserved-thawed PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells demonstrated comparable cytolytic potency against Capan-1 cells, highlighting their potential as an effective off-the-shelf therapeutic option (online supplemental figure 2B and C).

Having established the CAR-specific antitumor functionality of activated PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells, we proceeded to assess their capability for serial killing of tumor cells using the real-time cytotoxicity assay (RTCA).4749 Activated PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells underwent repeated exposure to target cells in a serial killing assay with the initial effector/target (E:T) ratio of 1:2, where Capan-1 target cells, but not effector cells, were replenished on two additional occasions within 7 days (online supplemental figure 2D) while the tumor cell growth index was measured as previously described.50 Results measured over time provide evidence of serial tumor killing by PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells in vitro (online supplemental figure 2D). The enhanced cytolytic function of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells was further confirmed by their elevated release of granzyme B (GZMB) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) that were significantly higher when cocultured with PSCAhigh tumor cells, compared with mock transduced Vδ1 T cells (online supplemental figure 2E). To further explore the key mediators of this cytotoxic property, we blocked NKG2D, DNAM-1, or NKp30 on PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells but did not observe a decrease in cytotoxicity of PSCAhigh tumor cells, underlining the dominant role of the PSCA CAR in the antitumor activity of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells (online supplemental figure 2F). In summary, the CAR engineering results in enhanced cytotoxicity of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells against PSCA-expressing PC tumor cells, perhaps mediated at least in part by GZMB and IFN-γ secretion (online supplemental figure 2G). PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells also demonstrate promising serial killing capacity against PSCAhigh Capan-1 cells. Comparable antitumor efficacy between fresh and cryopreserved PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells highlighted the PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells as a promising candidate for off-the-shelf immunotherapy.

Next, we compared functional cytotoxicity of expanded un-engineered and PSCA CAR-engineered Vδ1, Vδ2, and conventional αβ T cells in vitro (figure 2 and online supplemental figure 3). Non-transduced and CAR-transduced Vδ1, Vδ2, and αβ T cells were co-cultured with Capan-1 cells (online supplemental figure 3E). Cytotoxicity assays revealed no significant difference in capacity for tumor-killing among the three T cell subsets, with or without CAR expression (figure 2G and H, online supplemental figure 3F and G). We also saw no significant differences in the IFN-γ expression among these groups. However, CAR-Vδ1 and CAR-Vδ2 T cells exhibited notably higher levels of GZMB compared with CAR-αβ T cells (figure 2I). These data indicate that Vδ1 T cells exhibit antigen-specific cytotoxic activity at least comparable to that of Vδ2 and αβ T cells.

PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells display persistent tumor control and tumor infiltration in vivo

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells in vivo, we established a metastatic PC xenograft model in NSG mice. Cryopreserved PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells were thawed and infused as shown in figure 3A. PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells showed superior therapeutic benefit compared with the saline and mock-cryopreserved Vδ1 T cell groups by inhibiting the progression of metastatic PC in a dose-dependent manner as determined by monitoring bioluminescence imaging (BLI) in live animals over time and quantification of the BLI (figure 3B and C). Immunohistochemical staining revealed negligible PSCA protein expression in the liver, lung, pancreas, and spleen following treatment with PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells, suggesting effective tumor cell eradication (online supplemental figure 3A). To confirm that the absence of PSCA after PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell treatment represented tumor eradication rather than antigen loss, we stained the same samples with MUC1, a PC marker, and it showed a similar staining profile like that of PSCA (online supplemental figure 3B). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining also demonstrated no obvious damage to normal tissues after PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell treatment (online supplemental figure 3C). Furthermore, administration of a high dose (6×10⁶ PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells per infusion, delivered every other week for a total of three infusions, with each dose divided between intraperitoneal (i.p.) and intravenous routes for experimental and control groups) significantly extended the survival of treated mice compared with those receiving a low dose of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells (3×10⁶ PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells per infusion) or mock-transduced Vδ1 T cells, or saline (figure 3D).

PC is characterized by dense fibrotic stroma, or desmoplasia, which presents a major barrier to effective infiltration of adoptive cell therapies into solid tumors. To further understand the mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell therapy, including tissue-specific effects, we established an orthotopic mouse model to mimic the natural microenvironment and tumor growth characteristics of cancer within the pancreas. We implanted the PC cell line Capan-1_ Luc_ZsGreen into the pancreas of NSG mice and intravenously infused a relatively low dose of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells to treat the cancer and used mock transduced Vδ1 T cells as a control (figure 3E). Ten days after Vδ1 T cell infusion, mice were humanely sacrificed for evidence of infiltration into normal pancreatic tissues and PC tissues (figure 3E). Our analysis demonstrated that intravenously injected PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells can effectively infiltrate into the pancreas and tumor, as evidenced by flow cytometric analysis, while mock transduced Vδ1 T cells did not (figure 3F and G), again highlighting the importance of the PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells.

In vivo benchmarking of Vδ1, Vδ2, and conventional αβ T cells with PSCA CAR reveals comparable tumor control in pancreatic cancer

Despite several clinical trials exploring Vδ1 and/or Vδ2 cells and their CAR-engineered derivatives for cancer therapy (NCT05001451, NCT04735471, NCT03533816, and NCT04107142), no preclinical head-to-head comparison of safety and antitumor efficacy among Vδ1, Vδ2, and conventional αβ T cells has been conducted. To address this gap, we performed a preclinical comparison of Vδ1, Vδ2, and αβ T cells with and without PSCA CAR transduction using two distinct solid tumor models (figure 4A and E). We first assessed the antitumor efficacy of non-transduced Vδ1, Vδ2, and αβ T cells in a subcutaneous (s.c.) NSG mouse model implanted with Capan-1_Luc_ZsGreen PC cells (figure 4A). Following two intravenous infusions of each T cell type, Vδ1 T cell-treated mice exhibited a favorable, though not statistically significant, trend toward tumor growth suppression relative to saline-, Vδ2-, and αβ T cell-treated groups (figure 4B–D).

Subsequently, we evaluated the in vivo antitumor activity of PSCA CAR-Vδ1, PSCA CAR-Vδ2, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cells in a metastatic PC model established via intraperitoneal injection of Capan-1 cells (figure 4E). A single administration of these CAR-engineered T cells (via both intravenous and i.p. routes) significantly controlled tumor growth and delayed disease progression compared with saline controls (figure 4F–H), resulting in durable remission and, in some cases, complete regression with no discernible differences in efficacy among the three CAR-T cell types (figure 4F1). These findings were corroborated by our in vitro cytotoxicity assays (figure 2G and H; Online supplemental Figure 3F and G).

Overall, PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells demonstrated robust and comparable cytotoxic activity both in vitro and in vivo, relative to CAR-Vδ2 and CAR-αβ T cells, within our PC models.

Single-cell transcriptional profiling of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 and Vδ2 T cells reveals distinct profiles

We next performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to profile PSCA CAR-Vδ1, PSCA CAR-Vδ2, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cells following adoptive transfer (hereafter referred to as in vivo). An orthotopic model of PC was established in NSG mice by implanting Capan-1 PC cells into the pancreas. Seven days after intravenous and i.p. injection of PSCA CAR-Vδ1, PSCA CAR-Vδ2, or PSCA CAR-αβ T cells, each T cell subset was harvested for scRNA-seq analysis. The corresponding preinfusion PSCA CAR-Vδ1, PSCA CAR-Vδ2, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cell products served as in vitro controls. To minimize intersample variability and enable unbiased group comparisons, each sample was pooled from two independent tumor-bearing mice (figure 5A). Data visualization using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP),51 combined with unsupervised clustering, identified three principal cell types: PSCA CAR-Vδ1, PSCA CAR-Vδ2, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cells (figure 5B). Different T cell subsets (Vδ1, Vδ2, and αβ T cells) were recognized based on their TCR gene expression (figure 5C). Functional cluster analysis of PSCA CAR-Vδ1, PSCA CAR-Vδ2, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cells revealed four previously characterized cellular states: (1) cytolytic/effector, (2) exhausted, (3) naïve, and (4) proliferative, each differentially varied among these CAR-engineered T cell subsets (figure 5D and E).52 Further analysis of the PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells found that the cytotoxic/effector cluster defined by cytotoxic genes including CCL4, KLRB1, GZMK, NKG7, CST7, GZMA, HOPX, PRF1 and GNLY underwent a 2.2-fold decrease in expression after adoptive transfer (36.2% in vitro to 16.9% in vivo), possibly resulting from T cell dysfunction that may be induced by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Although the in vitro PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cell populations had a higher fraction of cytolytic/effector cells (60.4%) when compared with the in vitro PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells (36.2%), the PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cell population underwent a much more dramatic (8.8-fold) decrease of cytotoxic/effector cluster after adoptive transfer (60.4% in vitro to 7.4% in vivo) (figure 5F). The fraction within the naïve cluster increases when transitioning from in vitro to in vivo for both PSCA CAR-Vδ1 and PSCA CAR-Vδ2 subsets of T cells, yet decreases dramatically in PSCA CAR-αβ T cells as substantially more cells show a proliferative profile in vivo.52 In vivo, both PSCA CAR-αβ T cells (62.8%), and PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells have a much greater fraction of cells in the proliferative cluster (62.8% and 60.1%, respectively), when compared with the PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cells (∼23.7%) (figure 5E and F). These proliferative and phenotypic distinctions underscore the inherent resilience of the PSCA CAR-Vδ1 subset when compared with PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cells, but also the comparable robustness of PSCA CAR-αβ T cells. We examined the exhaustion signature (TIGIT, PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2, and LAG3) following in vivo exposure to tumor. While PSCA CAR-αβ T cells exhibit the lowest exhaustion score among the three groups, PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells exhibited significantly lower exhaustion score compared with PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cells (figure 5G and H).

Allogeneic PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell therapy is free of graft versus host disease and cytokine release syndrome

Graft-versus-host (GvH) responses represent a primary safety concern in allogeneic T cell therapies. However, γδ T cells are not reactive to mismatched HLA molecules or protein alloantigens and thus are not expected to elicit GvH responses. To assess the safety profile of our PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells, we conducted in vivo studies using tumor-bearing NSG mice (figure 6A). No consistent differences in body temperature were observed among the saline, PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell, and PSCA CAR-αβ T cell groups (figure 6B). Systemic GvHD was evaluated using a scoring system encompassing five clinical parameters: weight loss, posture (hunching), activity level, fur texture, and skin integrity.53 Injection of PSCA CAR-αβ T cells resulted in sustained losses in body weight that were not seen in the mice treated with PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells or in the saline-treated controls (figure 6C). Consistent with this, tumor-bearing mice treated with PSCA CAR-αβ T cells experienced obvious GvHD compared with mice treated with CAR-Vδ1 T cells or saline as evidenced by a significantly higher GvHD score (figure 6D). Indeed, PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T-treated mice maintained a significant survival benefit compared with the mice treated with PSCA CAR-αβ T cells who died of GvHD. Likewise, saline-treated mice died of tumor progression (figure 6E).

To assess whether mice treated with PSCA CAR-αβ T cells suffered inflammatory damage in conventional GvHD target tissues, we performed a histopathological analysis of the colon, liver, and small intestine at the end of the experiment. We found that mice receiving PSCA CAR-αβ T cells had obvious crypt loss in colonic tissues, inflammatory lymphocyte infiltration in the liver, and apoptotic bodies observed in the small intestine. In contrast, we did not observe such pathological changes following either PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cell treatment or saline treatment (figure 6F). Additionally, we confirmed that mice treated with Vδ1 T cells in the absence of the PSCA CAR were without evidence of acute GvHD (online supplemental figures 5A–C), indicating the PSCA CAR engineering does not change the safety profiles. Collectively, treatment of mice with human PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells did not result in an increased risk of GvHD in tumor-bearing NSG mice, compared with mice treated with PSCA CAR-αβ T cells.

Next, we examined the differences in cytokine release syndrome (CRS) triggered by infusion of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T versus PSCA CAR-αβ T cells. Using donor-matched PBMC humanized NSG mice (online supplemental figure 5D), we observed significant fever among mice treated with PSCA CAR-αβ T cells within 48 hours after infusion, coupled with significantly higher mouse Serum Amyloid A3 and CRS-related IL-6 cytokine release compared with mice receiving PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells (online supplemental figure 5E,G and H). Mice treated with either PSCA CAR-αβ T cells or PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells showed a similar body weight change curve (online supplemental figure 5F). Collectively, these results suggest that PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells are less likely to induce GvHD and severe CRS, highlighting their promising potential for safe allogeneic applications compared with their conventional PSCA CAR-αβ T cell counterparts.

Discussion

The development of allogeneic off-the-shelf cell therapies is at the forefront of the rapidly advancing field of cancer immunotherapy. Currently, four major categories of allogeneic cell products are under investigation: γδ T cells,54 engineered conventional αβ T cells derived from healthy donors,55 NK cells,43 56 or invariant NKT (iNKT) cells.5759 In the current study, we report the generation and functional characterization of allogeneic PSCA CAR-engineered Vδ1 T cells as a novel approach for managing pancreatic cancer (PC). These off-the-shelf PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells exhibit potent cytotoxicity against PC cells in vitro and eradicate established metastatic PC in vivo, while demonstrating a lower risk of GvHD and CRS compared with PSCA CAR-αβ T cells, and reduced exhaustion relative to PSCA CAR-Vδ2 T cells. This preclinical investigation introduces an allogeneic, off-the-shelf cell therapy candidate for PC with potentially minimal treatment-related toxicity and provides foundational insights to guide the continued advancement of T cell-based immunotherapies for solid tumors.

Conventional cellular immunotherapy with αβ T cells carries a risk of GvHD in allogeneic settings due to HLA incompatibility. To mitigate this, endogenous TCR expression must be ablated through gene editing.60 While this approach enables allogeneic application, it also increases manufacturing complexity.6163 Additionally, despite their robust in vivo clonal expansion, αβ T cells exhibit limited antitumor efficacy in solid tumors due to poor tissue infiltration. As an alternative, NK cell-based therapies pose a lower risk of GvHD, but their limited in vivo expansion and persistence remain key challenges in improving solid tumor reactivity and therapeutic potential.64 Similarly, iNKT cells may offer a promising platform for allogeneic therapy, as they are potent immune effector cells with low GvHD risk and the ability to target tumors with low immunogenicity. Preclinical studies using in vitro-derived iNKT cells from hematopoietic stem cells have demonstrated both strong tumor cytotoxicity and a favorable safety profile.57 58

Engineering innate type γδ T cells that have potent antitumor capacity while having low to no risk for GvHD represents an exciting direction in the development of allogeneic cell therapy for cancer, especially for solid tumors. Although both Vδ1 and Vδ2 T-cell-based cancer immunotherapies have attracted attention,236568 the differences in antitumor potency between these two distinct γδ T cell subsets have not been extensively explored and remain largely unknown, especially in the context of CAR T cell therapies of solid tumors. While our parallel comparative study provides direct evidence that PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells and PSCA CAR Vδ2 T cells have antitumor efficacy against PC comparable to that of conventional PSCA CAR αβ T cells, further investigation is necessary to fully determine which subset may prove superior and practical for therapeutic applications in clinic. Such work includes additional preclinical and clinical comparisons between PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells and PSCA CAR Vδ2 T cells in the setting of PC and other PSCA+ solid tumors that will provide important additional insights into the field of γδ T cell-based allogeneic immunotherapy.

Unlike Vδ2 T cells, which recognize butyrophilin complex 3A1 through a unique mechanism involving intracellular phosphoantigen sensing,69 the activation mechanism of Vδ1 T cells remains incompletely understood. Members of the CD1 family of MHC-like proteins specifically the lipid-presenting molecules CD1c and CD1d have been identified as ligands for certain Vδ1 TCRs; however, their capacity to elicit Vδ1 T cell responses in vivo is still uncertain.7072 Ongoing ligand discovery and characterization efforts aim to define the specific triggers of clonally expanded Vδ1 T cells, which could further enable their clinical application. In addition to the TCR, PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells also express innate NK cell receptors. For instance, activation of NKG2D by MICA/B and ULBP ligands promotes GZMB and perforin release,73 74 while DNAM-1 mediates cytotoxicity through recognition of CD155 and CD112, which are broadly expressed on both hematologic and solid tumors.75 However, the data presented in this report indicate that their contribution, relative to that of the PSCA CAR, is minimal at best.

In summary, we performed a head-on preclinical comparison of allogeneic PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells and PSCA CAR Vδ2 T cells and PSCA CAR αβ T cells in the context of PC, a devastating malignancy that has not had significant therapeutic advances in 40 years. Given the metastatic characteristics of the Capan-1 cell line used in our in vivo model, we adopted the combined intravenous and i.p. infusion strategy. The rationale for using both intravenous and i.p. routes for effector T cell and mock T cell infusion is based on our previously published study where we demonstrated that intravenously injected NK cells primarily accumulated in the liver and lungs, whereas i.p.-injected NK cells localized mainly to the liver and pancreas.43 Notably, we also employed this dual-route approach for PSCA CAR-macrophage therapy in a recent publication, which showed promising in vivo tumor-suppressive effects.46 Based on this collective evidence, we hypothesize that the intravenous and i.p. combination may similarly enhance the antitumor efficacy of γδ T cells. We found that while all three T cell types showed equal and rigorous antitumor activity against PC in vivo, PSCA CAR Vδ1 T cells appeared to be less toxic compared with PSCA CAR αβ T cells as measured by GvHD and CRS, and possibly more robust compared with PSCA CAR Vδ2 T cells as measured by scRNA-seq, although this must be validated in vivo in future studies. While significant progress has been made in expanding Vδ1 T cells, achieving reliable and scalable production of CAR-Vδ1 T cells in clinically relevant quantities continues to be a challenge. Exploring advanced enrichment methods and innovative approaches, such as the generation of iPSC-derived CAR-Vδ1 T cells, could provide critical insights and address current limitations, paving the way for more efficient and universally applicable therapies. Addressing these challenges will be essential for translating this platform into a robust and scalable clinical solution for cancer immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The human PC cell lines Capan-1, MIA PaCa-2, and PANC-1 were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). No additional authentication was performed, as all cell lines were sourced directly from ATCC. Capan-1 cells stably expressing the luciferase_ZsGreen reporter gene were generated via lentiviral transduction with a vector encoding Luc_ZsGreen. Early-passage cells were cryopreserved, and prior to experiments, they were thawed and passaged two to three times to achieve viability greater than 95%. All cell lines were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Plus Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza).

DNA constructs and retrovirus production

The CAR targeting PSCA was cloned into a clinical-grade retroviral replicating vector (RRV), from which non-human DNA sequences were minimized, as previously described.43 The PSCA CAR was designed to sequentially include a signal peptide, anti-PSCA single-chain fragment variable (scFv), hinge region, transmembrane domain, CD28, and CD3z. The anti-PSCA scFv-hinge-CD28-CD3z-T2A construct was inserted into the RRV at the NotI/SalI restriction sites. A tEGFR, serving both as a traceable marker and in vivo suicide switch, was linked in-frame to the PSCA CAR via a porcine teschovirus-1 2A (P2A)-like self-cleaving peptide43 and cloned into the RRV at the SalI/MluI site. Control plasmids expressing only tEGFR were generated by PCR amplification of the relevant sequence from the PSCA CAR-P2A-tEGFR construct and cloned into the retroviral vector at the NotI/MluI sites. Retrovirus expressing PSCA CAR-P2A-tEGFR was produced by cotransfecting the transfer plasmid with plasmid pRD114-TR, which encodes the envelope glycoprotein, into GP2-293T cells (Takara, San Francisco, California, USA) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, Carlsbad, California, USA). Supernatants were collected 48 hours post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane, and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen for future use.

T cell isolation, expansion, and transduction

PBMCs were isolated from whole blood leukocyte cones using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva) density gradient centrifugation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Freshly isolated PBMCs were either cryopreserved in 90% FBS with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for future use or resuspended at a density of 4×10⁶ cells/mL in Vδ1 T cell expansion medium. This medium consisted of Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (v/v), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (NEAA), 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME), 2.5 µg/mL Plasmocin Prophylactic, 1 µg/mL phytohemagglutinin (PHA), 1 µg/mL Concanavalin A, 100 IU/mL IL-2, 20 ng/mL IL-7, 10 ng/mL IL-15, 10 ng/mL IL-21, and 10 ng/mL IFN-γ.

An additional PBMC aliquot was resuspended in Vδ2 T cell expansion medium, composed of RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS (v/v), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% MEM NEAA, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 µM β-ME, 2.5 µg/mL Plasmocin Prophylactic, 200 IU/mL IL-2, and 5 µM zoledronic acid monohydrate, also at 4×10⁶ cells/mL. For αβ T cell expansion, PBMCs were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (v/v), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% MEM NEAA, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 µM β-ME, 2.5 µg/mL Plasmocin Prophylactic, and 200 IU/mL IL-2.

When cryopreserved PBMCs were used, they were thawed and rested overnight in prewarmed complete media at 4×10⁶ cells/mL prior to further processing to minimize lymphocyte stress. After 14–21 days of γδ T cell enrichment, Vδ1 and Vδ2 cells were sorted by flow cytometry based on the gating strategy hCD45+hCD3+TCR Vδ1+ or hCD45+hCD3+TCR Vδ2+, respectively. αβ T cells were isolated on the day of PBMC collection using hCD45+hCD3+TCR αβ+ gating. Sorted Vδ1 and Vδ2 γδ T cells were cultured at 1×10⁶ cells/mL in the optimized expansion medium described above. Irradiated K562 feeder cells were added weekly at a 1:100 ratio (γδ T:K562) to promote expansion. Culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline as needed. All cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Before PSCA CAR genetic modification, 1 million Vδ1 γδ, Vδ2 γδ, or αβ T cells were activated with 25 µL CD3/CD28 activator beads in 1 mL of culture medium for 48 hours. Cells were then transduced with either the PSCA retrovirus or the tEGFR control virus using Retronectin (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and expanded until use. Transduction efficiency was assessed by flow cytometric analysis of eGFR expression 48 hours post-transduction. Viable cells were counted by Trypan blue exclusion using a hemocytometer. Finally, transduced T cells were cryopreserved in 90% FBS+10% DMSO, and all functional assays were performed using thawed cells after recovery.

Annexin V/DAPI staining killing assay

To evaluate T cell–mediated tumor cell death, GFP+ Capan-1 tumor cells (5,000 cells per well) were co-cultured for 24 hours with either mock or PSCA CAR-engineered Vδ1, Vδ2, or αβ T effector cells at varying E:T ratios (1:2, 1:1, 2:1; n=3 donors). After co-culture, cells were stained with APC-conjugated Annexin V and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (BD Biosciences), and analyzed by flow cytometry. For intracellular cytokine staining, mock or PSCA CAR-engineered Vδ1, Vδ2, or αβ T effector cells were co-cultured with tumor cells in the presence of BD GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences) at 37°C for 4 hours. Following co-culture, cells underwent surface staining, fixation, and permeabilization using the Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences). Intracellular staining for GZMB and IFNγ was then performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

51Cr-based cell killing assay

Cytotoxicity assays were conducted as previously described. Target cells including Capan-1, MIA PaCa-2, and PANC-1 were labeled with ⁵¹Cr for 90 min at 37°C, then washed and co-incubated with effector cells (Vδ1 γδ cells, Vδ2 γδ cells, or αβ T cells) in 96-well V-bottom plates at varying E:T ratios for 24 hours at 37°C. Following incubation, supernatants were collected into scintillation vials for analysis. The standard formula of 100 × (cpmexperimental − cpmspontaneous) / (cpmmaximal − cpmspontaneous) was used to calculate percentages of specific lysis, which were displayed as the means of triplicate samples.

xCELLigence real-time cytotoxicity assay

The cytolytic activity of Vδ1 T cells was assessed using the xCELLigence RTCA Multiple Plates instrument (ACEA Biosciences), as previously described.4749 E-plates were calibrated with 100 µL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 10 min. Subsequently, 10,000 PSCA-expressing Capan-1 cells in 50 µL of DMEM with 10% FBS were seeded per well and allowed to adhere for 24 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Next, 5,000 CAR-expressing Vδ1 T cells were added at 1:2 E:T ratio in 50 µL Vδ1 T cell medium (RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (V/V), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin, 1% MEM NEAA, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 µM β-ME, 2.5 µg/mL Plasmocin Prophylactic, 1 µg/mL PHA, 1 µg /mL Concanavalin A, 100 IU/mL IL-2, 20 ng/mL IL-7, 10 ng/mL IL-15, 10 ng/mL IL-21, 10 ng/mL IFN-γ) per well to bring the final volume to 200 µL 24 hours after Capan-1 cell seeding. Tumor cell viability is measured as Tumor Cell Index (impedance per well),4749 and was recorded every 15 min during tumor cell growth. Once the Tumor Cell Index decreased nearing zero, indicating that adherent Capan-1 cells inducing impedance have been cleared by PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells, another 10,000 PSCA-expressing Capan-1 cells were added at two separate intervals over the following 7 days. Thus, by the end of the assay, a total of 30,000 tumor cells had been exposed to 5,000 activated PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells. Six different donors were assessed, and each condition was run in duplicate wells.

Pancreatic cancer mouse model

NOD.Cg-PrkdcSCIDIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NOD/SCID/IL-2Rγ−/−, NSG) mice were housed in the animal facilities at the City of Hope. Male and female mice aged 6-10 weeks (The Jackson Laboratory) were used for all experiments unless otherwise specified. On day 4, immunodeficient NSG mice were engrafted i.p. with 5×10⁵ Capan-1 cells expressing the luciferase_ZsGreen gene. Mice were then randomly assigned to vehicle or treatment groups. PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells or mock T cells (which are T cells transduced with the tEGFR without the PSCA CAR) were thawed from liquid nitrogen and administered via both i.p. and intravenous routes. For i.p. dosing, mice received either a high dose (4.5×10⁶ CAR+ Vδ1 T cells) or low dose (2.25×10⁶ CAR+ Vδ1 T cells); for intravenous dosing, high and low doses were 1.5×10⁶ and 0.75×10⁶ CAR+ Vδ1 T cells, respectively. A total of three doses of PSCA CAR-Vδ1 T cells, mock Vδ1 T cells, or vehicle (saline) were given on day 0, day 14, and day 31. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments, federal, state, and local guidelines with approval from the City of Hope Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Single-cell RNA sequencing library preparation

The scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell 5' Library & Gel Bead Kit v2 (Chromium Single Cell 5' Library & Gel Bead Kit v2, Cat# PN-1000265). Single cells were isolated from a cell suspension, ensuring they had suitable viability and concentration for single-cell capture. These cells were then captured and barcoded using the 10x Genomics Chromium Controller, where each cell was encapsulated into Gel Beads-in-Emulsion (GEMs) containing barcoded oligonucleotides. Within the GEMs, cell lysis and reverse transcription occurred, producing barcoded full-length complementary DNA (cDNA), which was subsequently purified and amplified via PCR. The amplified cDNA underwent library construction, including fragmentation, end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, and index PCR to add unique dual indices for multiplexing. Quality and quantity assessments of the cDNA and final libraries were performed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and a Qubit Fluorometer to ensure they met sequencing requirements. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform with a configuration of 151 bp paired-end reads and an additional 11 bp for sample indexing (151+11+11+151), providing comprehensive and high-quality data for downstream analysis.

Single-cell RNA-seq data analysis

Raw sequencing data from each sample were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the Cell Ranger count command, generating single-cell resolution expression data following the 10x Genomics protocol (https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-gene-expression/software/pipelines/latest/using/count). Downstream analysis was conducted using Scanpy.76 Cell quality was assessed based on two metrics: (1) the number of detected genes and total gene counts per cell, and (2) the proportion of mitochondrial and hemoglobin gene counts per cell. Cells were excluded if they had fewer than 300 detected genes, fewer than 300 or more than 7,000 total gene counts, mitochondrial UMI content exceeding 20%, or hemoglobin gene expression greater than 3%. Genes detected in fewer than four cells were also filtered out.

Subsequently, raw counts were normalized using Scanpy’s normalize_total function (with target_sum=1e4), followed by natural log transformation after adding a pseudocount of 1 to stabilize variance. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified within each sample using scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes with the parameter batch_key=“samples”. To account for unwanted variation, total counts and mitochondrial gene percentages were regressed out using scanpy.pp.regress_out, applied to the HVG expression matrix.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using scanpy.tl.pca, and the top 50 components were retained for further analysis. Batch effects were corrected using the Batch Balanced k-Nearest Neighbors (BBKNN) algorithm,76 which identifies each cell’s nearest neighbors within each batch rather than across the pooled dataset. BBKNN was executed via scanpy.external.pp.bbknn with parameters batch_key=“sample” and n_pcs=50. UMAP for dimensionality reduction was then applied using scanpy.tl.umap with default settings.

To identify Vδ subsets in the scRNA-seq dataset, we implemented the multimodal classifier hierarchical classification (MMoCHi) strategy,77 which is trained using events defined by thresholding the expression of user-specified marker genes. Specifically, TRDV1 and TRDV2 expression levels were used to annotate Vδ1 and Vδ2 subsets, respectively, thereby excluding contaminating cell populations.

GvHD model

GvHD severity was evaluated by monitoring body weight changes, a well-established indicator of systemic GvHD in this and other murine models.53 77 Transplanted mice were ear-punched for identification, and individual body weights were recorded on day 0 and weekly thereafter until analysis. A weight loss exceeding 10% was used as the threshold for moderate GvHD, based on prior reports identifying this level as indicative of significant disease. Systemic GvHD was further assessed using a clinical scoring system outlined in table 1, which incorporates five parameters: weight loss, posture (hunching), activity, fur texture, and skin integrity. At the time of analysis, mice from coded cages were scored from 0 to 2 for each parameter, and a clinical index was calculated by summing the scores (maximum possible score=10).

Time-lapse bioluminescence live animal imaging

Time-lapse BLI was performed using a Lago-X (Spectral Instruments Imaging) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The mice were anesthetized with 2-4% isoflurane and oxygen (1 L/min) in an imaging chamber. Immunohistochemistry and H&E assay

Tissues including pancreas, liver, lung, tumor, and spleen were harvested from experimental mice at the time of euthanasia, carefully trimmed of fat and surrounding connective tissue, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for up to 36 hours. Fixed samples were subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4-6 µm. Tissue sections were stained with H&E by the City of Hope Pathology Core in accordance with standard protocols. Stained slides were imaged at 20× and 40× magnifications using an Olympus BX51 upright microscope equipped with an Optronics Macrofire CCD camera (AU Optronics), and images were analyzed using Optronics PictureFrame software (AU Optronics).

Statistical analysis

For continuous endpoints assuming normal or symmetric distributions, data are presented as mean±SEM for each group. The Student’s t-test was used to compare two independent groups. The one-way analysis of variance model was used to compare three or more independent groups. For survival data, survival functions are estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All tests were two-sided, and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Holm’s procedure. A p value<0.05 was defined as statistically significant. GraphPad V.9.1.0 and SAS V.9.4 were used for statistical analysis in the current study. Flow cytometry, ELISA, and BLI are listed in the online supplemental Materials.
