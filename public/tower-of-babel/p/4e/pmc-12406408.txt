Effects of physical activity interventions on physical fitness in preschool children: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials and dose–response study
Authors: Yixin Liang, Yong Yang, Yuan Yuan, Lanzhi Chen, Tian Wang, Beibei Luo, Junyu Wang, Jie Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroudTo evaluate the effects of chronic physical activity (PA) interventions on physical fitness (PF) in preschool children and to determine the optimal weekly PA dose for improving specific fitness components through meta-analysis and dose–response analysis.MethodsLiterature searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from database inception to January 4, 2025. Eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involved healthy children aged 3–7 years, applied PA interventions lasting ≥ 4 weeks, and assessed at least one PF component, including muscular strength, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), flexibility, coordination, or balance. Meta-analyses used random-effects models to calculate standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Bayesian dose–response models with restricted cubic splines were used to explore non-linear associations.ResultsFourteen RCTs (n = 3376) were included. PA interventions significantly improved overall PF (SMD = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.22–0.38), as well as upper muscle strength (SMD = 0.30), lower muscle strength (SMD = 0.29), flexibility (SMD = 0.27), CRF (SMD = 0.29), coordination (SMD = 0.54), and static balance (SMD = 0.38). Dose–response analysis revealed optimal weekly doses of 270 min for overall PF, 260 min for lower muscle strength, 190 min for upper muscle strength and flexibility, 300 min for coordination, and 180 min for static balance. CRF improved linearly up to 380 min/week. Subgroup analyses indicated that session frequency, length, and total duration moderated intervention effects.ConclusionsPA interventions effectively improve physical fitness in preschool children. Optimal weekly doses range from 180 to 380 min, depending on the fitness component. Intervention effectiveness is further moderated by session frequency, duration, and total program length, highlighting the need for tailored PA strategies targeting specific physical fitness outcomes in early childhood. Backgroud

Physical fitness (PF) is generally defined as a set of health- and skill-related attributes that enable individuals to perform daily activities with vigor and reduce the risk of chronic disease. In preschool children, PF encompasses several key dimensions, including muscular strength, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), flexibility quality, coordination, and balance [1]. These early childhood years represent a critical window for establishing a foundation of healthy growth and motor development, as higher levels of PF during this period have been linked to better health trajectories, cognitive performance, and motor skill proficiency later in life [2]. However, increasing evidence suggests that the current PF levels of many preschool children are suboptimal, underscoring the need for targeted interventions and heightened public health attention [3].

Multiple factors shape PF in preschool-age populations, ranging from genetic predispositions to lifestyle behaviors. Among these, the 24-h movement framework—which encompasses physical activity (PA), sedentary behavior, and sleep—has recently gained prominence as an integrative lens through which to view children’s overall health [4]. Within this framework, PA stands out as one of the most modifiable environmental factors and, therefore, serves as a pivotal target for improving PF in early childhood. Recognizing the potential impact of PA, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that preschool children accumulate at least 180 min per day of physical activity, including moderate-to-vigorous intensity PA [5]. Such guidelines reflect the growing consensus that structured and habitual PA can help foster the multifaceted components of PF in this sensitive developmental stage.

An expanding body of literature has illustrated that PA interventions can produce meaningful improvements in preschool children’s PF [6]. Previous studies and meta-analyses have highlighted the beneficial effects of structured exercise on muscular strength, CRF, and motor skills in this demographic [7, 8]. Nonetheless, most extant research has focused on isolated outcomes, such as cardiorespiratory or musculoskeletal components, rather than evaluating PF comprehensively [9]. In addition, variations in exercise program design—such as session frequency, duration, and intensity—may critically influence the magnitude of observed benefits [10, 11], yet these dose-specific aspects have not been adequately assessed in existing reviews.

Given the advantages of systematically quantifying the dose–response relationship, the present study aims to fill these knowledge gaps by conducting a comprehensive meta-analysis and dose–response investigation of PA interventions among preschool children. Specifically, this research seeks to (1) consolidate evidence on the overall efficacy of PA in enhancing PF across multiple dimensions, and (2) determine the optimal weekly PA dose needed to achieve meaningful improvements in each fitness component. By addressing these objectives, the study endeavors to inform practitioners, policymakers, and researchers regarding more precise and effective exercise prescriptions for promoting robust PF development in early childhood.

Methods

This meta-analysis and systematic review strictly adheres to the 2020 edition of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12]. Supplementary 1 provides the PRISMA Checklist. Registration has been completed with the PROSPERO database, bearing the identifier CRD42025639632. Owing to its design as a meta-analysis, involving the aggregation and synthesis of existing published data, the study bypasses the necessity for direct ethical approval or consent forms.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Web of Science, systematically covering publications from the inception of each database to January 4, 2025. To ensure no relevant studies were overlooked, the reference lists of all included articles and the bibliographies of systematic reviews published within the last five years were meticulously reviewed. The screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts was independently and repeatedly performed by researchers (YY and LZC), with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third author (TW). The detailed search strategies for each database and platform have been comprehensively outlined in Supplementary 2.

Eligibility and study selection criteria

According to the principles of the “PICOS” inclusion criteria [13], the included studies comprised the following criteria: (1) participants were healthy (i.e., general population, including samples of children with overweight/obesity but not samples of children exclusively with a diagnosed medical condition) preschool children aged 3–7 years [14]; (2) the intervention involved any type of PA, included chronic PA interventions lasting ≥ 4 weeks to assess sustained effects on physical fitness [15]; (3) the comparators included non-intervention groups, or health education sessions; (4) outcomes reported included at least one physical fitness measure, such as flexibility quality, lower muscle strength; upper muscle strength; coordination; CRF; dynamic balance; static balance; (5) the study design was a RCT. Studies were excluded for the following reasons: (1) those reporting on the acute effects of exercise; (2) Participants were non-healthy preschool children, including those with diagnosed medical conditions, chronic illnesses, obesity, or elevated surgical risk; (3) multi-component lifestyle interventions that included exercise, such as combined nutritional and exercise interventions; (5) those not specifying the type of exercise or where the exercise dose could not be quantified; (6) studies where the mean and standard deviation of results were not obtainable or authors did not respond to requests for data.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (BBL and JYW) extracted relevant study detail, including basic study details (e.g., authors, titles, and publication years), study characteristics e.g., participant demographic), intervention specifics (PA session length, PA frequency, intervention duration), and outcome measures. The essential characteristics of are outlined in Supplementary 3. Change scores, standard deviations, and sample sizes were extracted to calculate effect sizes. Missing data were estimated using Cochrane Handbook methods [16], and authors were contacted up to four times over six weeks if necessary.

Where standard errors were reported, these were converted to standard deviations for consistency. Effect sizes for each included study were calculated based on the mean and scaled values of the differences between pre- and post- intervention scores in both the intervention and control groups. The formulas used to calculate these mean change and SD values were: ‘Meanchange = Meanpost − Meanpre’ and ‘SDchange = SQRT [(SDpre2 + SDpost2) − (2 × Corr × SDpre × SDpost)]’, with the correlation coefficient (Corr) set at 0.5 according to the Cochrane Handbook guidelines [17].

Outcome measures

This meta-analysis evaluated the effects of exercise interventions on preschool children’s physical fitness, focusing on the following key indicators: muscular fitness (assessing upper and lower muscle strength), CRF, flexibility quality, coordination, and balance (both dynamic and static). These indicators were selected to comprehensively capture the multidimensional aspects of physical fitness in preschool children. Detailed information on the specific tests used to assess each indicator is provided in Supplementary Table 3.2.

Data coding and management

In this study, PA interventions were defined as structured exercise programs designed to improve preschool children’s physical health through various forms of movement-based activities. Each intervention was categorized as “Physical Activity (PA)” to distinguish it from the “Control Group (CON)”, which consisted of children who did not participate in any structured exercise program.The dose of PA was quantified as the total weekly exercise time (minutes per week) [19], calculated using the following formula: PA Dose (min/week) = Duration per session (min) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} Frequency per week. For example, if a PA intervention involved 30 min of exercise per session and was conducted three times per week, the total PA dose would be 90 min per week. This approach provided a standardized method to quantify the amount of physical activity delivered across different studies, facilitating the evaluation of the relationship between PA dose and physical health outcomes in preschool children.

All coding and quantification processes were performed in accordance with the predefined definitions to ensure consistency and accuracy across the included studies.

Risk of bias and quality of evidence

The risk of bias was assessed according to the second version of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [20]. Three reviewers (YXL, YY, and JYW) assessed the study. The assessment items included randomized sequence generation, bias due to deviation from the intended intervention, incomplete data, bias in measurements, and selective bias in reporting results. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by the third author (LZC), who was responsible for re-examining controversial assessments and discussing with other reviewers to reach a consensus, ensuring the accuracy and consistency of the assessment results.

Data synthesis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software. Forest plots were generated for each outcome measure to assess the pooled effect sizes, with significance evaluated at p < 0.05. The pooled effect sizes were reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values interpreted as follows: < 25% (very low), 25– < 50% (low), 50– < 75% (moderate), and ≥ 75% (high). Random-effects models were applied in cases of significant heterogeneity (p ≤ 0.1), while fixed-effects models were used otherwise. Therefore, we chose the standardized mean difference (SMD) as the effect size for our study [21].

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential moderators of intervention effectiveness. Specifically, analyses were stratified by intervention duration (≥ 16 weeks vs. < 16 weeks), session length (≥ 40 min vs. < 40 min per session) [22], and intervention frequency (> 3 sessions per week vs. 3 sessions per week).

Following the traditional meta-analysis, dose–response analyses were performed for outcomes with statistically significant pooled effects to explore the relationship between intervention dose and effect size. These analyses were conducted using R software and the MBNMAdose and rjags packages. Weekly physical activity dose (minutes per week) was used as the dose metric.

Regarding the dose–response models, dose–response relationship plots were constructed to evaluate the non-linear association between PA dose and the effects on each outcome indicator. These plots provided a detailed visualization of the trends and potential dose-dependent effects of PA interventions on different outcomes.

To summarize the dose–response relationship between PA and preschool children’s physical health, we compared dose–response models (Emax, Exponential, Restricted cubic spline, and non-parametric) regarding fit indices, including the deviance information criterion (DIC), between-study standard deviation, the number of parameters in the models, and the residual values. Different doses of PA were meta-analyzed as variations of the same intervention, rather than independent and unrelated interventions, as detailed in Supplementary 5. The fit indices from each of the models are shown in Supplementary 6, with restricted cubic splines (RCS) demonstrating the best fit for our data. Ultimately, we opted for the random effect of RCS to evaluate the non-linear dose–response association, as detailed in Supplementary 7.

The RCS model was implemented within a Bayesian framework. We employed a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) model, utilizing three chains with 20,000 iterations each (the first 10,000 discarded as burn-in) and a thinning factor of 10. Through this model, we leveraged Beta coefficients derived from restricted cubic spline curves to estimate both the overall and varying effects of exercise doses on physical activity, identifying the exercise dose associated with the optimal effect. Additionally, we placed nodes at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the exercise dose to effectively visualize the results of our model fitting process [23].

In order to visualize our study data and model-fitting results, deviance plots were used to confirm the robustness of our model selection Supplementary 7 [24]. To ensure the consistency and reliability of our Bayesian dose–response models, we assessed model consistency by comparing the consistency model with the unrelated mean effects model.The differences in deviation, the number of estimated parameters, and the DIC indicated good consistency across models, as detailed in Supplementary 4. Our study considered different outcome measures. We used a 95% credible interval (95% CrI) to provide a range of values within which we are confident the true effect size lies [25].

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram outlining the systematic search and selection process. The initial electronic screening identified 5030 potentially relevant articles. After title and abstract screening, 103 studies were deemed eligible and underwent full-text review. Subsequently, 14 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were retained for meta-analysis.Fig. 1Flow chart of the study selection procedure

Flow chart of the study selection procedure

The included RCTs were published between 2005 and 2023 (median year, 2014), comprising a total of 3376 participants (1683 boys), aged 3–7 years. Sample sizes ranged from 30 to 1434, with a mean participant age of 4.98 years (SD = 0.51). Intervention durations spanned 8–48 weeks (mean 20.62 weeks, SD 13.50), frequencies ranged from 1 to 5 sessions per week (mean 3.28, SD 1.17), and session lengths varied from 15 to 90 min (mean 36.40 min, SD 17.12).

The number of independent data points differed by physical fitness outcome: upper muscle strength (10 studies) [26–31], lower muscle strength (14 studies) [6, 26–34], flexibility quality (10 studies) [26, 28–31, 34, 35], coordination (6 studies) [26–30, 33, 36][, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF; 11 studies) [6, 26, 28–32], dynamic balance (11 studies) [26, 28, 29, 31, 36–38], and static balance (10 studies) [6, 27, 28, 30, 33, 37, 38]. Detailed study characteristics are presented in Supplementary 3.

The results of pairwise meta-analysis

Summary of overall physical fitness outcomes

Seventy-three independent data points from 14 studies contributed to the analysis of overall physical fitness, which encompassed all measured sub-components. As depicted in Fig. 2, chronic PA interventions exerted a small but significant positive effect on overall physical fitness in preschool children (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.38, I2 = 72.8%, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that intervention duration (≥ 16 weeks vs. < 16 weeks), session duration (≥ 40 min vs. < 40 min), and frequency (> 3 times/week vs. ≤ 3 times/week) each influenced the pooled effect size.Fig. 2Effects of physical activity on preschool children’s physical fitness with different moderators

Effects of physical activity on preschool children’s physical fitness with different moderators

Muscle strength

PA interventions significantly improved upper muscle strength (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.46, I2 = 33.2%, p < 0.001). In subgroup analyses, interventions ≥ 16 weeks in duration (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.54, I2 = 41.0%, p = 0.001), session lengths < 40 min (SMD = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.62, I2 = 35.1%, p < 0.001), and frequencies ≤ 3 times/week (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.53, I2 = 20.0%, p < 0.001) all yielded significant gains in upper muscle strength.

PA interventions produced a significant improvement in lower muscle strength (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12 to 0.46, I2 = 75.5%, p = 0.001). Subgroup analyses indicated meaningful effects in both < 16 weeks (SMD = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.23 to 0.80, I2 = 44%, p < 0.001) and ≥ 16 weeks of intervention (SMD = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.38, I2 = 74.4%, p = 0.031). Session length (≥ 40 min: SMD = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.43, I2 = 61.9%, p = 0.045; < 40 min: SMD = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.65, I2 = 80.8%, p = 0.014) also influenced lower muscle strength. Of note, only the > 3 times/week subgroup displayed significant improvement (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.61, I2 = 83.3%, p = 0.005).

Flexibility quality

PA interventions significantly enhanced flexibility (SMD = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.52, I2 = 72.4%, p = 0.028). Subgroup analyses revealed that session lengths ≥ 40 min (SMD = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.59, I2 = 54.9%, p = 0.016) and frequencies > 3 times/week (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.18 to 1.13, I2 = 79.6%, p = 0.007) contributed substantially to the observed improvement.

CRF

PA interventions led to a significant increase in CRF (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.48, I2 = 72.4%, p = 0.003). Subgroup analyses indicated significant improvements with both < 16 weeks (SMD = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.54, I2 = 0%, p = 0.005) and ≥ 16 weeks of intervention (SMD = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.52, I2 = 77.0%, p = 0.022), as well as with PA frequencies of > 3 times/week (SMD = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.80, I2 = 85.5%, p = 0.031) and ≤ 3 times/week (SMD = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.39, I2 = 27.0%, p = 0.031). Interestingly, the < 40-min session subgroup (SMD = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.49, I2 = 43.2%, p = 0.015) also showed notable CRF improvements.

Coordination

PA interventions significantly enhanced coordination (SMD = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31 to 0.76, I2 = 57.5%, p < 0.001). All examined subgroups—longer (≥ 16 weeks: SMD = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.20 to 0.78, I2 = 65.7%, p = 0.001) versus shorter (< 16 weeks: SMD = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.34 to 0.91, I2 = 0.0%, p < 0.001) durations, longer (≥ 40 min: SMD = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.78, I2 = 68.4%, p = 0.001) versus shorter (< 40 min: SMD = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.31 to 0.96, I2 = 0.2%, p < 0.001) session lengths, and higher (> 3 times/week: SMD = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.26 to 0.75, I2 = 60.4%, p < 0.001) versus lower (≤ 3 times/week: SMD = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.13 to 1.26, I2 = 46.7%, p = 0.016) frequencies—exhibited statistically significant effects.

Balance

PA interventions significantly improved static balance (SMD = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.65, I2 = 80.7%, p = 0.007). Within subgroup analyses, frequencies of ≤ 3 times/week yielded a significant benefit (SMD = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.55, I2 = 43.8%, p = 0.003). All studies included in this analysis of static balance used ≥ 40-min PA sessions. No significant improvement was noted in dynamic balance, and subgroup analyses did not alter the null findings.

The results of dose–response relationship

Overall physical fitness

A non-linear dose–response curve was identified between weekly PA dose (0–380 min/week) and overall physical fitness (Fig. 3). The optimal dose was approximately 270 min/week (SMD = 0.557, 95% CrI: 0.347 to 0.825), indicating that moderate-to-high volumes of structured PA may maximize improvements in preschool children’s global fitness levels.Fig. 3Dose–response relationship between overall exercise dose and change in the physical fitness

Muscle strength

An inverted U-shaped association emerged between weekly PA dose (0–350 min/week) and upper muscle strength (Fig. 4a). The optimal dose converged at approximately 260 min/week (SMD = 0.337, 95% CrI: 0.068 to 0.735), suggesting that interventions beyond this range may yield diminishing returns.Fig. 4Dose–response relationship between different exercise doses and change in the different specific fitness components

Dose–response relationship between different exercise doses and change in the different specific fitness components

Lower muscle strength displayed a similarly inverted U-shaped pattern (Fig. 4b), with effective doses spanning 0–350 min/week and an estimated optimum of around 260 min/week (SMD = 0.424, 95% CrI: 0.235 to 0.746). This finding aligns closely with that of upper muscle strength, emphasizing the importance of balancing intensity and volume.

Flexibility quality

Flexibility quality improved with PA volumes up to 340 min/week (Fig. 4c). The peak effect occurred at roughly 190 min/week (SMD = 0.345, 95% CrI: 0.078 to 0.739), suggesting that, compared with muscle strength outcomes, lower cumulative exercise doses may suffice to enhance flexibility quality in preschool populations.

CRF

Dose–response modeling for CRF (Figure 4d) demonstrated continued gains up to 380 min/week, with the maximum benefit at the highest dose examined (SMD = 0.255, 95% CrI: 0.106 to 0.496). This suggests that higher weekly PA volumes may be particularly advantageous for improving aerobic capacity in young children.

Coordination

Coordination displayed a wide range of effective doses, from 0 to 380 min/week (Fig. 4e). The apparent optimal volume reached approximately 300 min/week (SMD = 0.419, 95% CrI: 0.075 to 1.082). This finding highlights that motor skill development—particularly coordination—benefits from relatively high PA volumes.

Static balance

Improvements in static balance were most pronounced at doses up to 180 min/week (Fig. 4f), peaking around that threshold (SMD = 0.431, 95% CrI: 0.224 to 0.821). Compared with other outcomes (e.g., CRF and coordination), static balance may not require as high a weekly PA volume to achieve meaningful gains.

Connectivity assessment

A connectivity assessment was undertaken to verify adequate linkages among all dose levels. Insufficient connectivity may compromise statistical power and yield biased findings when direct comparisons are infeasible. The assessment confirmed no connectivity deficits among the examined PA doses, thereby supporting the robustness of the subsequent dose–response modeling (Supplementary 11).

Quality assessment of evidence and risk of bias

Overall, 10 studies (40%) exhibited a low risk of bias, 10 (40%) had an unclear risk, and 5 (20%) demonstrated a high risk of bias. Study-level details of risk of bias are provided in Supplementary 8. Except for upper muscle strength, several outcomes displayed relatively high heterogeneity (Supplementary 9). Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s tests. Potential publication bias was noted for flexibility, lower muscle strength, coordination, CRF, and static balance (p < 0.05), warranting cautious interpretation. The p-values for all remaining outcomes exceeded 0.05, indicating no significant publication bias in those measures (Supplementary 10).

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of PA on PF in preschool children and their dose–response relationship by including 14 RCTs involving 3,376 children aged 3–7 years. The results demonstrated that chronic PA interventions significantly improved multiple PF components, including upper muscle strength, lower muscle strength, flexibility quality, CRF, coordination, and static balance. Subgroup analyses further revealed that the effectiveness of PA interventions varied by intervention duration, session length, and frequency. Specifically, upper muscle strength and static balance showed greater improvements with interventions lasting ≥ 16 weeks, while lower muscle strength, CRF, and coordination benefited more from durations < 16 weeks. Similarly, session lengths ≥ 40 min were more effective for static balance, flexibility quality, and coordination, whereas upper muscle strength and CRF responded better to sessions < 40 min. In terms of frequency, lower muscle strength and flexibility quality improved more with > 3 times/week, while static balance, upper muscle strength, and coordination were more effective with ≤ 3 times/week. Additionally, dose–response analyses highlighted distinct patterns based on whether metrics exhibited an inverted U-shaped trend. Metrics with an inverted U-shaped dose–response (upper muscle strength, overall physical fitness, coordination, and lower muscle strength) demonstrated optimal effectiveness at 190, 270, 300, and 260 min/week, respectively, with effectiveness declining beyond these doses. In contrast, metrics without an inverted U-shaped trend (flexibility quality, CRF, and static balance) showed continuous improvements or stable effects at higher doses, with optimal doses of 380, 380, and 180 min/week, respectively. These findings highlight the need for tailored physical activity interventions based on dose–response characteristics and subgroup-specific effectiveness.

PF is crucial for promoting healthy growth and long-term well-being in preschool children [7]. This study found that through chronic PA, preschool children demonstrated significant improvements in various physical fitness indicators, including lower muscle strength, flexibility quality, CRF, coordination, and static balance. This observation is consistent with previous research [7, 39–41]. From the perspective of exercise physiology, several mechanisms underlie these improvements. First, the skeletal and muscular systems of preschool children are in a rapid developmental stage [42, 43]. Chronic PA stimulates muscle growth and bone strengthening through mechanical loading [44]. Second, PA enhances neuromuscular coordination by improving proprioception and optimizing neural signal transmission [45], improving coordination and static balance [46]. Additionally, aerobic exercise effectively enhances CRF by increasing cardiac output, improving VO2max, and enhancing vascular endothelial function, leading to significant improvements in cardiorespiratory endurance [47]. Finally, modern PA interventions typically include stretching routines before and after exercise, which increase the elasticity of muscles and connective tissues, expand the range of motion in joints, and thereby improve overall flexibility quality. In summary, these indicators promote each other through the synergistic effects of energy supply, neural regulation, and the musculoskeletal system, collectively improving the overall physical fitness and health level.

The effects of exercise interventions are influenced by variations in duration, session length, and frequency [48]. Therefore, a subgroup analysis was performed on the included studies, revealing significant differences in the effects of various combinations of these factors on different indicators. Interestingly, our study revealed significant differences in the optimal intervention strategies for upper and lower muscle strength in preschool children: the former benefits more from low-frequency, long-duration, and long-session traning(> 3 times/week, ≥ 40 min/session, > 16 weeks), while the latter improves more with high-frequency, short-duration, and short-session training(> 3 times/week, < 40 min/session, < 16 weeks). Some studies align with this viewpoint [27, 49–51], Short-session training suits children’s short attention spans and leverages the rapid response capacity of lower muscles, combined with daily activities like walking and running, to achieve significant improvements [52]. Low-frequency training ensure sufficient recovery, preventing overtraining-realated fatigue [53, 54]. Long-session training fully activates upper muscles and nervous system, promoting muscle endurance and neural adaptation. Differences in muscle volume, strength, fiber types, and training adaptability between the upper and lower limbs may lead to varying responses to training stimuli [48]. The intervention plan for improving upper muscle is equally effective in improving static balance. However, some studies have reported conflicting findings [48, 55], possibly due to differences in age groups and training content, such as full-body versus targeted training.

In this study, it was also found that, the intervention protocol (≤ 3 times/week, < 40 min/session, < 16 weeks) improve preschooler’s CRF and coordination, while protocol (> 3 times/week, ≥ 40 min/session, > 16 weeks) enhance flexibility quality. These distinct outcomes may be attributed to the physiological and developmental characteristics of preschool-aged children. For CRF and coordination, low-frequency training ensures sufficient recovery, preventing excessive cardiac and neural load [56]. Short-session training, such as HIIT [57], rapidly elevates heart rate and oxygen uptake, aligning with children’s shorter attention spans, while allowing timely adjustments in content and intensity based on their progress. In contrast, high-frequency and long-duration stretching exercises benefit flexibility quality development. Preschoolers naturally have a large range of joint motion but underdeveloped muscle and ligament elasticity [58], which high-frequency training consistently stimulates, while long-duration interventions promote gradual improvements. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring intervention protocols to specific fitness components, considering the unique physiological and developmental needs of preschool-aged children.

Based on subgroup findings, this study further explores the dose–response relationship between PA interventions and PF improvement, revealing significant variations across different indicators. Specifically, while overall PF exhibits a subtle inverted U-shaped dose–response pattern. According to WHO recommendations, children aged 3 to 4 should engage in at least 180 min/d of PA, including at least 60 min of MVPA [5]. However, this study finds that a weekly PA intervention of 270 min yields optimal PF improvement, with further increases showing diminishing returns and an inverted U-shaped pattern. This discrepancy may arise because WHO guidelines target overall health, while this study focuses solely on PF. Preschoolers’ underdeveloped muscles, bones, and nervous systems make them prone to fatigue with excessive PA, reducing exercise benefits [59, 60]. Extended interventions may also exceed their attention span, causing boredom and reduced engagement.

This study identifies distinct dose–response patterns across PF indicators. Upper and lower muscle strength and coordination exhibit an inverted U-shaped relationship, with optimal doses at 190, 260, and 300 min/week, respectively, likely due to fatigue accumulation and limited recovery capacity. Appropriate PA stimulates muscle growth and neuromuscular adaptation, while excessive PA may cause neural fatigue, reducing coordination training effectiveness [33, 61]. On the other hand, flexibility quality, CRF, and static balance exhibit a linear dose–response relationship, with optimal doses at 380, 380, and 180 min/week, respectively, like due to their strong adaptability to PA. Flexibility quality improves through sustained stretching and movement [3], CRF throughvconsistent aerobic exercise stimulation [47], and static balance through continuous neuromuscular control training, with benefits increasing with higher PA level [33].

This study’s strengths include integrating RCTs with dose–response analysis in a Bayesian framework, ensuring robust findings. Multidimensional PF assessment and broad inclusion criteria enhance result generalizability, while dose–response and subgroup analyses provide insights into PA dosage effects, offering a scientific basis for preschool interventions. This study also has several limitations. It lacks subgroup analysis for overweight or obese preschoolers, potentially affecting generalizability. The limited number of included studies and high heterogeneity, along with possible publication bias, may impact findings. Additionally, the study does not classify PA interventions by intensity or type, limiting practical applicability. Furthermore‌, fundamental gaps in preschool exercise science—particularly the absence of validated metabolic equivalents (METs) for play-based activities—precluded intensity-specific recommendations, as conventional classification systems are incompatible with developmentally appropriate interventions. Insufficient standardization of dose quantification and the absence of long-term intervention evaluations further constrain the results. Moreover, habitual physical activity levels of children were not accounted for due to inconsistent reporting in primary studies, which may influence fitness outcome interpretations.

Future research should explore exercise intensities and PA types to identify optimal strategies, specifically implementing device-based intensity monitoring, conduct subgroup analyses for overweight and obese preschoolers, incorporate baseline PA monitoring, and integrate emerging technologies like machine learning for deeper mechanistic insights. Long-term effects should be evaluated, and dose quantification methods refined for practical implementation.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis confirms that chronic PA interventions significantly improve multiple components of PF in preschool children. Subgroup analyses identified four optimized PA protocols targeting specific PF outcomes, varying in frequency, session length, and intervention duration. Dose–response analyses revealed inverted U-shaped relationships for overall PF, upper/lower muscle strength, and coordination, with optimal weekly doses of 270, 190, 260, and 300 min, respectively. In contrast, flexibility, CRF, and static balance demonstrated linear dose–response patterns, peaking at 380, 380, and 180 min/week. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for tailoring PA prescriptions in early childhood, highlighting the importance of dose-specific strategies to maximize fitness gains. Future studies should investigate the role of PA intensity, modality, and long-term effects to further refine early-life intervention frameworks.
