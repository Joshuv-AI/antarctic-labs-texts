The tourism performance of spa destinations during crises in Central and Eastern Europe: An adjusted RCA index
Authors: Zsuzsanna Bacsi, Ernő Kovács, Zsuzsanna Lőke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tourism is affected by natural and man-made disasters, such as the recent COVID-19 pandemic and the Russia-Ukraine war. The impacts of these on tourism have been analysed at national and regional levels, and at the level of individual destinations. However, the impacts on spa towns near the Ukrainian border are much less researched. The paper focuses on seven Hungarian spa destinations, one spa destination in Romania, and one in Slovakia to analyse the changes in their revealed comparative advantages between 2017 and 2023, covering the years of the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russian-Ukrainian war outbreak. The index of revealed comparative advantage was modified to make it applicable at town level in tourism, comparing destinations of various sizes and types. Findings show that the large, internationally focused destinations lost their comparative advantage during the pandemic, while smaller spa towns, orientated towards domestic tourism, benefited from the border lockdowns. The war outbreak led to considerable losses of comparative advantage in the spa destinations, while their countries and capital cities could increase their comparative advantages. Despite the crises, the competitiveness of spa resorts was better than the European Union average, though their countries remained less competitive than the EU.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 had a disastrous effect on tourism, leading to a nearly 50 % decrease in revenues, and a 20 % job loss in 2020 [[1], [2], [3]]. Just after the first signs of recovery, the outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war, in February 2022, severely impacted world tourism again [4]. While the COVID-19 pandemic impacted tourism throughout the world, the war affected Eastern Europe more, especially the countries in the neighbourhood of Ukraine, including Romania, Slovakia and Hungary [4,5]. Besides, higher inflation and rising energy prices negatively affected disposable incomes in the source markets of various tourism destinations [6]. Managerial actions for improving the crisis resistance, and for speeding up post-crisis recovery require a clear understanding and reliable quantification of the impacts of crises.

The concept of destination competitiveness evaluates the availability of success factors of tourism, and their efficient utilisation [[7], [8], [9]]. Destination competitiveness can be assessed from the input side when it focuses on resources, endowments, infrastructure, and available services. Output-side evaluation refers to tourist arrivals, overnights, and revenues [9]. The concept of competitiveness is suitable for assessing how resilient various destinations are in the face of natural and man-made disasters. Competitiveness means the ability to turn resources into high market shares and high revenues. Crisis resilience means that a destination can maintain its competitive position not only during good times but during bad ones, too [[7], [8], [9]].

The present study aims to measure the competitiveness of spa destinations in Hungary, Romania and Slovakia, during the 2017–2023 period. These years were affected by the COVID-19 pandemic and the outbreak of the war between Russia and Ukraine. Spa destinations are not too prone to seasonality, therefore their resilience in crises may differ from holiday tourism destinations. The choice of the spas was motivated by their relative closeness to the Ukrainian border. The quantitative tool is an adaptation of the normalised revealed comparative advantage index (NRCA) [[10], [11], [12]]. The adaptation is needed, because we evaluate spa towns, for which data availability is often a problem. Therefore, the paper introduces the Normalised Tourism Revealed Comparative Advantage (NTRCA) index, which can be applied to town-level territorial units.

The outbreak of COVID-19 in 2020 had a major impact on the European tourism sector, with substantial variations recorded between regions. However, research is still limited to country-specific case studies. Multi-country comparisons at regional or local level are even rarer [13,14]. The resilience of destinations can be viewed through their competitiveness, comparing their abilities to maintain their position in the tourism market. Destination competitiveness, especially at the regional or local levels, is usually measured from the input side, evaluating endowments, services, infrastructure, and similar components. Analysis from the output side (i.e., by arrivals, overnights, spending) is rare for regional or local destinations, though country-level evaluations are abundant [1.4.5.6]. The present study addresses this research gap, providing an output-side competitiveness evaluation for 9 destinations of a specific type, namely spa resorts. The evaluation looks at the revealed competitive advantages of these destinations, with the intention of assessing their resilience in crises. Revealed comparative advantage has not been computed for such destinations, to our best knowledge.

Thus, the present study modifies the normalised revealed comparative advantage index for tourism at the level of towns or villages, bridging a data availability gap at this level. The novel contributions of the study to the current literature are: the computation of output-side competitiveness based on data available at the town level, the assessment of town-level destination performance during the pandemic in 3 countries, the assessment of town-level destination competitiveness changes after the outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war, in the neighbourhood of Ukraine, and the comparison of the spa towns to the average tourism performance of their countries. The time period is the years from 2017 to 2023, that cover the mentioned two crises.

The following research questions are analysed:Q1How resilient are spa destinations to crises, namely the COVID-19 pandemic, and the Russia-Ukraine war, according to their revealed comparative advantage index?Q2Does the competitiveness of spa towns differ from the average tourism competitiveness of their countries and capital cities?Q3Does NTRCA assess differently the competitive positions of destinations than traditional tourism demand measures, such as tourist overnights?

Literature review

Tourism and crises

Tourism may be afflicted by several types of crises or disasters, some of which can be of natural causes, others human-induced. These include economic crises (labour strikes, market crashes, major fluctuations or declines in earnings); informational crises (loss of proprietary and confidential information, or key computer information regarding customers and suppliers); physical crises (loss of key equipment, material suppliers; key facilities); human resource-related crises (loss of key executives, or personnel, increased vandalism, rise in workplace violence); reputation crises (slander, gossip, damage to corporate reputation); crises resulting from psychopathic actions (kidnapping, hostage taking, terrorism); natural disasters (diseases, earthquakes, fires, floods, hurricanes, explosions) [7,8]. The recent COVID-19 pandemic is an example of a natural disaster, while the outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war is a typical human-induced disaster.

The impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on tourism have been well established. Global tourism receipts decreased to 37.3 % of the 2019 value in 2020, and international arrivals were only at 27.8 %. Recovery has been slow, only at 75 % and 66 % of the 2019 value in 2022, for receipts and arrivals, respectively [5,6]. Recently, considerable research has been done about the impacts of COVID-19 on tourism at the national level, but there is only a limited number of case studies about the impacts at the regional or urban level [14]. The existing results are mainly about Croatia, Czechia, Spain, Poland, and Portugal, and these indicate that destinations relying on high shares of international tourism suffered stronger demand losses. The findings of a post-pandemic impact analysis for 35 European countries at the regional level showed, that the greatest annual losses in guest-nights were identified in city destinations, followed by urban mix destinations and rural destinations, while mountain and nature-based destinations suffered only slightly [14].

The outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war in early 2022 changed the pattern of recovery. Due to the war, Russia lost most of its former importance as a tourism market. Its shares in 2019, i.e., before the pandemic, were 2.43 % and 3.10 % in global international expenditures and departures, respectively. In 2020 these values fell to 1.65 % in expenditures, and 3.04 % in departures, but in the following year, with a steady increase, they reached 4.19 % in departures, and 1.80 % in expenditures. After the outbreak of the war, the departure share nearly halved to 2.33 %, reflecting the impacts of travel restrictions inflicted on Russian citizens, though the expenditure share still increased slightly, to 1.87 % [15].

Ukraine suffered less from the COVID crisis, and its share in international kept increasing from 2019 (1.97 %) to 2021 (3.13 %). However, following the outbreak of the war, this share fell back to 1.57 %. Expenditure shares kept increasing from 2019 to 2022, probably due to the fact that at the outbreak of war, the wealthier citizens escaped abroad [5,15].

Arrivals of Russian and Ukrainian tourists to Hungary, Romania and Slovakia show an even more striking pattern. While total foreign arrivals into these countries decreased seriously in 2020 (34.5 % of the 2019 value in Slovakia, 39.2 % in Romania and 51.5 % in Hungary), a steady recovery was seen up to 2022, reaching 63.0 % (Slovakia), 75.5 % (Hungary) and 99.1 % (Romania) of the 2019 figures. However, the share of Russian arrivals to these countries fell from 0.31 % in 2019 to 0.11 % in 2022 in Hungary, from 2.11 % to a neglectable level in Slovakia, and from 0.28 % to 0.08 % in Romania [15,16]. The Ukrainian arrival figures show a mixed picture, as after the war outbreak many Ukrainians fled to neighbouring countries in escape from the war [5,17]. As these data indicate, the tourism sector of these three countries suffered not only from the pandemic, but from the Russian-Ukrainian war, too.

Spa tourism in Hungary, Romania and Slovakia

Spa tourism is a significant and fast-growing sector in the tourism economy, and Europe is a leading actor in this field. The major source countries are Russia, Greece, Germany, and the UK [18]. Russia, as one of the world's most populous countries, is a major sender market in health and wellness tourism [19]. Therefore, a significant decrease in travels by Russians impacts spa destinations to a great extent. In Slovakia, 5.2 % of spa visitors and 7.9 % of international overnight stays were from Russian tourists in 2015 [20,21].

The global spa industry was growing at an annual rate of 9.9 % from 2015 to 2017, with over 149 000 spas worldwide, earning approximately $93.6 billion, and employing close to 2.5 million workers in 2017 [22]. Germany, Slovenia, Austria, Spain, Italy, Hungary and Poland are positioned among the top ten of the thermal spa destinations [18,23]. Many classic wellness and spa resorts exist in the Balkans, and in Central and Eastern Europe, including Hungary, Romania and Slovakia [24,25].

Hungary is one of the richest countries in thermal and medicinal water [26]. Out of its more than 30 famous spa-based tourism destinations, there are seven internationally renowned towns in which spa-tourism is the major economic activity. These are Bük, Gyula, Hajdúszoboszló, Harkány, Hévíz, Sárvár and Zalakaros. In these towns, most of the businesses and local inhabitants earn their living from spa tourism, either directly or indirectly. Their tourism appeal is based on their thermal and medicinal water resources. Besides tourists from Western Europe, the pre-pandemic years witnessed an increasing share of Russian and Czech visitors [27]. In the towns of Hajdúszoboszló, Sárvár, Gyula and Zalakaros, the spa is located in a well-defined area of the city, separated from the city centre. Sárvár and Gyula have an old historical town centre with cultural attractions. In Hévíz and Harkány, the entire town is defined by the presence of health tourism, while in Bük the spa area is separate from the town [26,28,29].

In Romania, health tourism is mainly focused on medical tourism in urban destinations and medical wellness/spa tourism in the mountain area [30]. Spa resorts possess about 10 % of the country's tourism accommodation facilities. Therapies in spas are based on natural therapeutic factors (bioclimatic, salt mine, thermal and mineral waters, sea and lake water) [30]. Sovata is one of the five most famous spa resorts in Romania, namely Sovata, Covasna, Băile Felix-1 Mai, Techirghiol and Băile Tusnad [31], and because of investments from the Danubius Group, its facilities and services are exceptionally highly developed, compared to other balneology sites of the country [32]. As demonstrated by Stupariu and Morar [21], Sovata has the least seasonal fluctuation of the five main spas regarding arrivals or overnight stays.

Slovakia also has excellent natural healing resources and long traditions in balneology. Among the 21 spa towns in Slovakia, Piešťany is one of the most frequented destinations visited by international tourists, besides Bratislava, the capital, the High and Low Tatras, the city of Žilina, Košice and a few other regional centres [33]. It is one of the most famous thermal springs, with a temperature of 70 °C, and significant mineral content in its water. It is second only to the High Tatras regarding visitor numbers, overnight stays, share of foreign visitors, and bed places [20].

The average distance of the mentioned nine destinations from the Ukrainian border is 323.4 km (aerial distance), according to the Distance Calculator website [34]. The closest ones are Hajdúszoboszló (125.5 km) and Sovata (154.6 km), followed by Piešťany (154.6 km) and Gyula (199.7 km), while the rest, i.e., the Western Hungarian destinations, range from 414.4 to 434.7 km. This closeness makes them less attractive to overseas tourists, as the area within a 500 km range of the war area may still be considered a danger zone [5]. However, there are no studies about the impacts of the Russian-Ukrainian war on these destinations, nor on any similar tourist town near the Ukrainian border.

Measures of destination competitiveness and comparative advantage

The competitiveness of a destination means its ability to increase tourism expenditure, attract visitors, and provide them with satisfying experiences, in a profitable, but sustainable way [9]. The concept of destination competitiveness, as measured by the Travel and Tourism Competitiveness Index (TTCI) [13], evaluates the factors that facilitate the success of the tourism sector in a country. According to this concept, the success of the travel and tourism sector differs widely depending on the area's tourist appeal, endowments, infrastucture and superstructure and many other aspects of society and economy [13]. There is no single set of indicators that can be generally used to assess destination competitiveness, though several measures may be employed for any specific factor [36]. Some indicators assess the quality and quantity of these facilitating factors, but not their results as reflected in arrivals, nights, spending, and similar outcome measures. Lozano-Oyola et al. [37] listed several specific quantitative indicators for the various input-side competitiveness factors, although these factors do not always translate to market success.

The output-side approach focuses on the outcomes of competitiveness, as is done by the index of revealed comparative advantage (RCA) defined by Balassa in 1965 [38,39], which relies on the export performance of the industry. RCA was successfully applied for various industries, including trade competitiveness of palm oil [40], dairy products [41], coffee [42], or comparing various countries, regions, and industries [[43], [44], [45], [46]]. RCA has been applied for the tourism industry, using international tourism revenues as the indicator of tourism exports on the country level [[47], [48], [49]], but analyses at town or city level are not known.

Materials and methods

The objective of the research is to assess the revealed comparative advantage of spa destinations (towns) during 2017–2023, covering the COVID-19 pandemic and the outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war. This required the adaptation of the revealed comparative advantage index to tourism at the level of subnational territorial units.

According to the original concept, higher RCA values reflect higher levels of comparative advantage, but RCA has many problems, which may distort this meaning [[10], [11], [12]]. The most frequently mentioned problems are: i) it is not symmetric, as non-competitive values fall in the 0–1 range, while competitive values range from 1 to infinity; ii) there is the so-called small-country bias, which means that when Xc is small relative to Xw, then RCA tends to be high, showing small countries more competitive than large ones. Several variations have been developed to amend these deficiencies. One such measure is the index of Normalised Revealed Comparative Advantage (NRCA), defined [10] as:(2)NRCAc,i = Xc,i / Xw – Xc × Xw,i/Xw2 = [ Xc,i/Xw,i – Xc / Xw] × Xw,i/Xwwith the same notations as in Eq. (1). The NRCA index is symmetric, ranging from −1 to +1, with the neutral value being zero, and it is free of the small-country bias. Besides, NRCA is additive, and transitive, and it correctly assesses temporal changes, i.e., it facilitates between-year comparisons [10]. When dealing with sub-national territorial units, specifically with towns or villages, the difficulty lies in the fact that ‘export’ values are not available at this level. Sub-national export values are usually not measurable; therefore, various proxies have to be considered. A destination's share in world tourism can be measured not only by its share in tourism receipts, but also by its share in arrivals (tourist numbers), tourist overnights in the destination, or by these values per inhabitant or area unit [50,51]. These all represent the ability of the destination to sell its services to the external world. The core concept of RCA is similar to this, as it measures the destination's ability to export its particular product (or service) to the external world, compared to its overall export market share. Proxies for subnational export values are needed not only in tourism, but in the manufacturing industries, too [52].

Our further analysis relies on the modification of the NRCA index [10], making it applicable to towns and villages as territorial units.

The export from subnational locations is not sales to foreign countries, but sales to any location outside the analysed one, which includes both international tourism and domestic tourism. Therefore, Xd,T will represent a value reflecting the incomes gained from both domestic and international tourism. This value can be approximated by the total tourist nights spent in the area as a proxy. The Xd value presents similar difficulties: instead of international exports, we should count all the revenues gained by the destination by selling anything to other locations.

Keeping in mind the concept of comparative advantage, i.e., the sector's performance is higher than what is expected considering its overall trade performance, other interpretations, or measurements may be reasonable in Eq. (4). In this equation, A measures the difference between the destination's share in world tourism and its share in world trade. The destination's share in world trade reflects its overall importance in the world. Therefore, other indicators can be reasonably applied, that reflect this concept. A destination's overall importance in the world economy can be estimated by its share in the global population, in the total area of the world, in total employment, or in productive resources. Choosing population or total area for this purpose is reasonable, because the larger a destination, the more human or physical resources may exist in it [50,51].

•Xw,T and Xw: the same measures are used for the world as for the destination. The ‘world’ can be either the whole world or a supranational region, e.g., the EU-27, as the reference area. Xw,T and Xw: the same measures are used for the world as for the destination. The ‘world’ can be either the whole world or a supranational region, e.g., the EU-27, as the reference area. In the latter case, the comparative advantage index will reflect the destination's competitiveness compared to any other destination in the EU-27.

With the above interpretation we will refer to the index as TRCA (Tourism Revealed Comparative Advantage) defined by Eq. (5):(5)TRCAd,T = [Xd,T/Xw,T – Xd / Xw ] × Xw,T/Xw

TRCA possesses all the favourable properties of NRCA, as will be shown below:

Properties of TRCA, derivation of NTRCA

The neutral value of TRCA is zero.

TRCAd,T = [Xd,T/Xw,T – Xd/Xw ] × Xw,T/Xw = 0 if and only if Xd,T/Xw,T = Xd/Xw which means that the destination's share of tourism nights in the world is the same as the share of its overall economic size in the world total. This means that the country has neither advantage, nor disadvantage. Its position is neutral, because its share in world tourism is exactly the same as its share in the world population (or area).

TRCA is symmetric.

The highest value of TRCAd,T = [Xd,T/Xw,T – Xd/Xw ] × Xw,T/Xw is achieved when Xd,T/Xw,T is as high as possible, and Xd/Xw is as small as possible, because 0 ≤ Xw,T/Xw is independent of the destination.

Similarly, the smallest value of [Xd,T/Xw,T – Xd/Xw ] × Xw,T/Xw is achieved when Xd,T/Xw,T is as small as possible, and Xd/Xw is as high as possible.

The values of Xd,T/Xw,T and Xd/Xw fall between zero and 1, as 0 ≤ Xd,T ≤ Xw,T and 0 ≤ Xd ≤ XwIf Xd,T = 0 and Xd = Xw, then TRCAmin = (0 – 1) × Xw,T/Xw = – Xw,T/XwIf Xd,T = Xw,T and Xd = 0, then TRCAmax = (1 – 0) × Xw,T/Xw = Xw,T/Xw

We could ‘normalise’ the TRCA value, dividing it by Xw,T/Xw, to transform it to the [−1; + 1] range. Then we define the Normalised Tourism Revealed Comparative Advantage as:(6)NTRCAd,T = TRCAd,T / [Xw,T/Xw ] = Xd,T/Xw,T – Xd / Xw

It is easy to see that NTRCA is symmetric, it ranges from −1 to +1, with zero as the neutral value.

Both NTRCA and TRCA are additive by destinations, i.e., if we add up two distinct destinations d1 and d2, then the NTRCA index for the union of them is the sum of their separate index values:NTRCAd1,T = Xd1,T/Xw,T – Xd1 / Xw and NTRCAd2,T = Xd2,T/Xw,T – Xd2/ XwNTRCA d1+d2,T = Xd1+d2,T/Xw,T – Xd1+d2 / Xw

As d1 and d2 are two separate destinations, Xd1+d2,T = Xd1,T + Xd2,T and Xd1+d2 = Xd1 + Xd2, then by simple algebra:NTRCA d1+d2,T = Xd1+d2,T/Xw,T – Xd1+d2/Xw = Xd1,T/Xw,T + Xd2,T/Xw,T – Xd1/Xw – Xd1/Xw==NTRCA d1,T + NTRCA d2,T

As NTRCA is additive, and TRCA is derived from multiplying NTRCA by a constant, TRCA is additive, too.

NTRCA is a transitive indicator by destinations, i.e., if NTRCAa,T ≤ NTRCAb,T and NTRCAb,T ≤ NTRCAc,T then NTRCAa,T ≤ NTRCAc,T

This follows from the definition of NTRCA, i.e.,

Xa,T/Xw,T – Xa/Xw ≤ Xb,T/Xw,T – Xb/Xw and Xb,T/Xw,T – Xb/Xw ≤ Xc,T/Xw,T – Xc/Xw, then.

Xa,T/Xw,T–Xa/Xw ≤ Xc,T/Xw,T – Xc/Xw holds. The transitivity condition is true for TRCA, too.

The NTRCA indicator gives a consistent value in time, i.e., if the competitive position of a destination improves from time t to time t + 1, then its NTRCA value will also increase.

The change of NTRCA from year t to t + 1 is:NTRCAd,T, t+1 – NTRCAd,T,t = [Xd,T, t+1/Xw,T,t+1 – Xd,t+1 / Xw,t+1 ] – [Xd,T,t/Xw,T,t – Xd,t / Xw,t ]=={[Xd,T, t+1/Xw,T,t+1 ] – [Xd,T,t/Xw,T,t ]} – {[Xd,t+1 / Xw,t+1 ] – [Xd,t / Xw,t ]}

The first term shows the temporal change of the destination's actual share in tourism performance, while the second term shows the change of the expected share in a neutral position. (Note: the neutral position means that Xd,T,t/Xw,T,t = Xd,t/Xw,t). If their difference is positive, it means that the destination's tourism performance increased more (or decreased less) than its expected share, i.e., it reveals increased comparative advantage. This means that the comparison of NTRCA values between years is meaningful, and a temporal increase reflects an improvement in the competitive position, and vice versa [10].

Finally, the TRCA and the NTRCA values are compatible with the so-called Kunimoto-Vollrath principle [11,12], which states that the specialisation of a location relative to other areas is measured by comparing its export performance to a theoretical value, and this theoretical value reflects absence of comparative advantages and disadvantages.

The values of 2022 were already affected by the outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war, though these impacts were more visible in 2023.

The COVID-19 pandemic, which broke out in 2020, caused a serious downturn for all tourist destinations, but a significant reorganization of the competitive positions took place between them, which is reflected in the annual change of their NTRCA values, compared to the previous year. In 2020, the pandemic caused the greatest decrease in Hévíz (−25.2 %), but a decline of a similar magnitude (−21.5 %) was also experienced in Bük. Sárvár (−6.6 %) and Hajdúszoboszló (−3.6 %) showed only a mild decrease, while growth was measured in the other spa towns, ranging from 3.5 % to 32.7 %. In comparison, country averages all decreased, at rates from −45 % to −56 %, and even more in capital cities (from −46 % to −113 %). As it is well known from relevant literature, the travel restrictions led to a severe decline in international tourism, therefore the destinations that could attract more domestic tourists were able to forge a comparative advantage from the pandemic [14]. This is reflected in the present results for Hévíz and Bük, which were orientated towards international tourists, and suffered a significant deterioration in their comparative advantage values. The smaller destinations, that were more focused on domestic tourists, i.e., Zalakaros, Harkány, Gyula, as well as Sovata and Piešťany, were able to increase their comparative advantages. Domestic tourism is generally associated with smaller travel distances, and it was not restricted as much as international travel. In Piešťany, a decline similar to that of Hévíz and Bük occurred, though not in 2020, but in the following year, as a delayed effect. As a comparison, the capital cities, as popular destinations for international tourism, experienced much larger decreases by our results. This is in line with [14], in which the impacts of the pandemic were found to be the worst in city destinations, and the smallest in nature-based and mountain destinations.

The recovery from the crisis showed a slow rearrangement of the competitive positions towards the pre-pandemic situation. The NTRCA index increased in each destination, except Piešťany, where the −30.9 % drop can be considered a delayed effect of the pandemic. A similar decrease is true for Slovakia as a whole, and its capital, Bratislava, which suggests that the pandemic lasted longer in this country than in the other two ones. The increased NTRCA values indicate that the tourism industry in the analysed destinations recovered from the crisis more successfully than the EU average. This is true for the spa towns, and for Hungary and Romania, and their capitals, too. As the pandemic affected the whole European Union similarly, the changes in the relative positions of countries, cities, and towns are explained by their specific ways of responding to the pandemic. Hungary and Romania returned rather fast to their pre-pandemic positions, while Slovakia, its capital, Bratislava, and its spa town, Piešťany showed slower recovery.

Previous research demonstrated the worldwide decline of spa and wellness tourism because of the pandemic [3,35,62,63]. Slovakia experienced a 31–65 % loss in visitor numbers compared to 2019 values, while overnights halved, and the share of foreign tourists was only 24 % in 2020 [64]. Similar tendencies were found in the health tourism of Hungary [65], although previous research showed that with growing concerns for health care, spas could increase their popularity after the pandemic [66]. In Romania, the impacts of the pandemic were still felt in 2021, tourism flows were under the 2019 level, and the recovery slowed down in 2022, due to the outbreak of the war [5,67]. However, these results were based on visitor numbers, overnights and revenues, while our research assessed the competitive positions of the destinations relative to their sizes within the EU. The disappearance of the previous high share of Russian and Ukrainian tourists led to a serious drop in every spa town, while in Sovata, Gyula and Hajdúszoboszló, which are located near the Ukrainian border, the geographical proximity of the war zone deterred foreign tourists coming from other regions, too. In 2023, each spa suffered losses in NTRCA, although still maintaining positive values. The drop was greater than the loss in 2020, NTRCA values were lower in 2023 than in 2020. Thus, the pandemic had caused less harm to the competitive positions of spas than the war. The pandemic was a global crisis, affecting the entire EU, while the war had more harmful effects near the war area. Therefore, the comparative advantages in these areas were expected to decrease. However, comparing the spa towns to the capital cities and country averages, we see that each country and each capital city improved its competitive position, so the decrease was felt only in the spa towns. This is a surprising fact and shows the specific vulnerability of the spa destinations. This cannot be attributed only to their distance from the Ukrainian border, as all the capital cities are closer to it than most of the spa towns. The difference may be due to the weaker resilience of the health tourism motivation than that of the mixed motivations of city tourism. However, this issue deserves more analysis in the future.

The impacts of the Russia-Ukraine war were not researched for spa resorts, and the past two years do not provide enough experience for deeper assessment yet. However, it is a well-established fact, that armed conflicts have both short-term and long-term negative impacts on economic and social development by damaging infrastructures, disrupting production and investment activities, and weakening the availability and quality of human resources [67,68]. Previous research showed that after the war outbreak, the post-pandemic recovery of the tourism sector was slower, but not stopped [4]. This was found in our study about capital cities and countries, but not about spa towns. The slow-down is explained by the tourists’ increased security concerns to come to Europe [4,67,68]. As previous research showed, Hungarian tourism has been affected by the Russian invasion of Ukraine, as travellers are afraid to come to a destination with war in its neighbourhood. Russian tourists had formerly flooded to spas (not only to Hungary but to the neighbouring countries as well). Thus, the slowdown of the post-COVID recovery was particularly felt in Hévíz, where Russian and Ukrainian visitors had contributed significantly to foreign turnover. Sárvár, Hajdúszoboszló, and Harkány had also depended heavily on the Russian tourism market [69]. Our results also show that decreases in overnights do not necessarily lead to worse competitive positions, because comparative advantages depend on relative changes, taking into account the whole tourism market of the EU.

Overall, the analysed spa destinations were found to be more competitive than the EU average, and Hungarian spas proved to be more competitive than the assessed spas in Romania and Slovakia. In the 2017–2023 period, only Zalakaros, Sárvár, Gyula could improve their positions permanently, while Bük and Piešťany, with decreasing NTRCA, and Sovata, with a moderately increasing NTRCA, lost their former stronger position. Although Harkány and Hajdúszoboszló significantly increased their NTRCA values, they were unable to move forward in the ranking. Former studies showed similar results. Bozóti [70] found, ranking 41 Hungarian health tourism destinations in Western Hungary, that the best three destinations were Zalakaros, Hévíz, and Bük, with Harkány and Sárvár at the 9th and 10th position. According to Bakucz et al. [71] the spas in Western Hungary performed much better than spas in neighbouring Austria, and Slovenia, with Hévíz, Bük and Zalakaros achieving outstanding results.

Conclusions

The present analysis assessed the competitiveness of spa destinations during the COVID-19 pandemic and the outbreak of the Russian-Ukrainian war. Spa destinations showed great resilience to the pandemic and fared better than capital cities and countries. Their resilience to the war was much weaker, and they performed worse than some capital cities, though their distance from the war zone was similar. This means that their abilities to handle the two different crises were different. The research established that the competitiveness of spa towns differed from that of their countries and capital cities. The spas proved to be more competitive than the EU average, while their countries were less competitive than that. Spa towns can be successful in the tourism market of the EU even when their countries are not. Our findings also demonstrated that the assessment of comparative advantage by the modified revealed comparative advantage index can reflect different aspects of destination competitiveness than assessments based only on absolute numbers of tourist overnights.

A theoretical implication of this research is the development of an output-side competitiveness index that can be applied to subnational destinations, cities, towns, and villages. This facilitates comparisons of towns and cities to regions and countries, and requires data that are readily available even at the town level. Another theoretical implication is the fact that spa destinations possess comparative advantages within the European Union, but this may not be reflected in overnights or arrivals. Therefore, the choice of the evaluation method is significant.

Practical implications suggest that while the impacts of the pandemic were mild in spas, this was not the case for capital cities. The war outbreak resulted in the opposite, the spas were badly hit by it, while countries or capital cities did not really notice its harmful effects. This requires a focused crisis management, and it requires better understanding of the tourists’ choice of destinations during local and global crises. The presented evaluation of spa towns is an addition to the very rare town-level empirical assessments of output-side competitiveness. The developed methodology can be applied to other types of destinations, and can help policymakers in decisions about destination development projects.

The limitations of the research are its short time frame and the small number of destinations. A methodology-related limitation is the application of destination area or destination population as a measure of size, instead of an economic indicator. This choice was determined by the need to use indicators that are widely available for cities, towns, or villages.

Future research will deal with expanding the time frame, and including more spa towns in the sample. Similar assessment can be done for other destination types, too. Other measures applied for the economic size of a destination can also be considered, such as the number of business entities, or the size of the labour force, keeping in mind the need to rely on indicators readily available at the town level.
