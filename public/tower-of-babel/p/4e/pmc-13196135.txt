Annular rupture in TAVI: considerations on the importance of on-site cardiothoracic surgery
Authors: Caelan Bains, Vipin Zamvar, Rong Bing
Published: 2026

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has proven itself as an effective equivalent to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) for patients with aortic stenosis of low-, medium- and high-risk strata [1, 2]. Despite this, there is a recognised risk profile for TAVI. The all-cause 30-day mortality for TAVI is estimated to be between 2.2% and 2.5%, although this value continues to decrease with time [3, 4]. Vascular access complications are the most frequent to arise, but are often managed conservatively or medically. In rarer circumstances, they may warrant endovascular or even surgical management [5–9]. Other significant complications include stroke, acute kidney injury and conduction disturbances necessitating pacemaker insertion. Whilst serious, these complications do not indicate expedient surgical management. Conversely, a much rarer subset of complications do require urgent surgical intervention. In the most extreme cases, bailout emergency cardiac surgery (ECS) may be indicated in complications such as coronary ostium obstruction, device embolisation or malposition, cardiac tamponade; and occasionally, annular rupture – which is the focus of this report [10–12].

The pathophysiology of annular rupture involves trauma to the annulus during the TAVI procedure. Radial forces applied by balloon expansion during implantation of the prosthesis may result in damage at the annular level or within the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). Risk factors associated with annular rupture include an oversized prosthesis (> 20%); the use of a balloon-expandable prosthesis; a small aortic valve annulus (< 20 mm diameter); the presence of significant calcification within the annulus, LVOT or subannular region; and a bicuspid aortic valve [13, 14]. The increased utilisation of multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) can help to identify higher-risk patients [14]. The incidence of annular rupture has rapidly decreased since the initial widespread uptake of TAVI. A recent Swiss retrospective cohort study reported a decrease in incidence, from 0.93% in 2007 to 0.33% in 2023 [15].

The presentation of annular rupture ranges from asymptomatic to major haemodynamic compromise secondary to cardiac tamponade [13, 16]. In patients with haemodynamic compromise, timely diagnosis is essential, and emergency cardiac surgical repair is indicated [17]. This report aims to highlight the importance of accessibility to cardiac surgical services and the implications of increasing access to TAVI within satellite centres where on-call cardiac surgery is not available.

Case presentation

In 2023, an 80-year-old female patient with a background of iron deficiency anaemia, coeliac disease, peripheral arterial disease, and previous bilateral total hip arthroplasties presented to her GP with new-onset exertional dyspnoea. An ejection systolic murmur was detected. A previous echocardiogram in 2016 revealed mild aortic stenosis, but she was lost to follow-up. The patient had previously been very active, having typically walked 10,000 + steps a day and travelled on a walking holiday only several months prior. She was referred to cardiology.

Pre-procedural workup

An up-to-date echocardiogram was obtained, demonstrating severe aortic stenosis (peak velocity 4.8 m/s, mean gradient 58 mmHg, aortic valve area 0.7cm2). There was no clinical evidence of cardiac congestion. The patient was referred for TAVI. A planning CT TAVI revealed a heavily calcified aortic valve with fusion of the right coronary cusp (RCC) and non-coronary cusp (NCC) (Fig. 1). The aortic valve calcium score was 3996 Agatston units. The annulus area was 469 mm2, with an area-derived mean diameter of 25 mm. The intercommissural distance was 28 mm, and the mean sinotubular junction diameter was 28 mm. The iliofemoral arteries were not diseased and were of suitable calibre for transfemoral TAVI. A 42 mm juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm with a mural thrombus was incidentally identified.

TAVI procedure

A pre-procedure team brief was performed and the patient was confirmed to be suitable for full escalation of care, including emergency surgery if indicated. Transfemoral TAVI was undertaken in a standard fashion. Right radial artery (6 F), right common femoral artery (14 F E-Sheath, Edwards Lifesciences; pre-closure with 1x Prostyle, Abbott) and right common femoral vein (6 F) sheath were placed and 100 u/kg intravenous unfractionated heparin administered. Pre-dilatation balloon aortic valvuloplasty was undertaken (20 mm Cristal, Balt). A 26 mm Sapien 3 Resilia (Edwards Lifesciences) was deployed under rapid left ventricular pacing. Eccentric underexpansion at the calcified raphe was noted. Routine post-deployment aortography was performed. This revealed an aortic perforation with florid extravasation of contrast (Fig. 2, see additional file 1 for video).

a (left): Aortogram performed before valve deployment, demonstrating anatomy of proximal aorta and RCA. b (right): Aortogram after deployment of Sapien valve, demonstrating leak (red circle) superior to RCA

The patient was initially asymptomatic. Recognising the time-critical nature of the complication, the following actions were undertaken in parallel by mobilising the in-lab team as well as other cathlab staff: (1) Pericardiocentesis kit prepared, (2) transthoracic echocardiogram performed, (3) cardiac arrest call, (4) cardiothoracic surgery call (dedicated emergency call to the on-call cardiothoracic registrar and on-call cardiothoracic anaesthetist). In the time taken to implement the above, the patient became progressively hypotensive and haemopericardium was confirmed on echocardiography. The patient’s systolic blood pressure nadir was 60 mmHg but she remained conscious throughout without any period of cardiac arrest. Her blood pressure immediately recovered upon placement of a pericardial drain and pericardiocentesis. From identification of aortic perforation to the insertion of pericardial drain took approximately 4 min.

Following the above, the next treatments steps were: (1) continuous autotransfusion from the pericardial drain to the right common femoral venous sheath, (2) administration of 50 mg protamine and (3) clarification of disposition. Fortuitously, a cardiothoracic surgical team were preparing for an elective case that had yet to commence. Thus, there was recourse for the patient to be transferred to theatres for emergency surgery. Having established this, we removed the Edwards Commander delivery system and E-sheath, achieving dry haemostasis with 1x ProStyle and 1 × 8 F Angioseal (Terumo). We then transferred the patient to theatre.

The patient remained conscious with systolic blood pressure consistently > 100 mmHg, continuous autotransfusion was never interrupted. Aspiration became slower but never stopped, confirming ongoing bleeding. The patient arrived at theatre within 10 min and induction of general anaesthesia and intubation were performed within 14 min of identification of perforation. Transoesophageal echocardiography probe was then passed, showing almost no pericardial effusion. However, blood continued to be aspirated at a slow rate. Autotransfusion was continuously performed as the patient was draped and prepared for surgery.

Emergency cardiac surgery

Skin incision began within 42 min of onset of aortic perforation. A fair amount of blood was noted in the pericardial cavity after pericardiotomy was performed. There was a large haematoma around the right side of the aortic root and the upper part of the right ventricular outflow tract. The aorta and the right atrial appendage were cannulated, cardiopulmonary bypass was instituted, and the patient was cooled to 32 degrees centrigrade.

Cardioplegia was given via the ascending aorta, and then transverse aortotomy was performed. The Sapien valve was removed, it was firmly embedded in the aortic root. Two Robert’s forceps held at 180 degrees were used to distort the valve and ease the valve out. The underlying anatomy of the cusps of the aortic valve could not be appreciated with certainty, as the native valve had been crushed with the TAVI valve.

The aortic valve was excised, and thorough decalcification was performed. A large tear was observed in the annulus just below the junction of the RCC and the NCC (Fig. 3a). This tear was repaired with a pair of pledgetted 4–0 Prolene sutures (Fig. 3b and c). The valve was then sized, and we decided to insert a size 21 Magna Ease valve.

Schematic diagrams illustrating the location and repair of the annular rupture. a (top left): Tear through annulus at the junction between NCC and RCC. b (top right): Insertion of two pledgetted 4 − 0 Prolene sutures. c (bottom): Repaired defect with pledgetted suture in-situ

2 − 0 Ti-Cron sutures were then placed within the annulus in an inside-out fashion. While passing the sutures through the region of the RCC, we noticed that there was another tear a few millimetres below the ostium of the right coronary artery (RCA) (Fig. 4a). This tear was then repaired with pledgetted 5–0 Prolene sutures (Fig. 4b). We then continued inserting the valve sutures across the right coronary annulus. The sutures were placed in an inside-out fashion; the needle was inserted into the tissue just below the annulus of the RCC, and the exit point was above the repaired tear. To avoid disturbing the repair site, the needle exited at a slightly higher level in the part of the aorta between the repair site and the lower margin of the coronary ostium (as shown by the dotted line in Fig. 4c). After these bites were taken, we were concerned that the annulus of the Magna Ease valve would compromise the ostium of the RCA. We inspected the legs and were satisfied that there would be a suitable saphenous vein available to graft the RCA in case of problems with perfusion in the RCA. It would not have been possible for us to remove the sutures and place them lower.

Schematic diagrams illustrating identification and location of secondary tear relative to RCA ostium, and repair approach. a (top left): Secondary tear identified inferior to RCA ostium. b (top right): Insertion of pledgetted 5 − 0 Prolene suture. c (bottom): Dotted line indicating the route of suture for the Perimount valve across the coronary annulus, note the close association of the suture path with the RCA ostium

The sutures were then passed through the annulus of the new valve, and the valve was parachuted down. The knots were tied. The annulus of the new valve was very close to the lower rim of the right coronary ostium. However, the cessation of cardiopulmonary bypass was uneventful, and there was no right sided ischaemia noted.

Post-operative outcome

The patient was cared for post-operatively within the cardiothoracic ICU. There was an initial requirement for vasopressor support. Viscoelastic rotational thermoelectrometry was utilised to gauge haemostatic function given the significant transfusion requirement intra-operatively, and the patient required two units of fresh frozen plasma and one unit of platelets within the ICU immediately post-op.

The patient was successfully extubated and stepped down to the HDU on post-op day 2. A chest X-ray revealed bilateral pneumothorax. However, serial chest X-ray on day 3 revealed a decrease in the size of the pneumothorax; therefore, the chest drains were removed. Vasopressor support was gradually weaned. By day 4, the patient was stepped down to the ward and was successfully discharged on day 9 post-op.

At the most recent review, 2 months post-op, the patient demonstrated remarkable improvement in functional status. Ongoing complaints of exertional dyspnoea and fatigue remain, but these symptoms are subjectively improving as the patient continues to engage with cardiac rehabilitation services.

Discussion

Annular rupture – diagnosis, risk factors and management

A swift diagnosis of pericardial tamponade is essential for obtaining a favourable outcome. The presentation of haemodynamic collapse following prosthetic valve deployment should immediately raise suspicion of aortic rupture and pericardial tamponade. If uncertainty remains, urgent echocardiogram and angiography can clarify the underlying diagnosis, although obtaining these investigations must not delay definitive surgical management [18].

Smaller ruptures may present without extensive bleeding into the pericardial space; instead, they may be self-contained by haematoma and managed conservatively. These small ruptures are often underdiagnosed given the absence of clinical symptoms and are typically identified incidentally with CT angiogram post-TAVI [19]. Consideration to reverse anticoagulation may be sufficient to manage contained rupture, these patients generally have good outcomes [17]. While progression is rare, contained rupture may develop into pseudoaneurysm as a late complication [20].

Conversely, larger free ruptures with continual bleeding into the pericardial space indicate immediate and expeditious surgical management [4, 18]. Immediate percutaneous pericardiocentesis is an effective stopgap measure to limit haemodynamic compromise, thus maintaining cerebral and cardiac perfusion. The use of autotransfusion can be helpful in minimising the use of blood products, but more importantly, it provides a rapidly accessible source of blood transfusion that would otherwise be discarded [18]. The use of cardiopulmonary bypass permits a viable timeframe for surgical exploration and repair of the aortic root [13, 21].

Role of on-site cardiac surgery

In this case, the patient received optimal and timely management, from immediate identification, pericardiocentesis and autotransfusion to the quick implementation of cardiopulmonary bypass and surgical aortic repair. This rapid sequence of events would not have been possible without on-site cardiac surgery. Although there are registry data on TAVI in non-surgical centres showing similar outcomes to TAVI in surgical centres, the practice is not commonplace and these scarce observational reports are confounded [22–24].

It has been suggested that an increased latency between the onset of annular rupture and emergency cardiac surgery (ECS) may contribute to lower mortality, a factor which highlights the importance of on-site cardiac surgery in such a serious complication [25]. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that TAVIs performed in hybrid suites and operating theatres have lower mortality rates than those performed in cardiac catheterisation labs; it may be hypothesised that the reduced latency to ECS is responsible for the improved outcome - an important factor if a patient is deemed suitable for surgical escalation [25].

Bailout ECS is performed in between 1.1% and 1.2% of TAVIs [25, 26]. Common indications for ECS can include valve dislodgement, myocardial perforation, aortic dissection, annulus rupture, coronary ostium obstruction or severe aortic regurgitation. The outcome for patients who suffer complications requiring ECS is very poor. The 30-day mortality for those who require bailout ECS in TAVI for any indication is between 48.5–51.9% [25–27]. Risk factors identified for patients requiring ECS include increasing age, female sex, small body surface area, increasing ejection fraction, non-elective TAVI and the use of non-femoral access [27]. Traditionally, annular rupture has been considered the most fatal complication indicating ECS; indeed, the 30-day mortality for annular rupture post-ECS is higher than those who undergo ECS generally - between 60–100% [21, 25–27]. The mortality associated with bailout ECS for TAVI remains very high but it is not futile. The utility of ECS is demonstrated by this case report and by the mortality rates – despite the high mortality, there exists a cohort of patients who benefit.

Vascular complications arising from TAVI are frequent, more so than cardiac complications. Transfemoral TAVI is associated with a combined minor and major complication rate of between 1.9 and 30.7% [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Frequent complications include vascular access complications (such as pseudoaneurysm, dissection, AV-fistula, or haematoma); distal embolization; or the requirement endovascular intervention. Complications can be classified into major or minor depending on whether the complication was considered life-threatening, or contributed to significant morbidity (e.g. stroke, amputation) [28]. Major complications also include aortic dissection or rupture [28]. If grouped by management approach, vascular complications can be stratified into those managed conservatively, those managed with endovascular input, and those managed surgically. In those with vascular access complications such as pseudoaneurysm, AV-fistula, or haematoma – many (28–76%) are managed conservatively, or with minimal intervention such as compression for haematoma, or thrombin injection for pseudoaneurysm [29, 30]. Fewer patients are managed endovascularly with ballon angioplasty or stenting (9–52%) [30], Endovascular input is indicated for complications such as dissection, device closure failure, stenosis/occlusion or, rarely, arterial perforation.[31] Patients who require endovascular management need not be treated exclusively in sites with surgical access. Any endovascular operator such as an interventional cardiologist could facilitate this management. Only a minority of patients (15–22%) require more invasive vascular surgical management and input [29, 30].

Similarly to cardiac complications, risk stratification of vascular complications can be somewhat pre-determined by the presence of specific risk factors. Patient specific risk factors include female sex, CKD, diabetes, iliofemoral calcification, diffuse vascular disease [31, 32]. Note that there is some overlap between vascular surgery and cardiac surgery complication risk factors. This could contribute to improved stratification of patient vascular and cardiac surgery risk, further facilitating safe patient allocation/selection.

Pre-procedure vascular access investigation via MDCT is frequently utilised to ascertain access risk, specifically, anatomical risk such as iliofemoral calcification or tortuosity can be identified. Procedurally, a large-sheath-to-iliofemoral artery ratio has been associated with a 7.5-times increased risk of vascular access complication [33]. Therefore, with adequate pre-planning, risk can be mediated or minimised. Importantly, while vascular access complications are common, this does not always equate to a significant need for access to vascular surgery. Only a small minority are noted to require vascular surgery, and these individuals could be risk-stratified pre-procedurally. Patients with unfavourable femoral artery anatomy could be performed in centres with access to vascular surgery.

Inaccessibility to TAVI

Nonetheless, the benefit of accessibility to bailout ECS in complicated TAVI must be balanced with the consequences of wider inaccessibility to TAVI overall. Permitting TAVI in centres without direct access to cardiac surgery would increase accessibility to TAVI. Increased accessibility to TAVI would reduce morbidity and mortality of those on waiting lists. Patients with symptomatic aortic stenosis are likely to deteriorate quickly. The 2-year mortality for patients awaiting TAVI is 50%, underscoring the importance of minimising waiting times [34]. In a Canadian cohort with a median wait time of 80 days, there was a 2% rate of mortality and a 12% incidence of heart failure-related admissions before receiving TAVI [34]. In the event of cardiac decompensation, urgent TAVI may be performed but is associated with poorer outcomes due to decreased functional capacity, as well as a longer duration of hospital admission; hence, elective TAVI is preferable [35–37]. In England in 2019, the median wait time for TAVI was 141 days, with 299 deaths of those waiting for TAVI, and within Scotland, 38% of TAVIs were performed urgently in 2023/24 owing to decompensation [38]. Evidently, there is a detrimental impact of poor access to TAVI; however, whether the benefit of increased access to TAVI would outweigh the risk of decreased access to ECS remains undetermined.

Existing literature exploring the viability of TAVI without on-site cardiac surgery is sparse. Some preliminary observational and registry studies suggest no difference in outcome [27, 39, 40]. The TRACS trial is a randomised control trial that aims to elucidate the differences in outcomes between TAVI patients managed with on-site cardiac surgery and those who could be offered TAVI without on-site cardiac surgery availability [41]. Proponents argue that the risk of developing complications requiring ECS is minimal and decreasing, and for those who require ECS, the outcome is poor; therefore, the use of TAVI is being limited because of a relatively small cohort of patients who benefit from on-site cardiac surgery. Furthermore, many patients undergoing TAVI are determined to be unsuitable for escalation to ECS pre-procedurally; thus, the presence or absence of cardiac surgery is inconsequential in these patients. One could foresee a scenario in which patients deemed not fit for surgery pre-procedurally could receive TAVI in centres without cardiac surgery. Whereas patients who are fit for surgery and are at relatively greater risk of developing complications could be treated at centres with on-site cardiac surgery.

Unfortunately, those patients who are deemed undesirable candidates for surgery typically have a higher likelihood of being anatomically complex and are therefore more prone to complications. This is because the conditions that encourage the development of complex anatomy in TAVI overlap with conditions that significantly impact physiological reserve – for example, excessive atherosclerosis, diabetes and CKD, which can encourage dense calcification development [13]. Patients with more risk factors are multimorbid, however, the fact that patients with multimorbid risk factors are more prone to complication is not necessarily impactful – this cohort of multimorbid patients are less suitable for surgery, regardless of their propensity for complication. Whilst multimorbid patients are more likely to have complications that indicate bailout ECS, this does not signify that they are likely to survive ECS. If patients are adequately counselled and consented on the procedural risk of TAVI in a centre without surgical services and are made aware of the adverse outcomes associated with delay in TAVI to await surgical access, then it is appropriate to offer patients this option. It becomes most pertinent when the decision to delay is not likely to be of benefit in major cardiac complication due to the patients’ unsuitability for cardiac surgery anyway. In relation to minor complications (which are frequently managed without cardiac or vascular surgery), the decision to perform TAVI with/without on-site cardiac or vascular surgery could be discussed and agreed with patients on an individual case-by-case basis, in keeping with shared-decision making principles.

This case represents the optimal patient for provision of TAVI at a surgical centre – an individual with symptomatic aortic stenosis, and good functional status despite complex calcified aortic valve anatomy. Patients who have been deemed too frail for surgery, despite probably having complex anatomy, would not be appropriate for TAVI at a surgical site.

Finally, the expansion of TAVI is predicated upon all centres and operators performing an adequate volume of procedures to develop and maintain expertise; TAVI, as with all medical procedures, has a clear case volume-outcome association [42]. Multiple, large observational studies have demonstrated a learning curve effect [42, 43]. Those who have completed less TAVIs (less than 225 cases) have a 2- to 4-fold higher 30-day mortality than those who have completed over 300 cases – however, given the exponential relationship of the learning curve, it is suggested that expertise begins to plateau after only as few as 30 cases [43]. Whilst mortality is noted to be worse in those with lower experience, the gains obtained with each rise in experience are progressively reduced, suggesting that whilst the learning-experience curve is steep, operators need only surmount a relatively small number of cases before becoming much more proficient and safe [43].

The expansion of TAVI to new operators and centres must be performed safely. However, the pragmatic response to a rising demand in TAVI is the expansion of TAVI. It is unreasonable to limit the ever-growing demand due to the hurdle of transiently reduced operator experience, to the contrary, more operators should be trained to meet this demand – with the caveat being that we must ensure those operators adhere to a minimum case-volume criteria. At present, the British Cardiovascular Intervention Society (BCIS) recommends a minimum of 100 cases per year in established centres and 50 cases per year in new centres [44]. This would surmount the recognised zone of significant risk, whereby centres with less than 50 cases per year are noted to have a 2.7-times higher 30-day mortality. This guidance, if implemented properly to novel centres, would aim to overcome the steepest phase of the learning curve [43].

Presuming that distribution of TAVI is gradually expanded, with adequate training of new operators, then any detriment on outcome will be transient and improve with increased uptake in new centres. This effect can be observed in locations where TAVI is already offered without on-site cardiac surgery, such as Germany, where minimal difference in outcomes is demonstrated [24].

Ultimately, research on this topic is insufficient, and no decision can be made until such data are available. At present, UK guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the BCIS still dictates the presence of on-site cardiac surgery as a requirement for centres performing TAVI [45, 46].

Conclusion

This report presents a case of annular rupture during TAVI that was salvaged by immediate management and bailout ECS. The patient was at increased risk of annular rupture due to calcified bicuspid aortic valve anatomy and was deemed a suitable candidate for emergency cardiac surgery due to her functional status. These factors - risk of a complication that might be salvaged by emergency cardiac surgery, and patient suitability for emergency cardiac surgery - are essential considerations if TAVI in non-surgical centres is to become more commonplace. The optimal patient for completion in a centre with cardiac surgery is one with good physiological reserve, but complex anatomy prone to complication – as exemplified in this case. If we are to begin to offer TAVI without on-site surgical services, we must be cautious in our patient selection. Cases such as this presents an example of those which should be closely considered for escalation, whereas others may be appropriate for completion without cardiac surgery availability. If permitted, this could ease the burden of morbidity and mortality when awaiting TAVI.
