A 5-week low-dose prednisolone regimen for progressive vitiligo: efficacy and safety in a single-center cohort study
Authors: Yue-Tong Qian, Jia-Wei Liu, Dong-Lai Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProgressive vitiligo lacks standardized glucocorticoid regimens, with prolonged treatments often causing adverse effects. This study evaluated a novel 5-week low-dose prednisolone strategy (0.5 mg/kg/day, tapered weekly) to balance efficacy and safety.ObjectiveTo assess the efficacy and safety of this regimen in halting disease progression and inducing repigmentation in progressive vitiligo.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study (January 2017 to January 2022), 319 progressive vitiligo patients (Vitiligo Disease Activity score, VIDA score ≥ +2) received the 5-week regimen. Outcomes included treatment efficacy (cessation of progression ± repigmentation), adverse events, and patient satisfaction (visual analog scale, VAS).ResultsThe efficacy rate was 88.71% (283/319), with 63.60% (180/283) achieving repigmentation (grade 1: 56.7%, grade 2: 25.6%, grade 3: 11.1%, grade 4: 6.7%). Disease severity significantly correlated with treatment response (p = 0.013). Mild-to-moderate adverse events occurred in 14.1% (45/319), including weight gain (8.2%), insomnia (3.1%), and gastrointestinal symptoms (2.8%), all resolving post-treatment. Patient satisfaction was high (mean VAS: 8.88/10; 65% scored 10/10).ConclusionThis 5-week low-dose prednisolone regimen rapidly stabilizes progressive vitiligo across ages and subtypes, with high efficacy, minimal adverse effects, and excellent patient compliance. It offers a practical alternative to prolonged steroid protocols, particularly for early intervention.Keywords: Progressive vitiligo, low-dose prednisolone treatment, efficacy, safety, patient satisfaction

Introduction

Vitiligo is an acquired autoimmune skin disorder resulting from progressive destruction of epidermal melanocytes, leading to the formation of depigmented skin patches. This condition affects approximately 0.5%–2.0% of the population [1,2]. The visual phenotype and recalcitrant treatment have a major impact on patients’ quality of life, often resulting in psychological distress [3]. Our previous research has identified the prevalence of vitiligo-related post-traumatic stress among patients, which can inflict severe psychological harm [4]. While the precise etiology of vitiligo is still unknown, growing evidence suggests that autoimmune mechanisms play a pivotal role in its onset and progression [2,5]. There is currently no universally accepted treatment for vitiligo. Available therapies aim primarily at stopping its progression and promoting the repigmentation of affected areas. Glucocorticoids (GCs) are potent immunosuppressive agents that operate by reducing the activity and volume of the lymphatic system and diminishing the concentrations of immunoglobulins and complement components. It has been proved that GCs can lower T lymphocyte activity, suppress B cell antibody responses and inhibit the production of various cytokines in vitiligo patients [6]. Consequently, systemic steroids have been recommended for the management of rapidly spreading vitiligo, which can sometimes result in varying degrees of repigmentation. However, progressive vitiligo lacks standardized glucocorticoid regimens, with prolonged treatments often causing adverse effects. In addition, although Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors have shown efficacy in vitiligo treatment, their off-label use is limited by safety concerns and high costs. Systemic glucocorticoids remain a cornerstone due to their widespread availability, low cost, and rapid efficacy. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a more effective and safe glucocorticoids treatment for progressive vitiligo. Preliminary observations from our institution indicated that a 5-week low-dose oral prednisolone regimen (0.5 mg/kg/day, weekly tapering) achieved disease stabilization in progressive vitiligo with minimal adverse events. To validate these findings in a larger, well-characterized cohort, we conducted a single-center retrospective analysis of patients treated under this protocol over a two-year period.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted in the dermatology outpatient clinic of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, a tertiary referral center, between January 2017 and January 2022. The protocol adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. S-K 1945) in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians (for minors <18 years).

Patients

All patients were clinically diagnosed as progressive vitiligo, which was clinically confirmed by two independent dermatologists using Wood’s lamp examination and Vitiligo Disease Activity (VIDA) score ≥ +2 (indicating active disease within 6 months, including new lesions, lesion expansion, or Koebner phenomenon). Only patients who could access the clinical records from baseline to follow-up and full-body photos (Canon G12, fixed illumination/background) could be enrolled. Children were defined as individuals aged <18 years.

Exclusion criteria: (1) recent use of glucocorticoids/immunosuppressants (within 3 months); (2) contraindications for systemic steroids (e.g. uncontrolled diabetes and osteoporosis); (3) active infections, malignancies, or confounding dermatoses; (4) Cognitive impairment affecting compliance.

Procedure

Dosing regimen: Patients received oral prednisolone at 0.5 mg/kg/day (max 30 mg/day for 60 kg adults), tapered weekly over 5 weeks. Specifically, for adults weighing 60 kg, the dosage starts at 30 mg/day and is adjusted every 7 days thereafter, reducing the dosage by 20, 15, 10, and 5 mg/day within 35 days.

Adjunctive therapy: Topical glucocorticoids (clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment) were applied to truncal/extremity lesions, while calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.1% ointment) were used for face/neck/genital areas.

Data collection: Baseline demographics, disease severity (graded by body surface area involvement: Grades 1–4), VIDA scores, and comorbidities were recorded. Standardized digital photography documented depigmented lesions at baseline and follow-ups (5 weeks, 2/4/6 months). All relevant drug adverse effects were thoroughly reported. Safety was assessed by monitoring and recording all adverse events throughout the study. Patients’ satisfaction was evaluated at 12 weeks.

During patient consultations, we systematically collected data on potential precipitating factors through structured interviews, beginning with a screening question: ‘Did you experience any identifiable events possibly associated with the onset or progression of vitiligo lesions within 6 months prior to their initial appearance?’ For affirmative responses, we conducted domain-specific assessments: (1) Psychological stress was evaluated by inquiring about major life events (e.g. job loss and bereavement) and chronic anxiety/depression using the Perceived Stress Scale; (2) Sleep disturbances were quantified by documenting frequency of late bedtimes (>11:30 PM) and insomnia episodes; (3) Skin trauma histories included surgical procedures, accidental injuries, and comorbid dermatoses (e.g. eczema and contact dermatitis); (4) Sun exposure patterns were assessed through recalled duration of acute sunburns and occupational/recreational UV exposure; and (5) Dietary imbalances were identified via dietary recalls focusing on restrictive eating and micronutrient deficiencies.

Outcome definitions: During these visits, the lesions were evaluated by wood lamp and comparison of clinical photographs from the previous visit. The treatment-effective group was defined by the complete cessation of development of all pre-existing lesions with no new lesions, with or without repigmentation of depigmented patches after each course of therapy. Treatment efficacy (%) = (Number of responders/Total patients) × 100%. Conversely, the treatment-resistant group was characterized by either the progression of pre-existing depigmented areas or the emergence of new lesions after each course of therapy.

Repigmentation evaluation

In accordance with the Chinese consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of Vitiligo (2024 edition) [7], vitiligo severity was classified into 4 grades: Grade 1: Mild – Involving less than 1% of the body surface area (BSA) with vitiligo lesions. Grade 2: Moderate – Involving depigmented area spanning 1%–5% of the BSA. Grade 3: Moderate to severe – Encompassing depigmented area covering 6%–50% of the BSA. Grade 4: Severe – Characterized by depigmented area exceeding 50% of the BSA.

Treatment response was evaluated based on the extent of repigmentation and categorized as follows: marked to excellent (76%–100% repigmentation), moderate (51%–75% repigmentation), fair (26%–50% repigmentation), poor (1%–25% repigmentation), and no response (no repigmentation observed).

Adverse event monitoring

Common adverse reactions, including weight gain, increased appetite, acne, gastric upset, bone pain, insomnia, and menstrual disorders, were documented, and their frequencies were recorded. Any other unlisted adverse effects were also documented directly. Mild adverse reactions were defined as causing slight discomfort but not significantly affecting daily life, and patients could endure them. Moderate adverse reactions were defined as causing discomfort that impacts daily life but can still be tolerated. Severe adverse reactions were defined as those significantly affecting daily life, difficult to tolerate, and necessitating treatment discontinuation.

Patient satisfaction assessment

Satisfaction of patients was assessed by a visual analog scale (VAS). Scale reading ranges from 0 to 10, indicating a personally perceived perception from not satisfied at all to extremely satisfied.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as their mean ± standard deviation (SD), and compared using the t-test. Counting data were demonstrated as percentage and compared with the χ2-test. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant difference statistically. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 13 statistical software and GraphPad Prism software.

Result

Clinical characters

The cohort comprised 319 patients (male:female = 160:159; adults:children = 192:127) with progressive vitiligo. The average duration of the disease was 3.07 ± 5.40 years, ranging from 1 week to 40 years. Disease subtypes included non-segmental (83.9%, 268/319) and segmental (16.1%, 51/319). Severity distribution was Grade 1 (58.0%, 185/319), Grade 2 (31.0%, 99/319), and Grade 3 (11.0%, 35/319). Patients with grade 4 were not included. 36 patients (11.29%) reported a family history of vitiligo. Common comorbidities were autoimmune thyroid disorders (4.7%, 15/319). Precipitating factors were reported by 133 patients, with 101 reporting one precipitating factor and 32 reporting two or more. The most frequently reported factors included emotional issues (22.3%, 71/319), irregular sleep patterns (insomnia, staying up late) (15.4%, 49/319), trauma, surgery, or secondary skin conditions (skin allergies and dermatitis) (7.2%, 23/319), sun exposure (5.0%, 16/319), and dietary imbalances (2.5%, 8/319).

5-week low-dose prednisolone regimen has minimal adverse effects and excellent patient compliance

Adverse events occurred in 14.1% (45/319). 37 patients (11.6%) reported mild adverse reactions, 8 patients (2.5%) reported moderate adverse reactions, and no serious adverse reactions were reported. Common adverse reactions included alterations in appetite, fluctuations in weight, gastrointestinal symptoms, bone pain, and muscle cramps. Additionally, isolated cases reported other adverse effects such as menstrual disorders, facial flushing, edema, increased blood pressure, dizziness, hand tremors, and memory loss. All adverse reactions completely resolved following the discontinuation of glucocorticoid therapy. Patient satisfaction was high (mean VAS = 8.88/10; 65% scored 10/10).

Discussion

Vitiligo is a depigmented disorder that can impact individuals of all ages and genders, which could be categorized into segmental and non-segmental types based on affected sites. Notably, a study involving 3742 Chinese vitiligo patients revealed that approximately 74% initially presented with focal vitiligo as their primary manifestation, with over half of these cases (54.7%) eventually progressing to generalized depigmented areas, possibly transforming into other clinical subtypes [8]. Consequently, aggressive and early intervention in progressive vitiligo is needed to stabilize the disease as soon as possible and minimize the extent of skin involvement. However, due to the complex pathogenesis of vitiligo, treatment remains a challenging endeavor. In recent years, various therapeutic modalities have emerged, encompassing phototherapy, topical drug therapy, physical therapy, surgical treatments and systemic drug therapy [9–12]. Nevertheless, the efficacy and side effect profiles of these regimens continue to elicit debate, particularly in the context of diverse patient populations, vitiligo subtypes, and disease stages [12].

For decades, systemic glucocorticoids have been recommended for progressive vitiligo treatment, effectively halting lesion development, modulating subtype conversion, and improving prognosis [12,13]. Several glucocorticoids treatment protocols had been proposed in the literature without consensus on the optimal course and dosage. Oral low-dose pulse therapy (OMP), entailing the oral administration of betamethasone or dexamethasone (5 mg) twice weekly, with treatment cessation upon full repigmentation or no new white spots for at least 4 months or a maximum medication duration of 24 weeks. This method has shown an effective rate of 88%–89% within 1-4 months in various studies [13–16]. Another regimen involves small dose reduction therapy, initiating with continuous oral prednisolone at 0.3 mg/kg/day for 2 months, followed by monthly dose reduction until cessation after 5 months, with a total effective rate of 87.7% [17]. Some studies have modified this approach, starting with oral methylprednisolone at 0.24 mg/kg/d, doubling the dose for the first 3 days, halving it monthly, and discontinuing at 3 months, achieving an effective rate of 93.0%. The use of other forms of glucocorticoids for the treatment of progressive vitiligo has also been reported. One study reported an 82.3% effective rate in adults and children who received three monthly diproterone injections (1.0 mL for adults, 0.015 mL/kg for children) [18]. In different investigations, a 3-day continuous intravenous injection of 500 mg/day of methylprednisolone (8 mg/kg/day in children) resulted in increased repigmentation areas in 56-86% of progressive vitiligo patients [19,20]. However, these treatment regimens, while capable of arresting advanced vitiligo, have limitations including small sample sizes, prominent adverse reactions, extended treatment durations, complex dosing schedules, frequent dose adjustments, demanding patient compliance, and variable treatment efficacy.

The adverse effects of systemic glucocorticoids are dose- and duration-dependent. Dose-dependent reactions include elevated blood glucose, peptic ulcers, hyperlipidemia, and psychiatric symptoms, while duration-dependent changes encompass hypertension, osteoporosis, Cushing’s syndrome, growth disturbances, opportunistic infections, and osteonecrosis [12]. Intramuscular glucocorticoids injections are often poorly tolerated due to pain, and they have relative contraindications in children, such as muscle atrophy. Furthermore, the accuracy of drug strength and dosage control during the absorption process poses monitoring challenges [12]. Hence, the quest for a treatment plan that combines high effectiveness, minimal adverse reactions, excellent patient compliance, and widespread applicability remains pivotal. Our findings align with this trend, supporting shorter courses as a viable strategy to mitigate steroid toxicity without compromising outcomes.

In our study, we modified the existing treatment paradigm by employing low-dose prednisolone administered over 5 weeks to rapidly control the disease while minimizing dose- and duration-dependent steroid adverse reactions. Adjunctive topicals were uniformly administered to eliminate site-specific confounding. Their negligible systemic absorption ensures observed outcomes reflect prednisolone’s effects. Our study revealed an 88.71% halting of disease progression in progressive vitiligo patients, consistent with previous treatments’ efficacy. Statistical analysis demonstrated no significant differences in treatment responses based on patient age, disease duration, or vitiligo type. A significant correlation was observed between treatment response and disease severity, which suggests that patients with extensive vitiligo may require prolonged therapy beyond 5 weeks to achieve stabilization. Rather than dose escalation—which heightens risks of hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, and Cushingoid effects—we recommend a repeat 5-week low-dose course (0.5 mg/kg/day, tapered weekly) for suboptimal responders. This strategy balances efficacy and safety, as evidenced by 76.5% stabilization upon retreatment. During our follow-up, four patients continued to experience disease progression despite two rounds of 5-week short-term low-dose prednisolone therapy. This result aligns with prior reports of 8.2%–56.2% incomplete lesion inhibition, suggest that a subset of patients may exhibit resistance to steroid therapy, necessitating further investigation into the underlying mechanisms [15,20,21]. For such cases, alternative immunosuppressants (e.g. methotrexate or JAK inhibitors) should be considered [22,23].

While our 5-week prednisolone monotherapy effectively stabilizes progressive vitiligo, combining it with phototherapy may synergistically enhance repigmentation through complementary immunomodulatory and melanogenic mechanisms [24]. Prednisolone rapidly reduces T lymphocyte activity, suppress B cell antibody responses and inhibits the production of diverse cytokines in vitiligo patients [6]. Phototherapy could stimulate melanocyte migration and proliferation [25]. Previous studies have incorporated narrowband-ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) or psoralen ultraviolet A (PUVA) therapy to enhance skin lesion repigmentation rates [26,27]. A study revealed that OMP and NB-UVB phototherapy could significantly reduce VASI score [28]. Another study found patients who received a combination of OMP, NB-UVB, and clobetasol cream 0.05% accelerated lesion stabilization by 37% compared to phototherapy/topicals alone [29]. In our study, 63.60% of patients who responded to treatment exhibited varying degrees of repigmentation. A pragmatic approach involves initiating NB-UVB after completing 5-week oral prednisolone regimen with twice-weekly sessions for 3–6 months may accelerate the speed of repigmentation. Future clinical trials should validate this sequential protocol in progressive vitiligo.

Furthermore, 41.69% of patients reported at least one triggering factor preceding disease exacerbation, with mental and psychological factors and factors inducing Koebner’s phenomenon being the most common. Thus, clinicians should advise patients to adopt relaxation techniques, maintain regular work-rest schedules, consume balanced diets, minimize sun exposure, and avoid trauma in daily life. When necessary, appropriate psychological interventions can be implemented.

Regarding safety, 14.1% of patients reported mild to moderate adverse reactions, all of which resolved upon discontinuation of glucocorticoid therapy. Long-term adverse reactions were not observed, indicating a favorable safety profile. Patients expressed high satisfaction with this treatment approach.

This retrospective single-center study provides clinically relevant insights. However, several limitations still warrant consideration. First, inherent selection bias may arise from the non-randomized design. To mitigate this, we rigorously adhered to STROBE guidelines by implementing predefined inclusion criteria. However, the absence of Vitiligo Area Scoring Index (VASI) quantification represents a methodological constraint, potentially limiting granularity in repigmentation analysis. In addition, more detailed studies are needed to illustrate the differences in the repigmentation rate of different anatomical sites. No Grade 4 patients were ultimately enrolled due to systematic data limitations. Future prospective studies should implement dedicated enrollment protocols for Grade 4 cohorts to establish steroid responsiveness thresholds.

Emerging data on JAK inhibitors show potentials to treat vitiligo, but with higher costs and infection risks. Prospective randomized trials, ideally comparing 5-week prednisolone against OMP or JAK inhibitors, are warranted. While our regimen remains a cost-effective first-line option, JAK inhibitors may bridge the gap for steroid-resistant subsets. While these findings highlight this regimen as a promising therapy for progressive vitiligo, further high-quality random control trials with larger sample sizes in multiple-centers, standardized treatment protocols, and long-term follow-up are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a 5-week low-dose prednisolone regimen (0.5 mg/kg/day, tapered weekly) effectively halts disease progression in progressive vitiligo, achieving an 88.7% efficacy rate with 63.6% repigmentation, while maintaining a favorable safety profile (14.1% mild-moderate adverse events, fully reversible post-treatment). The regimen’s short duration minimizes cumulative steroid exposure, addressing key limitations of prolonged protocols such as OMP, which require months of treatment with comparable efficacy but higher adverse risks. Notably, disease severity correlated with treatment response (p = 0.013), suggesting extended courses may benefit extensive cases. High patient satisfaction (mean VAS = 8.88/10) and applicability across ages/subtypes, including pediatric populations (127 children), underscore its clinical versatility. While JAK inhibitors show promise for refractory cases, their cost and safety concerns position this regimen as a cost-effective first-line option.
