Quantifying the trade-off between spring phenology and lethal frost risk: a meta-analysis
Authors: Zhengjie Yan, Cheng Chen, Yue Liu, Yujiang Li, Huiying Liu, Hao Wang, Tao Yan, Xin Jing, Shuai Ren, Hongbiao Zi, Yan Shi, Tao Wang, Jin-Sheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe timing of spring phenology represents a critical trade-off between growing season extension and frost risk avoidance, the balance of which governs plant fitness and shapes plant species’ distributions. However, few quantitative assessments exist, indicating that this trade-off is largely untested empirically. Here, we present a global meta-analysis encompassing 193 plant species across 126 study sites, finding consistently high freezing resistance (to −12 °C) with remarkably low lethal frost risk (safety margin of 17 °C) during spring emergence. Under projected climate warming scenarios, predictions indicate that advances in spring phenology do not affect the lethal frost risk under light and moderate warming scenarios. This is likely due to the reduced temperature sensitivity, consistent with trade-off predictions. By contrast, in the high-warming scenario, the freezing safety margin is predicted to expand (by ~1.7 °C), resulting in a lower lethal frost risk. This expansion may compensate for the diminished freezing resistance predicted under extreme warming. Our findings challenge the conventional view that simple environmental factors moderate spring phenology, and highlight the necessity of incorporating biotic factors (e.g., freezing resistance) and biotic processes (e.g., early growth versus frost risk trade-offs) into next-generation phenological models to enhance prediction accuracy under climate change.Subject terms: Phenology, Evolutionary ecology

Introduction

Plant phenology, or the timing of recurrent biological events during the year, plays a pivotal role in shaping species distributions, regulating water and nutrient cycles1–3. In recent decades, advanced spring phenology with warmer springs is widely reported4,5. However, it remains unclear whether this phenological advance, accompanied by warmer temperatures, alters the risk of lethal frost (LFR). Theoretically, spring phenology represents an adaptive compromise between maximizing the growing season and avoiding lethal frost damage, which reflecting an evolutionary adaptation shaped by long-term selection pressures6–10. The timing of spring phenology under climate warming arises from adaptive trade-offs between two competing phenological strategies. Emerging earlier provides a competitive advantage by extending the photosynthetic period, maximizing light-use efficiency and photosynthetic activity11,12, reducing herbivore/pathogen pressure13,14, and providing priority access to ecological niches15, all of which may enhance plant fitness and reproductive success16. On the other hand, potentially lethal spring frost events favor delayed emergence, which protect vulnerable tissues17 and enabling greater resource allocation to growth and reproduction instead of frost tolerance18,19 (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 1). Ultimately, natural selection favors a balance between these two opposing pressures, the benefits of early emergence versus the risk of lethal frost events, which allows individual species to optimize their spring phenology based on local environmental conditions. This evolutionary balance, known as the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS), provides a theoretical framework for predicting plant phenological responses to a changing climate.

Specifically, this balance manifests as a buffering mechanism: safeguarding against lethal frost damage during spring phenological stages while simultaneously maximizing individual fitness. Such trade-offs broadly shape plant evolutionary strategies and explain spring phenological variation across species and regions18,20. Therefore, the optimal phenological strategy for plants under climate change is to maintain this evolutionary balance. Lenz et al.9 propose a metric termed the ‘safety margin’, defined as the difference between the daily minimum temperature and freezing resistance during spring emergence, which operationalizes and quantifies this evolutionary balance (Fig. 1a). The key advantage of this dynamic metric lies in its incorporation of biotic factors (e.g., plant freezing resistance), rather than relying solely on abiotic factors21–25. In fact, plants must cope with stochastic freeze events throughout the year6. The capacity to withstand such events, termed freezing resistance, peaks during deep physiological dormancy in winter and declines sharply with spring leaf emergence7. Consequently, when both biotic and abiotic factors are considered simultaneously, a larger safety margin indicates greater tolerance of freezing (and lower frost risk), while narrower margins correspond to greater vulnerability (Fig. 1a).

Climate-change-induced shifts in spring phenology may fundamentally alter this risk-reward trade-offs in natural plant populations22,26, leading to three potential distinct risk scenarios (Fig. 1b). In the high-risk scenario, earlier springs confer transient competitive advantages, but a single extreme climatic event beyond a critical thermal threshold could result in local extinction7 (Fig. 1b). The low-risk scenario allows for near-complete frost avoidance, but comes at the cost of a competitive disadvantage that may lead to replacement by other aggressive species (Fig. 1b). The stable-risk scenario, which best aligns with the EES, illustrates the fundamental trade-off between advancing growth and avoiding lethal frost damage (Fig. 1b). However, empirical data supporting the existence of a spring phenology risk-reward trade-offs under warming conditions remain scarce, rendering this theoretical framework highly speculative.

In this study, we investigate (i) plant spring freezing resistance and associated safety margin, as well as their environmental drivers; (ii) how warming-induced spring phenological shifts may modify risk-reward trade-offs. To this end, we perform a global meta-analysis encompassing 193 plant species belonging to 336 orders, 54 families, and 122 genera across 126 sites (Fig. 2a, c). Data are collected from 88 peer-reviewed articles (Supplementary Data 1) published before September 1st, 2024 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The meta-analysis quantifies lethal frost risk during spring emergence and identifies key environmental drivers predicting freezing resistance and the safety margin. We also implement eight process-based phenological models to evaluate the trade-off between early emergence and frost risk under climate warming, as projected by three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). We show that biotic factors and the trade-off mechanisms play a critical role in regulating spring phenology.

Results

Low lethal frost risk during the spring phenology

Overall, study species exhibited strong spring freezing resistance (−12.42 °C ± 5.19 °C, mean value ± SD) relative to the daily minimum temperature during spring phenology (Fig. 2d), resulted in wide safety margin (17.14 °C ± 5.58 °C) which ensured an low lethal frost risk (Fig. 2e). The observed patterns of both freezing resistance (K = 0.059, p = 0.144) and safety margin (K = 0.048, p = 0.409) did not differ significantly from the null expectation under a Brownian motion model, indicating that the influence of phylogenetic relatedness did not differ from what would be expected by chance (Fig. 2d, e). Furthermore, convergent cross mapping (CCM) analysis identified a strong mutual causality between freezing resistance and safety margin, providing evidence for a fundamental adaptive trade-off within plants, balancing cold hardiness against growth opportunity (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 2).

Across studies, after accounting for potential sample effects (e.g., smaller n in shrubland, n = 17), plant species within the same ecosystem showed similar levels of freezing resistance and safety margin (Fig. 3). Neither freezing resistance nor the safety margin varied significantly among different life forms (i.e., trees, herbs, and shrubs) or across life cycles (i.e., perennial and annual species). However, early-spring species showed higher freezing resistance than medium-spring species, while medium-spring species exhibited a lower safety margin than both early- and late-spring species (Fig. 3). Surprisingly, freezing resistance estimates were sensitive to methodological variation. Values from differential thermal analysis (DTA) were systematically overestimated, leading to a consequent overestimation of the safety margin (Fig. 3). However, after excluding these observations, we found our results remain unchanged (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4).

Environmental and ecological drivers

For freezing resistance, random forest modeling identified the three most important predictors as annual maximum temperature (Tmax) (24.34%, p = 0.002), diurnal temperature range (DTR) (21.62%), and mean annual temperature (Tmean) (17.62%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4a). It is worth noting, that the relationship between freezing resistance and DTR (slope = 0.16, p = 0.33) was not significant in the linear mixed model analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Absolute latitude (slope = -0.06, p < 0.001) and frost day frequency (FRS) (slope = −0.02, p = 0.01) exhibit strong and moderate negative correlations, respectively. Daily minimum temperature during phenology (Phe-Tmin) (slope = 0.37, p < 0.001), Tmean (slope = 0.34, p < 0.001), Tmax (slope = 0.35, p = 0.002), evapotranspiration (Eva) (slope = 0.01, p < 0.001), solar radiation (slope = 1.18, p < 0.001) and sunshine duration (Ssh) (slope = 0.01, p < 0.001) show strong positive correlations, while Tmin (slope = 0.14, p = 0.01) showed a moderate positive correlation (Fig. 4b–f, j). For forest-inhabiting species, the most significant predictors of freezing resistance were Tmax, DTR, and Tmean, whereas for grassland species, precipitation and elevation were most influential (Supplementary Table 4). Freezing resistance in early spring species were more dependent on solar radiation and water availability, while mid- and late-spring species relied more on Tmax (Supplementary Table 4).

Spring phenology and safety margin under future warming scenarios

Based on projected future climatic data from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) (Supplementary Fig. 6), we compared spring phenology predictions (2020-2090) from eight models (Supplementary Fig. 7). Predictions were generally consistent across the models (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). Given this agreement, we generated an ensemble by averaging the outputs of all eight models. The results showed climate warming significantly advanced spring phenology, at a rate of −7.14, −6.82, and −5.71 days per degree Celsius under the low-, medium-, and high-warming scenarios, respectively (Fig. 6a). To account for these shifts when calculating the freezing risk, we extracted projected daily minimum temperature data from CMIP6 corresponding to the updated onset timings of spring phenology. Regarding future freezing resistance, numerous studies have demonstrated that warming leads to reduced chilling accumulation, which may impair freezing resistance8,19,27–29. However, the actual impact depends on the future climate scenarios30, data sources, and the chilling model used31. Therefore, we projected chilling accumulation for 2021–2099 using four climate scenarios and four process-based chilling models. Most models indicate that chilling accumulation was not always diminished under warming, however, half of the results show a strong decline only under the SSP5-8.5 scenario (Supplementary Fig. 10). Additionally, our in situ observations show no significant change in the freezing resistance of the 15 perennial herbs under moderate warming (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12, see methods). Despite the inherent methodological limitations of such manipulative studies, our results suggest that plant freezing resistance may remain stable at least two warming scenarios. Subsequently, we calculated the future safety margin using the same methods described previously (Supplementary Fig. 14, see methods). Interestingly, under both the low- and medium-warming scenarios, the safety margin predictions remained stable (p = 0.51 and 0.11, respectively; Fig. 6b), which aligns with the stable-risk scenario outlined in Fig. 1b. In contrast, under the high-warming scenario, the safety margin increased significantly, even though spring phenology exhibited the lowest temperature sensitivity (Fig. 6a). To address potential methodological biases from differential thermal analysis (DTA), we repeated all analyses after excluding DTA-derived observations. The similarity of the results reinforced confidence in our findings (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Discussion

Quantifying how the lethal frost risk varies with phenological shifts is critical for accurately predicting future spring phenology under climate warming. Although recent studies have highlighted the complex relationship between phenology and freezing events24,25, quantifying how spring phenology responds to frost risk dynamics remains challenging. Our global meta-analysis offers fresh insights into how phenological strategies balance the potential growth benefits of early emergence with the increased risk of lethal frost. We highlighted two key findings that enhance our understanding of how plant spring phenology may respond to climate change. First, we found that plants sustain enhanced freezing resistance during the spring emergence, resulting in an exceptionally low lethal frost risk, which may have been driven by the stress of extreme events. Second, although spring phenology advanced markedly under future warming, the lethal frost risk did not increase in any scenarios. These findings challenge the prevailing consensus that earlier spring phenology inevitably heightens lethal frost risk22,23,26.

We found that Tmax, DTR, and Tmin were key predictors of freezing resistance; however, the linear mixed model showed no significant relationship with DTR. This discrepancy is likely because distinct freezing-resistance strategies (e.g., freezing avoidance versus tolerance) entail different biochemical requirements6. Variations in methods used to assess freezing resistance can lead to inconsistent results. For instance, in differential thermal analysis (DTA), the rapid biophysical changes during metastable equilibrium may compromise measurement reliability32. This underscores the need for further research to validate and reconcile these methodological discrepancies.

The correlation between freezing resistance and various climatic factors, including temperature, evapotranspiration, and solar radiation, fits within an eco-physiological framework. This framework posits that the development of freezing resistance is shaped by the interplay of intrinsic physiological constraints and extrinsic ecological constraints. In terms of intrinsic factors, plant biochemistry, particularly the concentrations of non-structural carbohydrates (NSCs), such as antifreeze proteins (AFPs) and soluble sugars, is essential for freezing resistance. Notably, NSC concentrations in plant tissues are higher in colder regions33 and also in areas with greater diurnal temperature variation34. This physiological pattern offers a mechanistic explanation for why both Tmean and DTR emerged as strong predictors of freezing resistance in our analysis19,35. In addition, plant species inhabiting colder regions and those with earlier leaf-out dates typically experience more severe environmental conditions, necessitating greater freezing resistance7,22. This may explain the observed positive correlations between freezing resistance and both Tmin and Phe-Tmin. Extrinsic factors influence freezing resistance primarily through cold acclimation and de-acclimation processes. For example, non-lethal low temperatures promote cold acclimation and enhance freezing resistance, whereas warmer spells trigger de-acclimation, reducing freezing resistance6,36. This explains why Tmax was identified as a key predictor of freezing resistance in our study. We also observed positive correlations between freezing resistance and both evapotranspiration and solar radiation. The evapotranspiration relationship likely reflects a physiological trade-off, higher transpiration rates can increase tissue water content and metabolic activity, thereby reducing freezing resistance19. Similarly, under high light conditions, plants preferentially allocate more resources to growth versus defense mechanisms37,38, potentially explaining the negative effect of solar radiation on freezing resistance.

The above discussion outlines the predictors of freezing resistance identified in our global meta-analysis, as well as the relative importance of these predictors and underlying mechanisms. Our findings align with established eco-physiological principles concerning freezing resistance (Supplementary Table 6). Interestingly, we found that the relative importance of predictors varied across both ecosystem types and species with different phenological strategies. Among forest-dwelling species, solar radiation was a stronger predictor of freezing resistance than Tmax, likely due to their enhanced capacity for NSC synthesis and storage39. Conversely, grassland herbs face greater water limitations40, particularly at higher elevations, their shallow root systems must contend with frozen spring soils, which exacerbates hydrological constraints41 and impedes the development of freezing resistance. Plant phenological strategy further moderates these adaptive responses. Early-spring emerging species with shallow root systems must acquire resources (e.g., solar radiation and water) before canopy closure11,13,42,43. In contrast, mid- and late-season species, which preferentially invest in nutrient stores, rely on high storage capacity that is determined by peak photosynthetic activity, a process regulated by Tmax9,13.

Our meta-analysis demonstrates that enhanced freezing resistance significantly reduces lethal frost risk (Fig. 5b). This indicates that freezing tolerance is a primary mechanism for withstanding spring frost, as opposed to simple phenological escape11,13,42. Furthermore, we identified Tmean, Phe-Tmin, and FRS as key determinants of the freezing safety margin. This is critical because narrower safety margins, which are associated with a higher lethal frost risk, predominantly occur in cold regions characterized by more frequent and intense frost events7,9,20. Frequent frost exposure can reduce photosynthetic productivity24, potentially undermining the biochemical foundation (photosynthates, such as sugars, proteins, enzymes, and other non-structural carbohydrates) necessary for freezing resistance. This relationship between frost exposure and photosynthetic rate may explain the strong-to-moderate negative relationships we observed between the safety margin and both freezing resistance and FRS. Notably, we found a strong negative correlation between Tmin-var and safety margin (Supplementary Table 7). This suggests that in regions with high temperature variability, plants are exposed to a greater risk of severe frost damage, resulting in consistently narrower safety margins.

Our phenological models consistently showed that (i) climate warming significantly advanced spring phenology, though with diminishing sensitivity (days per degree Celsius), and (ii) safety margins remained stable under low- and medium-warming scenarios but increased significantly under high-warming scenarios. Our findings of diminishing phenological sensitivity align with previous long-term observational studies43,44. As expected from risk-reward trade-offs, safety margins remained stable under the low- and medium-warming scenarios. Although Tmin across our study sites responded more rapidly to warming than Tmax or Tmean (Supplementary Fig. 6), which substantially reduces future lethal frost risk and should theoretically support phenological advances. However, our analysis did not detect such shifts. Compared to low-to-moderate warming scenarios, plants under high-warming scenarios adopted more conservative strategies, which substantially increased safety margins despite the longer growing season. This paradoxical pattern will require further study to determine its underlying mechanisms. Here, we attempt to explain these findings within the trade-off framework. Contrary to common assumption, plants initiate spring phenology depend on multimodal environmental cues rather than relying solely on temperature45. An overreliance on thermal signals risks a premature start to the growing season, the well-documented ‘false spring’ phenomenon46. Consequently, by integrating supplementary cues such as photoperiod, plants can achieve greater phenological precision and thereby maximize frost avoidance45. Recent studies have documented substantial phenological plasticity, enabling plants to track fluctuating temperature regimes2,21,45,47,48. Together with the reliance on multimodal cues, this plasticity provides the substrate upon which natural selection can optimize individual responses to contemporary climate. This evolutionary framework is precisely what our stable-risk scenario represents. Therefore, both photoperiod constraints and the reduced phenological sensitivity resulting from insufficient chilling44,49 may represent adaptations that enable plants to optimize growth while minimizing lethal frost risk. However, this system seems to fall apart under high-warming scenarios (e.g., SSP5-8.5). One possible mechanism, a decline in freezing resistance under extreme warming (e.g., SSP-8.5), but currently lacks robust evidence. To our knowledge, only two studies to date have directly demonstrated that warming reduces freezing resistance, with the effect being contingent on the magnitude of warming50,51. For instance, Ettinger et al. (2019) showed that the stalling effects of chilling appear only above 4 °C of warming30, a pattern that aligns with our findings. A universal spring risk-reward trade-off would imply that wider safety margins can offset declining freezing resistance under high-warming scenarios, a pivotal hypothesis that now demands empirical testing.

Understanding how spring phenology is shaped by the lethal frost risk is critical for predicting ecosystem-level responses to global warming. Our comprehensive meta-analysis revealed that conservative phenological strategies systematically minimized the lethal frost risk during the spring phenology. Furthermore, models projections confirm the central role of spring risk-reward trade-offs in shaping phenological shifts under climate warming. Crucially, we found that biotic factors, such as freezing resistance, are key regulators of plant phenology. This finding underscores the necessity of incorporating biotic factors into next-generation phenological models, as current models primarily account for environmental drivers alone.

Caveats, limitations, and implications

Our study represents a significant step into the trade-off between spring phenology and lethal frost risk, providing a fresh perspective on predicting future spring phenology. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, plant phenology and freezing resistance data remain scarce for regions such as Africa, the Eurasian Steppe, and the Southern Hemisphere (Fig. 1a), limiting our ability to generate robust global predictions of terrestrial plant phenology under future climate warming scenarios. Second, fundamental challenges in modeling spring phenology persist due to two key limitations: (1) discontinuous phenological series can undermine the accuracy of future phenological predictions; (2) overly empirical parameterizations in current phenological models31. Third, and most importantly, there is a notable lack of data on how warming affects plant freezing resistance, especially in trees under extreme warming (e.g., SSP5-8.5 scenario), as implementing simulated warming on trees is particularly challenging. Addressing such constraints will require innovative approaches. Promising directions such as long-term monitoring of freezing resistance dynamics, which integrate controlled manipulations with advanced physiological measurements.

Methods

Data compilation

In this study, we present a global meta-analysis of plant freezing resistance and its relationship to lethal frost risk in the context of spring phenology. We searched for journal articles on the ISI Web of Science and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure using the following key word combinations: ‘freezing resistance*frost resistance*cold resistance’ OR ‘freezing tolerance*frost tolerance*cold tolerance’ OR ‘freezing hardiness*frost hardiness*cold hardiness’ OR ‘hardening*de-hardening’ OR ‘cold acclimation*cold de-acclimation*cold re-acclimation’ AND ‘leaf*foliage*bud*‘. We selected studies that satisfied the following criteria: (i) data were collected at the species level under natural conditions, excluding garden or glasshouse studies to avoid human management; (ii) freezing tests were conducted during spring phenology on new leaves/buds using standardized methods (Supplementary Fig. 1), excluding studies based solely on visual scores; and (iii) the study site location was clearly documented. For experimental studies, our meta-analysis included only data from control groups. Means, standard errors and/or standard deviations, and sample sizes were extracted from the study text, tables, and/or figures using the GetData (V. 2.26) image tool. To ensure the independence of data from individual studies, we averaged freezing resistance measurements for each species within a study site. In the final dataset (Supplementary Data 1), the selected studies spanned 110.53 ° of latitude (from 65.45 °N to 54.06 °S), 1 °-170.23 ° of absolute longitude (Supplementary Table 2), and almost all terrestrial biomes (Fig. 2b).

We quantified the lethal frost risk using the safety margin (SFM)9, which is defined as the difference between plant freezing resistance and the absolute daily minimum temperature during spring phenology (Fig. 1a):

where Phe-Tmin is the daily minimum temperature during spring emergency, LT50 means the freezing resistance (i.e., the lethal temperature of which 50% of samples died) (Supplementary Table 3). In context of climatic instability and unpredictable extreme cold events, a larger safety margin translates into a lower lethal frost risk. Based on this negative relationship, we quantified the species-specific lethal frost risk in spring (Fig. 1a).

From each study, we compiled ancillary site information spanning three categories: site spatial distribution, temperature regime, and water availability. To characterize the sites spatially, we recorded their geographic coordinates (latitude, longitude), elevation, and key solar parameters (solar radiation and sunshine duration). These data are critical because photoperiod cues can protect plants from a potentially fatal consequences, initiating growth at the wrong time by relying solely on ambient temperature48. We compiled key temperature parameters, including the annual means, extremes, variances, diurnal range, frost frequency, and daily minimum temperature during spring phenology. These variables are established drivers of cold acclimation/de-acclimation processes and the associated biochemical activities that determine freezing resistance36,37. We compiled site-level moisture data, including drought severity, evapotranspiration, and precipitation, due to their established role in governing freezing resistance mechanisms52 (Supplementary Table 3). Where possible, we extracted site-level data from the individual studies, missing data were obtained from the WorldClim Database and/or the Climatic Research Unit (CRU TS Version 4.06, provides daily climatic data with a spatial resolution of 1 ° from 1981 to 2021). Additionally, we recorded the ecosystem type (forest, grassland, or shrubland)53, life cycle (perennial or annual plant species), and life form (tree, herb, or shrub), these data were gleaned from the papers themselves, with missing data collected from online databases such as the USDA Plants Database or the China Flora/Fauna Handbook (Supplementary Table 2). Study species were classified into early-, mid-, and late-spring groups based on their emergence dates, reflecting distinct phenological strategies. For Southern Hemisphere observations, dates were standardized by subtracting 180 days, following the typical green-up period in the latter half of the year.

A central objective of this study is to project the future risk of lethal frost. This is achieved by integrating projected shifts in spring phenology and the potential changes in freezing resistance under warming scenarios. Such an integrated approach is critical, as the direct impact of climate warming on freezing resistance itself remains poorly understood. To evaluate the freezing resistance during spring phenology under warming conditions, we selected 15 perennial herbaceous species at the Qinghai Haibei National Field Research Station of Alpine Grassland Ecosystems (37 °29′-37 °45′N, 101 °12′-101 °23′E, 3200 m a.s.l., see our previous studies for site details54–56). The warming treatment increased air temperature by an average of approximately 1.8 °C over a ten-year period. Plant cutting samples (leaves) were cooled to each target temperature (using commercial freezers) and subsequently thawed at a rate of approximately 6 °C h-1 until room temperature was reached (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Initial assessment of freezing damage was conducted through visual inspection, such damage typically manifested as tissue discoloration due to polyphenol oxidation, or as a characteristic odor resulting from protoplast decompartmentalization and autolysis9. Following visual inspection, cutting samples were transferred to small beakers containing distilled water for electrical conductivity (Ec) measurement. These Ec assays were conducted hourly until values stabilized, a process typically requiring over ten hours (the slow leakage of electrolyte). Concurrently, fresh samples were boiled at 100 °C for 30 min and then cooled to room temperature to determine the maximum electrolyte conductance (Ecmax). In this study, freezing resistance was defined as the lethal temperature (LT50, °C) at which electrolyte leakage reached 50% of ECmax57–59, and this LT50 value serves as a common proxy for the temperature at which 50% of the sample cells died9 (Supplementary Fig. 11b).

Statistical analysis

We obtained a species-level phylogeny using the V.PHYLOMAKER package16, which utilizes the largest dated phylogeny for vascular plants (GBOTB.extended.tre). Next, we assessed whether evolutionary history was a key predictor of freezing resistance and the safety margin using Blomberg’s K (R package ‘phytools’, version 4.1.3)67. Blomberg’s K is a statistical metric that quantifies the phylogenetic signal in species traits by comparing their observed variance against a Brownian motion model of evolution:

Under this model, a value of K > 1 indicates that closely related species are more similar than expected under Brownian motion, reflecting a strong phylogenetic signal. Conversely, a value of K < 1 signifies that trait variation is more random than expected, making the pattern indistinguishable from randomness and demonstrating a weak or absent phylogenetic signal. However, the K value alone is insufficient and must be accompanied by a significance test to determine if it deviates from random expectation. We therefore employed a permutation test (n = 5000) to generate a null distribution of K values (Fig. 2d, e). The observed K was compared against this distribution, with significance assessed at 0.1 level, a more lenient threshold applied consistently across all subsequent analyses in this study. We applied convergent cross mapping (CCM)68, an algorithm designed to detect causal relationships, to explore the potential causality between freezing resistance and the safety margin. The method is grounded in the principle that if X causally influences Y, then the state of X can be reconstructed from the historical record of Y, because the evolutionary trajectory of Y embeds information about X69,70. We performed the analysis using the ‘rEDM’ package in R. We tested library sizes from 31 to 304 (τ = 1) to evaluate the convergence of prediction correlation (Rho). The optimal embedding dimension (E = 5) was determined via simplex projection over a range of dimensions (E = 1-20) (Supplementary Fig. 2). We assessed convergence using the twin-surrogate method with 10 bootstrapped iterations, and considered a causal relationship significant at 0.1 level.

To assess the relative importance of our study predictors, we used random forest models as implemented in R (R package ‘randomForest’, version 4.7-1.2)73. Variable importance was quantified using the percent increase in the mean squared error (%IncMSE), with higher values indicating greater contributions. Negative %IncMSE values indicate improved model predictions when excluding the predictor in question, suggesting that the predictor should be removed from the model. Many ecological processes covary, and the sheer number of possible combinations (of predictors) in our analyses made predictor ranking and the interpretation of significant interactions complicated, while also increasing the risk of model over-fitting33,74. Thus, we retained all predictor variables (without implementing stepwise backward elimination) in the following analyses, as most explanatory variables were statistically significant (p < 0.1) (Supplementary Tables 4, 5). Next, we evaluated associations between the two response variables (i.e., freezing resistance and the freezing safety margin) and seventeen predictors (i.e., five measures of site spatial distribution, eight temperature-related traits, three moisture-related traits, and one biotic trait (freezing resistance) using linear mixed models, with random intercepts among species and sites. To interpret the mixed model results, instead of significance testing with an arbitrary p-value cutoff, we implemented the gradual language of evidence recommended by Muff et al.75 (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6, 7), this approach allowed us to communicate model findings in a more nuanced way16.

To determine the phenological temperature sensitivity (that is, days per °C), we used ordinary least squares linear regression40 for the entire future period (2020–2099). We then compared future temperature sensitivity to predicted trends in spring phenology and freezing safety margins under future warming scenarios76. To assess differences in spring phenology and freezing safety margins among diverse warming scenarios, we applied linear mixed model with random intercepts among species and sites. We conducted all our statistical analyses using MATLAB R2022b and R 4.4.277.
