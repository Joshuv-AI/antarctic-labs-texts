Prevalence of hypertension and diabetes in Bangladesh: identifying gaps in awareness and misinformation
Authors: Raaj Kishore Biswas, Awan Afiaz, Md. Hasibul Islam Jitu, Bushra Chowdhury, Sharmin Sultana, Ahmed Moosa, Enamul Kabir, Jahar Bhowmik, Nicholas A. Koemel, Luigi Fontana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveNon-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a growing health burden in low- and middle-income countries, with hypertension and poor glycaemic control being key contributors. This study aimed to assess the prevalence, awareness, and misinformation surrounding hypertension and diabetes in Bangladesh, identifying high-risk groups.MethodsData from the nationally representative 2022 Bangladesh Demographic Health Survey were analysed, covering 13,344 adults aged 17–95 years. We applied complex survey weighted regression modelling, spatial analysis, and machine learning techniques to explore trends, regional disparities, and factors influencing NCD awareness.ResultsThe prevalence of hypertension (≥ 140/90 mm Hg) or diabetes (blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or 7.0 mmol/L) was 31%, rising to 52% among adults aged 50 + . Notably, 17% of affected individuals in this age group were unaware of their condition. Among adults aged 50 + , 34% had undiagnosed hypertension, while 38% across all ages were unaware of their diabetes. Females developed hypertension earlier than males. Regional disparities were evident, with the highest NCD prevalence in Feni (40%), Naogaon (37%), and Natore (36%). Awareness varied by age, sex, BMI, and mobile phone ownership, which was linked to misinformation. Adults aged 30–39 had 2.11 times higher odds of unawareness (95% CI: 1.75, 2.56), rising to 5.61 times in those aged 70 + (95% CI: 4.36, 7.20) compared to those under 30. Obese individuals were 3.07 times more likely to be unaware than those classified as underweight (95% CI: 2.33, 4.05).ConclusionsThis study reveals gaps in health literacy and screening access, contributing to the escalating NCD burden in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28453-3.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization and socioeconomic transitions have driven the global rise of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [1]. In 2021, NCDs accounted for at least 43 million deaths worldwide, 73% of which occurred in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. Premature mortality from NCDs, defined as death before 70 years of age, is also concentrated in LMICs, where 82% of such deaths occur [2]. In South Asia, the burden of diabetes and hypertension has increased substantially, driven by interacting social, environmental, and genetic factors, though public awareness remains alarmingly low. In Bangladesh, in particular, NCDs are responsible for 63.1% of all deaths [3], exacerbated by rapid urbanization, major dietary shifts, sedentary lifestyles, tobacco use, and increased exposure to air pollution. These factors, along with genetic predispositions, have led to a dual burden of undernutrition and NCDs [4].

Poor glycaemic control and hypertension are key contributors to this growing health crisis. Because both conditions are often asymptomatic, many affected individuals remain undiagnosed, untreated, or unaware of their disease status. Previous evidence suggests that hypertension affects approximately one in five adults in Bangladesh [5], while diabetes and pre-diabetes affect 7.8% and 10.1% of the population, respectively [6]. Together, hypertension and diabetes increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, disability, and premature mortality, while placing increasing pressure on the healthcare system. The World Health Organization recommends a primary healthcare approach focused on screening, timely treatment, and community-level health education. In Bangladesh, strengthening early diagnosis, referral pathways, chronic disease management, and lifestyle counselling could significantly reduce disease progression and preventable mortality [7].

This study used nationally representative 2022 BDHS data to assess hypertension and diabetes prevalence, awareness, and misinformation in Bangladesh. By comparing biomarker-defined disease status with self-reported diagnosis across sociodemographic and geographic groups, we identify key gaps to inform targeted screening, health literacy, and NCD prevention strategies.

Methods

Data overview

This study was a cross-sectional secondary analysis of the 2022 BDHS [8], which employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design. In the first stage, 675 enumeration areas were selected using probability proportional to enumeration area size. In the second stage, an average of 45 households per enumeration area was systematically sampled. Details on data collection, including anthropometric and biomarker measurements, are provided in Supplementary section (S1). The participant selection process, exclusions and final analytic sample are shown in supplemental eFigure 1.

Outcome variable

For this study, NCD status was operationalised as the presence of either hypertension or diabetes. Blood pressure was measured by trained biomarker specialists using a Multi-User Upper Arm Blood Pressure Monitor, with three readings taken at ≥ 5-min intervals; the average of the second and third readings was used for classification. Blood glucose was measured from overnight fasting capillary whole blood using a HemoCue 201 RT analyser. Values were recorded in mg/dL and converted to mmol/L for consistency with WHO cut-offs (detailed in supplementary section, S1). Participants were classified as non-diabetic (< 100 mg/dL), pre-diabetic (100–125 mg/dL or 6.9 mmol/L), or diabetic (≥ 126 mg/dL or 7.0 mmol/L); those taking diabetes medication were classified as diabetic regardless of measured glucose levels [9].

Hypertension was defined based on the 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines as normal blood pressure (systolic < 120 mmHg and diastolic < 70 mmHg), elevated blood pressure (systolic 120–139 mmHg, diastolic 70–90 mmHg), or hypertension (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg) [10]. Participants taking antihypertensive medication were classified as hypertensive regardless of measured blood pressure [11]. For defining NCD awareness, diabetes and hypertension were converted into binary categories due to small sample sizes across groups. Diabetes was defined as blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or current use of diabetes medication; hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medication using the more conservative European cut-off for blood pressure.

The outcome variable, NCD awareness, was derived by comparing participants' self-reported disease status with biomarker- or medication-defined hypertension or diabetes. Participants were categorized as: (1) correctly informed (if self-reported status matched biomarker- or medication-defined NCD data), (2) misinformed (if self-reported status was discordant with biomarker findings), and (3) unaware (if they did not report having hypertension or diabetes, but met biomarker criteria for one or both conditions) [12]. This classification should be interpreted as survey-response concordance or discordance rather than deliberate misinformation. Further details on disease classification and construction of the awareness variable are provided in Supplementary section S1.

Covariates

We assessed the association between NCD awareness and the following covariates: place of residence, region, sex, age, education, wealth index quintile, mobile phone ownership, and body mass index (BMI) [13]. Detailed definitions and categories of each covariate are provided in Supplementary section S1.

Statistical analyses

Systolic blood pressure and blood glucose distributions across age and sex were visualised through heatmaps and box plots. Values were winsorised at the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles to minimise the influence of sparse data and outliers. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize NCD awareness by diabetes/hypertension categories and sociodemographic characteristics.

Spatial mapping was utilized to visualise district-level variation in NCD awareness across Bangladesh, including the proportion of correctly informed, misinformed and unaware participants. Associations between sociodemographic factors and NCD awareness were assessed using multivariable-adjusted multinomial logistic regression. Generalized Variance Inflation Factors (GVIF) scores were calculated to detect multicollinearity. We employed an interpretable machine learning method, building a weight-adjusted classification tree to explore interactions among key predictors of NCDs. This conditional inference framework was selected to provide an interpretable, non-parametric assessment of predictor combinations while reducing the selection bias associated with traditional classification trees [14].

Moran's I was calculated to assess spatial autocorrelation in district-level NCD prevalence. Spatial Lag Models were then fitted to examine geographic variation in diabetes and hypertension prevalence after adjustment for district-level and socio-demographic factors [15, 16]. Missing data were handled using list-wise deletion under a missing-at-random assumption to ensure unbiased estimates [17]. Data curation and analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) [15], with key packages including ggplot2, dplyr, survey, ctree, spatialreg, and nnet [18].

Figure 1A and B show the age- and gender-specific percentage distribution of systolic blood pressure, while Fig. 1C and D show mean systolic blood pressure across successive age groups in Bangladeshi men and women. Women had lower mean systolic blood pressure than men in early adulthood, but showed a steeper age-related increase, reaching similar or higher levels than men from midlife onward. Median systolic blood pressure exceeded the elevated threshold of 120 mmHg at ages 60–65 years in men and earlier, at ages 50–55 years, in women. Education was associated with awareness: participants with no formal education had the highest prevalence of unawareness (21%). Spatial mapping

Spatial mapping revealed high regional variation in NCDs prevalence and awareness across Bangladesh (Fig. 3). The highest prevalence of hypertension or diabetes was observed in Narayanganj (44%) in central Bangladesh, Feni (43%) in Southeast of Bangladesh and Dinajpur (43%) and Naogaon (40%) in Northeast of Bangladesh. Moran's I indicated statistically significant but weak spatial autocorrelation in hypertension and diabetes prevalence (Moran's I = 0.004, Z = 8.65, p < 0.001), suggesting that the observed spatial pattern is unlikely to be due to random variation despite its small magnitude. However, spatial lag models did not show substantial district-level variation after adjustment for sociodemographic factors (eTable 5). Unawareness was highest in Dinajpur (36%) and Narayanganj (31%), while Kishoreganj had the lowest proportion of unaware respondents (5.6%) (eTable 3). Overall, 16.8% of the population was unaware of having hypertension or diabetes (Fig. 3). Among older adults (> 50 years), unawareness was highest in Dinajpur (45%), Lakshmipur (42%) and Panchgarh (40%) (eFigure 3).

The Sylhet division in the northeast region of Bangladesh had the highest prevalence of NCD misinformation. At the district level, misinformation was highest in Feni (5.9%), Chuadanga (5.6%), and Gopalganj (5.2%) (Fig. 3). Among older adults (> 50 years), the highest prevalence was observed in Bandarban (11%), Gopalganj (10%) and Sunamganj (9.4%) (eFigure 3). Detailed division-wise prevalence and awareness/misinformation distributions are provided in eTable 3 and eTable 4. Spatial lag models were not fitted for the misinformed or unaware groups because of small sample sizes (eTable 3).

Rural residents had 14% lower odds of unawareness than urban residents (AOR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.77, 0.97). Education was also significantly associated with lower odds of unawareness: compared with participants with no education, those with primary education had 16% lower odds (AOR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.74, 0.96), and those with higher education had 21% lower odds (AOR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.66, 0.96).

Higher BMI was moderately associated with greater unawareness. Compared with underweight participants, those with normal weight had higher odds of unawareness (AOR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.03, 1.38), while obese participants had more than twice the odds (AOR = 2.02; 95% CI: 1.60, 2.56).

Disease misinformed

The multinomial regression analysis also showed significant regional variation in misinformation about NCDs. Compared with respondents from Dhaka, those from Rangpur had 48% lower odds of being misinformed (AOR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.29, 0.94).

Women had 73% higher odds of being misinformed than men (AOR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.28, 2.32). Misinformation also increased with age. Compared with individuals aged < 30 years, the odds were higher among those aged 30–39 years (AOR = 2.16; 95% CI: 1.43, 3.27), 40–49 years (AOR = 2.24; 95% CI: 1.41, 3.55), 50–59 years (AOR = 2.76; 95% CI: 1.68, 4.54), 60–69 years (AOR = 3.41; 95% CI:2.01, 5.81), and ≥ 70 (AOR = 2.34; 95% CI: 1.12, 4.89).

Higher wealth was moderately associated with lower odds of misinformation. Compared with the poorest quintile, respondents in the richer and richest quintiles had lower odds (AOR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.38, 0.97 and AOR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.33, 0.92, respectively). In contrast, mobile phone ownership was associated with higher odds of misinformation (AOR = 1.78; 95% CI:1.25, 2.54). Assessment of multicollinearity using Generalised Variance Inflation Factors indicated no evidence of concern (eTable 6).

Discussion

This study provides nationally representative evidence on the prevalence, awareness, and discordance between self-reported and biomarker-defined hypertension and diabetes in Bangladesh. We identified substantial gaps in awareness, with notable regional disparities and greater unawareness among older adults and individuals with higher BMI. Discordant reporting varied by region, sex, age, wealth, and mobile phone ownership. These findings highlight important gaps in chronic disease literacy and support the need for targeted public health education, routine screening, and early lifestyle and pharmacological interventions for high-risk populations.

The age- and sex-specific heatmaps revealed clear differences in cardiometabolic patterns across demographic groups. Systolic blood pressure increased with age in both sexes, with a steeper rise among women, consistent with evidence from large population-based studies [19]. Notably, Bangladeshi women reached the elevated systolic blood pressure threshold of ≥ 120 mmHg earlier than men, at approximately 55–60 years compared with 60–65 years in men. This pattern is clinically important because cardiovascular risk increases progressively with rising blood pressure, doubling with each 20/10 mmHg increase from a threshold of 115/75 mmHg [10, 20]. In contrast, age-related differences in blood glucose were less pronounced, suggesting that hypertension may be a particularly important driver of cardiometabolic risk among older adults.

Our study revealed marked regional variation in NCD prevalence and awareness across Bangladesh, with higher burdens observed in parts of the northeast, southern districts near Chattogram, and among older adults in Dhaka and neighbouring districts. These patterns likely reflect the combined influence of urbanization, changing lifestyle behaviours, environmental exposures, healthcare access, and local information environments. Rapid urbanization in Bangladesh has been accompanied by changes in diet, physical activity, sleep, and exposure to air pollution, particularly in major urban centres such as Dhaka, all of which may contribute to rising cardiometabolic risk [21]. Prior studies have also reported sex differences in sleep disruption, with sleep difficulties affecting more than 40% of women compared with 23.6% of men [22, 23]. The higher unawareness among adults aged 30–39 years may reflect a transition period in which cardiometabolic risk begins to emerge, but routine screening remains uncommon and perceived susceptibility to chronic disease remains low. Because hypertension and diabetes are often asymptomatic, individuals in this age group may not seek testing until later in life or until complications develop, supporting the need to initiate community-based screening before older age. Although medical facilities, internet access, and mass media exposure are generally more concentrated in urban areas, our findings suggest that these advantages may not yet translate into sufficient screening coverage, accurate disease awareness, or sustained chronic disease management in Bangladesh. Future studies incorporating device-based measures of physical activity, sleep, and other behavioural exposures could clarify how lifestyle factors contribute to regional and urban–peri-urban differences in NCD risk [24]. These findings highlight the need to strengthen community-based screening, referral pathways, and chronic health literacy so that hypertension and diabetes are understood as long-term conditions requiring early detection, ongoing monitoring, and evidence-based treatment rather than episodic care alone.

Regional patterns in NCD awareness were heterogeneous and should not be reduced to an urban–rural or Dhaka-versus-non-Dhaka contrast. Pockets of low awareness and discordant reporting across the country suggest that regions may face distinct challenges, supporting region-specific screening, health communication, and chronic disease literacy strategies. BMI also emerged as an important factor in NCD awareness, reflecting broader dietary, lifestyle, and sociocultural influences. In Bangladesh, dietary patterns have shifted towards fewer vegetables and legumes, greater refined oil consumption, and heavy reliance on refined flours and white rice, which accounts for 69% of total food energy [25]. High salt intake may further contribute to endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and hypertension [20, 26, 27]. Rapid urbanization and rural-to-urban migration have also increased reliance on ultra-processed foods and sedentary lifestyles, contributing to overweight and obesity [28–30]. Even individuals with normal BMI may remain unaware of abdominal adiposity and long-term cardiometabolic risk. The association between higher BMI and greater unawareness may also reflect low screening uptake, poor continuity of care, asymptomatic disease, or low perceived susceptibility despite elevated cardiometabolic risk. These findings suggest that risk-based screening and counselling should not assume that individuals with higher BMI are necessarily more aware of their hypertension or diabetes status. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive risk-based screening and targeted health education.

Age played an important role in awareness of hypertension and diabetes, with older adults showing greater unawareness. In Bangladesh, many older individuals face limited healthcare access and chronic disease knowledge, a concern amplified by rising life expectancy [31]. Expanding smartphone use creates opportunities for e-health and telehealth platforms to improve health literacy, support medication adherence, and encourage healthier lifestyles [32–34]. However, understanding of central adiposity, diet, sleep, and physical activity remains limited among older adults, despite their importance for blood pressure and glycaemic control [35]. These findings highlight the need for age-tailored public health strategies to reduce undiagnosed and poorly managed disease in Bangladesh’s ageing population. [36]

Mobile phone ownership emerged as an important marker of how health information circulates in Bangladesh, but its association with misinformation requires cautious interpretation. In this context, “misinformation” should be understood as discordant reporting or inaccurate disease-status understanding, rather than direct evidence of deliberate misinformation. Formal education may support disease recognition and screening engagement, while greater household wealth may offer some protection against misinformation. However, mobile phone ownership remained associated with misinformation after adjustment for education, wealth, and residence, suggesting that information access alone does not ensure accurate or actionable health knowledge. This reflects a broader challenge in chronic health literacy. Smartphones offer opportunities for e-health interventions but may also increase exposure to unverified online content or social media health advice [37]. Women may be particularly vulnerable, consistent with evidence of gender disparities in hypertension and diabetes knowledge in Bangladesh [37, 38]. As digital technologies expand, health and digital literacy initiatives are essential to help individuals distinguish reliable from misleading information and apply health advice appropriately [39].

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including genetic, environmental, and behavioural influences, cannot be excluded. Lifestyle factors such as physical activity, sleep, and diet were unavailable; future studies should incorporate objective, device-based measures to better characterize NCD risk in Bangladesh [40]. The classification tree analysis was exploratory and intended to identify interpretable sociodemographic patterns, not to develop or validate a predictive model. Spatial lag models could not be fitted for misinformed or unaware groups because of small sample sizes. The “disease misinformed” category should also be interpreted cautiously as discordance between self-reported and biomarker- or medication-defined disease status, rather than deliberate misinformation. Finally, missing data from part of the original sample requires cautious interpretation despite the nationally representative design.

Conclusion

This nationally representative study shows that hypertension and diabetes are highly prevalent in Bangladesh, particularly among adults aged > 50 years, and that many affected individuals remain unaware of their condition. Women reached elevated systolic blood pressure at younger ages than men, while older adults, individuals with higher BMI, and residents of regions with greater unawareness emerged as priority groups for intervention.

Strengthening NCD control in Bangladesh will require both expanded community-based screening and clearer communication to improve chronic disease literacy and long-term disease management. Digital health platforms may support these efforts, provided they deliver verified, culturally appropriate messages integrated with primary healthcare and community outreach. Interventions should be tailored to local disease burden, healthcare access, and information environments. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether screening, communication, and lifestyle-based strategies lead to sustained improvements in NCD prevention and care.
