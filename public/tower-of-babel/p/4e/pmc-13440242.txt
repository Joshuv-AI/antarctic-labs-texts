Distinctive hydrocephalus-like phenotype in NOTCH2NLC-related neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease: clinicopathological features and therapeutic implications
Authors: Shaoping Zhong, Yangye Lian, Binbin Zhou, Jingzhen Liang, Yuzhe Li, Luyao Huang, Xing Chen, Yi Yan, Wei Sun, Rongkui Luo, Hui Lu, Jianying Liu, Fan Hu, Xin Wang, Jing Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNOTCH2NLC-related neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder with marked clinical heterogeneity and an increasingly recognized phenotypic spectrum. Although hydrocephalus-like presentations, i.e. ventriculomegaly with concomitant with cognition, motor, and/or bladder dysfunctions, have been observed in NIID sporadically, their prevalence, clinicopathological characteristics, and potential clinical implications remain poorly understood. This retrospective cohort study systematically characterized this newly recognized hydrocephalus-like phenotype. We conducted an MRI-based screening to identify previously undiagnosed NIID cases from a cohort of 498 adults with imaging diagnosis of communicating hydrocephalus. Four such cases were identified and incorporated into our NIID cohort, with genetic and pathological confirmation. A total of 68 NIID patients were analyzed, of whom 19 (27.9%) exhibited marked ventriculomegaly (Evans index > 0.34) disproportionate to parenchymal atrophy and were grouped as ventriculomegalic NIID. Compared with those without ventriculomegaly, these patients were associated with male predominance, more frequent walking difficulty, and less frequent tremor. Nearly half of ventriculomegalic cases (9/19) fulfilled the idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus-like clinical triad. Radiological features included markedly enlarged ventricles, smaller callosal angle, less frequent diffusion-weighted imaging hyperintensities, and overrepresented periventricular-dominant white matter lesion pattern. A subset of ventriculomegalic NIID patients (5/8) showed improvement in gait, cognition, and/or urinary function following cerebrospinal fluid drainage, and two patients who underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt surgery experienced sustained clinical benefit. Brain autopsy was performed in one ventriculomegalic NIID patient, revealing widespread intranuclear inclusions, particularly in ependymal and choroid plexus epithelial cells and astrocytes, alongside dysmorphic astrocytes and loss of aquaporin-4 polarization. Collectively, our findings suggest that a hydrocephalus-like phenotype with distinctive clinical and radiological features is relatively common in NIID and frequently misdiagnosed as idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus. Pathological alterations in the ependyma, choroid plexus, and astrocytes and subsequently disturbed cerebrospinal fluid dynamics may contribute to its pathogenesis. Introduction

Neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by widespread eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions [1]. Most of the NIID patients are adults with insidious onset and distinctive white matter lesion (WML) patterns on MRI, such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) hyperintensities at the corticomedullary junction (CMJ) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities in the paravermal region [1–3], while infantile and juvenile cases are also documented [4]. Since the discovery of NOTCH2NLC GGC trinucleotide repeat expansions as a genetic cause of NIID in 2019 [5–7], increasing clinical recognition has revealed a broad phenotypic spectrum encompassing dementia, movement disorders, autonomic dysfunction, peripheral neuropathy, episodic neurological events, myopathy, and extra-nervous manifestations [1, 5, 8–12]. These findings have led to the propose of a concept of NOTCH2NLC-related repeat expansion disorders [13]. Although NOTCH2NLC account for the majority of adult-onset NIID, particularly in East Asian populations, genetically unresolved cases have been reported [14], suggesting a genetic heterogeneity.

Recently, a few studies, including our own, have reported a small number of NOTCH2NLC-related NIID cases presenting with hydrocephalus-like presentations, characterized by marked ventriculomegaly accompanied by cognitive impairment, gait disturbance, and/or bladder dysfunction, closely mimicking idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) [15–17]. However, the prevalence, clinical profile, and pathological underpinnings of this new phenotype remain poorly delineated. Although two previously reported cases showed negative response to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tap-test [16, 18], systematic evaluation of the therapeutic value of CSF drainage or shunt procedures in NIID is lacking. Given the current absence of effective disease-modifying therapies for NIID, a clearer characterization of this potentially treatable phenotype is urgently needed.

This retrospective observational study aims to explore the clinical, radiological, and pathological characteristics of NOTCH2NLC-related NIID individuals with hydrocephalus-like presentations. In this study, ventriculomegaly refers to structural ventricular enlargement, whereas hydrocephalus-like phenotype denotes a clinical-radiological constellation resembling iNPH. These terms are not interchangeable. Our findings suggest that a hydrocephalus-like phenotype is frequent in NIID and exhibits features distinct from those patients without ventriculomegaly, which is prone to be misdiagnosed as iNPH due to a partial response to CSF drainage. This work extends the known clinical spectrum and highlights the potential therapeutic value of CSF shunt in this disease, adding to our knowledge of the involvement of CSF dynamics in genetic neurodegenerative disorders.

Materials and methods

MRI-based screening

All adult individuals that underwent brain MRI between January 1, 2019 and March 31, 2025 at Zhongshan Hospital were included in the initial screening. Their MRI reports were retrieved from the picture archiving and communication system (PACS). Patients with suspected communicating hydrocephalus (n = 498) were identified by searching for keywords (hydrocephalus, ventricular dilation, or ventriculomegaly) and excluding those with brain tumor, space-occupying lesions, or obstructive hydrocephalus based on the MRI reports. The original MRI images of these patients were then independently reviewed by two experienced neuroradiologists who were blinded to clinical information, and consensus was reached. Suspected NIID was identified based on the presence of DWI hyperintensities at the CMJ or paravermal FLAIR hyperintensities.

Subjects

All consecutive patients meeting the established diagnose criteria of NOTCH2NLC-related NIID [8, 15], including previously diagnosed and newly identified ones, were incorporated in this study. Clinical information and MRI scans obtained during routine care were collected, and all patients were evaluated by at least three neurologists. MRI examinations were performed on 1.5- or 3-T scanners. Brain tissue from patient P19 and controls was provided by the Shanghai Brain Bank, Fudan University.

The control iNPH cohort comprised all patients meeting following criteria: (1) male; (2) age ranging from 60 to 80 years; (3) receiving CSF tap-test between 2022 and 2024 in our center with confirmed diagnosis of iNPH; (4) receiving genetic test and having normal range of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeats (< 40).

Genetic analysis

Genomic DNA of NIID patients was extracted from fresh blood using a commercial kit (DP349, TIANGEN). Repeat-primed PCR (RP-PCR) and amplicon-length PCR (AL-PCR) were utilized for the initial identification of GGC repeat expansions and the determination of GGC repeat size in the NOTCH2NLC gene, as previously described [15]. Electrophoresis was performed on a 3700xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems) and analyzed by GeneMarker software (version 2.6.3). A saw-tooth tail pattern in the electropherogram of RP-PCR was considered as the disease-associated repeat expansion.

In seven cases, exact GGC repeat numbers could not be determined due to technical limitations of AL-PCR. However, all of them had a clear saw-tooth tail pattern on RP-PCR, confirming the presence of pathogenic repeat expansions, and skin biopsy demonstrated p62-positive intranuclear inclusions. These seven cases were included in all analyses except those requiring exact repeat size.

Imaging analysis

MRI scans from all 68 NOTCH2NLC-related NIID patients and from the 17 iNPH control patients were independently reviewed by two experienced neurologists and subsequently confirmed by a senior neuroradiologist. The white matter lesion distribution pattern of each NIID patient was evaluated according to previously published criteria [15]. The Evans index (EI) and callosal angle of each patient was quantified by an experienced neurologist blinded to clinical information and subgroup allocation, and verified by a senior neuroradiologist, following the Japanese iNPH Guidelines (Third Edition) [19]. Briefly, the EI was calculated as the ratio between the maximal width of the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles and the maximal inner skull diameter at the same axial level. The threshold of EI > 0.34 was selected as an operational definition to identify individuals with disproportionate ventriculomegaly within the NIID cohort, rather than to define a biologically distinct subtype.

The callosal angle, defined as the angle between the medial walls of the lateral ventricles, was measured on a coronal slice passing through the posterior commissure and perpendicular to the anterior–posterior commissure line. Lateral ventricular volume was automatically quantified using the uAI Discover Brain system (United Imaging Healthcare Co., Ltd., China).

Assessment of CSF tap-test

Patients in both the vNIID and iNPH cohorts who underwent CSF tap testing were adults aged ≥ 60 years with an Evans index (EI) > 0.30, at least one symptom of the classic triad (gait disturbance, cognitive impairment, or urinary incontinence), and without preceding conditions known to cause secondary ventricular enlargement (e.g., subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, traumatic brain injury, congenital or developmental hydrocephalus, aqueductal stenosis). These criteria are consistent with the diagnostic definition of possible iNPH in the Japanese iNPH Guidelines (Third Edition) [19]. For all vNIID patients, CSF tap-test was not performed for research purposes. Instead, it was retrospectively analyzed in patients who underwent clinical evaluation for suspected iNPH during routine care before receiving a confirmed NIID diagnosis.

Tap-test was conducted according to routine procedures at our center [20], with removal of 30–50 mL of CSF. Exceptionally, a patient (P109) had received CSF tap-test at a local medical center before transferring to our hospital and receiving the confirmed NIID diagnosis. All patients had CSF pressure < 200 mmH2O. Quantitative gait analysis was performed through the Timed Up & Go test (TUG) with a 3.0-m electronic portable walkway (GAITRite; CIR Systems, Inc, USA) at the baseline and at 8, 24, and 72 h after the tap-test. Key gait parameters were measured automatically, such as the step counter, step speed (m/s), and step length (m), stand-up time (s), turning 180° time (s). Clinical scales including the iNPH grading scale (iNPHGS) [21], Mini-mental state examination (MMSE), and The Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) were scored both at the baseline and at 72 h after drainage. A positive response to the tap test was defined as: (1) ≥ 20% improvement in any single gait parameter, or ≥ 10% improvement in at least two parameters (step speed, stand-up time, turning time); (2) improvement in iNPHGS score; or (3) improvement ≥ 3 points in MMSE or MoCA, based on Japanese and Chinese iNPH guideline criteria [19, 22].

Pathological assessment of skin biopsy and autopsied brain tissue

Skin biopsies were performed at baseline to confirm the diagnosis of NIID. Skin biopsy was taken at 10 cm above the external ankle. For the autopsy case (patient P19), the brain was removed 8 h postmortem and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Samples of the skin and brain were embedded in paraffin and sectioned in 8-mm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Samples for electron microscopic study were prepared as described previously [23]. Immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy were used to detect intranuclear inclusions and associated pathological changes.

The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit polyclonal anti-p62/SQSTM1 (18420-1-AP, Proteintech); mouse monoclonal anti-p62/SQSTM1 (66184-1-Ig, Proteintech); rabbit polyclonal anti-ubiquitin (10201-2-AP, Proteintech); rabbit monoclonal anti-SUMO1 (ET1606-53, HUABIO); rabbit polyclonal anti-uN2CpolyG/N2NLCpolyG (R4832, a custom-made antibody used previously [24]); mouse monoclonal anti-MBP (MA1-10937, Invitrogen); rabbit anti-NeuN (ab177487, Abcam); rabbit polyclonal anti-GFAP (Z0334, DAKO); rabbit polyclonal anti-AQP1 (20333-1-AP, Proteintech); rabbit polyclonal anti-AQP4 (1:500; 16473-1-AP, Proteintech); mouse monoclonal anti-Neurofilament (GB12763-100, Servicebio); rabbit polyclonal anti-IBA1 (1:500; 019-19741, WAKO); rabbit polyclonal anti-OLIG2 (OB-PRB009, Oasis Biofarm); rabbit polyclonal anti-Claudin 5 (GB11290-100, Servicebio); rabbit polyclonal anti-Fibrinogen-α (GB112088-100, Servicebio); and rabbit polyclonal anti-CD3 (GB11014-100, Servicebio). Additional staining, including Kluver-Barrera, followed standard procedures at the Shanghai Brain Bank, Fudan University.

For assessment of intranuclear inclusion density, we prepared sections of representative brain regions and performed p62 immunofluorescence staining. Five random microscopic fields per region were selected were imaged under a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000) using a × 20 objective lens and the number of total nuclei and p62-positive intranuclear inclusions were counted. The number of neurons in the frontal cortex, astrocytes in the frontal cortex and white matter, ependymal cells in the putamen, and epithelial cells in the choroid plexus were quantified to evaluate cell type-specific inclusion densities, which were assessed using combined evaluation of anatomical localization (ependymal epithelial cells and choroid plexus epithelial cells) and co-immunostaining with cell type-specific markers (anti-GFAP and anti-NeuN).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM-SPSS 22.0. Non-normal distributed data are represented as median and inter-quartile range (IQR), and normal data are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Numeration data are represented by fractions/percentages. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or chi-square tests. Continuous variables were compared using the rank-sum test or Welch’s t-test. Multivariable linear regression assessed contributions of sex, age, and GGC repeat size. Differences were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval ID: B2024-478R; B2025-132). All participants (or legal guardians) have provided written informed consent for data collection and publication.

Results

Identifying underdiagnosed NIID from a PACS-screened cohort with imaging diagnosis of communicating hydrocephalus

To evaluate the frequency of NOTCH2NLC-related NIID among patients initially considered as hydrocephalus, we conducted an MRI-based screening. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of searching for characteristic imaging markers within PACS to identify previously underdiagnosed NIID cases [16, 25]. The flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 498 adults with imaging diagnosis of communicating hydrocephalus were identified through key searching in the PACS reports (median age 69 years; 192 females), whose MRI images were reanalyzed by experienced neuroradiologists. Among these candidates, 6 individuals met the radiological criteria of suspected NIID (i.e. DWI hyperintensities at the CMJ or paravermal FLAIR hyperintensities), including one previously confirmed NIID case (P19) [15]. Informed consent was obtained from four of the remaining five suspected cases. Representative MRI findings are shown in Fig. 2A. All these four patients had prominent ventriculomegaly disproportionate to their brain parenchymal atrophy. Obstructive hydrocephalus was further excluded by enlargement of the third and fourth ventricles and negative findings on fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition (FIESTA) sequence (Fig. 2B). All four individuals carried > 60 GGC repeats in NOTCH2NLC (Fig. 2C), and intranuclear inclusions were confirmed by skin biopsy (Fig. 2D, E). Their primary manifestations, including cognitive decline, gait disturbance, bladder dysfunction, and peripheral neuropathy, were consistent with NIID. These patients were therefore diagnosed as NOTCH2NLC-related NIID and included in our NIID cohort.

Distinctive clinical and imaging features of ventriculomegalic NIID

The cohort ultimately included 68 genetically confirmed NOTCH2NLC-related NIID patients, including 43 females and 37 familial cases. The median age at onset was 62.0 years (IQR 56.0–66.0), and the median disease duration was 5.0 years (IQR 2.5–10.0). The median GGC repeat size was 111 (IQR 100.5–124.0; range 84–384). The most frequent symptoms were cognitive impairment, autonomic dysfunction, gait disturbance, tremor, and episodic encephalopathy. P62-positive intranuclear inclusions were detected in all 45 individuals who underwent biopsy. Continuous DWI hyperintensities at the CMJ were present in 57/68 (84%).

Ventricular measurements showed a median Evans index (EI) of 0.297 (IQR 0.280–0.348) and median lateral ventricular volume (LvV) of 64.83 (IQR 46.39–99.96) mm3 (Fig. 3A). EI and LvV were strongly correlated (Spearman r = 0.811; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that age and sex—but not GGC repeat size—were positively associated with larger LvV (Supplementary Fig. 1B–E). Given the high prevalence of cerebral atrophy in NIID, the classical EI cutoff (0.30) was insufficient to distinguish disproportionate ventriculomegaly. The EI threshold of 0.34 was selected based on a natural demarcation in the distribution of EI values in our cohort (Fig. 3A), and its robustness was confirmed in sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs (0.32, 0.35, 0.36, 0.38), with most group differences remaining consistent (Supplementary Table 1).

Nineteen patients (including newly screened cases) met the criterion for vNIID; the remainder were grouped as non-ventriculomegalic NIID (nvNIID) in this study (Fig. 3B). The most frequent symptoms of vNIID were cognitive impairment (18/19), walking difficulty (15/19), and bladder dysfunction (11/19). Nine patients exhibited all three, closely mirroring the classic iNPH triad. Compared with nvNIID, vNIID was enriched for males (12/19; P = 0.005), showed more walking difficulty (15/19; P = 0.011) and less tremor (5/19; P = 0.047), while onset age, repeat size, and cognitive measures were comparable (Table 1). The density of p62-positive inclusions in the skin tended to be lower in the vNIID group (2.86% ± 0.72% of nuclei), although not reaching statistical significance.

WML burden on FLAIR was strikingly heterogeneous in vNIID, ranging from mild, predominantly periventricular lesions to extensive involvement (Fig. 3C). In addition to the operationally defined ventriculomegaly, MRI characteristics of vNIID included a smaller callosal angle (97.2°, IQR 90.5–110.3°; P < 0.001) and a lower frequency of continuous DWI hyperintensities (11/19; P = 0.008) or extensive cerebral WMLs (12/19; P = 0.010) (Table 1; Fig. 3C). Because the cohort stratification was based on an EI threshold, comparisons involving EI itself are descriptively. Long-term imaging (≥ 5 years) showed age-related increases in LvV in both groups, but the slope was markedly steeper in vNIID (Fig. 3D), suggesting accelerated ventriculomegaly. Longitudinal scans of representative vNIID patients (P19, P55, P106, P109) demonstrated early-onset ventriculomegaly (in early 60 s) and subtle DWI hyperintensities (Supplementary Fig. 2). Our previous study proposed a classification framework to describe diverse WML developing pattern in NIID, including periventricular-subcortical, periventricular-dominant, and CMJ-dominant subtypes [15]. Reviewing WML patterns showed an overrepresentation of periventricular-dominant subtype in the vNIID group (7/19; P < 0.001).

Response to CSF drainage and shunt

Among the 19 vNIID patients, eight had undergone CSF tap-test during routine clinical evaluation for suspected iNPH before receiving a confirmed NIID diagnosis. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Median CSF pressure was 132.5 mmH2O (range: 80–150), with unremarkable results of CSF routine and biochemistry tests, except slightly elevated protein in four cases (0.51–0.71 g/L). Three patients showed no response, including two males with iNPH triad and hypermyotonia (P19, and P54), and a female (P106) with prominent cognitive decline and tremor.

In contrast, the other five male patients demonstrated measurable functional improvement following CSF drainage. These individuals had previously been clinically diagnosed as probable iNPH before subsequent identification of NOTCH2NLC-related NIID, and are descriptively referred to as drainage-responsive vNIID here. Three of them (P96, P109, P115) exhibited > 20% improvement in stand-up time and/or step speed at 24 and 72 h post-drainage (Fig. 3E). Two (P52, P67) showed improved iNPH grading scale scores in dementia and urinary incontinence. Cognitive gains were most notable in P109 (MMSE scores from 16 to 21) and P115 (MoCA scores from 16 to 20). Although limited sample size precluded identifying significant symptomatic or radiological differences between responders and non-responders, we observed a trend toward lower frequency of extensive WMLs on FLAIR in responders (1/5 vs. 2/3).

Among the drainage-responsive vNIID patients, P67 and P115 underwent ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt. Their postoperative follow-ups over 1–2 years revealed sustained gait improvement, such as discontinuation of walking aids and the ability to resume 30-min daily walks, without shunt-related complications. The gait performance of patient P115 (pre- and post-operatively) is shown in the Video (Supplementa Material 1). Despite the decreased LvV following surgery, this patient exhibited a progression of frontal subcortical WMLs on FLAIR at the 2-year follow-up (Supplementary Fig. 3). No obvious progression of cognitive decline was observed.

Comparisons with NOTCH2NLC-negative iNPH patients

To delineate distinguishing features, we compared drainage-responsive vNIID with 17 age-matched male probable/definite iNPH patients, all of whom showed positive response to CSF tap-test and/or VP shunt between 2022 and 2024 in our center and had normal range of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeats (< 40) (Table 2). Compared with iNPH, drainage-responsive vNIID showed younger onset age (59.2 ± 7.3 years; P = 0.032) and several unique signs, including episodic encephalopathy (3/5; P = 0.007), bilateral miosis (2/5; P = 0.043), and family history of dementia/gait disturbance (3/5; P = 0.007). Although bladder dysfunction was common to both, urinary retention, rather than incontinence, was significantly overrepresented in vNIID (4/5; P = 0.009).

Brain pathologies in a case of ventriculomegalic NIID

Patient P19, whose longitudinal MRI follow-up has been reported in our previous study [15], died at age 74 due to long-term bedridden and heart failure. Autopsy revealed brain atrophy with a weight of 1160 g. HE and Kluver-Barrera stains showed cortical thinning and diffuse white matter pallor (Fig. 4A, B), particularly in deep and periventricular regions. Subcortical U-fibers were preserved, consistent with MRI (Fig. 4C). Spongiotic changes were not observed. Eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions were observed, positive for p62, ubiquitin, SUMO1, and uN2CpolyG (Fig. 4D–I). These inclusions (0.3–5 μm in diameter) comprised dense fibrils, with multiples forms and compact levels under the transmission electron microscope (Fig. 4J–L). Quantification of p62-positive inclusions demonstrated a widespread distribution across the brain, with higher densities in the gray matter (Fig. 4M) and high frequencies in ependymal cells, choroid plexus epithelial cells, and astrocytes, rather than in neurons (Fig. 4N; Supplementary Fig. 4A, B).

Nissl staining showed cerebral cortical neuronal pallor (Fig. 4O). Purkinje cell degeneration was supported by the observation of shrinkage, nuclear condensation, and empty Purkinje cell nests (Fig. 4P; Supplementary Fig. 5A). Myelin basic protein staining confirmed demyelination (Fig. 4Q). However, axons were preserved, as revealed by neurofilament staining (Fig. 4R). Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) immunostaining revealed remarkably reactive and dysmorphic astrocytes in the cerebral white matter, including swollen cell bodies and fragmented and clustered processes (Fig. 4S; Supplementary Fig. 5B), while these astrocytic pathologies were not obvious in the cortex (Supplementary Fig. 5B). GFAP signals were reduced in the cerebellum (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Microglial activation was observed (Fig. 4T; Supplementary Fig. 5D). Oligodendrocytes were not decreased (Supplementary Fig. 5E, F). Scattered mild tau and amyloid-β deposits were observed in limited regions, consistent with age-related co-pathologies, whereas significant tauopathy, amyloid plaque burden, or α-synuclein pathology was not identified.

Previous studies have demonstrated the role of aquaporins (AQPs) in the pathogenesis of hydrocephalus, with AQP1 and AQP4 mainly contributing to the production and the clearance of CSF, respectively [26]. We observed increased parenchymal AQP1 and AQP4 expression in this vNIID case, with the disappearance of AQP4 polarization toward perivascular astrocyte endfeet (Fig. 4U, V; Supplementary Fig. 5G, H); and choroid plexus degeneration (Supplementary Fig. 5I) with comparable AQP1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 5J). Ependymal cilia are essential regulators for maintaining CSF circulation, and their reduction has been mechanistically linked to the development of hydrocephalus [27]. Aligned with a high density of intranuclear inclusions within ependymal cells, a significant decrease in ARL13B-positive cilia was observed (Supplementary Fig. 5K, L). No vascular amyloid deposition (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B), vessel wall disruption (Supplementary Fig. 6C), fibrinogen leakage (Supplementary Fig. 6D), or lymphocytic infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 6E) was observed.

Discussion

This study expands the clinical spectrum of NOTCH2NLC-related NIID by systematically delineating an underrecognized hydrocephalus-like phenotype (vNIID). Our principal findings reveal that: (1) prominent ventriculomegaly is not a rare or incidental presentation in NIID; (2) patients with this phenotype exhibit clinical and radiological presentations resembling iNPH but retaining NIID-specific features; (3) a significant subset shows a positive, albeit partial, response to CSF drainage and shunt, suggesting a potentially treatable component; and (4) ependymal and astrocytic dysfunction may underlie disturbed CSF dynamics in this phenotype.

The identification of four individuals with NOTCH2NLC GGC repeat expansion among 498 adult patients initially supposed as communicating hydrocephalus is striking. Their symptomatic features and pathological findings definitely support the diagnose of NIID. This underscores an important diagnostic pitfall: NIID, particularly in East Asian populations, may masquerade as hydrocephalus in older adults. This is especially problematic because vNIID frequently lacks DWI hyperintensities—one of the most widely recognized imaging markers for NIID [1, 28]—making misdiagnosis as iNPH more likely. Of particular note is that the PACS-based searching strategy may have introduced selection bias toward more typical or advanced imaging phenotypes, and 4/498 should not be interpreted as the prevalence of NIID among adult communicating hydrocephalus patients. Future studies with large-scale genetic screening are needed to determine such a prevalence. Nevertheless, our MRI-based screening highlights the need for heightened diagnostic vigilance among neurologists and neurosurgeons evaluating presumed hydrocephalus. Particularly, routine consideration of NIID testing in adults with unexplained ventriculomegaly may prevent misdiagnosis.

In addition to the relatively high prevalence of disproportionate ventriculomegaly in a NIID cohort, we noted the clinical and imaging distinctions between the vNIID and nvNIID groups. These findings suggest that vNIID may represent a distinct clinical-radiological phenotype rather than a nonspecific comorbidity. The predominance of gait disturbance and reduced tremor frequency aligns vNIID with the classic presentation of iNPH [19]. The marked male predominance parallels known sex differences in iNPH [29, 30], suggesting possible sex-linked modifiers of susceptibility. Radiologically, vNIID is characterized by a smaller callosal angle, a lower frequency of DWI hyperintensities, a milder overall WML burden, and overrepresentation of a periventricular-dominant WML pattern. These features were also presented in some previously reported “DWI-negative” NIID cases [15, 31]. Additionally, longitudinal imaging demonstrated earlier onset and more rapid progression of ventriculomegaly in vNIID, arguing against ventricular enlargement as merely a consequence of late-stage atrophy.

A key therapeutic implication of our study is the objective improvement in gait and cognition following CSF drainage in 5/8 of vNIID patients, suggesting a substantial CSF-dynamics component superimposed on neurodegeneration. This contrasts with two earlier case reports where NIID patients with iNPH-like manifestations showed negative tap-test responses [16, 18]. A plausible explanation lies in the differing extents of WML burden: most responders in our study (4/5) exhibited mild and periventricular-predominant WMLs on FLAIR, whereas extensive WMLs were present in two of the three non-responders here and in all prior non-responders. Because periventricular region is composed of long white matter tracts that are sensitive to transependymal movement of ventricular CSF [32, 33], its selective vulnerability may represent a CSF-dynamics-related, potentially reversible component within the NIID spectrum. However, such a relationship between WML burden and CSF responsiveness remains speculative and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Despite sustained motor improvement following VP shunt in one patient, WML progression was observed during follow-up, echoing reports in NIID patients undergoing deep brain stimulation (DBS) who showed durable tremor improvement but later cognitive decline and imaging deterioration [34]. Although our prior longitudinal study demonstrated an age-related WML burden increase is part of the natural course of NIID [15], these observations do not exclude the possibility that intracranial interventions may elicit stress-related responses and accelerate white matter pathology in a vulnerable brain. Importantly, postoperative cognitive deterioration was not observed in our shunted patients or in another case receiving DBS during available follow-up [35]. Thus, these observations do not argue against CSF diversion, but emphasize careful patient selection and close surveillance.

Our neuropathological analysis, although based on a single vNIID case, provides important mechanistic insights. The typical NIID pathological features of this case—widespread p62- and uN2CpolyG-positive intranuclear inclusions [36, 37], abundant in astrocytes [38, 39], alongside preserved axons and demyelination with intact U-fibers [40]—support the toxic polyG theory previously proposed by Boivin et al. and our group [24, 36]. While various ultrastructural structures of inclusion fibrils have been reported [41, 42], we demonstrated multiple types coexisting within a single brain. Moreover, microglial activation was prominent, aligned with our prior study of polyG-driven neuroinflammation [23]. However, subcortical spongiosis—a pathological change linked to DWI hyperintensities [40]—was absent, consistent with vNIID imaging features. Unlike a recent report of vascular pathology [43], this case showed no amyloid angiopathy or blood–brain barrier disruption, underscoring pathological heterogeneity within NIID. Mild age-related tau and amyloid-β co-pathologies were also observed, which are common in elderly brains, including NIID [44–46], and were considered unlikely to account for the major clinicopathological features in this case.

An unexpected finding was the remarkable enrichment of inclusions in ependymal and choroid plexus epithelial cells, both of which contact the CSF directly may therefore contribute to disrupted CSF production and circulation. Although inclusions in ependyma and choroid plexus have been described in a few previous autopsy studies [14, 39], those cases either lacked genetic confirmation or were NOTCH2NLC-negative, and systematic quantification of inclusion burden was not performed. Our analysis further revealed a striking reduction in ARL13B-positive cilia on the ependymal surface. Given the essential role of ependymal cilia in generating directional CSF flow, such ciliary loss may contribute to impaired CSF dynamics. These observations raise a possibility that, in NOTCH2NLC-related NIID, the regional distribution of inclusions may be more relevant to the development of the hydrocephalus-like phenotype than the overall inclusion burden, molecular composition, or ultrastructural morphology. In addition, dysmorphic astrocytes and loss of AQP4 polarization in the white matter, observed both in a few recent studies [47, 48] and in our case, highlight astrocytic dysfunction as an important mechanism in NIID. Given the role of AQP4 in glymphatic transport and CSF-interstitial fluid exchange, its mislocalization may impair glymphatic clearance and disrupt CSF homeostasis in NIID [26, 49]. Collectively, these findings provide a potential structural and cellular basis for disturbed CSF dynamics and hydrocephalus-like phenotype in a single vNIID case, although validation in additional autopsied cases is necessary.

The high prevalence of hydrocephalus-like presentations and positive tap-test responses in our cohort, together with their distinctive pathology, indicates the presence of a mechanistically distinct hydrocephalus-like NIID phenotype. This phenotype appears to differ from—and may even attenuate—canonical NIID phenotypes, such as CMJ lesions, rather than simply representing NIID coincident with iNPH or a variant of complex NPH [50, 51]. This is not unprecedented in neurodegenerative disorders. Beyond NIID, ventriculomegaly disproportionate to parenchymal atrophy, with at least transient responsiveness to CSF drainage or shunt, has been reported in subsets of Alzheimer disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, and Huntington disease [52–54]. Particularly, they have also been noted in fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS), another GGC repeat disorder sharing phenotypic and mechanistic parallels with NIID [55, 56].

Given shared polyG-mediated toxicity and astrocytic involvement in both NIID and FXTAS [24, 57], we propose a unifying, albeit tentative, pathogenic model for the hydrocephalus-like phenotype in polyG diseases: accumulation of toxic polyG aggregates within ependymal/choroid plexus epithelial cells and astrocytes leads to disturbed CSF dynamics, gliosis with reducing brain compliance, and glymphatic dysfunction through loss of polarized AQP4. The confluence of these factors predisposes to ventriculomegaly and periventricular-dominant WML pattern, leading to this phenotype. The lack of association between GGC repeat size and ventriculomegaly observed in this study, although verifications by larger cohort are needed, suggests that additional modifiers—such as sex, modifying genes, or environmental factors—may facilitate such a pathophysiological process. Future studies incorporating volumetric, CSF dynamic, and longitudinal biomarkers are required to establish biologically grounded classification frameworks for NIID.

Our observations underscore the importance of distinguishing NIID from pure iNPH to avoid diagnostic misclassification and to identify NIID patients who may benefit from CSF diversion. Obviously, typical iNPH markers such as elevated EI and acute callosal angle [19] are insufficient for differentiation. As genetic test and skin biopsy can establish NIID definitively, several clinical clues may raise suspicion for NIID in patients presenting with presumed iNPH from a practical perspective. These include younger onset age, family history of dementia or gait disturbance, autonomic dysfunction (especially urinary retention and miosis), episodic encephalopathy, and paravermal FLAIR hyperintensities. Although not formally evaluated in this cohort, peripheral neuropathy may provide an additional discriminator [18]. The absence of DESH in our vNIID group, while not statistically definitive, suggests a potentially mechanistic divergence from classic iNPH. Overall, these distinguishing features should be interpreted as exploratory observations rather than definitive discriminators.

Several limitations of our study warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, the EI-based definition, while practical and data-informed, may not fully capture the biological spectrum of ventriculomegaly in NIID, and future studies should incorporate volumetric and CSF dynamic measures. Therefore, the term “vNIID” in this study should be interpreted as a descriptive imaging-based subgroup, not a claim of a distinct disease entity. Secondly, the interpretation of CSF drainage and shunt responsiveness should be approached with caution. The sample size, though substantial for a rare disease, remains limited for robust subgroup analyses, and the long-term outcome data are inadequate. The absence of a control group of vNIID patients receiving sham operation, and potential short-term fluctuations in gait and cognition limit definitive conclusions. While our findings suggest that CSF-based intervention may provide clinical benefits in selected vNIID patients, whether such improvement represents a transient and symptomatic relief in a neurodegenerative context, or a disease-modifying effect in accordance with iNPH-like pathophysiology remains uncertain. Finally, neuropathological interpretations are based on a single autopsy case without direct comparison to nvNIID or iNPH controls, which should therefore be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

In conclusion, we identify a reproducible hydrocephalus-like phenotype associated with NOTCH2NLC gene and characterized by prominent ventriculomegaly, distinctive clinical-radiological-pathological features, and partial responsiveness to CSF drainage and shunt, highlighting the need for diagnostic vigilance in adult-onset communicating hydrocephalus. Our findings challenge the traditional view of NIID as an entirely progressive and untreatable neurodegenerative condition, suggesting that CSF circulation dysfunction may partially contribute to its neurological dysfunctions. Further validation by larger, controlled, and longitudinal studies are required to clarify the mechanisms, natural history, and optimal management of this phenotype.
