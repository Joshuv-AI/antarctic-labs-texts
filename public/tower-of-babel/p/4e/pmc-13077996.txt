Health workforce planning methods in rural and remote primary care: a scoping review
Authors: Geoff Argus, Belinda Gavaghan, Jean Spinks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRural and remote communities experience poorer health outcomes and lower life expectancy than their metropolitan counterparts. This is in part due to the lack of access to an appropriately skilled health workforce to meet their primary healthcare needs. Various health workforce planning and forecasting methods have been described in the literature at the country and regional level; however, methods designed specifically for rural and remote communities may be required. The aim of this scoping review is to identify and critically analyse existing methods for health workforce planning and forecasting in rural and remote primary care to improve access to primary care services in those communities.MethodsThe review was conducted using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). A comprehensive search strategy was developed and conducted in PubMed, SCOPUS, EMBASE and CINAHL for peer-reviewed articles published in English between January 2005 and July 2025.ResultsThe search yielded 3938 records for screening and 57 full-text studies were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 26 articles included in the final review. Articles originated from 10 countries with the majority from Australia. Data sources used in planning included administrative registries, census and demographic data, health service utilisation records, workload and service statistics, population surveys, and qualitative inputs such as expert consensus and stakeholder engagement. Five approaches to workforce planning were identified: Workload Indicators of Staffing Need (WISN), geospatial accessibility and catchment models, stock-flow forecasting models, skill-mix and competency-based planning, and synthetic estimation modelling.ConclusionsFindings highlight a lack of clear guidance for policymakers in how to choose planning methods aligned to the models of care used (or visioned) in rural and remote areas. Whilst a “whole of system” approach at a local level shows promise, there is a lack of clearly accepted methods and empirical evidence evaluating the effectiveness of such approaches. Additionally, the limited integration of workforce competency metrics alongside traditional headcount represents a notable gap, constraining opportunities for competency and skills-based planning at scale.Planning undertaken in isolation from the different models of care employed in rural and remote areas is likely to exacerbate workforce maldistribution. Progress requires the creation of integrated, longitudinal, competency-linked workforce datasets that link administrative, clinical and population domains to capture the dynamic realities of practice, including geography, mobility, workload intensity, models of care, scopes of practice and role flexibility to inform responsive rural and remote primary care workforce planning and resource allocation.

Introduction

Health workforce planning is fundamental in sustaining and improving the quality of healthcare services [1]. Effective planning ensures that population health needs are met through the supply of an available, equitably distributed and suitably skilled health workforce sufficient to meet current and future healthcare needs [1]. This is particularly critical in primary health care (PHC), which is foundational to the overall healthcare system [2, 3]. PHC adopts a whole-of-society approach to address the health needs of the population [3]. Primary care, defined as the service-fronting component of PHC, aims to support the physical, mental and social wellbeing of individuals and communities, and is delivered by a range of health and community care professionals operating within their specific scope of professional practice [2]. Core functions of primary care are first contact accessibility, comprehensiveness, continuity, coordination and people-centredness, empowering people to take an active role in their health and healthcare needs [2]. Thus, effective health workforce planning in primary care is critical for jurisdictions to meet the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal target 3.8 of universal health coverage [4].

In rural and remote areas, health workforce planning is challenged by unique social, economic, and geographical factors that influence both the supply of and demand for healthcare services [5]. These include geographic spread, low population density, infrastructure limitations, higher costs of delivering healthcare and persistent maldistribution of the health workforce, particularly for primary care services [6]. Additionally, rural populations often face higher rates of chronic disease and multimorbidity, lower life expectancy, and greater barriers to accessing culturally appropriate and accessible healthcare [7]. Ensuring that health workforce planning methods in the rural primary care context are fit-for-purpose is therefore of paramount importance.

Health workforce planning is a strategic process designed to ensure that staffing levels align with service delivery goals [8]. It includes the development of clear objectives and policy alongside a process of continuous evaluation [1]. The use of a structured planning framework can help to define the approach to be taken. In particular, deciding whether to maintain a workforce sufficient to meet current primary care service levels, which includes the strong assumption that current service levels are adequate (historical service utilisation approach), versus adopting a population-based approach where the health service needs are determined using an epidemiological analysis (needs-based approach) [9].

Workforce forecasting or projection is the result of methods used to determine the gap between current and future workforce supply based on the demand for health services, providing a way to operationalise the workforce plan [10]. Accurately forecasting workforce supply is likely to be affected by whether demand is measured by either service utilisation or needs-based approaches. Forecasting approaches must therefore align with broader health policy objectives and workforce strategies, inclusive of adopted models of care, skill-mix, practitioner scope of practice and population needs. A recent scoping review by Lee et al. identified several forecasting methods, with the traditional stock-and-flow approach being the most common [11]. Other methods include utilisation-based population-to-provider ratios, system dynamics and agent-based modelling [11]. These methods have tended to be used for health workforce planning at the national or subregional level and do not necessarily address the equitable distribution or adequacy of the workforce at the level of smaller geographical areas [11].

This limitation is particularly critical in less populated areas where the health workforce is often scarce and solutions to ‘thin’ markets may involve consideration of the skill-mix of practitioners working to top of scope or even extended scope of practice, including rural generalism and employing alternative fit-for-purpose models of care [12]. In the absence of a universally agreed gold standard for rural and remote health workforce planning, these considerations raise questions of whether centrally based planning approaches and methods are effective for rural and remote areas or whether they need to be adjusted to reflect the unique context in which health services are delivered. The aim of this scoping review is to identify and analyse contemporary research on primary care health workforce planning in rural and remote areas with a view to identifying potential fit-for-purpose strategies to inform policy development. The review describes existing approaches and methods, identifies gaps in the literature and provides future research directions to improve universal access to rural and remote primary care services.

Methods

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological framework was used in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) statement [13].

Search strategy

To determine the appropriate search terms, a preliminary literature review was conducted to identify key terms and MeSH terms. A search was conducted specifically to identify any current reviews on the topic, and reference lists were examined. The final search terms were developed by and agreed between authors GA and JS in consultation with two university librarians who advised on the terms independently. Database searches of PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and CINAHL were conducted on 4 July 2025. The searches were limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English between 1 January 2005 and 1 July 2025. Peer-reviewed literature was considered the highest level of evidence available, and a 20-year time horizon was considered sufficient to include the most contemporary methods used in health workforce planning. The search excluded editorials, letters to editors, conference abstracts, and commentaries, and manuscripts written in a language other than English. Study selection

All records identified through the database searches were imported into Covidence [14], a web-based platform designed for conducting scoping reviews that enables the tracking of records from initial screening to full-text review. Duplicate records were identified in Covidence, checked and manually removed. Titles and abstracts of identified records were independently screened by reviewers (GA, JS) and where there were discrepancies, discussions were held between reviewers to resolve conflicts. Following the exclusion of records based on title and abstract screening, a full-text assessment of the remaining articles was conducted independently by GA and BG using the PCC (population, concept, context) framework (Table 1). Data extraction

A comprehensive data extraction tool was developed in Excel, adapted from the JBI Manual of Evidence Synthesis [13]. The tool was agreed by all reviewers and trialed with several articles before finalisation. Data were extracted from the final set of articles, including author, title, year, country, aims, study design, included professions, data sources, geographical level of analysis, workforce planning method utilised (supply and/or demand), key findings, strengths/weaknesses. Data extraction was completed by one reviewer (GA) and then checked for accuracy by JS and BG with agreed revisions implemented. No study was excluded based on quality as the purpose of the scoping review was to provide an overview of the existing literature rather than a critical appraisal of the quality of individual studies.

Results

Database search results yielded 9147 articles, of which 5209 were duplicates, leaving 3938 for title and abstract screening once duplicates were removed. Of these, 57 articles were determined eligible for full-text review with a final 26 articles meeting eligibility criteria for inclusion in the review. Whilst most studies focused on medical workforce planning, particularly general practitioners (GPs)/family physicians (n = 24, 92%), several also included nurses and midwives (n = 16, 62%), allied health professionals (n = 11, 42%) and community health workers (n = 6, 23%). Ten studies (38%) considered GPs only. Leach et al. [18] also included complementary medicine providers (e.g., chiropractors) in the mix of rural service utilisation. Two studies [27, 30] incorporated competency-based models which moved beyond health profession categories and instead mapped population health needs to required professional skills and functions.

Across all studies the central focus was rural and/or remote primary health care, although some studies addressed specific diseases or populations including diabetes [27], HIV and malaria [38], ageing and dependency [36], and mental health [39]. In Australia, several studies specifically considered the needs of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander (First Peoples) populations [22, 24, 25].

The studies examined workforce planning across various geographical units of analysis reflecting the various spatial contexts in which rural and remote primary care workforce challenges are addressed. Through the extraction process, the authors used an inductive approach to group articles into seven geographical categories: facility (n = 3)[23, 30, 33], small area catchment (n = 2)[27, 40], community/town (n = 6)[19, 22, 26, 28, 29, 37], district (n = 6)[15, 21, 31, 36, 38, 39], regional (n = 6)[18, 20, 24, 25, 32, 34], state/province (n = 2)[17, 35], national (n = 1)[16]. Facility was defined as planning occurring at the facility or clinic level within a rural or remote context. Small area catchment was defined as planning that occurred at the small population level with no specific geographic boundaries, as opposed to community/town level which was specific to identified rural and remote community or town boundaries. District was defined as planning that occurred at a local government health service level incorporating several communities/towns within the catchment, whereas regional was defined as planning that occurred at a larger, multi-district level but smaller than a state/province level. National refers to Harrison and Britt’s [16] Australian workforce projection study that examined the distribution of the general practitioner workforce relative to patient demand across all geographic levels in the country.

Data sources

Across the studies, data sources for health workforce planning were diverse across both supply and demand sides. Sources included administrative registries, census and demographic data, health service utilisation records, workload and service statistics, population surveys, and qualitative inputs such as expert consensus and stakeholder engagement.

Supply-side data sources

The most common supply-side data sources included workforce registries and administrative datasets (n = 9), which were predominantly used to generate provider-to-population ratios [20, 21, 24–26, 31, 35, 37, 40]. Census and demographic datasets were also employed to provide denominators for population ratios and to underpin geospatial access models with studies in Australia, Japan, and the Netherlands incorporating small-area census data into workforce distribution models [19, 20, 37, 40]. Some studies incorporated training and workforce pipeline data, such as registrar placements and retirement projections to build stock-and-flow simulations to forecast future workforce availability [19, 36, 37].

Demand-side data sources

Demand-side data sources included census and demographic data, epidemiological and morbidity data, workload and service utilisation data, accessibility modelling inputs and service-user surveys. Health service utilisation records, insurance data, and consultation statistics were used in studies in Australia, the United States and Canada [16, 17, 21, 35]. These data allowed estimation of service demand based on actual historical service utilisation but did not account for unmet need in populations with poor access. Several studies used area-level indicators of health status and deprivation. Butler et al. [15] linked GP location to measures of socioeconomic disadvantage, whilst Mu and Hall [21] modelled regional variation in GP use against morbidity and disability indices. McGrail et al. [19] utilised a combination of population size, health needs weightings, travel behaviour and demographic change to develop an index of service access. Pagaiya et al. [36] and Goma et al. [38] linked service requirements to demographic change, ageing, and disease burden (i.e. HIV, malaria). Some studies relied on facility-level workload and service statistics, particularly those using the Workload Indicators of Staffing Need (WISN) approach [23, 32, 33], where service logs and time standards were used to calculate staffing requirements directly.

Other demand data sources included patient-reported surveys and expert or participatory inputs. Leach et al. [18] used a population survey to capture expressed demand for both conventional and complementary health services in South Australia, highlighting patterns of need not available in administrative datasets. Thomas et al. [28] employed a Delphi consensus method with rural health experts to define minimum population thresholds for resident services. Panzera et al. [22] and Ramsden et al. [24] used participatory action research to incorporate stakeholder and community feedback into regional workforce planning. Competency-based models drew on clinical guidelines, psychosocial benchmarks, and cost data to translate needs into functional workforce requirements [27, 30].

Workforce planning and forecasting methods

A wide range of workforce planning and forecasting methods were identified, reflecting both supply-side and demand-side approaches. Methods included workload-based staffing tools, geospatial accessibility modelling, microsimulation and forecasting, utilisation-based approaches, service- and needs-based frameworks and broader conceptual or integrated models. Whilst methods varied by setting and research focus, most studies incorporated both supply and demand elements, although the extent of integration differed. A summary of the approaches is provided below.

Workload indicators of staffing need (WISN)

Three studies applied the WHO WISN approach to estimate staffing requirements by comparing available staff capacity with workload demands [23, 32, 36]. Supply was operationalised as available working time, existing headcount/full-time equivalent (FTE), and health discipline mix, whilst demand drew on service statistics aligned to service standards and epidemiological indicators. WISN applications highlighted substantial staff shortages, excessive workloads, and scope-of-practice gaps in rural and remote settings.

Geospatial accessibility and catchment models

Seven studies incorporated geospatial approaches, including the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method, travel-time analysis and other Geographic Information System (GIS)-based analyses, to examine access to primary care services [15, 19, 20, 26, 29, 34, 40]. Supply was generally defined by provider-to-population ratios, FTE, and geographic distribution of health professionals. Demand incorporated population counts, demographic and socioeconomic indices, and travel time thresholds. These analyses consistently showed poorer primary care access in rural and remote communities.

Stock-flow forecasting models

Three studies modelled workforce inflows and outflows to forecast future supply relative to population needs [20, 30, 41]. Supply elements included training pipelines, migration, attrition, and retirement, whilst demand was estimated from demographic change, service utilisation and morbidity trends. These stock-flow approaches were used to test policy scenarios, anticipate rural shortages and model retention interventions.

Skill-mix and competency-based planning

Several studies prioritised skill-mix and competencies rather than profession-specific headcounts to examine how planning around functions, tasks and capabilities enabled flexible team design to support rural and remote health service delivery. Goma et al. [38] developed a competency-based workforce planning framework aligning staffing requirements with the epidemiological burden of disease. Instead of focusing on profession-specific categories, the approach identified competencies required for priority services, supporting redistribution of roles and optimisation of available skills. Segal et al. [27] translated population health needs into functions, competencies and team structures, emphasising role substitution, equity, financing, and training and education pathways, providing a conceptual skill-mix model for needs-based planning in treating and managing type-2 diabetes. Panzera et al. [22] linked health workforce training pipelines with skill-mix optimisation by focusing on competencies developed through training pathways to develop a fit-for-purpose workforce, particularly in remote settings. Pagaiya et al. [36] and Petersen et al. [39] incorporated task-shifting assumptions which allow professions to substitute for one another to demonstrate how redistribution of tasks could address rural workforce shortages more effectively than profession-specific ratios.

Synthetic estimation modelling

De Graaf-Ruizendaal and de Bakker [37] employed small-area synthetic estimation to predict local general practice requirements. Registry-based workforce supply data were combined with socio-demographic predictors of service-use demand and adjusted for accessibility to produce fine-grained estimates to guide workforce resource allocation.

Discussion

This review provides a comprehensive overview of contemporary approaches to health workforce planning in rural and remote primary care. The findings demonstrate that health workforce planning in rural and remote primary care is multifaceted and data dependent. Across 26 studies, methodological diversity was evident across country contexts, professional scopes, rurality classifications and spatial units of analysis, reflecting the diverse ways in which workforce planning is operationalised within rural primary care contexts. Planning methods were applied across high, middle and low-income settings with rural and remote contexts ranging from small communities to large regional catchments and across multidisciplinary primary care teams incorporating medical, nursing, allied health and community-based roles. Planning approaches ranged from static, supply-driven forecasting to dynamic, needs-based and competency and skill-mix focused planning. The findings illustrate that planning approaches share the challenge of how to reconcile the measurable components of workforce supply and demand with the contextual realities of rural and remote health service delivery, geographic inequities and evolving workforce competency, professional scopes of practice and skill needs. No one method or approach was found to be suitable in every context. A small number of papers explicitly chose a particular planning method to align with health system objectives, best reflected in Panzera et al. [22], where systems thinking was incorporated with a needs-based planning approach. Participatory and/or local-level approaches such as this hold promise, but further work is required to establish recognised methodologies and evaluate outcomes.

The variability of data sources across studies emerged as both a defining feature and a limiting factor. Workforce planning methods are constrained by the structure, granularity, and interoperability of underlying data sources. Supply-side models often depended on administrative registries and practitioner registration data, providing information for workforce projections but offering limited insight into workforce configuration, such as part-time work, cross-sector employment, or skill-mix and variation [15, 16, 19–21, 25, 30, 38]. These administrative datasets focus on specific health professions, excluding others who lack comprehensive registration data. Demand and needs-based models relied heavily on utilisation and epidemiological datasets, which may undercount populations who are traditionally underserved or who experience barriers to accessing primary care services [17–19, 22–24, 26–31, 33, 34, 36, 37, 39, 40]. The inclusion of measures of socioeconomic disadvantage and disability such as those used by Butler et al. [15] and Mu and Hall [21] may help to address this issue.

This variability in data sources produces not only inconsistency across methods, but also the potential tendency for models to reproduce the data systems that sustain them. For instance, where routinely collected service data are used for planning models, unmet need remains unaccounted for. Similarly, where existing profession-specific registry data dominate, issues of professional skill, competency and scopes of practice may not be considered. The findings suggest that data heterogeneity is not necessarily a methodological flaw but likely reflects existing structures across health and data governance systems. McGrail et al. [19, 20] and Suzuki et al. [40] demonstrate the potential of data integration to transform modelling from a static descriptive tool into a dynamic, decision-support system. Thus, data systems should be viewed as critical infrastructure for evidence-informed rural and remote primary care health workforce planning and governance.

Across the included literature, workforce availability was conceptualised through several dimensions: sufficient numbers, functional capacity, accessibility and responsiveness. Collectively, these approaches suggest the determinants of workforce availability are interdependent. For example, a rural or remote area may exhibit an apparent shortage of healthcare workers by traditional supply metrics yet meet community needs if contemporary models of care are implemented with flexible, competency-based teams [27, 34, 36, 38, 39]. Conversely, apparently sufficient staffing levels may still mask inequitable access when geographic, socioeconomic disadvantage, workload and practice scope factors are not considered [15].

There is growing recognition that workforce capability, models of care and professional scopes of practice are as important as workforce capacity to meet the primary care needs of rural and remote communities. Several studies[22, 23, 27, 32, 36, 38, 39] conceptualised workforce requirements through the lens of functional competency and task configuration. For example, Segal et al.[27] translated care guidelines into workforce competencies, allowing estimation of functional rather than profession-specific need, whilst Petersen et al.[39] and Goma et al.[38] demonstrated how redistributing tasks across professions could expand services in resource-limited settings. These findings suggest that competency-based planning provides an important link between local service realities and macro-level workforce forecasts. By focusing on the skills and competencies needed, rather than profession-specific forecasts, such models can accommodate local adaptation and support multidisciplinary, generalist practice, critical for effective rural and remote primary care service delivery. However, health systems and professional bodies do not routinely capture competency data or publish contemporary clinical guidelines with sufficient detail to enable task configuration, which was highlighted by Segal et al.[27] in the development of their Workforce Evidence-Based (WEB) planning model for diabetes. Without longitudinal information on training, roles, and evolving professional competencies and productivity, these approaches remain conceptually strong but operationally constrained. The collective evidence underscores the need for workforce information systems that embed competency metrics alongside traditional headcount data to enable function, competency and skills-based planning at scale.

Geospatial analyses provided insights into the relationship between workforce distribution and accessibility. Approaches such as the 2SFCA method [19, 29], travel-time and distance analysis [34], and catchment-based demand estimation [35] revealed that workforce adequacy should also be understood in relation to geospatial context. Across studies, the accessibility of primary care services decreased with greater remoteness, even when overall workforce numbers appeared sufficient for community size. These findings further highlight that geography is a persistent determinant of healthcare access. Geospatial frameworks not only quantify workforce distribution but may also indicate the need for a range of different interventions such as outreach services, telehealth, or targeted incentives for workforce recruitment and retention. However, geographic accessibility does not encompass other aspects of service accessibility such as cost, cultural safety, or barriers to physical accessibility such as available transport and/or building accessibility and therefore should not be mistaken as a global measure.

Workload-based methods, particularly those using the WISN framework, linked observed service activity to projected staffing requirements [23, 28, 32, 33]. These approaches assess productivity (technical efficiency) and provide an understanding of workload intensity to facilitate workforce allocation aligned with service demand at the rural facility level. Studies that employed workload-based methods indicated that inequity stems from maldistribution rather than shortage, with some facilities experiencing persistent understaffing and others operating with staffing surpluses [32]. Standardised time norms and activity ratios may underestimate the complexity of work in rural settings, where staff often undertake multiple roles and travel substantial distances to perform outreach functions. When combined with spatial and competency data, workload models may provide important diagnostic tools for identifying efficiency gaps and informing equitable primary care resource allocation in rural and remote communities.

Methodological sophistication is likely to achieve impact when aligned with institutional structures and decision-making processes. Approaches that embed health workforce planning within participatory or system-level governance frameworks are likely to lead to greater policy translation [22, 24, 25, 27, 30]. Collaboration between policymakers, health service leaders, professional bodies and communities is required to facilitate refinement of planning models and stronger accountability. Findings suggest workforce planning can be framed as an element of adaptive health service governance, a process that links data, analysis and policy action through continuous feedback. When disconnected from governance, even advanced health workforce planning models risk limited policy influence.

Limitations

The review examined studies from peer-reviewed literature and as such did not consider grey literature. It is important to note, however, that methods employed in grey literature may be useful in further understanding the complexities of rural and remote primary care workforce planning. The search terms were also limited to papers published in English only. There was variability in reporting detail and methodological transparency among included studies. Differences in data availability and terminology limited direct comparison of workforce planning models. Some papers provided incomplete methodological details, particularly regarding assumptions in simulation and spatial models. Furthermore, publication bias towards high-income settings, especially Australia, may overrepresent data-rich methods relative to low-resource contexts.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that health workforce planning for rural and remote primary care is diverse and multidimensional, requiring the alignment of data, workload, competency, and geospatial approaches within responsive and adaptive governance structures. Workload-based frameworks translate local service activity into operational benchmarks, whilst geospatial approaches demonstrate the spatial aspects of healthcare access and workforce distribution. Competency-based and skill-mix approaches demonstrate the functional and adaptive dimensions of workforce requirements, whilst data-driven projection and simulation models enable scenario testing and policy foresight. When considered together, these approaches may form a complementary ecosystem of potential methods rather than competing paradigms. Their integration provides a comprehensive understanding of workforce needs and enables alignment between planning, service delivery and policy design. Critically, this review identified limited evidence on how the incorporation of practitioner scope of practice and alternative models of care are specifically addressed in current planning methods, representing a significant gap in the literature.

Our findings underscore the lack of guidance for policymakers and that the choice of workforce planning approach and methodology to date has been as much a question of data availability, leadership and governance as of modelling techniques. The sophistication of planning is limited by the quality, interoperability and granularity of the data on which it depends. Fragmented or outdated information systems may constrain innovation and weaken accountability. Future progress therefore hinges on the creation of integrated, longitudinal and competency-linked workforce datasets that bridge administrative, clinical and population domains. These systems should capture the dynamic realities of practice, including geography, mobility, workload intensity, models of care, scopes of practice and role flexibility to inform responsive rural and remote primary care workforce planning and resource allocation.

Sustainable and equitable workforce development depends on continuous linkage between information, analysis, and decision-making. Building interoperable workforce data systems, embedding participatory modelling in decision-making processes, and strengthening institutional capacity will be crucial to advancing sustainable and equitable workforce planning across diverse rural and remote primary care contexts.
