Perioperative and short-term oncological outcomes of laparoscopic versus open pancreaticoduodenectomy: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with GRADE evaluation
Authors: Israa Ahmed Qutob, Asmaa Soliman, Amr Mohamed Shawkat, Ahmed Adel Abdel Azim, Mahmoud Mohamed Gad, Aliaa Gamal, Eman Ayman Nada, Sally Seliem, Weam Aldiban, Mohanad Yaser Assem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical resection through pancreatoduodenectomy (PD) represents the only curative option for periampullary and pancreatic head tumors. Recently, laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy (LPD) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to the open approach (OPD). However, the safety, feasibility, and short-term oncologic adequacy of LPD compared to OPD remain a matter of controversy. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare perioperative and short-term oncologic outcomes of LPD versus OPD in patients with periampullary and pancreatic head primaries.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for RCTs comparing LPD and OPD in adult periampullary tumor patients. Key outcomes analyzed included operative time, blood loss, hospital length of stay, complications, mortality, and lymph node harvest. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB−2 tool, and the certainty of evidence was graded via GRADE.ResultsSeven RCTs were included, revealing that LPD is associated with longer operative times (MD~50.90 min) but significantly reduced blood loss (MD−88.83 mL), shorter hospital stays (MD−1.33 days), and fewer transfusions (RR 0.75). No significant differences were observed in 90-day mortality, overall or severe complications, lymph node retrieval, or R0 resection rates. The GRADE rating was high for clinically relevant gastric emptying, and moderate or low certainty for most other outcomes.ConclusionLPD provides statistically significant perioperative advantages without compromising oncological outcomes, suggesting it is a safe and effective alternative to OPD in experienced centers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03832-0.Keywords: Laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy, Open pancreaticoduodenectomy, Perioperative outcomes, Oncological outcomes, Mortality and morbidity

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD), commonly referred to as the Whipple procedure, is considered the standard surgical approach for resectable periampullary and pancreatic head tumors. However, PD is a technically complex operation because of the intricate anatomical relationships between the pancreatic head and major vascular structures, including the portal vein, hepatic artery, and superior mesenteric vessels [1–3].

Traditionally, PD has been performed via an open approach (open pancreatoduodenectomy, OPD). With advances in minimally invasive surgery, laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy (LPD) has emerged as an alternative technique. LPD was first introduced by Michel Gagner and Alain Pomp in 1994, initially for staging or palliative procedures in pancreatic malignancies. Subsequent technological progress and growing surgical expertise have enabled the application of LPD for curative resection in selected patients. Nevertheless, its adoption remains limited due to the substantial technical demands, steep learning curve, and the risk of pancreas-specific complications such as postoperative pancreatic fistula and hemorrhage [4, 5]. The proximity of the pancreas to major vascular structures and the intricate reconstructive steps involved contribute to the steep learning curve associated with this technique. Despite these challenges, LPD offers potential advantages typical of minimally invasive surgery, such as reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery. While laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy has been widely accepted, total laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy (TLPD) remains underutilized in many centers due to its complexity and longer operative times [6].

Regarding postoperative hospital stay, evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remains limited and somewhat inconsistent. Two single-center RCTs- PLOT and PADULAP- demonstrated that laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy (LPD) was associated with shorter hospitalization times compared to open pancreatoduodenectomy (OPD) [7, 8]. In contrast, the LEOPARD−2 trial, a multicenter phase 2/3 RCT, was prematurely terminated due to increased rates of severe complications in the LPD group, raising important concerns about the safety and generalizability of the approach in broader clinical settings [9]. More recently, a large multicenter RCT conducted by Wang et al. found that LPD was associated with a modest but statistically significant reduction in hospital stay compared to OPD [10, 11]. Additionally, a trial by Yoon et al. suggested that LPD may be associated with a slight improvement in the time to functional recovery, particularly when performed by experienced surgeons in carefully selected patients [12].

Given the ongoing debate and variability in the literature, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to comprehensively evaluate perioperative and oncological outcomes of laparoscopic versus open pancreatoduodenectomy for periampullary and pancreatic head tumors.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis based on the principles of the PRISMA guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook for Interventions [13]. We had prospectively registered this study in PROSPERO and can be found with the number (CRD420251174732).

Study selection

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English that evaluated adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing pancreatoduodenectomy for periampullary and pancreatic head tumors. The intervention of interest was LPD, compared to open OPD. Eligible studies were required to report at least one of the following outcomes: postoperative morbidity, mortality, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, time to oral intake, or oncological outcomes (defined in this study as short-term surrogate oncologic parameters including R0 resection rate and lymph node yield). Where reported, information regarding preoperative tumor staging and the use of neoadjuvant therapy (e.g., chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy) was also extracted. Preoperative staging was generally performed using contrast-enhanced computed tomography and multidisciplinary evaluation according to institutional protocols. However, reporting of staging methods and neoadjuvant treatment was inconsistent across trials; therefore, these variables were summarized descriptively and not used as eligibility criteria.

We excluded non-English publications, theses, observational studies, case reports, reviews, non-randomized studies, conference abstracts, and studies with insufficient or non-extractable data.

Literature search strategy and screening

A thorough literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science without applying any filters. To enhance sensitivity, both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords were utilized. Additionally, we had verified the completeness of our search by cross-checking the included studies against the ISGPS Evidence Map of Pancreatic Surgery, which represents a continuously updated, living overview of available trials in this field. All trials listed in the evidence map that met our eligibility criteria were already identified through our database search, confirming the completeness of our strategy [14].

Regarding screening, duplicate records were identified and removed using the Rayyan platform, which also facilitated the process at the title, abstract, and full-text levels. All screening procedures were independently performed by two reviewers, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus or, when necessary, adjudicated by a third reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was carried out independently by two authors using a standardized spreadsheet. The extracted variables included: (1) study design and setting, (2) characteristics of the study population, (3) reported outcomes, and (4) risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB−2 tool. When available, additional variables were extracted, including preoperative staging methods (e.g., computed tomography or multidisciplinary assessment) and the reported use of neoadjuvant therapy. These data were summarized descriptively because they were inconsistently reported across the included trials. When multiple publications referred to the same trial, we treated them as a single study. Outcome data were extracted from each report as needed. The most recent and complete report was used for any overlapping outcomes to avoid double-counting.

For quality assurance, one reviewer performed the initial data extraction, which was subsequently cross-checked by a second reviewer. The Cochrane RoB−2 tool evaluated bias across five key domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer [15]. Additionally, the certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach, implemented using GRADEpro GDT [16].

Data analysis

For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., mortality, morbidity, margin positivity), we calculated pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous outcomes (e.g., operative time, blood loss, hospital stay), we used mean differences (MDs) or standardized mean differences (SMDs) depending on the consistency of measurement scales. Meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4. A random effects model was used owing to the surgical nature of this study.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our results by excluding one study at a time to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall effect size.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Studies were stratified based on the included population, whether suffering malignant tumors only or benign, premalignant, and malignant ones. Separate meta-analyses were conducted within each subgroup using a random-effects model. Differences between subgroups were evaluated using the χ² test for subgroup differences, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Heterogeneity assessment

Heterogeneity was assessed by visual inspection of forest plots and quantified using the Chi-square (χ²) test and the I² statistic. An I² value ≥ 50% or a p-value < 0.1 was considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity, consistent with Cochrane recommendations.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 436 records were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, seven RCTs with eight reports were included in the final analysis [5, 7–10, 17–19]. Figure 1.

The included studies involved patients undergoing either LPD or OPD for periampullary and pancreatic head tumors. Baseline characteristics were comparable across groups in most studies. However, differences were noted in surgical expertise, perioperative protocols, and geographic settings, especially between Asian and Western cohorts, which contributed to heterogeneity in certain outcomes. Quality assessment

In the risk of bias assessment, four randomized controlled trials were judged to have an overall risk of bias of “some concerns,” primarily due to potential deviations from the intended interventions and issues related to outcome measurement. Three RCTs were rated as having a high risk of bias, attributed primarily to concerns in the measurement of outcomes. These findings indicate that while some studies provided moderately reliable evidence, the results from the other studies should be interpreted with caution. Figure 2.

Risk of bias Assessment of included studies (*Qin 2024 and Wang 2021 are two reports of the same RCT)

Primary outcomes

Operative time

LPD was associated with a significantly longer operative duration compared to OPD (MD = 50.90 min; 95% CI: 35.94 to 65.85; p < 0.00001). High heterogeneity was observed (I² = 83%) that could not be resolved. The test for subgroup differences was insignificant (p = 0.07). Figure 3.

Forest plot illustrating the comparison of operative time (minutes) across studies

Intraoperative blood loss

LPD significantly reduced blood loss (MD=−88.83 mL; 95% CI,−153.56 to -24.11; p = 0.007), though heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 97%). This heterogeneity could not be mitigated. Subgroups showed insignificant differences (p = 0.99). Figure 4A.

A. Pooled analysis of intraoperative blood loss across included studies. B. Pooled analysis of intraoperative blood transfusion rates

Intraoperative transfusion

The need for transfusion was significantly lower in the LPD group (RR = 0.75; 95% CI, 0.61 to 0.94; p = 0.01), with no statistical heterogeneity (I² = 0%). Tumor-specific subgroup differences were insignificant (P = 0.58). Figure 4B.

Hospital stay

Patients who underwent LPD experienced shorter hospitalizations (MD =−1.33 days; 95% CI, -2.57 to−0.09; p = 0.04). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 25%). There were significant subgroup differences (p = 0.04), in which patients with malignant tumors who underwent LPD had a more clinically profound shorter duration of hospital stay compared with those with mixed tumors and both were still significantly favouring LPD over OPD (MD = -6.00 days; 95% CI, -10.57 to -1.43 Vs MD = -1.04 days; 95% CI, -1.93 to -0.15). Figure 5 A.

A. includes the pooled analysis of hospital stay across the included studies. B. the pooled analysis of ICU stay. C. the sensitivity analysis of ICU stay

ICU stay

Initially, no significant difference was observed between LPD and OPD (MD = -0.23 days; 95% CI, -1.01 to 0.56; p = 0.58), with both significant heterogeneity (I² = 77%) and subgroup differences (p = 0.003). The subgroup with malignant tumours treated with LPD had significantly longer ICU stay than OPD (MD = 0.62 days; 95% CI, 0.16 to 1.08), while the mixed remained insignificant (MD = -0.58 days; 95% CI, -1.21 to 0.05). However, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis revealed significantly shorter ICU stays with LPD (MD =−0.47 days; 95% CI,−0.81 to−0.13; p = 0.006), with the exclusion of Wang et al. 2023. This discrepancy was primarily driven by the Wang et al. trial, which included a higher proportion of malignant cases and reported different institutional ICU admission protocols with longer routine postoperative monitoring. Figure 5 B, C.

90-day mortality

No significant difference was found between groups (RR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.341 to 2.207; p = 0.764). The results were consistent across studies (I² = 0%). Supplementary Fig. 1.

Secondary outcomes

Hospital readmission

There was no statistically significant difference in readmission rates (RR = 0.96; 95% CI, 0.64 to 1.43; p = 0.84). Heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0%), along with subgroup differences (p = 0.27). Supplementary Fig. 2.

Overall complications

The overall complication rate did not differ significantly between groups (RR = 0.95; 95% CI, 0.81 to 1.12; p = 0.56). Minimal heterogeneity was observed (I² = 18%), with absent subgroup differences (p = 0.44). Supplementary Fig. 3.

Severe Complications (Clavien-Dindo > III)

No statistically significant difference was found (RR= 0.97; 95% CI, 0.67 to 1.39; p = 0.87). Heterogeneity was moderate and significant (I² = 50%). Subgroup differences were not observed (p = 0.32). This heterogeneity was addressed by removing Poves 2018 (I² = 13%). There still was no statistically significant difference (RR = 1.14; 95% CI, 0.88 to 1.47; p = 0.032). Supplementary Fig. 4.

Clinically relevant pancreatic hemorrhage

Rates of pancreatic hemorrhage were similar (RR = 0.99; 95% CI, 0.67 to 1.48; p = 0.98). Results were consistent (I² = 0%) and so were the subgroups (p = 0.98). Supplementary Fig. 5.

Clinically relevant pancreatic fistula

Incidence of clinically relevant pancreatic fistula was comparable between LPD and OPD (RR = 0.86; 95% CI, 0.59 to 1.27; p = 0.45). Neither heterogeneity (I² = 0%) nor subgroup differences (p = 0.60) were observed. Supplementary Fig. 6.

Clinically relevant delayed gastric emptying

A non-significant trend favoring LPD was noted (RR = 0.80; 95% CI, 0.63 to 1.02; p = 0.08). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%). Tests for subgroup differences were insignificant (p = 0.84). Supplementary Fig. 7.

Lymph node harvest

No significant difference was found in lymph node yield between groups (MD = 0.34; 95% CI, − 1.20 to 1.87; p = 0.67), with substantial heterogeneity observed (I² = 82%). Subgroup analysis revealed a significantly higher lymph node yield with LPD compared to OPD in patients with malignant tumors (MD = 1.84; 95% CI, 1.27 to 2.41; p < 0.00001), whereas studies including mixed tumor types showed no significant difference (p = 0.53). Excluding Yoon et al. (2024) resolved the heterogeneity without altering the direction or significance of the overall or subgroup effects. Supplementary Fig. 8.

R0 resection

R0 resection rates were similar between groups (RR = 1.05; 95% CI, 0.97 to 1.14; p = 0.24), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) and no subgroup differences (p = 0.70). Supplementary Fig. 9.

Bile leakage and gastrointestinal leak

No significant difference was noted for bile leakage (RR = 1.23; 95% CI, 0.79 to 1.91; p = 0.36), with both consistent findings (I² = 0%) and consistent subgroup differences (p = 0.82). Gastrointestinal leak events were rare and balanced between groups. Supplementary Fig. 10.

Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI)

Initially, no significant difference in CCI was found (p = 0.67; I² = 84%). However, sensitivity analysis excluding Wang et al., 2021, favored LPD (MD−3.2; 95% CI,−5.8 to−0.6; p = 0.02), suggesting study-level variability. Supplementary Fig. 11.

GRADE assessment

The certainty of evidence for key outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE approach and is summarized in Table 2. High-certainty evidence supports the benefit of laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy (LPD) in reducing the incidence of clinically relevant gastric emptying only. Moderate-certainty evidence was found for several outcomes, including intraoperative blood transfusion, 90-day mortality, overall postoperative complications, clinically relevant pancreatic hemorrhage, hospital readmission, length of hospital stay, operation time in minutes, and bile leakage. In contrast, the certainty of evidence was rated as low for intraoperative blood loss, severe complications (Clavien-Dindo grade > III), clinically-relevant pancreatic fistula, lymph node harvest, R0 resection, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI), primarily due to concerns about inconsistency and imprecision across studies. Discussion

This meta-analysis provides an updated and comprehensive evaluation of laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy (LPD) compared to open pancreatoduodenectomy (OPD), focusing on perioperative safety, technical feasibility, and short-term oncologic outcomes. By synthesizing evidence from seven randomized controlled trials conducted across different geographic and healthcare settings, this study provides a higher level of evidence than many previous analyses that relied largely on observational studies. The results demonstrate several consistent advantages of LPD, including significantly lower intraoperative blood loss, reduced transfusion requirements, and shorter hospital stays. However, although the reduction in hospital stay reached statistical significance, the absolute difference was modest and its clinical impact may be limited. While LPD was associated with a longer operative time, this did not translate into increased 90-day mortality, higher rates of severe complications, or inferior oncologic parameters such as lymph node yield or R0 resection rates. The Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) also showed no significant difference between the two approaches overall. These findings suggest that LPD may represent a safe and feasible alternative to OPD when performed in experienced centers with appropriate surgical expertise.

The results of this analysis are in line with findings from several well-designed studies in the literature. Randomized trials by Palanivelu et al., as well as Poves et al., reported reduced intraoperative blood loss and lower transfusion needs with LPD [7, 8]. Similarly, Bhingare et al. observed fewer wound complications in patients undergoing the laparoscopic approach [5]. Large multicenter studies conducted by Wang and colleagues in 2021 and 2023 further confirmed that LPD is associated with better perioperative outcomes without an increase in morbidity or mortality [10, 18]. Our findings therefore reinforce the growing body of evidence supporting the feasibility of minimally invasive pancreatoduodenectomy, particularly when implemented in specialized centers with sufficient experience.

One consistent observation across the literature, which our study also highlights, is the longer operative time associated with LPD. This likely reflects the technical complexity of the procedure, the need for precise vascular dissection, and the multiple reconstructive steps required during pancreatoduodenectomy. Importantly, the absence of worse outcomes despite this increased duration reinforces the safety and effectiveness of LPD in appropriately selected patients. By including a broader and more contemporary sample of trials, our study builds on previous work and enhances the generalizability of these findings to a wider range of clinical settings.

Several sources of heterogeneity were identified in the present analysis, particularly for outcomes such as operative time and intraoperative blood loss. This variability likely reflects differences in surgeon experience, institutional case volume, perioperative management protocols, and patient selection criteria among the included trials. Geographic differences between Asian and Western healthcare systems may also contribute to variations in surgical techniques and postoperative management strategies. Because the number of included randomized controlled trials was limited, meta-regression analysis was not feasible; therefore, potential sources of heterogeneity were explored through sensitivity analyses and qualitative interpretation. The surgical learning curve represents another important factor influencing outcomes of laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy. Previous studies suggest that surgeons may require approximately 40–60 procedures to achieve technical proficiency in LPD. Most of the randomized trials included in this meta-analysis were conducted in high-volume centers with specialized pancreatic surgery programs, where surgeons had already surpassed the initial learning phase. As a result, the favorable perioperative outcomes observed in this study may not be fully generalizable to lower-volume institutions or centers with limited experience in minimally invasive pancreatoduodenectomy. Institutional practices may also affect several perioperative outcomes observed in this analysis, including variations in transfusion thresholds, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, postoperative ICU admission policies, and hospital discharge criteria. For example, the sensitivity analysis examining ICU stay demonstrated that the trial conducted by Wang et al. had a significant influence on the pooled estimate, likely due to differences in routine ICU monitoring protocols and the inclusion of a higher proportion of malignant cases.

With respect to oncologic adequacy, the present study focused on short-term surrogate oncologic parameters, specifically lymph node yield and R0 resection rates, which are commonly used indicators of surgical quality in pancreatic cancer surgery. The pooled analysis demonstrated no significant differences between LPD and OPD for either lymph node retrieval or margin-negative resection, suggesting that the laparoscopic approach does not compromise immediate oncologic surgical quality. However, it is important to emphasize that these measures represent only surrogate indicators of oncologic success. Long-term oncologic outcomes such as overall survival, disease-free survival, and recurrence rates were not consistently reported across the included randomized trials and, therefore, could not be included in the meta-analysis. Consequently, definitive conclusions regarding long-term oncologic equivalence between LPD and OPD cannot be established based on the currently available randomized evidence.

The present study has several important strengths. It exclusively included randomized controlled trials, which minimizes selection bias and strengthens the validity of the conclusions. Additionally, the inclusion of studies conducted in diverse healthcare settings improves the generalizability of the findings across different clinical environments. Furthermore, a broad range of perioperative and oncologic outcomes was evaluated, and the certainty of evidence for each endpoint was assessed using the GRADE framework, providing a transparent evaluation of the strength of the available evidence. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity was observed for certain outcomes, particularly operative time and intraoperative blood loss. Although sensitivity analyses were conducted, the underlying sources of variability could not be fully explained, likely reflecting differences in surgeon expertise, institutional volume, and perioperative protocols. Importantly, many trials did not report the number of prior LPD cases performed by participating surgeons, precluding a direct assessment of the impact of the learning curve on outcomes. In addition, tumor stage and grade were inconsistently reported across studies, which prevented subgroup analyses according to disease severity. The inclusion of mixed tumor types—pancreatic head adenocarcinoma and other periampullary malignancies—further limited the feasibility of tumor-specific analyses and may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity. The absence of long-term oncologic outcomes, such as disease-free and overall survival, and the relatively small number of randomized trials also restricted the ability to perform more advanced analyses, including meta-regression.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered, multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized reporting of surgical experience, case volume, perioperative management pathways, and detailed tumor characteristics. Long-term follow-up assessing survival, recurrence patterns, and patient-centered outcomes—such as postoperative quality of life and functional recovery—will be essential to more definitively define the role of laparoscopic pancreatoduodenectomy in contemporary practice.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that LPD may provide certain perioperative advantages, including reduced blood loss, lower transfusion requirements, and slightly shorter hospital stays, without compromising short-term oncologic parameters such as lymph node yield or R0 resection rates. However, the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low for many clinically relevant outcomes. These findings support the cautious adoption of LPD in experienced, high-volume centers, while further high-quality trials with standardized reporting and long-term oncologic follow-up are required.
