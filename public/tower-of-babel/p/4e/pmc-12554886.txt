Preparation of colon-targeted pellets loaded with filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride and Their application in ulcerative colitis therapy
Authors: Jinglong Wang, Zhixuan Kuang, Binglian Chen, Chenxi Yan, Bowen Shi, Guofei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis (UC) is a recurrent-remitting autoimmune disease of the colon, the pathogenesis of which remains incompletely understood. Recent studies have revealed gut dysbiosis and immune imbalance mediated by the JAK-STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways are closely associated with the onset and progression of UC. In particular, the interplay between gut dysbiosis and immune imbalance has become a key factor in the rapid progression of UC, which also contributes to the suboptimal efficacy of existing therapeutic drugs. A growing number of scholars believe that simultaneously regulating gut dysbiosis and immune imbalance to synergistically combat UC may represent a critical breakthrough in UC treatment. Based on this premise, this study utilized extrusion-spheronization and fluidized-bed-coating technologies to prepare colon-targeted pellets co-loaded with berberine hydrochloride and filgotinib, and evaluated their potential for synergistic UC therapy. The results demonstrated the prepared pellets exhibited satisfactory pharmaceutical characteristics, including high sphericity, uniform particle size, and intact coating integrity. Moreover, the drug release behavior of the pellets showed high consistency in both in-vitro and in-vivo settings, displaying distinct pH-responsive release properties that meet the requirements for colon-targeted formulations. Furthermore, the pellets exhibited significant therapeutic effects in DSS-induced UC model, with beneficial changes observed in key indicators such as gut microbiota and immune. Immunohistochemistry, multiplex cytokine assays, and gut microbiota sequencing results all confirmed the synergistic effect of berberine hydrochloride and filgotinib in UC treatment. In conclusion, the findings of this study provide new insights for further research on UC and the development of synergistic UC therapies.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Colon-targeted pellets, Gut microbiota dysbiosis, Immune imbalance, Berberine hydrochloride, Filgotinib

Introduction

Ulcerative Colitis (UC) is a recurrent autoimmune disease characterized by chronic abdominal pain, diarrhea with mucus and bloody stools (Kobayashi et al., 2020; Chang and Longo, 2020). Its pathological features include persistent inflammatory infiltration and tissue damage in the colonic mucosal and submucosal layers (Chang and Longo, 2020; Eisenstein, 2018). Current UC treatments primarily rely on pharmacological interventions such as 5-aminosalicylates, sulfasalazine, systemic or topical steroids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, yet therapeutic outcomes remain suboptimal (Rubin et al., 2019). Therefore, in-depth research into UC pathogenesis and subsequent drug development may hold greater value.

Studies indicate that UC pathogenesis involves multiple factors, including gut microbiota dysbiosis (Ni et al., 2017), immune system dysregulation (Xiong et al., 2023; He et al., 2022), genetic susceptibility, and abnormal cytokine networks (Pawlak et al., 2020). Recent advances in cell signaling pathway research have uncovered key mechanisms underlying UC, with critical pathways including JAK/STAT, TGF-β/Smad, PI3K/Akt, Wnt, and NF-κB (Cho, 2025). Among these, JAK/STAT and NF-κB serve as major transcriptional regulators linking inflammation and cancer, governing numerous physiological and pathological processes and playing pivotal roles in UC development and progression (Salas et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2024). Concurrently, gut microbiota dysbiosis and immune imbalance are not only hallmarks of UC but also key drivers of its progression and recurrence, making the “gut microbiota-immune axis” a major research focus in UC pathogenesis. Thus, selectively targeting JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathways to modulate microbiota and immune dysfunction may represent a breakthrough in UC treatment.

Janus kinase (JAK), a non-receptor protein tyrosine kinase, comprises four subtypes (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2), with JAK1 being the most critical mediator of immune and inflammatory responses (Salas et al., 2020; Macaluso and Rodríguez-Lago, 2020). Representative JAK inhibitors, including tofacitinib, filgotinib, baricitinib, and ruxolitinib, are widely used in autoimmune disease treatment (Traves et al., 2021). Among them, filgotinib(FILG), a next-generation highly selective JAK1 inhibitor, has demonstrated superior clinical efficacy in moderate-to-severe active UC and Crohn's disease compared to traditional therapies (aminosalicylates, glucocorticoids), while significantly reducing adverse effects associated with non-selective JAK inhibitors like tofacitinib (Mannucci et al., 2022; Kutschera et al., 2022).

Berberine hydrochloride (BBR), a natural plant-derived alkaloid traditionally used for bacterial dysentery, has recently been shown to restore gut microbial balance by selectively enriching short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) (Liao et al., 2019; Czajkowska and Szponar, 2018)-producing bacteria and increasing Bifidobacterium populations, which enhance mucosal protection and intestinal barrier integrity (Sun et al., 2022). Additionally, BBR improves epithelial barrier function by modulating Treg/Th17 balance, inhibits lipid peroxidation, and suppresses IL-6/STAT/NF-κB signaling to alleviate UC symptoms (Zhu et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). Notably, BBR's extremely low oral bioavailability (Yang et al., 2023) (∼5 %) allows high intestinal retention, facilitating localized microbiota modulation. In line with the exploration of natural product-based strategies, several other plant-derived compounds have also been investigated in UC therapeutic formulations. For instance, α-Terpineol was reported to mitigate DSS-induced colitis in rats through anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic mechanisms (Khan et al., 2023); similarly, caffeic acid-conjugated budesonide nanomicelles (Mishra et al., 2023) and beta-sitosterol-conjugated sinapic acid nanoliposomes (Kanika et al., 2025) have been designed as biomucoadhesive or enzyme-responsive systems for targeted treatment of ulcerative colitis, highlighting a growing interest in natural compound-driven formulation approaches. However, some studies suggest that while BBR can ameliorate UC symptoms via microbiota regulation, it fails to significantly suppress disease progression, possibly due to insufficient immunomodulatory effects (Wang et al., 2024a). Therefore, combining BBR and FILG for multi-targeted UC therapy—simultaneously addressing immune suppression, microbiota regulation, and barrier repair—is mechanistically feasible and innovative.

The unique anatomical site of UC poses significant drug delivery challenges (Mohajeri et al., 2022), necessitating site-specific drug release to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. Current strategies leverage the distinct UC microenvironment—characterized by elevated pH (7.4–7.8 vs. normal 5.7–7.0) (Abbasi et al., 2019), high redox levels (Tan et al., 2022), inflammatory cytokine overexpression (Wang et al., 2024b), and positive surface charge (Sun et al., 2023)—to achieve colon-targeted and stimuli-responsive drug delivery. For instance: (Lee et al., 2019). developed pH-responsive infliximab-loaded nanoclay/Eudragit S100 composites, where Eudragit S100 (dissolving at pH >7.0) ensured colon-specific release. At colonic pH, electrostatic repulsion between positively charged infliximab (pI ∼7.6) and nanoclay triggered drug release, demonstrating rapid payload discharge at pH 5.5 in vitro (Naeem et al., 2018). designed cyclosporine A-loaded Eudragit FS30D-PLGA nanoparticles, which exhibited pH-dependent release (minimal in upper GI tract, rapid in colon) and significantly improved disease activity scores in DSS-induced colitis mice. These studies highlight the promise of pH-responsive nanocarriers for UC therapy.

In this study, we developed dual-loaded (BBR/FILG) pH-responsive colon-targeted micropellets via extrusion-spheronization and fluidized-bed coating. Key investigations included:Pharmaceutical characterization: Evaluating micropellet stability and pH-dependent drug release. Pharmacokinetics: Assessing in vivo-in vitro release correlation. Therapeutic efficacy: Measuring histopathological scores, colon length, and inflammatory markers in DSS-induced UC rats. Mechanistic insights: Analyzing cytokine profiles (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), oxidative stress (MDA, SOD), tight junction proteins, and gut microbiota shifts, with emphasis on microbiota-immune crosstalk.

Our findings provide valuable insights into UC treatment via simultaneous microbiota-immune modulation, paving the way for advanced multi-targeted UC therapies.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tofacitinib (purity>98 %) and Filgotinib (purity>98 %) were obtained from MedChemExpress (Beijing, China). Berberine (purity>99 %) was sourced from Shanghai Hushi Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, sodium dihydrogen phosphate, and disodium hydrogen phosphate were purchased from Shanghai Hushi Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, molecular weight: 36,000–50,000 Da) was purchased from MP Biomedicals (Santa Ana, CA, USA). Eudragit® L100 and S100 were purchased from Evonik (Germany). Microcrystalline cellulose was obtained from Asahi Kasei Corporation(Tokyo, Japan)HPLC-grade acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH) were acquired from Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Tetrahydropalmatine and Diazepam were purchased from the National Institute for the Control of Pharmaceutical and Biological Products (Beijing, China). Ultrapure water (UPW) was obtained using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore Corp., USA).

Preparation of drug-loaded pellets

Berberine hydrochloride (20 % w/w), filgotinib (8 % w/w), and microcrystalline cellulose (72 % w/w) were sieved through a 100-mesh screen and homogeneously blended. An appropriate amount of purified water (as a wetting agent) and HPMC solution (3 % w/w, as a binder) were added, and the mixture was processed in a high-shear granulator until adequately wet granules were formed. The wet mass was then transferred to an extruder-spheronizer, where it was extruded at 20 rpm through a 1.0 mm screen to form cylindrical extrudates. These extrudates were subsequently spheronized at 800 rpm for 20 min to obtain spherical pellets. The wet pellets were dried overnight in an oven at 45 °C, followed by sieving through 30-mesh (600 μm) and 40-mesh (425 μm) screens. Pellets with a diameter between 425 and 600 μm were collected for further analysis. The pH-responsive coating was prepared using a fluidized bed coating system (Bosch Coater, Germany). Through preliminary process optimization studies, the optimal operational parameters were determined as follows: batch loading of 50 g, spray rate of 8 rpm, inlet air temperature of 30–35 °C, and atomization pressure of 1.6 bar. For coating formulation optimization, a single-factor experimental design was employed to systematically investigate the effects of critical formulation variables on coating performance, with in vitro dissolution behavior serving as the primary evaluation criterion. The key variables examined included: (1) coating solvent type (absolute ethanol, 90 % ethanol, 80 % ethanol), (2) coating weight gain (5–25 %, w/w), (3) Eudragit® L100/S100 ratio (1:1 to 1:8), and (4) plasticizer (triethyl citrate) content (5–15 %, w/w).

Microscope and scanning electron microscope (SEM) studies

The macroscopic morphological characteristics of the pellets were observed using a BX53 optical microscope (Olympus, Japan) under 100× objective lens magnification. Samples were directly mounted on glass slides and examined under bright-field illumination. Furthermore, the surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the coated pellets were investigated using an SU8100 field-emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) operated at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV. For cross-sectional observation, selected pellets were carefully bisected with a surgical blade and sputter-coated with gold prior to SEM imaging.

Dissolution tests

The drug release testing was performed using Apparatus 1 (basket method) specified in Appendix XC of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 Edition) with a dissolution tester (Tianjin Tianda Tianfa Technology Co., Ltd., China). Experimental parameters were set as follows: basket rotation speed of 100 rpm, release medium volume of 1000 mL/vessel, and temperature controlled at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C. A pH-gradient release medium system was prepared according to pharmacopoeial specifications: simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.2 hydrochloric acid solution) for 0–2 h, simulated intestinal fluid (pH 6.8 phosphate buffer) for 2–4 h, and simulated colonic fluid (pH 7.4 phosphate buffer) for 4–8 h. Aliquots (6 mL) were withdrawn through 0.45 μm microporous membranes at predetermined intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 12 h), with immediate replenishment of equal volumes of preheated blank medium to maintain sink conditions. The filtrates were quantitatively analyzed using a Shimadzu UV-1800 ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) at characteristic wavelengths of 264 nm (Berberine hydrochloride) and 304 nm (Filgotinib). Cumulative drug release percentages were calculated using the external standard method with validated calibration curves.

For drug content determination, an accurately weighed amount of pellets was thoroughly triturated in a mortar with a small quantity of distilled water. The resulting slurry was quantitatively transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask, followed by three successive washings of the mortar with distilled water. After combining all washings, the solution was sonicated (250 W, 40 kHz) for 5 min and allowed to cool to room temperature before being diluted to volume with distilled water. The solution was mixed well and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter, with the initial filtrate discarded. The subsequent filtrate was then analyzed using the established UV spectrophotometric method to determine drug concentration and calculate pellet content.

Storage stability

The stability of pellets was systematically evaluated under various stress conditions. For high-temperature testing, samples were stored in suitable containers at 60 °C for 3 months, with drug content and dissolution profile analyzed at 0, 1, and 3-month intervals. High-humidity testing was conducted by maintaining pellets at 25 °C/90 % ± 5 % RH in sealed containers for 3 months, followed by the same analytical assessments. Photostability testing involved exposure to 4500 ± 500 Lux illumination for 3 months with identical sampling time points. Additionally, long-term stability was assessed under ambient conditions (25 °C ± 2 °C/60 % ± 10 % RH) for 6 months, with analyses performed at 0, 2, 4, and 6 months. All stability samples were evaluated for both chemical stability (drug content) and physical stability (dissolution characteristics) using validated analytical methods.

Pharmacokinetic study

Animals

The pharmacokinetic study was conducted using male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 ± 20 g) purchased from Beijing Huafukang Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Study design

The animals were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): (1) berberine hydrochloride suspension group (63 mg/kg); (2) filgotinib suspension group (21 mg/kg); and (3) self-made colon-targeted filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride pellets group (63/21 mg/kg). All suspensions were prepared in 0.5 % sodium methylcellulose solution (berberine hydrochloride: 12.6 mg/mL; filgotinib: 4.2 mg/mL). The rats were kept in a breeding chamber at 25 °C, with humidity levels of 60 ± 5 % and a 12-h alternating light-dark cycle. They were provided with standard rodent feed and tap water without restriction. Prior to the experiments, the animals underwent a one-week acclimation period under these controlled conditions. Before drug administration, the rats were fasted for 12 h but allowed unlimited access to water. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University. (Approval No. 2023PS763K; Date: April 27, 2023).

Blood sampling

Blood samples (∼0.3 mL) were obtained from the retro-orbital venous plexus at specified intervals (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h) and stored in heparinized collection tubes. Plasma was isolated via centrifugation (4000 rpm, 5 min), and a 90 μL aliquot was mixed with 20 μL of IS (50 ng/mL) and 10 μL methanol in a 5 mL centrifuge tube. Following 1 min of vortex agitation, 2 mL of dichloromethane-ether (3:2, v/v) was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 min. After centrifugation (4000 rpm, 5 min), the organic phase was transferred to a 10 mL pointed-bottom tube and dried under nitrogen at 40 °C. The residue was redissolved in 500 μL of mobile phase, centrifuged (15,000 rpm, 10 min), and 2 μL of the supernatant was analyzed by chromatography.

HPLC-ESI-MS/MS method

The HPLC system was equipped with a Phenomenex Kinetex column (3.0 mm i.d. × 50 mm length, 2.6 μm particles) and an Agilent Zorbax ODS pre-column (2.1 mm i.d. × 12.5 mm, 1.7 μm). For separation, a gradient elution program was implemented using two mobile phase components: methanol modified with 0.1 % formic acid and an aqueous solution of 2 mM ammonium acetate with 0.1 % formic acid. The method parameters included a 0.6 mL/min flow rate, column temperature of 40 °C, and 2 μL sample injections. Diazepam was used as the internal standard for FILG, while tetrahydropalmatine served as the internal standard for BBR.

Ionization was achieved through electrospray ionization (ESI) in a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The operational parameters included an ion source temperature of 400 °C, curtain gas at 20 units, and an ion spray voltage of 4500 V. Detection was conducted in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with positive ion scanning.

Statistical analysis

The pharmacokinetic analysis was conducted using DAS 3.0 software to determine key parameters: maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to Cmax (Tmax), area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), plasma clearance (CL), and elimination half-life (t1/2). Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For statistical evaluation, GraphPad Prism software was employed. Prior to analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test complemented by Q-Q plot examination, while variance homogeneity was verified with Levene's test. These diagnostic checks confirmed the appropriateness of using an unpaired t-test for group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Pharmacodynamic study

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 ± 20 g) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10 % humidity) with free access to food and water. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University (Approval No. 2023PS763K; Date: April 27, 2023) and conducted in compliance with institutional guidelines for animal welfare.

Study design

Rats were randomly divided into six groups (n = 6/group): (1) normal control, (2) DSS-induced colitis model, (3) berberine hydrochloride pellets, (4) filgotinib pellets, (5) low-dose filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride pellets, (6) medium-dose filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride pellets, and (7) high-dose filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride pellets. Except for the normal control group, all rats received 5 % dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, molecular weight: 36,000–50,000 Da; MP Biomedicals, USA) in drinking water for 7 days to establish the ulcerative colitis (UC) model.

After modeling, a 1-week pharmacodynamic study was conducted with daily drug administration (see Table 1 and Fig. 1 for dosing regimen). The DSS model group received no treatment. Body weight changes, stool consistency, and occult/bloody stools were monitored throughout the experiment. After the intervention period, all rats were euthanized for tissue collection.

To comprehensively evaluate the progression of colitis, multiple parameters were dynamically monitored in this study. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was recorded daily by assessing body weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding, with higher scores indicating more severe inflammatory damage. The scoring criteria were as follows: degree of weight loss (0: none; 1: 1–5 %; 2: 5–10 %; 3: 10–15 %; 4: >15 %), stool consistency (0: normal; 2: loose stools; 4: diarrhea), and rectal bleeding (0: none; 1: mild; 2: moderate; 4: severe). The DAI total score (range: 0–12) was calculated as the sum of these three subscores.

Body weight was measured daily, and the percentage change from baseline was calculated. Progressive weight loss served as a key indicator of colitis progression. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized, and spleens were immediately excised and weighed to determine the spleen index (spleen-to-body weight ratio), which reflects the severity of systemic inflammatory responses. The entire colon (from the cecum to the anus) was rapidly isolated, and its length was measured. Colon shortening strongly correlated with pathological changes, including inflammatory cell infiltration, tissue edema, and fibrosis.

HE staining

Colon tissues were rinsed with ice-cold saline (0.9 % NaCl) to remove impurities, then fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4, 4 °C, 24 h). After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through graded ethanol (70 %–100 %, 30 min each), cleared in xylene (2 × 20 min), and embedded in paraffin (60 °C, 2 h). Sections (4 μm) were cut, floated on a 45 °C water bath, and mounted on poly-l-lysine-coated slides. For H&E staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin (5 min), differentiated (1 % acid alcohol, 3–5 s), blued (15 min), and counterstained with eosin (3 min). After dehydration (85 %–100 % ethanol) and xylene clearing, slides were mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological analysis was performed by light microscopy using standardized scoring criteria.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Paraffin-embedded rat colon sections (4 μm) were baked overnight for adhesion. Slides were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and rinsed in PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (microwave heating). After blocking with normal goat serum, sections were incubated with primary antibody (4 °C, overnight), followed by biotinylated secondary antibody (1 h, RT) and streptavidin-peroxidase (30 min, RT). DAB substrate was used for color development (microscopically monitored), with hematoxylin counterstaining. Sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam for microscopic analysis.

ELISA assay

The supernatant of PBS-homogenized rat intestinal tissues was collected for multi-analyte ELISA detection. Following the specific protocols of different commercial kits, standards and samples were processed through loading, incubation, washing, and chromogenic steps. The optical density was measured by a microplate reader, and target molecule concentrations were calculated using standard curves, with all procedures adjusted according to the respective manufacturer's instructions.

16S rDNA determination

16S rDNA gene amplification and sequencing were conducted by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted and assessed for quality and concentration. The V4 region of 16S rDNA genes was amplified by PCR, purified using AMPure XT Beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics), quantified by Qubit (Invitrogen), and qualified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with Kapa Biosciences kits. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE250).

The raw sequences were processed using QIIME 1.80, where DADA2 was applied for denoising and generating amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The ASVs were taxonomically annotated against the GreenGene Database. Microbial diversity was analyzed through α-diversity (mothur v1.31.2), PCA (community similarity), and PCoA (β-diversity visualization). LEfSe identified differential taxa (LDA score threshold), while PICRUSt predicted functional potential using Wekemo BioinCloud and STAMP v2.1.3 for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, CA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Neurological score was evaluated using Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical analysis between the two groups were evaluated by a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test. Statistical analysis in more than two groups were evaluated by One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Significant differences were considered at * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 and *** p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Result of microscope and scanning electron microscope

The formulation of the drug-containing pellet cores consisted of filgotinib (8 g), berberine hydrochloride (20 g), microcrystalline cellulose (72 g), and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (3 g). The extrusion-spheronization process was carried out using an extrusion speed of 20 r/min, a spheronization speed of 800 r/min, and a spheronization time of 20 min, resulting in 95 g of drug-loaded pellets. For the coating process, a mixture of Eudragit® L100 and Eudragit® S100 (1:8 ratio) was used as the coating material, with triethyl citrate as the plasticizer and 90 % ethanol as the solvent at a coating concentration of 11 %. The coating parameters included a batch size of 50 g, a spray rate of 8 rpm, an inlet air temperature of 30–35 °C, and an atomization pressure of 1.6 bar.

The morphological characteristics of the filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride colon-targeted pellets were examined using optical microscopy and SEM, as shown in Fig. 2A.

(B) dissolution profiles of FILG and BBR from the pellets. Optical microscopic examination revealed the prepared filgotinib /berberine hydrochloride colon-targeted pellets as discrete, spherical particles with uniform pale-yellow coloration and smooth surface morphology under both bright-field and polarized light illumination. The pellets exhibited excellent sphericity with a narrow size distribution ranging from 0.4 to 0.6 mm in diameter, as confirmed by digital image analysis.

SEM observations revealed that the pellets exhibited a smooth and glossy surface, indicating the successful formation of a homogeneous and continuous film coating. The film layer demonstrated excellent uniformity and density, with no visible cracks, pores, or irregularities. Furthermore, cross-sectional SEM images clearly confirmed the presence of a well-defined and structurally intact coating layer, which maintained consistent thickness(∼40 μm) around the pellet core. These results suggest that the coating process effectively formed a compact and uniform film, crucial for ensuring the desired colon-targeted drug release properties.

Release of BBR and FILG from pellets coated with Eudragit L100/S100

As shown in Fig. 2B and C, the micropellets developed in this study exhibited remarkable pH-dependent release characteristics in simulated gastrointestinal environments. During the gastric phase (pH 1.2, 0–2 h) and intestinal phase (pH 6.8, 2–4 h), the cumulative drug release remained below 5 %, demonstrating excellent gastroprotective properties. This performance is attributed to the Eudragit L100/S100 coating system: under acidic conditions, the carboxyl groups of both polymers remain protonated, adopting a coiled conformation that forms a dense protective layer; while in weakly acidic environments, S100 undergoes partial deprotonation, the structural integrity of the coating is maintained by the sustained stability of L100.

When the pH reached physiological colonic conditions (7.4), the micropellets displayed rapid and complete drug release, achieving over 90 % cumulative release within 4–12 h with a characteristic sigmoidal release profile. Optical microscopy observations revealed intact and smooth coating morphology during the 0–4 h gastrointestinal simulation, with no visible pores or defects, confirming the coating's superior erosion resistance at low pH. Following transition to colonic conditions (post 4 h), significant morphological changes occurred: the coating structure became progressively porous with partial disintegration, accompanied by darkening of the pellets and formation of distinct drug release channels.

These morphological transformations correlated precisely with the release kinetics, demonstrating the pH-selective erosion mechanism of the Eudragit L100/S100 blend. At the molecular level, this pH-responsive behavior stems from polymer conformational changes: complete deprotonation of carboxyl groups at pH 7.4 induces chain extension through electrostatic repulsion and enhanced hydrophilicity. Crucially, the differential dissolution thresholds of the polymers (L100 ≈ 6.0, S100 ≈ 7.0) create a “relay-style” dissolution pattern that ensures coordinated erosion in the colon. Furthermore, the optimized L100/S100 ratio forms an interpenetrating network at the molecular level (Akhgari et al., 2005), which not only prevents dose dumping associated with single polymers but also guarantees complete release at the target site.

These findings conclusively demonstrate that the Eudragit L100/S100 coating system enables precise colon-targeted delivery through a single pH-triggered mechanism, effectively protecting the drug payload during upper gastrointestinal transit while ensuring rapid release in the colonic environment.

Stability studies

The stability study of filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride colon-targeted pellets demonstrated excellent stability under various storage conditions. After 3 months of storage under high temperature (60 °C ± 2 °C), high humidity (75 %RH ± 5 %RH), and strong light exposure (4500Lux ± 500Lux), as well as 6 months at room temperature (25 °C ± 2 °C), all critical quality attributes remained stable (Fig. 2D, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5). The drug content consistently maintained 98.0 %–102.0 % of initial values across all test groups, with no significant differences in dissolution profiles compared to baseline data. The pellets retained their characteristic pH-dependent release behavior, showing less than 10 % cumulative release in pH 1.2 and 6.8 media within 2 h, while achieving over 80 % release in pH 7.4 medium at the same time point. These results confirm the formulation's excellent physical and chemical stability, with the protective coating effectively maintaining its integrity and functionality under diverse environmental conditions. The data collectively demonstrate that the pellets meet stringent stability requirements for pharmaceutical products, ensuring reliable performance throughout their intended shelf life.

HPLC-MS/MS method validation of BBR and FIG in rat blood

The plasma concentrations of BBR and FIG were determined using an established HPLC-MS/MS method developed by our research group. For specific chromatographic/mass spectrometric conditions, methodological validation data (including selectivity, linearity, precision, accuracy, matrix effects, stability, and other relevant parameters), as well as detailed operational procedures, please refer to our previously published research article (Wang et al., 2025). This fully validated method has been successfully applied in multiple in vivo pharmacokinetic studies and meets all requirements for quantitative analysis of both compounds in the current investigation.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study in SD rats

As illustrated in Fig. 3 and Table 6, the pharmacokinetic study in rats demonstrated that the berberine hydrochloride/filgotinib colon-targeted pellets significantly altered the in vivo behaviors of both drugs. Compared to the suspension formulation, the pellets maintained lower drug concentrations within the first 4 h post-administration, followed by a rapid increase in plasma concentration thereafter, showing distinct time-dependent pharmacokinetic characteristics. The marked difference in time-to-peak concentration (Tmax) between the suspension and pellet formulations further confirmed this unique release pattern. This phenomenon can be attributed to the protective coating that effectively prevented drug release before reaching the colon, while enabling rapid drug release in the colonic environment-consistent with the in vitro dissolution results and demonstrating clear colon-specific release properties.

Although drug release primarily occurred in the colon, the multi-particulate nature of the pellets prevented localized high drug concentrations that could cause adverse effects. On the contrary, this multi-unit delivery system actually enhanced drug absorption and bioavailability. Specifically, the pellets improved the bioavailability of berberine hydrochloride and filgotinib by 1.4-fold and 1.9-fold respectively compared to the suspension. This enhancement is particularly noteworthy for berberine hydrochloride, which typically exhibits poor oral bioavailability (<5 %) that remains largely unaffected by conventional absorption-enhancing approaches. The achieved high local colonic concentration of berberine hydrochloride may provide therapeutic advantages for UC treatment, as it can help restore gut microbiota balance and repair damaged intestinal barriers-both critical for managing UC where severe gut dysbiosis is a hallmark feature.

Pharmacodynamic study

Pharmacodynamic results

UC, as a common intestinal disorder, is characterized by clinical symptoms including recurrent diarrhea and weight loss. Fig. 4 presents the changes in body weight, colon length, spleen index, and disease activity index (DAI) scores across experimental groups. Data in Fig. 4A demonstrate that all rats receiving 5 % DSS solution showed significantly retarded weight gain compared to the blank control group, with some animals exhibiting weight loss. During the treatment phase, different regimens variably influenced weight recovery. The filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride medium-dose pellet group showed the most pronounced effect on weight restoration, followed by the low- and high-dose combinations. While filgotinib or berberine hydrochloride monotherapies showed relatively weaker effects, they still significantly improved weight recovery compared to the untreated model group, confirming their therapeutic potential against UC.

The DAI score, which comprehensively evaluates weight loss, stool consistency, and hematochezia, objectively reflects disease severity (Kawakatsu et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 4B, all DSS-treated rats exhibited markedly elevated DAI scores versus controls. Therapeutic interventions differentially modulated DAI scores, with the medium-dose combination pellets achieving superior reduction compared to low/high-dose groups. This optimal effect likely stems from the medium-dose design based on clinical dosing references, balancing efficacy and safety. Although monotherapies showed limited DAI reduction, they still significantly ameliorated disease progression relative to the DSS model.

Chronic inflammation and ulceration of the colonic mucosa represent hallmark pathological features of UC. Persistent inflammation triggers mucosal damage, atrophy, and fibrosis, ultimately causing colon shortening-a key indicator of disease severity (Deepak and Bruining, 2014). Fig. 4C reveals significant colon shortening in all DSS-exposed rats versus controls. The medium-dose combination pellet group exhibited the greatest colon length preservation, demonstrating effective inhibition of mucosal damage and promotion of tissue repair. Other treatments showed modest benefits but were outperformed by the medium-dose combination.

The spleen index reflects immune system activation. During disease progression, immune hyperactivation often increases spleen weight (Mebius and Kraal, 2005). As illustrated in Fig. 4D, the medium-dose combination pellets most effectively normalized spleen indices, likely through precise immunomodulation that attenuated excessive immune responses without compromising protective immunity. This targeted immunoregulation contrasts with the less pronounced effects of monotherapies or non-optimal combination doses.

Intestinal barrier function

The combined results from HE staining (Fig. 5A and B) and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 5C and D) systematically demonstrated the multi-level impacts of UC on intestinal barrier integrity. HE staining revealed intact mucosal architecture with tightly arranged epithelial cells and well-structured crypts in controls, whereas the DSS model group showed characteristic barrier destruction including epithelial disruption, crypt loss, and significant inflammatory infiltration. At the molecular level, immunohistochemistry confirmed markedly reduced and disorganized Occludin expression in model animals, with these tight junction abnormalities directly correlating with the structural damage observed histologically, together explaining the UC-associated barrier dysfunction (Kinchen et al., 2018). Notably, treatment with the filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride combination demonstrated near-complete restoration of mucosal structure in HE staining alongside normalized Occludin expression levels, indicating that the combined therapy effectively repaired tight junctions and restored barrier function through coordinated structural and molecular improvements.

Oxidative stress evaluation in UC model

Assessment of oxidative stress markers revealed that DSS-induced model rats exhibited characteristic oxidative stress imbalance in colonic tissues, manifested by significantly elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels alongside markedly reduced superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and glutathione (GSH) content. As shown in Fig. 6E to G, all treatment groups effectively reversed this oxidative stress status, with the combination therapy group demonstrating optimal regulatory effects-not only significantly reducing MDA accumulation but also more efficiently restoring SOD activity and GSH levels to near-normal ranges. These findings confirm the synergistic effect of the combined regimen in rectifying oxidative stress imbalance.

This disturbance in the oxidative-antioxidant system may exacerbate ulcerative colitis progression through multiple mechanisms: Firstly, excessive reactive oxygen species directly damage intestinal epithelial tight junction proteins (Lin et al., 2025) (e.g., Occludin), compromising gut barrier integrity-consistent with our previous immunohistochemistry and HE staining observations. Secondly, oxidative stress activates inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB), creating a vicious cycle of “oxidative stress-inflammation”, while GSH depletion further aggravates mitochondrial dysfunction by diminishing cellular antioxidant capacity (Makhezer et al., 2019).

Notably, all treatment groups showed significant improvement in these pathological alterations, with the berberine hydrochloride/filgotinib combination exhibiting particularly remarkable synergistic antioxidant effects. This synergy likely stems from: (1) berberine hydrochloride's activation of the Nrf2/ARE pathway to enhance antioxidant enzyme expression (Ashrafizadeh et al., 2025), and (2) filgotinib's selective inhibition of JAK1 signaling to reduce inflammation-mediated ROS production (Van Rompaey et al., 2013). Additionally, the combination may regulate gut microbiota metabolism to decrease exogenous oxidative stressor generation (a mechanism requiring further investigation).

Of particular interest, the medium-dose combination group showed optimal antioxidant efficacy, potentially attributable to ideal drug accumulation kinetics and concentration ratios in colonic tissues.

Pellets ameliorate UC inflammatory status via modulation of IL-6/IL-1β and TNF-α

As shown in Fig. 6A to F, The DSS model group exhibited a characteristic pro-inflammatory cytokine storm, with significantly elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. This phenomenon likely stems from four pathological mechanisms: (1) direct regulation through the JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Xu et al., 2020), (2) modulation via the NF-κB pathway (Hong et al., 2012), (3) influence on macrophage polarization (M1/M2 phenotype switching) (Liu et al., 2025), and (4) regulation of Th1 and Th2 cell (Zhu et al., 2004) balance through controlling both the quantity and functionality of regulatory T cells (Zeng and Chi, 2015) (Tregs).

Pharmacological intervention with the filgotinib/berberine hydrochloride combination demonstrated synergistic anti-inflammatory effects: Filgotinib selectively inhibited the JAK1-STAT3 pathway, significantly reducing TNF-α and IL-6 production, while berberine hydrochloride suppressed NF-κB nuclear translocation by blocking IκBα degradation, thereby decreasing IL-1β levels. The combination therapy not only targeted distinct inflammatory signaling nodes but also exerted synergistic immunomodulatory effects through Treg/Th17 balance regulation.

Of particular note were the dynamic changes in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Its paradoxical elevation may reflect compensatory mechanisms, including: (1) p38MAPK pathway-stimulated IL-10 secretion by damaged epithelial cells (Liu et al., 2017); (2) CD103+ dendritic cell-derived IL-10 induction due to microbial dysbiosis (Ansaldo et al., 2025); and (3) direct drug activation of the AMPK-SIRT1 regulatory axis (Park et al., 2012). In the combination group, IL-10 levels decreased relative to the model group, suggesting that the treatment restored immune homeostasis, gradually normalizing compensatory IL-10 hypersecretion.

16S rDNA determination results

Gut microbiota dysbiosis has been established as a critical pathological characteristic in the development of ulcerative colitis. To investigate the regulatory effects of therapeutic interventions on intestinal microbiota, we conducted comprehensive analysis of microbial composition across experimental groups using 16S rDNA sequencing technology. As shown in Fig. 7A, our alpha diversity analysis demonstrated significant improvements in microbial diversity, as measured by the Shannon index, and species richness, assessed through Chao1 index and observed species numbers, in all treatment groups compared to the model group. As shown in Fig. 7B and C, principal coordinate analysis of beta diversity revealed distinct clustering patterns among control, model, and treatment groups, indicating substantial differences in gut microbial community structure. While principal component analysis did not exhibit clear group separation, the combined results suggested potential drug-induced modifications to the gut microbiota architecture.

At the taxonomic level(Fig. 7D), family-based analysis showed marked alterations in DSS-induced model rats, including increased abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae and Enterobacteriaceae alongside decreased Ruminococcaceae levels compared to controls-dysbiotic patterns that were effectively normalized following drug treatment. More refined genus-level examination through LEfSe analysis(Fig. 7E) identified several differentially represented taxa, with the DSS group showing enrichment of potentially pathogenic Escherichia-Shigella and barrier-disrupting Desulfovibrio and Fusicatenibacter, while therapeutic intervention promoted the growth of beneficial microbes including Akkermansia, Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014, and Paramuribaculum, known for their roles in short-chain fatty acid production, immune modulation, and mucosal protection.

Functional prediction analysis via PICRUSt2 uncovered 30 significantly altered metabolic pathways between the DSS-induced model group and BFM (berberine hydrochloride/filgotinib medium-dose combination) group, with several demonstrating particular relevance to colitis pathogenesis. As shown in Fig. 7F, the carbohydrate metabolism pathway emerged as a key regulator of mucin glycosylation and mucus layer integrity, while lysine degradation, renin-angiotensin system, and insulin signaling pathways collectively influence epithelial barrier function through modulation of intercellular signaling, vascular permeability, inflammatory responses, and cellular energy metabolism. Additional pathways including bacterial chemotaxis and motility proteins contribute to spatial organization of microbial communities, and selenocompound metabolism enhances epithelial antioxidant defenses.

Our integrated analysis reveals three fundamental mechanisms of microbiota-mediated UC regulation: First, the immune modulation axis involves probiotic-mediated suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines coupled with enhanced anti-inflammatory responses, operating in opposition to pathogen-induced inflammatory signaling activation (Yang et al., 2020). Second, the barrier repair axis demonstrates a dynamic equilibrium between pathogenic impairment of tight junction integrity and therapeutic restoration of mucosal protection through enhanced mucin production and epithelial renewal (Li et al., 2022). Finally, the redox homeostasis axis illustrates the contrasting roles of harmful microbes in promoting oxidative damage versus beneficial microbiota in activating endogenous antioxidant defenses (Singh et al., 2019). These coordinated multi-system interactions establish microbiota modulation as a comprehensive therapeutic strategy that simultaneously addresses immune dysfunction, barrier disruption, and oxidative stress in UC.

Conclusion

We successfully prepared colon-targeted pellets co-loaded with berberine hydrochloride and filgotinib, which exhibited satisfactory pharmaceutical properties. Pharmacokinetic results confirmed that the prepared pellets could intelligently release the drugs in response to the specific pH of the colon, significantly increasing drug concentration at the colonic site and improving the bioavailability of filgotinib.

Pharmacodynamic studies demonstrated that the pellets exhibited favorable effects in improving body weight, disease activity index (DAI) scores, and colon length in UC model rats. Additionally, the prepared pellets showed significant synergistic effects in modulating inflammatory cytokine expression, optimizing intestinal barrier function, regulating oxidative stress status, and improving the distribution of gut immune-microbiota.

In conclusion, the combined use of berberine hydrochloride and filgotinib as a synergistic therapy for UC is feasible and holds promising potential for clinical application.
