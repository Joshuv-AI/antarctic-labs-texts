Irreversible Brainstem Atrophy After Fibrin-Sealant Hypersensitivity: A Neurorehabilitation Perspective: A Case Report
Authors: Yeonsu Oh, Jong Mi Park, Yong Wook Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypersensitivity reactions to fibrin sealants used in microvascular decompression (MVD) are rare, and delayed eosinophilic meningoencephalitis with progressive brainstem injury has been scarcely reported. We present a case of severe delayed immune-mediated brainstem injury following fibrin-sealant application during MVD for trigeminal neuralgia, with an emphasis on its neurorehabilitation implications. A male patient with long-standing trigeminal neuralgia underwent MVD using a Teflon pledget secured with Beriplast® fibrin sealant. Immediately after surgery, he developed profound quadriplegia (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade 1 in all extremities). Magnetic resonance imaging on postoperative day (POD) 0 revealed ventral medullary and pontine T2/FLAIR hyperintensity without diffusion restriction, consistent with acute vasogenic edema. Despite complete surgical material removal on POD 1, deficits persisted. By POD 8, new diffusion-restricted lesions appeared, indicating secondary cytotoxic injury. Delayed eosinophilia peaked at 21.0% on POD 62, accompanied by a diffuse rash. Comprehensive workup excluded vascular, infectious, autoimmune, and drug-related causes. On transfer to our rehabilitation center (POD 251), motor strength was MRC 3+ in upper and 2– in lower extremities; the patient remained wheelchair-dependent with severe functional limitation and required assistance for all activities of daily living. Markedly prolonged somatosensory evoked potential and motor evoked potential latencies confirmed irreversible corticospinal and sensory pathway disruption, suggesting a potential ceiling for neuroplasticity-driven recovery in this case. Despite one month of structured inpatient neurorehabilitation, no meaningful functional gain was achieved. This case underscores the importance of early immune-mediated injury recognition and highlights the critical role of neurophysiological assessment in prognostication and rehabilitation goal-setting following catastrophic brainstem injury.

INTRODUCTION

Microvascular decompression (MVD) is a widely accepted treatment for trigeminal neuralgia and frequently involves the placement of Teflon pledgets and fibrin-based hemostatic sealants [1]. While generally safe, fibrin sealants may provoke hypersensitivity reactions, particularly in preparations containing bovine-derived components or aprotinin [23]. Reported complications have mostly involved aseptic meningitis or acute allergic responses [45], and delayed eosinophilia with progressive central nervous system injury is exceedingly uncommon. Neuroinflammation following exposure to intraoperative foreign biomaterials, especially processes culminating in fixed structural injury of the brainstem, remains poorly characterised. We describe a rare case of immediate postoperative quadriplegia, delayed eosinophilic meningoencephalitis, and eventual medullary atrophy after exposure to fibrin sealant during MVD. This case provides important insights into the chronology, radiologic evolution, and mechanistic underpinnings of persistent fibrin-sealant–associated neuroinflammation.

CASE DESCRIPTION

Immediate postoperative course

A 52-year-old male patient with a 10-year history of left trigeminal neuralgia underwent MVD on February 4, 2025 (postoperative day [POD] 0). Prior to surgery, he had been receiving gabapentin (300 mg twice daily), alprazolam (0.125 mg twice daily), and ginkgo biloba extract (80 mg twice daily). The chronological sequence of key postoperative events is summarized in Fig. 1. Intraoperatively, a Teflon pledget reinforced with Beriplast® fibrin sealant was inserted between the superior cerebellar artery and the trigeminal nerve root entry zone. Immediately after emergence from anesthesia, he developed profound quadriplegia (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade 1 in all extremities). Urgent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed ventral medullary and pontine T2/FLAIR hyperintensity without diffusion restriction, indicating acute vasogenic edema rather than infarction (Fig. 2A). The surgical team performed re-exploration on POD 1 with complete removal of all Teflon and fibrin-sealant materials; however, his neurological deficits persisted without improvement.

Early evolution of brainstem lesions

MRI on POD 8 demonstrated new diffusion-restricted lesions involving the anterior medulla and left lower pons (Fig. 2B). These findings suggested secondary cytotoxic edema and ischemic injury, absent on the immediate postoperative scan. Between POD 1 and 24, eosinophil counts remained low (0.0%–1.9%). By POD 36, eosinophils began rising steadily, ultimately peaking at 21.0% (2.6 × 103/µL) on POD 62, coinciding with the appearance of a diffuse erythematous rash. MRI performed on POD 65 revealed new bilateral anterior medullary T2 hyperintensity, consistent with a subacute inflammatory process superimposed on earlier injury (Fig. 2C).

Transfer and comprehensive reassessment at tertiary rehabilitation center (POD 251)

The patient was transferred to our department on POD 251 for comprehensive neurorehabilitation assessment. Neurological examination revealed MRC grade 3+ strength in the upper limbs and 2– in the lower limbs. He was fully wheelchair-dependent, unable to stand independently, and required maximal assistance for all basic activities of daily living (ADL) including feeding, grooming, and transfers. On admission, the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) was 16/100, reflecting profound functional dependence. The Functional Independence Measure (FIM) score was 54/126, with full marks on the cognitive subscale but markedly reduced motor subscale scores, indicating that functional limitations were entirely attributable to physical impairment rather than cognitive deficit. The Berg Balance Scale (BBS) score was 4/56, with the patient able to maintain only unsupported sitting for approximately 2 minutes, consistent with severely impaired postural control and high fall risk. Bladder management was conducted via cystostomy, with bladder stretching exercises performed twice daily through scheduled clamping trials to facilitate bladder capacity restoration. Magnetic resonance venography and whole-spine MRI demonstrated no venous thrombosis, demyelinating lesions, or spinal cord pathology. Electrodiagnostic studies showed chronic L5–S1 radiculopathy but, critically, markedly prolonged somatosensory evoked potential (SEP) and motor evoked potential (MEP) latencies, indicating severely impaired conduction through brainstem sensorimotor pathways. An extensive allergy-focused and infectious workup was performed; MAST IgE testing, parasitic serology, and autoimmune panels were unremarkable. No perioperative medication demonstrated a temporal association with eosinophilia, and pregabalin discontinuation produced no hematologic improvement, supporting a non-drug-induced mechanism.

Late-stage neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation

MRI on POD 268 revealed residual leptomeningeal FLAIR hyperintensity surrounding cranial nerves V and VII/VIII and along the brainstem surface. Parenchymal abnormalities in the pons, medulla, and cerebellum had largely regressed compared with POD 0 (Fig. 2D); however, comparison with preoperative imaging showed medullary thinning and widening of the pre-medullary CSF space (Fig. 3). Mild loculated CSF collections were present in the cerebellopontine-angle cistern without enhancement. Lumbar puncture on POD 274 demonstrated normal opening pressure and bland CSF (5 white blood cell/µL), with no infectious, demyelinating, or autoimmune etiology identified. In consultation with neurology, the clinical and radiologic findings were deemed consistent with a chronic fixed deficit without evidence of ongoing active inflammation. Corticosteroids or intravenous immunoglobulin were not initiated, as the clinical and radiological picture was consistent with a chronic fixed deficit without active inflammation, making immunomodulatory treatment unlikely to yield benefit.

Inpatient rehabilitation course and functional outcome

The patient underwent a structured one-month inpatient neurorehabilitation program comprising five days per week of multidisciplinary therapy. Physical therapy was provided five times weekly (30 minutes per session), focusing on sitting balance training, table-holding sit-to-stand practice, and standing tolerance using a prone stander. Lokomat robot-assisted gait training was provided five times weekly (30 minutes per session) to facilitate lower-limb motor relearning through repetitive, task-specific locomotor patterning. Functional electrical stimulation (FES) was applied five times weekly (30 minutes per session) to the quadriceps, gluteus medius, and tibialis anterior bilaterally, to augment voluntary activation and counteract disuse atrophy. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the lesional-side motor cortex was administered five times weekly (30 minutes per session) at 20 Hz, 90% resting motor threshold, with 1,500 pulses per session (100 pulses per train, 15 trains, 55-second inter-train interval) [6]. Electromyography (EMG) biofeedback training for the tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, and vastus medialis muscles was provided twice weekly (30 minutes per session) to enhance motor unit recruitment and provide real-time neuromuscular feedback. Occupational therapy was provided five times weekly (30 minutes per session) and included bilateral hand activity training and ADL retraining encompassing personal hygiene, dressing, and eating tasks.

Despite this intensive multimodal program, no clinically meaningful functional improvement was achieved. FIM at discharge was 60/126, representing a gain of 6 points on the motor subscale from admission, while the MBI remained unchanged at 16/100 and the BBS score was 4/56, identical to admission values. Motor strength showed no meaningful change, with MRC grade 3+ in the upper limbs and 2− to 2 in the lower limbs at discharge, consistent with a plateau in neurological recovery. The patient remained wheelchair-dependent and required assistance for most ADL. Repeat neurophysiological testing was not performed during the admission. The patient was discharged with a long-term wheelchair mobility plan, caregiver education, and home modification recommendations.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates a rare, multi-phasic neuroinflammatory response likely triggered by exposure to fibrin sealant. The patient’s immediate postoperative deterioration and early MRI findings—T2/FLAIR hyperintensity without diffusion restriction—strongly suggest innate immune–mediated vasogenic edema. Fibrinogen, thrombin, and aprotinin can activate complement, macrophages, and neutrophils, promoting endothelial permeability and rapid tissue swelling [7]. Such reactions have been described in fibrin-sealant–induced aseptic meningitis [48] and perioperative allergic responses [25].

The development of diffusion-restricted lesions by POD 8 indicates progression to cytotoxic edema and secondary ischemic injury, likely due to local microcirculatory compromise from inflammation-induced swelling rather than primary vascular occlusion [9]. This pattern is consistent with reported foreign-body hypersensitivity reactions to surgical biomaterials.

The subsequent emergence of marked delayed eosinophilia, peaking at 21%, along with rash, is characteristic of a Type IVb delayed hypersensitivity reaction. This systemic response to a foreign substance is further suggested by reported cases of generalized dermatitis and eosinophilia following metal hypersensitivity to implanted medical devices [1011]. Eosinophilic infiltration has been well documented in association with exposure to bovine-derived surgical products, including fibrin sealants that contain aprotinin [23]. Although Teflon itself can induce delayed aseptic meningitis [12] or granuloma (Tefloma) formation [13]. the unique clinical trajectory observed in this case was more consistent with a fibrin-sealant–related mechanism [14]. Although lymphocyte stimulation testing and ELISA-based antigen identification were unavailable, the temporal association, imaging evolution, and exclusion of alternative etiologies further support this interpretation. Late imaging findings of medullary atrophy, together with persistent conduction delays on SEP/MEP, were consistent with irreversible structural damage to the brainstem long tracts, providing a plausible explanation for the limited neurological recovery observed in this case.

Early identification of postoperative inflammatory changes and careful selection of hemostatic agents—particularly avoidance of bovine-derived or aprotinin-containing fibrin sealants—is essential to prevent devastating neurological outcomes. Notably, human-derived sealants that do not contain aprotinin can be safely used for intraoperative hemostasis with a lower risk of allergic reactions [3], and the use of Autologous Fibrin Glue may be an effective and complication-free alternative in MVD [15].

From a neurorehabilitation perspective, this case highlights that early immune-mediated injury involving compact brainstem pathways may establish a significant barrier to functional recovery, as observed in this case, regardless of rehabilitation intensity. The corticospinal and medial lemniscus tracts traverse the anterior medulla in a highly compact arrangement; even focal injury may produce disproportionately severe and permanent deficits. In our patient, markedly prolonged MEP latencies and severely attenuated SEP responses confirmed structural pathway disruption, providing an objective neurophysiological substrate for rehabilitation non-responsiveness. Preserved MEP responses—even with prolonged latencies—are associated with partial motor recovery potential through neuroplasticity-based interventions. Conversely, severely attenuated MEPs reflect corticospinal disconnection that limits motor learning capacity regardless of the rehabilitation strategy. In this case, MBI remaining at 16/100, BBS at 4/56, and motor strength plateauing at MRC 3+ in the upper and 2−–2 in the lower limbs at discharge confirmed a structural basis for non-recovery, shifting the rehabilitation paradigm from recovery-oriented to compensation-focused and caregiver-centered care.

Despite a comprehensive multimodal program incorporating Lokomat robot-assisted gait training, FES, rTMS and EMG biofeedback, the total FIM gain over 30 days was only 6 points (54 to 60), well below the minimally clinically important difference for neurological rehabilitation. Early neurophysiological assessment during the acute phase could have enabled timely adjustment of rehabilitation goals and facilitated earlier psychosocial support and discharge planning.

Structured rehabilitation was delayed until POD 251, well beyond the window of peak neuroplasticity. Earlier rehabilitation medicine liaison—even for passive range-of-motion, positioning, and bladder care—could have mitigated secondary complications such as contracture and deconditioning. Future cases of suspected fibrin-sealant–related neuroinflammation should involve early rehabilitation medicine consultation during the acute management phase.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates a rare but catastrophic fibrin-sealant–associated neuroinflammatory process leading to delayed eosinophilic meningoencephalitis and irreversible brainstem atrophy following MVD. Beyond the surgical implications, this case carries important lessons for neurorehabilitation practice: early identification of immune-mediated brainstem injury is critical not only to prevent structural damage, but also to enable timely neurophysiological prognostication, realistic rehabilitation goal-setting, and appropriate psychosocial counselling for patients and families facing permanent functional disability. Neurophysiological biomarkers—specifically MEP and SEP assessment—may serve as valuable prognostic tools in rehabilitation planning following severe brainstem injury. Strict avoidance of bovine-derived or aprotinin-containing hemostatic agents in MVD remains essential to prevent this devastating and irreversible outcome.
