Synergistic remediation: Integrated adsorption and degradation techniques for multiphase environmental decontamination
Authors: Shama Hegde, Sriraksha Rao, Sowmya R Holla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review critically examines integrated adsorption–degradation strategies for the remediation of air, water, and soil contaminants, with emphasis on recent advances reported since 2020. Rather than treating adsorption and degradation as standalone processes, the review highlights how adsorption functions as an effective pretreatment step that enhances degradation efficiency by increasing the pollutant concentration at reactive interfaces, improving mass transfer, and stabilizing reactive species. Comparative analyses across environmental media have shown that most studies target aqueous systems, while integrated approaches for air and soil remain underexplored. Representative systems—including TiO2-based photocatalysts, MOF-supported Fenton-like catalysts, and biochar-assisted electrochemical electrodes—demonstrate improved removal efficiency, reaction kinetics, and material reusability compared with single-process treatments. Key mechanisms underlying adsorption–degradation synergy are discussed, along with material design strategies that enable multifunctionality. This review identifies critical knowledge gaps in multiphase systems, real-field applications, and medium-specific optimization and outlines future research directions toward scalable, energy-efficient, and environmentally sustainable remediation technologies aligned with global sustainability goals. This review aligns with SDGs 6, 11, 12, 13, and 15 to promote clean water, sustainable urban environments, responsible production, climate action, and land restoration.Keywords: adsorption, photocatalysis, biodegradation, chemical, electrochemical

1. Introduction

Environmental pollution issues have become more significant in both rural and urban areas as a result of the acceleration of global urbanization. 1 Numerous industries, specifically the textile, pharmaceutical, agricultural, printing, cosmetics and food processing industries, generate wastewater containing pollutants such as dyes, heavy metals, endocrine disruptors (EDCs), and pesticides. 2 Typically, these contaminants can be found in water, soil and air. Owing to their convenient accessibility, waterways such as rivers, lakes and oceans are the most commonly used locations for the disposal of recyclables. 3 Owing to their poisoning, mutagenicity and carcinogenicity, petroleum hydrocarbons in soil pose a major risk of pollution. 4 The primary sources of air pollution are industrial outputs, construction activities, vehicle emissions and the burning of coal. 5 Because of this, human health and aquatic ecosystems are becoming increasingly at risk, which is a serious concern. Environmental pollution remains difficult to manage because of the varied nature of contaminants. Collectively, these factors indicate that environmental pollution has become a critical global challenge, simultaneously impacting air, water and soil ecosystems.6,7 Traditional methods such as filtration or adsorption frequently transfer contaminants rather than eliminate them, which can result in subsequent contamination and prolonged toxicity. This highlights the necessity of treatment techniques that eliminate contaminants and completely transform them into safer forms.8,9 Adsorption is notable because of its simplicity, mild operating conditions and high efficiency. 10 Since traditional single-step adsorption is inadequate for handling complex pollutant mixtures, this demonstrates a clear technological gap and emphasizes the necessity of integrated and multifunctional remediation techniques. 11 Thus, to increase the effectiveness of eliminating organic pollutants, there is increasing interest in combining adsorption with other treatment techniques.12,13 The literature analyzed in this review was selected from major databases (ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Web of Science) focusing on peer-reviewed research published between 2020 and the present. However, the statistical data used to generate publication trend figures were obtained from keyword-based searches performed in the ScienceDirect database. These values reflect global research intensity and publication volume in the field.

Despite the effective study of adsorption and degradation techniques, most research examines them separately and focuses on a single environmental medium. The significant advantage of using adsorption as a preconcentration step to enhance photocatalytic biodegradation, chemical degradation and electrochemical breakdown is underexplored. Moreover, comprehensive studies comparing this combined approach across water, air and soil are limited. In this study (Figure 1), a range of methods, including adsorption, photocatalysis, biodegradation, chemical degradation and electrochemical degradation, were used to address the issues of air, water, and soil contamination14–17 Integrated adsorption and degradation refer to a synergistic approach in which adsorption captures the pollutant onto active surfaces, enhancing photocatalytic, biodegradation, chemical and electrochemical degradation. Adsorption is not treated as a separate terminal process but as a complementary step that improves overall pollutant breakdown efficiency. Adsorption is primarily a physical phase-transfer process that moves pollutants from one medium to another without achieving complete mineralization. In contrast, degradation processes such as photocatalysis, biodegradation, and chemical and electrochemical degradation aim to chemically transform toxic compounds into safer forms such as CO2 and H2O. Adsorption and photocatalytic methods are gaining traction as economical and eco-friendly solutions for environmental cleanup because they generate fewer byproducts and offer better separation processes. 18 In water, it targets heavy metals such as Pb2+, Mn2+, and fluoride; dyes such as methylene blue, methyl orange, Rhodamine B, and Congo Red; and antibiotics such as norfloxacin, tetracycline, and ciprofloxacin.19–23 It eliminates NOx, ammonia, PM and VOCs from air 24. It reduces petroleum hydrocarbons, agrochemicals and antibiotics in soil. g-C3N4-, BiVO4-, LDH- and TiO2-based composites are important photocatalysts. Collectively, these innovative materials represent the forefront of emerging remediation technologies.24–26

Additionally, biological degradation is favored for its minimal upfront investment. This approach is instrumental in detoxifying pollutants, rendering it a highly effective choice for waste management. 27 Pharmaceuticals, dyes and petroleum derivatives have been shown to be highly degraded in aquatic systems by microbial consortia and enzymatic treatments, often coupled with adsorption. 28 Persistent pollutants in the soil, such as pesticides, antibiotics and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), are broken down by native and introduced species. 29 Although less studied, air biodegradation has demonstrated potential through the use of microbially assisted particulate capture and biodegradable filters. 30 Pseudomonas, Cupriavidus, Acinetobacter and Klebsiella are some of the commonly used microbial species for both soil and water degradation.31–34

The following pollutants are targeted for chemical degradation: petroleum hydrocarbons, dyes, antibiotics, and heavy metals such as Pb(II) and Ni(II) in water; phenanthrene, anthracene, and petroleum contaminants in soil35–38; and NOx, hydrocarbons, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) such as benzene and toluene in the air. MOFs such as MIL-88A(Fe) and CeO2 nanoparticles for hydrocarbons and Pd-doped TiO2 nanotubes are important chemical agents that are utilized, indicating their important role in pollutant breakdown across environments.37,38 Adsorption‒electrochemical degradation technology has been widely used for the treatment of emerging pollutants because of its high removal efficiency, low energy consumption, and lack of secondary pollution. 39 These methods use electro-Fenton reactions and radical generation to target pollutants such as NOx, VOCs, heavy metals, dyes, antibiotics, and hydrocarbons.40–43

However, integrated adsorption and degradation technologies capable of addressing multiphase pollution remain underexplored. Most existing studies focus on either a single environmental medium or only one degradation pathway, with limited research combining photocatalysis, biological, chemical, and electrochemical methods along with adsorption. To fill this gap, the objective of this review is to systematically evaluate adsorption-assisted degradation strategies across water, soil and air, highlighting their synergistic mechanisms, comparative performance and existing limitations. This review differs from the literature by providing a unique multiphase comparison across air, water and soil systems simultaneously. The specific aim is to evaluate how adsorption acts as a universal preconcentration step to enhance four distinct degradation pathways (photocatalytic, biological, chemical and electrochemical), thereby addressing the current lack of synthesized data on the transition from laboratory-scale success to real-field application. Studies have reported that water remains a prominent research focus. This disparity is largely due to the high mobility of pollutants in aqueous media, which facilitates mass transfer. Furthermore, light-driven processes such as photocatalysis are more easily applied in translucent water than in opaque soil matrices where light penetration is limited. Photocatalytic degradation using cutting-edge materials such as doped TiO2 and composite photocatalysts such as Bi2WO6/TiO2/BC has demonstrated high efficiency in breaking down organic contaminants, with notable removal rates for pollutants such as Pb(II) and norfloxacin under visible light. The use of microbial strains such as Acinetobacter sp. M9 and Enterobacter sp. H1 for biodegradation provides a sustainable and eco-friendly method that works particularly well with antibiotics such as sulfadiazine in soil. Chemical degradation—which includes processes powered by catalytic systems such as Pd-doped TiO2 and MIL-88A(Fe)—shows promise in the decomposition of complex pollutants such as phenanthrene and hydrocarbons, with less research on soil applications. Emerging contaminants such as naproxen in water can be removed quickly and completely via electrochemical techniques, such as electro-Fenton processes aided by nanomaterials such as FeCuNPS@TWM. While many studies focus on either adsorption or a single degradation method applied to specific environmental media, such as air, water or soil, this work integrates adsorption with multiple degradation methods across all three media, offering an effective approach for pollutant remediation (Figure 2). In contrast to other reviews, this study provides a critical and integrative synthesis of multifunctional remediation processes, emphasizing the complementary relationship between advanced degradation techniques and adsorption. This method highlights innovative ways to increase multiphase environmental decontamination sustainability and efficiency. The specific aims are to evaluate the efficiency of adsorption-assisted degradation across four pathways and to identify scalable materials for environmental restoration.

2. Photocatalytic degradation

Photocatalytic degradation uses light-activated semiconductors to generate reactive oxygen species that oxidatively breakdown pollutants into smaller, less hazardous molecules. Unlike adsorption alone, photocatalysis often achieves full mineralization and chemical transformation under UV or visible light conditions. Adsorption enhances this process by improving the charge-transfer efficiency and concentrating contaminants on catalyst surfaces. Photocatalysis is typically used to treat dyes, medications, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and hydrocarbons in water, air, and soil. Materials such as TiO2, g-C3N4, LDHs, and Bibased complexes enable visible-light activity. This section focuses on the combination of adsorption and photocatalysis as a sustainable multimedium remediation strategy. 44

2.1. Photocatalytic degradation of water pollutants

Photocatalysis has gained widespread attention for water treatment because it not only efficiently degrades organic contaminants under light but also becomes more effective when combined with adsorption. Several composite photocatalysts incorporating carbon-based materials have shown promising results because of their improved adsorption properties and enhanced charge separation. For example, a Bi2WO6/TiO2/BC composite material was developed for the removal of norfloxacin and Pb(II) contaminants. The composite exhibited remarkable adsorption of norfloxacin onto the biochar surface, and the removal of Pb(II) reached 97%. Under visible light, 99.8% of norfloxacin was further degraded through photocatalysis, which was activated by the formation of reactive oxygen species and heterojunctions. This indicates the efficiency of the composite in removing mixed organic and inorganic pollutants by simultaneous adsorption and degradation. 45 Similarly, SnO2/Se-doped QDs are effective at removing the pollutant methylene blue (MB) via both adsorption and photocatalytic degradation. The synthesized SnO2/Se-GLUT exhibited excellent MB adsorption (94.3 mg/g) and facilitated efficient degradation under visible light, confirming its dual functionality despite a reduced rate of degradation. 46

Metal‒organic framework (MOF)-derived photocatalysts have also attracted attention because of their tunable porosity and enhanced surface properties. Ti-based metal organic frameworks (Ti-MOFs) as precursors to synthesize Bi2O3@TiO2 photocatalysts for the degradation of pollutants under visible light. Among the various composites prepared and evaluated, the optimized 10%Bi2O3@TiO2 sample displayed improved surface acidity, which greatly enhanced the adsorption of water contaminants, which serve as a critical factor for significant photocatalysis. This composite showed a pseudo-first-order kinetic rate constant (k) of 0.212 min-1 and caused the complete degradation of Eriochrome Black T (EBT) at a concentration of 50 ppm after 30 minutes. Additionally, it exhibited greater photocatalytic activity toward other dyes, decolorizing almost 99% of Congo Red, 95% of Malachite Green, 81% of Methylene Blue, and 69% of Safranine O within 15--30 minutes. 47 In addition to MOF-derived materials, layered double hydroxides (LDHs) represent another class of effective photocatalysts. Thermally activated Mg/Al layered double hydroxides (LDHs) significantly removed Direct Blue 71 dye via a synergistic mechanism of adsorption and UV-induced photocatalysis. Compared with the Fe-doped LDHs, the undoped LDHs were superior, with an 81% dye removal efficiency and a 53--59% removal efficiency. The LDHs showed complete mineralization within 120 minutes. 48

In certain photocatalytic systems, adsorption plays a supporting rather than a dominant role in pollutant removal. For example, methyl orange (MO) can be removed by Cu-CuO/TiO2 nanocomposite hollow fibers. This adsorption mechanism is dominated by physisorption destruction but constrained by pore diffusion, reaches a maximum capacity of approximately 6.7 mg/g and follows pseudo-first-order kinetic behavior well. Photocatalytic destruction also occurs very efficiently, following Langmuir‒Hinshelwood kinetic models. The availability of dissolved oxygen greatly increased the rate of degradation to approximately 65% after 3 h, which is greater than the 40–73% achieved under oxygen-limiting conditions and varies with the pollutant concentration. Furthermore, the reusability of the nanocomposite was excellent, with more than 92% retention of its initial efficiency after five cycles of reuse at moderate concentrations of dye. 49 Similarly, a sodium alginate graft copolymer loaded with silver nanoparticles (SACP5Ag) was used to remove methylene blue (MB) dye. This material exhibited outstanding batch (245 mg/g, 98%) and column (18.87 mg/g) adsorption abilities. It was also identified as an effective photocatalyst that degrades 98% of MB by sunlight in 80 minutes at a rate constant of 0.02 min-1. 50

While strong adsorption is not observed for all photocatalysts, their efficient degradation ability compensates for it. For instance, a silver-doped zinc oxide catalyst (Ag@Z) with extremely low adsorption efficiency (below 8%) for both triclosan (TCS) and methylene blue (MB) has been reported. Upon irradiation with UV-C light, the 5% Ag@Z catalyst exhibited excellent photocatalytic activity, degrading over 97% of both pollutants. TCS was degraded in 50 minutes, whereas MB was degraded in 80 minutes, indicating a much higher degradation rate than that of undoped ZnO. 51 Silver-functionalized zinc/magnesium ferrite nanoparticles (Zn0.5Mg0.5Fe2O4@Ag) were synthesized via sol-gel and solvothermal processes for malachite green (MG) removal. Adsorption was the primary removal mechanism, especially for the purified nanoparticles. Sol-gel-derived NPs (NPsAC@Ag) removed 95% of the MG under visible light (k=0.091 min-1), of which 89% was removed through dark absorption. Solvothermal NPs (NPAC@Ag) exhibited 87.6% total removal (k=0.017 min-1) and 83% dark adsorption. 52 These observations highlight that the relative contributions of adsorption and photocatalysis strongly depend on the catalyst composition and structural properties.

Recent advances have focused on membrane-based photocatalytic systems, which combine separation and degradation processes. Mo-BiVO4-coated ceramic ultrafiltration membranes efficiently extract tetracycline. Under dark conditions, as a result of significant adsorption on the membrane surface, up to 83% of the tetracycline in the permeate stream was eliminated. Additionally, under both UV and visible light irradiation, the Mo-bivo4 coating allowed for the photocatalytic breakdown of tetracycline. Analysis of total organic carbon (TOC) has demonstrated that this type of photocatalysis results in significant mineralization of pollutants (up to 44% under visible light) without the production of notable intermediates. 53 Other hybrid photocatalysts simultaneously remove metals and organics. Hydrothermal synthesis of mesoporous FeTiO3 prepared for the elimination of diverse contaminants. The design showed notable photoFenton performance upon solar irradiation, resulting in the effective degradation of organic dyes. In particular, 96% of the rhodamine B decomposed within 240 minutes, with a rate constant of 0.013 min-1. Methylene blue was completely degraded within 30 min through simultaneous adsorption and catalysis, with a greater rate constant of 0.12176 min-1. Furthermore, the material showed high rates of fluoride adsorption, with more than 40% being removed in the first 15 minutes. The removal of fluoride peaked at approximately 55.5 (1.5 mg/g) after 90 minutes. 54 SimiThe use of Ag/TiO2 nanoparticles for UV-driven photocatalysis and adsorption for dye removal. Initially, 90.9% of malachite green was broken down by the catalyst, indicating its high efficiency. It maintained reusability and high activity, although the performance decreased over three reuse cycles (83.9%, 78.8% and 68.5%). Moreover, the material has demonstrated efficacy against a range of dyes, with a focus on its potential for environmental remediation. 55 Carbon-based materials such as graphene oxide further demonstrate strong adsorption capacity because of their surface area. Graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets with different oxidation times maximize methylene blue (MB) adsorption. GO oxidized for 30 minutes had the highest rate of adsorption (k=3*10-3 min-1), and it adsorbed 43.7% of the MB within 180 minutes. Owing to structural instability, longer oxidation decreased the efficiency. Although GO is photocatalytic in this research, the focus was on degradation. 56

Hybrid photocatalysts combining adsorption supports with semiconductor materials have also demonstrated improved performance. BiOBr-TiO2-attapulgite composite-based photocatalysts and photocatalysts greatly enhanced the adsorption performance, allowing it to achieve equilibrium within 30 minutes and complete the degradation of 20 mg/L sodium ethyl xanthate (SEX) under visible light in 20 minutes, with a rate constant of 0.11699 min-1. This effective and rapid photocatalytic process, which is dominated by photogenerated holes, also has wide applications in degrading different xanthate pollutants. 57 Similarly, a novel composite material, F-Si-TiO2/AC, in which silicon and fluorine are introduced into a TiO2/activated carbon matrix, is used to improve the pollutant adsorption efficiency. The material showed superior adsorption performance for the removal of 57% methyl orange (MO) in 60 minutes in the dark. After adsorption, the composite also exhibited excellent photocatalytic activity under visible light, with a value of 97.7%. 58 Structural variations in photocatalysts can also significantly influence catalytic activity, as demonstrated by α-, β-, and γ-phase Bi2O3 nanomaterials being fabricated from a flower-like BiO2CO3 precursor. Photocatalytic activity was analyzed using four dyes, namely, methylene blue, rhodamine B, methyl orange, and Congo red, under visible light after 60 min of dark absorption and 120 min of irradiation. Β-Bi2O3 presented the best performance, with a photodegradation percentage of 96.97% for Congo Red, 85.30% for Rhodamine B, 92.59% for methyl orange and 94.10% for methylene blue. By kinetic analysis, β-Bi2O3 was confirmed to be the most efficient photocatalyst. 59

Other recent photocatalysts, such as CaTiO3 nanoparticles (CTO-NPs), were prepared via the Pechini method with the aim of targeting two new pollutants: levofloxacin (LVF) and methyl orange (MO). In this study, both the adsorption and photocatalytic degradation capacities were examined. The results revealed low adsorption of the pollutants by the CTO-NPs before UV illumination. However, after UV irradiation, high photocatalytic degradation was observed with pseudo-first-order kinetics. Under the best conditions, approximately 88.1% of the MO was broken down after 120 minutes with a q 5 g/L dose of the catalyst. Remarkably, the material had high photocatalytic activity, with 98.1% of the LVF degraded after 180 minutes when a 10 g/L concentration of the catalyst was used 61. Similarly, the thermal polymerization of g-C3N4 through urea (UCN) for photocatalytic rhodamine B degradation (RhB) was performed. UCN exhibited better activity because of its larger BET surface area and pore volume, with improved pollutant adsorption. After 40 min, 99.61% of the RhB was removed by visible light, and 10 mg/L RhB was completely degraded within 20 min under sunlight, resulting in efficient O2--based photocatalysis and enhanced adsorption. 60 Chitosan‒sodium lignosulfonate‒silver nanoparticle (CS‒LS‒AgNP) composites for the removal of synthetic dyes from wastewater through adsorption combined with photocatalytic degradation. After 30 minutes, the composites displayed 55% degradation of Rhodamine B (RhB) under optimal conditions. They further showed excellent effectiveness against other dyes, with 95% and 97% degradation rates of Telon Red A2R and Direct Dark Brown ME, respectively. These findings indicate the strong potential of the material for the treatment of numerous dye pollutants in wastewater. 61

Layered composite photocatalysts have further demonstrated strong synergy between adsorption and photocatalysis. BiVO4/MgAl-LDH composite prepared via the memory effect of calcined MgAl-LDHs upon reformation with BiVO4 for effective cationic dye removal in terms of methylene blue (MB). The material exhibited rapid adsorption, with 66.1% depletion of the MB in 20 minutes, followed by visible light-mediated photocatalytic degradation, which eliminated 92.4% of the residual dye in 100 minutes. The significant benefits are easy, low-cost synthesis, improved functionality as a result of high surface area and photocatalytic nature, and excellent recyclability, with 86% efficacy preserved after four cycles. 62 Similarly, silver-decorated MXene (Ag@Ti3C2Tx) nanocomposites were synthesized to examine their visible light photocatalytic activity. Owing to the high surface area of MXenes, these materials ensure the effective adsorption of safranin dye. Among the prepared composites, Ag5O@Ti3C2Tx showed superior performance, resulting in 99.6% dye degradation in 15 minutes when only 5 mg of catalyst was used, which is more efficient than that of the Ag2O@Ti3C2Tx counterpart. 63 Overall, these results indicate that combining adsorption and photocatalysis significantly enhances the efficiency of pollutant removal by combining rapid pollutant capture with subsequent oxidative degradation. However, there are still challenges with long-term recyclability, large-scale application and catalyst stability. To enable useful wastewater treatment applications, future research should focus on designing multifunctional photocatalysts with improved adsorption capacity, enhanced visible light utilization, and stable catalytic performance to enable practical wastewater treatment applications.

2.2. Photocatalytic degradation of air pollutants

In addition to aqueous systems, photocatalysis is also employed for gaseous pollutants such as VOCs, NOx, biological contaminants, and indoor air toxins and is often coupled with adsorption through porous membranes or filters. Nanostructured photocatalytic filters have shown promising performance in VOC removal because of their high surface area and enhanced light absorption properties. For instance, carbon-modified porous TiO2 with an internal hydrophobic interface was synthesized through the pyrolysis of a titanium-based metal–organic framework (MIL-125). The porous structure enhances the adsorption and diffusion of toluene molecules, whereas the gradient carbon distribution within the pores promotes efficient photogenerated electron transfer. The hydrophobic interface also reduces the competitive adsorption between water vapor and VOC molecules, thereby improving the photocatalytic performance. The optimized catalyst obtained by calcining MIL-125 at 400°C for 1 h exhibited superior photocatalytic activity for gas-phase toluene degradation. Notably, its degradation efficiency was nearly twice that of a commercial TiO2 photocatalyst (P25). 64 Similarly, a Cu2O-NP/TiO2-NT composite material showed great performance in the concurrent photocatalytic destruction of airborne indoor bacteria and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Pollutant removal occurred through an adsorption process on the catalyst surface via the Langmuir‒Hinshelwood mechanism, followed by degradation. Compared with pure TiO2-NTs, the composite displayed notably higher degradation rates. The generation of chlorine radicals through chloroform decomposition has a synergistic effect, enhancing the collation removal efficiency of both pathogens and other pollutants. 65 Several studies have focused on modifying TiO2-based catalysts to extend their activity to the visible light region and improve their degradation efficiency. Fe‒N-codoped TiO2 (FeNTiO2) nanoparticles act as catalysts to break down gaseous toluene, a contaminant found at building construction sites. The authors enhanced the photocatalytic activity in the visible range via ultrasound-assisted sonoprecipitation. This ultrasonic processing increased the dispersion of the particles and surface area, and the band gap was narrowed to 2.83 eV for FeNTiO2(5)-U. Consequently, the catalyst resulted in a 63.5% rate of toluene degradation after 15 s, which was a much superior result compared with its nonultrasonically prepared counterpart. 66

Photocatalytic removal of NOx has been demonstrated using nitrogen oxide (NOx/NO2) emissions from automotive exhaust. This study investigated the application of nano-TiO2 embedded in a cement slurry as an air-cleansing material. The process is based on two important steps: the pollutants are first trapped on the porous and rough surface of the slurry and then undergo photocatalytic destruction assisted by the nano-TiO2 upon exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light. However, laboratory experiments have proven the efficiency of the method. The environmental conditions also had a considerable influence on performance. A field test near a highway toll booth validated the material’s usability under real-world conditions. The photocatalytic degradation rate of NO2 was estimated to be approximately 0.28 mg/m2/day from measurements of the resulting nitrate (NO3-) concentrations. 67

Furthermore, sulfur-doped TiO2 nanoparticles with visible-light activity were supported over a silica (SiO2) matrix. The addition of silica facilitated the adsorption of pollutants and the homogenous distribution of the photocatalyst, whereas sulfur doping increased the material’s sensitivity to the visible range. Among the photocatalytic activities tested for the degradation of air pollutants, the S(4)-TiO2/SiO2 composite showed the highest efficacy, removing acetaldehyde by approximately 26% and oxidizing NO by 12%, thereby causing an overall nox removal rate of 4.5% after one hour of visible light irradiation. 68 TiO2-modified cotton fabric for self-cleaning against the influenza A virus. Compared with plain cotton, the TiO2 coating improved virus adsorption and photoactivation under UV light. Viral components, such as RNA, are completely degraded after 3–6 h, and the virus becomes undetectable via PCR. The material exhibited high potential for eliminating dangerous biological and chemical contaminants via combined adsorption and photocatalysis. 69

Hybrid air purification systems that integrate photocatalytic oxidation with conventional filtration technologies have also demonstrated significant improvements in terms of pollutant removal efficiency. For example, a system consists of a HEPA filter used to trap particulate matter (PM) and a photocatalytic oxidation (PCO) module used to degrade organic PM and VOCs. The PCO unit contributed 99% of ultrafine PM elimination within 10 minutes and removed more than 94% of the VOCs within 2 hours, which is more effective than HEPA alone. 70 Recent studies have also explored advanced composite photocatalysts to improve pollutant adsorption and charge separation. The incorporation of graphene into the TiO2 mats significantly enhanced the photocatalytic degradation of gaseous methanol and acetaldehyde. The enhancement is attributed to several factors: increased pollutant adsorption, as verified by kinetic studies; a reduced bandgap in TiO2-GO composites; and increased electron mobility contributed by the presence of graphene, effectively hindering electron mobility recombination. Thus, the composites were able to completely degrade acetaldehyde in 15 to 20 minutes, whereas pure TiO2 took approximately 40 minutes for the same process. 71 Similarly, Z-scheme heterostructured photocatalysts have been developed to increase charge separation efficiency and improve photocatalytic performance. A Z-type g-C3H4/(A-R)TiO2 composite catalyst (ARTCN) for pollutant degradation. Owing to its Z-scheme heterostructure, the modified catalyst, ARTCN-0.5, displayed higher dispersion, stronger light absorption and improved carrier separation. This study revealed the photocatalytic breakdown of gaseous ammonia with a focus on air pollution. ARTCN-0.5 demonstrated good repeatability, achieving a high degradation rate of 88.865 at an initial ammonia concentration of 50 mg/m-3 under 0.9 ml/min moist air flow conditions. Z-scheme charge transfer facilitates the effective production of active species, i.e., O2- and OH, via this mechanism. 72

In addition to semiconductor heterostructures, MOF–semiconductor hybrid catalysts have recently attracted attention for use in air purification applications. For example, HKUST-1@TiO2 nanocomposites were prepared via a new sol‒gel process for dual applications: adsorption and visible-light-induced photocatalytic degradation of indoor formaldehyde (HCHO). The optimized nanocomposite offered a high adsorption capacity of 4.89 mmol/g and 100% photocatalytic conversion at 600 W, with a turnover frequency (TOF) of 3.64 × 10-3 min-1. Its improved performance is attributed to an increased surface area, structural defects, and a decreased band gap (1.77 eV). An increased HCHO concentration and light intensity also resulted in further improved degradation (TOF up to 4.79 × 10-3 min at 1600 W), whereas increased humidity negatively affected the efficiency. 73

Overall, adsorption-assisted photocatalysis has emerged as an effective strategy for the removal of airborne pollutants. The combination of heterojunction materials, hybrid filtration systems, and nanostructured photocatalysts significantly increases the efficiency of pollutant adsorption and photocatalytic degradation. Sustaining consistent performance in real-world environments is still challenging, particularly when humidity and complex pollutant mixtures are present. Therefore, future research should focus on developing long-lasting photocatalysts with increased visible activity and higher adsorption capacity for practical air purification applications.

2.3. Photocatalytic degradation of soil pollutants

Photocatalysis has also been applied to contaminated soils, primarily for PAHs, agrochemicals, antibiotics, and metals. Soil systems benefit from adsorption because of high solid‒liquid interfaces, and they also provide oxidation pathways for persistent compounds. Several studies have reported enhanced soil remediation using composite photocatalysts that combine high adsorption capacity with efficient charge separation. For example, a composite material of graphitic carbon nitride and biochar (BC/g-C3N4) was used to remediate petroleum-contaminated soil. The process involved adsorption and photocatalytic degradation. The combination of these factors led to a 54.5% decrease in total petroleum hydrocarbon (TPH) content, which was greater than that resulting from the use of either material individually. The addition of biochar supported the photocatalytic activity of g-C3N4 while increasing its adsorption ability. The removal kinetics adopted a pseudo-second-order mechanism, which was an indication of the overall process rate. 74 Similarly, advanced semiconductor modifications have been applied to degrade persistent agrochemicals. A novel photocatalyst, AuNPs/Cl-doped In2O3, for the degradation of long-lasting agrochemical pollutants. Compared with undoped In2O3, the synergistic effect of chlorine doping and gold nanoparticle decoration considerably improved the photocatalytic activity by enhancing visible light absorption and facilitating effective charge separation. With CFL light illumination, the catalyst shows excellent efficiency, with more than 96% degradation of both carbaryl and 2,4-d within 120 min, indicating pseudo-first-order kinetics. It also retained robust performance, with degradation efficiencies greater than 91% under outdoor sunlight of over 88% and significant concurrent removal in mixed-contaminant systems, i.e., more than 89% for carbaryl and 99% for 2,4-d, indicating its actual usability. 75 Recent developments have focused on hybrid catalytic systems that combine photocatalysis with enzymatic or persulfate activation processes to enhance pollutant degradation. A C3N5/laccase (C3N5/Lac) photoenzyme catalyst with a van der Waals heterojunction to facilitate charge separation in heterogeneous catalysis. It activated persulfate (PDS) under visible light to decompose tetracycline (TCH), reaching 86.3% removal within 60 minutes (k=0.04976 min-1). The enhanced catalytic efficiency is due to increased charge transfer and strong PDS adsorption, which facilitates the formation of reactive species such as 1O2 and O2-.76

A lotus-structured composite material (BiWO6/BiS3/g-C3N4) for degrading the antibiotic norfloxacin (NFX) under visible light. The photocatalyst degraded up to 92.4% of the NFX after 120 minutes. This high activity is connected to the synergistic effect of the components, which improved the charge carrier separation and light harvesting. The treatment also significantly minimized the toxicity of the NFX solution. 77 In addition to organic pollutants, organic photovoltaic materials are deposited onto coconut shell charcoal to remove manganese (Mn) from water bodies and surrounding soils. Through adsorption coupled with sunlight-powered photocatalysis, the best material (PM7:SDIPBI:IXIC-4Cl) achieved 91.6% removal of Mn(II) after one hour. The reactive species generated upon illumination upon initial adsorption exhibited pseudo-first-order kinetics with a rate constant of 0.396 min-1. The material also showed excellent reusability. 78 Overall, these studies demonstrate that integrating adsorption materials with photocatalysts significantly improves pollutant capture and degradation in soil remediation systems. However, compared with research on water and air treatment, research on soil remediation remains relatively limited because of challenges such as catalyst recovery, soil heterogeneity and light penetration limitations. Therefore, further studies are needed to develop more efficient photocatalysts and scalable strategies for practical soil decontamination. In this review, the reported studies were compared primarily on the basis of degradation efficiency (%) and reaction time, which indicate the effectiveness and kinetics of the remediation processes. Operational costs and environmental impacts are discussed qualitatively because of the limited availability of standardized economic data in laboratory-scale studies. The data summarized in the tables represent a compilation of results reported in independent studies and are intended to provide an overview of reported remediation performances.

In contrast, the degradation efficiency of photocatalysis was lower or slower in air and soil environments. The photocatalytic degradation of the indoor air pollutants toluene, acetaldehyde, formaldehyde, methanol and NO2 has shown promising potential, although it remains less researched. Ag–TiO2 fibers achieved approximately 90% removal of toluene in 120 minutes, whereas the BC/g-C3N4 system degraded only approximately 54.5% of the total petroleum hydrocarbons in soil over 28 days, indicating that mass transfer limitations and light penetration constraints significantly affect solid-phase photocatalytic processes. Similarly, for gaseous pollutants such as acetaldehyde and nitric oxide, the degradation efficiency was modest (12–26%), suggesting the need for better catalyst dispersion and light–surface interactions. Challenges such as gas-phase diffusion limitations and surface optimization of the catalyst remain less focused. Air photocatalysis presents a promising path for improving indoor air quality.

Soil photocatalysis is less explored than water and air systems are, particularly for compounds such as norfloxacin, petroleum contaminants, agrochemical pollutants and heavy metals such as manganese. Catalysts such as g-C3N4, AuNPs/Cl-doped In2O3 and Bi2WO6 are applied under visible light or xenon lamps, but their degradation in soil is slower, usually with a time span of 28 days. This is due to restricted penetration by light, the complex structure of the soil, and poor catalyst‒soil interactions, with efficiencies ranging from 48–87%.

Overall, the data reveal that while TiO2 remains a core material in photocatalysis, its modification with other semiconductors (e.g., Bi2WO6, CuO, FeTiO3, g-C3N4) significantly enhances performance under visible light. The incorporation of metals (Ag, Cu, and Fe) or carbon-based supports (g-C3N4 and BC) enhances the photocatalytic efficiency by reducing electron–hole recombination. Visible light and solar-driven systems achieve high efficiencies comparable to those of UV systems, offering energy-saving alternatives. Photocatalysis in water is most efficient; air and soil media show lower rates because of poor light penetration and mass transfer limitations. However, despite high degradation rates under laboratory conditions, real environmental applications—especially in air and soil systems—still face challenges related to efficiency, durability, and scalability. Studies on industrial wastewater, soil, or gaseous pollutants are limited, indicating the need for more applied research.

3. Biodegradation

Biodegradation represents another major strategy for environmental remediation, which relies on the metabolic capabilities of microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, consortia) and environmentally friendly catalytic systems (bioenzymes, biochar, immobilized cultures, and biomicromotors). Like photocatalysis, biodegradation often couples with adsorption, in which solid matrices increase microbial or catalytic access while concentrating contaminants. 79 Recent research on soil, water and air pollution biodegradation is categorized in this section.

3.1. Biodegradation of soil pollutants

Soil biodegradation is strongly influenced by soil physiochemical composition (pH, SOM, and ions), pollutant solubility, and nutrient/microbe interactions. Several studies have demonstrated diverse approaches ranging from microbial bioaugmentation to catalytic biomicromotors. Hybrid catalytic-biological systems have been developed for soils. For example, multifunctional micromotors based on MnO2 and MnFe2O4 nanoparticles coated on natural Kapok fibers have been synthesized. These micromotors efficiently eradicate pollutants via two main mechanisms: heavy metal adsorption, such as lead, by virtue of their active sites and high surface area, and the degradation of antibiotics, such as tetracycline, i.e., over 90% within 30 minutes via the catalytic production of •OH and SO4•- radicals from PMS (peroxymonosulfate) and H2O2. 80 Microbial bioaugmentation has been widely studied for the degradation of antibiotics and other emerging contaminants in soils. For example, the bacterial strains Acinetobacter sp. M9 and Enterobacter sp. H1 degrade the antibiotic contaminant sulfadiazine (SDZ) from soil via microbial degradation. When the contaminated soil was inoculated with these strains, the degradation of SDZ was significantly greater than that in the uninoculated controls. The degradation efficacy increased by 24.99% when strain M9 was used and by 30.05% when strain H1 was used at high contamination levels. 81

Other studies have explored chemical‒biological cotreatment approaches for improving the degradation of persistent contaminants in soil systems. For instance, ferrous-activated persulfate (Fe-PAP)-based methods for degrading triclosan (TCS), triclocarban (TCC) and naproxen (NPS) in nine types of soil were used. The decomposition rate substantially varied within 4 h; the decomposition rate of the NPs ranged from 47–100%, that of the TCS ranged from 11–92%, and that of the TCC did not decrease. This is due to the soil organic matter (SOM), which tends to suppress the breakdown process by binding to pollutants and reducing reactive species. The physiochemical properties of the contaminants and the soil composition controlled the degradation performance, with the SOM acting as an especially limiting factor. 82 In addition to chemical oxidation systems, plasma-assisted catalytic processes have been studied, and the improved degradation of phenanthrene (Phe) in soil by a dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma system connected with a MnFeO4 catalyst has been reported. The mesoporous structure, rich active sites, and high surface area (42.51 m2/g) of the catalyst favor strong adsorption of Phe and reactive species such as ozone. Its redox-active elements (Fe(II)/Fe(III) and Mn(II)/Mn(III)) synergistically cooperate with plasma to produce reactive oxygen species (OH,O2-), stimulating pollutant removal. It performed better than DBD alone by attaining 90.21% Phe elimination in 5 minutes 84. In addition to catalytic systems, microbial bioaugmentation has been explored for enhancing the degradation of pesticide-derived contaminants in soil. For example, the degradation of the soil pollutant 3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCP) was emphasized. A local bacterial strain, Cupriavidus sp. JL-1, was rapidly detected as a dominant microorganism that can degrade TCP in the soil environment via DNA-stable isotope probing (DNA-SIP). Owing to its continuous activity and high adaptability in contaminated soils, the application of this native strain for autochthonous bioaugmentation significantly enhanced the in situ degradation process. The TCP removal efficiency was 80.1% after 28 days, which is approximately 1.65 times higher than the rate of degradation with a nonnative microbial strain 83.

Soil amendments have also been shown to regulate adsorption behavior and microbial activity during pollutant degradation. For instance, the environmental fate of sulfamethoxazole (SMX) was studied in unamended soil and soil amended with holm oak biochar (BC) or urban pruning green compost (GC) to examine the behavior of sulfamethoxazole (SMX). In nonamended soil, SMX showed low adsorption (3–14%) and a degradation half-life (DT50) of 6.9 days. BC amendment increased SMX adsorption (14–39%) but decreased microbial degradation, increasing the DT50 to 11.9 days, due to decreased bioavailability. Conversely, GC stimulated microbial activity and hence quicker breakdown of DT-50 by 2.6 days, moderate adsorption (10--31%), and greater leaching. 84 Similarly, the use of carrier-based immobilized microbial systems has demonstrated promising results for the removal of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) from contaminated soils. Decontamination of phenanthrene (Phe) and pyrene (Pyr) from soil by employing a loofah-based carrier with immobilized mixed cultures of Pseudomonas sp. SDR4 and Mortierella alpina JDR7. Although dead cells and loofah both play crucial roles in PAH adsorption through chemisorption (quasisecond-order kinetics), active mixed cultures greatly improved PAH degradation. The treatment resulted in 68.32% destruction of Phe and 47.24% destruction of Pyr in 60 days, with estimated half-lives of 18.98 and 46.32 days, respectively, via first-order kinetics. 85 In addition to microbial carriers, enzyme-assisted remediation strategies have been developed. For example, sorption-modified bentonite in combination with crude enzyme immobilization greatly improved benzo[a]pyrene (bap) removal from polluted soil. Compared with adsorption alone, the combined strategy resulted in a maximum bap removal of 91.69% within 30 days. Modified bentonite rapidly enriched the contaminant, while the degradation of the immobilized enzymes increased. This process also affects soil microbial populations, increasing aerobic activity and the abundance of PAH-degrading bacteria such as Streptomyces. The combination of adsorption and biodegradation aided by enhanced soil conditions offers an effective and environmentally friendly approach for remediating recalcitrant soil contaminants 86

Hybrid catalytic materials capable of simultaneous adsorption and degradation have also attracted attention for use in soil remediation. An energy-efficient wet chemical method for creating Cu-C (copper-cellulose) nanocomposites that uses a novel dual-channel catalytic mechanism that combines direct electron transfer and classical radical oxidation to adsorb and degrade a variety of organic soil pollutants, such as tetracycline, naproxen and sulfamethoxazole, is proposed. These Cu‒C catalysts removed up to 96% of pollutants under a variety of environmental circumstances, such as different pH values and ion concentrations, within 30–60 minutes. The catalyst offered stability, reusability, quick and efficient recovery of contaminated soils and a significant reduction in toxicity in treated soils, which promoted plant growth. 87 Microbial consortium-based strategies have also been explored for the degradation of herbicides in contaminated soils. A microbial consortium (G2) from glycophosphate-contaminated tea soil demonstrated significant herbicide degradation ability. The bioavailability of herbicides is reduced, and their breakdown is slowed in acidic, organic-rich soils. Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, Chloroflexi, Actinobacteria, and Myxococcota are major degraders, and 400 mg/L can be removed in seven days by an enriched G2 consortium that is dominated by Afipia, Dokdonella, and Cohnella. Microbial access is influenced by site-specific adsorption strength, which is weak in KL soil and strong in JM soil. As a result, half-lives ranging from approximately 13 to 268 days have been reported, indicating that glyphosate can persist for extended periods of time under specific circumstances. 88

Furthermore, the interaction between adsorption and biodegradation processes has been shown to significantly influence the removal efficiency of pharmaceutical contaminants such as naproxen (NPS), triclosan (TCS), and triclocarban (TCC). Strong adsorption to soil organic matter (SOM) was demonstrated by the presence of hydrophobic compounds such as TCS and TCC, which limited their breakdown. Although not the primary focus, biodegradation seemed less efficient than chemical oxidation. There were significant differences in degradation efficiency; TCC slightly decreased, whereas TCS and NPS varied between 10.92 and 91.62% and between 46.59 and 99.81%, respectively. Degradation is accelerated by elements such as high solubility, low pKa, and advantageous molecular characteristics. Furthermore, the composition of SOM affects the degradation efficiency and pollutant reactivity in various soil types. 89 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that adsorption significantly influences the bioavailability and degradation behavior of soil pollutants.

3.2. Biodegradation of water pollutants

Biodegradation in aquatic systems benefits from increased microbial accessibility but is affected by pollutant solubility and microbial compatibility. Combined adsorption biodegradation (CAB) systems have become relevant for pharmaceuticals, hormones, dyes and hydrocarbons. For example, a combined batch method can be used to treat wastewater from pharmaceuticals. First, doxycycline and norfloxacin were adsorbed onto the rice straw charcoal and torrefied, respectively, for 90 minutes. The remaining pharmaceutical dyes diclofenac and paracetamol were subsequently biodegraded by a microbial consortium of Klebsiella (DY7, DY10, DY11, DY18) and Pseudomonas (dcα4, prs10) species for ten days. This process resulted in 92.31% COD removal. The degradation showed variable kinetics, with an initial order of 0.736 and gradually increasing to 2.019, with a maximum specific rate of degradation (µmax) of 2.18 per day. 90 Similarly, advanced polymer-based materials have been explored for their ability to simultaneously enhance adsorption and biodegradation. Chitosan-based porous cryogels formed through glutaraldehyde crosslinking and NaBH4 reduction demonstrated high adsorption capacities for water pollutants, with values of 88.73 ± 4.25 mg/g for Cu(II) and 3261.05 ± 36.10 mg/g for Congo red dye. The presence of porous amine and hydroxyl groups, as well as the interconnected rigid scaffold, positively contributes to adsorption and microbial degradation. The application of chitosanase, cellulase, lysozyme and trypsin for enzymatic degradation with these blocks confirms the high biodegradability of the copolymers, which is advantageous in terms of environmentally friendly waste treatment and bioremediation. 91

In groundwater remediation, CAB systems have also shown promising results for the removal of chlorinated solvents. A coupled adsorption‒biodegradation (CAB) method for trichloroethylene (TCE) removal from groundwater using pinewood biochar (PWB) as an adsorbent and polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) as a hydrogen source. TCE capture and Dehalococcoides mccartyi-dominated biofilm growth were sustained by PWB, while fermentation of PHB supplied hydrogen for reductive dichlorination. The system processed 1300 L of water over eight months, eliminating approximately 102 mg of TCE per day, indicating the effectiveness of adsorption coupled with biodegradation. 92 Apart from biochar-supported systems, immobilized microbial communities have also been employed for hydrocarbon removal in aquatic environments. For example, the microbial communities of Rhodococcus ruber S103, Mycolicibacterium parafortuitum J101 and Mycolicibacterium austroafricanum Y502 were immobilized on plastic bioballs for the treatment of waterborne fuel oil pollutants. Nonpathogenic biosurfactant-producing bacteria have been isolated from PAH-enriched river sediments and have a broad substrate range. In freshwater model systems of synthetic and natural origin, more than 50% of the high fuel oil content (3000 mg/L) was removed within 7 days. Approximately 46% of the oil that was immobilized on the bioballs biodegraded within a period of time. This immobilization method provides a viable method for remediating long-term petroleum contaminants in water bodies. 93

Microbial degradation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) has also been explored using newly identified bacterial strains. For example, newly identified strains of Neorhizobium phenanthrenickasticum sp. Nov. Rsf11 obtained from the rhizosphere soil of Medicago sativa L. exhibited significant bioremediation activity. The strain was able to break down 58% of the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon phenanthrene (0.2 g/L initial concentration) over a period of 10 days, and 1-hydroxyl-2-naphthoic acid was identified as the major byproduct. Extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs) of bacteria are important for resistance to nickel toxicity, mainly through surface-based adsorption at concentrations of up to 1.3 mm. However, in the presence of phenanthrene, nickel inhibits microbial growth but not enzyme function, resulting in a decrease in phenanthrene degradation. 94 Similarly, biofilm-based treatment systems have been developed for the removal of antibiotic contaminants from wastewater. A high-performance bioremediation system and Pseudomonas putida mnb1 biofilm on manganese-coated sand for the removal of antibiotics such as doxycycline (DOX), moxifloxacin (MOX) and ofloxacin (OFLO) from wastewater. The system integrates manganese sand adsorption with enhanced biodegradation and production of biogenic manganese oxides and singlet oxygen (O2). The DOX removal efficiency ranged between 82.36% and 93.25%, with a maximum rate of 99.62% within 206 days. During a 30-day trial, the system removed DOX, MOX and OFLO at average rates of 97.71%, 89.32% and 81.6%, respectively. 95

Enzyme-assisted adsorption–biodegradation systems have also been developed for the removal of plasticizers from water. A novel lipase@MOF biocomposite, Cys-CRL@GA@MAF-507, for effective removal of phthalate esters (PAEs), especially dibutyl phthalate (DBP), from water. The system combines Candida rugosa lipase (CRL) immobilized in a cysteine-modified and glutaraldehyde-cross-linked MAF-507 matrix. This single-pot synthesis produces a double-purpose material that facilitates fast absorption (88.56% of DBP in 5 minutes) followed by enzymatic biodegradation (94.6% in 48 hours). Biocomposites present an effective solution for combined adsorption–biodegradation-based treatment of PAE contaminants. 96 Advanced catalytic systems have also been explored for antibiotic degradation in water environments. A Fenton-like catalyst, FG@PAM-Fe3O4, was prepared from polyacrylamide-modified waste foam glass. Efficiently degraded tetracycline (TC) in water. With low initial adsorption, 100% of the 20 mg/L TC was removed within 30 min through rapid catalytic degradation. The catalyst showed more than 80% efficiency even after ten cycles of reuse and for ten days of continuous flow operation. Biotoxicity tests validated the reduced toxicity of treated TC, emphasizing the strong potential of these catalysts for eco-friendly water bioremediation 99.

The effects of biochar-supported microbial degradation on the removal of PAHs in water have also been investigated. Woodchip-derived biochars obtained at different pyrolysis temperatures (150--700°C) were used to evaluate phenanthrene removal via Sphingobium yanoikuyae B1. Higher temperatures favored adsorption from enhanced pore filling, whereas desorption and intracellular degradation decreased. A lower temperature (≤300°C) resulted in a maximum of 92.5% degradation of phenanthrene, which occurred mainly through intracellular processes. Conversely, at higher temperatures (≥600°C), extracellular degradation occurs through the formation of siderophores, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals in Fenton-like reactions. The degradation rate constants varied between 0.07 and 0.14 h-1 under all conditions, indicating that the biochar properties strongly influence the degradation pathways. 97 Furthermore, recent studies have investigated the role of biodegradable microplastics in influencing pollutant adsorption and biodegradation behavior in aquatic systems. Interaction between Cu(II) and oxytetracycline (OTC) with starch-based biodegradable microplastics (PPT: 70% PBAT, 5% PLA, 20% thermoplastic starch) under microbial colonization and degradation. The adsorption of both contaminants was increased by microbial activity through pseudo-second-order kinetics. As degradation proceeded, OTC adsorption, principally through hydrophobic partitioning, remained stable, but Cu(II) adsorption decreased because of the destruction of surface functional groups and increased crystallinity. Notably, in binary pollutant systems, biodegraded PPT was more adsorptive of Cu(II) (20.27%) and OTC (8.63 times) than single pollutant arrangements were. These results indicate that microbial degradation changes the surface of PPT, which increases its capacity for binding pollutants. 98

3.3. Biodegradation of air pollutants

The biodegradation of air pollutants involves mainly biodegradable filters that adsorb traps and then naturally decompose after use, reducing secondary waste. Biodegradable intake filters have been developed using protein‒polymer nanofibers. For instance, protein‒polymer nanofiber membranes composed of zein and polylactic acid (PLA) have been developed as biodegradable intake filters. These zein/PLA nanofiber membranes with high surface areas and tunable hydrophobicity were effective at adsorbing oily impurities and particulate matter from water. When the ratio of PLA to zein was equal to 1:1, the membranes provided maximum filtration efficiencies of 98.14% for PM2.5 and 97.39% for PM10, with a quality factor (QF) value of 0.00738 Pa-1. Biodegradation experiments under soil conditions indicated that the membrane containing zein completely degraded after 21 days, in contrast to pure PLA. This increased degradation was notably observed in membranes with greater contents of zein since the zein protein enabled enzymatic assault against the polymer structure. These findings demonstrate the potential of the membranes for green pollutant elimination as well as for use in bioremediation processes. 99

Similarly, biodegradable nanofiber filters based on electrospun cellulose acetate nanofibers and cellulose fibers in a dual-network, spider-web-like design were used to create a biodegradable air filter that improved the electrostatic adsorption and biodegradation of air pollutants. The addition of binary electrets (silicon nitride and tourmaline) greatly enhanced the charge retention and achieved a greater than 99% PM0.3 filtration efficiency, with a low pressure drop of 45.7 Pa. With only a 0.88% decrease in efficiency over 85 days, the filter demonstrated exceptional reusability. It also continued to function well for more than 12 hours at 90% humidity. Within 80 days, it breaks down naturally, providing an ongoing approach for reducing airborne particulate matter. 100

Overall, this highlights the adaptability and efficiency of bacterial and fungal species in degrading pharmaceuticals, hydrocarbons, and persistent organic compounds across aquatic and terrestrial systems.

In aqueous and wastewater environments, bacterial consortia and immobilized enzymes demonstrated high degradation efficiencies under aerobic conditions. For instance, a bacterial consortium comprising Klebsiella sp., Pseudomonas sp., and related strains achieved ∼95% degradation of diclofenac, paracetamol, and indigo carmine within 10 days in a continuously aerated reactor. Similarly, an immobilized lipase enzyme system (Cys-CRL@GA@MAF-507) efficiently degraded dibutyl phthalate (94.6%) within 48 hours, highlighting the potential of biocatalytic systems for the rapid removal of emerging contaminants. Rhodococcus and Mycobacterium strains immobilized on plastic balls degraded fuel oil by more than 50%. Phenanthrene (58%) was broken down by Sphingobium sp. after ten days. After 206 days, a manganese-oxidizing biofilm was used to remove up to 93.25% of the doxycycline. Candida rugosa enzymes were used to break down dibutyl phthalate by 94.6% in 48 hours. According to these studies, water-based biodegradation has considerable potential.

Despite being less studied than aquatic systems are, soil biodegradation still provides important information about the removal of pollutants on land. In soil-based studies, microbial activity is generally slower because of limited nutrient diffusion and lower bioavailability of pollutants. For example, Acinetobacter sp. and Enterobacter sp. degraded sulfadiazine at rates less than 30% in 45 days, whereas native soil microbes achieved nearly 100% sulfamethoxazole degradation in compost-amended soils, demonstrating the positive influence of organic amendments and native microbial synergy. The biodegradation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) such as phenanthrene and pyrene was moderate (47–68%) after 60 days, indicating the persistence and recalcitrance of such compounds in soil.

Some studies, such as those involving Dehalococcoides mccartyi for trichloroethylene (TCE) degradation, required long-term operation (8 months) because of the complexity of anaerobic dechlorination pathways but achieved nearly complete pollutant removal (∼99%). Overall, compared with single strains, microbial consortia and immobilized enzyme systems resulted in faster and more efficient degradation, especially in controlled aqueous systems.

The results indicate that biodegradation is highly dependent on environmental conditions, microbial type, and pollutant characteristics. While aqueous systems show rapid removal under aerobic conditions, soil and groundwater remediation often require longer incubation times and supportive amendments to sustain microbial activity and substrate bioavailability.

Owing to the lack of studies using standard procedures and particular microbial data, the biodegradation of air pollutants, particularly gaseous contaminants such as VOCs, remains underexplored. Clear degradation results and the identification of effective strains are lacking in most related studies. Isolating efficient microorganisms, improving biofiltration systems, and carrying out useful experiments for practical applications are the main goals of future research.

4. Chemical degradation

Chemical degradation describes pollutant breakdown via nonbiological pathways such as ozonation, radical oxidation, photocatalysis, sonochemical cleavage, redox reactions and advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). Chemical degradation, in contrast to simple adsorption, breaks down pollutants into smaller, frequently less harmful species. Adsorption improves reaction kinetics by concentrating impurities at catalyst interfaces or within reactive zones, which occurs before chemical degradation. 101 The chemical degradation of water, soil, and air pollutants is the focus of this section, where adsorption and degradation occur simultaneously.

4.1. Chemical degradation of water pollutants

In aqueous environments, chemical degradation is dominated by AOPs, photocatalysis, piezocatalysis, sonolysis, and Fenton-like redox mechanisms, many of which benefit from the preadsorption of pollutants. An ultrasonic technique for the degradation of PFAS (perfluoro sulfonic acids), whereby the compounds initially adsorb onto cavitation bubble surfaces—an essential step prior to their chemical breakdown starting with headgroup cleavage. This adsorption, which is dictated by the hydrophobic nature of the compound, affects the rate of degradation. More hydrophobic longer-chain PFAS degrade more quickly than shorter-chain PFAS do. Thus, PFCAs (perfluoro carboxylic acids) tend to degrade more effectively than PFASs do. Aqueous sonolysis offers ideal conditions for such degradation. 102 Similarly, the use of adsorption-assisted photocatalytic systems to increase degradation efficiency has been explored. Photo-responsive PNIPAM-rose Bengal microgels that remarkably improved the adsorption of pollutants such as furoic acid and diclofenac through localized concentrations. Upon exposure to sunlight, these microgels produce singlet oxygen, allowing effective photocatalytic destruction of pollutants. Diclofenac was destroyed by more than 99% within approximately 160 minutes. This process further facilitates the green synthesis of 5-hydroxy-2(5H)-furanone. These microgels display high reusability over many cycles, function efficiently in water-based systems, are cost effective and can be aligned with green chemistry principles by utilizing solar power and minimizing waste. 103

In addition to polymer-based systems, biomass-derived materials have been investigated for use in adsorption-assisted chemical degradation. Lignocellulose-derived hydrothermal carbon (HTCC) for the effective removal of tetracycline (TC). Lignin-derived HTCC (HTCC-L) was the most efficient, eliminating 63.5% of the total cholesterol (TC) in 120 minutes. Its superior performance is due to its nonradical charge transfer mechanism and carboxyl groups, which facilitate strong chemisorption via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. 104 Furthermore, semiconductor heterojunction catalysts enhanced charge separation and reactive species formation. A GOD/BiOCl-Vo heterojunction photocatalyst where graphene quantum dots (gods) facilitate electron transfer and serve as photosensitizers and engineered oxygen vacancies (Vo) facilitate O2 adsorption, which is essential for the generation of reactive species. The synergetic effect, in addition to dye sensitization, allowed for fast degradation of Rhodamine B (RhB), with 98% removal within 20 minutes. The crucial factors include increased light absorption, efficient charge separation based on GQDs and Vo, and the prevailing role of H+ and ·O2- radicals in the degradation process. 105

Similarly, lignocellulosic waste-based catalysts have been explored for pharmaceutical degradation. Removal of acetaminophen (ACE) from water by using manganese carbonate-modified lignocellulosic wastes such as banana pseudo stems (BPS) and potato peels (BPP). Lignin-based BPS facilitated 42% ACE degradation in 5 minutes through PMS activation and reactive oxygen species, whereas BPS-Mn completely removed it in 10 minutes because of improved adsorption. Low lignin BPP resulted in only adsorption with limited degradation. 106 In addition to these systems, multifunctional composite catalysts have shown promising performance in antibiotic removal. A green and effective CuAl-layered double hydroxide/cellulose (CuAl -LDH/CL) composite for the removal of doxycycline (DOX), a recalcitrant antibiotic, from water. The material is multifunctional and involves both adsorption and visible-light-induced photocatalysis, and the degradation efficiency reaches 96.2%, with a high adsorption capacity of 150.16 mg/g. It has a high spontaneity (ΔG = -24.27 kJ/mol) and a rapid degradation rate of k=0.0116 min-1. Some of the key features are a vast surface area, enhanced light absorption and active radical formation (OH•, O2•-, h+), resulting in 73.11% TOC removal. The material also provides a green solution for antibiotic-contaminated wastewater. 107

Carbon-based nanocomposites have attracted attention because of their superior adsorption capacity and catalytic activity. A graphene and iron oxide nanocomposite doped with nitrogen exhibited high activity as a photocatalyst for the removal of water contaminants through adsorption and chemical degradation. Iron oxide nanoparticles increase the light absorption, charge transfer and conductivity of graphene. The nanocomposite degraded 94.5% methylene blue in 60 minutes, far exceeding that of pure graphene (58%) and iron oxide (28%). This synergy leads to the rapid degradation of pollutants through enhanced reactive oxygen species generation, increased adsorption and increased charge separation, thus making it a prime candidate for sustainable wastewater treatment. 108 Similarly, biochar-based materials modified with carbon nanotubes have been applied for the removal of complex contaminants. Efficient removal of Ni(II) and crude oil from water by carbon nanotube-modified biochar (CNT3-CBC) prepared from cattle dung. This CNT3-CBC exhibited high adsorption capacities of 318.19 mg/g for crude oil and 32.87 mg/g for Ni(II) because of its functional groups and large surface area. It has chemisorption kinetics and is stable under competitive conditions. The material also exhibited good reusability over several cycles. These results indicate that CNT3-CBC is an efficient and sustainable adsorbent that can remove complex water contaminants via both chemical and physical adsorption. 109

In addition to adsorption-assisted degradation systems, piezocatalytic materials have been explored for water purification. Kaolin-modified polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) mats exhibit great promise for water purification; better adsorption of heavy metals such as Ag, Cr, Pb, and Zn; and rapid degradation of pollutants via piezocatalysis. Under ultrasonic vibration, they produced reactive species such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals with up to 90% Pb and full Cr removal at pH 4 within 60 minutes. The 0.5% KA/PVDF sample was kinetic with pseudo-second-order behavior and large rate constants. These mats also displayed excellent antibacterial activity, especially against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, indicating their potential for environmental purposes. 110 Metal-doped semiconductor photocatalysts have shown significant activity in water treatment. Zn-Co-codoped TiO2 nanoparticles (TDNPs) were encapsulated by both polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and green (Tinospora cordifolia) for water treatment. The TDNPs exhibited significant photocatalytic activity, where up to 99% degradation of contaminants such as methyl orange and methylene blue was attained upon exposure to UV light for 120 minutes. This activity is enhanced by the lower particle size, larger surface area, and improved crystallinity, enabling the effective generation of reactive oxygen species. Moreover, they display excellent antioxidant activity of up to 96%, which makes them potential candidates for environmental and biological applications. 111

Apart from photocatalytic systems, combined adsorption and electro-oxidation techniques were utilized for the removal of Eosin Yellow dye. COOH-functionalized multiwalled carbon nanotubes supported ultrasensitive detection of EY with a detection limit of 1.24 nm. Additionally, phytosynthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NP band density of 3.24 eV) from Cassis fistula resulted in 99% degradation of EY within 90 minutes at pH 5. The improved rate of degradation was due to the strong adsorption of EY on the positively charged surface of CeO2 and its fragmentation by photogenerated holes and hydroxyl radicals. 112 Clay-based catalytic systems have demonstrated considerable potential in ozonation processes for the degradation of contaminants such as atrazine, bisphenol A, diazinon and diclofenac sodium. Clays are effective adsorbents and catalysts that facilitate pollutant degradation. Fe(II)Mt clay significantly improved the degradation of bisphenol A and diclofenac, with complete removal occurring within 10–25 minutes. Diazinon degraded most rapidly, with ∼50% removal within less than a minute, in the absence of catalysts. These include the pollutant structure, composition of the clay, ozonation time, pH and intermediates, which influence the ecotoxicity against Lemna minor. In general, adsorption inhibits the spread of pollutants and facilitates their degradation. 113

4.2. Chemical degradation of soil pollutants

Chemical degradation in soils involves low mobility, strong pollutant sorption and limited oxidant diffusion. Common approaches include adsorptive amendments, redox catalysis, photocatalysis and mechanochemical oxidation. For instance, the use of composted straw, biochar, and polyacrylamide (PAM) can increase the quality of urban greenbelt loessial soil. Both pollutant adsorption and soil permeability were strongly improved by these amendments. Among the experimental groups, the mixture of biochar and straw (SSBJ) was most effective at sequestering heavy metals, with capture rates for zinc, copper, and cadmium exceeding 86%. In parallel, the biochar-dominated treatments (SSB) were the most successful at lowering nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and chemical oxygen demand (COD) levels. The leaching of pollutants stabilized within 48 hours, suggesting that adsorption was the dominant mechanism for lowering pollutant mobility. 114 Surface-active nanomaterials enhanced soil pollutant transformation. Effects of nickel nanoparticles (NPs) on Rhodococcus bacteria, a genus that is proficient at degrading recalcitrant compounds. Nickel NPs can adsorb soil pollutants. At sublethal concentrations (0.01–0.1 g/L), the surface area increased by 1.25 to 1.5 times with increasing roughness and adhesion, whereas the elasticity decreased, and the Young’s modulus decreased by 2‒8 times. Toxicity at relatively high levels of NPs has been demonstrated, but these changes seem to favor surface colonization and pollutant degradation, thereby paving the way for enhanced bioremediation. Instead of measuring degradation rates, the focus is on the factors linked to improved degradation. 115

Catalyst-assisted photocatalytic systems further improve the removal of persistent soil contaminants. MIL-88A(Fe) efficiently desorbed phenanthrene (PHE) from saline-alkaline soil via synergistic adsorption and photocatalysis. Its large surface area allows fast adsorption, concentrating the pollutants at active sites. Under visible light, the catalyst produces reactive species (⋅O2-, h+, ⋅OH, and 1O2) and achieves 86.2% PHE degradation in 30 min according to pseudo-first-order kinetics (k=0.048 min-1). This strategy not only facilitates the decomposition of pollutants but also enhances soil fertility by lowering the soil pH and increasing the soil nitrogen content. 116 Mechanochemical approaches have also been reported for soil remediation. Oxygen-enriched ball milling is a viable process for the remediation of petroleum-polluted soil, with a significant degradation of 93.84% of the total petroleum hydrocarbons. The treated soil obtained (BCS-O) exhibited efficient adsorption of Cu2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ because of increased carbonate and metal oxide contents. Moreover, BCS-O and its metal-loaded counterparts efficiently catalyze persulfate-induced aniline degradation through electron transfer and singlet oxygen with graphitized carbon- and oxygen-rich groups as active sites. This dual function presents a green remedy for both wastewater and soil treatment 119. Sensitive analytical techniques also emphasize the importance of adsorption in monitoring and facilitating chemical transformation processes in soils. An adsorptive stripping voltammetry (AdSY) method was developed for the determination of ionic titanium in soil leachate. The method successfully tracks low-level soil contamination with a detection limit of 1.2 ng/mL, recovery yields of 100–101% and accuracy of 1–2%. It also facilitates the chemical degradation of titanium dioxide under reducing conditions, which enhances its mobility and readsorption in soil. The selectivity of the method for ionicizing titanium prevents interference from TiO2 particles to ensure precise and quick analysis. 117

Redox-active cerium dioxide nanoparticles (CeO2-NPs) efficiently removed soil contaminants such as anthracene via a synergistic combination of adsorption and chemical degradation. The removal efficiency for anthracene increased with increasing nanoparticle concentration, with an effectiveness of approximately 63% at a nanoparticle concentration of 15 mg/L. The nanoparticles chemically degrade complex pollutants through the production of reactive oxygen species. Significantly, alkaline conditions in the soil increase the degradation of anthracene to approximately 80%. This dual action highlights the potential of CeO2-NPs for use in soil remediation. 118 Sustainable adsorbent materials have also been designed to address oil contamination in soils. A superhydrophobic sustainable lignin-based polyurethane foam (LPUF) for the effective adsorption of oil pollutants. The foam showed large adsorption capacities of up to 20 g/g for dichloromethane and 8.1 g/g for crude oil, along with more than 97% oil‒water separation efficiency. Photothermal functionality improved the absorption of viscous oils. Importantly, the foam is completely degradable in an alkaline methanol-NaOH solution at 60°C within 5 hours, which provides an environmentally friendly disposal method after use (containing pollutants). 119

4.3. Chemical degradation of air pollutants

Air-phase chemical degradation relies primarily on photocatalysis, plasma oxidation, heterogeneous catalysis, and redox-active materials, typically requiring preadsorption to overcome low gas concentrations. Nitrogen-doped TiO2 (N-TiO2) nanoparticles were synthesized via the sol‒gel method and subsequently treated for the degradation of pollutants, including air pollutants. The processes involved in removal include the photocatalytic degradation of adsorbed pollutants driven by visible light. Direct photolysis and surface adsorption. A kinetic model was created to estimate the corresponding individual degradation rate constants (k1 for the adsorption rate constant, k2 for the photocatalytic degradation rate constant, and k3 for photolysis). Annealing conditions strongly influence the crystallinity, particle size, and nitrogen content of N-TiO2, which can further influence the uptake and removal efficiency and photocatalytic degradation efficiency. 120 A dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma system with sio2 glass beads was used to investigate the adsorption and catalytic degradation of sulfuryl fluoride (SO2F2), which is an environmentally hazardous gas. SiF4 and SO2 are favorable breakdown products, and decomposition on the surface of SiO2 is more efficient. Simulations estimated that SO2F2 fracturing at reactive Si interfaces liberates heat and gives rise to enduring Si–F and Si–O bonds. The excellent catalytic performance results in a destruction and removal efficiency (DRE) of 91.48% and an energy yield (EY) of 10.71 g/kwh. 121

A Pd/TiO2 nanotube photocatalyst created through gamma irradiation effectively removes NO gas under visible light through both adsorption and chemical degradation. The sample treated with 14 kGy achieved an impressive 53.3% degradation rate, which was 1.6 times better than that of untreated TiO2, and demonstrated a strong preference for producing green byproducts while maintaining NO2 formation at a minimum. Its remarkable performance can be attributed to its reduced band gap of 2.47 eV, enhanced charge separation, robust photocurrent and high quantum efficiency of 10.8 × 10-3%. This makes it a promising option for sustainable air pollution control. 122 Air purification through photocatalytic cement mortar loaded with nano-TiO2 recycled red brick (RRB) and waste glass (Wgs). Wgs allow light to penetrate and enhance the photocatalytic activity, whereas rrbs adsorb gases and maintain pollutants because of their porosity. An optimum mixture (RRB:WG = 75:25 with 3 wt.% TiO2) resulted in 9% NOx degradation. However, excess RRB compromised the compressive strength because of increased porosity. An environmentally friendly way of recycling waste while simultaneously improving air quality through construction materials. 123

Fe-doped TiO2 on ACF, with an evenly distributed electric field meant for the purpose of degrading air pollutants, with cyclohexane being the issue. The synergy of adsorption and photocatalysis can overcome the rate mismatch; moreover, iron doping enables TiO2 to be active under visible light and ensures charge separation, and the electric field aids this by further enhancing electron‒hole pair separation; thus, ca. 90% degradation is achieved under optimum conditions. Kinetic modeling also facilitates catalyst maintenance, while in situ regeneration supports continuous VOC removal, a breakthrough in pollutant degradation technology. 124 A boron‒carbon nitride loaded with platinum (Pt/BCN) featuring atomically dispersed Pt/N4 sites. This innovative catalyst effectively breaks down benzene in air at room temperature. It achieves a remarkable 100% removal of benzene at 30 °C, with a concentration of 450 mg m-3 and a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 50,000 mL (g h)-1. This performance is significantly greater than that of pristine BCN, which only achieved a mere 15.77% efficiency. The superior performance of these catalysts is due to their ability to adsorb benzene and produce reactive hydroxyl radicals, which facilitate the rapid oxidation of benzene into carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O). Additionally, the Pt/BCN catalyst shows impressive stability and can be reused effectively for several cycles. 125

Surface defect-engineered BiOX semiconductors improve the removal of air pollutants through chemical degradation and adsorption. When exposed to ultraviolet light, surface imperfections increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), increase the absorption of toluene, and activate O2 and H2O. Using a quantum yield of 1.04×10-3 molecules/photon, BiOCl-R degraded toluene by 98.6%. ROS promote breakdown by helping intermediates such as benzoic acid open their rings. According to these results, defect engineering is essential for enhancing adsorption and reducing energy barriers for effective pollutant degradation. 126 Pd/uio-66(Ce) catalysts, which are made via a variety of chemical reduction techniques, can be used to degrade airborne volatile organic compounds (VOCs), such as benzene, toluene, and o-xylene. With 90% o-xylene degradation at 192°C, the nabh4-reduced variant performed the best. Although mixed vocs caused competitive adsorption, which decreased the efficiency (e.g., o-xylene decreased to −0.60 eV with benzene), the adsorption energy analysis revealed strong VOC‒catalyst interactions, particularly for o-xylene (−1.20 eV). A stepwise oxidation mechanism that produces intermediates such as acids and aldehydes was validated by DRIFTS and GC‒MS analyses. 127

A dual-anionic imidazolium, specifically DKP-4Br, was strongly effective at capturing and breaking down air pollutants. The nucleophilic Br- and CH3COO- ions DKP-4Br can transform CO2 into cyclic carbonates, achieving an impressive degradation rate of 91.2% in just 14 hours under mild conditions (70°C). The ability of the polymers to adsorb iodine vapor, reaching a capacity of 3.9 g/g, is due primarily to a combination of physical adsorption and some chemical interactions. These results highlight the potential for creating stable, cost-effective bifunctional materials that could play a significant role in the cleaning of air. 128 Similarly, a bifunctional ST/SiO2-Mn hydrogel composite intended for air purification focused on the carcinogenic pollutant benzo(a)pyrene (B(a)P). When exposed to UV light, the material exhibited efficient PM2.5-B(a)P adsorption and rapid photocatalytic degradation, with 88.5% elimination in 240 minutes and complete breakdown of 50 µg/LB(a)P in 20 minutes. This approach provides an economical way to lower airborne PAHs in contaminated areas because of its porous structure and Mn-doped TiO2, which also improve adsorption, radical generation (particularly •O2- and h+), and degradation efficiency (k = 0.170 min-1). 129

The compiled studies, as shown in Table 3, demonstrate the diverse applications of chemical degradation methods—including photocatalysis, advanced oxidation, catalysis, and adsorption—in treating pollutants across aqueous, soil, and air environments. Overall, the data reveal that chemical degradation processes are highly effective for rapid pollutant removal under controlled laboratory conditions, but performance varies significantly depending on the environmental medium, reaction conditions, and pollutant type. Chemical processes involving photooxidation demonstrate superior performance and rapid kinetics in water treatment. Specifically, diclofenac and 2-furoic acid were found to be almost entirely eliminated (>99%) in two hours under solar or visible light through singlet oxygen pathways, 103 highlighting the effectiveness of advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) in pharmaceutical wastewater. Similarly, the removal of eosin yellow by the Phyto synthesized CeO2 nanoparticles was 98.3% in just 90 minutes under sunlight, confirming the catalytic potential of the green-synthesized nanomaterials.

In gaseous systems, chemical and photocatalytic oxidation processes exhibit diverse performances depending on the pollutant type and catalyst design. For instance, Fe-doped TiO2 on activated carbon fibers achieved ∼90% removal of cyclohexane within 20 minutes, particularly when enhanced by a uniform electric field, which improved the charge transfer and oxidation kinetics. Moreover, Pt-loaded boron carbon nitride (Pt/BCN) catalyzed complete benzene oxidation (100%) for more than 45 hours, indicating high activity but extended reaction times because of the stable aromatic structure of benzene.

Pd-nanoparticle-loaded TiO2 nanotubes (Pd-TNTs) achieved ∼53% NO removal under visible/solar light, which, although moderate, reflects the difficulty of gaseous-phase photocatalytic reactions under ambient conditions. Moreover, a dual-anionic imidazolium polymer (DKP-4Br) achieved 91.2% CO2 conversion under mild conditions, revealing emerging CO2 utilization and reduction pathways as a chemical degradation strategy for greenhouse gases.

Chemical treatments in soil were less rapid but still resulted in substantial degradation under optimized conditions. For instance, the MIL-88A(Fe) metal–organic framework (MOF) exhibited 86.2% phenanthrene removal within 30 minutes under visible light, demonstrating the potential of MOF-based photocatalysts in solid-phase degradation. Ball milling under oxygen-rich conditions also led to 93.84% removal of total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPHs)129, illustrating the mechanical–chemical synergy that enhances oxidation and surface activation.

However, studies by Zhou et al. (2024) on loessial soil stabilization revealed that while composted straw and biochar improved nutrient immobilization (e.g., N, P, and heavy metals), complete degradation was not achieved within 72 hours, indicating a transformation or stabilization process rather than full mineralization. Similarly, the use of CeO2 nanoparticles in anthracene-contaminated soil resulted in 60–80% degradation in 48 hours, indicating efficiency but limited penetration and long-term stability challenges in heterogeneous matrices.

When compared across environmental media, aqueous systems show the highest and most consistent degradation efficiencies (90–100%), primarily because of better mixing, mass transfer, and light penetration. Soil systems demonstrate moderate performance (60–93%) and are often limited by pollutant diffusion, soil particle heterogeneity, and catalyst dispersion. Air-phase degradation is highly variable (53–100%) and strongly influenced by the volatility of pollutants and the design of the catalytic system.

While these studies collectively demonstrate the high potential of chemical degradation for pollutant removal, several limitations remain. Most experiments were performed under controlled laboratory conditions and lacked evaluation under real environmental matrices. Reports of byproduct formation, toxicity assessment, and catalyst reusability, which are essential for real-world applications, are limited. Moreover, many processes rely on energy-intensive light sources (e.g., xenon lamps and ball milling), raising concerns about scalability and sustainability. Future work should emphasize low-cost, visible-light-active, and reusable materials, along with pilot-scale validation under environmental conditions.

5. Electrochemical degradation

During electrochemical degradation, electrically driven redox reactions produce reactive oxidizing species (such as •OH and SO4•-), which can mineralize contaminants into simpler and less dangerous compounds. Unlike pure adsorption, electrochemical methods chemically breakdown pollutants rather than transferring them between phases. Adsorption, which involves the concentration of contaminants at electrode or catalyst surfaces and increases electron transfer and reactive species production, frequently occurs before this stage. 122 This combination of adsorption–electrochemical techniques has been effectively used for persistent pollutants in soil and water, while new electrochemical air-treatment platforms that employ conductive catalysts and plasma-assisted oxidation are showing increasing promise.

5.1. Electrochemical degradation of water pollutants

Electrochemical advanced oxidation processes (EAOPs) have emerged as effective methods for treating persistent pesticide, dye and pharmaceutical contaminants. Adsorption-assisted oxidation is a common mechanism in which conductive or catalytic materials preconcentrate pollutants and then promote radical or nonradical pathways (e.g., •OH, SO4•-, H2O2, 1O2 or direct electron transfer). The role of support derived from carbon and biochar in facilitating such dual functionality is highlighted by a predominant cluster of recent studies. Large pore volume and surface functionalities are provided by biochar made from a variety of wastes, including sawdust, tea waste, bamboo biomass, and aquatic reeds, for the initial adsorption of pharmaceuticals, which is followed by electro-Fenton or electro-oxidation mineralization. For instance, naproxen (14.26 mg g-1) was first adsorbed by FeCuNPs@TWM composites supported on tea waste, and within 15 minutes, all emerging contaminants were completely removed via an electro-Fenton mechanism while recyclability was retained for several cycles. 131

Similarly, biochar derived from bamboo was used as an electrode to capture medications (acetaminophen, sulindac and carbamazepine) and enable >99% electrochemical degradation under pulsed conditions with high mineralization efficiency. 132 Furthermore, waste-derived carbon cathodes from Spergularia rubra and Phragmites australis enhanced H2O2 electrogeneration, increasing •OH formation and facilitating rapid amoxicillin degradation within 30 minutes at low energy consumption (0.1096 kWh m-3 order -1) and low effluent toxicity, underscoring their suitability for green water remediation. Taken as a whole, these systems highlight how adsorption‒oxidation coupling and resource upcycling increase mass transfer, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability. DFT confirmed that {310}-dominated MnO2 achieved near-complete p-chloroaniline degradation in ∼3 min across broad pH conditions through a nonradical electron-transfer route, acting as an electron bridge between PMS and the pollutant. These findings highlight the significance of conductive interfaces, vacancy/electron-bridge mechanisms, and crystal facet exposure in determining the efficiency of the EAOP. To support circular remediation strategies, electrochemical degradation has also been extended to natural silicates and spent adsorbents. The high methylene blue adsorption capacity of natural bentonite (237 mg g -1) was thermally converted into a conductive carbon-silicate electrode that can produce HO2 radicals in alkaline media, resulting in approximately 70% dye degradation and offering a way to recycle saturated adsorbents rather than discard them. 133 Other hybrid materials, such as silica-coated Fe-based magnetic nanoparticles, combined the electrochemical breakdown of paraquat with adsorption, resulting in low detection limits (0.22 µM) and heterogeneous multilayer binding (Freundlich, Dubinin–Radushkevich behavior), allowing for on-site sensing and remediation on the same platform. 134

Similarly, a green electrochemical method was created to degrade the water pollutant amoxicillin (AMX) using waste-derived carbon cathodes and IrO2- Ta2O5|Ti anodes. The cathodes produced by Phragmites australis and Spergularia rubra increased the production of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which made it easier for hydroxyl radicals (•OH) to form and contribute to the breakdown of pollutants. With optimal aeration and 2.54 mA/cm2, the combined effect of carbon adsorption and radical action resulted in effective AMX removal in 30 minutes with minimal energy consumption (0.1096 kWh/m3/order). The technique demonstrated low toxicity and high mineralization, indicating its potential for long-term water purification. 135 Magnetic nanoparticle systems further demonstrate the potential for integrated sensing and remediation. The toxic pesticide paraquat (PQ) can be effectively adsorbed and electrochemically degraded by silica-coated FeO4 magnetic nanoparticles (SiMNPs). Freundlich and Dubinin–Radushkevich isotherms were followed by adsorption, suggesting ion-exchange and multilayer, heterogeneous binding mechanisms (E = 13.13 kJ/mol, 1/n = 0.92). With a low detection limit of 0.22 µM, differential pulse voltammetry allowed for sensitive and wide-ranging PQ detection (0.4–876 µM). This system is appropriate for quick, portable water pollution monitoring because of its high degradation and recovery efficiency (83.79–103.09%). 136

5.2. Electrochemical degradation of soil pollutants

Electrochemical degradation has recently been explored as an effective strategy for the remediation of soil contaminants, particularly persistent antibiotics and organic pollutants that strongly bind to soil matrices. For instance, a cobalt‒titanium oxide catalyst (10Co2TiO4@SAC) supported on supercapacitor-activated carbon was used to degrade soil contaminants such as norfloxacin. High adsorption (∼50%) concentrated pollutants on the catalyst surface, whereas oxygen vacancies and strong electrochemical interactions drove enhanced peroxymonosulfate activation (Table 4). Because of this, reactive oxygen species (SO4•-, •OH, O2•-, and 1O2) formed quickly, and within 10 min, 96.55% degradation was achieved, with a high rate constant (0.347 min-1). Its effectiveness, stability, and practicality are highlighted by the minimal metal leaching and low toxicity of its byproducts. 131

These studies illustrate the growing effectiveness and versatility of electrochemical oxidation, reduction, and photo electrocatalytic processes in degrading pharmaceutical and organic contaminants across various aqueous environments. These methods rely on either direct electron transfer at electrodes or the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl and sulfate radicals, resulting in high degradation rates, short reaction times, and strong control over reaction conditions. However, performance varies with electrode composition, electrolyte type, and operating parameters such as pH, current density, and applied potential.

Nearly all the studies achieved high pollutant removal efficiencies (>90%), underscoring the potential of electrochemical systems for advanced water treatment. The mixed metal oxide (Ti/RuO2) anode coupled with a carbon-PTFE air diffusion cathode achieved 100% degradation of acetylsalicylic acid, ibuprofen, and naproxen within 15 minutes 142]. This efficiency is attributed to the formation of reactive oxygen species and oxygen reduction reactions at the cathode, which were enhanced by the use of CuFe nanoparticles as a catalytic mediator.

The electrode composition plays a decisive role in pollutant degradation. Biochar-based electrodes 134 achieved >99% removal of acetaminophen, sulindac, and carbamazepine within minutes, demonstrating that carbon-based materials with high surface areas and functional groups promote rapid redox cycling and electron transfer.

All reviewed studies were conducted in aqueous or wastewater media, where the ionic conductivity supports effective electrochemical processes. Pharmaceuticals (e.g., ibuprofen, norfloxacin, acetaminophen, amoxicillin) and dyes (e.g., methylene blue) were degraded efficiently, demonstrating the broad applicability of electrochemical treatment. Wastewater matrices were slightly less efficient than ultrapure water systems because of interference from natural organic matter and ions, but most studies still achieved near-complete degradation within short timescales.

The reviewed data affirm that electrochemical degradation is a rapid, controllable, and highly efficient approach for removing persistent organic pollutants. However, notable challenges remain. Most studies are laboratory-scale and do not assess energy consumption, electrode fouling, or long-term stability. The role of adsorption versus true mineralization is often unclear, as seen in the CdS–Co@C system. Furthermore, toxic intermediate formation and incomplete mineralization are rarely reported, limiting environmental risk assessment. Some systems also rely on precious metal electrodes (RuO2, Pt, BDD), which may increase costs and limit scalability. Future work should emphasize green electrode materials, energy-efficient operation, and mechanistic studies to ensure both performance and sustainability in real wastewater applications.

6. Comparative analysis of various degradation methods across environmental media

6.3. Photocatalytic systems

Adsorption-assisted photocatalytic degradation remains a highly effective approach for treating diverse pollutants in water, soil, and air. Under visible or solar light, TiO2-based and carbonaceous nanocomposites (e.g., Ag@ZnO and Bi2WO6/TiO2/BC) achieve >90% degradation of dyes and pharmaceuticals. Enhanced systems employing LDHs, chitosan–TiO2 blends, and MXene-based heterostructures benefit from high surface area, efficient charge separation, and dual adsorption–oxidation capabilities. In soil remediation, heterojunction photocatalysts and enzyme-assisted hybrids (e.g., C3N5/Lac and BiWO6/Bi2S3/g-C3N4) effectively degrade antibiotics, agrochemicals, and metal ions. For air purification, Ag–TiO2 and MOF–TiO2 composites efficiently remove NOx, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and bioaerosols through photocatalytic oxidation. However, challenges persist—namely, poor catalyst stability, light-dependent performance, and a lack of field validation for large-scale applications. Soil systems, in particular, suffer from light attenuation and limited pollutant diffusion. Future research should therefore emphasize Z-scheme heterojunctions, bandgap-engineered composites, and biopolymer-supported catalysts that improve durability and scalability while minimizing secondary pollution.

6.4. Biodegradation systems

Adsorption-enhanced biodegradation offers a biologically sustainable alternative for removing persistent organic pollutants (POPs), heavy metals, antibiotics, and dyes. When integrated with adsorptive supports such as biochar, MOFs, and cryogels, microbial consortia and immobilized enzymes exhibit improved pollutant accessibility, stability, and resistance to toxicity. Systems employing Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas strains demonstrate high removal efficiency in aqueous media, whereas biofilm-based and enzymatic bioreactors maintain long-term functionality.

In soil environments, biodegradation efficiency is constrained by low bioavailability, low pH variability, and nutrient limitations. In air treatment, biodegradable nanofiber filters present a promising avenue for capturing and degrading particulate and gaseous contaminants. Although slower than photocatalysis, biodegradation is selective, self-sustaining, and low-energy, and when coupled with oxidative or adsorption processes, it achieves hybrid system stability with minimal secondary pollution.

6.5. Chemical degradation systems

Adsorption-assisted chemical oxidation (e.g., Fenton, photoFenton, or persulfate activation) remains the most mature and widely applied remediation approach. Functionalized catalysts such as BiOCl–Vo, codoped TiO2, MIL-88A(Fe), and lignin-based composites integrate adsorption and chemical oxidation, improving pollutant capture and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Adsorptive materials such as graphene, lignin, and biochar not only preconcentrate pollutants but also modulate redox conditions, pH, and radical availability to increase degradation kinetics.

In soil systems, such materials efficiently degrade hydrocarbons and antibiotics, whereas in aqueous media, they demonstrate excellent adaptability across diverse contaminant classes. The major advantages of these methods are rapid degradation, low residual toxicity, and reduced secondary waste compared with conventional oxidation techniques.

6.6. Electrochemical systems

Adsorption–electrochemical degradation has emerged as a promising route for pollutant removal, particularly in water treatment. Hybrid electrodes such as CdS/Co@C, facet-engineered MnO2, and FeCu nanoparticle–biochar composites demonstrate high electron transfer efficiency and enhanced degradation of pharmaceuticals and emerging contaminants. Bamboo biochar electrodes and tea waste-supported FeCu nanocatalysts illustrate the potential of sustainable carbon-based supports in electro-Fenton systems. In soil, limited progress has been made—only a few studies (e.g., Co2TiO4@SAC with PMS activation) report high removal efficiencies (∼96.55% for norfloxacin). To date, no significant studies have investigated adsorption–electrochemical systems for air purification, indicating a major research void. Future directions should involve cross-media system design, electrode material optimization, and energy-efficient operation for large-scale deployment.

6.7. Comparative understanding

Recently, significant research has been directed toward the development of advanced remediation technologies for different environmental media. Among these, water remediation has been more extensively investigated than soil and air remediation systems because aqueous environments provide relatively controlled reaction conditions that facilitate catalyst dispersion, mass transfer, and light penetration during photocatalytic processes. Additionally, pollutants in water are generally more accessible for adsorption and degradation. In contrast, soil systems present heterogeneous matrices with limited pollutant mobility and challenges related to catalyst recovery, whereas air remediation often requires specialized reactor configurations and highly stable catalysts to operate effectively under variable environmental conditions. Among all methods, electrochemical degradation combined with adsorption has the highest overall efficiency for water purification because of enhanced electron transfer and pollutant preconcentration at electrode surfaces. In contrast, chemical oxidation supported by adsorptive materials shows superior performance in soil systems, effectively targeting hydrocarbons and antibiotics under complex environmental conditions. For air remediation, adsorption-coupled photocatalysis remains dominant owing to its strong oxidative power under visible light, enabling rapid VOC and NOx removal at ambient temperatures.

Despite these advances, soil and air applications remain underdeveloped, and adsorption-enhanced thermal degradation has been reported primarily in aqueous systems. Addressing these gaps requires a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of adsorption–reaction coupling, standardized efficiency metrics, and scalable reactor designs capable of operating under real environmental conditions. Such developments will be essential for translating laboratory successes into integrated, field-ready remediation technologies that ensure sustainable pollution control across all environmental media. Since the reported studies were conducted under different experimental conditions (e.g., different pollutant concentrations, catalyst types, and reaction environments), the comparisons presented in this review are intended to highlight general performance trends rather than direct quantitative equivalence.

7. Mechanistic summary: Role of adsorption in enhancing degradation

Adsorption plays a crucial role in improving pollutant degradation efficiency across all environmental remediation technologies by increasing local contaminant concentrations at reactive sites, facilitating charge or electron transfer, and reducing diffusion limitations. The synergistic coupling of adsorption with catalytic, biological, and electrochemical processes results in faster reaction kinetics, improved selectivity, and reduced secondary pollution. 7.2. Adsorption–biodegradation mechanism

Adsorption–biodegradation systems operate through synergistic interactions between pollutant enrichment and microbial degradation pathways. Initially, organic contaminants are adsorbed onto materials such as activated carbon, biochar, cryogels, or other porous matrices, which serve as supports for microbial growth. This adsorption step increases the local concentration of pollutants at the biofilm interface, thereby increasing substrate availability and facilitating microbial uptake. 142

In addition, the adsorbent materials function as immobilization platforms for microorganisms and enzymes, providing a stable microenvironment that protects microbial cells from toxic effects and environmental fluctuations. This immobilization enhances microbial stability, preserves enzymatic activity, and improves overall degradation efficiency. 143

Following adsorption, the pollutants undergo intracellular enzymatic degradation. Complex organic molecules are initially transformed into intermediate compounds via enzyme-mediated reactions, including oxidation and reduction processes, and are subsequently converted into simpler metabolites. 144 Depending on environmental conditions, biodegradation occurs via either aerobic or anaerobic pathways. Under aerobic conditions, oxygen acts as the terminal electron acceptor, leading to the formation of CO2, H2O, biomass, and energy (ATP). In contrast, under anaerobic conditions, alternative electron acceptors such as nitrate (NO3-) and sulfate (SO42-) are utilized, resulting in the production of CH4, H2S, and CO2. 145

Overall, the integration of adsorption with biodegradation significantly enhances pollutant removal efficiency by improving pollutant–microbe contact, reducing diffusion limitations, and enabling continuous degradation. This synergistic approach leads to faster reaction kinetics, enhanced system stability, and more effective mineralization than standalone biodegradation processes. 7.3. Adsorption–chemical degradation mechanism

Adsorption–chemical degradation systems operate through synergistic interactions between pollutant enrichment and the in situ generation of highly reactive species. In advanced oxidation and reduction processes (AOPs), such as Fenton, persulfate activation, and ozonation, adsorption plays a crucial role by concentrating pollutants and oxidants at the reactive interface. Porous adsorbents, including graphene-based materials, biochar, lignin-derived carbons, and silica composites, act as both pollutant concentrators and catalytic platforms for radical generation. 146

Initially, organic contaminants are adsorbed onto the surface of the functionalized adsorbent, resulting in the localized enrichment of pollutants. This preconcentration step minimizes mass transfer limitations and ensures proximity between contaminants and reactive species. Overall, the integration of adsorption with chemical oxidation significantly enhances degradation performance by improving pollutant–oxidant contact, increasing radical utilization efficiency, and enabling rapid and complete mineralization compared with standalone chemical processes.

8. Challenges and future directions

Recently, several emerging materials have shown significant potential for integrated adsorption–degradation systems. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and their derivatives are promising because of their high surface area and catalytic metal centers, which facilitate Fenton-like and photocatalytic reactions. MXenes and other two-dimensional heterostructures exhibit excellent electrical conductivity and hydrophilicity, enhancing adsorption-assisted electrochemical and photocatalytic processes. In addition, engineered biochar-based nanocomposites have attracted attention as sustainable and low-cost materials that can function as both pollutant adsorbents and catalytic supports for efficient degradation. Despite the promising performance of integrated adsorption‒degradation systems reported in laboratory studies, significant challenges remain that limit their practical implementation. While real environmental matrices contain complex mixtures of salts, organic salts and multiple pollutants that may significantly affect treatment efficiency, most studies have been carried out under controlled laboratory conditions using synthetic solutions such as pure water or artificially contaminated soils, organic matter and multiple pollutants that may significantly influence treatment efficiency. Therefore, future studies should prioritize validation under real-field conditions to better evaluate the performance and reliability of these systems. Another important research gap exists in the application of electrochemical degradation technologies for air purification. While electrochemical methods have shown great efficacy in treating water, their application in the elimination of gaseous contaminants remains underexplored. Although many integrated studies demonstrate promising pollutant removal efficiencies and potential scalability, detailed economic feasibility analyses remain limited in the current literature. Furthermore, the long-term stability and durability of advanced materials remain critical challenges. Consequently, future research should focus on improving catalyst stability, recyclability, and long-term operational performance over extended treatment cycles. Addressing these challenges will be essential for translating laboratory-scale advances into sustainable and scalable environmental remediation technologies.

9. Conclusion

Recent advances in environmental remediation clearly demonstrate the potential of integrated adsorption–degradation strategies to achieve efficient and sustainable removal of pollutants across air, water, and soil systems. The synergistic combination of adsorption with photocatalytic, biodegradation, chemical, and electrochemical techniques enhances contaminant capture, accelerates degradation kinetics, and minimizes secondary pollution. Multifunctional catalysts—such as doped metal oxides, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), layered double hydroxides (LDHs), and biochar-based composites—have shown remarkable improvements in activity, selectivity, and reusability. Despite these encouraging advancements, a number of challenges still need to be overcome. Compared with research on water treatment systems, research on air and soil remediation remains relatively limited because of matrix complexity, lower pollutant mobility and catalyst stability challenges under variable environmental conditions. Moreover, the scalability and long-term performance of many advanced materials have yet to be validated beyond laboratory settings. In addition, compared with conventional remediation approaches, integrated adsorption–degradation systems offer improved environmental sustainability by reducing chemical consumption and secondary pollution, while catalyst reusability and enhanced treatment efficiency may provide long-term economic advantages. These integrated systems can also be incorporated into existing wastewater treatment infrastructures, where adsorption units and catalytic reactors may be integrated into conventional treatment processes with minimal modification. Future work should prioritize the design of robust, low-cost, and environmentally benign materials capable of operating effectively under real-field conditions. An emphasis on multifunctional hybrid systems, medium-specific optimization, and the integration of adsorption–reaction kinetics will be key to developing next-generation remediation technologies. Addressing these limitations will require interdisciplinary approaches combining advanced material design, reactor engineering and realistic environmental testing. Ultimately, advancing these integrated approaches offers a viable path toward cleaner, safer, and more resilient environmental systems.152–155
