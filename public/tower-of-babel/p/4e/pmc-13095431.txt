Mechanistic insights into human carboxylesterase 2 (CES2) inhibition by the CES1 prodrug substrate remdesivir
Authors: Abhinav Pentyala, Sylvie E. Kandel, Jed N. Lampe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
COVID-19 remains a significant health threat, particularly to people over the age of 65 with existing comorbidities like diabetes, hypertension, cancer, and viral infections. Despite expedited drug approvals, drug-drug interaction profiles for COVID-19 antiviral drugs have not yet been completely defined. The antiviral drugs remdesivir and molnupiravir are ester prodrugs with carboxylesterases (CES) playing a critical role in their bioactivation. In this study, we investigated the effect of COVID-19 antiviral drugs on CES hydrolysis activity. Of the 3 drugs tested, remdesivir inhibited 50% of CES2 activity in a nanomolar concentration range. Furthermore, time-dependent inhibition of CES2 activity by remdesivir was identified with an IC50 shift of nearly 3-fold from 0.188 μM after 5-minute preincubation to 0.068 μM following 40-minute preincubation. Remdesivir inactivation of CES2 was characterized by a kinact/KI value of 1.6 × 103 M−1.s−1. Through equilibrium dialysis and substrate protection experiments, we were able to further substantiate previous findings of the irreversible CES2 inhibition by remdesivir. Finally, in silico docking analysis of remdesivir to CES2 supported the proposed mechanism for covalent modification of the CES2 active site involving the catalytic triad serine via the phosphate group of remdesivir.Significance StatementThis study provides insights into human carboxylesterase inhibition by COVID-19 drugs and these findings demonstrate the underlying inhibition mechanism by remdesivir of recombinant human carboxylesterase 2.

Introduction

The ultimate cause of the COVID-19 pandemic, the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), remains a significant source for respiratory infections, particularly among the immunocompromised or those with pre-existing comorbidities. The initial urgency in combating COVID-19 mortality led to accelerated antiviral drug approvals by the United States Food and Drug Administration without complete characterization of their potential drug-drug interaction (DDI) profiles.1 DDIs are one of the key and predictable drivers of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) resulting in increased treatment costs and hospitalizations,2,3 which greatly impact the elderly population because of their vulnerability to polypharmacy.4, 5, 6 Several studies concerning ADRs reported that 40%–60% of these may be preventable.7, 8, 9 Furthermore, nearly half of the DDIs observed in the clinic are pharmacokinetic (PK) related, from which majority would be metabolism-mediated.10, 11, 12

Recently approved COVID-19 antiviral drugs include nirmatrelvir,13 a protease inhibitor, remdesivir,14 a protide converted to a viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) inhibitor,15,16 and molnupiravir,17 an emergency use authorization drug which acts on RdRp by overwhelming the viral genome with copy errors.18 Whereas nirmatrelvir is metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4, the metabolic activation of the ester prodrugs remdesivir and molnupiravir are primarily mediated by carboxylesterases (CES), which contribute both to their efficacy and PK profiles.19, 20, 21 CES enzymes are a member of the serine hydrolases that catalyze ester bond cleavage via a conserved serine residue in their active site. The primary human CES isoforms that have been investigated so far belong to the CES1A and CES2A families expressed in the epithelia of the liver, gut, kidney, lung, adipose tissues, and leukocytes.22 CES1 is primarily expressed in the human liver, in contrast CES2, also expressed in the liver, is most abundantly found in the small intestine, with both sharing 49% sequence identity and displaying different substrate specificities.23, 24, 25 The CES enzymes are known for their metabolic capabilities with a wide range of drugs and environmental toxicants, and are also important for lipid and cholesterol metabolism.26, 27, 28, 29 In addition, they are involved in the metabolism of over 50% of all marketed prodrugs.30,31 The CES1 enzyme catalyzes the hydrolysis of the prodrug remdesivir which constitutes 80% of all known metabolic processes yet identified for remdesivir, while the CES2 enzyme metabolizes the prodrug molnupiravir to its active form.

Interestingly, inhibition of CES2 by remdesivir was previously reported in a short communication that suggested the covalent modification of CES2 based on data measuring remaining CES activity in a native gel electrophoresis.32 Covalent enzyme inhibitors typically exhibit characteristic time-dependent inhibition (TDI). The so called “TDI effect” is due to slow kinetic behavior between the inhibitor and the enzyme, which occurs when an inhibitor interacts covalently or in a slow/tight binding mode, thereby inactivating the enzyme. Slow or absent recovery can be observed due to TDI from irreversible covalent inhibition, as enzyme activity restoration depends on the rate at which the tissue can synthesize new enzyme.33 An example is the irreversible inhibition of CES2 by sofosbuvir and its effect on the substrate tenofovir hydrolysis in lysates from CES2 transfected cells.34 Although TDI is not often investigated in the case of non-CYP enzymes, covalent inhibition of serine hydrolases is recognized for its significant risk for serious adverse events (AEs).35 Despite incomplete clinical trial information for remdesivir, several investigations have identified hepatic adverse effects associated with its dosing, including increased alanine transaminase and aspartate transaminase levels.36, 37, 38 In addition to their ability to metabolize drugs, CES enzymes are also involved in lipid homeostasis with their dysregulation contributing to the development of steatosis,39, 40, 41 yet the potential for remdesivir interactions with CES endogenous functions remain unknown.

We undertook this study to better understand how the CES enzymes interact with newly emerging antivirals and how these drugs might inhibit these important enzymes. Determination of the inactivation kinetic constants for CES inhibition may help in both structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies and in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation to assess the requirement for further clinical DDI investigation. Here, we investigated the potential for covalent inhibition of CES by remdesivir and other COVID-19 antiviral drugs (Fig. 1), characterized the nature and potency of CES2 inhibition by remdesivir, including the determination of its bimolecular inactivation kinetic constants. Materials and methods

Recombinant enzymes and chemicals

Recombinant human CES1 (catalog #CYP152) and 2 (catalog #CYP153) enzymes expressed in Escherichia coli membranes were purchased from BioIVT. Remdesivir (CAS: 1809249-37-3), fenofibrate (CAS: 49562-28-9) and simvastatin (CAS: 79902-63-9) were obtained from Cayman Chemical. Nirmatrelvir (CAS: 2628280-40-8) and molnupiravir (CAS: 2492423-19-5) were purchased from MedChemExpress. 4-Nitrophenyl acetate (4-NPA; CAS: 830-03-5), 4-nitrophenol (4-NP; CAS: 100-02-7), fluorescein diacetate (FD; CAS: 596-09-8) and fluorescein sodium salt (CAS: 518-47-8) were all obtained from MilliporeSigma. All other chemicals were of analytical grade and were obtained from standard suppliers.

Recombinant human CES1 and CES2 in vitro inhibition assays

The human CES in vitro inhibition experiments included a preincubation step with each COVID-19 drug tested, followed by an incubation step with a specific CES probe substrate. The probe substrate reactions were carried out under initial velocity conditions where the enzyme activity is linear with time and CES enzyme concentrations. Inhibition of CES enzymes was assessed using 10- and 100-μM concentrations of the COVID-19 drugs.

The human CES1 in vitro inhibition assays were conducted in triplicate in 96-well clear flat bottom microtiter plates (Greiner Bio-One; catalog #655101) in a 160 μL volume. The reactions contained 50 μg/mL CES1 in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Stock solutions of remdesivir, molnupiravir, and nirmatrelvir were prepared in DMSO and were aliquoted to the reaction mixtures to yield final concentrations of 10 μM and 100 μM. DMSO concentration was kept below 2% of the final reaction volume.42 Enzyme/drug mixes (80 μL) were subjected to a 15-minute preincubation at 37 °C, and the probe substrate reactions were initiated by the addition of 80 μL 4-NPA prepared from a stock solution of 20 mM in ethanol and further diluted in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to obtain a final reaction concentration of 100 μM (< Km).43 The probe, 4-NPA, undergoes spontaneous hydrolysis in water, hence dilutions were prepared in cold PBS buffer to control for pH and limit nonenzymatic hydrolysis.44 The probe substrate reactions were incubated for up to 5 minutes at 37 °C under agitation and the formation of 4-NP was monitored by measuring absorbance at a wavelength of 405 nm with a Tecan Infinite 200 PRO plate reader. The absorbance values obtained at the 5-minute time-point were used to assess the CES1 activity, as product formation was determined to be linear with time at the 5-minute mark.45,46 Simvastatin (10 μM) was used as a positive control for CES1 inhibition.47 Incubations with DMSO alone served as solvent control and incubations without CES1 were used as a negative control and for background absorbance subtraction.

The human CES2 in vitro inhibition assays were done using the 96-well black polystyrene microtiter plates (Costar, catalog #3915) in a similar setting to that of CES1 experiments, except that the reactions contained CES2 (2.5 μg/mL) in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Following a 15-minute preincubation of the enzyme/drug mixes at 37 °C, the probe substrate reactions were initiated by the addition of 80 μL of 6 μM FD. FD stock solution was prepared in DMSO and then diluted in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The fluorescein signal deriving from the fluorescein formed in incubations was monitored over 5 minutes using the Tecan Infinite 200 PRO plate reader set at an excitation/emission wavelength of 483/525 nm.45 Fenofibrate (10 μM) was used as a positive control for inhibition, and DMSO control reactions served as solvent control.47 In these CES1 and CES2 incubation assays, solvent control incubations were used as the 100% control activity to calculate the percent remaining CES activity. GraphPad Prism 10 software for MacOS (version 10.4.0) was used for data processing and graphing.

For remdesivir dose-response curves, the experimental procedures were similar to those described previously. The reactions contained remdesivir at concentrations ranging from 3.7 to 200 μM for CES1 and from 0.003 to 0.8 μM for CES2. Serial dilutions were performed in DMSO from a 40 mM remdesivir stock solution to obtain the different concentration ranges of working solutions. No CES enzyme control was included as a negative control and DMSO was used as a solvent control for 100% activity. Percent control activity was plotted against drug concentrations and fitted to a nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 10 software for MacOS (version 10.4.0) to determine the half maximal IC (IC50) value.

Time-dependent inhibition of human recombinant CES2

As an initial assessment for potential time dependency of CES2 inhibition by remdesivir, dose-response experiments were carried out at 2 preincubation times, 5 and 40 minutes. The assay conditions followed the same procedure described previously and included the same remdesivir concentration range. The IC50 values were determined for both dose-response curves using GraphPad Prism 10 software for MacOS (version 10.4.0), and an IC50 shift was calculated based on the ratio of the IC50 value after 5-minute preincubation to the IC50 value after 40-minute preincubation.

To determine the bimolecular rate constants of CES2 inhibition by remdesivir, the preincubation time-dependent inhibition with the dilution approach was followed.48 The inhibition phase reactions containing human recombinant CES2 enzymes (25 μg/mL) were preincubated with remdesivir at 0.04, 0.08, 0.16, 0.24, 0.32, 0.48 and 0.6 μM concentrations in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. At preincubation time points of 1, 8, 15, 28, 40, and 60 minutes, a 12 μL aliquot of the enzyme/drug mixtures was transferred to the probe substrate reaction (120 μL final volume) containing 25 μM FD (∼5 × Km). Stock solution of FD (20 mM) was prepared in DMSO and diluted in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The probe substrate reactions were incubated for 5 minutes and terminated by the addition of an equal volume of ice-cold acetonitrile. Fluorescence signal from fluorescein formed was measured with the Tecan Infinite 200 PRO plate reader at an excitation/emission wavelength of 483/525 nm. The observed rate constant (kobs) for CES2 inhibition was estimated by using linear fit of the mean natural logarithm of the percent remaining enzyme activity versus the preincubation time. The second order rate constant describing the efficiency of enzyme inactivation, kinact/KI, was determined based on the kobs versus the remdesivir concentration in the preincubation using GraphPad Prism 10 software for MacOS (version 10.4.0).

Reversibility assessment of CES2 inactivation by remdesivir using equilibrium dialysis

The reversibility of the time-dependent inhibition was investigated by an equilibrium dialysis experiment, using a concentration of remdesivir that should yield over 90% inhibition of CES2 activity. The preincubation reactions consisted of 25 μg/mL CES2 in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and 0.6 μM remdesivir in a 144 μL reaction volume. After 60 minutes at 37 °C, a 12 μL aliquot of the recombinant CES2/remdesivir preincubation mix was added to 108 μL of the probe substrate FD, to yield a FD concentration of 25 μM in a 120 μL reaction volume. After incubating for 5 minutes, the reactions were ended by adding an equal volume of ice-cold acetonitrile and CES2 activity was assessed by measuring fluorescence signal from fluorescein. Subsequently, the remaining enzyme/drug preincubation mixes were transferred into slide-a-lyzer mini dialysis units (0.1 mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog #69570) of 10 kDa molecular weight cut off, placed in a float buoy (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog #69588) and dialyzed against 2 L of 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C with low stirring. The dialysis buffer was switched after 2- and 4-hours, and dialysis was conducted for up to 24 hours. The remaining enzyme activity was assessed at 2 hours, 5 hours, and 24 hours after starting dialysis, with a solvent control (DMSO) and positive control (fenofibrate; 16 μM preincubation concentration) included for each set.

CES2 substrate protection against remdesivir inhibition

The substrate protection experiment was done following similar experimental procedures as the time-dependent inhibition assay, except that the preincubation reactions contained 4-NPA as a competitive CES substrate, 4-NPA would then compete with remdesivir for CES2 binding at the active site. The inhibition reactions consisted of 25 μg/mL CES2 in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) with 0.6 μM remdesivir and 4-NPA at concentrations of 200, 400, and 800 μM in a 576 μL reaction volume in the preincubations. CES2 remaining activity was assessed by incubating 12 μL of the remdesivir/4-NPA/enzyme mix with the probe substrate FD to a concentration of 25 μM in a 120 μL reaction volume at each of the following preincubation time points: 1, 8, 15, 28, 40, and 60 minutes. The natural logarithm of the percent remaining CES2 activity versus preincubation times was plotted for each of the 4-NPA concentration tested and compared to a no 4-NPA control.

Molecular docking to human CES enzymes

To obtain structural information on the CES molecular interactions with remdesivir and other ligand types, molecular docking studies were performed for CES2 with remdesivir and the inhibitor sofosbuvir, and for CES1 with remdesivir, using AutoDock Vina software (v. 1.1.2).49 The 3-dimensional (3D) structure data format (sdf) files of ligands were obtained from the PubChem website (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and converted into 3D Protein Data Bank (PDB) files using Open Babel tool (http://openbabel.org/docs/GUI/GUI.html). The ligands were prepared for docking using AutoDock Tools (v. 1.5.7; https://ccsb.scripps.edu/mgltools/) by adding polar hydrogens and identifying rotatable bonds before saving the resulting structure as a PDBQT (Protein Data Bank, Partial charges (Q), & Atom Type (T)) file. The AlphaFold structure of the human CES2 homology model was downloaded from the UniProt website (UniProt ID, O00748; https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/O00748/entry). The predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores were examined, and the active site residues displayed high confidence (pLDDT > 90), supporting its use as a reliable model for performing docking. The human CES1 crystal structure with the naloxone methiodide bound was obtained from the PDB website (PDB ID code, 1MX9). The CES1 and CES2 structures were prepared for docking by removing any additional chains, all other ligands, and solvent molecules in the UCSF ChimeraX tool (v. 1.8). Furthermore, polar hydrogens and kollman charges were added to these modified protein structures and saved as PDBQT files using AutoDock Tools (v. 1.5.7). A grid box covering the catalytic site was generated using the GridBox function in AutoDock Tools. The docking grid parameters defined for CES2 were center_x = −2.555, center_y = −2.820, and center_z = −8.214 and those for CES1 included: center_x = −0.227, center_y = 5.976, and center_z = 36.469. Configuration files (.txt) were prepared with the PDBQT file names of the protein and ligand, grid box-center parameters and dimensions, and an exhaustiveness value of 24. AutoDock Vina was run with the command prompt and by using the configuration files to execute docking. Screening was done with grid box dimensions set to 40 × 40 × 40 Å for docking to the entire active site, whereas the targeted approach used grid box dimensions set to 20 × 20 × 20 Å, focused on the active site catalytic triad. The results from docking were analyzed using UCSF ChimeraX interface (v. 1.8) for 3D interactions.

Results

Inhibition of human CES enzymes by COVID-19 antiviral drugs

To assess the inhibitory potential of remdesivir, molnupiravir, and nirmatrelvir (Fig. 1) for the human CES1 and CES2 enzymes, 2 distinct spectroscopic enzymatic assays were implemented. For CES1, hydrolysis of the 4-NPA probe substrate was surveyed by absorbance43, and for CES2, hydrolysis of the FD probe substrate (Fig. 1) was measured by fluorescence.45 The initial inhibition experiments were performed using a preincubation of the esterases with 10 and 100 μM antiviral drugs. Molnupiravir and nirmatrelvir showed only negligible effect on the CES1 and CES2 enzyme activities (Fig. 2), with < 20% decrease in activity observed even at the highest concentrations tested. However, remdesivir significantly inhibited both CES1 and CES2 hydrolysis reactions. Of the 2 CES isoforms, remdesivir displayed potent inhibition of CES2, with complete loss of hydrolytic activity at 10 μM concentration (percent control of 0.24% ± 0.04) (Fig. 2B). Time-dependent inhibition of human recombinant CES2 by remdesivir

The IC50 shift observed with remdesivir and CES2 further compelled us to characterize the TDI of the recombinant human CES2 through kinetic analysis of the observed inhibition. Increasing preincubation time and inhibitor concentrations were tested with CES2 and remdesivir, however, here the dilution methodology was instead used for the probe substrate hydrolysis activity. The decrease of CES2 activity was both time and inhibitor concentration dependent (Fig. 5A). The derived rate constants (kobs) for each concentration of remdesivir tested were then plotted against the remdesivir concentrations in the preincubation (Fig. 5B). The hyperbolic fit from the nonlinear regression analysis materialized unexpectedly as linear, similarly to what would be expected from inhibition with an underlying 1-step reaction mechanism.48 The inactivation efficiency kinact/KI or kchem, determined from the slope of the linear fit,50 was found to be 1.6 × 103 M−1.s−1 (95% CI, 1.4 × 103–1.8 × 103 M−1.s−1) (Fig. 5B).Fig. 5Time-dependent inhibition of CES2 fluorescein diacetate hydrolysis by remdesivir. This was followed by applying a straight line fit (solid line) from which the kinact/KI value was determined to be 1.6 × 103 M−1.s−1. Statistical comparison between the 2 fits was done using Akaike Information Criterion function (ΔAICc = −0.905, supporting the simplest model, the linear fit, as best fit).

Reversibility of CES2 inactivation by remdesivir

To investigate whether the covalent inhibition observed is caused by reversible or irreversible binding of remdesivir with CES2, an equilibrium dialysis experiment was carried out (Fig. 6). Before dialysis, CES2 was preincubated with either remdesivir or fenofibrate, used here as a control for reversible inhibition. In the presence of fenofibrate, CES2 enzyme activity was fully restored after 2-hour dialysis compared to the corresponding solvent control. Effectively, a decrease in enzyme activity was observed for the solvent control with increasing dialysis time, which is to be expected with the extended incubation at 4 °C. Effect of substrate protection

4-NPA, a CES2 substrate, was used here to assess if it could protect the inactivation of CES2 by remdesivir through competitive binding at the active site. It was added to the preincubation reactions which included remdesivir and CES2. The addition of 4-NPA in the preincubation reactions attenuated the loss of CES2 activity over time compared to remdesivir itself (Fig. 7), indicating an overlap for the interaction site between remdesivir and 4-NPA. Different concentrations of 4-NPA including those near to its Km, 2 × Km, and 4 × Km value for CES2 were tested to cover different levels of its occupancy at the enzyme’s active site. However, nearly complete protection was evident at the lower 4-NPA concentration tested and the effect was not well differentiated in a dose-dependent manner. Molecular docking

Initial docking studies of remdesivir to CES2 conducted with grid box dimensions covering almost the entire active site resulted in 9 binding conformations with ΔG scores of −6.8 to −6.2 kcal/mol (Supplemental Table 1). All output poses were bound near the side-door region and the best pose is presented in Supplemental Figure 1. Our subsequent docking attempt with a targeted approach focused near the active site triad led to relatively less favorable output poses with ΔG scores between −5.7 and −3.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 8A, Supplemental Table 2). One of the output poses with a ΔG score of −4.5 kcal/mol that displayed 2 hydrogen bonds between the active site triad serine residue (SER228) and the phosphate group of remdesivir, is placed at a distance of <2.5Å to the nucleophilic serine making it compatible for a covalent bond formation (Fig. 8B). The interaction of remdesivir in the CES2 active site is similar to the interaction seen with a known covalent inhibitor of CES2, sofosbuvir (Fig. 8, C and D). The output pose of sofosbuvir with a ΔG score of −6.3 kcal/mol (Supplemental Table 3) that displayed H-bond contact with the active site serine residue supports an identical mechanism of inhibition via the phosphate group. In contrast, docking to CES1 of remdesivir showed variation in its interaction with the active site (Fig. 8, E and F, Supplemental Table 4). Discussion

Although COVID-19 infection was declared no longer a public health emergency in May 2023 by the World Health Organization, the virus still affects several hundred thousand worldwide each year.51 Complementary to vaccination, COVID-19 antiviral drugs represent effective treatment options. In fact, antiviral drugs are recommended for use in mild-to-moderate COVID-19 patients who are at a risk of progression to severe disease.52 Despite the availability of the nirmatrelvir-ritonavir combination in an oral formulation efficient to reduce the risk of hospitalization by 89%, remdesivir is the only approved antiviral drug with supporting results for use in COVID-19 hospitalized patients.53,54 CES are major enzymes involved in the activation of remdesivir and molnupiravir. However, the risk of DDIs from covalent inhibition of CES2 by remdesivir is still unclear. Here, we assessed the potential for inhibition of CES enzymes by the COVID-19 antiviral drugs, remdesivir, molnupiravir and nirmatrelvir (Fig. 1), using in vitro CES inhibition assays. For the compounds demonstrating significant inhibition, we further determined their CES inhibition potency and propensity for in vitro TDI effect, in accordance with the Food and Drug Administration’s guidelines for investigating DDIs.55,56

From our initial screening at 10 and 100 μM, we found that among the 3 drugs tested, only remdesivir inhibited CES activity of both isoforms by >50% (Fig. 2). Remdesivir is reported to be a CES1 substrate and given its high structural similarity to sofosbuvir, a known CES1 substrate and CES2 inhibitor,34 this could explain the observed inhibitory effect for both CES enzymes. In contrast, and somewhat surprisingly, no effect was observed with molnupiravir on CES2, for which it is a substrate. Interestingly, the remdesivir IC50 value of 36.2 μM for CES1 (Fig. 3A) is near its Km value,21 indicating its competitive interaction with 4-NPA for the CES1 active site. It should be noted that remdesivir has an average peak plasma concentration of 5.02 μM after intravenous infusion, with rapid decay after 1 hour to a concentration of 0.130 μM.57,58 A higher Km value than the physiologically relevant concentration might be an indication of incomplete saturation of CES1 enzyme with remdesivir. However, an IC50 value in the nanomolar range was measured for CES2 hydrolysis of FD (Fig. 3B), reflecting potent CES2 inhibition by remdesivir, as previously suggested by Shen et al 2021.

In general, such strong inhibitory effects are seen with covalent inhibitors. Covalent and slow binding inhibitors often present characteristic time-dependency in their inhibition profile as the residence time of the inhibitor exceeds the enzyme lifetime. The strong binding of the inhibitor renders the active site unavailable, thereby reducing the amount of free enzyme that could interact with the probe substrate. An IC50 shift of >1.5-fold is generally considered indicative of a TDI.56,59 Therefore, with an IC50 shift of 3-fold, remdesivir showed indication of a TDI (Fig. 4, Table 1). Although a time- and concentration-dependent decrease in the enzyme activity can be observed with either slow reversible inhibition or covalent inhibition, the loss of enzyme activity following a dilution method experiment (Fig. 5A), together with the data from a previous report based on a native gel electrophoresis study,32 strengthen the probability of covalent binding of remdesivir to CES2. The covalent inhibition can be of 2 types: it can be a 2-step mechanism involving the formation of an initial reversible enzyme-inhibitor complex at an inhibitory constant equal to KI, which is followed by the formation of a covalent enzyme-inhibitor adduct at a reaction rate of kinact. The second type is a 1-step mechanism where the covalent adduct is directly formed without any intermediate step. A linear plot is seen when the highest inhibitor concentration in the study is less than its KI which refers to a case of a high rate of inactivation from a specific inhibitor with a 2-step mechanism or a nonspecific reaction with a 1-step inactivation mechanism (eg, affinity labels).50 Although it is not possible to accurately estimate the inactivation kinetic constants from the linear plot, the overall inactivation efficiency (kinact /KI) can be obtained from the initial linear slope.60 Determination of remdesivir inactivation kinetic constants for CES2, featured as a linear plot, led to an inactivation rate equal to 1.6 × 103 M−1.s−1 (Fig. 5B). Although the kinact /KI value observed is less than that expected from target-specific inhibitors which are in the range of 104 to 106 M−1.s−1, it is still higher than the range of values for the affinity of 1-step inactivators whose bimolecular constant ratios are in the range of 10−2 to 102 M−1.s−1.50,61 While there are no CES2 covalent inhibitors that are used clinically, inhibition estimates range from 102–104 M−1.s−1 for several irreversible enzyme inhibitors developed to target CES, other serine hydrolases and serine proteases.48,62, 63, 64 The CES2 inactivation efficiency by remdesivir is within this range.

To confirm covalent binding and to differentiate between a slow reversible and an irreversible mechanism, further evaluations were carried out with equilibrium dialysis, substrate protection and docking studies. Enzyme-inhibitor complex formed from irreversible interactions can be uncovered using an equilibrium dialysis experiment, where the enzyme activity lost due to noncovalent reversible inhibition is recovered after dialysis. Our findings have shown that remdesivir irreversibly inhibits CES2, as effectively the enzyme activity is not recovered after 24-hour dialysis (Fig. 6). Additionally, we have demonstrated that when remdesivir was coincubated with over a 1000-fold higher concentration of another CES2 substrate (4-NPA), it attenuated the observed inactivation from remdesivir (Fig. 7). At a higher 4-NPA concentration, the substrate molecules occupy the active site rendering it unavailable for remdesivir binding. This would imply that the inactivation observed with remdesivir is due to its interaction with the active site catalytic triad residues. Serine covalent inhibition can be expected between a reactive electrophile and the nucleophilic serine hydroxyl group. Apart from the ester moiety in remdesivir, which is expected to be hydrolyzed by the active site serine, the structure includes a phosphoramidate group which can act as an electrophile. A proposed reaction mechanism of irreversible inhibition of CES2 by remdesivir is presented in Supplemental Figure 2. The formation of the reversible intermediate might be absent if it is a 1-step inactivation.

In order to gain further insights into CES2 remdesivir interactions, we performed molecular docking studies using the AlphaFold CES2 structure model. Remdesivir was positioned at the side-door region in its most stable output form (Supplemental Fig. 1). It is important to note that the side-door region of the CES enzyme is also known for its ligand binding potential.65,66 However, in the case of CES2 and remdesivir, the substrate protection experiment confirmed binding of remdesivir near the CES2 active site. Typically, routine ligand docking is conducted against low-energy and conformationally static structures, oblivious to the fact that enzymes are dynamic entities which can undergo significant conformational rearrangements at flexible points while binding to a substrate, which can be overlooked while docking a ligand to a static protein structure. Therefore, we reattempted docking by specifically targeting the active site region of CES2. This targeted approach resulted in an output pose where the phosphate group of remdesivir is located near the catalytic triad residue S228 forming 2 H-bonds with it (Fig. 8, A and B). This corresponds well with studies where covalent modification of the serine residue has been observed with diarylphosphate drugs and other classes bearing the phosphate moiety, which are being investigated as inhibitors to target serine enzymes.67, 68, 69 Moreover, our docking results with remdesivir are comparable to those of sofosbuvir which is a known CES2 covalent inhibitor (Fig. 8, C and D). Unlike the CES2 binding mode, remdesivir, a CES1 substrate, has its hydrolysable ester carbonyl positioned near the CES1 active site serine (Fig. 8, E and F). This highlights remdesivir specificity for CES1 and CES2, interacting with distinct binding modes within each isoform. Together the TDI, dialysis, and docking results support the concept of covalent binding to CES2 of remdesivir through its phosphate group.

Although the definitive clinical DDI risk of remdesivir for CES2 remains to be determined, our study provides a refined quantitative analysis of CES2 inactivation by remdesivir with rates comparable to irreversible inhibitors of serine hydrolases.48,62, 63, 64 In addition, it raises substantial concerns for the physiological impact of remdesivir on the liver functions controlling lipid homeostasis, especially after the adverse hepatic effects observed with remdesivir dosing.36, 37, 38 In mice, reduced hepatic CES2 level is associated with increased plasma transaminase levels and steatosis, and CES2 protein content was decreased by ∼50% in patients with simple steatosis,39 indicating that CES2 irreversible disruption by remdesivir could have undesirable consequences on the liver and would merit further investigation to answer inherent safety concerns.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that remdesivir is a potent time-dependent inhibitor of CES2 via an irreversible interaction with active site residues. Although the physiological relevance of remdesivir CES inactivation is unclear due to insufficient clinical data, it offers valuable insights into the structural determinants of remdesivir driving its selectivity and mode of interaction between the human CES isoforms. Lastly, our findings include beneficial knowledge to possibly reduce off-target effects and refined selectivity during early stage drug design while developing protide prodrugs.
