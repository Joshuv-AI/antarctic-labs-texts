Ocular Surface Regeneration by Limbal Stem Cells Therapies: State of the Art, Challenges, and Perspectives
Authors: Clémence Bonnet, Sheyla González, Sophie X Deng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimbal stem cells (LSCs) are adult stem cells located at the limbus ensuring the continuous renewal of the corneal epithelium, critical to maintain an optimal visual function. Damages to the LSCs or their niche microenvironment lead to limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), a potentially blinding disease. Transplantation of LSCs as a treatment for severe to total LSCD has gained popularity since 1980s, owing to the clinical success of the first direct limbal autograft transplantation. Recent advances in the understanding of the LSCs’ molecular identity and regulation have enabled preclinical and clinical advancements of promising LSCs therapies. However, lack of standardization of the diagnostic methods, staging of the disease severity, manufacturing process, and clinical outcome measures have hindered the advancement of the therapy. To move these therapies to the clinic, optimization and standardization of the diagnostic strategy, cell product manufacturing, and assessment of clinical efficacy with potency assays are key points to the development of customized therapies. Recent findings suggest that residual LSCs exist in eyes presenting with clinical signs of total LSCD, which opens new therapeutic strategies for eyes with partial LSCD. Prospective, randomized, multicentric controlled clinical trials are necessary to determine the efficacy of different LSCs therapies for different stages of LSCD using a set of standardized outcome measures.

Significance Statement

Decades of advancements in the understanding of limbal stem cell biology, pathophysiology of limbal stem cell deficiency, and transplantation outcomes have accelerated the development of limbal stem cell therapies. The implementation of standardized culture methods using xenobiotic-free reagents and manufacturing processes that are in compliance with the current good manufacturing practices and regulatory requirements is ongoing. Standardization of disease diagnosis and staging using multimodal in vivo imaging and molecular markers is required for comparisons of clinical outcomes across different therapies worldwide. Prospective, randomized, and controlled clinical trials are necessary to determine the safety and the long-term success rate of limbal stem cell therapies.

Introduction

Limbal stem cells (LSCs) are adult stem cells located at the basal layer of the limbal epithelium.1 They play a critical role in the maintenance of the corneal epithelial homeostasis and wound healing. LSCs maintain a physical barrier between the conjunctiva and the cornea2 and ensure the continuous centripetal renewal of the corneal epithelial cells following the widely accepted XYZ hypothesis in human.3 Any damage to the LSCs or their niche could lead to replacement of the corneal epithelium by the conjunctival epithelium, defining a disease called limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD).4

Treatments of LSCs have evolved, since the 1980s’. As outlined by the global consensus report,4 the ocular surface should be optimized prior to surgical intervention. Achieving a sufficient LSC pool for the maintenance and renewal of corneal epithelial cells could stabilize the ocular surface. Recent findings that residual LSCs could be detected in eyes with clinical signs of total LSCD5,6 open the possibility of new strategies to restore corneal epithelial function by rejuvenating the residual LSCs in situ. This concise review summarizes the current LSC therapies available, the challenges raised by translating basic experimental LSC research from bench to bedside, and new therapeutic approaches for partial LSCD.

Diagnosis and Management of LSCD: State of the Art

LSCD has a low incidence.7 In the US, the most frequent etiologies include glaucoma surgery and antimitotic agents, contact lens wear, chemical/thermal burn, and congenital causes such as aniridia.4 Recent guidelines established the criteria for diagnosis staging and stepwise approach to manage the disease.4 Yet, the clinical signs of LSCD as detected by fluorescein staining can be subtle and are often not specific of LSCD in severe stages. Therefore, phenotypic analysis of cells on the corneal surface is recommended to evaluate the in vivo LSC function.4 Biomarkers including the central corneal epithelial thickness measured by anterior segment optical coherence tomography, the basal central corneal cell density, morphology, and sub-basal nerve density determined using in vivo confocal microscopy have proven to be necessary for a comprehensive evaluation of the disease to avoid misdiagnosis (Fig. 1).8 Impression cytology identifying goblet cells or markers of conjunctival cells on the cornea also confirm the presence of conjunctival epithelial cells, which is the hallmark of LSCD.9

The first step of the management of LSCD is treating any ocular surface comorbidities by optimizing the ocular surface. Then, depending on the laterality and stage of the disease, efficacy, complication, and availability of each treatment, experience of the surgeon, and preference of the patient, a treatment is recommended. Autologous LSC transplantation is the most desired option because of its highest success rate and lowest complication profile. Different surgical techniques (direct transplantation of limbal biopsy or cultivated limbal epithelial cells transplantation-CLET) and donor sources (autologous or allogeneic) are available. Autologous transplant is preferred as it does not require systemic immunosuppression as in allogeneic transplantation and it might be available for bilateral disease in the future. Direct transplantation can be further divided into kerato-limbal autograft (KLAU), conjunctival limbal autograft, conjunctival limbal allograft, keratolimbal allograft, and simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET).10 Two recent reports showed variable outcomes of LSC transplant. The first one is a meta-analysis including 2202 eyes from direct and ex-vivo LSC transplantation.11 It shows that autologous LSCs transplantation might have higher rate of success and lower complications than allogeneic LSCs transplantation (85.2% vs. 60.5%). The second report is a systematic review including 623 eyes from cLSC transplantation cultivated in good manufacturing practice (GMP) facilities.12 The finding did not show a difference between autologous and allogeneic cLSCs transplantation (67.1% vs. 60.1%). While the inclusion criteria differ among these 2 studies, both concluded that there is insufficient evidence to confirm the safety and efficacy of LSCs therapy, because of the multiple sources of heterogeneity within and between the studies identified.

The initial KLAU developed by Kenyon and Tseng was the first successful autologous LSCs transplantation but requires a limbal donor tissue of 4-6 clock hours.13 Modern approaches focus on reducing the biopsy size to preserve the unaffected eye.14 SLET has gained popularity as it requires only 1-2 clock hours of donor tissue. The main advantage of SLET is omission of cultivation step. However, demonstration of its success in severe to total LSCD is still lacking. CLET is still desired and necessary, as it requires the least amount of donor tissue and is repeatable. Ever since Pellegrini et al reported that autologous transplant of cultivated LSCs obtained from a 1-2 mm2 limbal biopsy could restore the corneal surface in eyes with total LSCD from chemical burn,15 accumulating evidence supports a success rate up to 76.6%.16 A great deal of effort has been made to improve cultivated LSC therapy by removing animal products during the manufacturing process, and expanding cell source to pluripotent stem cells and other adult stem cells. Currently, there are 5 ongoing clinical trials on new cultivated LSC transplantation (Supplementary Table S1).

Amniotic membrane (AM) is the most frequently used substrate in CLET because of its presumed biological properties.17 It might provide a niche-like environment for LSCs18 possibly through heavy chain-Hyaluronan/Pentraxin 3 that can preserve the LSCs fate decision.19 To alleviate the risk of infectious disease transmission, alternatives to AM are explored, including collagen scaffolds,20,21 fibrin gel,22 and synthetic hydrogels combined with a contact lens.23,24 Most of these biomaterials have not reached the clinical use, but it is an area of active research.

Challenges and Opportunities in Improving LSC Therapies

Most cell-based LSC therapies initially developed varied in the cultivation process were not GMP compliant and used animal products.16,25-27 Standardizing LSC manufacturing process and improving the safety of the cultivated LSCs complying with current GMP (cGMP) are a high priority in the field. A standardized cGMP process will ensure consistency, traceability, appropriate environmental controls, and quality of the final product (graphical abstract). Several groups have developed xenobiotic-free LSC expansion methods.17,25,28-30 The success rate of cultivated, xenobiotic-free, and GMP-compliant LSC transplantation is estimated at 67.1%.12 To manufacture products of high and controlled quality, and to predict the outcome success after transplantation and improve the long-term outcomes of LSC therapy, potency assays are critical. Pellegrini et al showed that more than 3% of p63αbright cells (a commonly accepted LSCs marker) is associated with a high rate of success of autologous CLET.16 Yet, it requires sacrificing a fragment of the final product before transplantation. Developing a potency assay that does not require the destruction final product is a current challenge. Soluble biomarkers of LSCs are being investigated, such as miR-6723-5p, which is associated with cultures containing high proportion of LSCs.31 Such cell-free biomarkers of LSCs are potential candidates for predicting LSC potency.

Until recently, the lack of standardization in the diagnosis and staging of the disease has hampered the improvement of LSC therapy. Patient’s selection is critical as surgical intervention is only required in severe to total LSCD.10 Objective monitoring of the disease progression and outcome measure of LSC therapies are also critical to compare the efficacy of these therapies worldwide. Clinical examination alone is insufficient in determining LSC function and may not correlate with the severity of LSCD.5,6 There have been reports of mis-diagnosed or mis-staged LSCD based on slit lamp biomicroscopy alone. Corneal opacity and neovascularization are often mistaken as LSCD. A recent review shows that only about 25% of eyes undergone LSC transplantation had a confirmatory test of LSCD.32 Therefore, a confirmatory diagnostic test performed before any surgical intervention is of paramount importance to avoid unnecessary LSC transplant.

Future Direction in LSC Therapy

Reconstruction of the niche is of paramount attention given the importance of the microenvironment to LSCs. As such, a parallel approach is necessary to treat LSCD: restoration of the LSCs pool and restoration of a suitable microenvironment.

We previously showed that certain Wnt ligands are upregulated in the human limbus.33 Moreover, activating Wnt signaling pathway in vitro could promote LSCs proliferation in an undifferentiated state.34,35 Using an in vitro model of chemical injury to the LSCs, our preliminary data suggest that Wnt activation promote the restoration of the LSCs phenotype and proliferation. Notch signaling is another pathway expressed in the limbus.36 When Notch signaling is inhibited using small molecules, LSC proliferation was favored in an undifferentiated state.37 On contrary, activating Notch by Jag-1 led to reduction of LSC population and stratification.38 Unraveling the complexities of Notch and Wnt signaling pathways as well as other signaling pathways important in LSC regulation will shed light on the design of small molecules modulating specific components of these signaling pathways. An appropriate regulation of LSC proliferation and differentiation could increase the efficiency of LSC cultivation. Such small molecules could also pave the way of in situ LSC rejuvenation in partial LSCD.

Restoration of the LSC microenvironment, given its complex molecular and cellular networks, and unique mechanical and adhesion properties, remains an area of active investigation. Options include the delivery of biological factors including serum/platelets derived products, cytokines and growth factors, and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs).39 MSCs, with their immunomodulatory properties and ability to produce trophic and extracellular matrix (ECM) factors to support LSCs, are promising candidates for restoring the limbal niche. Improvement of LSC expansion on 3-dimensional scaffolds and matrices with adequate stiffness and containing vascular endothelial cells, melanocytes, and other cells normally found in the LSC niche is also being explored.40,41 Research strategies have been focused more on targeting repopulation of LSCs than limbal niche reconstruction.42 Ultimately, combined approaches could improve the long-term outcomes of LSC therapies.26,43

Translating LSC therapies from bench to bedside poses challenges that require continuous effort to adopt a standardized set of quality control during the LSC expansion and a standardized set of outcome measures to determine efficacy. First, maintaining and confirming stemness of LSCs during the expansion process is the most important attribute of the product intent to restore LSC function. A better understanding of the molecular identity and regulation of LSCs will provide fundamental insights into their function.35,44 Many groups use murine or rabbit animal models to study the disease and potential therapies. While a necessary step during drug development, they present several limitations including biological variability in the disease induction, and lack of the use of ancillary tests to confirm LSCD.45 Second, the fate of transplanted LSCs on the ocular surface is still uncertain after LSC transplantation. The presumed mechanism of action of transplanted LSCs has been repopulation of the host corneal epithelium. Other hypotheses of LSC transplantation include, donor migration to the host niche, donor cell creation of a neo-niche in the peripheral cornea, and donor-stimulating corneal epithelial cells proliferation through paracrine signaling.46,47 However, several reports have shown that host epithelium repopulated the corneal surface after allogeneic LSC transplantation, while residual limbal epithelial cells are detected in eyes with clinical signs of total LSCD. Such findings suggest that either the initial diagnosis of LSCD was incorrect or the transplanted LSCs provided factors for regeneration of residual host LSCs, highlighting the importance of an accurate staging of LSCD before and after any treatment. These findings also provide a strong rationale for targeted biopsy of residual LSCs for expansion to treat ipsilateral and bilateral severe LSCD, to which SLET is not applicable.

Third, storage of the final product and transportation outside of tertiary referral centers equipped with cGMP facilities is still being developed. Ensuring worldwide delivery of a final product meeting all the safety and efficacy requirements will be a major step toward the adoption of the stem cell therapy, especially when the technique will be available for bilateral disease.48,49 Yet, accessibility to cGMP facilities, highly trained personnel in evaluation of LSCs function in vivo and manufacturing, and high cost of manufacturing, are still major hurdles, limiting its accessibility.50

Conclusion

Significant progress has been made in the diagnosis and treatment of LSCD. Multimodal in vivo imaging enables an accurate diagnosis and staging of LSCD, which could also serve as the basis of standardized outcome measures for comparing the efficacy across different LSC therapies. Standardization of quality controls during the LSC expansion under cGMP condition is necessary for patient safety. A better understanding of LSC regulation has opened the door for next generation of LSC therapy by improving LSCs stemness maintenance and niche reconstruction. The unmet need to provide accessible LSC therapy will require continuous collaborations among clinicians, scientists, regulatory agencies, governments, and pharmaceutical industry to translating laboratory findings into large-scale accessible clinical treatment. In parallel, continuous efforts to better understand the molecular mechanisms of signaling pathways involved in LSCs regulation could lead to the development of small molecules promoting in vivo rejuvenation of LSCs.
