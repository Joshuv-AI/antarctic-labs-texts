Protocol to quantify protein phosphorylation in mouse extended pluripotent stem cell-derived blastoids
Authors: Tianxia Xiao, Yuxin Luo, Yang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryProtein phosphorylation is an important post-transcription modification regulating essential cellular activities. Here, we present a proteomics protocol to quantify protein phosphorylation in the extended pluripotent stem cell (EPSC)-derived blastoids from Mus musculus with a standardized phosphoproteome analysis workflow. We describe steps for culturing blastoids, preparing samples, enriching phosphopeptides, and fractionating peptides. We then detail procedures for acquiring and analyzing high-resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) data. This protocol provides a framework for reproducible analysis of phospho-proteomes of mouse blastoids.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Min et al.1Subject areas: Developmental biology, Proteomics, Stem Cells, Systems biology

Before you begin

Blastoids are in vitro-derived 3D structures that can resemble key architectural and lineage features of the natural blastocyst, offering a scalable and ethically accessible model for studying early mammalian development.

Researching phosphorylation in blastoid cells is essential because this dynamic post-translational modification regulates nearly all aspects of embryogenesis, including: cell polarization and compaction (via proteins such as Par/aPKC complexes2), lineage specification (e.g., through phosphorylation-mediated activation of transcription factors like YAP3), and signal transduction in pathways such as Hippo, Wnt, and TGF-β, which direct cell fate decisions.4

Quantifying phosphorylation events provides functional insight into signaling dynamics that underpin successful blastoid formation and offers a readout of developmental fidelity compared to in vivo embryos.

Innovation

This protocol advanced the current methodology by establishing the first integrated and reproducible workflow for quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis of EPSC-derived blastoids. Unlike existing generalized phosphoproteomic methods, this approach is specifically optimized for the unique challenges of blastoid models, including complex culture procedures and the dynamic signaling events governing embryonic patterning. Key innovations include precise temporal sampling aligned with critical developmental milestones (Day 3 and Day 5) and an integrated workflow combining efficient IMAC-based phosphopeptide enrichment with high-pH fractionation to maximize depth. By providing a robust framework that directly links phosphorylation signaling to blastoid development, this protocol enables systematic investigation of embryogenesis in an accessible and scalable model system, overcoming technological and ethical limitations of traditional embryo-based studies.

Timeline to harvest blastoids (“Generate EPS blastoids”)

Passage isolated mEPSCs for at least five generations to achieve homogeneity and full reprogramming. At this point, cryopreserve the mEPSCs for future use. Initiate the blastoid experiment by seeding mEPSCs in AggreWell, designated as Day 0. By Day 3, use phase-contrast microscopy to assess if cell clusters have formed a small cavity, and harvest early blastoid samples to evaluate embryo development. By Day 5, successful blastoid development is indicated by a distinct blastocoel and a dense cell cluster at one pole, resembling an ICM-like structure.

Proteomics sample preparation for TMT labeling

Timing: 2 days

This section describes the procedure to process blastoids for quantitative proteomics. In this procedure, blastoids from Day 3 (polarization and compaction) and Day 5 (lineage specification) are collected, and a 6-plex TMT kit is used to quantitatively assess the relative abundance of peptides.

Expected outcomes

This protocol yields the identification of thousands of phosphopeptides and can map them onto 2000+ proteins (Figure 6). Among the phosphorylated sites, there are around a thousand hyperphosphorylated sites and hypophosphorylated sites at a similar magnitude. About 24% (5039/21318) of identified peptides do not have a TMT label, slightly limiting the resolving power of this quantitative approach. However, at the protein level, this limitation attenuates as only 3% (97/3168) of proteins do not have TMT labels, and the identification of phosphorylated sites is the same across replicates. In addition, less than 0.5% of identified peptides do not have the same status of TMT labels between replicates, suggesting good reproducibility of this approach. Most phosphosites are serine (>90%) while tyrosine contributes a small fraction (<1%), agreeing with eukaryotic phosphorylation precedence. This result confirms active phosphorylation activity in mouse blastoids, with an example showing changes in the phosphorylation status of PDHA1 from Day 3 to Day 5 (Figure 7). During the rapid and precise events of early embryogenesis, post-translational regulation—particularly through phosphorylation—serves as a critical mechanism to swiftly control protein activity without requiring new gene transcription. This process acts as a master switch, coordinating essential developmental programs. For instance, phosphorylation directly activates key signaling pathways like MAPK and PI3K/AKT, which govern fundamental cell behaviors including proliferation, differentiation, and survival.11 It also establishes cellular architecture, as seen when the phosphorylation of polarity complexes (Par3/Par6/aPKC) dictates apical-basal polarity and forms tight junctions to create distinct tissue layers.2 Furthermore, phosphorylation meticulously drives the cell cycle by activating and deactivating cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases, ensuring timely cell divisions.12 Finally, it regulates gene expression at the nuclear level by modifying transcription factors to recruit them to DNA or by altering histones to open chromatin, allowing specific genes to be read.13 Thus, through these multifaceted roles, phosphorylation provides the dynamic and immediate control necessary to orchestrate the complex dance of early development.

Limitations

EPSC-derived blastoids may not fully replicate in vivo embryo phosphorylation dynamics: for example, a significant hyperphosphorylation of glycolysis is observed in blastoids but not in embryos, potentially due to the artificial media component and high glucose content in the in vitro environment.1,14 Future exploration of the phosphoproteome of alternative cell models should be encouraged to examine which system best simulates in vivo phosphorylation events. These alternative cell models can be “assembloids” with trophoblast stem cells and extraembryonic endoderm cells or even human naïve pluripotent stem cells-derived blastoid models.15,16 Multi-omics analysis, especially metabolomics analysis, enabled by the large-scale production of blastoids, can also aid the interpretation of embryonic developmental events.17 In addition, spliceosome is increasingly phosphorylated from 2-cell stages to blastocysts in vivo, but the phosphorylation of spliceosome is less significant in vitro, potentially due to the lack of a maternal molecular landscape that is rich with maternal RNA inputs.1,14 The protocol relies on an in vitro system lacking maternal tissue interactions, which might change the post-translational modifications of relevant pathways. Introduction of maternal tissues, such as a 3D co-culture system with endometrial organoid assemblies,18,19 might better emulate in vivo phosphorylation environments. Yet, the rest of the protocol can be adapted to examine phosphoproteomics of in vivo embryos or more complex in vitro systems suggested above, or the data can be compared to existing phosphoproteomics data for cross-validation.14 On a brief note, although in vivo data did not observe significant phosphorylation of glycolysis, RNA stabilization is predominantly regulated by phosphorylation and thus supports the observation in blastoids.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low mEPS viability (before you begin, step 19b).

Potential solution

Include 10 μM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632) in the N2B27-LDCM media.

Problem 2

Protein digestion is incomplete (Step 2).

Potential solution

Firstly, check if trypsin activity is at its optimum. One possible caveat with the current protocol is the protease inhibitor cocktail in the lysis buffer. We intend to overcome the inhibitor activity by two steps of trypsin digestion. If the problem persists, an alternative solution is to use sodium deoxycholate (SDC) 4% (wt/vol) in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) as a lysis buffer at 4°C and quickly heat-treat the lysate for 5 min at 95°C to stabilize the proteome according to Humphrey et al.20 However, our current method uses inhibitors and keeps samples at low temperatures because heat treatment might still reduce the maximum number of peptides detected.21

Problem 3

Inconsistent sample input (Step 1b).

Potential solution

Normalize by protein content instead. After lysis/sonication (Step 1c), measure protein concentration (BCA, Step 1e) before proceeding. Use equal protein amounts (e.g., 50 μg) for digestion. Blastoid size/lysis efficiency may vary; protein normalization ensures comparable input.

Problem 4

Suboptimal trypsin digestion (Step 2e/f).

Potential solution

Use enzyme-to-protein mass ratio (1:50) based on BCA-quantified protein, not fixed μg. Run SDS-PAGE on 5% of lysate pre-/post-digestion to confirm complete cleavage.

Problem 5

Biased IMAC enrichment of multi-phosphorylation over mono-phosphorylation (Step 8).

Potential solution

Combine IMAC with TiO2 enrichment in a sequential or parallel workflow. TiO2 captures monophosphorylated peptides more efficiently, improving the coverage of tyrosine sites. Validate with phosphopeptide standards to ensure balanced representation.

Problem 6

Limited fractionation depth on low-abundance phosphopeptides (Step 13).

Potential solution

Increase fractionation to 12–24 pools (non-adjacent concatenation) if instrument time is available.

Problem 7

Omitting biologically important phosphosites with ambiguous fragmentation (Step 18).

Potential solution

Cross-validate sites using complementary tools like Mascot Delta Score or PTMProphet to rescue borderline probability sites (0.6–0.75). Prioritize low-probability sites in enriched pathways (e.g., glycolysis/Hippo) for manual MS/MS spectrum validation.
