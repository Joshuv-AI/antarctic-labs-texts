Coastal dune management affects above and belowground biotic characteristics
Authors: Andrew E. White, Nicholas Cohn, Elizabeth H. Davis, Christopher J. Hein, Julie C. Zinnert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Dune vegetation mediates dune-building through trapping wind-blown sand and reduces dune erosion by attenuating wave energy via above- and belowground biomass. Despite the role of vegetation in dune functions, the amount and distribution of biomass within a dune remains poorly quantified due to a lack of ample data. Our objectives were to determine the effects of management history and elevation on (1) dune belowground biotic structure and aboveground community composition and (2) to determine best predictors of belowground biomass. We sampled belowground biomass and sedimentology across the dune profile at sites in the Outer Banks, North Carolina, USA. Dunes were classified as either unmanaged (no anthropogenic interventions) or managed (sand fencing, vegetation planting, dune construction). Living belowground biomass was higher in unmanaged dunes and decreased with depth. Non-living belowground biomass was 50% higher than living biomass and with greater abundance in unmanaged dunes. Elevation was a significant covariate of living and non-living belowground biomass, vegetative cover and species richness. Plant community composition varied less in managed dunes and differed significantly from unmanaged dunes. Vegetative cover, species richness, elevation, sedimentology and management history were predictors of belowground biomass. These results underscore the influence of management and geomorphology on dune plant communities, which may influence erosion resistance.

Introduction

Coastal foredunes are widely considered the first line of defense against storms along sandy coastlines1. Researchers, coastal managers and community members are increasingly recognizing the value of coastal dunes for protecting life and property during severe storm events2. This protective value, however, is determined by many complex and interacting variables such as pre-storm beach and dune geomorphology3–5 and the amount and type of vegetation present6. Understanding and predicting the functional role of dunes, as well as the protection and viability of dunes in the future, are priorities for coastal planners in view of population growth, sea level rise, and increase in the intensity and frequency of storms7,8.

As coastal-protection features, sand dunes are naturally responsive to wind and wave erosion compared with hard-engineering features like jetties, groins, sea walls and revetments that can disrupt the movement of sediment along sandy coasts2,9. Vegetation presence on coastal foredunes can reduce dune erosion and amplify accretion due to both above- and belowground plant effects on sediment transport transport10. Vertical and horizontal biomass patterns within these dunes are dependent on a range of factors, including the plant species and the depositional history of the dune11. When partially buried, dune plants extend stems and roots, allocate biomass aboveground and vegetatively expand with rhizomes and stolons12. As burial occurs, dunes increase in sediment volume with a proportional increase in biomass belowground13. The burial-vegetation growth feedback also allows dunes to recover following erosion via wave collision, provided adequate sediment availability, living vegetation and time between disturbance events14. During storms and high-water events, aboveground structures (e.g., leaves, stems and stolons) of vegetation seaward of the dune crest slow wave run-up10, whereas belowground structures (e.g., roots, belowground stems and rhizomes) hold sediment in place and reduce the erosive power of waves that collide with, rather than overwash, the foredune10,15.

Vegetation characteristics that interact with wind and waves may differ substantially based on the mechanisms responsible for dune formation, including the impacts of management, defined here as the suite of anthropogenic practices intended to stabilize or grow a dune. These practices include beach nourishment16, dune-vegetation planting17 and installation of sand fencing17,18. For example, natural dune vegetation establishes bare sand from propagules transported by wind, waves, tides, ocean currents and animals, creating a plant community that is heterogeneously distributed and develops slowly over time with sediment accretion14. Unmanaged dunes are not planted or directly altered to accelerate sediment capture. Conversely, managed dunes are often built and graded using heavy machinery and supplemented with sand fencing to encourage sediment capture and dune growth. Managed dunes may be planted with one or a few species that are often uniformly distributed based on planting practices to further promote dune growth and protection for adjacent coastal infrastructure19. The uniform spacing of vegetation planted on managed dunes does not reflect the inherent variability and patchiness of natural, unmanaged dune plant communities20,21. These projects may result in plant community composition and living and non-living biomass distributions that do not mimic natural dunes.

To date, studies of belowground dune composition document that most roots occur in the top 30 cm of soil22,23. Accordingly, the majority of belowground biomass measurements in coastal dunes focus on biomass in the top 30–60 cm of soil and are collected using pit excavation or small, manually-collected cores23–25 (e.g., auger, slide hammer, etc.). Shallow sampling of belowground biomass in coastal dunes may underestimate deep roots (i.e., located > 60 cm beneath the soil surface), which are ubiquitous in ecosystems characterized by water and temperature stress26, like dunes. Past research on rooting depth in arid and desert regions demonstrates the extent to which roots will penetrate soil and sediment to reach groundwater, a more reliable source of moisture in systems that experience infrequent precipitation26 and low nutrient availability27. In the context of coastal foredunes, access to the water table and sparse available nutrients may necessitate the growth of relatively deep (> 60 cm depth) root systems. Deep roots would influence the erosion of a vegetated dune through attenuation of wave energy when deep roots are exposed through erosion by wave collision10,15.

Although belowground biomass in dunes has been quantified at relatively shallow depths (e.g., ≲ 60 cm), it remains unclear how belowground biomass is distributed at greater depths and how it changes across elevations and among dunes with different management histories. Here, we present belowground biomass measurements (up to 150 cm) across the dune profile in the Northern Outer Banks, North Carolina, U.S.A. (Fig. 1, Figure S1) to extend the vertical coverage of earlier studies. Our objectives were to determine the effects of management history on (1) dune belowground biotic structure and aboveground community composition and (2) to determine what geomorphological and biological variables best predicted belowground biomass. We hypothesized that (1) unmanaged dunes would have more belowground biomass, more complex root structure and higher species richness as compared to managed dunes. We also hypothesized that (2) belowground biomass would increase with elevation across a dune profile and with vegetation abundance. Elevation was chosen as a variable of interest because it can be used as a proxy for distance from shoreline and distance above the water table, which both dictate vegetation growth28. Results

Dune belowground structure

Management interventions altered the amount of belowground biomass in dunes. Total belowground biomass was highest in unmanaged dunes (F = 33.3, p <0.0001), decreased with depth (F = 14.8, p <0.0001) and increased with elevation (Fig. 2). Total biomass was similar in managed and unmanaged dunes across the top 90 cm of soil and declined at 91–120 cm and 121–150 cm depths in managed dunes. When partitioned into the living and non-living components, the same trends were observed: unmanaged dunes contained the highest living and non-living biomass concentrations (living: F = 20.2, p <0.0001; non-living: F = 16.9, p <0.0001), and biomass decreased with depth (living: F = 4.6, p = 0.002; non-living: F = 8.3, p <0.0001) and increased with elevation (Tables S1, S2). Despite the presence of both living and non-living belowground biomass, soil organic matter content was low. Soil organic matter content was higher in managed dunes (0.42 ± 0.06%; F = 4.5, p = 0.035) relative to unmanaged dunes (0.31 ± 0.03%). Soil organic matter content did not differ by depth. Soil %N was low (< 0.02%) across sites and depth classes with no significant differences found. Vegetative cover and community composition

Management history (T = -12.11, p < 0.001) had significant effects on species composition (Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling stress = 15.3, Figure S4). When visualized with NMS, managed dunes had less variation in species composition than unmanaged dunes. Vegetative cover did not differ by management history, but differed across sites (F6, 140 = 3.62, p = 0.002, Figure S5a) and increased with elevation (F3, 140 = 47.3, p < 0.001, Figure S5b). Management did not affect species richness, but species richness differed based on site (F6, 140 = 5.73, p < 0.001) and increased with elevation (F3, 140 = 72.7, p < 0.0001). Vegetative cover increased with species richness (r2 = 0.69, p < 0.001; Fig. 4) across all community composition plots. Dune species composition

Sixty-seven percent of the coring plots were composed of >1 species. Summary of the dominant species at each study location (Table S4) is based on the community composition plots spanning the dune. Forty-three percent of these plots had species richness > 1 with only 3% of these plots occurring on the dune toe. Dune toe plots at all sites were dominated by 1–2 species, typically the annual forb Cakile edentula, or a dominant dune grass (e.g., Spartina patens, Uniola paniculata, Ammophila breviligulata). These dominant dune grasses and Panicum amarum were common on the dune face across the study region. Managed sites (which are frequently planted) tended to be dominated by Ammophila breviligulata. The plant community on the dune face at FRF South was unique relative to that position at other sites: here, the shrub Iva imbricata and the invasive sedge Carex kobomugi dominated. Forbs and lianas emerged as dominant species at the dune crest across all sites. Although it is known that belowground biomass differs based on species identity11, we were unable to determine species identity from our belowground biomass sampling due to the occurrence of multiple species in each plot.

Discussion

In the coming decades, coastal areas will be subjected to stronger and more frequent storms and rising sea levels, particularly along coastlines surrounding the northern Atlantic Ocean8,29. Quantifying how and why the internal architecture (i.e., biomass, sedimentology) varies within coastal foredunes is vital to maximizing their utility as protective features. Such insights may become key parameters for future numerical models and machine learning techniques which are being actively applied as predictive tools to forecast coastal morphological changes30. Previous research on coastal dunes focused on the effects of succession and individual species on living belowground biomass11,22,24, but a knowledge gap remains about how belowground biotic composition and structure vary among dunes with different management history. Here we demonstrate that total belowground biomass (living and non-living) is distributed across the upper 150 cm of dune sediment, a depth greater than those commonly studied22,31. Management history, depth and elevation are important contributors to the distribution of living and non-living belowground biomass of a given section of dune. Management reduces variation in vegetative composition and cover, variables which were found to be predictors of belowground biomass. Vegetative cover, elevation, management, species richness and sedimentologic metrics (i.e., grain size and sorting) explained >80% of variation in belowground biomass, demonstrating the importance of these variables for structuring internal biotic material within dunes.

Vegetative cover and species richness were predictive variables in multiple top models of total belowground biomass. Vegetative cover had high predictive power of the belowground biomass within the dune (55% for living biomass and 54% for total biomass) and may aid management by providing a method for rapid estimation of belowground biomass. Further, the relationship between species richness and vegetative cover across dunes sampled in this study suggests that higher dune vegetation diversity may increase belowground biomass. Community composition differences in managed dunes relative to those not actively managed in >5 years is likely influenced by artificial plantings that tend to focus on fewer species (S. Cross, Senior Planner, Town of Duck, NC, personal communication, 2021). Considering these findings, coastal managers may seek to maximize species composition on the dune, thereby increasing belowground biomass and, consequently, dune resilience.

The amount and composition of living and non-living belowground biomass is an important feature of coastal dunes, as it plays a role in prevention of, and recovery from, wave erosion erosion15,32. The presence of exposed vegetation (both above- and belowground components) can provide resistance to erosion during storms6,13. For example, living and non-living belowground biomass can be exposed when vertical dune scarps form during storms33. The newly exposed biomass can reduce the volume of dune sediment eroded by further wave action as compared with bare sand sand13,15. Because scarps can be several meters high34, the depth of root penetration and the presence of biomass may play an important role in magnitude and style of potential erosion under the collisional wave regime. Specifically, the more limited biomass at deeper depths below the surface (>1 m) in managed dunes, relative to their unmanaged counterparts (e.g., Fig. 2a), suggests that once scarped, newly planted dunes may be more susceptible to rapid retreat due to increased erodibility of those sediments.

Following a storm event, both exposed living belowground biomass and vegetation located landward of the escarpment can act as reservoirs of living plant material. This vegetation can expand (e.g., through stem, leaf and rhizome lengthening) in response to the input of sediment through slumping and avalanching along the front of the dune, or through aeolian sediment transport from the adjacent beach14,35. Dune vegetation at the edge and base of an escarpment colonizes avalanched sediment and facilitates recovery of the dune, provided an adequate sediment supply and low disturbance frequency. This process is mediated by species-specific differences in burial response and lateral growth36,37; however, the existing plant community is itself influenced by management (e.g., through planting a species with a specific suite of functional traits) and elevation21.

In contrast to previous studies which sampled to 40 cm depth22, we found that living belowground biomass was consistent throughout at least the top 90 cm of the dune profile. Past studies which limited sampling to the upper 40–60 cm of sediment from the sediment surface22,24, therefore, likely underestimated dune belowground biomass. Considering the erosion-preventing and dune-recovery capabilities of plant biomass in coastal foredunes, researchers and land managers predicting dune response to storms require the improved estimates of belowground biomass provided by deeper sampling.

Biomass declined below 90 cm and root tissue density was also reduced with depth. Higher living biomass within the upper 90 cm of soil and higher root tissue density indicates plant foraging of shallow moisture generated by episodic precipitation events but overall lower soil moisture availability, whereas deeper belowground biomass and lower root tissue density indicates foraging of moisture closer to the water table38. Plants in arid, non-dune habitats are known to source water from both near the soil surface, where precipitation events periodically increase available moisture, and deep within the soil where groundwater is accessible39,40. Lower specific root length in managed dunes reflects the coarser grain size and lower biomass in these dunes, creating an overall environment with lower soil moisture-holding capacity13,41.

The observed role of elevation on controlling multiple variables associated with dune resilience and ecology (e.g., living and non-living belowground biomass, vegetative cover and species richness) highlights the close coupling between geomorphology and plant biology. Elevation, distance from shoreline, beach slope and vegetation in the backshore moderate the extent of wave run-up and collision at the dune42. Depending on the morphology of the nearshore (e.g., beach width and slope), beach and dune plants at lower elevations are exposed to more frequent disturbance, altering the plant community and successional stage43. At our study sites, the presence of bare sediment, low vegetative cover and pioneer species such as Cakile edentula at the dune toe typify early successional stages of the dune plant community. Increased vegetative cover and species richness, and the presence of lianas and shrubs at the dune crest/back, indicate longer periods of post-disturbance stability and later successional stages43, even among managed sites. Spatial heterogeneity in aboveground community composition due to elevation has consequences for belowground biomass distribution as species differ in belowground allocation and structure11,15,31, resource acquisition43, disturbance response37,44 and biotic interactions21,45. Both along- and cross-shore community composition heterogeneity may result in alongshore variability in dune erosion response due to varying species abundances and differences in the quantity and characteristics of plant biomass components11,31. The presence and functional traits of dune vegetation are well-established determinants of erosion1,6,10. Our results suggest that common management interventions (beach nourishment, dune construction, sand fencing, dune vegetation plantings) influence these determinants, although the mechanism of influence was not demonstrated in these results.

Our study shows that species composition varies across the dune profile, thereby influencing the distribution of living belowground biomass and fine root surface area within a dune. Of our sampled sites, one (FRF South) had extremely high living belowground biomass and fine root surface area due to high vegetative cover of the invasive plant Carex kobomugi. This non-native sedge has higher root:shoot ratio and living belowground biomass than native Ammophila breviligulata, a characteristic that likely contributed to lower erosion rates at Carex kobomugi-dominated sites compared to those dominated by Ammophila breviligulata during Hurricane Sandy in New Jersey24. Although erosion resistance by an invasive species with high living belowground biomass may be attractive to coastal managers and homeowners, care must be taken when weighing the ecosystem services provided by non-native dune vegetation against native flora46. Non-native and invasive species typically out-compete native species, reducing native plant richness and negatively impacting native biota46,47. Our results show that higher species richness is related to increased vegetative cover and belowground biomass, thus, reducing biodiversity on dunes may have negative effects on erosion resistance6,48.

The relationship observed in our study between total vegetative cover and living belowground biomass has potential implications for future coastal management, including the ability to develop new parameters of erosion resistance provided by vegetation into predictive numerical modeling frameworks. Future work could help to improve formulations within process-based morphodynamic models that aim to synthesize the complex interaction between vegetation, waves and sediment transport during storms to better inform how these above- and belowground biomass effects may enhance dune stability in the context of rising sea levels. Although we found that multiple metrics predicted belowground biomass (e.g., management, elevation, living cover, species richness and sedimentology), vegetative cover was a good predictor of belowground biomass. Vegetative cover was likely a greater predictor of belowground biomass than aboveground biomass due to different resource allocation strategies and diverse plant communities in the region11. Notably, numerous process-based coastal dune evolution models that account for wind-blown sediment transport use vegetation cover in functions related to shear stress reduction and trapping of sediment by vegetation49. These existing predictive tools therefore may provide a platform to similarly include ecological effects on wave-driven erosion rates, through incorporation of empirical equations into subaerial dune tools50 or through extension of coupled marine-aeolian-ecological tools51,52, by incorporating defined relationships between aboveground vegetative cover and belowground properties.

This link is further supported by our finding that vegetative cover increased with higher species richness. Site differences in vegetative cover and species richness, as well as management effects on community composition demonstrates the need for increased belowground sampling of coastal dunes to depths > 30 cm to thoroughly quantify belowground biomass and assess the universality of these findings. However, our finding that vegetative cover and elevation accounted for > 80% of the variation in belowground biomass provides an opportunity to relate spatial heterogeneity in vegetative properties to observed dune erodibility.

Finally, although this work utilized manual vegetation surveys, vegetative cover can be quantified via remote sensing (e.g., UAV and LiDAR surveys) combined with data-analysis technologies such as machine learning. These methods can allow for rapid, relatively inexpensive vegetation mapping, as well as three-dimensional topographic measurements53. These methods are also less invasive, as they do not require the deployment of machinery or technicians directly onto a dune to estimate vegetative cover, reducing the impact to sensitive aboveground and belowground plant structures. Future research investigating relationships between remote estimations of vegetative cover and living belowground biomass can provide quick and inexpensive approaches for assessing the protective function of different dunes.

Conclusions

In view of future climate changes, protective coastal dunes in some regions will be subjected to rising sea levels and more frequent and intense storms. Our study is an important step toward characterizing managed and unmanaged dunes subject to increased rates of sea-level rise and beach erosion. We found that dune management significantly impacted living and non-living belowground biomass and root structure within the top 150 cm of sediment and dune plant community composition. Elevation had a significant effect on multiple above- and belowground variables (e.g., biomass, vegetative cover, species richness), likely as a result of fluctuating abiotic stressors (e.g., aeolian deposition, depth to water table, salinity) and stage of succession across a dune profile in response to erosion and recovery. Importantly, we found linkages between vegetative cover, elevation, and belowground biomass, which may be used to rapidly estimate belowground biomass from surface measurements in the future. Numerical modeling, expanded sampling of belowground biomass patterns and continued surveillance of dune plant community changes will be necessary to understand how these systems respond to sea-level rise and climate change to develop best management practices for maintenance of the ecosystem services they provide.

Methods

Study locations

This study focused on a 35 km stretch of beach and dune in the Outer Banks of North Carolina, which encompasses coastal dunes with various management histories (Fig. 1). The northern Outer Banks is a net-erosive barrier island system, although communities in this region conduct beach nourishment projects every five years16. Beaches in this region are intermediate with variable widths and slopes54,55 (Table 3). Dunes in the northern Outer Banks are generally characterized by a continuous foredune ridge fronted by beaches that can vary in width depending on local sedimentology and nourishment practices. There is no wide, embryonic dune field, as is common in net-accretionary systems, at any of our study sites. Overwash is atypical in the northern Outer Banks, although it does occur during very severe storms in areas south of our study sites, which are generally characterized by low and/or discontinuous dunes56. Coordinates and elevation of the dune surface at the location of each sampling plot were collected with a Real-Time Kinematic (RTK)-GPS surveying system. Topographic profiles were collected from the dune heel to the beach face at the time of coring. The maximum curvature approach was used to calculate the dune toe position in pybeach package in Python3,57. Beach slope and beach width were calculated using an endpoint method with the dune toe and mean high-water shoreline (0.36 m [NOAA tide gauge, Duck, NC – Station ID: 8651370]) set as the landward and seaward limits, respectively41,58.

Belowground sampling and processing

Sediment vibracores (n = 15 managed, 10 unmanaged) were collected through vertical insertion of a 3-m long, 7.5-cm diameter, thin-walled aluminum pipe into the dune using vibration produced by a cement vibrator head attached to the core pipe (reviewed in59). We collected these cores between September and December 2020 at the dune toe, dune face, crest and back along a single transect at each site (n = 3–4 cores for belowground biomass per site; Fig. 1, Figure S1).

Cores were stored at 4 °C and processed within one week of collection to prevent root degradation. Cores were bisected longitudinally and the sediment from each half was segmented into 30 cm sections from the ground surface (i.e., 0–30 cm, 31–60 cm, 61–90 cm, 91–120 cm, 121–150 cm), resulting in 1325 cm3 of sample volume per section. Sediment samples were bagged and frozen until further processing. To separate belowground biomass, core sections were wet sieved using stacked 3.36 mm, 1.0 mm and 0.5 mm mesh-size sieves. Living belowground biomass was characterized as the sample portions with roots, rhizomes and belowground stems that were still flexible and did not exhibit signs of decomposition. All other biotic material was classified as non-living biomass (e.g., decayed plant material, twigs, seeds, wrack).

Within the living belowground biomass component, live roots were separated from other belowground structures (rhizomes, belowground-stems) and scanned using an Epson Perfection V800 Photo electric scanner calibrated for image analysis with WinRhizo Pro 2019a by Regent Instruments (Regent Instruments Inc, Quebec City, Quebec, Canada). Images were analyzed to quantify root surface area by diameter size class. Fine roots were defined as those with < 1 mm diameter60. All living and non-living belowground biomass was oven-dried at 60 °C for 72 hours and weighed. All biomass variables were scaled to g m-2 at each depth for comparison to other systems. Specific root length and root tissue density were calculated from the WinRHIZO scans and dried biomass. Specific root length is the ratio of root length to biomass and root tissue density is the ratio of root biomass to root volume for a given sample.

Soil organic matter content was quantified by loss-on-ignition following manual removal of roots. Samples (1 g of sediment from each core section) were baked in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 5 hours and reweighed to estimate soil organic matter content (%). Aliquots (300 mg) of each core segment were submitted to the Cornell Stable Isotope Laboratory for % carbon (C) and % nitrogen (N) analysis. Due to cost, only the top 90 cm of soils were sent for C and N analysis.

Internal dune sedimentology and stratigraphy were characterized from replicate cores at each location of biomass sampling. All sediment cores were split, photographed, described for sedimentary structures, texture (as compared to standards), mineralogy and color61, and sampled for analysis of grain size and grain shape. Samples were collected at a minimum of 10 cm intervals and averaged in 30 cm increments for comparison with biological sampling. Sediment size and shape were analyzed from oven-dried samples using a CAMSIZERX2. Grain-size statistics were calculated using the Graphic Method62,63. Median D50 (mm and Φ), sorting (Φ), skew and kurtosis were used to characterize sedimentology. D50 is the particle diameter, in mm and Φ (a logarithmic sediment size scale64), representing the 50% cumulative percentile particle diameter in the distribution of sediment grain sizes. Skew and kurtosis also describe that distribution. Sorting (Φ) represents the variation in sediment grain sizes of a sample. Sedimentology is described and further analyzed in Davis et al.41.

Aboveground sampling and processing

Vegetation surveys were conducted during Summer 2021 (Figure S6). We established vegetation survey plots (0.25 m2 plot size) centered on each exact coring location. These plots will henceforth be referred to as “coring plots”. We also established additional cross-shore vegetation survey transects (n = 7–16, depending on the alongshore extent of dune accessible for sampling) to characterize the full species composition at the study sites (Figures S1, S6). Additional cross-shore vegetation survey transects were spaced in 10-m intervals alongshore, north and south of the original coring transect. Along each additional vegetation survey transect we quantified community composition in 0.25 m2 plots spaced every 5 m across the shore-perpendicular dune profile. These additional vegetation survey plots are hereafter referred to as “community composition plots”. Coordinates and elevation of community composition plots were collected with a Trimble RTK-GPS. In coring plots and community composition plots, vegetative cover (estimated as a visual percentage of surface area within 0.25 m2 plot), stem count and height were collected for each species. Cover of bare ground and dead plant material were also estimated in each plot using the same visual estimate described above. Adjacent to the coring plots only, aboveground biomass (within a 0.1 x 1 m plot) was collected to complement belowground biomass sampling at all sites except Duck, where grasses had been manually planted and permission to harvest aboveground biomass was not granted to maintain the vegetation on the dune face. Aboveground biomass was oven-dried at 60 °C for 72 hours, weighed and scaled to g m−2.

Importance values for each species were calculated to determine the dominant species across the dune profile at each site based on multiple characteristics that influence sediment dynamics. Relative stem density (stem count within coring and community composition plots), relative vegetative cover and relative frequency were summed to calculate the importance value of each species present. Importance values were calculated at each dune position within each site using data collected from community composition plots.

Statistical analysis

The following variables were log+1 transformed to satisfy assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance for analyses and account for zeros in data: total belowground biomass, non-living belowground biomass, living belowground biomass, fine root surface area, specific root length, root tissue density, aboveground biomass, soil organic matter content, soil %N, soil carbon, %C and vegetative cover.

Above and belowground analysis from coring plots

We analyzed belowground and aboveground variables collected at the coring plots using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) with elevation as a covariate. The following belowground variables were assessed for differences among depth (i.e., 0–30 cm, 31–60 cm, 61–90 cm, 91–120 cm, 121–150 cm) and management history: total belowground biomass, living belowground biomass, non-living belowground biomass, fine root surface area, specific root length, root tissue density, soil organic matter content, soil %N and soil %C. Differences in management history and site were assessed for species richness and vegetative cover from coring plots and community composition plots. Post-hoc tests for significant differences among depths were analyzed with Tukey Honest Significant Difference test and between management history with Student’s T-test. Results of ANCOVA are reported with the F statistic and the p-value.

Species composition community analysis

Multiple Response Permutation Procedure (MRPP; using Bray-Curtis Distance) was used to determine multivariate differences among shore-perpendicular dune positions, sites and management histories for species composition at coring plots and community composition plots. Post-hoc multiple pairwise comparisons were assessed for significance with a Bonferroni corrected alpha value based on the number of comparisons. Differences in species composition were visualized with Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling ordination (NMS)65. All multivariate analyses were performed in PC-ORD version 7 (PCA, PC-ORD software v. 7.0, MJM Software Design, Gleneden Beach, Oregon, USA).

Predictors of belowground biomass

Multiple regression was used to determine the most significant factors determining belowground biomass (living and non-living) in the top 150 cm of soil. Management history, elevation, vegetative cover, species richness and sedimentologic metrics (Median D50 (mm), ΦD50, sorting, skew and kurtosis) were used as predictor variables. Aboveground biomass was excluded as a term due to lack of data at one managed site. We analyzed all possible models and used Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to determine top models. Simple linear regressions were used to visualize top predictors of belowground biomass. An alpha value ɑ = 0.05 was used for all univariate statistical analyses which were carried out in R Studio (R Core Team, R version 4.1.0).
