Physical crowding in 3D niche regulates neural stem cell differentiation via Cx43-mediated gap junction communication
Authors: Yuyun Liang, Xiaonan Wang, Chaoyong He, Zhiyan Wu, Jing Yang, Ziyan Chen, Hexu Zhang, Cheng Zhao, Liyang Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeural stem cells (NSCs) maintain central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis through self-renewal and differentiation into neurons and glia. Although physical crowding shapes the NSC niche during CNS development, its role in fate determination remains poorly understood. We investigated how NSC crowding influences intercellular junctions and lineage specification in 2D and 3D environments. While crowding promotes neuronal differentiation in both environments, robust junctional remodeling occurred only in 3D. Specifically, 3D crowding uniquely upregulated connexin 43 (Cx43)-mediated gap-junction assembly. Pharmacological Cx43 inhibition selectively attenuated 3D crowding-induced neuronal differentiation, demonstrating that gap-junction signaling is essential for fate determination in 3D. These findings highlight that the regulatory influence of NSC crowding is dimension-dependent and mediated through Cx43 gap-junction communication. By elucidating how biophysical context integrates with intercellular signaling to guide NSC behavior, this study offers mechanistic insights into stem cell biology and informs biomimetic 3D culture systems and regenerative strategies for neural tissue repair.

Introduction

Neural stem cells (NSCs) have an impressive ability to self-renew and differentiate into a variety of neurons and glial cells, which are crucial for both the development and maintenance of the central nervous system (CNS).1,2 During CNS development and maturation, NSCs undergo dynamic changes influenced by their surrounding environment, particularly shifts in cell crowding, which refers to the density and spatial arrangement of cells within their specialized niche.3 In the early stages of CNS development, NSCs undergo symmetric divisions to increase their numbers, significantly raising cell density in the embryonic niche.4 This increased crowding acts as an important physical signal during this phase. As the CNS matures, NSC density markedly declines in neurogenic regions such as the subventricular zone,5 and further decreases with aging in the hippocampus.6 Although these spatiotemporal patterns of cell crowding are well-documented, their functional implications for NSC fate determination remain incompletely understood.

Cell-cell junctions, including tight, gap, and adherens junctions, play a critical role in facilitating communication and coordination between cells.7,8,9 These junctions are essential for maintaining the normal physiological processes of the CNS, preserving tissue integrity, and supporting its function and stability.9 For example, during CNS development, NSCs express key tight junction proteins, such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin, and claudins (specifically claudin-1, -3, -5, and -12), which help regulate the clustering of NSCs and guide their differentiation.10 Gap junctions, on the other hand, rely on proteins called connexins and structures known as hemichannels to enable direct intercellular communication.11 In neurons, connexins are critical for forming electrical synapses and supporting synaptic plasticity.12 In astrocytes, connexins contribute to cell adhesion, energy metabolism, and processes linked to neurodegeneration.12 N-cadherin-based adherens junctions play a central role in regulating the maintenance, proliferation, and differentiation of neural progenitors,13 and in vitro studies further show that the HAVDI motif of N-cadherin supports both NSC stemness and neurogenesis.14,15 These cell junctions are crucial for understanding NSC function in CNS development.16 Therefore, investigating their formation provides valuable insights into NSC behavior within the cell crowding niche.

According to previous studies, changes in cell density per unit area induce the cell-crowding niche in two-dimensional (2D) culture plates, driving spatiotemporal lineage specification in stem cell epithelium.17,18 For NSCs, cell density regulates the expression of Hippo, NOTCH, and WNT signaling genes, effectively mediating cell fate decisions.19 Although these studies demonstrate the impact of cell density on the regulation of NSC fates, the cells were cultured in 2D conditions, which differ significantly from the three-dimensional (3D) microenvironment of NSC niches. Compared to 2D culture, 3D extracellular environments provide cells with adhesion, topographical, mechanical, and soluble cues from all directions, rather than just the X-Y plane.20 Many studies have shown that cell fates in 2D cultures can become senescent or phenotypically aberrant, particularly in contexts relevant to pathology and stem cell biology.21,22,23,24 Mechanical cues are essential regulators of NSC behavior, as NSCs are highly mechanosensitive. For example, Piezo1-mediated stiffness sensing modulates the regenerative capacity of oligodendrocyte precursor cells,25 and neural differentiation is strongly favored on compliant hydrogels that approximate the softness of brain tissue.26 Hydrogels, as polymeric networks formed through physical and/or chemical crosslinking, can recapitulate the structural and mechanical characteristics of the natural extracellular matrix, making them well-suited for supporting NSCs in 3D culture. For example, elastin-like protein hydrogels have been used to encapsulate NSCs in 3D, regulating their differentiation capacity and maintaining stemness based on the properties of the cell niche.27,28 Human-induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived NSCs have been cultured inside hydrogels, where they differentiate into neurons and astrocytes and exhibit spontaneous calcium signaling.29 Therefore, hydrogels provide ideal 3D culture platforms for investigating the effects of physical crowding cues on cellular behavior through cell packing density.

In this study, we investigated the effects of cell crowding on the formation of connections and differentiation of NSCs under both 2D and 3D culture conditions. Using RNA sequencing, RT-qPCR, western blotting, and immunofluorescence, we quantified NSC differentiation and found that high cell density promotes neuronal differentiation in both 2D and 3D systems. Notably, only in 3D cultures did NSCs exhibit robust upregulation of connexin 43 (Cx43), the principal gap junction protein in the CNS, under crowded conditions. Pharmacological inhibition of Cx43-mediated gap junction formation significantly attenuated NSC differentiation, underscoring its functional role in crowding-dependent fate determination. Together, our findings highlight the critical importance of 3D culture context in shaping NSC responses to cell crowding and provide mechanistic insights into how microenvironmental constraints regulate stem cell fate through intercellular communication.

Results

NSC differentiation induced by crowding in a 2D environment is independent of cell junctions

NSCs were harvested from the cerebral cortex of newborn Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, suspended them in proliferation medium, and stained for stemness markers (Nestin and Sox2) before digesting the cell spheres (Figure S1). To investigate the impact of cell crowding on NSC behavior in 2D culture, NSCs were cultured at two different cell densities (L-2D group, 0.05 million/mL; H-2D group, 0.25 million/mL) in differentiation medium for 7 days, and transcriptome sequencing was then performed (Figure 1A). Principal component analysis (PCA) successfully distinguished between the two groups, demonstrating high internal consistency within each (Figure 1B). Specifically, the H-2D group exhibited 1,278 upregulated and 821 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to the L-2D group (Figure 1C). The heatmap of DEGs highlighted significant shifts in the transcriptomic profile of NSCs regulated by cell crowding in 2D culture (Figure 1D). We further analyzed the pathways and functions of these DEGs using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses. KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of the “neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction” and “calcium signaling pathway” in both groups. Notably, pathways related to the extracellular matrix (“glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-heparan sulfate/heparin” and “ECM-receptor interaction”) as well as intercellular communication (“cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction”) were enriched in the L-2D group (Figure 1E). In contrast, pathways related to synaptic function, such as “GABAergic synapse,” “glutamatergic synapse” and “cholinergic synapse” were enriched in the H-2D group (Figure 1E). The heatmap of bulk RNA-seq data shows the differential expression of marker genes associated with immature neurons, mature neurons, and astrocytes (Figure S2C). The reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) results were consistent with the RNA-seq results (Figure 1F). Additionally, we used western blotting and immunofluorescence staining to assess NSC differentiation at the protein level. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed these findings: Expression of the neuronal marker Tuj-1 was significantly increased in the H-2D group compared with the L-2D group, whereas the expression of the astrocytic marker GFAP remained unchanged (Figures 1G–1I). These results suggest that cell crowding up-regulates neuronal differentiation and down-regulates astrocyte differentiation at the gene and protein levels in the 2D environment. Next, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to examine the expression of genes related to gap junctions, tight junctions, and adherens junctions. However, none of these pathways reached significant enrichment (Figure 1J). These findings suggest that while cell crowding affects the fate of NSC differentiation in a 2D environment, it does not regulate the expression of cell-cell junctions during NSC differentiation.

Characterization of the 3D hydrogel niche for NSC culture

The kinetics of fibrillogenesis were monitored using turbidity measurements at 310 nm (Figure 2B). Both 0.2% and 0.5% (w/v) Col concentrations followed a characteristic three-phase self-assembly profile: (I) a lag phase corresponding to fibril nucleation (0–5 min); (II) a rapid fibril growth phase (5–20 min); and (III) a plateau phase marking fibril maturation (>20 min).31 It was demonstrated that the gelation of collagen with different concentrations is in the same self-assembly process. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy further confirmed Col Ⅰ structural transitions, where the triple-helical signature—characterized by a negative peak at 198 nm and a positive peak at 221 nm—became enhanced and slightly shifted upon gelation, indicating fibril formation (Figure 2C).32

Rheological analyses demonstrated concentration-dependent mechanical properties. Time-sweep measurements showed faster gelation at higher collagen concentrations (Figure 2D), while frequency-sweep experiments indicated that both hydrogels possessed soft, brain-like mechanics, with storage moduli (G′) ranging from ∼40 Pa (0.2%) to ∼190 Pa (0.5%) (Figure 2E), consistent with the stiffness of native neural tissue.33

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a well-organized, porous fibrillar network (Figure 2F). Quantitative pore-size analysis showed a significant decrease in pore diameter with increasing collagen concentration, reflecting a denser matrix architecture. Such interconnected porosity supports efficient nutrient diffusion and cell-matrix interactions.

Finally, both hydrogel formulations exhibited excellent structural stability over 7 days in culture, retaining 80–95% residual mass and showing minimal loss of mechanical integrity (Figures 2G and 2H). These findings confirm that the Col Ⅰ hydrogels provide a robust, physiologically relevant, and stable 3D niche suitable for long-term NSC culture.

Influence of collagen concentration and cell density on the viability of 3D-cultured NSCs

To establish an optimal 3D cell crowding model for NSCs, we investigated the effects of Col concentration and cell density on NSC viability and morphology. Single cells were dissociated from NSC neurospheres, suspended in Col solutions, and packed in 3D hydrogel networks (Figure 3A). After being encapsulated in hydrogels, NSCs were transferred into differentiation medium for 7 days of differentiation. Live/dead staining revealed that higher Col concentrations resulted in a greater number of dead cells, while the proportion of live cells declined (Figure 3B). Quantitative analysis of live cell ratios at 1 and 7 days showed that NSC viability was maintained at 80–85% in 0.2% w/v Col hydrogels, whereas it dropped to approximately 70% in 0.4% and 0.5% w/v hydrogels (Figure 3C). To further examine the effects of cell density on NSC viability, we cultured cells at four different densities (0.75, 1.5, 3, and 6 million cells/mL) within a 0.2% w/v Col hydrogel (Figure 3A). Live/dead staining showed that cell viability remained above 75% when the density ranged from 0.75 to 3 million cells/mL (Figure 3D). However, at 6 million cells/mL, viability decreased from 77% to 67% over the 7-day culture period (Figures 3D and 3E). Quantitative analysis confirmed that higher cell densities led to a significant reduction in cell viability, with the most pronounced effect observed at the highest density (Figure 3E). These findings suggest that excessive cell crowding negatively impacts the survival of NSCs. Based on these results, subsequent 3D cell crowding experiments were conducted within a cell density range of 0.75–3 million cells/mL to minimize the effects of cell death on experimental outcomes. Cell numbers remained unchanged before and after differentiation in both 2D and 3D cultures, ruling out proliferation and confirming that the observed crowding effect is solely a function of initial seeding density (Figure S3).

NSC crowding in a 3D environment regulates transcriptional expression

To explore the impact of cell crowding on the transcriptional profile of NSCs in the 3D environment, we encapsulated NSCs at low (L group, 0.75 million/mL), medium (M group, 1.5 million/mL), and high (H group, 3 million/mL) densities within Col hydrogels. The cells were cultured for 7 days under differentiation conditions, after which RNA-seq was performed to analyze widespread transcriptional changes (Figure 4A). Four biological replicates were sequenced for each group, and gene expression levels were normalized using counts per million (CPM) (Table S1). PCA demonstrated high consistency among replicates within each group (Figure S4A). Furthermore, correlation coefficients between replicates exceeded 0.95, indicating strong intra-group reproducibility (Figure S4B). To identify DEGs associated with NSC crowding, we performed pairwise comparisons between the L, M, and H groups (Figure 4B and Table S2). Compared to the L group, the H group exhibited 1,725 upregulated and 948 downregulated DEGs. In the H versus M comparison, 549 genes were significantly upregulated, while 293 genes were downregulated. Additionally, 699 upregulated and 359 downregulated DEGs were identified when comparing the M and L groups. To further examine cell-crowding-dependent gene expression patterns, DEGs were categorized into five clusters using K-means clustering (Figure 4D, Tables S3 and S4). Notably, genes in Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 showed strong negative and positive correlations with cell crowding, respectively. KEGG analysis was then performed on these two clusters to identify associated pathways and functions (Figure 4E and Table S5). KEGG analysis revealed that the “neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction” and “calcium signaling pathway” were enriched in both clusters. However, Cluster 2 was uniquely associated with signaling pathways such as “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “MAPK signaling,” “TGF-β signaling,” and “Hippo signaling.” In contrast, Cluster 3 was predominantly enriched in pathways related to neuronal synapses and cell adhesion, including “gap junction,” “glutamatergic synapse,” “cell adhesion molecules,” “dopaminergic synapse,” “tight junction,” “ECM-receptor interaction,” and “focal adhesion.” Further GO analysis of Cluster 3 identified terms closely associated with neurogenesis and cell contacts, including “axon guidance,” “positive regulation of neurogenesis,” “axonogenesis,” “tight junction,” “actin-based cell projection,” “presynaptic membrane,” “postsynaptic membrane,” “neuron-to-neuron synapse,” and “actin binding” (Figure S4C). Cell crowding regulates NSC differentiation and cell junction formation

To investigate neural differentiation in three-dimensional cell crowding, we performed gene and protein level validation. Gene expression heatmaps showed that associated gene expression patterns in immature and mature neurons were positively correlated with cell crowding (Figure S4D). In contrast, the expression of genes related to astrocytic differentiation remained largely unchanged across different density conditions (Figure S4D). To validate these findings at the protein level, we performed Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining. Quantitative analysis revealed a density-dependent increase in Tuj-1-positive neuronal cells, whereas GFAP-positive astrocytes showed no significant difference across groups (Figures 5A and 5B). Western blot results were consistent with the immunofluorescence trends (Figure 5C). Building on the observed transcriptional changes related to cell-cell junctions, we conducted GSEA for gap junctions, tight junctions, and adherens junctions in NSCs differentiated at varying cell densities in a 3D environment. Significant enrichment was observed in the gap junction and tight junction gene sets, whereas adherens junction genes showed no significant changes (Figure 5D). To examine how cell density affects NSC morphology, we performed confocal 3D reconstruction imaging. NSCs cultured at high densities displayed extensive multidirectional spreading and interconnected cellular networks, particularly after 7 days of 3D culture (Figure 5E). Quantification of the circularity index, a measure of cell shape, revealed that in high-density cultures, the index decreased from 0.44 on day 1 to 0.33 on day 3 and further to 0.19 on day 7. In contrast, NSCs in the low-density condition retained a more rounded morphology, with a circularity index of 0.80 at day 1 and 0.54 on day 7 (Figure 5F). To examine the transcriptional upregulation of intercellular junction genes, we visualized the DEGs associated with intercellular junctions (Figure S5A and Table S6). The heatmap showed significant alterations in multiple gap junction-related genes (e.g., Itpr2, Prkcb, Prkcg, and Tuba1a) and tight junction-related genes (e.g., Cldn3, Cldn11, Cldn14, and Tjp3). Gja1 encodes the core gap-junction protein Cx43, whereas Itpr2 and Prkcg are included not as structural junctional proteins but as functionally coupled regulators: IP3R modulates intracellular Ca2+ flux that gates gap-junction permeability, and PKC phosphorylates Cx43 at Ser368 to modulate channel conductance.34 To more sensitively detect expression differences of key genes involved in junction formation, we needed to focus on the genes that encode junctional proteins (e.g., Gja1, Cldn3, Cldn11, and Tjp3) and key downstream signaling molecules. RT-qPCR was subsequently conducted to verify the expression of gap junction-related genes (Gja1, Itpr2, and Prkcg) and tight junction-related genes (Cldn3, Cldn11, and Tjp3) across L-, M-, and H-density groups. Compared with the L group, the H group exhibited significantly increased expression of Gja1, Itpr2, Prkcg, Cldn3, and Cldn11; the M group exhibited significantly increased expression of Itpr2 and Prkcg (Figure 5G). Compared with the M group, the H group significantly increased the expression of Gja1 and Cldn3 (Figure 5G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that cell crowding in 3D culture plays a critical role in regulating cell junction formation and driving NSCs toward neuronal differentiation.

Cell crowding in 3D promotes NSC differentiation through Cx43-mediated gap junctions

To determine whether crowding-dependent changes in intercellular junctions contribute to NSC fate specification in 3D cultures, we examined the most significantly regulated junctional components. Cx43-positive cells in the H group exhibited more extensive elongated protrusions and interconnected networks compared with the L group (Figure S5B). We next investigated whether Cx43-mediated gap junctions are functionally required for crowding-dependent neurogenesis. Then we treated NSCs with Gap27, the selective mimetic peptide inhibitor of Cx43 channels. Gap27 treatment did not affect NSC viability (Figures 6C and 6D); however, 0.3 μM and 0.5 μM Gap27 markedly decreased Cx43 fluorescence intensity in the high-density group, with a clear concentration-dependent trend (Figures S5C and S5D). Immunofluorescence co-staining and quantitative analysis of the neuronal marker Tuj-1 and the astrocytic marker GFAP showed that Gap27 markedly attenuated the proneurogenic effect of high cell density (Figures 6E and 6F). Western blot analysis further confirmed that 0.3 μM Gap27 reduced Tuj-1 protein levels by more than 50%, while 0.5 μM Gap27 also significantly decreased GFAP expression (Figures 6G and 6H). To compare with the 2D system, we examined the same intercellular junction-related genes and Cx43 protein. Quantitative analyses showed that gap junction- and tight junction-associated genes, as well as Cx43 protein levels, did not change significantly across different cell densities in 2D cultures (Figures S6A–S6C). Upon treatment with Gap27, NSCs maintained high viability and exhibited reduced Cx43 fluorescence intensity (Figures S6D–S6G). However, this alteration did not affect the neuronal differentiation ratio of NSCs in the 2D environment (Figures S6H and S6I). Thus, while Cx43 is expressed in both environments, only the 3D microenvironment induces a density-dependent upregulation and functional engagement of Cx43-mediated gap junctions. This 3D-specific modulation of Cx43 appears to be a critical mechanism underlying crowding-enhanced neurogenesis.

Together, these results demonstrate that high cell density within the 3D collagen matrix promotes Cx43 gap junction assembly and biases NSCs toward neuronal differentiation, whereas pharmacological blockade with Gap27 abrogates this effect (Figure 6I). Collectively, these findings establish Cx43-dependent gap-junction signaling as a mechanistic link between physical crowding and neurogenesis in 3D NSC cultures.

Discussion

NSCs are central to CNS development and homeostasis because of their capacity for self-renewal and differentiation into neuronal and glial lineages. Their fate decisions are tightly controlled by dynamic biophysical and biochemical cues within their 3D niches.1,2 Although traditional 2D cultures have yielded important insights, they fail to replicate the spatial organization and mechanical constraints of in vivo microenvironments.23,24,25,26,27,28 Our hydrogel system replicates native brain mechanics with high porosity and stable architecture over 7 days. This biomimetic platform integrates biophysical cues with gap-junction signaling to guide NSC fate determination. In this study, we investigated how cell crowding influences NSC differentiation and intercellular junction formation under 2D and 3D conditions, revealing a dimension-specific regulatory mechanism.

Cell-cell junctions serve as essential mediators of intercellular communication, coordinating biochemical and mechanical signaling through gap junctions, tight junctions, adherens junctions, and other complexes. These structures are particularly critical for cerebral cortex maturation, where the transition from a simple neuroepithelium to a complex network requires finely tuned intercellular signaling pathways.35 Gap junctions act as critical conduits for ions, metabolites, and second messengers, thereby coordinating collective cellular responses and maintaining neural network function.36,37,38 Previous studies have shown that gap junctions enhance synaptic communication and protect against synaptopathies.39,40 They also enable astrocyte-astrocyte coupling and help regulate neuronal synaptic plasticity.41 Cx43, a principal gap junction protein, is highly expressed in neurogenic niches such as the subventricular zone and the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus.42 In our 2D culture systems, Cx43 expression was unaltered by crowding, and NSC differentiation was unaffected by Cx43 inhibition (Figures S6A–S6C, and S6F–S6I), indicating that paracrine signaling dominates under planar conditions.43,44 Consistent with recent findings, 3D culture has been demonstrated to enhance intercellular connectivity and elevate Cx43 expression in stem cells compared with two-dimensional culture systems.45 In contrast, 3D cultures showed pronounced upregulation of Cx43 under high-density conditions (Figures 6A–6C). This indicates that the mechanical context of 3D crowding directly promotes junction-mediated signaling pathways. Indeed, the inhibition of Cx43 with the peptide Gap27 significantly reduced 3D crowding-induced neuronal differentiation, providing direct evidence that gap junctional signaling is a mechanistic driver of density-dependent fate determination in 3D. The role of Cx43 in neurogenesis has been explored across various experimental contexts. In 2D culture, Cx43 deficiency in hNPCs has been shown to enhance neuronal differentiation, although this effect appears to be independent of Cx43-mediated gap junctional communication.46 Consistently, an in vivo study reported that conditional knockout of Cx43 in NSCs increased neurogenesis in the olfactory bulb.47 With respect to the channel-independent functions of Cx43, its interaction with β-catenin may facilitate neural differentiation through the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.48,49 In contrast, another study demonstrated that pharmacological closure of Cx43 gap junction channels reduced neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus, suggesting that gap junction-mediated intercellular communication is also required for proper hippocampal neurogenesis.50 Collectively, these findings indicate that the regulatory mechanisms governing channel-form and non-channel-form functions of Cx43 in neural differentiation may differ, and that additional in vivo factors may further modulate its role in neurogenesis.

Beyond junctional communication, interactions between NSCs and the ECM also shape developmental outcomes. Our data show that low-density NSCs in a 2D environment preferentially upregulate ECM synthesis pathways (Figures 1E and S2), suggesting reliance on ECM-mediated signaling. Conversely, in 3D cultures, crowding enhanced ECM-receptor interactions (Figure 4E), indicating that cell-ECM signaling is more dynamic under these conditions. Prior studies have established that ECM cues influence stem cell fate by regulating cell shape and cytoskeletal organization.51,52 Together, these findings suggest that while both 2D and 3D crowding promote neuronal differentiation—likely by reducing intercellular distances and amplifying cytokine signaling—only in 3D does crowding induce robust cell-cell junction formation, facilitated by the collagen hydrogel. This 3D-specific response reflects the scaffold’s ability to recapitulate in vivo-like conditions that support both cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions.

Our aim was not to recreate the full complexity of neural development but rather to isolate and dissect the specific contribution of cell crowding to NSC fate decisions. By standardizing extracellular volume while varying only cell density, we employed a deliberately reductionist model that minimizes confounding influences such as vascular flow, immune surveillance, and region-specific patterning cues. This approach enabled us to define the autonomous effect of crowding on differentiation programs. The “cellular crowding” physical cue has previously been applied in two-dimensional embryonic stem cell studies by regulating cell density per unit area.18 Here, we extend this framework into three dimensions by quantifying density within identical spatial volumes, thereby providing a complementary model for investigating how physical constraints regulate stem cell behavior. It remains experimentally difficult to fully disentangle the contributions of cell crowding from those of paracrine factor accumulation. Although collagen hydrogels lack specific affinity for growth factors53 and thus do not substantially sequester secreted proteins, physical crowding itself has been shown to enhance paracrine signaling efficiency.43,44 Despite these considerations, our results consistently indicate that Cx43-mediated gap junction communication plays a dominant regulatory role in the differentiation outcomes observed in the 3D crowded NSC cultures.

Our study thus provides mechanistic insight into how 3D physical crowding cooperates with biochemical cues to regulate NSC behavior. By establishing Cx43-mediated gap junction signaling as a critical determinant of crowding-dependent differentiation, we highlight the importance of dimensionality in modeling NSC niches. These findings advance our understanding of NSC biology and have practical implications for regenerative medicine and neural tissue engineering. Optimized 3D culture models that incorporate controlled crowding may improve neuronal differentiation efficiency and enhance the functional integration of transplanted cells in clinical applications. Furthermore, our results emphasize the need to design biomaterials that support both intercellular junctional signaling and cell-ECM interactions as the next generation of scaffolds for neural repair.

In summary, our work demonstrates that the regulatory influence of NSC crowding on differentiation is profoundly dimension-dependent. While crowding promotes neurogenesis in both 2D and 3D systems, only in 3D does it drive Cx43-mediated gap junction formation, which is mechanistically required for fate determination. These findings reveal a novel dimension-sensing mechanism through which biophysical cues orchestrate stem cell fate via junctional remodeling, providing both fundamental insights into stem cell biology and a rational framework for developing NSC-based therapeutics.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we employed a reductionist 3D model standardizing extracellular volume while varying cell density to isolate crowding effects. However, this model excluded critical physiological components of the native niche, including vascular flow, immune surveillance (e.g., microglia), and region-specific patterning cues. These exclusions limit our ability to account for niche-extrinsic mechanisms that may cooperate with crowding responses. Furthermore, although the collagen hydrogel provided a biomechanically relevant 3D environment, its lack of specific affinity for growth factors made it challenging to fully decouple the effects of physical crowding from those of concomitant paracrine factor accumulation. Engineered biomaterials with tunable growth factor-binding capabilities may have enabled better resolution of these distinct contributions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Primary neural stem cell isolation and culture

NSCs were isolated from the cerebral cortices of postnatal day 1 (P1) Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. A total of 200 neonatal rats were used for NSC isolation. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Biology, Hunan University (ethical approval number: HNUBIO202201002). Sex was not determined as P1 neonates were processed immediately for tissue isolation. The mixed cortical cells were first passed through a 100 μm nylon cell strainer mesh, then plated into tissue culture flasks. NSCs were expanded as neural spheres at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 3–4 days in proliferation medium containing DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% L-glutamine, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% NEAA, 2% B27, 0.6% (w/v) glucose, 0.02 ng/mL EGF, 0.02 ng/mL bFGF, 1.83 ng/mL heparin, and 1% streptomycin penicillin. The proliferation medium was replaced every 2 days. Thereafter, the neural spheres were gathered by centrifugation (300×g, 5 min) and digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at 37°C for 18 min. After terminating the digestion with 10% FBS, the cells were collected by centrifugation and filtered through 70 μm and 40 μm mesh cell strainers successively to obtain a single-cell suspension of NSCs.

Method details

Col hydrogel preparation and characterization

2 mg of Type Ⅰ collagen (Col Ⅰ) (DB Wunderegen) was dissolved in 1 mL of 0.25 mol/L acetic acid solution overnight followed by neutralization with NaOH solution. To measure the turbidity during the transition of Col sol to gel, the absorbance for the Col system was recorded by UV spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 310 nm. For circular dichroism (CD) measurements, 0.2% (w/v) Col solution and gel were placed in the liquid and solid sample pools, respectively. Thereafter, CD spectra were recorded in the range of 190–260 nm with 1 nm interval using Jasco-815 spectrometer under a nitrogen atmosphere. To measure hydrogels gelation points, the storage (G′) and loss (G″) modulus of 500 μL of 0.2–0.5% (w/v) Col solution as a gel precursor were recorded with time-sweep rheological experiments at 37°C with the rheometer equipped with a 15 mm parallel plate. For the frequency sweep of rheological experiments, 500 μL of as-prepared Col hydrogels were recorded for G′ and G″ from 0.1 to 1 Hz at fixed 1% stain. To study the in vitro stability of the hydrogels, 0.2–0.5% (w/v) Col hydrogels were incubated in 37°C PBS, and the hydrogel mass was monitored. Gel residual ratios were calculated by dividing the hydrogel mass at different time point by the initial hydrogel mass before swelling. At least three hydrogel replicates were used for above calculation. To study the microstructures of freeze-dried Col hydrogels, a scanning electron microscope was used to observe the porous morphology.

In vitro differentiation of NSCs

For 2D differentiation cultures, cell densities referenced density-dependent differentiation paradigms established in NPC systems.19 Cells were seeded in 24-well poly-L-lysine-coated plates at densities of 1.25×104 cells/cm2 (L-2D) and 6.25×104 cells/cm2 (H-2D). Then the cells culture with differentiation medium replenishment cycles every 48 h (50% volume replacement).

For 3D culture systems, NSC suspensions were homogenously incorporated into type Ⅰ collagen matrices. A 0.2% (w/v) collagen solution (pH 7.4) was mixed 1:1 with single-cell NSC suspensions to obtain final concentrations of 7.5×105, 1.5×106, 3×106, and 6×106 cells/mL, corresponding to low, moderate, and high packing density regimes based on the 2D system and the estimated neuronal diameter (∼20 μm) in 3D. In parallel experiments assessing collagen concentration effects, a standardized NSC density of 3 × 106 cells/mL was encapsulated within hydrogel matrices of varying collagen content: 0.2%, 0.3%, 0.4% and 0.5% (w/v). The cell-collagen admixture underwent gelation at 37°C for 30 min to establish dimensionally stable cell-laden hydrogels. These constructs were subsequently maintained in differentiation medium under standard culture conditions for 7 days, with 50% volume medium replacement every 48 h.

To inhibit gap junction formation of NSCs in 3D culture, Gap27 (at the indicated concentration) was included in the differentiation medium and replenished during medium changes.

The differentiation medium included DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% glutamine, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% NEAA, 2% B27, 0.6% (w/v) glucose, 5% FBS and 1% streptomycin/penicillin. Moreover, for the different Col concentrations based cell studies fixed 3 million/mL NSCs were encapsulated into 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5% (w/v) hydrogels, respectively.

Cell viability assay and immunostaining

For cell viability evaluation, cell-laden Col gels were incubated with Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit for 30 min according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

For immunostaining, both 2D monolayers and 3D hydrogel constructs underwent standardized immunofluorescence processing. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, followed by three 5-min washes with PBS. Permeabilization was performed using 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min. Thereafter, nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% (w/v) FBS in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies anti-GFAP, anti-Tuj1, anti-Cx43, anti-Cldn3, anti-Cldn11 were incubated at 4°C overnight. After washing with PBS three times, the secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 546 and goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 was incubated for 2 h and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was incubated for 30 min at room temperature, followed by final PBS rinses. For cytoskeleton staining, actin-Tracker Green-488 was incubated for 4 h at room temperature. Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal microscope and analyzed using Fiji and Zeiss ZEN.

Flow cytometry

Collagen hydrogel was digested with collagenase and cells were collected by centrifugation. After fixation and washing, the primary antibodies was incubated at 4°C overnight by rotation. The secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 546 was incubated at room temperature for 1h. After washing, cells were suspended in PBS and analyzed using a Flow Cytometer. A minimum of 10,000 events were acquired for each sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

For 2D cultures, after removing medium, the samples were washed with PBS, then Trizol was added under ice bath conditions. For 3D culture systems, after freezing in liquid nitrogen, the cell-laden Col hydrogels were ground with Trizol in a tissue grinding instrument. The total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT reagent kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7300 Real-Time PCR system and the TB Green premix EX Taq Kit. The results were normalized to Gapdh using the 2ˆ(-ΔΔCT) relative method.

Western blotting

The 2D culturing samples washed with PBS, then lysate contained PMSF, protease/phosphatase inhibitor was added under ice bath conditions. Cell-laden Col hydrogels were frozen in liquid nitrogen and lysate contained PMSF, protease/phosphatase inhibitor were added under ice bath conditions. The total proteins were collected by centrifugating at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C after grinding the samples. The concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay. The samples were denatured in SDS-PAGE loading buffer at 95°C for 5 min. The protein was separated using an SDS-PAGE gel at 120 V for 90 min and transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk solution for 1h, the PVDF membrane was incubated with primary antibody at 4°C overnight with gentle shaking. After washing 6 times in TBST, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies for 1h. Finally, the membrane was incubated in BeyoECL Plus and imaged using the OI900 Touch imaging and analysis system.

RNA-seq library preparation

Total RNA was isolated from each sample using the RNeasy Mini Kit. RNA quality was examined by gel electrophoresis and with Qubit. Strand-specific libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA sample preparation kit, through reverse transcription using S1000TM Touch Thermal Cycler at 53°C (45 min) - 85°C (5 min) - 4°C cycle. The generated cDNA was amplified, and quality estimated using Agilent 4200. The sequences of libraries were finally obtained using the Illumina Novaseq6000 sequencer with pair-end 150 bp (PE150).

Reads mapping and gene expression quantification

cDNA library samples were sequenced as described above. After removing the reads with adapters, any reads containing “n” (>10%) and low-quality reads (sQ ≤ 5), the remaining clean reads were checked by FastQC (v.0.11.5).54 Clean reads were aligned to the Rattus norvegicus Rnor 6.0.98 using STAR (v.2.5.3a)55 and the raw counts matrix were obtained by featureCounts. The expression level of genes was calculated using CPM as a metric, and filtered using the sum of relative abundance to eliminate the effects of sequencing depths.

PCA analysis, DEG obtain and clustering

Normalized gene expression values were used to calculate the correlation coefficient among biological replicates for PCA and hierarchical clustering. The PCA was calculated with the prcomp function in R with default settings.61 Hierarchical clustering was performed using the pheatmap package with default settings using Pearson’s correlation.58 The DEGs in pairwise comparisons were identified by the R package edgeR,56 which performs data normalization using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method. Genes with at least a 2-fold change in expression and DFR value <0.05 were considered to be expressed differentially. A k-means approach was used (R package pheatmap) to cluster all the DEGs identified through pairwise comparisons.62

Functional category enrichment analysis

Using the Blast-KOALA tool from the KEGG database for pathway enrichment analysis, and utilizing the KEGG mapper tool to delve deeper into the pathway modules within it.63 GO enrichment analysis for differentially expressed genes was conducted using the TopGO package (v.2.42.0).57 Adrian Alexi’s improved weighted scoring algorithm and Fisher’s test were used to define the significance of GO term enrichment. Functional enrichment analysis and GSEA were performed using the “enricher” and “GSEA” function from the clusterProfiler package (v.3.18.1),59 respectively. Pathway annotations and gene sets for GSEA analysis were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) database.60

Quantification and statistical analysis

All values are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For two-group comparisons, unpaired Student’s t test was used. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Exact sample sizes (n) are provided in figure legends, where n refers to the number of independent replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
