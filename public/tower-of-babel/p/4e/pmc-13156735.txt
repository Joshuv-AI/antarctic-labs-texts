A unique triple association: intravascular leiomyomatosis, benign metastasizing leiomyoma, and early-stage endometrial carcinoma
Authors: Taha Berk Çimen, Murat Cengiz, Onur Can Zaim, Bilal Esat Temiz, Murat Gültekin, Utku Akgor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) and benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML) are uncommon variants of uterine smooth muscle tumors that can demonstrate extrauterine spread despite their benign histological appearance. Both lesions are known to be estrogen-dependent, yet their coexistence with gynecologic malignancies—particularly endometrial carcinoma—is exceedingly rare. We present a 51-year-old postmenopausal woman who was admitted with progressive abdominal distension and ecchymotic skin changes over the abdominal wall. Radiologic evaluation revealed a large retroperitoneal mass with multiple solid pelvic lesions. The patient underwent total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and resection of a para-aortic mass. Histopathological examination identified 3 concurrent entities: intravascular leiomyomatosis confined to the uterus, benign metastasizing leiomyoma involving para-aortic lymph nodes, and an incidental FIGO stage IA1 endometrioid adenocarcinoma confined to an endometrial polyp. Immunohistochemistry confirmed smooth muscle differentiation (Desmin, h-caldesmon), retained FH expression, low proliferative index (Ki-67 ≈ 2%–3%), and wild-type p53 pattern. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and adjuvant hormonal suppression with letrozole was initiated. The patient remains disease-free during 6 months of follow-up. This case represents an exceptionally rare triple coexistence of IVL, BML, and early-stage endometrial carcinoma. Recognition of such unique associations broadens the understanding of estrogen-dependent uterine neoplasms and emphasizes the need for thorough histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. A multidisciplinary approach with long-term hormonal surveillance is recommended to detect recurrence or additional hormone-responsive lesions.

Introduction

Intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) is a rare clinical entity characterized by the intraluminal proliferation of benign smooth muscle cells within venous channels that occurs in young and middle-aged women aged 35-50 years before menopause, usually with a clear history of uterine leiomyoma.1 Although histologically benign, IVL can behave in an aggressive manner, extending into the inferior vena cava (IVC) unilaterally :through the uterine veins to the iliac veins and then to inferior vena cava (IVC) or from the ovarian veins directly reaching the IVC.2 Even reaching the right atrium, occasionally leading to life-threatening complications. Complete surgical resection is considered the treatment of choice, as recurrence rates are high following incomplete removal.3

Benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML) represents another unusual growth pattern of uterine leiomyomas, defined by the presence of histologically benign leiomyomatous tissue in extrauterine sites, most commonly the lungs, but also in the retroperitoneum and lymph nodes. Other affected areas include the omentum, pelvis, abdomen, mediastinum, vertebrae, nervous system, bone, skeletal muscle, skin, heart (about 15 cases reported in the literature), breast, trachea, esophagus, and liver.4 While BML itself is a rare entity, total of 150 cases are present in the literature,5 its coexistence with gynecological malignancies is exceptionally uncommon, with only a few cases reported in association with ovarian and endometrial carcinoma. Importantly, BML lesions may mimic true metastatic disease, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.3

Endometrial carcinoma is the most common gynecological malignancy. Similar to uterine leiomyomas, the majority of these lesions are estrogen-dependent. For this reason, associations with both IVL and BML have been documented and reported in the literature. However, to the best of our knowledge, the coexistence of IVL, BML, and endometrial carcinoma has not been previously reported. Recognition of such rare combinations is clinically important, as they expand the spectrum of uterine smooth muscle tumors and highlight the need for meticulous pathological evaluation

Here, we present a rare case demonstrating the unique coexistence of 3 distinct entities.

Case report

A 51-year-old multiparous woman with 3 previous term deliveries had been postmenopausal for 7 years. She was referred to our tertiary university hospital obstetrics and gynecology clinic with complaints of progressive abdominal fullness accompanied by ecchymotic lesions on the abdominal wall. She reported a long-standing history of abdominal discomfort dating back approximately nine years. At that time, a pelvic mass had been identified, and surgical intervention was recommended; however, the patient refused the procedure and was subsequently lost to follow-up.

9 years later from her initial presentation, she presented again with worsening abdominal symptoms. On physical examination, firm and non-tender mass was identified in umbilical region, which couldn’t be mobilized vertically or horizontally. Further, no hepatosplenomegaly was identified, and no mass was felt on liver or spleen, additionally, non-shifting dullness to percussion was noted in the umbilical and bilateral flank regions.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a massive, lobulated, homogeneous mass originating from the uterus, measuring approximately 21 × 12 cm in the axial plane. On T2-weighted images, the mass appeared hypointense relative to the myometrium, a characteristic finding for leiomyomatous tissue, and was supplied by prominent, tortuous vascular structures. No intratumoral hemorrhage was detected. Similar T2-hypointense lesions were observed in the pre-caval, aortocaval, and right subdiaphragmatic regions, with the largest measuring 9 × 6 cm. Additionally, a 6.2 cm T2-hypointense nodular lesion without restricted diffusion was noted in the spleen. The scan also identified bilateral inguinal and para-iliac lymphadenopathy (short axis up to 16 mm) and dilated ovarian veins. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed a retroperitoneal mass measuring 21 × 12 cm with marked, with marked vascularity and lateral displacement of the aorta and inferior vena cava. Preoperative biopsy was deferred in favor of primary surgical exploration, as the large, symptomatic nature of the mass mandated resection regardless of the histologic subtype. Laboratory investigations demonstrated an elevated CA-125 level of 55.8 U/mL, while CA 19-9 (4.8 U/mL), CA 15-3 (5.5 U/mL), LDH(175 U/L) and CEA (0.52 U/mL) were within normal limits.

A mid-line laparotomy via a xipho-pubic incision was performed. Exploratory laparotomy revealed a solid, fragile, and soft mass that initially appeared to originate from the right adnexa. A right unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy was performed for intraoperative frozen section analysis, which reported a “malignant tumor, possibly mesenchymal” and noted a suspicion of Endometrial Intraepithelial Neoplasia (EIN). Given these findings, the procedure was converted to maximal cytoreductive surgery. This included total abdominal hysterectomy, left salpingo-oophorectomy, total omentectomy, and excision of superficial tumoral implants on the rectum. Additionally, a large retroperitoneal para-aortic mass was resected en bloc with a segment of the gonadal vein (Figure 2). Optimal cytoreduction was achieved, leaving no residual macroscopic disease. Immunohistochemistry confirmed smooth muscle differentiation (Desmin, h-caldesmon), retained fumarate hydratese (FH) expression, low proliferative index (Ki-67 ≈2%–3%), and wild-type p53 pattern.

The postoperative recovery was uncomplicated, and the patient was discharged on the third postoperative day. Letrozole therapy was initiated shortly after discharge, given the estrogen-dependent nature of the identified lesions and the reported overexpression of aromatase enzyme in leiomyomas. During 6 months of monthly follow-up visits, no significant abnormalities were detected.

Discussion

Extrauterine leiomyomas are exceedingly rare, constituting a small minority of leiomyomatous disease, and are subclassified into benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML), intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL), disseminated peritoneal leiomyomatosis (DPL), and parasitic leiomyomas.

The surgical management of this case presented distinct technical challenges beyond a standard gynecologic oncology procedure. The primary difficulty lay in the extensive retroperitoneal involvement. The largest mass (19 × 11 cm) significantly distorted the retroperitoneal anatomy, displacing the aorta and inferior vena cava. Dissection in this plane carried a high risk of catastrophic hemorrhage, particularly given the vascular nature of intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL), which can have direct intraluminal continuity with major vessels. Furthermore, the intraoperative frozen section diagnosis of "malignant tumor" mandated a shift in strategy from simple excision to radical cytoreduction (debulking). This required a multidisciplinary surgical competence to perform en bloc resection of the para-aortic mass and the involved gonadal vein while ensuring negative margins. This case illustrates that when IVL and BML present with such extensive extra-pelvic disease, the surgical approach must mimic that of advanced ovarian malignancy (maximal effort cytoreduction) rather than benign leiomyoma surgery.

Intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL) is a rare benign neoplasm of uterine smooth muscle origin, which can extend into venous structures and potentially reach vital organs. Despite its benign histology, the clinical course can be aggressive, and symptoms may range from vague abdominal pain to life-threatening cardiac complications.6

Our patient also demonstrated benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML), an even rarer condition. BML typically involves the lungs, heart, lymph nodes, skeletal muscle, or pelvic cavity. It is commonly seen in patients who have received surgery before.7 And commonly seen with leimyomatosis peritonealis disseminata (LPD).8 However, significant retroperitoneal lymph node involvement, as seen in this case, is exceptionally rare.

Given the atypical presentation, it was critical to rule out leiomyosarcoma and other malignant neoplasms using both histopathological and immunohistochemical methods. While the exact pathogenesis of BML remains unclear, previous reports have associated it with low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma, suggesting a possible hormonal or molecular link.

Review of the literature (Table 1) reveals a lack of consensus on the management of extrauterine leiomyomatosis, with strategies ranging from hormonal suppression alone to surgical resection. However, our case is unique in its "triple association" with endometrial carcinoma. Unlike cases reported by Siatecka et al. and Mahendru et al., where disease was limited to the pelvis or managed conservatively, our patient’s massive retroperitoneal burden necessitated aggressive surgical intervention. While Yoon et al. reported a similar association with malignancy, their case involved a smaller nodal burden (2 cm). Our successful management suggests that even in cases with massive, "unresectable-appearing" retroperitoneal disease (15 cm bulky nodes), complete surgical resection is feasible and, when combined with aromatase inhibitors (Letrozole), can result in disease-free survival. Although immunohistochemical evaluation for estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression was not routinely performed in this case, the decision to initiate adjuvant hormonal therapy was driven by the inherent biology of the identified tumors. Both Intravascular leiomyomatosis and benign metastasizing leiomyoma are well-established as estrogen-dependent entities that regress or stabilize upon withdrawal of hormonal stimulation. Furthermore, the patient’s concurrent type I endometrioid adenocarcinoma (FIGO Grade 1) is classically associated with a state of unopposed estrogen.

In postmenopausal women, the aromatase enzyme in peripheral adipose tissue becomes the primary source of estrogen production. Consequently, the administration of Letrozole (an aromatase inhibitor) was selected to achieve maximal estrogen suppression, targeting both the potential microscopic residual disease of the leiomyomatosis and providing adjuvant endocrine management for the endometrial malignancy.

13Long-term follow-up is essential due to the estrogen-sensitive nature of these tumors and the potential for recurrence or development of additional hormone-dependent neoplasms.

In clinical practice, retroperitoneal lymph node metastasis—especially in estrogen-responsive tumors like endometrial adenocarcinoma—should be considered in the differential diagnosis, even in the absence of overt symptoms.

Because IVL, BML, and DPL are estrogen-dependent entities, the potential coexistence of endometrial carcinoma should be considered in clinical practice. In our case, endometrial carcinoma was detected incidentally and was confined to the endometrium (FIGO stage IA1); therefore, no additional surgical intervention was required. Recognition of such rare presentations underscores the importance of maintaining vigilance for concurrent malignancy in similar clinical scenarios.
