Widespread tropical cyclone activity during the Late Paleozoic Ice Age
Authors: Qing Yan, Sophia Macarewich, Nanxuan Jiang, David B. Kemp, Dubin Huan, Christopher J. Poulsen, Jinzhe Zhang, Zhongshi Zhang, Huijun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whether tropical cyclone (TC) activity prevailed during the Late Paleozoic Ice Age (LPIA)—the longest icehouse of the Phanerozoic—remains debated, owing to the paucity and ambiguity of sedimentary evidence. To help resolve this conflict, we combine a global compilation of 126 storm records spanning the LPIA ( ~ 360–260 Ma) with high-resolution (~25-km) TC-resolving simulations. We reveal a quasi-symmetric meridional distribution of TC genesis across the tropics during the LPIA, with TC tracks extending into high latitudes (>60°N/°S). This pattern aligns with the compiled widespread storm deposits from subtropical and tropical regions. During CO₂-driven glacial–interglacial transitions within the LPIA, we observe reduced global TC frequency and enhanced intensity, coinciding with poleward shifts in TC activity. The shifts are linked to spatially heterogeneous warming patterns and associated changes in atmospheric circulation. Our results help reconcile controversies regarding icehouse TC behavior and highlight that warming-induced poleward migration of TC activity is likely a climate state-independent characteristic of the Earth system.

Introduction

Alternations between icehouse and greenhouse states represent Earth’s most fundamental climate rhythm during the past half-billion years, evidenced by the oscillatory decay and growth of continental ice sheets1. The Late Paleozoic Ice Age (LPIA) persisted for ~100 Myrs (ca. 360–260 Ma)2 and was the longest-lasting Phanerozoic glaciation1,3,4. Its maximum latitudinal extent of land ice rivals that of the Last Glacial Maximum (~27–19 ka)5, although the precise extent remains sensitive to uncertainties in paleogeographic reconstructions. The LPIA exhibited dynamic glacial–interglacial cycles operating at sub-million-year (105-yr) timescales, driven by substantial fluctuations in atmospheric CO2 concentration3. Interestingly, this icehouse climate hosted vigorous storm activity extending from the tropics to high paleolatitudes (i.e., >60°S; also see Fig. 1), as evidenced by widespread storm-related deposits preserved in sedimentary strata (e.g., sedimentary structures such as hummocky cross-stratification)6–9. However, because these structures can form under a wide range of conditions—including tropical cyclones (TCs), extratropical storms, and tidal waves, attributing observed storm deposits to a specific origin is challenging10. Numerical simulations offer an effective means of reconstructing the distribution of climate systems through Earth history and can hence help constrain the origin of storm deposits. The sole such modeling effort7 indicates that TC-favorable environments were restricted to the Northern Hemisphere low latitudes during cold intervals of the LPIA (i.e., Early Permian), with minimal cyclogenesis potential in the Southern Hemisphere, suggesting very limited TC activity at that time. However, this fails to explain the large number of storm deposits emerging in tropical belts during the LPIA (especially over the Southern Hemisphere; Fig. 1), which are generally interpreted as TC‑generated, given their low paleolatitudes12,13. This model–data mismatch raises questions about whether TC could prevail during the LPIA and our understanding of TC dynamics under icehouse climates. Moreover, the presence or absence of TC activity has implications for our understanding of other key climate processes such as extreme precipitation patterns, vertical ocean mixing, and meridional heat transport—mechanisms that play an essential role in shaping both ancient and modern climate systems14–16.

The model–data discrepancy in LPIA TC activity arises from the physically unconstrained origins of geological storm deposits and limitations of earlier climate models (e.g., relatively coarse spatial resolution and intermediate complexity). Notably, since the earliest synthesis studies in the 1980s/1990s6,7, there has been an increased recognition of sedimentary storm deposits across the LPIA and marked improvements in climate models (e.g., TC-resolving simulation). This underscores the need for a systematic re-evaluation of storm activity through integrated proxy-model approaches. Furthermore, the LPIA provides a potential analog to decipher how TC activity might have responded to large atmospheric CO2 perturbations under a near-future-like glaciated world, complementing our understanding of TC behavior during ice-free hothouse climates. To address these challenges, we combine a global compilation of published sedimentary evidence for LPIA storms with high‑resolution (~25 km) TC‑resolving climate simulations. This integrated approach provides a comprehensive reconstruction of storm activity during the longest‑lived Phanerozoic icehouse, helps identify the TC-generated storm deposits, and sheds light on TC behavior under future CO₂‑induced warming.

Results

Spatial pattern of TC activity during the LPIA

To provide a first‑order approximation of storm distribution, we compile 126 records of storm‑related activity spanning the LPIA (~ 360–260 Ma) (Data S1 and Fig. S1) and reconstruct storm paleo-locations using a plate tectonic model (“Methods”). Given the ~100-Ma timespan, we broadly divide the LPIA into three stages based on reconstructed glacial frequency and area3, including inception (360–320 Ma), peak (320–280 Ma), and deglaciation (280–260 Ma) stages. Our compilation confirms that storm activity extended from the tropics to mid‑ and high‑latitude regions in both hemispheres during the LPIA (Fig. 1a–c), reflecting a combination of different storm types (e.g., TCs and extratropical storms). However, the latitudinal pattern of storm deposits varied across stages. Storm activity over the Southern Hemisphere was pronounced during all the stages of the LPIA, whereas the Northern Hemisphere experienced relatively more frequent storm activity during the peak stage (Fig. 1d). As a caveat, this pattern may be biased by uncertainties in deposition, preservation, and sampling of storm evidence. Nevertheless, despite inherent temporal and spatial uncertainties, the widespread storm deposits in tropical regions (30°S–30°N) during the LPIA imply persistent TC activity during icehouse conditions.

To evaluate the evidence-based TC patterns, we conducted high-resolution (~25 km) TC-resolving simulations only for the apex of the LPIA (~ 300 Ma; see “Methods”), given large changes in paleogeography across the LPIA. Our simulations under 280 ppmv CO2 conditions illustrate a latitudinal band distribution of TC genesis across the Panthalassic and Paleo-Tethys Oceans (Fig. 2a). Although the majority of TCs form between 10° and 20°N/S (consistent with modern TC corridors), the LPIA climate also supports occasional TC genesis (i.e., <5 events per year) between 40° and 50°N/S (Fig. 2b). Notably, the simulated TC frequency in the Northern Hemisphere is relatively lower than in the Southern Hemisphere—the reverse of the modern hemispheric asymmetry. This is attributed to the colder ocean temperature at the eastern tropical Panthalassic Ocean of the Northern Hemisphere produced by coastal upwelling17 (Fig. S2), creating conditions unfavorable for TC formation. Focusing on the peak stage of the LPIA, the modeled TC trajectories broadly align with low‑latitude empirical evidence, with paleo‑storm locations overlapping predicted TC activity zones (Fig. 2c). The modeled blank zone of TC activity over the equatorial eastern Panthalassic Ocean suggests that storm evidence found there (e.g., ref. 18) may have been caused by processes other than TCs. At the mid‑high latitudes (30°–60°N/S) of both hemispheres, several storm deposits19–24 may be attributable to TC activity given the prevailing TC tracks in those regions, although the contribution of winter storms cannot be fully excluded. In addition, storm evidence beyond ~60°S is likely caused by winter storms or other weather phenomena. These lines of evidence highlight pervasive TC activity, at least, in tropical to subtropical regions during the LPIA.

TC responses during glacial–interglacial cycles within the LPIA

The LPIA experienced sub-million-year (105-yr) glacial–interglacial cycles modulated by atmospheric CO₂ fluctuations, coupled with synchronous variations in ice-sheet extent, eustatic sea level, and tropical biome distribution3. To quantify the response of TC during glacial–interglacial transitions, we conduct paired sensitivity experiments simulating a CO₂ doubling (“Methods”), as constrained empirically via paleo-CO2 proxy data (from ~280 to 560 ppmv)25,26. Our simulations reveal divergent responses in TC frequency and intensity across such a glacial–interglacial transition. Specifically, global TC frequency decreases significantly by ~15.1% (P < 0.05) under elevated CO₂ in the interglacial relative to the glacial, while lifetime maximum intensity and mean intensity show an increase of ~3.5% (P < 0.05) and 2.4% (P < 0.05), respectively, in the interglacial (Table S1). Furthermore, TC responses are not uniform across hemispheres, with a more profound decrease in TC frequency in the Southern Hemisphere (~18.9%; P < 0.05) than in the Northern Hemisphere (~9.7%; P < 0.05). By contrast, the intensification of TCs is relatively greater in the Northern Hemisphere than the Southern Hemisphere (4.2% vs. 2.8% in terms of lifetime maximum intensity). In addition, the CO2-induced warming enhances the probability of extreme intensity storms, evidenced by the expansion of the upper tail of the lifetime maximum intensity distribution (Fig. S3).

Spatially, higher CO₂ during interglacial phases of the LPIA leads to a poleward migration of TC activity, marked by TC depletion at the tropics and increase at the mid-latitudes, particularly in the Northern Hemisphere (Fig. 3). Quantitatively, mean genesis latitudes shift poleward by ~3.6° (P < 0.05) in the Northern Hemisphere and by ~1.5° (P < 0.05) in the Southern Hemisphere during the interglacial relative to the glacial, with the positions of lifetime maximum intensity migrating ~3.5°N (P < 0.05) and 1.0°S (P < 0.05), respectively (Table S1 and Fig. S3). Notably, this meridional redistribution exhibits regional heterogeneity, especially over the Southern Hemisphere (Fig. 3c). Mechanisms behind the poleward shifts in TC activity

To elucidate the mechanisms driving poleward migration of TCs with the glacial–interglacial transitions, we analyze large-scale environmental controls using a genesis potential index (GPI) that integrates thermodynamic and dynamic precursors of cyclogenesis31,32 (see “Methods”). Our results illustrate that GPI variations under CO₂ doubling during the LPIA largely capture the latitudinal shift of the TC activity, showing significantly decreased genesis favorability at the low latitudes of the tropics (~5°–15°N) and enhanced favorability at the relatively higher latitudes (Fig. 4a). In the Northern Hemisphere, the poleward shifts largely arise from changes in vertical wind shear (Fig. 4b, d), which show a dipole structure with reduced shear at the north of 20°N and enhanced shear to the south (Fig. S4). Enhanced genesis potential at the higher latitudes (20°–40°N) is further amplified by increased potential intensity, and suppressed GPI at the low latitudes (~5°–20°N) also results from decreased absolute vorticity and enhanced moisture entropy deficit (Figs. 4b–f and S4). Similarly, GPI anomalies in the Southern Hemisphere are dominated by wind shear redistribution. Regional analysis reveals that the reduced TC favorability within the 10°–20°S band is largely dominated by increased vertical wind shear and amplified moisture entropy deficit, whereas the enhanced genesis potential at higher latitudes (>25°S) is driven by higher potential intensity and smaller wind shear (Figs. 4b–f and S4).Fig. 4Changes in genesis potential index (GPI) and the contributions of its components in response to CO₂ doubling (280–560 ppmv) analogous to a glacial–interglacial transition within the Late Paleozoic Ice Age (at 300 Ma).a Spatial pattern of GPI differences between the interglacial (CO₂ = 560 ppmv) and glacial (CO₂ = 280 ppmv) experiments during the storm season (see “Methods”). The latitudinal shift of cyclogenesis factors during the glacial–interglacial transitions is rooted in spatially heterogeneous atmospheric/oceanic warming patterns arising from higher CO2. In the Northern Hemisphere, the CO2-induced warming during boreal storm season (Jun–Nov) is relatively less intense between ~10 and 30°N compared to the deep tropics (<5°N) and higher latitudes (>30°N) at the sea surface, coupled with amplified mid-to-upper tropospheric heating relative to surface (Fig. 5a). This differential warming drives a significant weakening of the Hadley Cell, associated with anomalous descents between ~10 and 30°N but ascents along its two margins (Figs. 5b, S5, and S6). Concurrently, temperature increase at the upper troposphere is more profound at the tropics than at the higher latitudes (Fig. 5a). This leads to enhanced meridional temperature gradients at the south of 20°N but reduced gradients to the north, establishing a dipole structure in upper-level zonal winds and hence in vertical wind shear (Figs. 5 and S5). In addition, enhanced sea surface warming at ~30°–40°N relative to the lower latitudes (i.e., higher relative temperature) elevates potential intensity at these latitudes33,34 (Figs. S4 and S5), attributed to larger air–sea thermodynamic disequilibrium with increasing sea surface temperature. A parallel warming asymmetry emerges during austral storm seasons (December–May) in the Southern Hemisphere, characterized by amplified sea surface and upper-tropospheric warming in the deep tropics (<5°S) relative to higher latitudes (Fig. 5d). The uneven atmospheric warming significantly weakens the Hadley Cell (Figs. 5e and S6), driving anomalous subsidence over 10°–20°S (Fig. S5). At the same time, enhanced westerlies at 200 hPa between ~15 and 30°S and weakened zonal winds on either side mirror the tripolar pattern of the meridional temperature gradient, leading to increased vertical wind shear over the 15°–30° S band and reduced shear to the south (Figs. 5f, S4, and S5). Moreover, amplified mid-tropospheric warming relative to the surface weakens vertical thermal contrast, contributing to elevated moisture entropy deficit across tropical latitudes and suppressing storm genesis (Figs. S4 and S5). In addition, sea surface temperature rise is conducive to higher potential intensity, with more intense change at the higher latitudes that exhibits amplified warming (Figs. 5d, S4, and S5). In sum, these significant environmental conditions lead to an overall reduction but a poleward shift of TC genesis under doubled CO2 conditions during glacial–interglacial transitions, echoing the patterns modeled in the LPIA Northern Hemisphere.

Discussion

Through high-resolution climate simulations, we illustrate vigorous TC activity during Earth’s most prolonged Phanerozoic icehouse, characterized by pervasive TC genesis across tropical/subtropical latitudes and TC trajectories extending to high latitudes (~50–60°N/S). This modeled pattern aligns with widespread low-latitude storm deposits documented across both hemispheres during the LPIA (Fig. 1), hence resolving the paradox of icehouse TC behavior inferred from previous modeling7. The sustained TC potential likely arises from the unique paleogeography during the LPIA, which was characterized by less land area over the mid-high latitudes and reduced global surface albedo compared to the present day and supported stronger ocean heat transport and warmer high-latitude sea surface temperatures17. This offsets the relatively lower solar luminosity (~2.5% lower than modern) and maintains global temperatures comparable to preindustrial levels17. Under the simulated CO₂ doubling analogous to LPIA glacial–interglacial transitions, we identify an overall reduction in TC frequency but higher intensity, accompanied by poleward migration. The poleward shifts suggest that the geological storm deposits across the LPIA at mid-high latitudes may be the product of TCs. Nevertheless, the contribution of winter storms cannot be fully excluded, as these records are also from locations where winter storms were likely active (Fig. S7). Moreover, the shifts may contribute to enhanced landfalls over coastal regions of Gondwana during the LPIA (Fig. S8), potentially contributing to expanded vegetation (i.e., increased leaf area index) from glacial to interglacial states during the LPIA35, together with higher temperature. Additionally, enhanced TCs likely intensify upper-ocean vertical mixing and hence modify meridional and vertical thermal gradients14—a process with implications for marine provinciality and biodiversity fluctuations during this interval36,37. However, understanding the full environmental impacts of LPIA TCs remains a key target for future investigations.

The latitudinal expansion of TC activity under higher CO₂ forcing, as observed in our LPIA glacial–interglacial transition simulations, emerges as a robust feature across various warming scenarios. Modern observations document poleward migration of both TC genesis and lifetime maximum intensity under current warming38,39, albeit with regional heterogeneity. Paleostorm proxies corroborate this trend: showing northward-shifted landfall zones in the western North Pacific during warm intervals of the last millennium40 and latitudinal expansion of TC tracks over the South Indian Ocean41 and the North Atlantic42 during the warm mid-Holocene. Deep-time warming analogs further validate this pattern, with expanded cyclogenesis latitudes inferred for the mid-Pliocene warm period (~3 Ma)28, the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (~55 Ma)29, and the early Toarcian hyperthermal30. Despite different external forcings across past warming climates, this consensus underscores that warming-induced poleward migration of TC activity is likely a pervasive and climate state-independent characteristic of the Earth system43, although the quantitative magnitude and regional details may remain different. Indeed, poleward expansion of TCs is a key prediction of future warming scenarios27,44, and highlights the value of past warm periods for contextualizing future extreme climates.

In terms of the caveats to our analyses, we note that the low spatiotemporal resolution of storm deposits across the LPIA (e.g., better exposure or study of specific basins and time intervals) and the associated uncertainties in paleostorm locations complicate high-fidelity reconstructions of storm distributions. Moreover, storm deposits at the very low and mid-high latitudes during the LPIA can also be induced by large ocean swells generated by other processes and winter storms, respectively, which remain difficult to quantify. These factors preclude a full validation of the simulated TC patterns during the LPIA and the poleward displacement of TC activity during glacial–interglacial transitions. As such, the model-proxy comparisons presented here remain tentative. On the other hand, our use of prescribed sea surface temperatures (rather than active air–sea coupling) likely overestimates TC frequency and intensity45, and the 25-km resolution is insufficient to fully resolve Category 4-5 TCs46. However, the relative change between glacial and interglacial states should not be significantly influenced. To emphasize the role of varying CO2 concentration, we do not consider sea-level change in the glacial–interglacial experiments. Previous sensitivity experiments confirm that sea‑level fall during the LPIA has a limited effect on the pattern of global climate17 and thus may not bias the TC distribution predicted here. In addition, our simulations focus on the apex of the LPIA (~ 300 Ma) using published paleogeographic reconstructions47. Although these boundary conditions carry inherent uncertainties, we show that the zonal band pattern of TC genesis observed here is fully reproduced using an alternative reconstruction at 300 Ma48 (Fig. S9). Notably, paleogeographic conditions varied substantially across the ~100‑Myr duration of the LPIA, which may lead to marked changes in sea surface temperatures and consequently affect TC patterns. Despite these constraints, the integration of paleostorm proxies with high-resolution modeling offers a new perspective on icehouse TC activity and refines our understanding of LPIA climates. Our results may help improve understanding of extreme weather/climate events under future warming scenarios.

Methods

Sedimentary storm deposits

Marine sedimentary deposits provide key archives of deep-time storm activity. Although pioneering syntheses in the 1980s–1990s6–8 established a foundational framework for Paleozoic–Cenozoic storm records, the marked rise in documented storm deposits over recent decades calls for a focused re-evaluation of storm patterns during the LPIA. Here, we compiled 126 published sedimentary deposits that satisfy two essential criteria (Data S1 and Fig. S1): (i) the presence of storm-related sedimentary structures (e.g., hummocky cross-stratification) and (ii) deposition constrained temporally to the LPIA interval (~360–260 Ma). For each record, we extracted the following attributes from the original sources: location, stratigraphic unit, age, sedimentary features, depositional environment, climate regime, and storm type. Our compilation relied strictly on published interpretations, and we did not reassess whether individual records could be alternatively interpreted as non-storm depositional systems. Below, we detail the methodology used to derive the key attributes.

Numerical age

Ages (in Ma) were initially assigned based on the reported stratigraphic unit, primarily referenced to the Geological Society of America (GSA) geologic time scale. Where possible, ages were further constrained using recently published high-resolution stratigraphic and temporal frameworks. For example, the latest Carboniferous–Permian integrated stratigraphy and timescale for China49,50 was applied to deposits from this region. For eastern Australian records, we employed the high-resolution chronostratigraphic framework of Fielding et al.51. Moreover, because the majority of storm deposits were only constrained to a GSA age (at best) rather than a biozone or radio-isotopic date, we accounted for additional chronological uncertainties by adopting both minimum and maximum age bounds for each deposit (e.g., ~359–347 Ma for Tournaisian).

Paleolatitudes and paleolongitudes

Paleo-positions were reconstructed using the global plate tectonic model from the PALEOMAP Project52, ensuring consistency with the paleogeographic reconstruction used in our climate modeling47. In this way, the uncertainty in storm positions arises only from the estimated numerical ages (Data S1). However, it is important to note that paleo-coordinate estimates can differ substantially between alternative plate motion models—particularly between paleomagnetic and mantle reference frames—with increasing divergence back in time53,54.

Sedimentary features

Storm events disrupt ambient hydrodynamic conditions, commonly resulting in the deposition of discrete event beds (tempestites) within shallow marine environments55,56. A classic tempestite sequence (from bottom to top) comprises an erosional base, parallel lamination, hummocky cross-stratification (HCS), cross‑flat lamination, and bioturbated mudstone11. Among these, HCS is considered a key diagnostic structure for storm deposition. To standardize our compilation, we applied the following criteria: deposits preserving a relatively complete vertical succession as described above are termed “Tempestites”; those containing HCS alongside other storm-generated features (e.g., graded bedding) are classified as “HCS +”; all other diagnostic sedimentological evidence is explicitly described.

Depositional environment

Depositional settings were taken directly from source publications and range from backshore, shoreface, and offshore marine to lacustrine/fluvial. Preservation potential and depositional style vary substantially across these environments due to differences in hydrodynamic energy and sediment supply10.

Climate regime

We categorized the climatic context of each deposit at a broad scale (million-year resolution) as either glacial or non-glacial51,57, where possible. This long-term classification differs from higher-frequency (10⁵‑yr scale) CO₂-driven glacial–interglacial cycles, which remain hard to resolve owing to the temporal constraints of the deep-time storm record.

Storm type

Inferred storm origins include TCs, winter storms, tsunamis, and other weather events (e.g., flood-generated surges). Assignments fully follow the interpretations provided in the original references, with no additional inference introduced here. Accordingly, we did not evaluate the confidence level of those interpretations.

Data quality

Each record was assigned a quality rating (high or low) based on the completeness of the sedimentological evidence. High-rated records contain detailed sedimentological documentation, such as a complete vertical profile or multiple storm-diagnostic features. Low-rated records are those with only a single reported storm indicator or limited descriptive support. This rating reflects the detail of the deposit description, not the interpreted origin of the storm.

In addition, several storm deposits were located near the equator (Fig. 1), which seem poorly explained by TC activity. Although uncertainties exist in reconstructed paleo-locations, modern observations demonstrate that TCs can develop close to the equator, e.g., Typhoon Vamei at 1.4°N, which is partially attributed to cross-equatorial surge winds generating the background cyclonic vorticity required for equatorial TC genesis58. Furthermore, climate simulations performed here and for other hothouse states29 support the formation of TCs near the equator, but at a significantly lower frequency than in higher tropical latitudes (5–20°N/S). These results highlight the possibility of the formation of near-equatorial TCs during the LPIA, albeit rarely.

Community Earth System Model (CESM)

CESM1.2.259 is the state-of-the-art Earth system model developed primarily by the National Center for Atmospheric Research and has been widely used in paleoclimate studies. CESM1.2.2 is composed of four components that represent processes in the atmosphere, land, ocean, and sea ice, which exchange information via a central coupler. The atmospheric and land components used here are the Community Atmosphere Model version 5 (CAM5.3)60 and the Community Land Model version 4 (CLM4)61, respectively. The ocean component comes from the Parallel Ocean Program version 2 (POP2)62, with the Los Alamos Sea Ice Model (CICE) serving as the sea ice component. Regarding the fully coupled simulations for the LPIA, the horizontal resolution of CAM5.3 and CLM4 is set to 1.9° × 2.5°, with 30 vertical levels in CAM5.3. POP and CICE have a nominal 1° horizontal resolution, and POP has 60 vertical levels with 10-m resolution in the top 15 levels that increase to 250-m resolution for the deepest ocean. For the TC-permitting simulations, the horizontal resolution of CAM5 and CLM4 reaches 0.23° × 0.31°, with prescribed sea surface temperature and sea ice concentrations.

Experimental design

We used a two-step modeling framework to identify TC activity during the LPIA. First, we use the fully coupled version of CESM1.2.2 to model the global climate for the glacial and interglacial cases of the LPIA (i.e., CO2 = 280 and 560 ppmv according to the multiproxies-based reconstructions25,26), corresponding to Pennsylvanian endmembers. The two simulations use the same land–sea configurations at ∼300 Ma47, the evidence-constrained Carboniferous ice extent over Gondwana63, and the reconstructed middle-late Pennsylvanian biomes64. Total incoming solar irradiance is reduced by 2.5% to 1333 W m−2 relative to present65. The two experiments are integrated for >2000 years to let the modeled climates reach quasi-equilibrium (i.e., with no significant long-term trend in the last 100 years of model outputs). Detailed information on the experimental design is provided by Macarewich et al.66 and Macarewich and Poulsen17.

Next, we use the atmosphere-land coupled configuration of CESM (i.e., CAM5 and CLM4) at ~25-km resolution to perform TC downscaling simulations. In the ~25-km-resolution simulations, CAM5 is forced by prescribed monthly sea surface temperature and sea ice concentrations produced by the CESM coupled experiments. The boundary conditions, including land–sea configurations, CO2, and ice extent, are kept the same as those in the coupled runs. A pair of high-resolution (~25-km) glacial and interglacial experiments is performed with CO2 concentrations fixed at 280 and 560 ppmv, respectively. Each experiment is integrated for 31 years, and the last 28 years of model outputs are used for analysis.

TC detection and tracking

We use the TempestExtremes67 tool and the suggested methods68 to detect and track TCs using 6-hourly model outputs from the high-resolution simulations, which have been validated against the observations to produce the highest “hit rate minus false alarm rate” differential68. At each 6-h interval, we identify candidate storms as the points simultaneously having a sea-level pressure minimum and an upper-level warm core, ensuring the exclusion of extratropical disturbances. For the sea-level pressure minimum, we require that the mean sea-level pressure increases by 200 Pa within a 5.5° great-circle radius (GCD) from the candidate point. For the upper-level warm core, we require that geopotential height decreases by ≥6 m between 300 and 500 hPa over a 6.5° GCD, using the maximum value of this field within 1° GCD as reference. Note that we apply closed contour criteria in detecting sea-level pressure and geopotential height fields, and require spatial alignment between the local pressure low and upper-level warm core. In addition, candidates were discarded if a stronger geopotential minimum existed within 6° GCD, and we require that the lifetime maximum intensity is larger than 17 m s-1.

Next, we connect the candidate storms at each time step to establish TC trajectories only when they meet all the following criteria. First, we require that the maximum distance that a feature can move between subsequent detections must be within 8.0° GCD, in tandem with the persistence time of each trajectory ≥54 h and the maximum duration between two sequential detections ≤24 h. Next, wind speed at 10 m level is larger than 10 m s-1 for at least ten time slices, ensuring cyclonic features meet minimum TC intensity standards. Meantime, we require the latitudinal position of the feature to lie between 50°S and 50°N for at least ten time slices, aiming to remove any extratropical features that could not have existed as tropical storms. Finally, we require the feature to exist at an elevation below 150 m for at least ten time slices to eliminate false alarms caused by rough topography.

To estimate the relative contribution of each genesis factor to the GPI change during the glacial–interglacial transitions, we recalculate the GPI with three genesis factors fixed at the glacial state and the remaining one during the interglacial state, using climatological means to avoid the influence of interannual variability. In this way, the contribution of an interested factor is defined as the difference between the recalculated GPI and the original GPI, which is then converted to a percent change by dividing the original GPI change between interglacial and glacial cases.
