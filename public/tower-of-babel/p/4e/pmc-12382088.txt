Multimodal imaging in young male with bicuspid aortic valve, right-sided aorto-atrial fistula and single coronary artery
Authors: Marco Fabio Costantino, Gianpaolo D’Addeo, Stefano Mancino, Luisiana Stolfi, Teresa Mannarino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A 35-year-old male, without significant cardiovascular history, presented with recurrent palpitations. Initial echocardiographic evaluation demonstrated eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy, mild systolic dysfunction, suspicion of a ventricular septal defect, bicuspid aortic valve, and right ventricular dilation. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed an aneurysmal dilation of the right coronary sinus with an aorto-atrial/ventricular fistula, further confirmed by contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography. Coronary angiography identified a single anomalous coronary artery with left dominance and absence of the right coronary artery. Surgical repair successfully closed the fistula, with mild post-operative aortic regurgitation. Follow-up at one year indicated normalization of cardiac dimensions and function, with stable moderate aortic valve regurgitation. Genetic sequencing found no identifiable mutations. Regular monitoring was recommended due to the potential risk of complications related to the bicuspid aortic valve and coronary anomaly.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old male presented to the emergency department with recurrent episodes of palpitations. He reported intermittent sensations of rapid heartbeats over the past few months, occasionally accompanied by mild shortness of breath. He denied chest pain, syncope, or other cardiovascular symptoms. His medical history was unremarkable, with no known congenital conditions or prior cardiac interventions. Initial physical examination revealed a regular heart rhythm with no audible murmurs. Vital signs were within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was preformed to assess cardiac structure and function. The study revealed eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy with mild systolic dysfunction. Additionally, there was a suspicion of a ventricular septal defect (VSD) with bidirectional shunt flow. The right ventricle was dilated; however, its systolic function remained preserved. The patient had a systemic arterial pressure of 120 mmHg, with an estimated pressure gradient of 45 mmHg across the shunt, resulting in a right ventricular pressure of 75 mmHg. These findings supported the presence of significant left-to-right volume overload and were consistent with the right ventricular dilation observed on imaging. The aortic valve appeared bicuspid but was neither stenotic nor regurgitant and therefore there was no immediate indication for valve replacement. To further evaluate these findings, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed (Fig. 1). This imaging modality provided detailed visualization of an aneurysmal dilation of the right coronary sinus with an evident fissure, leading to a left-to-right shunt into the right atrium. Notably, a single coronary artery was identified, originating anomalously. For accurate 3D delineation of the fistula’s trajectory and its anatomical relationships with surrounding structures, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) angiography with three-dimensional coronary reconstruction was undertaken (Fig. 2). This imaging was particularly valuable given the suspected involvement of both atrial and ventricular chambers and the coexisting complex coronary anomaly, allowing detailed non-invasive anatomical assessment to guide surgical planning. The CT confirmed a right-sided aorto-atrial/ventricular fistula with a 17 mm communication near the tricuspid valve (Figs. 3 and 4). The right coronary sinus was ectatic, corroborating the left-to-right shunt observed in echocardiographic studies. There were no clinical, echocardiographic, or laboratory signs of infective endocarditis—specifically, no fever, vegetations, positive blood cultures, or elevated inflammatory markers—at presentation or during follow-up. While a congenital origin of the fistula is favored given the associated structural anomalies, a previous undiagnosed endocarditic event cannot be entirely excluded. On coronary angiography, coronary circulation was characterized as left-dominant, with a high origin of the left main coronary artery (Fig. 5). The right coronary artery was absent, functionally replaced by a diminutive branch stemming from the mid-left anterior descending artery. Given the complexity of the anomalies and the risk of progressive cardiac dysfunction, surgical intervention was deemed necessary. The patient underwent an aortotomy, during which the perforation in the anterior coronary sinus was closed. Intraoperative TEE confirmed the successful closure of the shunt, with only mild aortic valve regurgitation observed post-repair. At the time of surgery, the bicuspid aortic valve was neither stenotic nor hemodynamically regurgitant, and a conservative management strategy was adopted to avoid unnecessary valve replacement in a young patient with potentially reversible ventricular dysfunction, and to minimize surgical risk. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. At both 3-month and 12-month follow-ups, TTE assessments demonstrated normalization of cardiac dimensions and function. The bicuspid aortic valve exhibited moderate regurgitation, while the right ventricular and atrial dimensions and functions remained within normal parameters. A myocardial perfusion single-photon emission tomography performed one year post-surgery showed normal perfusion. To explore potential genetic associations among the bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), the aorto-atrial/ventricular fistula, and the single coronary artery, comprehensive genetic sequencing was conducted. The panel included major genes associated with congenital heart disease and BAV (e.g., NOTCH1, SMAD6, GATA5), aortic arch anomalies, and coronary malformations. The analysis did not reveal any mutations known to link these anomalies.​ Although no pathogenic variants were identified, regular follow-up and, when appropriate, familial screening were recommended given the possibility of undetected or novel genetic factors. Discussion

This case presents a rare constellation of cardiovascular anomalies: a BAV, aorto-atrial/ventricular fistula, and a single coronary artery. While each of these anomalies has been documented individually, their concurrent presentation is exceedingly uncommon. Aorto-atrial fistulas are abnormal communications between the aortic root and the atrial chamber, often resulting from congenital defects, infective endocarditis, or trauma. Such fistulas can lead to significant left-to-right shunting, volume overload, and eventual heart failure if left untreated [1]. In our patient, invasive pressure measurements confirmed a significant gradient of 45 mmHg across the shunt, with elevated right ventricular pressure (75 mmHg) and preserved systemic pressure (120 mmHg), supporting the diagnosis of relevant volume overload and the need for surgical correction. Onorato et al. [2] reported a case of a ruptured posterior non-coronary Valsalva sinus aneurysm leading to an aorto-right atrial fistula, highlighting the potential for sudden heart failure in such scenarios. In our case, no evidence of infective endocarditis was found—clinically or on imaging—supporting a congenital origin. However, the possibility of a previous, subclinical or healed endocarditic process cannot be completely ruled out. The presence of a single coronary artery, a rare congenital anomaly where only one coronary artery arises from the aortic trunk, further complicates the clinical picture. The association between bicuspid aortic valve and single coronary artery is notably rare. Rashid et al. [3] documented a case where these two anomalies coexisted, emphasizing the importance of thorough diagnostic evaluation in such patients. Furthermore, Bertin et al. [4] described a patient undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation with both a single coronary artery and a bicuspid valve, underscoring the complexities involved in managing such cases.​​ The coexistence of these three anomalies together has been scarcely reported ([5]– [6]).

The absence of identifiable genetic mutations linking these anomalies in our patient suggests a sporadic occurrence rather than a syndromic association. The extended genetic panel included relevant genes implicated in BAV and congenital heart malformations (NOTCH1, SMAD6, GATA5, among others), but no mutations were identified. Nonetheless, given the complexity of the case, ongoing clinical monitoring and consideration of familial evaluation remain appropriate, underscoring the necessity for individualized surveillance and management strategies. This case emphasizes the importance of multimodal imaging in diagnosing complex congenital heart defects. The combined use of TTE and TEE was essential to detect the shunt and valvular abnormalities, while contrast-enhanced CT angiography was crucial for defining the anatomical relationships and confirming the presence of a single coronary artery. This comprehensive imaging approach was especially helpful for surgical planning and reduced diagnostic uncertainty. Regular follow-up is essential to monitor the progression of valvular function and detect any potential complications early.
