Identification of key genes associated with overwintering in Histia rhodope larva using gene co-expression network analysis
Authors: Haibo Yang, Shien Pang, Shanshan Guo, Yue Wang, Mengchao Kong, Pinhong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHistia rhodope (Cramer) (Lepidoptera: Zygaenidae) is an important pest that damages the landscape of Bischofia polycarpa (Levl.) trees. Its larval feeding on the leaves impairs tree growth and urban landscapes, causing significant impacts on local economic and ecological benefits. The insect often overwinters as a cocoon of mature larvae in places such as leaf litter, brick crevices, and tree bark. The low temperatures in winter directly affect the distribution of insect populations, so it is important to explore the molecular mechanisms of overwintering in H. rhodope.ResultsRNA-seq technology was used to obtain transcriptome data from 5 different overwintering stages of H. rhodope, and 30 genes related to cold tolerance were screened from the annotation information of differentially expressed gene database (DEGs). Two modules (blue and green) were found to be associated with overwintering by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), identifying translocon-associated protein, thioredoxin, glycine cleavage system H protein, ribosomal proteins, and ATP synthase genes as hub genes. In addition, cytochrome P450 genes were screened. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis showed that 60 S and 40s ribosomal proteins, putative serine/threonine protein kinase, and heat shock protein 90 had the highest number of direct interactions with other proteins. They might help the larvae successfully overwinter. Ten selected genes were subjected to qRT-PCR to verify the reliability of RNA-seq results.ConclusionsIn this study, we obtained transcriptomic data from five different overwintering stages of H. rhodope, revealing key genes associated with this process. This provides a theoretical basis for further research on the molecular mechanisms of overwintering in H. rhodope.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12136-1.Keywords: Histia rhodope, Overwintering, Cold tolerance, RNA-seq, WGCNA, Hub gene

Introduction

Temperature affects insect growth, development, and population dynamics [1, 2].Cold temperatures in winter threaten the survival of insects. Some insects avoid cold temperature stress through behavioral strategies. For example, the fall armyworm, Spodoptera frugiperda (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), escapes damage fromcold temperatures by migrating [3], while Anomala corpulenta (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae) larvae burrow deep into the soil to avoid physiological damage caused by exposure to low temperatures [4]. In addition, some insects can also adapt to low temperatures by regulating physiological and biochemical substances to improve cold tolerance. For instance, Streltzoviella insularis (Lepidoptera: Cossidae) larvae reduce water content during overwintering to mitigate damage caused by freezing [5]. Freezing avoidance insects, such as Patrobus septentrionis(Coleoptera: Carabidae) and Calathus melanocephalus (Coleoptera: Carabidae) enhance cold resistance by removing ice nucleating agents from their bodies [6, 7]. The ability of insects to tolerate cold directly affects population distribution, dispersal, and the dynamics of their occurrence in the following year [8, 9].

Insect adaptation to low-temperature stress involves the synergistic regulation ofmultiple genes. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) can reveal gene expression under specific stress conditions and is also applicable to insects without a reference genome [10–12]. This greatly expands the scope of its application; as a result, many studies have used RNA-seq to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying insect adaptation to extreme temperatures, such as in Thaumatotibia leucotreta (Lepidoptera: Tortricidae) [13] and Locusta migratoria tibetensis (Orthoptera: Oedipodidae) [14]. However, the data obtained from transcriptome sequencing is massive. Rapidly finding key genes within the transcriptome sequencing data is crucial for subsequent research and experimental validation, which saving significant time and resources. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) can efficiently integrate a large amount of data and screen it for key gene modules and hub genes that are highly associated with traits [15]. For instance, transcriptome sequencing combined with WGCNA has revealed key genes associated with overwintering in Anoplophora glabripennis (Coleoptera: Cerambycidae) [16] and wax biosynthesis in Ericerus pela (Hemiptera: Coccidae) [17].

Histia rhodope (Cramer) (Lepidoptera: Zygaenidae) is an important pest that damages the landscape of Bischofia polycarpa (Levl.) trees [18]. This insect has four generations per year in China, with four life stages: egg, larva, pupa, andadult. Its larvae feed on the leaves, which affects the growth of trees. In severe cases, they can consume all the leaves, leading to the death of the trees. The mature larvae of H. rhodope spin silk threads to descend, disrupting pedestrian traffic and urban aesthetics. The insect often overwinters as a cocoon of mature larvae in places such as leaf litter, brick crevices, and tree bark [18, 19]. The wintering period generally begins around late October and ends in April or May of the following year. This pest is primarily distributed in Southeast Asia, including Myanmar and India, as well as in the southern regions of the Yangtze River in China [20]. In recent years, it has also been reported in Henan and Shandong. With global warming, ecological changes, and the frequent occurrence of extreme weather, the range of H. rhodope is showing a tendency to continue spreading northward [21, 22]. At present, studies on the cold tolerance of H. rhodope are focused on the determination of physiological substances such as glycerol, lipids, and water content, as well as the supercooling point [23]. Although the study of biochemical substances in the overwintering process has provided an important basis for understanding the cold tolerance mechanism, the expression of relevant regulatory genes during overwintering remains unknown. Clarifying the molecular mechanisms of cold tolerance in H. rhodope can help predict and manage the dynamics of its population occurrence.

To obtain the transcriptomic information on the overwintering process of H. rhodope and to explore the key genes related to this process, this study utilized RNA-seq technology combined with WGCNA to investigate the molecular mechanisms involved in the overwintering of H. rhodope.

Materials and methods

Collection of test insect sources and temperature data

Mature larvae of the overwintering generation of H. rhodope were collected in late October 2022 on the campus of Henan University of Science and Technology (112.45°E, 34.64°N) in Luoyang City, Henan Province, China. The collected larvae were packed into 50-compartment cryogenic boxes (L×W×H = 150 mm×70 mm×60 mm), with one larva placed in each compartment for cocooning.The boxes were then stored outdoors for future use. Normal cocooned larvae were used as test insects on October 28th, 2022, November 23rd, 2022, January 3rd, 2023, February 16th, 2023, and March 9th, 2023, respectively. The dates October 28th, 2022, November 23rd, 2022, January 3rd, 2023, February 16th, 2023, and March 9th, 2023 correspond approximately to the solar terms of First Frost, Light Snow, Minor Cold, Rain Water, and Awakening of Insects, respectively. The average temperatures on these five dates were 15.27 °C, 10.55 °C, 2.22 °C, 6.17 °C, and 16.89 °C. The samples were labeled as overwintering stages A, B, C, D, and E according to the five sequential collection times. Five larvae were collected at a time, and were replicated 3 times, i.e., a total of 15 larvae collected each time. Outdoor temperatures were recorded with a HOBO data logger (U12-012, Onset Computer Corporation, Bourne, MA, USA). Recorded four times a day. Based on the monitoring results, the average temperature, average maximum temperature, and average minimum temperature of each month were counted.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

According to the standard extraction method, TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to extract total RNA from larvae of H. rhodope. The purity, concentration, and integrity of the RNA samples were evaluated using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA), an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. Library construction was performed using the Illumina TruseqTM RNA sample prep Kit. After library construction, initial quantification was performed using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, and the library was diluted to 1.5 ng/µL. The insert size of the library was then detected using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Upon confirming the insert size met expectations, the effective concentration of the library was accurately quantified via qRT-PCR (with library effective concentration > 2 nm) to ensure library quality. After the libraries passed quality control, they were pooled based on their effective concentrations and the desired data yield, followed by paired-end sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, with a read length of 2 × 150 bp. Three biological replicates were set for each RNA sample. The extraction of RNA, cDNA library construction, and sequencing were completed by Shanghai Origingene Bio-Pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. As there is no reference genome available for the H. rhodope transcriptome, the quality of the raw sequencing reads should be assessed using the FastQC (version 0.10.1). The quality filtering of raw sequencing data involves removing adapter sequences from reads and trimming low-quality read ends (Phred score < Q20). The filtered reads were then assembled using Trinity (v2.8.5) software to obtain transcripts. The longest transcript from each gene cluster was selected as a unigene for subsequent analysis. The assembly yielded 231,780 unigenes and 337,639 transcripts, with an N50 contig length of 863 bp. Raw sequencing data from this study have been submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), accession number PRJNA613658.

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

To obtain functional annotation information, the resulting unigenes were compared with the NR, Swiss-Prot, STRING, GO, and KEGG databases using Diamond (v2.0.8) software, with the comparison threshold set at e-value < 10−⁵. STRING is a database for predicting and visualizing protein-protein interaction networks. GO is a database designed to provide a unified description of the biological functions of genes or proteins in organisms. KEGG is a major bioinformatics database that provides diverse biological information, including genomes, metabolic pathways, diseases, and drugs. DESeq2 was used for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis between the two treatment groups (A vs. B, B vs.C, C vs. D, D vs. E). The Benjamini-Hochberg method was employed to correct for multiple hypothesis testing of the p-value from the difference test, with an FDR < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 set as the screening conditions for DEGs.

The distribution of DEGs based on gene function annotations was analyzed inthe GO database (http://www.geneontology.org/), categorizing genes according tobiological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF).Analysis of metabolic pathways and signal transduction pathways involving DEGs was conducted for enrichment using the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). The reference organisms were selected from closely related species within the order Lepidoptera. A corrected p-value < 0.05 was considered significant for GO terms and KEGG pathways.

Construction and analysis of weighted co-expression networks

WGCNA is a classical analytical method used in the field of systems biology to resolve gene-trait association patterns. WGCNA was performed using the R package, which is available at https://horvath.genetics.ucla.edu/html/CoexpressionNetwork/Rpackages/WGCNA/. The co-expression modules were identified by clustering based on intergenic dissimilarity using the dynamic tree cut algorithm. Heat mapping of modules was conducted in association with different overwintering stages, along with further analyses. We chose β = 12, minimum module size = 30, and merge cut height = 0.25 (Fig. S1).

Screening of hub gene

The regulatory network among the genes was further explored by WGCNA analysis, and the hub gene within the module was identified. The genes in the hub module were analyzed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org/) to construct protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. Interaction network visualizations were created using Cytoscape software.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Ten genes were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. Primers were designed using Primer (v6.00), and β-Actin was chosen as the reference gene. qRT-PCR analysiswas performed using the Bio-Rad CFX Manager and SYBR Green Master Mix (Table S1). Each reaction contained 5 µL of SYBR Green Mix, 0.5 µL of each primer, 2 µL of cDNA template, and 2.5 µL of ddH2O. The qRT-PCR reaction program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 44 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. Standard dissolution curves were generated from 60 °C to 90 °C to analyzethe specificity of target amplification. Each sample included three biological replicates and technical replicates.

Statistical analysis

The relative expression of genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Before data analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data, and the Levene’s test was used to test for homogeneity of variances. Statistical analysis of qRT-PCR data was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and least significant difference (LSD) method using with IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Overwintering stage A served as the control group.

Analysis of DEGs among different overwintering stages

A total of 8,649 DEGs were identified across the 5 overwintering stages of H.rhodope, with more DEGs found in the comparisons of A vs. B and D vs. E, which had 2,334 and 4,963 DEGs, respectively. Moreover, 765 and 578 DEGs were identified in the comparisons of B vs. C and C vs. D, respectively (Fig. 1A). The four comparison groups of overwintering stages share three DEGs. Thirteen genes are shared among A vs. B, C vs. D, and D vs. E, with enrichment in oxidoreductase activity. Thirty-seven genes are shared in B vs. C and Cvs. D, with enrichment in ribonucleotide binding (Fig. 1B).

Functional annotation and analysis of DEGs

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

GO analysis of DEGs from comparison groups of overwintering stage (A vs. B, B vs. C, C vs. D, D vs. E) was conducted, with classification into three categories: MF, BP, and CC. A total of 33 significantly enriched terms were identified, with 27 and 6 significantly enriched GO items in C vs. D and D vs. E, respectively. The highest enrichment in C vs. D was for ‘structural molecule activity’ and ‘structural constituent of ribosome’ in MF, which included 35 and 30 DEGs, respectively. Among the most enriched terms in D vs. E were ‘non-membrane-bounded organelle’ and ‘intracellular non-membrane-bounded organelle’ in CC, both containing 84 DEGs. Among these significantly enriched GO terms, the most significant GO terms in MF, CC, and BP are respectively ‘structural molecule activity’, ‘ribosomal subunit’, and ‘translation’ (Table S3).

To further clarify the distribution of DEGs across pathways in the comparison groups of the four overwintering stages of H. rhodope, DEGs were subjected to KEGG functional enrichment analysis. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the pathway that was significantly enriched in A vs. B was ‘Peroxisome’ (ko04146), which included seven DEGs. ‘Ribosome’ (ko03010) was a significantly enriched pathway in both C vs. D and D vs. E, with 29 and 30 DEGs, respectively (Table S4).

DEGs associated with cold tolerance

Based on functional annotation information, a total of 30 DEGs related to cold tolerance were screened, including heat shock proteins (Hsp), glycerol phosphate dehydrogenase (GPDH), glycerol kinase (GK), fatty acid desaturase (FAD), trehalose transporter (TRET), lipase, and other related genes (Table S5). Six genes (DN17335_c1_g1, DN277_c0_g5, DN6620_c0_g1, DN12150_c1_g1, DN600_c0_g1, DN1007_c0_g1) and 3 genes (DN53553_c0_g1, DN1591_c2_g1, DN1591_c2_g2) were highly expressed in the overwintering C and D stages, respectively, mainly consisting of glycerol kinase and heat shock proteins. Thirteen genes (DN2785_c0_g2, DN3395_c0_g1, DN4146_c1_g1, DN25526_c0_g1, DN1360_c0_g1, DN3960_c1_g2, DN3513_c0_g1, DN377_c0_g1, DN2074_c11_g1, DN2762_c0_g1, DN43456_c0_g1, DN1327_c0_g2, DN500_c5_g1) were expressed at higher levels in the overwintering stages B, C, and D, mainly comprising heat shock proteins, lipase, and fatty acid desaturase (Fig. 2). Among the ambient temperatures in the five stages of overwintering, the temperatures in stages B, C, and D were lower compared to those in stages A and E, with stage C reaching the lowest (Fig. S2). The genes with higher expression levels during the overwintering stages B, C, and D were associated with enhanced cold resistance.

Gene co-expression network analysis

A dynamic cut tree algorithm was used for clustering and module division of genes, separating them into eight modules. The blue module and the green module showed a positive correlation in stages C and D, and both were significantly positively correlated in stage D (Fig. 3A, B). Ambient temperature trends within the A-E overwintering phase were also considered. Stages A and E experienced relatively high environmental temperatures, while stages B, C, and D had relatively low temperatures (Fig. S2). The expression of genes in the blue and green modules showed different degrees of increase during the overwintering period, suggesting that the genes in these two modules play an important role in overwintering in H. rhodope (Fig. 3C).

qRT-PCR validation

To verify the reliability of RNA-seq, 10 genes were selected (DN1327_c0_g2, DN25526_c0_g1, DN43456_c0_g1, DN12150_c1_g1, DN3960_c1_g2, DN3513_c0_g1, DN16573_c0_g2, DN1702_c0_g1, DN2398_c0_g1, DN1055_c0_g1) for qRT-PCR analysis. The results showed that the expression change trends of these genes were generally consistent with those obtained from RNA-seq (Fig. 5). The expression levels were higher in the overwintering stages B, C, and D than in stages A and E. This analysis confirmed the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing.

Discussion

Temperate and boreal insects have developed a well-established cold-tolerance strategy over a long evolutionary period to adapt to low winter temperatures [9]. In this study, we screened 30 genes that may be related to cold tolerance, mainly including lipase, GPDH, FAD, TRET, Hsp, and other relevant genes.

Insects that stop feeding during overwintering consume fat for energy [24]. During this period, lipase can break down fat to form glycerol [25]. Our previous research on the physiological changes during the overwintering period of H. rhodope larvae showed that the fat content is highest at the beginning of overwintering and then decreases throughout the overwintering process [23], a trend also observed in Hylurgus ligniperda (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) [26]. In this study, the gene expression of lipase (DN1327_c0_g2, DN25526_c0_g1) increased during overwintering and decreased in the later part of the overwintering period. We speculate that these two genes are involved in fat breakdown during overwintering.

Cloning of a desaturase gene (Dadesat), which possibly encodes Δ9-acyl-CoA desaturase from Delia antiqua (Diptera: Anthomyiidae), demonstrates that the up-regulation of desaturase gene expression is associated with enhanced cold tolerance [27]. Moreover, the polyunsaturated fatty acid content of Lipoptena cervi (Diptera: Hippoboscidae) was high in late autumn, which was beneficial for maintaining the fluidity of lipid membranes [28]. In this study, the gene expression of FAD (DN3513_c0_g1) was increased during the overwintering process, with the highest expression found in the stage D. We hypothesize that the increased expression of this gene during overwintering produces unsaturated fatty acids that prevent membrane lipids from crystallizing at low temperatures [29].

Glycerol is a common cryoprotectant [30, 31]. Glycerol synthesis is associated with GPDH and GK [32]. Helicoverpa assulta (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) exhibited elevated expression levels of Has-GPDH and Has-GK after rapid cold hardening (RCH) treatment [33]. It was shown through RNAi experiments that GPDH and GK enhance the cold tolerance of red imported fire ant, Solenopsis invicta (Hymenoptera: Formicidae), by participating in glycerol synthesis [34]. In this study, the GK genes (DN1007_c0_g1, DN277_c0_g5) and the GPDH gene (DN600_c0_g1) were highly expressed during the stage C. We hypothesize that these genes may be involved in glycerol synthesis in H. rhodope larvae during overwintering. To further validate this, the glycerol content and cold resistance of H. rhodope could be examined by knocking down the GK and GPDH genes using RNA interference.

Trehalose is the hemolymph sugar of insects and serves as an energy and protective agent [35–37]. Research on physiological changes in overwintering Ocneria terebinthina (Lepidoptera: Lymantriidae) has found that trehalose levels increase with decreasing environmental temperatures [38]. Increased levels of the trehalose transporter were observed in Lissorhoptrus oryzophilus (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) adults treated with RCH [39]. The results showed that the expression of TRET(DN3960_c1_g2) increased during the overwintering process, reaching the highest level at the stage D. This gene is involved in the transport of trehalose synthesized in the fat body to the hemolymph in response to low temperature stress [40].

HSP respond to external stimuli and help organisms cope with environmental stresses [41]. In the diapause termination study of Sitodiplosis mosellana (Diptera: Cecidomyiidae), it was found that the expression levels of Hsp70 and Hsp90 increased under a low temperature treatment of 4 °C [42]. The expression of Hsp90 in the larvae of S. exigua was observed in response to high-temperature stress [43]. In this study, the expression of Hsp gene (DN43456_c0_g1) was up-regulated in stages B and D, while the expression level of Hsp gene (DN12150_c1_g1) increased during the overwintering stages B, C, and D, reaching its highest level at stage D. We speculate that heat shock proteins play a role in overwintering in H. rhodope, but the mechanism of regulation of cold hardiness may differ due to functional variations within the Hsp family. Their specific functions require further validation.

In the gene co-expression network, TRAP, TRX, GCSH, acyl-CoA desaturase 4-like, 40 S ribosomal protein, 60 S ribosomal protein, and ATP synthase subunit were identified as hub genes. TRAP is an endoplasmic reticulum membrane protein involved in the transport of nascent polypeptides [44]. The TRAP gene(DN3151_c0_g3) may be associated with the transport of nascent proteins involved in the cold resistance of H. rhodope. The GCSH gene (DN2273_c0_g1) is related to amino acid metabolism [45], and low temperature also affects amino acid metabolism. ATP synthase is involved in ATP synthesis [46, 47]. The expression of the ATP synthase subunit gene (DN2398_c0_g1) increased during overwintering, reaching a maximum at stage D. The cold temperatures during overwintering stimulated ATP synthase to produce ATP.

Under extreme temperatures, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulate and induce antioxidant defense systems to counteract oxidative stress [48]. Oxidoreductase activity was also detected in the differentially expressed genes shared among A vs. B, C vs. D, and D vs. E. Thioredoxin has antioxidant properties [49]. Up-regulation of the thioredoxin gene occurs in Ostrinia nubilalis (Lepidoptera: Crambidae) larvae following cold acclimation [50]. We also observed a cytochrome P450 (CYP450) gene in the gene co-expression network. The cytochrome P450 family metabolizes a variety of exogenous and endogenous compounds [51]. The CYP450 gene (AccCYP336A1), isolated from the Chinese honeybee Apis cerana cerana (Hymenoptera: Apidae), plays a role in defense against ROS damage [52]. Additionally, CYP450 genes are upregulated in three rice planthoppers in response to low temperatures [53]. In this study, we demonstrated that the CYP450 gene (DN90_c1_g1) and Trx gene (DN1725_c1_g1) may contribute to the adaptation of H. rhodope to winter cold temperatures by participating in antioxidant stress responses.

In the PPI, ribosomal proteins were found to have the highest number of interactions and were also screened in the gene co-expression network visualization. Ribosomes are essential for cell growth and play a key role in protein synthesis [54]. The results showed that the expression of ribosomal protein genes RPS23 (DN1702_c0_g1), RPL40 (DN16573_c0_g2) and RPS8 (DN1055_c0_g1) was highly elevated during the overwintering D stage. ‘Ribosome’ was also identified in the KEGG enrichment analysis. The enhanced expression of ribosomal protein genes during overwintering may be related to the synthesis of antifreeze proteins and HSP. Additionally, STPK was identified in the PPI. STPK catalyzes the phosphorylation of proteins involved in multiple signaling pathways and energy metabolism [55, 56]. The STPK gene identified in cold tolerance research of A. c. cerana [57]. We hypothesize that STPK is involved in the cold tolerance of H. rhodope, but its specific role requires further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified genes involved in cold tolerance (Hsp, FAD, GK, Lipase, and TRET), mined hub genes that regulated the overwintering process (TRAP, TRX, GCSH, ATP synthase, and RP), and found that the PPI analysis revealed the STPK genes. These genes may play a key role in the overwintering cold tolerance of H. rhodope. This study provides a theoretical foundation for further elucidating the molecular mechanisms of overwintering in H. rhodope, predicting population dynamics by utilizing gene expression levels to forecast overwintering survival rates, and developing novel biological agents.
