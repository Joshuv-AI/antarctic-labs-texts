Early-pregnancy glucose–lipid signatures and metabolic heterogeneity of gestational diabetes with extension to hypertensive comorbidity in singleton and twin pregnancies: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Minhuan Lin, Zhaoran Meng, Yiqing Chen, Shuting Xia, Yingnan Ye, Ting Ye, Jian Cai, Linhuan Huang, Li Wen, Yanmin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly pregnancy is a critical window for metabolic adaptation, marked by coordinated regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism. Disruption of this balance may contribute to gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a condition increasingly recognized as metabolically heterogeneous. Whether early-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices capture this heterogeneity and extend to hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter cohort included 13,419 pregnancies (11,765 singleton and 1,654 twin pregnancies). Fasting glucose and lipid measurements obtained at 5–16 weeks of gestation were used to derive eight composite indices, including the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, SPISE, AIP, CHG, NHHR, and lnRC. Multinomial logistic regression evaluated associations with GDM subtypes (post-load, isolated fasting, and combined GDM), using normoglycemia as the reference. Extension analyses assessed GDM–HDP phenotypes (GDM-only, HDP-only, and combined GDM–HDP) relative to normoglycemic–normotensive pregnancies. Restricted cubic spline models examined dose–response relationships, and receiver operating characteristic analyses assessed discriminative performance.ResultsEarly-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices showed a clear gradient across GDM subtypes, with progressively stronger associations from post-load GDM to isolated fasting GDM and combined GDM; adjusted odds ratios (ORs) per unit increase in TyG index were 2.54, 6.21, and 8.91, respectively. When analyses were extended to hypertensive outcomes, the strongest association was observed for combined GDM–HDP (adjusted OR, 4.81; 95% confidence interval, 3.35–6.89). Significant interaction with prepregnancy overweight status was observed for TyG-BMI, with more pronounced heterogeneity across HDP-related phenotypes, whereas associations were directionally similar across singleton and twin pregnancies after accounting for center composition. For the highest-risk phenotypes, area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.786 for combined GDM (TyG index and TyG-BMI) and 0.811 for combined GDM–HDP (TyG-BMI). A significant interaction between fasting glucose and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol was detected for both combined GDM (interaction OR 3.61) and combined GDM–HDP (interaction OR 3.79).ConclusionsEarly-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices are associated with a graded pattern of metabolic heterogeneity across GDM subtypes, and a similar pattern is observed for hypertensive comorbidity. These findings are consistent with shared metabolic perturbations underlying glycemic and vascular complications during pregnancy and may inform future approaches to early-pregnancy risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03009-x.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Insulin resistance, Lipid metabolism, Pregnancy-induced hypertension, Twin pregnancy, Triglyceride–glucose index

Background

Pregnancy is characterized by progressive insulin resistance (IR) and dynamic lipid remodeling that support fetoplacental growth [1, 2]. Although these adaptations are physiological, disruption of metabolic homeostasis may contribute to the development of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) as well as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), both of which are associated with long-term cardiometabolic risk [3–6].

GDM is now widely considered a metabolically heterogeneous disease rather than a uniform glycemic entity [7]. Subtypes defined by fasting and post-load glucose abnormalities reflect differences in IR, hepatic glucose production, and β-cell compensation [8]. In parallel, dyslipidemia, particularly lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentrations and higher triglyceride (TG) levels, has emerged as a key component linking metabolic disturbance to vascular dysfunction in pregnancy [9].

Composite glucose–lipid indices derived from fasting TG and glucose measurements, such as the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index, serve as convenient indicators of integrated metabolic status [10–12]. Although these indices have been associated with overall GDM risk, their relationships with GDM subtypes remain insufficiently characterized [13–15]. Moreover, GDM and HDP share overlapping metabolic and endothelial pathways [9], raising the question of whether early metabolic profiles associated with GDM heterogeneity also extend to GDM–HDP phenotypes.

Twin pregnancies are characterized by increased metabolic demand and altered lipid metabolism [16–18], yet evidence in this population remains limited. Evaluation of glucose–lipid indices across singleton and twin gestations may help distinguish shared metabolic patterns from context-specific metabolic patterns.

Therefore, this study examined associations between early-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices and clinically distinct GDM subtypes and further evaluated whether these associations extend to hypertensive comorbidity in singleton and twin pregnancies. By jointly evaluating GDM subtypes and GDM–HDP phenotypes, this study sought to address a gap in the current understanding of metabolic heterogeneity during pregnancy.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in China (Hospital A: the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University, Guangzhou; Hospital B: Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Chongqing). The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of both institutions. Given the de-identified, retrospective design, informed consent was waived.

All deliveries at Hospital A between August 2018 and March 2025 and at Hospital B between December 2021 and June 2025 were screened. The two hospitals applied identical clinical inclusion and exclusion criteria. However, during the study period, routine early-pregnancy lipid screening at Hospital B was implemented primarily for twin pregnancies; therefore, only twin pregnancies from Hospital B met the biochemical eligibility criteria for inclusion in the analytic cohort. Inclusion criteria were: (1) singleton or twin pregnancy; (2) availability of early-pregnancy fasting plasma glucose (FPG) together with fasting lipid measurements, including HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), and TG, obtained at 5–16 weeks of gestation after at least 8 h of fasting; and (3) delivery at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation. Exclusion criteria included preexisting diabetes, prediabetes, chronic hypertension, stillbirth, or missing second-/third-trimester FPG or oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results.

Plasma glucose was measured using the glucose oxidase method. TC and TG were quantified using enzymatic colorimetric assays, whereas HDL-C and LDL-C were measured using direct enzymatic methods. Both laboratories participated in the National Center for Clinical Laboratories External Quality Assessment (EQA) program throughout the study period, supporting intersite comparability of biochemical measurements.

Data collection and outcome

Baseline characteristics were extracted from electronic records and included maternal age, gravidity, parity, conception mode (assisted reproductive technology [ART]), prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), gestational weight gain, chorionicity in twin pregnancies, and family histories of hypertension or diabetes. Prepregnancy BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Laboratory data included fasting lipid and glucose profiles, OGTT results, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), where applicable.

Pregnancy outcomes were defined as follows.

GDM was diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria using a 75-g OGTT performed after 20 weeks of gestation [19]. Diagnostic thresholds were FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. Early-pregnancy fasting glucose values between 5.1 and 5.5 mmol/L were not considered diagnostic and were followed by repeat OGTT screening at 24–28 weeks. Participants meeting the diagnostic criteria for pregestational diabetes, defined as FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-h glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L in the presence of classic symptoms, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, were excluded. Women with prediabetes, defined as FPG levels of 5.6–6.9 mmol/L, were also excluded. To capture metabolic heterogeneity, GDM was further categorized into three mutually exclusive subtypes: (1) post-load GDM, defined as normal FPG with elevated post-load glucose levels (1-h ≥ 10.0 mmol/L and/or 2-h ≥ 8.5 mmol/L); (2) isolated fasting GDM, defined as elevated FPG (≥ 5.1 mmol/L) accompanied by normal 1-h and 2-h values; and (3) combined GDM, defined as elevated FPG accompanied by elevated 1-h and/or 2-h values. Women with normoglycemia served as the reference group.

HDP was defined as hypertension first occurring after 20 weeks of gestation and confirmed by two blood pressure measurements obtained at least 4 h apart (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) [20, 21]. Preeclampsia was defined as HDP with proteinuria, defined as any of the following: protein excretion ≥ 0.3 g per 24 h, dipstick ≥ 1 +, or a urinary protein/creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3, or, in the absence of proteinuria, the development of new maternal organ dysfunction or uteroplacental dysfunction [21]. Severe preeclampsia was defined as preeclampsia with severe hypertension (≥ 160/110 mmHg) or severe maternal organ dysfunction.

To assess the effects of coexisting GDM and HDP, a four-level composite variable was constructed: (1) normoglycemic–normotensive (reference); (2) GDM only; (3) HDP only; and (4) combined GDM–HDP.

Calculation of glucose–lipid metabolic indices

Eight early-pregnancy surrogate indices representing glucose–lipid metabolism and IR were derived from fasting measures: TyG index, TyG-BMI, metabolic score for IR (METS-IR), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), single-point insulin sensitivity estimator (SPISE), cholesterol–HDL–glucose (CHG) index, non-HDL to HDL cholesterol ratio (NHHR), and remnant cholesterol (RC). To ensure consistency in index calculation, lipid and glucose measurements were converted from mmol/L to mg/dL using the following conversion coefficients: cholesterol × 38.67, TG × 88.57, and glucose × 18.02. Statistical analysis

Depending on their distribution, continuous variables are detailed as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data are presented as numbers (%). To assess between-group differences, the Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or χ2 test was used. Baseline characteristics were compared between singleton and twin pregnancies, between hospital sites, and between included and excluded participants. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients among early-pregnancy metabolic biomarkers and glycemic measures were calculated using pairwise complete observations.

The relationships of metabolic indices and categorical metabolic phenotypes were assessed using multinomial logistic regression. For analyses of GDM subtypes, normoglycemia was used as the reference category. For analyses of GDM–HDP phenotypes, normoglycemic–normotensive pregnancies were used to serve as the reference category. Sequential models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for maternal age and hospital site), and Model 3 (fully adjusted with outcome-specific covariates). Covariates were selected based on clinical considerations and evidence from previous studies. For GDM subtype analyses, the fully adjusted model included maternal age, hospital site, parity, plurality, ART, and family history of diabetes. For GDM–HDP phenotype analyses, the same covariates were included with additional adjustment for family history of hypertension. Because several composite indices mathematically incorporate BMI (e.g., TyG-BMI), BMI was not included in the primary fully adjusted model to preserve comparability across indices and avoid structural overadjustment. Additional BMI-adjusted sensitivity analyses were performed for indices that did not mathematically contain BMI.

Exponentiation of the regression coefficients yielded odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous metabolic indices were analyzed per 1-unit increment. The lowest quartile (Q1) was designated as the reference category for quartile analyses. Bonferroni correction was applied to within-model quartile contrasts. To evaluate linear trends across quartiles, the median value of each quartile was entered into the model as a continuous variable.

For visualization, heatmaps were generated to display ORs per 1-SD increase in each metabolic index derived from the fully adjusted multinomial models. Color intensity represents log₂-transformed ORs. Metabolic indices were ranked by mean OR across GDM subtypes.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles were incorporated into multinomial logistic regression models to assess potential nonlinear associations. Likelihood ratio tests comparing spline models with linear models were used to evaluate nonlinearity. Using the median exposure value as the reference, adjusted ORs (aORs) and corresponding 95% CIs were estimated and displayed on a logarithmic scale.

The discriminative ability of each metabolic index for GDM subtypes was evaluated using one-vs-rest receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses. For each subtype, adjusted area under the curve (AUC) values were estimated separately, and macro-AUC was calculated as the unweighted mean of subtype-specific AUCs. For indices inversely associated with GDM risk (e.g., SPISE), values were multiplied by − 1 to ensure consistent risk direction in the ROC analysis. To obtain robust CIs and enable comparisons between indices, nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 2,000 replicates was performed. For each bootstrap sample, subtype-specific AUCs and macro-AUCs were recalculated. The bootstrap distribution of macro-AUCs was used to derive mean estimates and percentile-based 95% CIs. The index with the highest apparent macro-AUC in the original dataset was defined as the best-performing reference index. For each bootstrap replicate, pairwise differences in macro-AUC (Δ) between each index and the reference index were calculated, and 95% CIs were obtained from the bootstrap distribution. Significance was determined by whether the corresponding 95% CI crossed zero; intervals excluding zero indicated statistical significance. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), defined as − 2 × log-likelihood + 2 k, where k represents the number of parameters in the multinomial logistic regression model.

For the highest-risk phenotypes (combined GDM and combined GDM–HDP), discriminative performance was additionally assessed separately in singleton and twin pregnancies using ROC curves. Differences between ROC curves were evaluated using DeLong's test. Optimal diagnostic cutoffs were determined by maximizing the Youden index.

Stratified multinomial analyses were conducted for prespecified factors including plurality (singleton vs twin), prepregnancy overweight status, maternal age (< 35 vs ≥ 35 years), gestational age at first sampling (≤ 10 vs > 10 weeks), and hospital site. Interaction effects were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without interaction terms.

Primary analyses used a complete-case approach because the derivation of composite metabolic indices required concurrent availability of fasting glucose and the full lipid panel. Missingness was driven predominantly by unavailable early-pregnancy biochemical screening (approximately 35–37% across fasting metabolic variables), whereas missingness in nonbiochemical covariates was negligible (< 0.3%). To evaluate robustness against potential selection bias, multiple imputation using predictive mean matching (m = 20) was performed as a sensitivity analysis, with all analytic variables included in the imputation model.

To investigate potential glucose–lipid effect modification, interaction terms between early-pregnancy FPG and lipid indicators (TG, HDL-C, LDL-C) were incorporated into multivariable logistic regression models for the highest-risk phenotypes (combined GDM and combined GDM–HDP). Subgroup-specific effect estimates were derived from the corresponding interaction models, and P values for the interaction were obtained from likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without the cross-product terms. Additional sensitivity analyses included stratification by plurality and hospital site and evaluation of three-way interactions.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1. All statistical tests were two-sided. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless Bonferroni correction was applied for predefined multiple comparisons.

Results

Study population characteristics

Among the 23,889 screened pregnancies, 13,419 met the inclusion criteria, including 11,765 singleton and 1,654 twin pregnancies (Fig. 1). Included and excluded pregnancies were broadly similar with respect to several baseline characteristics. However, differences were observed in some maternal and pregnancy features (Table S1). Exclusion was primarily attributable to the absence of early-pregnancy biochemical screening required for metabolic-index derivation. Twin pregnancies exhibited a distinct metabolic and obstetric profile compared with singleton pregnancies (Table 1 and S2). Twin pregnancies had higher rates of nulliparity (81.3% vs 61.8%) and ART use (67.6% vs 16.0%), as well as greater gestational weight gain (median 15.8 [IQR 12.5–19.0] vs 12.5 [10.0–15.2]). Early-pregnancy TG and composite IR-related indices were significantly higher in twin pregnancies, whereas HDL-C, LDL-C, SPISE, and NHHR were lower.

Across GDM subtypes, OGTT glucose concentrations differed substantially (Table S3). Combined GDM exhibited the highest median fasting (5.20 mmol/L), 1-h (10.90 mmol/L), and 2-h (9.20 mmol/L) glucose levels, consistent with progressively increasing metabolic severity (all P < 0.001).

Metabolic signatures of GDM subtypes

Correlation analyses showed strong clustering among IR-related metabolic indices (Figure S1).

In multinomial logistic regression models comparing each GDM subtype with normoglycemia (Table 3; Tables S4–S5; Fig. 2A), the TyG index and related composite indices demonstrated progressively stronger associations across GDM subtypes. A 1-unit increment in the TyG index was associated with higher odds of post-load GDM (aOR 2.54; 95% CI 2.22–2.90), isolated fasting GDM (aOR 6.21; 95% CI 4.18–9.24), and combined GDM (aOR 8.91; 95% CI 6.47–12.27) in the fully adjusted models. Similar gradients were observed for other IR-related indices (Table S4). Conversely, SPISE showed a negative association with GDM subtypes, consistent with its role as an indicator of insulin sensitivity.

The distribution of TyG index values across GDM subtypes further illustrated this metabolic gradient, with progressively higher levels from post-load GDM to combined GDM (Fig. 2B).

Nonlinear associations and extension to GDM–HDP phenotypes

Because TyG-BMI incorporates adiposity into the TyG construct and demonstrated comparable associations with GDM subtypes, subsequent analyses extended these indices to GDM–HDP phenotypes (Table S6). In fully adjusted models, stronger associations were observed for combined GDM–HDP than for isolated outcomes. For each 1-unit increase in the TyG index, the aOR for combined GDM–HDP was 4.81 (95% CI 3.35–6.89). Additional adjustment for prepregnancy BMI in indices that did not structurally incorporate adiposity (TyG index, AIP, CHG index, NHHR, and ln[RC]) yielded materially unchanged associations across both GDM subtype and GDM–HDP phenotype analyses (Tables S7–S8).

RCS analyses indicated largely linear risk trajectories for the TyG index, whereas TyG-BMI showed evidence of a nonlinear association with combined GDM–HDP (Fig. 3).

Stratified spline visualizations showed similar exposure–response patterns across singleton and twin pregnancies (Figure S2). Consistent with this finding, formal interaction tests between plurality and metabolic indices were not statistically significant.

Discriminatory performance of metabolic indices

In ROC analyses for GDM subtypes (Fig. 4A; Table S9), macro-AUC values ranged from 0.573 to 0.675, with the highest value observed for the TyG index (0.675), followed by TyG-BMI (0.670), METS-IR (0.669), and SPISE (0.664). Bootstrap comparisons showed no significant differences among these four indices. For GDM–HDP phenotypes (Fig. 4B; Table S10), macro-AUC values ranged from 0.544 to 0.641, with TyG-BMI showing the highest value (0.641), followed by SPISE and METS-IR. The TyG index yielded a macro-AUC of 0.627. Although fasting glucose in early pregnancy alone showed substantial discrimination, adding the TyG index improved both macro-AUC and model fit, supporting incremental discriminatory information provided by lipid context beyond glycemia alone (Table S11).

Separate analyses of the highest-risk phenotypes showed that the AUC was 0.786 for combined GDM (TyG index and TyG-BMI) and 0.811 for combined GDM–HDP (TyG-BMI), compared with 0.788 for the TyG index (Tables S12–S13). Discrimination did not differ between singleton and twin pregnancies (DeLong test, all P > 0.05; Figure S3).

Subgroup and interaction analyses

Associations between the TyG index and GDM subtypes were consistent across prespecified maternal age (< 35 vs ≥ 35 years), gestational age at blood sampling (≤ 10 vs > 10 weeks), and hospital-site strata. In addition, interaction tests were not significant across all subgroups (all interaction P > 0.05; Table S14).

Similarly, associations between TyG-BMI and GDM–HDP phenotypes were largely consistent across maternal-age and gestational-age strata (Table S15). In pooled analyses, interaction tests were significant for study center and prepregnancy overweight status (both interaction P < 0.001; Table S15; Fig. 5). A statistical interaction with plurality was also detected in pooled models; however, analyses stratified by center–plurality groups suggested that this pattern was largely attributable to between-center differences rather than to a clear biological effect of twin pregnancy (Figures S4–S5). Consistently, restriction to the Hospital A cohort, in which singleton and twin pregnancies were concurrently represented, showed no significant plurality interaction (interaction P = 0.875; Figure S6). In analyses restricted to twin pregnancies, additional adjustment for chorionicity and chorionicity-stratified analyses demonstrated directionally consistent associations without statistically significant interaction (Table S16).

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple-imputation analyses yielded effect estimates broadly consistent with those from complete-case analyses across both GDM subtype and GDM–HDP phenotype models (Tables S17–S18).

Glucose-lipid interaction analyses

Interaction analyses between FPG and lipid parameters are shown in Tables S19–S21 and Figures S7–S8. Most interaction terms were not statistically significant after Bonferroni correction.

However, a significant interaction between FPG and HDL-C was observed for both combined GDM (interaction OR 3.61; 95% CI 1.37–9.49) and combined GDM–HDP (interaction OR 3.79; 95% CI 1.42–10.05).

Stratified analyses suggested that among singleton pregnancies with low HDL-C levels, the risk of combined GDM–HDP remained elevated across the FPG spectrum, whereas in higher HDL-C strata, risk increased primarily at elevated FPG levels (Fig. 6). Three-way interaction tests suggested potential modification by hospital site for combined GDM–HDP in FPG × HDL-C models, whereas interactions involving plurality were generally not significant (Table S20).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that early-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices were associated with a graded pattern of metabolic heterogeneity across GDM subtypes, with progressively stronger associations from post-load GDM to isolated fasting GDM and the strongest associations for combined GDM. A similar graded pattern was observed when analyses were extended to hypertensive phenotypes, with the strongest associations observed for combined GDM–HDP. Directionally similar associations were observed across singleton and twin pregnancies.

The observed gradient across GDM subtypes is biologically coherent and aligns with the pathophysiological continuum of IR. Fasting hyperglycemia is more closely linked to hepatic IR and impaired basal glucose regulation, whereas post-load abnormalities more strongly reflect peripheral insulin sensitivity and β-cell response [23]. This distinction parallels clustering frameworks described in type 2 diabetes, where individuals characterized by severe insulin-resistant phenotypes exhibit higher cardiometabolic risk than those with predominantly insulin-deficient or mild hyperglycemic profiles [8]. For example, Ahlqvist et al. identified distinct diabetes clusters, including a “severe insulin-resistant diabetes” subgroup with markedly increased risks of nephropathy and cardiovascular complications [24]. The current findings suggest that a comparable metabolic stratification may already be detectable during pregnancy, with glucose–lipid indices preferentially capturing IR-dominant phenotypes rather than isolated glucose intolerance.

Consistent with prior epidemiological evidence, TyG-based indices appear to reflect early metabolic perturbations preceding overt GDM. In a large population-based cohort, prepregnancy TyG index was independently associated with incident GDM, with an approximately 1.8-fold increase in risk per unit increment [25]. In addition, first-trimester studies have reported that the TyG index performs comparably to conventional lipid ratios for identifying women at risk of GDM, with moderate discrimination (AUC ~ 0.70) during midpregnancy screening [26, 27]. Rather than focusing solely on discrimination, the present analysis extends these observations by demonstrating that these indices capture increasing effect sizes across clinically meaningful GDM subtypes, reinforcing their role as markers of underlying metabolic severity.

This study expands the existing literature by incorporating hypertensive outcomes into this metabolic framework. Previous studies have shown that a high TyG index is associated with increased risks of both GDM and preeclampsia [28]. The stronger associations observed here for combined GDM–HDP support the presence of shared metabolic–vascular pathways linking dysglycemia and hypertension.

Mechanistically, IR and dyslipidemia contribute to endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, which are processes central to the pathogenesis of HDP [9]. These metabolic disturbances may also impair placental vascular remodeling and angiogenic balance, further linking early metabolic dysregulation to hypertensive complications [29]. The stronger effect estimates observed for combined GDM–HDP in this study provide empirical support for a shared metabolic-vascular continuum, rather than two independent disease processes.

The interaction observed between fasting glucose and HDL-C provides additional pathophysiological insight. HDL-C is known to reduce inflammation, counteract oxidative stress, and protect endothelial function [30]. Reduced HDL-C levels may therefore exacerbate vascular susceptibility to hyperglycemia-induced damage. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that dysfunctional HDL is associated with impaired endothelial repair and increased cardiovascular risk [31]. Within the present framework, this interaction suggests that combined disturbances in glycemic and lipid pathways may act synergistically to amplify vascular injury, particularly in pregnancies progressing to combined GDM–HDP.

Adiposity-integrated indices such as TyG-BMI showed comparable or slightly stronger associations with combined GDM–HDP. This finding is consistent with the central role of maternal adiposity in driving gestational IR. Lin et al. indicated that TyG-BMI is independently associated with incident GDM, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors [32]. By incorporating both metabolic and anthropometric components, such indices may better capture the systemic metabolic load contributing to both glycemic and vascular dysfunction.

An additional contribution of this study lies in its inclusion of twin pregnancies, a population often underrepresented in metabolic research. Twin gestations are characterized by increased placental mass and heightened secretion of diabetogenic hormones, leading to amplified physiological IR [18, 33–35]. Consistent with this physiology, higher TG levels and a greater prevalence of adverse outcomes were observed in twins. However, associations between metabolic indices and hypertensive phenotypes were numerically attenuated, without statistically significant evidence of effect modification. This pattern may be consistent with differences in the relative contributions of placental and maternal metabolic mechanisms in multifetal gestations. Circulating factors such as sFlt-1 and PlGF play central roles in preeclampsia pathogenesis [36], and their dysregulation may partially overshadow metabolic contributions in multifetal gestations. These findings highlight that the interpretation of metabolic risk markers may require consideration of the pregnancy-specific biological context.

From a clinical perspective, the observed gradient across GDM subtypes and GDM–HDP phenotypes suggests that early-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices may help distinguish pregnancies with differing degrees of metabolic vulnerability. Because these markers are routinely available in clinical practice, they may complement existing approaches to early risk stratification and support more tailored monitoring of women at increased risk of adverse metabolic and hypertensive complications. Further research is required to evaluate their incremental value within clinical prediction frameworks.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the multicenter design, the inclusion of both singleton and twin pregnancies, and the use of multinomial modeling to preserve phenotypic heterogeneity across metabolic outcomes. Additional strengths include spline-based assessment of nonlinear associations, bootstrap-based comparison of discriminative performance, and formal interaction testing.

Several limitations should be considered. First, because inclusion in the analytic cohort required complete early-pregnancy biochemical profiling, some degree of selection bias cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, multiple-imputation analyses yielded generally consistent association patterns, suggesting that missing biochemical data were unlikely to fully account for the primary findings. Second, the observational design precludes causal inference. Third, unmeasured behavioral variables, like dietary habits and physical activity, may still introduce residual confounding. Fourth, because one center contributed exclusively twin pregnancies, residual confounding between study site and plurality cannot be fully excluded despite stratified analyses. Moreover, gestational age at HDP onset was unavailable; therefore, distinction between early- and late-onset disease was not possible. Because late-onset HDP may more strongly reflect maternal metabolic dysfunction, whereas severe early-onset disease is often more closely linked to placental pathology, this limitation may have attenuated phenotype-specific mechanistic interpretation. Furthermore, the exclusion of stillbirths and deliveries before 28 weeks may have underrepresented severe early placental disease. Finally, these indices should currently be viewed as exploratory markers for future risk-stratification research and cannot be recommended for practical clinical application until they are validated in independent studies.

Conclusions

Early-pregnancy glucose–lipid indices are associated with a graded pattern of metabolic heterogeneity across GDM subtypes, and similar patterns are observed in pregnancies with co-occurring GDM and HDP. These findings are consistent with the concept of shared metabolic disturbances underlying glycemic and hypertensive complications during pregnancy. If confirmed in external populations, incorporation of such metabolic information into early-pregnancy risk assessment strategies may help identify women who could benefit from closer monitoring during pregnancy.
