Polyethyleneimine-modified Laminarin nanoparticles enhance antigen delivery to lymph nodes by inducing transient inflammatory responses at the injection site
Authors: Ruihong Yu, Lulu Zhang, Zuchen Song, Lina Jiao, Zhimin Zhang, Shun Zhang, Entao Li, Yuechao Sun, Zhenguang Liu, Guangliang Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Functionalized nanoparticles hold great potential as vaccine adjuvants. In our previous study, we demonstrated that polyethyleneimine-modified Laminarin nanoparticles (CLam/OVA) exhibit potent adjuvant effects. However, it is still unclear how the administration of CLam/OVA affects cell mobilization, the interactions between immune cells in the body, and the transportation of draining lymph nodes. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate how CLam/OVA enhanced and modulated vaccine-induced immune responses. Results indicate that following CLam/OVA injection, the injection site secretes IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, forming a transient, adjuvant-induced inflammatory microenvironment. Concurrently, macrophages and dendritic cells are recruited and activated at the site of injection. Subsequently, antigen-loaded dendritic cells homing to lymph nodes drive follicular helper T cells differentiation, germinal center responses, and memory B cell generation, establishing robust and durable adaptive immunity. In summary, this study not only elucidates the immune adjuvant of CLam/OVA but also provides robust support for the design of novel vaccine adjuvants.

Introduction

Immunization plays a crucial role in the prevention and control of infectious diseases [1]. Nevertheless, significant challenges persist in optimizing vaccine performance, particularly in enhancing efficacy, immunogenic precision, and the duration of protective immunity [2,3]. These challenges have driven the strategic utilization of adjuvants that amplify antigen-specific responses through the dual modulation of innate and adaptive immunity [4,5]. Adjuvants can reduce the dose of antigen required and the frequency of immunizations needed, thereby facilitating a more rapid and durable immune response [6,7]. Although aluminum-based adjuvant (Alum) is still the widely applied adjuvant in vaccines, its historical dominance in Th2-biased vaccine formulations is accompanied by a suboptimal capacity to induce cytotoxic T-cell immunity [8]. This fundamental limitation highlights the urgent need for the discovery of novel adjuvants and the rational selection of appropriate adjuvants for next-generation vaccine development.

Nanoparticles serve as adjuvants, carriers, and antigen-presenting platforms to enhance immune responses, holding significant importance in vaccine development and immunotherapy. Compared with traditional adjuvants, nanoparticles can effectively deliver antigens to immune cells and promote their uptake. However, interactions between vaccine components and immune cells can be modulated by adjusting nanoparticle size, shape, and surface properties [9,10]. Research indicates that nanoparticles between 10 and 100 nm effectively enter lymph nodes (LNs) via lymphatic vessels, while larger nanoparticles (> 100 nm) are primarily transported to LNs by migrating dendritic cells (DCs). Furthermore, positively charged nanoparticles (> 200 nm) are more readily internalized by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [11,12]. Nanoparticles exhibit multifaceted immunostimulatory capabilities through diverse immune mechanisms. Nanoparticles can directly modulate immune cell signaling and function, such as via TLR signaling pathways, cGAS-STING signaling pathways, and other pattern recognition receptor signaling pathways [[13], [14], [15]]. Furthermore, nanoparticles act as adjuvants by forming storage depots at the injection site through sustained antigen release; induce inflammatory responses at injection sites, generating pro-inflammatory cytokines; and promote APCs maturation and cross-presentation of antigens [16,17]. Consequently, developing novel vaccine adjuvants and delivery systems based on nanoparticle properties holds considerable promise.

Polysaccharides derived from traditional Chinese medicines possess inherent immunostimulatory activity and favorable biocompatibility, making them attractive candidates for vaccine adjuvant development [[18], [19], [20]]. Laminarin (Lam) is a polysaccharide composed of a (1 → 3) -β-D-glucan backbone with immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and antitumor activities [21]. However, its clinical application is hindered by limitations such as the requirement for high dosages and rapid metabolism in vivo [22]. Nanoparticles possess dual functions of antigen delivery and immune stimulation, so functionalized polysaccharide nanoparticles may exhibit superior adjuvant properties [23]. In our previous research, polyethyleneimine-modified Laminarin was mixed with ovalbumin to produce CLam/OVA nanoparticles [24]. CLam/OVA exhibits a small particle size, stable physicochemical properties, and enhanced DCs function. Furthermore, CLam/OVA significantly enhances both humoral and cellular immune responses. However, we still know little about the initial innate immune response triggered at the injection site after CLam/OVA injection, and how it interacts with immune cells in vivo.

In this study, we demonstrated that CLam/OVA nanoparticles triggered a transient inflammatory response at the injection site, promoting the recruitment and activation of APCs. CLam/OVA can ensure durable immune protection by homing to LNs, promoting the germinal center (GC) response, and promoting B cell maturation (Fig. 1). These findings indicate that CLam/OVA modulates antigen-specific immune responses by enhancing innate activation and establishing a robust immunostimulatory environment, and providing strong support for the rational development of adjuvant vaccines.Fig. 1CLam/OVA enhances vaccine efficacy by promoting both intrinsic and adaptive immunity. Materials and methods

Reagents and animals

Laminarin (Lam) was acquired from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Aluminum-based adjuvant (Alum) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. (Massachusetts, USA). Ovalbumin (OVA) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. (St. Louis, USA). TRIzol was purchased from Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR reagents were obtained from Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Flow cytometry antibodies were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. (Massachusetts, USA), Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and BioLegend Inc. (California, USA). Antibodies included FITC-monoclonal antibodies CD80, MHCⅡ, GL7, CD3, and CD38; PC7-monoclonal antibodies CXCR5, CD11c, CD69, and F4/80; APC-monoclonal antibodies CD40, CD11b, Fas, CD138, MHCⅡ and CD4; and PE-monoclonal antibodies CD86, MHCⅠ, B220, CCR7, CD8, F4/80, and PD-1. Cyanine5.5 (Cy5.5) NHS ester was obtained from APExBIO Technology LLC (Houston, USA).

Female BALB/C mice were purchased from the Institute of Comparative Medicine, Yangzhou University (Yangzhou, China). All animal experiments were performed following institutional guidelines for animal welfare and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Agricultural University.

Preparation of CLam/OVA nanoparticles

CLam/OVA nanoparticles were prepared as previously described [24]. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of CLam/OVA were 380.07 nm, 0.18, and +43.1 mV, respectively. In brief, 38 mg of Lam was dissolved in pyridine, succinic anhydride, and 4-(dimethylamino)pyridine. Mixture was subsequently combined with dimethyl sulfoxide, 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride, and N-hydroxysuccinimide. Polyethyleneimine (PEI, molecular weight: 10 kDa) was finally introduced to acquire cationic Laminarin (CLam). Subsequently, CLam and OVA were mixed in PBS and placed on a shaker to obtain CLam/OVA nanoparticles. Finally, Laminarin mixed with ovalbumin (Lam/OVA) and aluminum-based adjuvant mixed with ovalbumin (Alum/OVA) were prepared using the same procedure.

Cell cytotoxicity of RAW264.7 cells

Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin). Cells (5 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with Lam/OVA or CLam/OVA for 24 h. Subsequently, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (TWIN200, TENCAN, Switzerland).

Cellular uptake of RAW264.7 cells

Cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with FITC-labeled OVA conjugated to Lam or CLam for 12 h. Cells were washed with PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry (Accuri C6, BD, USA). For confocal microscopy, cells were inoculated into confocal dishes. After staining with DAPI solution, OVA-FITC expression was visualized using confocal microscopy (ECLIPSE Ti, Nikon, Japan). For endocytosis inhibition studies, cells were pretreated with cytochalasin D (CD), chlorpromazine (CPZ), filipin, and methyl-β-cyclodextrin (M-β-CD) for 2 h before the addition of FITC-labeled OVA conjugated to CLam. After 12 h of incubation, cellular uptake was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Detection of maturation and antigen cross-presentation of RAW264.7 cells

Cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were incubated with Lam/OVA or CLam/OVA nanoparticles for 24 h. Cells were incubated with FITC-CD80, PE-CD86, APC-MHCⅡ, and PE-MHCⅠ to assess maturation and cross-presentation of RAW264.7 cells.

ELISA assay

Cytokine levels (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using a TRIzol reagent, and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR was performed using 5 × TransScript® Uni All-in-One SuperMix and 2 × PerfectStart® Green Qpcr SuperMix with primers for Il6, Tnf, and Il1b (Table S1).

RNA sequencing analysis of RAW264.7 cells

Total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 cells stimulated with Lam/OVA or CLam/OVA for 24 h. RNA concentration and RNA integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 and Agilent 5300 system. After mRNA enrichment by Oligo dT, mRNA fragmentation, cDNA trans-synthesis, adaptor ligation, fragment screening, and library enrichment were performed. Finally, RNA sequencing was performed by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed genes were selected for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis based on the criteria |log2FC| > 1 and p-adjust < 0.05.

Recruitment of antigen-presenting cells at the injection site

Mice were injected with PBS, OVA, Alum/OVA, Lam/OVA, or CLam/OVA nanoparticles. Each mouse received 50 μg of OVA, 250 μg of Alum, 400 μg of Lam, or 400 μg of CLam. At 24 h, tissues from the injection site were collected and digested in Hank's balanced salt solution at 37 °C. Single-cell suspensions were obtained by filtering through a cell strainer and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min. To analyze macrophages at the injection site, cells were incubated with APC-CD11b and PC7-F4/80 at 4 °C for 30 min. To analyze DCs at the injection site, cells were incubated with PC7-CD11c at 4 °C for 30 min. To assess DC activation, cells were further incubated with FITC-CD80 and PE-CD86 antibodies.

Supernatants of cell suspensions were obtained for cytokine quantification (IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) using ELISA kits. Expression of Il6, Ifng, and Il1b mRNA was analyzed by qRT-PCR (Table S1).

Antigen uptake at the injection site

Mice were administered a single dose of FITC-labeled OVA conjugated to Alum, Lam, or CLam and euthanized 12 h post-injection. Each mouse received 50 μg of OVA-FITC, 250 μg of Alum, 400 μg of Lam, or 400 μg of CLam. Tissues from the injection site were collected, digested, and processed into single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with PC7-CD11c, APC-CD11b, and PE-F4/80 antibodies for flow cytometry analysis.

In vivo lymph node targeting assay

Cy5.5-labeled OVA was used to evaluate the in vivo distribution of antigen-loaded nanoparticles. Briefly, Cy5.5 NHS ester was conjugated to OVA and incubated in the dark, followed by purification through dialysis to remove unreacted Cy5.5 NHS ester. A total of 400 μg of CLam was loaded with OVA-Cy5.5 (50 μg) and injected into the mice. After the injection, the distribution of OVA-Cy5.5 in the injection site, LNs, and organs was evaluated using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA).

Flow cytometric analysis of lymph nodes

LNs were collected and prepared for cell suspensions. Flow cytometry analysis of antigen uptake in LNs was performed using PC7-CD11c, APC-CD11b, and PE-CD8α antibodies. For the maturation of DCs, cells were incubated with PC7-CD11c, APC-CD40, and PE-CD86 at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. To assess antigen presentation of DCs in the LNs, cells were incubated with PC7-CD11c and FITC-MHCⅡ antibodies. To study the expression of CCR7 in the LNs, cells were incubated with PC7-CD11c and PE-CCR7 antibodies. T cell activation was assessed by staining with FITC-CD3, APC-CD4, PE-CD8, and PC7-CD69 antibodies.

Germinal center formation analysis

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from LNs and spleens. To detect GC B cells, cells were incubated with FITC-GL7, APC-Fas, and PE-B220 antibodies. Additionally, tissue sections of LNs and spleens were stained with FITC-GL7 and PE-B220 antibodies, and GC formation was observed using confocal microscopy.

Detection of follicular helper T cells, memory B cells, and plasma cells in vivo

LNs were collected and gently ground through a nylon mesh to acquire a single-cell suspension. For the detection of follicular helper T (Tfh) cells, LNs cell suspensions were incubated with FITC-CD3, APC-CD4, PE-PD-1, and PC7-CXCR5 antibodies. Furthermore, cells were incubated with APC-CD138, FITC-CD38, and PE-B220, incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min to assess the percentage of memory B cells. Bone marrow was collected to prepare single-cell suspensions for the detection of long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs), which were stained with APC-CD138 and PE-B220 antibodies.

RNA sequencing analysis of lymph nodes

To investigate the effect of immune response in LNs, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. RNA concentration and RNA integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 and Agilent 5300 system. After mRNA enrichment by Oligo dT, mRNA fragmentation, cDNA trans-synthesis, adaptor ligation, fragment screening, and library enrichment were performed. Finally, RNA sequencing was performed by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed genes were selected for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis based on the criteria |log2FC| > 1 and p-adjust < 0.05.

In vivo biocompatibility evaluation

To analyze the biocompatibility, the blood samples were collected to evaluate serum biochemistry indicators and blood routine examination. The main organs (heart, liver, lung, spleen, and kidney) were isolated and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA was used to calculate the statistical significance of differences between groups. (∗) p < 0.05, (∗∗) p < 0.01, (∗∗∗) p < 0.001. Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism.

Results and discussion

Cellular internalization of CLam/OVA in RAW264.7 cells

DCs and macrophages play a crucial role in the immune response [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CLam/OVA enhances the function of DCs [24]. Hence, the immunomodulatory effects of CLam/OVA on macrophages were investigated. Lam/OVA (0–100 μg/mL) and CLam/OVA (0–12.5 μg/mL) exhibited no significant cytotoxicity (Fig. S1). Phagocytosis is a key indicator for assessing macrophage immunoreactivity [26]. Compared with the OVA and Lam/OVA groups, the cellular uptake of CLam/OVA by RAW264.7 cells was significantly enhanced (Fig. 2A). The intracellular distribution of OVA-FITC was observed by confocal microscopy. The FITC fluorescence values of OVA, Lam/OVA, and CLam/OVA were 84.84 ± 2.33, 96.48 ± 19.01, and 209.29 ± 28.74, respectively (Fig. 2B and C). The result revealed a marked increase in fluorescence intensity within the cytoplasm after 12 h of co-incubation with CLam/OVA. To elucidate the cellular uptake pathways of CLam/OVA in RAW264.7 cells, endocytosis inhibitors were employed. CLam/OVA enhances the function of RAW264.7 cells

Co-stimulatory molecules serve as key markers of macrophage maturation [31]. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that CLam/OVA markedly upregulated CD80 and CD86 expression in RAW264.7 cells, whereas OVA and Lam/OVA treatment did not induce a comparable effect (Fig. 2E). Macrophage maturation is closely associated with the ability to present antigen [32]. The percentages of MHCII and MHCI also increased after CLam/OVA stimulation compared with the OVA and Lam/OVA group (Fig. 2F and G). Collectively, these findings indicate that CLam/OVA effectively promotes macrophage activation and enhances antigen presentation capacity.

CLam/OVA promotes the secretion of cytokines

The upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules further amplified cytokine production. IL-6 plays a crucial role in the proliferation of T and B cells, whereas TNF-α exhibits both proinflammatory and antitumor effects. IL-1β, predominantly produced by macrophages, regulates cellular immune activation [33,34]. Compared to OVA and Lam/OVA, CLam/OVA treatment significantly enhanced the secretion of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Fig. 3A). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis showed that expression levels of Il6, Tnf, and Il1b mRNA were markedly upregulated following CLam/OVA stimulation (Fig. 3B). In order to investigate the gene signatures and enriched pathways associated with the stimulatory effects of CLam/OVA, RNA sequencing was performed on RAW264.7 cells following CLam/OVA treatment. PCA demonstrated distinct clustering of samples within each group, indicating high reproducibility and clear separation between treatment conditions (Fig. 3C). CLam/OVA-treated RAW264.7 cells exhibited significant upregulation of genes involved in cytokine activity and regulation of inflammatory responses, including Tnf. Additionally, genes related to chemokine signaling, such as Ccl4 and Ccl22, were markedly upregulated in the CLam/OVA group. In contrast, genes associated with anti-inflammatory macrophage activity (Tnfaip3, Nr1d1, and Zfp36) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 3D). GO annotation showed that the DEGs were enriched in pathways related to macrophage migration and cytokine and chemokine receptor activity, suggesting that CLam/OVA effectively regulates the expression of immune-related genes (Fig. 3E).

Collectively, CLam/OVA facilitates endocytosis via micropinocytosis and the clathrin-mediated endocytosis pathway. Moreover, CLam/OVA promotes macrophage maturation by upregulating CD80 and CD86, enhances antigen processing and presentation, and stimulates cytokine production to enhance the immune responses.

CLam/OVA induced a transient inflammatory response at the injection site

The local inflammatory response triggered by vaccine adjuvants plays a critical role in initiating immune activation and adaptive immunity [35]. At the injection site, levels of IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α were significantly higher in the CLam/OVA group compared to the OVA and Lam/OVA group (Fig. 4A). Similarly, CLam/OVA significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of Il6, Ifng, and Il1b at the injection site (Fig. 4B). Retention of antigens at the site of injection provides continuous stimulation to the immune system, thereby amplifying and sustaining the immune response [36,37]. OVA was labeled with Cy5.5 to assess its dynamic distribution in vivo. Quantification of fluorescence intensity at different time points revealed no significant difference between the Lam/OVA and CLam/OVA groups at 3 h. However, at 12 h and 24 h post-injection, the fluorescence intensity of OVA-Cy5.5 was significantly upregulated in the CLam/OVA group compared to the Lam/OVA group. The fluorescence signal gradually decreased over time and was markedly reduced by 7 d (Fig. 4C). Results indicate that CLam/OVA facilitates antigen retention at the injection site and contributes to prolonging antigen delivery to draining LNs.

CLam/OVA promotes immune cell recruitment at the injection site

The recruitment of immune cells to the vaccination site is essential for facilitating interactions between antigens and immune cells [38]. CLam/OVA could induce the recruitment of CD11b+F4/80+ cells and CD11c+ cells to the injection site (Fig. 5A and B, and S2). Furthermore, CLam/OVA significantly promoted the maturation of CD11c+ cells at the injection site, as evidenced by the upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules (Fig. 5C and D). To investigate whether the antigen was internalized by immune cells, FITC-labeled OVA conjugated to CLam was injected into mice. The results showed that the uptake of CLam/OVA by CD11b+F4/80+ cells was significantly higher than that of Lam/OVA and Alum/OVA (Fig. 5E). Additionally, CLam/OVA was efficiently internalized by CD11c+ cells (Fig. 5F). CLam/OVA enhances antigen transport to lymph nodes

The transient inflammation induced by CLam/OVA at the injection site suggests its possible involvement in and facilitation of lymphatic delivery. Subsequently, antigen-carrying APCs migrate to lymph nodes, contributing to T cell activation and the initiation of adaptive immunity [39]. To evaluate whether CLam facilitates antigen delivery to LNs in vivo, Cy5.5-labeled OVA was injected into mice. At both 24 h and 7 d post-injection, antigen accumulation in the axillary LNs and inguinal LNs was significantly higher in the CLam/OVA group compared to the Lam/OVA group (Fig. 6A). These results indicate that CLam/OVA effectively enhances antigen transport to LNs and prolongs antigen retention. To confirm that the nanoparticles were not distributed to major organs, the kidney, lung, spleen, liver, and heart were excised and examined using the imaging system. No significant fluorescence signals were detected in these organs (Fig. 6B). CCR7 mediates the homing of immune cells to lymphoid organs, playing a crucial role in immune responses [[40], [41], [42]]. The results showed that CLam/OVA significantly enhanced CCR7 expression on migratory DCs (CD11c + MHCIIhi) compared with control, OVA, and CLam/OVA groups (Fig. 6C). The results suggested that CLam/OVA enhanced DC migration and antigen accumulation in LNs, which may promote increased DC-T-cell interactions.Fig. 6CLam/OVA enhances antigen transport to LNs. Conventional DCs in the LNs can be classified into CD8α DCs, and CD11b DCs. CD8α DCs are specialized for cross-presentation and play a pivotal role in activating CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and promoting Th1 responses. CD11b DCs are efficient in priming CD4+ T cells and are associated with Th2 and Th17 differentiation [36,43]. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the majority of OVA in the CLam/OVA group was internalized by DCs, with a subset localized within CD11b DCs (Fig. 6D). The differences in antigen uptake between CD11b DC and CD8α DCs may be related to their specific tissue locations, the availability of antigens, and the ability of antigen presentation [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. These findings suggest that CLam/OVA can preferentially target LNs and promote antigen uptake and subsequent immune activation by DCs in LNs.

CLam/OVA activates dendritic cells and T cells in lymph nodes

The activation of APCs and the enhancement of antigen uptake in LNs are critical immune events that initiate antigen presentation and the induction of antigen-specific immune responses [48]. Flow cytometry analysis showed that compared to the OVA, Lam/OVA, and Alum/OVA groups, the expression of CD40 and CD86 was markedly higher in the CLam/OVA group (Fig. 7A and B). Furthermore, compared with OVA and Alum/OVA, the expression of MHCⅡ in CLam/OVA was significantly enhanced (Fig. 7C). Simultaneous upregulation of MHCⅡ and co-stimulatory molecules upon APC activation is a crucial intrinsic immune event for efficient antigen presentation and the initiation of antigen-specific T cell responses [49]. To further evaluate the immune activation effect of CLam/OVA, we assessed the expression of CD69, a marker of early T cell activation, on T cells. The percentages of CD69+CD4+ and CD69+CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in the CLam/OVA group compared with the Lam/OVA group, by 1.43-fold and 1.39-fold, respectively (Fig. 7D–F). Collectively, these findings suggest that CLam/OVA promotes DC activation and antigen presentation, and robustly stimulates T cell activation to initiate strong immune responses.

CLam/OVA elicits potent germinal center responses

Tfh cells, a specialized subset of CD4+ T cells, play a crucial role in facilitating B cell maturation and supporting GC formation [50]. Compared to the OVA group, CLam/OVA treatment significantly increased the proportion of Tfh cells in LNs, from 0.55 % to 2.08 % (Fig. 8A). Given the critical importance of GCs in generating high-affinity and long-lived antibody responses [51], we further examined GC formation in the LNs and spleen. CLam/OVA significantly promoted the generation of GC B cells in the LNs and spleen (Fig. 8B and S3). To further validate the enhanced GC response induced by CLam/OVA, we performed fluorescence microscopy analyses of LNs and spleens collected from immunized mice (Fig. 8C and S4). Robust GC structures were observed in the CLam/OVA group, whereas minimal GC formation was detected in the OVA and Lam/OVA groups. Moreover, the high levels of GL7+ cell localization and B220+ cell activation observed following CLam/OVA administration further confirmed the induction of a strong adaptive immune response. CLam/OVA promotes an immune memory response

Long-term humoral immunity depends on the persistence of LLPCs and memory B cells. LLPCs serve as a primary defense barrier against reinfection by rapidly producing antibodies upon re-exposure to the pathogen [52]. Flow cytometric analysis of LLPCs in the bone marrow revealed that both CLam/OVA and Alum/OVA treatments significantly increased the expression of LLPCs compared to the control group, with CLam/OVA inducing the highest number of LLPCs (Fig. 8D). Memory B cells act as a secondary defense mechanism by rapidly reactivating upon antigen re-encounter, leading to a robust antibody response [53]. The percentage of memory B cells was significantly higher in the CLam/OVA group than in the OVA and Lam/OVA groups (Fig. 8E). PCA demonstrated apparent clustering within each group and distinct separation between groups, indicating high reproducibility of the samples (Fig. 8F). GO annotation analysis revealed that most DEGs were associated with the positive regulation of B cell activation, antigen processing and presentation, and chemokine-mediated signaling pathway (Fig. 8G). In the CLam/OVA group, genes related to humoral immunity, antigen binding, innate immunity, and cytokine activity were significantly upregulated (Fig. 8H). Additionally, the CLam/OVA group exhibited enrichment in the TLR signaling pathways and RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 8I). These findings suggest that CLam/OVA enhances the generation of Tfh cells, which secrete cytokines that promote B cell proliferation and differentiation. The activation of GC responses further facilitates the polarization of B cells into LLPCs and memory B cells.

Evaluation of in vivo toxicity

The in vivo biocompatibility of the adjuvant was explored. The body weight record revealed that there was no significant difference among the different groups. 14 d post-injection, serum biochemical parameters (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and UREA) remained within normal ranges across all groups (Fig. S5). As shown in Fig. S6, H&E staining images revealed no evident inflammation or tissue damage, thus demonstrating the favorable biosafety profile of CLam/OVA.

Conclusions

In summary, CLam/OVA promoted the uptake, maturation, and antigen presentation efficiency of RAW 264.7 cells in vitro. In vivo experiments revealed that CLam/OVA provides sustained stimulation through prolonged retention and continuous antigen release at the injection site, inducing an inflammatory response and creating a localized immunogenic microenvironment characterized by cytokine infiltration and the recruitment of immune cells. Upon internalization of the nanoparticles, immune cells are activated and subsequently migrate to the LNs to stimulate antigen-specific T cells, and initiating a robust adaptive immune response. Moreover, the sustained antigen release enhances the activation of GC B cells and Tfh cells. Overall, CLam/OVA triggered a transient innate immune response and also successfully initiated and sustained a potent adaptive immune response. These results provided valuable insights into the design of nanoparticle-based vaccine adjuvants.
