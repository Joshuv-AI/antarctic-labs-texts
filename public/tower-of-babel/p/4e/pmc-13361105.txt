TIM-3 in AML: a janus-faced orchestrator of immune exhaustion and leukemic self-renewal
Authors: Ahmed Hjazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) remains a therapeutic challenge due to relapse driven by therapy-resistant leukemic stem cells (LSCs) and a profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment. The immune checkpoint T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) emerges as a uniquely Janus-faced orchestrator in AML pathogenesis. This review synthesizes emerging evidence that TIM-3 operates dually: on one edge, it acts as an important contributor to immune exhaustion, suppressing T-cell, natural killer (NK) cell, and dendritic cell function within the AML niche. On the second edge, TIM-3 serves as a cell-autonomous driver of leukemic stemness, where it marks functional LSCs and promotes their self-renewal, survival, and metabolic reprogramming via non-canonical signaling networks like β-catenin and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB). TIM-3 is also detectable on subsets of immature blasts, but the biologic and clinical meaning of blast-level expression appears more context dependent than its LSC-associated role. This dual biology positions TIM-3 at a compelling therapeutic intersection. We examine the current landscape of TIM-3-targeted agents, from monoclonal antibodies like sabatolimab to bispecifics and CAR-T cells, and critically evaluate rational combination strategies with hypomethylating agents, venetoclax, and other immunotherapies. Finally, we discuss the challenges of patient stratification, resistance mechanisms, and on-target toxicity, outlining a roadmap for converting the multifaceted biology of TIM-3 into transformative clinical benefit for AML patients.Graphical abstract Keywords: TIM-3, Acute Myeloid Leukemia, Immune Exhaustion, Leukemic Stem Cells, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia is a biologically heterogeneous malignancy of myeloid progenitors that, despite incremental therapeutic advances, continues to carry a high risk of relapse and mortality, particularly among older adults and those with adverse-risk genetics [1, 2]. AML is also genetically diverse, with recurrent mutations such as FLT3, NPM1, IDH1/2, and TP53 contributing to disease classification, prognosis, and treatment response; modern ELN-based risk stratification and mutation-directed therapy reflect this biology [3–6]. The persistence of a therapy-refractory compartment of leukemic stem cells that retains self-renewal capacity and the ability to repopulate the disease is widely regarded as the principal mechanistic basis for relapse after initial cytoreduction, and these cells exploit quiescence, niche protection, and adaptive signaling to survive cytotoxic regimens [7, 8]. Contemporary standard-of-care approaches, including intensive cytarabine-anthracycline induction, consolidation chemotherapy, hypomethylating agents, targeted small molecules, and allogeneic transplantation, have improved outcomes for some patients; however, overall long-term survival remains unsatisfactory, with AML 5-year survival remaining only about one-third overall (33.4% 5-year relative survival in SEER; approximately 32% estimated 5-year OS in a recent review), and treatment-related toxicity and clonal evolution continue to limit durable disease control [1, 9, 10].

The clinical success of immune checkpoint blockade in a range of solid tumors has reshaped oncologic therapeutics by demonstrating that relief of T-cell inhibitory pathways can produce deep and durable remissions. In AML, efficacy was anticipated because multiple biologic and clinical observations had already suggested checkpoint dependence: primary specimens often contained exhausted TIM-3+/PD-1 + T cells within a suppressive marrow niche, TIM-3 was identified on bona fide leukemic stem cells, and Galectin-9/TIM-3 signaling was shown to support stemness and survival through β-catenin- and NF-κB-linked programs. Early phase clinical experience with checkpoint inhibitors in AML and MDS, particularly in combination or post-transplant settings, further reinforced the rationale for activity in this disease. Yet the translation of this paradigm to myeloid malignancies has remained limited [7, 11–15]. In AML, checkpoint blockade has shown limited clinical benefit overall. In relapsed/refractory AML, azacitidine plus nivolumab achieved an overall response rate of 33% with 22% CR/CRi, while after allogeneic transplantation ipilimumab produced a 23% complete response rate and a 9% partial response rate at 10 mg/kg, with median duration of response not reached in responders; by contrast, a randomized phase II maintenance study found that single-agent nivolumab did not improve progression-free or overall survival. Together, these data show that responses remain uncommon outside post-transplant relapse or hypomethylating-agent combinations, highlighting a fundamentally different tumor immune ecology in the bone marrow [12, 16–19]. Multiple mechanisms likely underlie this resistance, including low mutational neoantigen burden relative to many solid tumors, marked genetic diversity and clonal selection, an immunosuppressive bone marrow microenvironment with regulatory cells and suppressive cytokines, and intrinsic checkpoint redundancy and compensatory inhibitory circuits that blunt effector T-cell reactivation [11, 20–22]. In addition, AML poses broader therapeutic barriers, including marked inter- and intrapatient heterogeneity and shared antigen expression between leukemic and normal hematopoietic cells, which narrow the therapeutic window for antibody-, engager-, and CAR-based approaches.

These observations have prompted a shift away from a one size fits all checkpoint strategy and toward a model that views AML as an immune-evasive ecosystem in which malignant clones, stromal elements and infiltrating immune cells engage in dynamic cross talk to enforce tolerance and protect leukemic stemness [8, 20]. Within this framework, soluble mediators such as galectins and locally expressed ligands can both suppress cytotoxic lymphocyte function and sustain leukemic cell programs that promote survival and self-renewal, thereby coupling immune evasion to intrinsic oncogenic signaling [23–25]. Recognizing checkpoint pathways that participate simultaneously in immune regulation and stem cell biology offers a rational path to interventions that aim not only to reinvigorate anti-leukemic immunity but also to directly target the cells responsible for relapse.

One receptor that exemplifies this Janus-faced biology is T cell immunoglobulin mucin-3, commonly referred to as TIM-3, which was originally described as an exhaustion-associated marker on dysfunctional T cells and subsequently identified as a selective marker of functional leukemic stem cells in a large fraction of AML cases [7, 16]. Mechanistic work has shown that TIM-3 is engaged by ligands such as galectin-9 and that this interaction mediates both inhibitory effects on effector T cells and cell intrinsic pro-survival signaling in TIM-3 expressing AML blasts and stem cells, linking immunosuppression to the maintenance of stemness through downstream pathways including nuclear factor kappa B and beta catenin related programs in some experimental models [7, 23, 24]. Likewise, TIM-3 positive leukemic populations have been reported to secrete galectin-9, thereby creating an autocrine and paracrine circuit that reinforces an immune privileged niche [26, 27].

Given this dual role, TIM-3 occupies a unique therapeutic intersection: an axis that could be exploited to simultaneously release anti-tumor immunity and deplete the leukemic stem cell reservoir, but that also demands nuanced targeting because blockade may have distinct effects across cellular compartments [20, 28]. The aim of this review is to synthesize emerging mechanistic and translational data that position TIM-3 as a multifunctional signaling node in AML, to examine how TIM-3 driven circuits reconcile immune exhaustion with leukemic self-renewal, and to evaluate opportunities and challenges for TIM-3 directed therapies that seek to convert the Janus-faced biology of this pathway into clinical benefit [8, 11]. Unlike earlier reviews that largely emphasized TIM-3 expression on T cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, and leukemic stem cells, this review also highlights the spatial organization of the TIM-3 axis within the bone-marrow niche and updates the rapidly changing translational landscape. Recent spatial transcriptomic studies in AML bone marrow have begun to localize HAVCR2/TIM-3 within lymphoid and immune-rich aggregates, supporting a compartment-specific view of TIM-3 biology rather than a purely cell-type-based one [29–31].

The first edge: TIM-3 as an important contributor to immune suppression in the AML microenvironment

Although TIM-3 is an important immunoregulatory axis in AML, the leukemia microenvironment is multifactorial, with PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4, regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, suppressive cytokines, and other checkpoints also shaping immune escape [11, 21, 32, 33].

Canonical TIM-3 signaling in immune cells

On T cells TIM-3 has emerged as a context dependent molecular rheostat: during chronic antigenic stimulation it can contribute to a transcriptional and functional program of exhaustion, but during normal immune responses it may also be transiently upregulated on recently activated effector T cells, where it acts as a physiological brake that limits excessive activation and helps shape contraction/homeostasis [34, 35]. Mechanistically, ligand engagement drives phosphorylation of conserved tyrosines in the TIM-3 cytoplasmic tail, dissociation of the protective adaptor Bat3 and consequent recruitment of inhibitory kinases such as Fyn that blunt Lck-mediated propagation of T-cell receptor (TCR) signals and downstream nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activity, thereby reducing cytokine production and proliferative capacity [34–36]. This program is not siloed: TIM-3 is frequently co-expressed with other checkpoints on exhausted T cells, particularly exhausted CD8 + T cells; in this context, the best-supported relationship with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3) is functional synergy and co-expression-associated exhaustion rather than a direct receptor–receptor signaling interaction, so that checkpoint co-expression helps stabilize a terminally dysfunctional state and creates resistance nodes to single-agent checkpoint blockade [37, 38]. Recent structural and cell biological work has also revealed that the functional outcome of TIM-3 ligation can be modulated by which extracellular ligand predominates and by phosphatidylserine engagement, which allosterically reshapes the IgV domain and thereby toggles TIM-3 between inhibitory and context dependent co-stimulatory signaling modes in T cells [39, 40].

In the myeloid compartment TIM-3 expression on dendritic cells and macrophages enforces immune silence via multiple convergent mechanisms. Tumor associated dendritic cells that express TIM-3 are impaired for nucleic acid sensing because TIM-3 sequesters the alarmin HMGB1 and blocks recruitment of tumor-derived nucleic acids into endosomal Toll-like receptor compartments, a process that damps type I interferon and cross-priming of CD8 T cells [41]. Parallel work has shown that loss of Bat3 in TIM-3-expressing DCs drives a tolerogenic transcriptional program that elevates glucocorticoid synthesis and anti-inflammatory cytokines while suppressing co-stimulatory molecules, thereby subverting effective T cell priming in the leukemia niche [42]. In macrophages, TIM-3 appears to contribute to an alternatively activated, immunoregulatory phenotype, but this effect likely acts together with leukemic cytokines and other bone-marrow niche cues. This TIM-3-associated state is linked to increased IL-10 and TGF-β secretion and reduced antigen-presentation capacity, thereby impairing CD4 + T-cell priming and promoting T-cell anergy and leukemic immune escape [43–45].

Natural killer cells illustrate the greatest functional ambiguity in AML. Multiple groups report enrichment of TIM-3 on intratumoral and bone marrow NK cells in AML and associate this marker with altered granule components; however, the functional consequences extend beyond granule loading alone. In canonical NK-cell assays, TIM-3 ligation has been reported to enhance IFN-γ production without changing degranulation, whereas AML cohort studies link TIM-3 expression either to improved cytotoxicity or to preserved perforin/granzyme B abundance without a proportional gain in direct leukemia-cell killing [46–48]. Taken together, these data suggest that TIM-3 can shape both cytokine secretion and effector killing in a context-dependent manner, and that in AML this uncoupling likely contributes to immune evasion by allowing an apparently activated NK-cell phenotype that still fails to eliminate leukemic targets [47, 48]. TIM-3 expression and functional roles across cell types in AML

TIM-3 also has immunometabolic consequences beyond signaling. In chronically stimulated T cells, TIM-3 expression is associated with altered glucose metabolism, and in macrophages TIM-3 directly inhibits glycolysis through HK2, indicating that the checkpoint can restrain immune-cell metabolic fitness as well as activation state. In AML, these metabolic effects would be expected to weaken anti-leukemic immunity and complement the suppressive functions of TIM-3 on DCs and NK cells; however, direct metabolic rewiring has been demonstrated most clearly in T cells and macrophages, whereas evidence in AML dendritic cells and NK cells remains limited [24, 49–51]. By inference, this would weaken anti-leukemic immunity in AML and favor immune escape and poorer responses to therapy. In contrast, the DC and NK literature is still dominated by functional readouts: TIM-3 suppresses cGAS-STING/HMGB1-dependent nucleic-acid sensing in dendritic cells, and in NK cells TIM-3 is best viewed as an activation-dependent marker whose function depends on the inducing stimulus, so direct metabolic rewiring in these cells remains less clearly defined [49, 51–54].

The ligand-receptor tango in the AML niche

Galectin-9 remains the archetypal TIM-3 ligand and sits at the intersection of leukemic self-renewal and immune suppression in AML. Galectin-9 engagement of TIM-3 was first shown to induce Th1 cell death and later was implicated in tumor contexts where Gal-9, secreted by leukemic blasts, ligates TIM-3 on immune cells and, in TIM-3-expressing leukemic cells, functions in an autocrine loop that promotes AML stem cell self-renewal via NF-κB and β-catenin signaling [56, 61]. Multiple transcriptomic and patient cohort studies document dysregulated expression of LGALS9 (the gene encoding galectin-9) in AML blasts and bone marrow stromal elements, and elevated galectin-9 has been associated with chemoresistance and adverse clinical features in several series, supporting a model in which galectin-9 amplifies TIM-3 dependent immune suppression while directly influencing leukemic cell biology [62, 63].

Phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) is the nonprotein ‘eat me’ signal that binds the FG-CC′ cleft of TIM family IgV domains and, in the AML microenvironment, becomes an axis for efferocytosis-driven immune silencing. Efferocytosis is the phagocytic clearance of apoptotic cells by macrophages and dendritic cells. Structural and biochemical studies identify a discrete PtdSer pocket on TIM-3 whose occupancy induces conformational changes that modulate downstream signaling; functionally, PtdSer recognition enables TIM-3 positive macrophages and dendritic cells to engulf apoptotic leukemic debris and to convert that cargo into tolerogenic signals, limiting antigen cross-presentation and fostering regulatory cytokine production [40, 64, 65]. Pharmacological and antibody tools that selectively block TIM-3:PtdSer interactions abrogate efferocytosis and can reawaken antigen presentation in preclinical models, pointing to efferocytosis as a central mechanism by which TIM-3 sustains immune silence in AML [65, 66].

High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and CEACAM1 add further nuance by interfering with danger signaling and by co-regulating receptor availability. TIM-3 binding to HMGB1 competitively prevents HMGB1 from chaperoning nucleic acids into endosomes and thereby suppresses innate sensing of nucleic acid danger signals, an effect directly demonstrated in tumor infiltrating DCs and relevant to the poorly immunogenic cell death that follows cytotoxic AML therapy [41]. CEACAM1 was proposed to operate as a heterophilic partner that chaperones TIM-3 to the cell surface and potentiates tolerance in activated T cells, including the mouse adoptive-transfer colitis model described by Huang et al. and the exhausted-T-cell states observed in chronic viral infection and tumor settings; however, later human T-cell reporter-system and binding studies did not confirm a universal direct cis/trans CEACAM1:TIM-3 interaction, indicating that CEACAM1 modulates TIM-3 biology in a context-dependent fashion rather than as an obligate ligand [67, 68].

TIM-3 ligand engagement in AML is governed more by local ligand abundance and membrane context than by a single universal affinity hierarchy. Galectin-9 is the best-established TIM-3 ligand in AML, but TIM-3 is not limited to galectin-9; phosphatidylserine, HMGB1, and CEACAM1 can also engage TIM-3 in a context-dependent manner [39, 56, 68]. Galectin-9 is often the best-established ligand in AML, especially in blast- and stroma-rich disease, where it can form autocrine and paracrine lattices; phosphatidylserine becomes the dominant cue in apoptosis- and therapy-damaged niches because it is displayed on apoptotic bodies and extracellular vesicles; HMGB1 is most relevant in necrotic or chemotherapy-damaged marrow where it is released as a DAMP; and CEACAM1 is restricted to cells that coexpress it with TIM-3, with some studies supporting a heterophilic interaction and others not detecting a direct interaction [39, 67–70]. Head-to-head quantitative affinity comparisons across all four ligands remain limited and assay dependent, so the practical determinant of which ligand predominates is usually ligand density, valency, receptor co-clustering, and the activation state of the marrow niche rather than a fixed affinity ranking. This framework explains why TIM-3 signaling can appear pro-survival on leukemic stem cells yet inhibitory on dendritic cells or exhausted lymphocytes, depending on which ligand is most available in a given microenvironment [39, 67–70].

This body of work supports a layered view: in AML microenvironments HMGB1 sequestration, PtdSer-mediated efferocytosis and galectin-9 driven lattices converge to convert TIM-3 into a multifaceted inhibitor of both innate danger sensing and adaptive antileukemic responses [38, 41, 65]. Clinical correlates

Clinical and translational studies link higher TIM-3 expression in AML immune and leukemic compartments, most often assessed in peripheral blood and/or bone marrow, depending on the cohort, and compared with healthy donor or TIM-3–low controls, to adverse disease biology, therapy resistance, and relapse after transplant. Peripheral-blood T cells from AML patients also express TIM-3, particularly within IFN-γ + CD4 + and CD8 + subsets, and higher TIM-3 on CD4 + T cells has been linked to poorer relapse-free and event-free survival in some cohorts, so the T-cell signal should be read as a compartment-specific immune-exhaustion readout rather than as a generic AML-wide increase [74, 75]. More precisely, TIM-3 is generally more informative as a marker of the primitive LSC compartment, whereas blast expression is heterogeneous and appears to depend on cytogenetic subtype. TIM-3 is a reproducibly enriched marker of human AML leukemic stem cells and of measurable residual disease populations that seed relapse, but it is not universal across all LSC-containing specimens; longitudinal post-transplant analyses show that persistence or re-emergence of TIM-3-positive MR-LSCs correlates with higher relapse risk and inferior event-free survival, and lower TIM-3 + MR-LSC burden after allo-SCT has been associated with only 22.2% relapse and a median EFS of 1,581 days [7, 57, 76, 77]. Importantly, TIM-3 is not restricted to the LSC compartment: it is also detectable on CD34⁺CD38⁺ immature blasts, and whole-blast TIM-3 levels correlate with the TIM-3 pattern in LSC-enriched fractions, suggesting that TIM-3 marks a spectrum of immature leukemic cells rather than a single blast subtype. In bulk blasts, reported effects are context dependent; one cohort linked higher blast TIM-3 to core-binding-factor AML rather than outcome, while another showed that TIM-3 blockade on blasts altered growth and apoptosis after idarubicin exposure in vitro [58, 60, 78]. Correlative immune profiling across multiple AML cohorts finds that high frequencies of TIM-3 positive T cells and myeloid cells associate with impaired interferon and cytotoxic signatures, with reduced responses to standard chemotherapy in some series, and with shortened relapse free intervals in patients undergoing allogeneic transplantation [58, 79]. These clinical observations have motivated therapeutic strategies that pair TIM-3 blockade with hypomethylating agents or with other checkpoint modulators in early clinical studies, and they underscore TIM-3’s Janus face in AML: a direct leukemic stem cell dependency and a parallel role as an important mediator of microenvironmental immune suppression within a broader, multifactorial immune landscape that together make TIM-3 an exceptionally attractive but mechanistically complex therapeutic target [56, 80].

Collectively, mechanistic, structural and clinical data place TIM-3 at the fulcrum of leukemic persistence and immune exhaustion in AML, where multiple ligands, not only galectin-9, but also phosphatidylserine, HMGB1, and CEACAM1, and cell-type-specific signaling converge to silence innate sensing, blunt adaptive cytotoxicity and preserve leukemic self-renewal. Understanding exactly which TIM-3 ligand dominates in a given patient niche, and how TIM-3 cooperates with other checkpoints, will be essential to rationally deploy TIM-3 targeted agents in combinations that dismantle both the stem cell compartment and the suppressive microenvironment [38, 41, 56].

Spatial organization of the TIM-3 axis in the AML niche

Although direct patient-level spatial maps of TIM-3 remain limited, the emerging spatial transcriptomics literature suggests that the TIM-3 axis is not uniformly distributed across the marrow. In pediatric AML, spatial transcriptomics has already identified lymphoid aggregates in which HAVCR2/TIM-3 appears as part of the local immune program, and 2025 single-cell spatial profiling showed that immunotherapy remodels the marrow neighborhood by changing the abundance and positioning of monocytes, dendritic cells, and T-cell populations. Together, these findings suggest, by inference, that future spatial profiling of HAVCR2, LGALS9, HMGB1, and phosphatidylserine-associated signals across vascular, endosteal, and inflammatory compartments may clarify where TIM-3 blockade is most likely to reprogram immune suppression and leukemic stem-cell persistence [29, 30, 81].

The second edge: The cell-autonomous role of TIM-3 in leukemic stemness and survival

TIM-3 as a highly selective, but not universal, marker of the leukemic stem cell compartment

A convergent body of translational and functional data now places TIM-3 (HAVCR2) within the phenotypic and functional signature of human acute myeloid leukemia leukemic stem cells, but not as a universal pan-LSC marker. The strongest enrichment is seen in the primitive CD34⁺CD38⁻ fraction, where TIM-3 marks cells with xenogeneic engraftment and long-term self-renewal while being largely absent from normal hematopoietic stem cells; however, human AML can also contain genetically distinct CD34⁻ LSC populations, and TIM-3 expression can extend beyond the CD34⁺CD38⁻ gate in some cases, including more mature CD34⁺CD38⁺ leukemic fractions, its interpretation is strengthened when combined with CD123 and CLEC12A/CLL-1 to resolve biologically distinct LSC subsets and avoid overgeneralizing a single immunophenotype [55, 56, 76, 77, 82–85]. Clinical flow cytometry series corroborate these observations, showing that the frequency of CD34⁺CD38⁻TIM-3⁺ cells is higher at diagnosis and frequently persists or expands at relapse and after chemotherapy, linking the TIM-3 + fraction to measurable residual disease and adverse outcome [57, 86]. Functionally, antibody targeting and genetic perturbation of TIM-3 impair serial reconstitution in xenograft models, demonstrating that TIM-3 is not merely a surface correlate but a functional LSC marker whose blockade or depletion selectively reduces leukemic repopulating capacity while sparing normal human HSC activity in preclinical systems [28, 56]. Mechanistically, this LSC-associated biology is reinforced by the TIM-3/Gal-9 autocrine loop that activates NF-κB and β-catenin to sustain self-renewal, and by later work showing that TIM-3 can hijack canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling to maintain cancer stemness in AML. These orthogonal lines of evidence together support TIM-3 as a discriminating LSC identifier and a context-dependent therapeutic window in a subset of AML: TIM-3 can separate malignant self-renewing clones from the bulk blast population and from normal stem cells, but TIM-3 is not universal across all LSC-containing specimens, and therapy can remodel the LSC compartment so that TIM-3-low/negative escape remains a plausible route to relapse. Accordingly, TIM-3-directed interventions should be framed as subtype- and state-dependent, ideally paired with complementary LSC markers and longitudinal clonal monitoring [56, 87].

Non-canonical, ligand-independent intracellular signaling

Beyond its role as a surface marker, TIM-3 engages intracellular signaling programs in LSCs that deviate from classical ligand-triggered immune checkpoint paradigms. The canonical paradigm in AML has emphasized an autocrine TIM-3/galectin-9 loop that drives co-activation of NF-κB and β-catenin to sustain self-renewal; seminal work showed that TIM-3 + LSCs secrete galectin-9 and that interruption of this autocrine circuit abrogates β-catenin-dependent stemness [56]. Layered onto this, there is substantial mechanistic evidence from immunology and cancer biology that TIM-3 can bind the alarmin HMGB1 and modulate intracellular endosomal and nucleic acid sensing pathways; classical studies in tumor associated dendritic cells showed that TIM-3–HMGB1 interactions limit HMGB1-mediated nucleic acid sensing and downstream innate immune activation [41, 71]. In the context of AML, HMGB1 is abundant in the marrow microenvironment and has been implicated as a prosurvival factor, promoting autophagy, angiogenesis and chemoresistance, and several reports infer that HMGB1–TIM-3 engagement contributes to leukemia progression and survival programs [72, 73]. While the precise topology of HMGB1–TIM-3 engagement in LSCs remains an active area of investigation, current data support a model in which intracellular or pericellular HMGB1 can cooperate with TIM-3 to sequester pro-apoptotic nucleic acid signals and to bias downstream transcriptional responses away from cell death and toward survival and self-renewal [41, 73]. Within the AML marrow niche, TIM-3 ligand engagement is context dependent rather than governed by a single fixed hierarchy. Galectin-9 is the best-established leukemic ligand and is supplied by AML blasts and surrounding stromal/myeloid cells, whereas HMGB1 is enriched in DAMP-rich or therapy-damaged marrow, phosphatidylserine is concentrated on apoptotic bodies and stressed cells, and CEACAM1 contributes mainly where it is coexpressed on TIM-3-positive immune or leukemic cells. Structural and cell-biological studies further indicate that TIM-3 binds phosphatidylserine through the IgV domain and that PS engagement can directly tune downstream signaling, while antibody-mapping experiments show that TIM-3 ligands occupy overlapping but not identical receptor surfaces. Consequently, the dominant ligand is determined by ligand abundance, membrane context, and local coexpression state; however, the precise affinity ranking among Gal-9, HMGB1, CEACAM1, and phosphatidylserine in primary AML remains incompletely resolved [39, 40, 65, 70, 73, 88].

Mechanistically, recent proteomic and biochemical dissection has begun to reveal TIM-3 proximal partners that link the receptor to canonical oncogenic effectors. Sakoda et al. showed that TIM-3/Gal-9 signaling recruits HCK and p120-catenin to form an LRP6 signalosome, thereby activating canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling in AML LSCs independent of Wnt ligands [78]. Concomitantly, TIM-3 activation co-stimulates NF-κB, a central node that fosters proliferation, inflammatory crosstalk and expression of anti-apoptotic genes; early functional genomics and pathway analyses of TIM-3high AML point to enrichment of NF-κB and JAK–STAT signatures that can transcriptionally upregulate survival factors and mitochondrial regulators [56, 79, 89]. Direct biochemical coupling between TIM-3 signaling and anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family regulation is less explicitly mapped, but several lines of evidence imply functional crosstalk: TIM-3–driven NF-κB and metabolic reprogramming create an intracellular milieu that favors expression and activity of BCL-2 family proteins, and clinical correlations link AML samples with elevated TIM-3/HAVCR2 expression, typically assessed in the relevant blast- or LSC-enriched compartment by flow cytometry or transcriptomic stratification, with pathways known to mediate resistance to BCL-2 inhibition [90, 91]. In short, TIM-3 in LSCs operates through both ligand-dependent autocrine loops and through ligand-independent intracellular assemblies, recruiting kinases and scaffolds that feed β-catenin, NF-κB and survival networks to sustain self-renewal and limit apoptotic susceptibility [56, 57].

Metabolic reprogramming via TIM-3

A frontier area in TIM-3 biology is its emerging role as a metabolic rheostat for LSCs; importantly, this cell-intrinsic metabolic program should be distinguished from the immunometabolic effects of TIM-3 in T cells and macrophages discussed in Sect. 2.1, which likely promote immune escape in AML. Leukemic stem cells are metabolically distinctive: many rely on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and on pathway flexibility that includes fatty acid oxidation and amino acid metabolism to survive chemotherapy and survive in the niche [49–51]. New experimental work shows that engagement of the TIM-3/Gal-9 axis rewires metabolic programs in leukemic cells: in AML cell lines, galectin-9 stimulation increases markers of glutamine utilization, including induction of glutaminase and glutamate handling enzymes and activation of mTORC signaling, consistent with an axis by which TIM-3 signaling can steer carbon flow into anabolic and bioenergetic routes that support proliferation and stress tolerance [24]. Complementary studies and reviews have linked TIM-3/Gal-9 signaling to changes in glucose and lipid metabolism and have proposed that TIM-3-expressing LSCs exploit fatty acid oxidation as a reserve pathway to fuel oxidative phosphorylation when amino acid substrates are limited or when venetoclax selectively blocks BCL-2–dependent metabolic vulnerabilities [87, 90]. Mechanistically, the recruitment of kinase modules such as HCK and downstream activation of β-catenin and NF-κB plausibly reprograms transcriptional networks toward expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters that favor glutaminolysis and FAO, and recent proteomic atlases of AML identify metabolic enzymes and mitochondrial regulators among nodes that co-segregate with TIM-3 high phenotypes [93, 94]. Functionally this metabolic plasticity affords chemoresistance: LSCs that engage TIM-3–dependent metabolic programs are better able to preserve mitochondrial membrane integrity, to buffer oxidative stress and to bypass pro-apoptotic signals, thereby reducing sensitivity to genotoxic therapy and to BCL-2 inhibition [90, 92]. Taken together, the cell-autonomous facet of TIM-3 activity in AML converts a surface immune checkpoint into an intracellular conductor of stemness, survival and metabolic plasticity. The mechanistic picture that emerges is not monolithic but modular: TIM-3 is enriched on and functionally helps define the LSC compartment; beyond serving as a surface marker, it scaffolds non-canonical intracellular signaling via HCK/p120-catenin modules to activate β-catenin and NF-κB programs that support self-renewal and survival, cooperates with alarmins such as HMGB1 to dampen pro-death innate signaling, and reprograms metabolic wiring toward glutamine- and fatty-acid-based fuels that support survival under therapeutic stress [24, 41, 56, 57]. This integrated biology provides a compelling rationale for combinatorial strategies that pair TIM-3 directed agents with metabolic or BCL-2 pathway inhibitors and for focused proteomic interrogation of TIM-3 complexes to expose additional, druggable nodes in the LSC survival apparatus [91, 93].

The therapeutic crucible: Sharpening the sword for clinical use

Current landscape of TIM-3-targeted agents

The clinical translation of TIM-3 biology has proceeded along multiple parallel paths, each designed to exploit the receptor’s dual placement on dysfunctional immune cells and on leukemic stem and progenitor compartments. Seminal work that first identified TIM-3 as a selective marker of leukemic stem cells in primary AML specimens, together with subsequent studies showing patient-level heterogeneity and incomplete universality, provided the biological rationale for therapeutically exploiting HAVCR2 in acute myeloid leukemia [55, 57, 76, 77]. These biological data have been complemented by a thorough preclinical characterization of therapeutic antibodies, of which sabatolimab (MBG453) is the most advanced, showing ligand-blocking activity plus measurable immune and myeloid effects in mechanistic studies: increased T-cell-mediated killing of TIM-3 + targets, increased dendritic-cell inflammatory cytokine production, and increased phagocytic uptake/ADCP of TIM-3-expressing cells, as assessed in co-culture killing, cytokine, and phagocytosis assays [95]. Clinical development of TIM-3 agents in myeloid disease has advanced to patient-facing trials; a concise summary of completed and ongoing human studies (including sabatolimab/MBG453 programs) is given in Table 3. The translation of those laboratory observations into human studies has produced tolerable combination regimens and encouraging signals in early-phase work, with a phase Ib study of sabatolimab plus decitabine or azacitidine reporting manageable safety and clinical activity in high-risk myeloid neoplasms [96]. These data formed the backbone for randomized evaluation: the STIMULUS-MDS1 phase II trial tested sabatolimab plus a hypomethylating agent versus hypomethylating agent (HMA) alone and, although the study did not meet its primary endpoints, it established a safety profile rather than confirming a clear efficacy signal [97]. Importantly, sponsor documents state that Novartis decided in December 2023 to early terminate the sabatolimab clinical development program after the negative STIMULUS-MDS1 and STIMULUS-MDS2 results, both of which failed to meet their primary objectives, and the STIMULUS-AML1 record was likewise stopped at the sponsor level [97, 98]. The most accurate framing is therefore that sabatolimab generated important translational data, but its broader clinical development was curtailed rather than continuing as an active corporate program [97–101].

Mechanistically, monoclonal TIM-3 antibodies do not act through a single uniform mode of action but rather through a combination of effects that may vary by clone and isotype. Detailed biochemical and cellular work indicates that sabatolimab is a high-affinity, ligand-interfering IgG4 antibody that can partially block Galectin-9 interactions while also engaging immune effector mechanisms that influence myeloid cell function and leukemic self-renewal [95, 105]. Other clinical anti-TIM-3 antibodies such as cobolimab (TSR-022) have been engineered to modulate receptor internalization and to synergize with PD-1 blockade, and early clinical programmes have emphasized combination safety and proof-of-mechanism rather than monotherapy activity [106, 107]. Collectively, the preclinical literature supports at least two convergent therapeutic mechanisms for TIM-3 antibodies: blockade of ligand-driven, cell-intrinsic leukemic signalling that sustains self-renewal, and relief of immune exhaustion through checkpoint blockade and Fc-mediated myeloid reprogramming [95, 105].

Beyond monospecific antibodies, multispecific biologics are an active area of development. Emerging TIM-3 modalities, including alternative antibody clones, bispecific checkpoint reagents, and CAR-based approaches, are summarized in Table 4 (selected early clinical and preclinical programmes). Bispecific and trispecific constructs that simultaneously engage TIM-3 and either T-cell activating modules or myeloid antigens seek to combine direct anti-LSC targeting with enforced cytotoxic recruitment, and early programmatic reports and conference abstracts indicate the appearance of TIM-3/PD-1 bispecific formats and multivalent AML engagers designed to limit antigen escape [108, 109]. This multispecific strategy has two clear rationales: to direct innate or adaptive effectors selectively to TIM-3-positive leukemic clones and to forestall rapid antigen loss by requiring coexpression of multiple targets for effective engagement [110–112].

Cellular therapies directed at TIM-3 are similarly advancing from concept to proof of principle in preclinical models. However, TIM-3 is not strictly restricted to malignant cells: it is present on normal hematopoietic populations, and integrative protein-expression analyses have detected TIM-3 in a limited range of non-hematopoietic tissues, especially organs with immune infiltration, underscoring the need for careful antigen-distribution assessment before clinical CAR translation [76, 116, 117]. Anti-TIM-3 CAR constructs produce potent cytotoxicity against TIM-3-expressing AML lines and primary blasts in xenograft studies, supporting the concept of directly eliminating the TIM-3-positive LSC compartment with engineered lymphocytes [118, 119]. More sophisticated CAR architectures, including split, dual or bispecific CARs that pair TIM-3 recognition with a second AML antigen such as CD13, CD33 or CD123, are being explored to increase selectivity and reduce on-target/off-tumour hematopoietic toxicity [120–122]. Taken together, the monoclonal, multispecific and cell-based TIM-3 platforms form a complementary toolkit for clinical deployment that can be adapted to distinct disease states and combinations. Therapeutic modalities targeting TIM-3: mechanism and clinical status

The dual-targeting imperative: Rational combination therapies

The dual biology of TIM-3 provides a preclinical/translational rationale for combination regimens that attack both tumor cell-intrinsic survival machinery and the immunosuppressive microenvironment; however, the clinical evidence supporting any given partner drug remains early and uneven. For hypomethylating agents (HMAs), the rationale is mainly preclinical/translational: azacitidine and decitabine can increase antigen presentation and immunogenicity in AML, which supports combination with TIM-3 blockade, whereas the human evidence currently comes from early-phase sabatolimab studies and trial-level reporting rather than mature randomized data [96, 125–127]. For venetoclax, the synergy with TIM-3 blockade is still best described as a mechanistic/preclinical hypothesis: venetoclax lowers apoptotic thresholds in AML, while TIM-3 blockade is expected to reduce LSC persistence and immune exhaustion; the AML clinical evidence remains limited to early-phase development of the sabatolimab-azacitidine-venetoclax triplet and conference/trial reporting [98, 99, 128, 129]. For other immunotherapy combinations (for example PD-1, CD47/SIRPα, and vaccine-based strategies), the evidence in AML remains predominantly preclinical or translational, with only limited early clinical support; they should therefore be presented as promising hypotheses rather than established clinical strategies [17, 96, 125, 130, 131].

Finally, conventional cytotoxic approaches and transplantation remain central to curative intent in AML and must be considered in TIM-3 targeted strategies. The unique identification of TIM-3 on measurable residual leukemic stem cells after allogeneic transplantation and its strong association with post-transplant relapse make TIM-3 an attractive peri-transplant target for MRD-directed eradication [55, 57, 132, 133]. However, timing and modality are critical: immune-based interventions can interact with graft-versus-host biology and marrow recovery, and randomized clinical data such as STIMULUS highlight that biologic plausibility does not always translate into additive benefit without careful selection of patient populations and endpoints [97, 134]. In short, the most promising clinical strategy for TIM-3 in AML appears to be rational, biomarker-driven combinations that synchronously blunt leukemic self-renewal, lower apoptotic thresholds and reinvigorate both innate and adaptive immunity while preserving hematopoietic recovery.

Challenges and future directions

Biomarkers for patient stratification

Biomarker selection for TIM-3-directed therapy should be tiered by assay maturity and clinical utility. At present, the most clinically actionable readouts are standardized measurable residual disease (MRD) approaches, especially multiparameter flow cytometry-based LSC/MRD panels and, where genotype permits, molecular MRD, because MRD is already embedded in ELN consensus guidance for prognosis, therapy guidance, and trial endpoints. By contrast, soluble Gal-9, TIM-3 ligand profiling, PtdSer-rich apoptotic-body readouts, and T-cell clonality/exhaustion signatures are better framed as translational or exploratory biomarkers: they are biologically informative and useful for trial enrichment, but they remain insufficiently standardized for routine clinical decision-making. Within this exploratory set, Gal-9/TIM-3 axis measurements and MR-LSC frequency are the most mature and most directly aligned with TIM-3 pharmacology, whereas PtdSer engagement and T-cell clonality should currently be reserved primarily for correlative studies and hypothesis generation [135–138]. Within this exploratory set, Gal-9/TIM-3 axis measurements and MR-LSC frequency are the most mature and most directly aligned with TIM-3 pharmacology, whereas PtdSer engagement and T-cell clonality should currently be reserved primarily for correlative studies and hypothesis generation [39, 65, 135–138].

Finally, integrating genomic subtypes into prediction algorithms is critical because specific genotypes, including FLT3-ITD, NPM1-mutated, IDH1/2-mutated, and TP53-mutated disease, are associated with different ELN risk groups, therapeutic sensitivities, and immune-microenvironment features; therefore combining TIM-3 ligand landscape, LSC burden, T-cell clonality and mutational status will yield superior stratification for TIM-3–targeted approaches [3–6, 139].

Mechanisms of resistance to TIM-3 blockade

Resistance to TIM-3 inhibition in AML is likely to be multifactorial and to mirror patterns already observed with other immunotherapies, namely compensatory checkpoint upregulation, antigenic or phenotypic escape of target clones, and remodeling of the ligand milieu that sustains oncogenic signaling. Preclinical and clinical data indicate that blockade of a single checkpoint commonly leads to adaptive increases in PD-1, LAG-3 and TIGIT expression on T cells and myeloid elements, thereby blunting clinical benefit unless concerted combinations are used [140, 141]. Second, AML is clonally heterogeneous and LSCs can segregate into TIM-3-high and TIM-3-low phenotypic states, as defined in the cited studies by relative TIM-3/HAVCR2 abundance in the LSC-enriched compartment rather than by a patient-level binary label; selective pressure from TIM-3–targeted agents could therefore favor outgrowth of TIM-3-negative or transcriptionally reprogrammed LSC clones, producing antigen escape that parallels what has been observed after targeted therapies and cellular immunotherapies [22, 142, 143]. Third, because TIM-3 biology depends on its ligands, therapeutic perturbation may alter secretion or display of galectin-9, HMGB1 and phosphatidylserine thereby reprogramming paracrine loops and stromal interactions; early clinical and translational reports of ligand modulation after immune interventions support routine monitoring of the ligand landscape as both a pharmacodynamic endpoint and a potential resistance mechanism [24, 56]. Together these mechanisms counsel for trial designs that prespecify serial immune and clonal monitoring, multi-arm combination strategies to preempt compensatory pathways, and adaptive biomarker-driven escalation to salvage emerging resistance. Mitigating on-target, off-tumor toxicity

A central safety concern for TIM-3–targeted therapies in AML is that TIM-3 is not restricted to malignant cells; it is expressed physiologically on IFN-γ-producing effector T cells, subsets of NK and myeloid cells, and has been reported on mast cells in some inflammatory contexts, raising the possibility that broad TIM-3 antagonism provokes immune perturbations or autoimmunity if not carefully constrained [158–160]. Moreover, TIM-3 participates in homeostatic processes such as regulation of dendritic cells and possibly efferocytosis through its PtdSer interaction, so indiscriminate blockade risks impairing clearance of apoptotic debris and precipitating inflammatory sequelae [65, 161]. Practical mitigation strategies include antibody engineering to tune Fc effector function, local or marrow-directed delivery to minimize systemic exposure, and developing selective reagents that spare physiologic TIM-3 roles on healthy cells [95]. Importantly, clinical development programs must embed high-resolution immune monitoring and standardized assays for autoimmunity, because preclinical models incompletely predict on-target effects in the complex human hematopoietic niche [97, 162].

Next-generation therapeutic strategies

Several emerging modalities promise to convert TIM-3 from a blunt clinical target into a precise therapeutic lever. First, ligand-specific blockers that selectively disrupt the TIM-3:Gal-9 interaction while sparing TIM-3:PtdSer engagement are conceptually attractive, although direct head-to-head affinity data remain limited; structural and functional mapping suggests partially distinct but overlapping ligand footprints on the TIM-3 IgV domain, supporting the idea that ligand engagement is biased by local ligand abundance and membrane context rather than a single universal affinity order [39, 40, 65]. Second, signal-directed therapies that target intracellular effectors downstream of TIM-3, for example HCK, p120-catenin or the LRP6/β-catenin module exploited by LSCs, offer the potential to uncouple pro-leukemic signaling from TIM-3’s immunoregulatory roles; such approaches could use small molecules, peptide inhibitors, or targeted degraders to achieve LSC-specific cytotoxicity [57, 163, 164]. Third, advanced cellular therapies bring modular engineering options: TIM-3 armored CAR constructs that secrete TIM-3 decoys, TIM-3-knockout or checkpoint-edited allogeneic CAR-T and CAR-NK cells, and CARs directed against TIM-3-expressing LSCs have all shown promising preclinical activity and provide routes to overcome both immune exhaustion and antigen heterogeneity [118, 165, 166]. In aggregate these strategies argue for integrated clinical development where biomarker-defined cohorts receive the appropriate biologic modality and where iterative translational readouts guide the next round of combination and engineering choices.

Conclusion

TIM-3 stands at a critical juncture in the AML landscape, embodying a dual biological nature that intertwines the suppression of anti-leukemic immunity with the direct promotion of leukemic stemness. This review has delineated how TIM-3 functions as an important node within a broader suppressive network, contributing to T-cell and myeloid cell exhaustion in the tumor microenvironment and activating intrinsic pro-survival and self-renewal pathways within LSCs. This unique positioning makes it an exceptionally attractive, albeit complex, therapeutic target. The emergence of TIM-3-directed therapies, including blocking antibodies, multispecific engagers, and cellular products, heralds a new era of immune-based strategies in AML. However, the path to clinical success is not straightforward. It necessitates biomarker-driven patient stratification that integrates LSC burden, ligand landscape, and immune contexture. Furthermore, AML should be viewed as a broader ecosystem shaped by genetic heterogeneity, marrow niche signals, and compensatory checkpoints such as PD-1, LAG-3, and TIGIT; accordingly, TIM-3 blockade is most likely to succeed as part of rational combination therapy rather than as a stand-alone solution. By sharpening our understanding of its Janus-faced biology, we can forge precision tools to turn TIM-3 from a mechanism of disease persistence into a lever for durable remission.
