Reliability and Construct Validity of Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System Physical Function in Patients With Peripheral Artery Disease
Authors: Teryn A. Holeman, Blake Findley, Cassidy Chester, Julie Hales, Yingjia Wei, Benjamin S. Brooke, Amy Cizik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The construct validity of the National Institutes of Health—developed Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System physical function (PROMIS-PF) instrument has not been established in patients with peripheral artery disease (PAD). This study evaluates the reliability and construct validity of PROMIS-PF compared to general and PAD-specific measures.Methods:Patients with PAD were recruited at a large academic medical center and completed PROMIS-PF, 36-item short-form (SF-36), Vascular Quality of Life (VascuQoL), walking impairment questionnaire (WIQ), and PROMIS-depression instruments at two time points. Intraclass correlation coefficients assessed test-retest reliability. Spearman rank correlations between PROMIS-PF and similar functional measures evaluated convergent validity, while emotional wellbeing measures served as a discriminant comparison.Results:Twenty-one patients completed questionnaires for both time points with a mean age of 71.5 ± 8.2 y. The cohort was 33% female, with 53% living in moderate or high areas of deprivation. Nineteen percent had chronic limb ischemia, and 76% underwent prior revascularization for PAD. The reliability of the PROMIS-PF was 0.96 (confidence interval [CI] 0.89-0.98) compared to 0.96 (CI 0.92-0.98), 0.95 (0.91-0.98), and 0.79 (CI 0.50-0.92) for the VascuQoL, SF-36 physical function component score, and WIQ, respectively. PROMIS-PF had the strongest correlations with the WIQ (rho = 0.86, P < 0.0001) and VascuQoL (0.85, P < 0.0001) and had the lowest correlation with the SF-36 emotional wellbeing component score (0.15, P = 0.52).Conclusions:PROMIS-PF is a reliable and valid measure of physical function in PAD, demonstrating strong correlations with PAD-specific function measures and discriminant validity with unrelated constructs. These findings support its use in clinical research and PAD patient care.

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is caused by stenosis or occlusion of lower extremity arteries and is estimated to affect over 200 million individuals globally.1,2 Patients with PAD often experience extreme pain, impaired physical function, nonhealing wounds, and decreased quality of life (QoL).2,3 Disease progression and functional status for patients with PAD are routinely assessed using ankle-brachial index perfusion measurements, physical examination, and physician estimation of patient function.4 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) allow for the assessment of physical limitations and QoL, which are often not captured by traditional clinical measures. PROMs aim to complement traditional clinical indicators and provide insight to a patient’s estimation of their overall health and QoL. PROMs focused on physical function are particularly interesting in PAD as many therapies and interventions aim to improve function and maintain activities of daily living. As the number of available PROMs increases and their use in clinical care becomes more common, it is important to investigate the validity and reliability of each instrument in patients with PAD.

Several PROM instruments are available and commonly used to assess the disease-specific effects of PAD.5 The Vascular Quality of Life (VascuQoL) instrument is considered the preferred PROM for assessing QoL among patients with PAD. It has been used in multiple clinical trials, including the landmark Bypass versus Angioplasty in Severe Ischemia of the Leg trial.5,6 Disease-specific instruments can effectively provide data about disease burden and treatment effectiveness. However, generic PROM instruments may be preferable for evaluating patients with multiple health conditions because they do not attribute symptoms or functions to a single health condition.3 The clinical application of a generic PROM is of particular interest to patients with PAD as the majority have cardiovascular or metabolic comorbidities that influence their overall clinical picture.7,8 Additionally, the implementation and cost of using disease-specific PROM within a hospital system or practice would be more cumbersome and costly compared to implementing a single physical function instrument that could be applied to multiple patient populations and disease processes.

The 36-item short form (SF-36) is one such generic instrument and is considered the gold standard for evaluating QoL before disease-specific measures were developed.5,9 The SF-36 scores a patient’s overall health by assessing eight different health domains: physical functioning, pain, role limitations due to physical health problems, role limitations due to emotional problems, emotional wellbeing, social functioning, energy, and general health perceptions. The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System physical function (PROMIS-PF) developed by the National Institutes of Health is another generic instrument that uses item response theory and computerized adaptive testing (CAT) to assess a patient’s QoL and physical function.10 Item response theory and CAT testing means PROMIS uses fewer questions to score function accurately within 2-5 min, improving the implementation of PROMS into busy clinic workflows.11,12 PROMIS-PF has been validated in patients with arthritis,13–15 inflammatory bowel disease,16 and heart transplant,17 among others. When compared to the SF-36, PROMIS-PF has shown a high correlation with the SF-36 physical functioning domain in some patient populations (r = 0.82, r = 0.95).18,19

Our group has previously demonstrated how PROMIS-PF can be used as a clinical outcome in both medically and surgically treated PAD to track patients’ physical function and patients believe PROMs are valuable in their clinical care.8,20 However, to our knowledge, the validity of PROMIS-PF in patients with PAD has not been described. Understanding how the PROMIS-PF instrument compares to other generic and disease-specific PROMs in patients with PAD is particularly important, as Centers for Medicare and Medicaid have initiated using PROMIS instruments in reimbursement for elective total joint procedures in 2026.21 It is not implausible that reimbursement for other elective procedures, like revascularization for PAD, could follow. The validity and reliability of PROMIS-PF to accurately measure functional changes related to PAD over time would be essential to its application in reimbursement and clinical care.

We designed this study to evaluate the construct validity and reliability of PROMIS-PF in patients with PAD. Construct validity is the extent to which an instrument accurately measures the theoretical construct or trait intended to be measured. For example, the PROMIS-PF instrument should correlate with an existing measure of physical function and not correlate as well to a measure of social function because they measure different constructs or traits. Reliability is the extent to which an instrument yields the same result over time when the evaluated construct or trait has not changed.22 An example is when we ask patients to fill out the measure at different times and do not expect their answers to change because there was no intervention. Together, these two attributes can determine if PROMIS-PF is an appropriate instrument for assessing physical function in patients with PAD.

Methods

Study design and cohort selection

This study is a prospective cohort of patients with diagnosed PAD at a large academic medical center. Patients were eligible if they were older than 18 y, could provide informed consent, could complete PROMs in English, and had a confirmed diagnosis of PAD based on patient history, examination, and ankle-brachial index results. Patients were mailed participation assessments to their homes or collected in person during clinic appointments. A researcher called patients who had mailed materials to discuss the informed consent document and answer any questions regarding study materials. All patients were counseled on how to complete the assessments and were instructed to complete them all in one setting. No controls were in place for the order in which the patients completed the assessments.

A power calculation was performed to estimate the sample size prior to study recruitment. It determined the sample size required for a target correlation of >0.75 and a correlation for the null hypothesis of 0.35. Using a significance criterion of alpha = 0.05 and power = 0.8, the minimum sample size needed is at least 20 patients. Eighty-six patients with PAD who had appointments with a vascular surgeon were approached for recruitment by a member of the research team by mail or in person during their clinic visit from May 2023 to December 2023 (Fig. 1). Thirty-seven patients completed the first set of assessments but were lost to follow-up or only partially completed the assessment bundle. A final cohort of 21 patients completed all PROM assessments (SF-36, VascuQoL, walking impairment questionnaire [WIQ], PROMIS-depression [PROMIS-Dep], and PROMIS-PF) at two time points, 3.5 to 13 wk apart, with an average of 6 ± 2 wk between each time point. Patients did not receive any compensation for their participation. Patient characteristics were collected via chart review. Area deprivation was used as a surrogate measure of socioeconomic status based on national zip code. Patients’ preoperative frailty status and overall health were scored by a surgical provider using the 9-point clinical frailty scale, a validated frailty assessment tool that incorporates their functional status and level of activity.23,24

The University of Utah Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol. All patients signed an informed consent document after discussing the risks and benefits of participating in the study. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines were followed when designing the study and preparing the manuscript.25

Patient-reported outcome assessments

We selected psychometrically sound and commonly used PROMs in patients with PAD to compare to the PROMIS-PF instrument.26 Comparative measures were classified as convergent or discriminate based on expert knowledge of constructs (TH and AC). The comparative assessments include disease-specific and generic instruments designed to measure similar and dissimilar constructs to function.5

PROMIS-PF

PROMIS-PF instrument (PROMIS Bank v2.0—physical function CAT) assessments were administered electronically via a link that was emailed and texted to the patient before their appointment, as previously described.27 PROMIS instruments are assumed to follow a normal distribution at the population level, with a mean T-score of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. Higher scores represent higher physical function, and lower scores represent lower function. Question banks for the PROMIS instruments can be downloaded on the PROMIS website free of charge.10

PROMIS-Dep

The PROMIS-Dep instrument (PROMIS Bank v2.0—depression CAT) was chosen as a discriminate construct. PROMIS-Dep assessments were administered electronically via a link emailed and texted to the patient before their appointment, as previously described.27 Higher scores represent higher depression, and lower scores represent lower depression. The PROMIS-Dep instrument has a smaller sample size of n = 15.

The Medical Outcomes Study SF-36

The SF-36 is a multipurpose, short-form health survey with 36 items assessing eight health domains: physical functioning (10 items), pain (2 items), role limitations due to physical health problems (4 items), role limitations due to personal or emotional problems (4 items), emotional wellbeing (5 items), social functioning (2 items), energy (4 items), and general health perceptions (5 items).28,29 Scores for each domain range from 0 to 100, with a higher score defining a more favorable health state.

WIQ

The WIQ was one of the first disease-specific PAD questionnaires specifically designed to assess functional status.30,31 The WIQ consists of 22 items assessing pain, distance, walking speed, and stair climbing. The WIQ is a purely functional instrument developed to correlate with treadmill performance measures in PAD patients.

VascuQoL questionnaire

The VascuQoL consists of 25 items assessing pain, symptoms, activities, and social and emotional impact of PAD.32 It was developed as a PAD-specific QoL tool created by an expert multidisciplinary team, including vascular surgeons, rehabilitation medicine, and diabetic foot specialists. The VascuQoL has been identified as an ideal disease-specific instrument because it explicitly targets QoL domains relevant to PAD. This study used the VascuQoL-6 item questionnaire, which demonstrated equivalency to the 25-item original metric and was used in this study to help reduce patient survey fatigue.33 Each question is scored 1-4. The total score is achieved by summarizing the score on each question, resulting in a score between 6 and 24. A higher value indicates better health status.

Statistical analysis

Reliability

The test-retest intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were used to assess the consistency of scores obtained by the same subjects across the two testing occasions. ICC for the PROMIS-PF instrument was compared to the other disease-specific and generic measures over time. An ICC value > 0.9 demonstrates excellent reliability, 0.75-0.9 is good reliability, 0.5-0.75 is moderate reliability, and <0.5 demonstrates poor reliability.34 Negative values are equivalent to an ICC of 0, corresponding to no reliability. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) for the ICC values are reported.

Construct validity

Spearman rank correlations assessed the construct validity of the PROMIS-PF and corresponding conceptually similar and divergent measures at both time points.17,18,35 Instruments measuring similar constructs of walking impairment and function constructs were compared for convergent construct validity. Dissimilar measures of QoL and emotional wellbeing were utilized as a discriminate comparison. Construct validity will be established if PROMIS-PF is strongly associated with the magnitude of change assessed by the absolute average correlations between different measures of the same domain (i.e., monotrait-heteromethod) and not associated with different domains (i.e., heterotrait-heteromethod). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata BE 17.0 and R 4.1.0 statistical software.36,37 P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 shows the participants’ characteristics at the time of the assessment bundle. Participants were aged 53-81 y (mean, SD: 71.5 ± 8.2 y), 33% were female, and 95% were Caucasian. Area deprivation was used as a surrogate measure of socioeconomic status, with 48% (n = 10) living in areas of low deprivation, 43% (n = 9) living in moderate deprivation, and 10% (n = 2) living in an area of high deprivation. Nineteen percent of the cohort had progressed to more severe PAD with chronic limb-threatening ischemia, and 76% underwent surgical intervention associated with their PAD prior to assessment.

Results related to the convergent validity and reliability are illustrated in Table 2. The test-retest reliability of all convergent measures was >0.75 (Table 2). More specifically, PROMIS-PF test-retest reliability across the two time points for the cohort was 0.96 (CI 0.89-0.98) compared to 0.96 (CI 0.92-0.98), 0.95 (0.91-0.98), and 0.79 (CI 0.50-0.92) for the VascuQoL, SF-36 physical function component score, and WIQ, respectively. Table 2 also shows the correlations of individual instruments with the PROMIS-PF instrument at both time points. The construct validity of the PROMIS-PF instrument for patients with PAD had the strongest correlations with the WIQ speed component at time point 1 (rho = 0.86, P < 0.0001) and SF-36 physical function domain at time point 2 (0.92, P < 0.0001).

Discriminate measure validity and reliability are shown in Table 3. The PROMIS depression instrument had the highest reliability of the discriminate measures, at 0.94 (0.82-0.98). Several test-retest reliability ICC values for discriminate measures were negative, including the SF-36 emotional wellbeing component score (ICC = −0.11, CI −0.52 to 0.34) due to the high score variability between time points (Table 3). For discriminate validity, PROMIS-PF had the strongest correlations with the SF-36 energy fatigue component score at time point 1 (rho = 0.61, P = 0.003) and SF-36 social functioning score at time point 2 (0.62, P = 0.003). PROMIS-PF had the lowest correlation with the SF-36 emotional wellbeing domain at time point 1 (0.15, P = 0.52) and PROMIS-Dep at time point 2 (−0.19, P = 0.42). Overall, the convergent validity for PROMIS-PF with all similar measures for both time points had an average correlation ≥0.70, while the average discriminate validity was ≤0.41 at both time points, Table 4.

Discussion

It is crucial to demonstrate that the PROMIS-PF instrument accurately and reliably assesses physical function in patients with PAD for its future use in clinical care. The PROMIS-PF instrument has been validated in many patient populations. However, its validity in patients with PAD, to our knowledge, has not been previously shown. Our results show that PROMIS-PF demonstrates high reliability in tracking patients with PAD over time, greater than or equal to other disease-specific and general measures used clinically. The WIQ, SF-36, and VascuQoL instruments have been previously validated in patients with PAD and demonstrate a high correlation with the constructs for function. The PROMIS-PF instrument demonstrated a high correlation with these similar constructs of function and a low-moderate correlation with dissimilar constructs like emotional wellbeing and depression. Overall, we have provided evidence of its construct validity and reliability in this patient population with equivalent intraclass correlations to other disease-specific and generic measures and convergent validity of PROMIS-PF with similar constructs in patients with PAD.

Reliability and validity are essential for the clinical application of the PROMIS-PF instrument. Reliability is the extent to which an instrument yields the same result each time it is administered when the measured construct has not changed.22 Reliability, often called test-retest reliability, is assessed using intraclass correlation.38 We found that the PROMIS instruments have excellent test-retest reliability measured across the two time points. The interval between time points in this study was short and patients did not have any intervention, therefore we did not anticipate significant fluctuations in scores. PROMs should all have high reliability when retested. However, the SF-36 emotional wellbeing, SF-36 energy fatigue, and SF-36 Social functioning instruments had negative ICC values. The negative ICC values suggest that within-group variability exceeds between-group variability, meaning an individual’s scores fluctuated more over time than the differences observed between individuals at a single time point. Negative ICCs reflect an inherently unstable or highly variable measure. In this case, negative ICCs were calculated for three relatively subjective measures that may be more easily influenced by external factors when compared to more stable and relatively objective measures, such as physical function.

Content validity assesses whether an instrument comprehensively captures all relevant aspects of the construct it is intended to measure and is generally evaluated qualitatively using groups and expert opinion. Criterion validity assesses how well an instrument correlates to a bench-mark measure. However, it is usually not applicable as PROM instruments have no established gold standard. Construct validity assesses whether an instrument accurately measures the theoretical construct it is intended to measure and is generally considered the favored approach when evaluating PROM instruments.22,39 However, no experimental design or statistical methodology is commonly used to assess PROM validity. This is partly due to the fact that psychometric instruments can be evaluated on three subtypes of validity: content, criterion, and construct.22

Construct validity is a broad concept encompassing many terms commonly used in the literature. Common terms in this category include convergent validity, discriminate validity, and known-group validity. Convergent validity is how a PROM correlates with known similar measures. Discriminate validity is the degree to which a PROM shows no or little correlation to known dissimilar measures. Known-group validity is how well a PROM can differentiate between groups known to differ in a construct.39 These terms have been used in prior PROMIS-PF validation studies. For example, known group validity was recently used to demonstrate the construct validity of PROMIS-PF in patients with osteoarthritis.15 In comparison, convergent validity was used to demonstrate the construct validity of PROMIS-PF in foot and ankle surgical populations.40 However, while many methods can be used to demonstrate construct validity, the literature recently favored methods that demonstrate convergent and discriminate validity.22 Together, the discriminate validity and convergent validity support both aspects of what the PROM construct is expected to measure and what it should not measure. This study shows that the PROMIS-PF instrument only has a low-moderate correlation with dissimilar constructs like emotional wellbeing and depression. Although factors of health can be related, for instance, people who have an acute change in physical function can become depressed, they are measuring different aspects of the patient’s health status and should have a lower correlation than instruments measuring similar constructs.

Overall, we found that the convergent validity for PROMIS-PF with all similar measures for both time points had an average correlation of greater than or equal to 0.70, while the average discriminate validity was less than or equal to 0.41 using the multitrait-multimethod matrix. This is preferred over a single method and provides more support to the study’s findings.41 While there is no universally accepted cutoff for heterotrait-heteromethod correlations from this method to establish discriminant validity, a common heuristic is that these correlations should be significantly lower than the monotrait-heteromethod correlations used to establish convergent validity. The authors feel that the difference between 0.70 and 0.41 is great enough to satisfy this heuristic criterion.

PROMIS-PF demonstrated several advantages over the legacy instruments in this study for clinical application for patients with PAD. VascuQoL and WIQ are disease-specific instruments validated in patients with PAD.42,43 However, they often ask patients to attribute their function to one disease process because they are disease specific. The WIQ is an objective measure of walking distance and speed and has shown no statistical difference in patients with less than or greater than two comorbid conditions.30 The PROMIS-PF instrument had a high correlation with the WIQ instrument in this study (rho ≥ 0.73). Additionally, PROMIS-PF had a high correlation (rho ≥ 0.78) with the VascuQoL PAD instrument, used in the multicenter randomized PAD-specific Bypass versus Angioplasty in Severe Ischemia of the Leg clinical trial,6 demonstrating its ability to measure PAD functional deficits appropriately. The PROMIS-PF has already demonstrated its applicability and sensitivity as an outcome to measure changes in PAD-related physical function over time and how it can be used as a preoperative predictor for surgical success.20,44 The VascuQoL and WIQ, being disease-specific instruments, may be cumbersome and costly to implement compared to a generic instrument like PROMIS-PF that could be applied to multiple patient populations and disease processes.

The SF-36 is a generic instrument for broad use in various medical conditions. One of its disadvantages, however, is that because it is based on classical test theory and consists of 36 questions, completing the questionnaire can be time-consuming and inconvenient in a clinical setting. This is in line with feedback we received from study participants, which indicated excessive survey burden was a common reason for dropping out. The PROMIS-PF and SF-36 instruments have previously demonstrated a high correlation in previous studies.18,19 In this study, the PROMIS-PF instrument showed moderate and high correlation with the SF-36 functional component (rho = 0.66, 0.92 at time points 1 and 2, respectively). However, the shorter survey length and use of item response theory and CAT make PROMIS-PF the preferred generic instrument for implementation.40 Because PROMIS-PF is a generic instrument validated in many patient disease processes and efficiently reduces responder survey burden, it is an appealing option to implement in future clinical trials and across vascular divisions.

Our study demonstrated the reliability and construct validity of PROMIS-PF using the preferred methods of the recent literature: intraclass correlation for reliability and both convergent and discriminate validity for construct validity. While our study has a strong foundation in our methods, it also has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, with 21 participants. Although this exceeded the estimated sample size needed in our power calculations, it is less than ideal. The authors suspect two main reasons for this small sample size. First, the prevalence of PAD in Utah and the surrounding mountain west states is lower than the national average (2.3% versus 7%).45 It is unclear whether this prevalence is due to underdiagnosis or a true decreased disease burden in the population. Either way, patients in our catchment area are less likely to present to our vascular clinic for treatment. Second, we had a significant number of patients lost to follow-between time points. Feedback from patients indicated that the survey burden was too high or that they were not comfortable with the emailed surveys to be completed at home, which differed from electronic surveys administered in clinic where staffs were available to assist. Also notable is that our participants were predominantly White and non-Hispanic. The limited sample size and cohort demographics may mean our results are not generalizable to all populations. Finally, our study did not assess the responsiveness of PROMIS-PF. Responsiveness is the instrument’s ability to capture change over time and is an attribute that often complements the validity and reliability of PROM instruments.22

Our study provides strong evidence for the reliability and construct validity of PROMIS-PF in patients with PAD. This indicates that it is an appropriate instrument for evaluating patients with PAD and decreasing the number of instruments/questionnaires needed, which would reduce patient dropout and data missingness and increase longitudinal follow-up. Future studies of the responsiveness of PROMIS-PF can further support its use in this population. This study contributes to a body of literature that demonstrates the validity of PROMIS-PF in many patient populations and supports its use in vascular clinics and future clinical trials.
