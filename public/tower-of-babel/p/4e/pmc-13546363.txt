A case report of CD4− CD8+ CD56− TCRγδ+ T-large granular lymphocytic leukaemia with severe aplastic anemia and a PNH clone
Authors: Yufei Du, Jinling Zhang, Shengyu Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
T-large granular lymphocytic leukemia (T-LGLL) is a rare, indolent lymphoproliferative disorder of lymphocytes. Clonal expansion of T cells, driven by dysregulation of Fas-mediated apoptosis following activation, is a hallmark of this disease. In recent years, research on large granular T-cell leukemia has grown substantially compared with the past; however, most reports have focused on TCRαβ+ cases, while studies on other subtypes remain limited, and diagnostic experience with coexisting conditions requires further refinement. This study reports, for the first time, a case of CD4− CD8+ CD56− TCRγδ+ T-LGLL complicated by severe aplastic anemia (AA) and a paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) clone. The case is characterized by positive TCRγδ gene rearrangement, with T-cell–mediated hematopoietic suppression leading to the development of AA and the PNH clone. We hope this study will address a gap in the current literature. Here, we summarize the common pathophysiological mechanisms and guiding treatment principles for these three diseases.

Introduction

T-large granular lymphocytic leukemia (T-LGLL) is a rare lymphoproliferative disorder characterized by clonal expansion.Most patients exhibit a classic lymphocyte immunophenotype: CD3+ CD4− CD8+ CD16+ CD57+ TCRαβ+ [1]. Other subtypes are comparatively rare.T-LGLL most commonly presents with pure red cell aplasia, cases presenting with aplastic anemia are rare. We report a case of CD4− CD8+ CD56− TCRγδ+ T-LGLL with severe aplastic anemia (AA) and a paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) clone that was recently admitted to our department. We also review the literature to enhance understanding of this disease.

Case report

A 50-year-old woman was admitted to the Department of Hematology at our hospital for further evaluation due to fatigue accompanied by generalized mucosal bleeding. The patient had been diagnosed with paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria seven years prior. During the course of the illness, she experienced fatigue and dizziness but had no fever, cough, sputum production, night sweats, or weight loss. Discussion

Large granular lymphocytes (LGLs) are a subset of lymphocytes with distinctive morphological features, accounting for 10% to 15% of normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells [2]. Large granular lymphocytic leukemia (LGLL) is a rare chronic lymphoproliferative disorder characterized by the presence of coarse, aniline-blue-staining granules in the cytoplasm of clonal lymphocytes. These granules originate from CD3+ cytotoxic T cells and CD3− natural killer (NK) cells and are classified as a mature lymphocytic neoplasm. Based on phenotype, LGLL can be classified into two types: T-cell LGLL (T-LGLL) and natural killer (NK) cell LGLL (NK-LGLL), accounting for 85% and 15% of cases, respectively [3]. Based on TCR classification, T cells can be divided into αβ-T cells and γδ-T cells. The vast majority of T-LGLL cases exhibit the classic phenotypic characteristics of CD3+CD4−CD8+CD16+CD57+ and TCRαβ+. γδ+T-LGLL is a rare mature T-cell proliferative disorder, accounting for approximately 5% of CD3+ T-LGLL cases. γδ+T-LGLL cells are typically CD3+ CD4− CD8+ CD16+ CD57+, CD56 may be detected in some cases, with the CD4−CD8− double-negative phenotype being significantly more common than in αβ-T-LGLL patients [4]. In contrast, the patient in this case presented with the rarer immunophenotype CD4−CD8+CD56−TCRγδ+. The clinical presentation of γδ T-LGLL is predominantly characterized by cytopenia, with a small number of cases presenting primarily with splenomegaly or autoimmune disease. Diagnosis

The diagnosis requires the fulfillment of at least two essential criteria and one desirable criterion. Essential criteria include an elevated count of cytotoxic T cells in peripheral blood (typically greater than 2 × 10⁹/L, but may be lower); abnormal T-cell immunophenotype (manifested by downregulation of CD5 and/or CD7 and expression of CD16 and NK cell-associated receptors); and T-cell monoclonality. Desirable criteria include a sinusoidal infiltration pattern of cytotoxic lymphocytes in the bone marrow and the presence of STAT3 or STAT5B gene mutations [5]. Confirmation of clonality is a critical step in the diagnosis of T-LGL. Significant progress has been made by incorporating the novel TRBC1 antibody into flow cytometry T-cell assay panels, yielding results superior to the time-consuming and labor-intensive TCR Vβ library analysis method [6, 7]. The detection of TCRβ or TCRγ gene rearrangements can distinguish clonal LGL leukemia from reactive LGL proliferation, particularly in patients with persistently increasing LGL counts [2]. Additionally, patients presenting with cytopenia, splenomegaly, rheumatoid arthritis, and B-symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss) should be evaluated for large granular lymphocyte leukemia [8]. Before confirming a diagnosis of T-cell LGL leukemia, reactive LGL lymphocytosis and persistent polyclonal LGL lymphocytosis caused by viral infections (cytomegalovirus, EBV, HIV, etc.), post-splenectomy, aging, and hematopoietic cell transplantation must be considered [1, 9]. The diagnosis of hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (typically CD4−CD8−CD56+) must also be ruled out [10]. Most patients with T-cell LGL have a normal karyotype, while fewer than 10% exhibit certain karyotypic abnormalities that may include trisomy of chromosomes 3, 8, and 14; deletions of chromosomes 6 and 5q; and inversions of 12p and 14q [11].

Pathogenesis

It is currently believed that the pathogenesis of LGLL involves an initial expansion triggered by chronic antigenic stimulation, followed by clonal proliferation of large granular lymphocytes (LGLs) under the sustained influence of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the inflammatory microenvironment, various signaling pathways [12], epigenetic alterations, resistance to apoptosis, and abnormalities in the immune microenvironment [10]. The pathogenesis of aplastic anemia includes defects in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), an abnormal bone marrow microenvironment, immune dysregulation, and genetic factors. Among these, defects in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) result from elevated levels of apoptosis in bone marrow hematopoietic cells; this apoptosis is induced by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) expressing FasL recognizing Fas-expressing antigens [13]. PNH is a clonal, non-malignant hematological disorder caused by somatic mutations in the X-linked phosphatidylinositol glycan class A (PIG-A) gene in hematopoietic stem cells. Somatic mutations in the PIG-A gene result in the absence of a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors, preventing GPI-anchored proteins from properly localizing to the cell membrane and performing their functions. Among known GPI-anchored proteins, the absence of the complement regulatory proteins CD59 and CD55 leads to uncontrolled activation of the complement cascade, thereby increasing susceptibility to complement-mediated cell lysis. Plasma levels of TNF-α, TGF-β, and IFN-γ are elevated in PNH patients compared with healthy individuals. The frequency of IFN-γ–producing lymphocytes is negatively correlated with circulating white blood cell and platelet counts, suggesting that IFN-γ exerts a hematopoietic-suppressive effect in PNH [14].

The core pathogenesis of LGLL lies in the fact that, following an initial expansion (presumably triggered by chronic antigenic stimulation), the persistent presence of monoclonal large granular lymphocytes (LGLs) results from the combined effects of resistance to Fas-induced apoptosis, a pro-inflammatory cytokine environment, activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and epigenetic alterations [10]. Aplastic anemia (AA) shares the same pathogenesis as LGLL, with abnormal T-cell activation in both conditions; immunosuppressive therapy is effective for both. This is consistent with the patient in this case presenting with aplastic anemia concurrent with a diagnosis of T-LGLL. The vast majority of PNH cases arise from the selective expansion of PIGA-mutant stem cells that evade the immune system against the backdrop of T-cell-mediated autoimmune damage to the bone marrow; AA is the key pathological basis for the development of PNH, and both conditions belong to the spectrum of autoimmune bone marrow failure disorders. In AA patients, following T-cell-mediated destruction of the bone marrow, a large number of normal GPI+ HSCs undergo damage and apoptosis; GPI− mutant stem cells, lacking the GPI anchor structure, evade T-cell killing and gain a survival advantage, leading to their massive expansion through environmental selection and ultimately progressing to PNH [15]. Furthermore, similar to AA, oligoclonal expansion of CD8+ T cells is observed in PNH patients [14]. This is consistent with the present patient, diagnosed with aplastic anemia 7 years after the initial diagnosis of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH). TCR sequencing has demonstrated that T-cell clonal expansion is present in both AA and PNH patients and is a primary factor contributing to bone marrow failure [15].T-cell–mediated hematopoietic suppression contributes to the pathogenesis of both aplastic anemia (AA) and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH). Patients with AA and PNH typically respond to immunosuppressive therapy, suggesting that T cells exert a direct inhibitory effect on hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs).

The co-occurrence of T-large granular lymphocyte leukemia (T-LGLL), aplastic anemia (AA), and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is not merely coincidental. Rather, these conditions share similar pathophysiological mechanisms, including immune dysregulation, dual injury to hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), and clonal expansion. Against a background of chronic antigenic stimulation and genetic susceptibility, T-cell receptors undergo clonal rearrangement. Gain-of-function mutations in STAT3, STAT5B, and other genes drive sustained activation of the JAK-STAT pathway, confer resistance to apoptosis and promote abnormal proliferation in clonal large granular lymphocytes, thereby generating T-LGLL clones [16].This abnormal clone continuously secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-15, which—together with activated CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in AA patients—directly kill HSPCs via Fas/FasL, perforin–granzyme, and the JAK-STAT pathway, while also inhibiting the self-renewal and differentiation of normal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. This results in bone marrow failure and constitutes the immune-mediated basis of AA [17]. Within this immunocytotoxic microenvironment, some HSPCs acquire X-linked somatic mutations in PIG-A, impairing glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor synthesis and causing loss of surface complement regulatory proteins such as CD55 and CD59 [18, 19]. These PNH clones evade T cell and NK cell–mediated immunity because they lack GPI-anchored target antigens, gaining a survival advantage and gradually proliferating, ultimately triggering complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis. Furthermore, telomere shortening caused by mutations in telomerase genes (TERT/TERC) and genomic instability arising from epigenetic alterations in TET2 and DNMT3A further exacerbate HSPC functional exhaustion and clonal evolution. At the same time, dysregulated cytokine networks and dysfunction of bone marrow stroma create a suppressive hematopoietic microenvironment, driving a vicious cycle of T-LGLL–mediated immune attack, AA-induced hematopoietic failure, and PNH clonal expansion. Clinical manifestations

In this case, the patient was relatively young at illness onset, with initial clinical manifestations primarily characterized by fatigue and generalized mucosal bleeding. In the later stages, the patient remained immunosuppressed due to neutropenia, and repeated blood transfusions led to iron overload, ultimately causing a pulmonary infection by Microsporum gypseum [20]. Clinically, neutropenia-associated infections predominate; they carry the highest mortality risk, accounting for about 15–39% of cases, and can involve the skin, oropharynx, perirectal region, and lungs. Some patients develop persistent neutropenia without infection. Splenomegaly occurs in 20–50% of cases, while hepatomegaly and lymphadenopathy are relatively rare.Fatigue and B symptoms are observed in 20–30% of cases. Tissue infiltration of the skin, nervous system, or kidneys has been reported occasionally [10].

Anemia is the most common cytopenia in Asia, primarily caused by pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), affecting 29–42% of LGLL patients. Cases of aplastic anemia, hemolytic anemia, and immune thrombocytopenia have been reported on rare occasions [21]. Go et al. reported nine cases of T-cell LGLL presenting as AA [22], and Wang Lan et al. reported a case of a rare phenotype of CD4−CD8−CD56+ γδT-LGLL presenting as AA [23].

LGLL lies at the intersection of hematology and immunology, with autoimmune diseases reported in approximately 25–32% of cases [24]. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is the primary autoimmune disease associated with LGLL, affecting 10–15% of patients and typically diagnosed before or concurrently with LGLL. Sjögren’s syndrome, autoimmune endocrine disorders, vasculitis, and autoimmune cytopenias have also been reported [25].

Treatment

Large granular lymphocytic leukemia (LGLL) is a lymphoproliferative disorder characterized by an indolent course and currently has no curative treatment. The largest retrospective study to date specifically focusing on patients with γδ+T-LGLL shows that the 10-year overall survival (OS) rate for this disease is > 70%. At diagnosis, approximately half of patients may initially be managed with watchful waiting and follow-up, but the majority of these patients will subsequently require long-term, continuous drug therapy as the disease progresses [4]. Severe neutropenia (neutrophil count < 0.5 × 10⁹/L), neutropenia-related infections, transfusion-dependent anemia, and symptomatic anemia are the primary indications for treatment. Even in the absence of symptomatic cytopenia, the presence of an autoimmune disorder may indicate the need for treatment [6]. Monotherapy with cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and cyclosporine is recommended as a first-line treatment. For patients with LGLL complicated by aplastic anemia, cyclosporine A is typically considered.

Efficacy assessment began four months after the start of treatment. Hematologic complete remission (CR) was defined as ANC > 1.5 × 10⁹/L, absolute lymphocyte count < 4.0 × 10⁹/L, HGB > 110 g/L, PLT > 1.5 × 10⁹/L, and normalization of the absolute peripheral blood LGL count (< 2.5 × 10⁹/L). Partial hematologic remission (PR) was defined as ANC > 0.5 × 10⁹/L, HGB > 80 g/L, PLT > 50 × 10⁹/L, and independence from blood transfusions. Treatment failure was defined as the failure to achieve PR or CR within 4 months [26]. In this case, the patient received oral cyclosporine for one month; however, due to the extremely short interval between diagnosis and death, it was not possible to objectively assess the treatment efficacy.

Conclusion

T-large granular lymphocytic leukaemia is a chronic lymphoproliferative disorder most commonly seen in the elderly. Its onset is often insidious; most patients have no obvious symptoms or signs and are discovered during routine physical examinations, while some seek medical attention for symptoms related to anemia or infection. Platelet counts are often normal or only mildly reduced. Typically, bone marrow biopsies in patients with LGLL show bone marrow cell counts that meet or exceed age-appropriate norms, with no significant morphological abnormalities in the three hematopoietic lineages [7]. An increase in clonal LGLs can be detected in peripheral blood or bone marrow. This case of CD4−CD8+CD56− TCRγδ+ LGLL complicated by severe aplastic anemia (AA) with a PNH clone represents the first reported combination of these three conditions. It enables a thorough investigation of the common pathophysiological mechanisms and the development of a treatment plan tailored to the individual’s circumstances. It also confirms that the clinical presentation of γδT-LGLL is predominantly pancytopenia with a poor prognosis. However, this report has several limitations. First, following diagnosis, the patient was referred to multiple hospitals due to poor physical condition, frequent complications, and interruptions in treatment. Second, due to financial constraints, medication use during follow-up was suboptimal.
