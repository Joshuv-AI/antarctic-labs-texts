Age determination based on external morphometric measurements in live raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides): a method considering animal welfare and research efficiency
Authors: Bong Kyun Kim, Soo Hyung Eo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate age determination is crucial for interpreting the behavior and ecology of raccoon dogs; however, age estimation is often overlooked due to the absence of practical, non-invasive methods or criteria. Existing approaches to age estimation tend to be overly invasive or challenging to implement in field settings, thereby limiting their utility.ResultsIn this study, we analyzed external morphological traits of 37 raccoon dogs, whose age was verified via radiographic analysis of growth plate closure. We characterized morphological differences between adults and juveniles and developed discriminant functions to estimate the age of individuals of undetermined age. In males, adults exhibited significantly larger body weight, body length, tail length, hind foot length, ear length, head length, and neck circumference than juveniles. In females, all measured traits, except for head length, were larger in adults than in juveniles. The discriminant functions based on external morphology successfully classified adults and juveniles with 100% accuracy for both males and females. Multiple discriminant functions combining three morphological variables were generated for each sex, with body length identified as the most important predictor for age estimation in raccoon dogs.ConclusionsTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to present sex-specific, morphometry-based discriminant functions for age estimation in raccoon dogs. With perfect classification accuracy, these functions offer a reliable tool for age estimation. The non-invasive and field-applicable nature of this method offers distinct advantages regarding animal welfare and research practicality. This approach aligns with societal demands for integrating research ethics and animal welfare by minimizing the burden on study animals, thereby enabling more precise and long-term ecological studies.Keywords: Age determination, Animal welfare, Body length, Discriminant function, External morphology

Background

Age-related behavioral and ecological differences shape population dynamics and life history strategies in wild mammals. Adults and juveniles frequently diverge in natal dispersal, social interactions, and foraging behavior [1–3], making accurate age determination essential for wildlife management and life histories studies [4, 5]. Disasters such as wildfires or abrupt environmental changes can directly affect individual survival and thereby alter population structure [6, 7], potentially resulting in age-specific impacts. Therefore, evaluating how such disturbances affect different age classes is essential. However, field discrimination between adult and juvenile individuals remains difficult owing to the subtle and gradual nature of age-related morphological changes [8].

Conventional methods for age estimation, such as analyzing growth layer groups in dentin and cementum through tooth sectioning [9, 10] or evaluating growth plate development via radiographic imaging [11–13], are invasive, potentially harmful, and impractical for field use [8]. These limitations underscore the need for non-invasive and field-feasible alternatives. However, few studies have classified adult and juvenile individuals based on external morphological traits (e.g., body weight or length). Additionally, these approaches have been limited to only a few species, including the marmots (Marmota vancouverensis; Marmota caligata), the galápagos sea lion (Zalophus wollebaeki), the brown-nosed coati (Nasua nasua), the crab-eating fox (Cerdocyon thous), the eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx), and the lesser treeshrew (Tupaia minor) [4, 8, 14–16]. To address these gaps, we focused on the raccoon dog (Nyctereutes procyonoides), a species of growing ecological and epidemiological concern.

The raccoon dog is native to East Asia, encompassing Korea, Japan, and China. Its range has rapidly expanded across central and northern Europe following its introduction into the former Soviet Union in the mid-20th century [17, 18]. Recent research has indicated that this species may be instrumental in transmitting zoonotic diseases, including rabies, and parasitic infections (e.g., liver flukes, roundworms, and sarcoptic mange), thus elevating its status as an important ecological and public health concern [17, 18]. In response, studies have increasingly focused on various aspects of the spatial ecology of this species, such as its life history, home range, dispersal, and movement patterns, to inform population management and control strategies [19–22].

Even when conducting invasive research on wild animals, it is essential to consider biological characteristics such as age and growth stage. For example, tracking devices are often attached to wild individuals to enable spatial ecological research. However, attachment methods and device loads may adversely affect animals [23–27]. For instance, a study involving tracking devices on immature, still-growing individuals revealed behavioral differences relative to untagged control animals: postural changes, reduced head support, and decreased sleep duration [26]. Furthermore, neck- or back-mounted devices can exert increasing pressure as animals grow, potentially causing physical injuries and, in severe cases, respiratory distress or circulatory disorders [27, 28]. These findings suggest that attaching tracking devices can impose considerable physical and psychological burdens on juveniles. Therefore, decisions regarding tracking device deployment and attachment must consider the biological characteristics of the animal, including age-related developmental status, sex, and overall health.

However, limited research has systematically investigated the morphological characteristics of raccoon dogs to support these biologically based decisions. Most morphological studies of raccoon dogs have focused on basic body metrics, such as body weight or length [29, 30], typically demonstrating male-biased size dimorphism [31, 32]. Although several studies have analyzed skull, mandible, or tooth size to investigate variations related to geographic distribution or sex [33–36], the morphological traits identified in these studies are only applicable when skeletal remains are available from deceased individuals, thus limiting their utility in live-animal or field-based research.

In this study, we aimed to identify sex- and age-related differences in external morphological traits among live raccoon dogs and develop discriminant functions capable of classifying individuals into juvenile or adult categories based on these traits. To accomplish this, we collected morphometric data from 37 rescued individuals, assessed age via radiographic analysis of growth plate closure, and evaluated morphological differences using statistical comparisons and multivariate modeling. Our objective was to develop a reliable, non-invasive, and field-applicable method for age estimation of individuals of unknown age. We also aimed to highlight the importance of integrating animal welfare with research efficiency in wildlife studies by providing practical and ethical tools for field-based age determination. The findings of this study are anticipated to advance our understanding of age-related morphological variation in raccoon dogs and provide a theoretical basis for the development of non-invasive, welfare-conscious tools for wildlife demographic assessment and management.

Methods

Information on raccoon dog individuals

From 2021 to 2024, we examined the external morphology of 37 raccoon dogs rescued and admitted to the Chungnam Wild Animal Rescue Center in South Korea. Among these, 26 individuals were either confined within artificial structures or infected with Sarcoptes scabiei. When injuries were present, measurements were conducted only after medical treatment was completed. The remaining 11 individuals were immature raccoon dogs rescued after losing maternal care. All raccoon dogs in this study were rescued under conditions that did not involve physical trauma and were confirmed to have no injuries that could cause structural deformation of the body. Therefore, they were deemed appropriate for external morphological assessment.

Some individuals underwent rehabilitation in artificial enclosures for up to three months prior to release. We considered the potential influence of such conditions on external morphology, particularly body weight. To account for this, all rescued individuals were evaluated for body condition using the Body Condition Score (BCS) system. Following the criteria of Laflamme [37], animals with scores ≥ 7 were classified as potentially obese, while those with scores of ≤ 3 were classified as potentially underweight. Individuals falling outside the standard range were excluded from analysis. All 37 raccoon dogs in this study scored between 4 and 6, indicating normal body condition.

Age and sex classification

Prior to measuring external morphology, raccoon dogs were classified by age and sex. This classification served as a reference for evaluating the alignment of estimates derived from external morphometrics with actual age and sex determinations. Age was assessed via radiographic examination to determine the stage of skeletal development and potential for further growth [11–13]. We examined the closure of growth plates in four limb bones: the humerus, ulna, femur, and tibia (Fig. 1). Individuals exhibiting complete closure of growth plates in all four bones were classified as adults, whereas those with partially or fully open growth plates were classified as juveniles. Among the 37 raccoon dogs examined, 26 were classified as adults and 11 as juveniles; most adults were estimated to be older than one year of age, while juveniles were typically 5–6 months old.

Radiographic evaluation of age in raccoon dogs. a, b: Lateral limb views of an adult individual; c, d: Lateral limb views of a juvenile individual; arrow: Growth plate

Sex was assessed through visual inspection of the external genitalia and nipples located on the ventral surface, specifically focusing on their position and morphology. Following standard criteria for canids, individuals exhibiting a genital opening located immediately caudal to the anus and possessing well-developed nipples were classified as females. Conversely, those exhibiting a genital opening more anteriorly on the ventral surface, possessing a penis and testes, and lacking developed nipples were classified as males [38]. Based on these criteria, 18 males and 19 females were identified among the 37 raccoon dogs.

Combining both age and sex classifications, the final distribution comprised 12 adult males, 14 adult females, 6 juvenile males, and 5 juvenile females.

External morphological measurements

We measured seven external morphological traits from raccoon dogs (Fig. 2): (1) body weight; (2) body length (from snout tip to anus); (3) tail length (from the dorsal base of the tail, immediately above the anus, to the tail tip); (4) hind foot length (from the tip of the longest toe’s tip to the posterior end of the heel); (5) ear length (from the inner base of the external ear to its outermost tip); (6) head length (from the snout tip to the most protruding point of the occipital region along the median plane); and (7) neck circumference (at the midpoint of the neck). Measurement procedures were adapted from previous studies involving canids [29, 39–42].

External morphological measurements of raccoon dogs. a: Body length and neck circumference; b: Head length and ear length; c: Hind-foot length; d: Tail length

Body weight was measured using an electronic scale (CAS SW-1 S; precision 5 g). Body length, tail length, hind foot length, and ear length were measured with a ruler (Shinwa; precision ± 1 mm). Head length was measured with a vernier caliper (Mitutoyo CD-PS; precision ± 0.01 mm), and neck circumference with a measuring tape (precision ± 1 mm). External morphometric measurements were performed on individuals under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. All measurements were conducted by a single researcher to minimize inter-observer bias [43], and the mean value of three repeated measurements was recorded for each external morphological trait.

Data analysis

Based on the classification criteria described above, individuals were assigned to four groups by sex (male or female) and age class (adult or juvenile). External morphometrics were measured for each group, and differences in trait size were assessed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on normality test results.

We performed discriminant function analysis (DFA) to assess the potential of external morphological traits as predictors of age. DFA combines multiple morphological variables to generate functions capable of accurately classifying individuals based on age or sex, thereby allowing quantitative assessment of morphological characteristics and group differences [44, 45]. Initially, we constructed a discriminant model that included all variables to evaluate predictive accuracy and statistical significance. Subsequently, we applied a stepwise backward selection procedure to identify the most efficient subset of variables for age prediction.

Neck circumference was excluded from the DFA. This variable may be affected by both growth stage and nutritional status, potentially confounding interpretation [46]. Moreover, neck circumference has not been widely recognized as a standard external morphometric trait in previous studies on canids [29, 39–41]. Consequently, neck circumference was deemed unsuitable for inclusion in the discriminant models developed for age prediction.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Shapiro–Wilk, Student’s t- test, and Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted using the ‘stats’ package (version 4.3.3; R Core Team), and DFA was performed using the ‘MASS’ package. For all analyses, a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison between adult and juvenile males

In male raccoon dogs, all external morphological traits exhibited significant differences between adults and juveniles (p < 0.05), with adults exhibiting larger values across these traits (Fig. 3).The respective mean (± SE) values for adults and juveniles were as follows: body weight 5,681.7 ± 149.6 g vs. 4,893.3 ± 627.6 g; body length 573.1 ± 10.2 mm vs. 519.2 ± 14.2 mm; tail length 266.3 ± 4.2 mm vs. 245.7 ± 4.0 mm; hind foot length 112.5 ± 0.9 mm vs. 110.3 ± 1.4 mm; ear length 51.3 ± 1.0 mm vs. 43.8 ± 2.3 mm; head length 143.3 ± 1.1 mm vs. 137.0 ± 2.1 mm; and neck circumference 249.3 ± 6.2 mm vs. 226.8 ± 5.7 mm. The greatest relative difference were observed in ear length (17.1%), followed by body weight (16.1%), followed by body length (10.4%), neck circumference (9.9%), tail length (8.4%), head length (4.6%), and hind foot length (2.0%).

Comparative analysis of morphometric variables between juvenile and adult male raccoon dogs

To predict age in male raccoon dogs, three discriminant functions with high predictive accuracy were derived (Table 1). The variables included in each function were as follows: MD1 — body weight, body length, and tail length (Wilks’ λ = 0.0645, F (3, 14) = 67.63, p < 0.05); MD2 — body weight, body length, and ear length (Wilks’ λ = 0.0789, F (3, 14) = 54.48, p < 0.05); MD3 — body weight, body length, and hind foot length (Wilks’ λ = 0.1403, F (3, 14) = 28.60, p < 0.05). All three discriminant functions achieved 100% classification accuracy in distinguishing between adult and juvenile males (Fig. 4).

Comparison between adult and juvenile females

In female raccoon dogs, external morphological traits except head length exhibited significant differences between adults and juveniles. The respective mean (± SE) values for adults and juveniles were as follows: body weight 4,828.6 ± 360.2 g vs. 4,212.0 ± 452.0 g; body length 545.9 ± 12.8 mm vs. 510.2 ± 12.3 mm; tail length 257.9 ± 3.4 mm vs. 238.6 ± 6.6 mm; hind foot length 111.2 ± 1.1 mm vs. 108.4 ± 1.5 mm; ear length 47.4 ± 2.1 mm vs. 41.4 ± 2.7 mm; head length 136.3 ± 2.2 mm vs. 134.2 ± 2.6 mm; and neck circumference 238.9 ± 3.8 mm vs. 221.6 ± 5.3 mm. Except for head length (p = 0.16), all external morphological traits measured in female raccoon dogs exhibited significant differences between adults and juveniles (p < 0.05), with adults exhibiting larger values across these traits (Fig. 5). The greatest relative differences were observed in body weight and ear length (both 14.6%), followed by tail length (8.1%), neck circumference (7.8%), body length (7.0%), hind foot length (2.6%), and head length (1.6%).

Comparative analysis of morphometric variables between juvenile and adult female raccoon dogs

To predict age in female raccoon dogs, three discriminant functions with high predictive accuracy were derived (Table 1). The variables included in each function were as follows: FD1 — tail length, ear length, and head length (Wilks’ λ = 0.0996, F (3, 15) = 45.18, p < 0.05); FD2 — body length, tail length, and head length (Wilks’ λ = 0.1016, F(3, 15) = 44.24, p < 0.05); FD3 — body length, ear length, and head length (Wilks’ λ = 0.1968, F (3, 15) = 20.41, p < 0.05). Similar to the discriminant functions for male raccoon dogs, all three functions included three variables each and achieved 100% classification accuracy in distinguishing between age classes in female raccoon dogs (Fig. 6). Notably, head length was included in all three female discriminant functions despite showing no significant difference when analyzed individually (p = 0.16), suggesting its contribution to age classification becomes apparent in multivariate models.

Discussion

In this study, we performed DFA to estimate the age of raccoon dogs of unknown age based on variations in external morphological traits. Three distinct discriminant functions were established, each consisting of combinations of three variables, for both male and female individuals. These functions achieved 100% classification accuracy in distinguishing adult from juvenile within the study population. This classification accuracy exceeds that of a previously developed discriminant function for age estimation based on cranial morphology in another canid species, the culpeo, which reported approximately 85% accuracy [40]. The discriminant functions developed in this study therefore offer a potentially reliable and practical method for estimating the age of raccoon dogs of unknown age based on external morphological measurements. However, direct comparisons of classification accuracy between external morphometric measurements and those based on cranial measurements have limitations. Moreover, because age classification generally exhibits clearer morphological separation than sex classification, evaluating methodological superiority between studies based solely on classification accuracy is not appropriate.

Among the six external morphological traits used to develop the discriminant functions, body length emerged as the most frequently selected variable. It appeared in five of the six discriminant functions across both sexes, indicating its crucial role in age estimation irrespective of sex. This finding suggests that body length is the most important and effective external morphological trait for estimating age in raccoon dogs. Additionally, tail length and ear length were each included in three discriminant functions for both sexes, confirming their utility as key variables.

Previous studies have similarly highlighted the importance of external morphological traits in age estimation. For example, Jeglinski et al. [8] developed discriminant functions to estimate age in Galápagos sea lions and identified canine length, body length, body weight, and chest circumference as key variables. Similarly, Marti and Ryser-Degiorgis [15] reported significant age-related differences in body length, body weight, and tail length in Eurasian lynx. Together with previous research, our findings indicate that body length may represent a critical variable for assessing age-related differences and for developing discriminant functions to estimate age in mammals. This highlights the need for systematic and consistent collection of body length and associated external measurement data. Moreover, given that the morphological traits serving as key predictors may differ between species [4, 14], identifying which specific external characteristics should be prioritized when developing discriminant functions for age estimation is important. Consequently, species-specific evaluation of trait importance is essential to ensure accurate and reliable age classification across different taxa.

Body size parameters, including body length, are widely recognized in ecological and physiological research as key indicators reflecting the age, evolutionary adaptations, and environmental responses of animals. For instance, studies have documented that body size tends to decrease in populations with increased densities [40]. Additionally, environmental factors, such as foraging strategies and physiological demands, contribute to body size variations [47, 48]. Body length therefore functions as an important proxy for age estimation in mammals and serves as an informative metric for understanding the ecology, physiology, and adaptive evolution of species.

However, some studies have reported limitations in using external morphology for age estimation. For example, Woodman et al. [16] identified considerable overlap in body length, tail length, hind foot length, and ear length across age classes in the small-eared shrew, concluding that external morphology was inadequate for accurate age estimation in that species. These findings suggest that the suitability of age estimation based on external morphology may differ across species. Evaluating the suitability and effectiveness of these methods for the target species prior to implementation is therefore essential.

Collar-type tracking devices, which encircle the neck, are widely preferred for studying mammals due to their stability, low risk of detachment, and minimal physiological burden. The use of these devices is internationally standardized in medium- to large-mammal studies [26, 49]. However, attaching collars constructed from fabric or leather around the neck may pose risks including respiratory distress or impaired blood circulation [28]. Measuring neck circumference prior to collar attachment is therefore critical for determining appropriate collar size and fastening methods. In this study, neck circumference was measured, but was ultimately exclude from the discriminant functions for several reasons. First, neck circumference varies with growth stage and nutritional status [46], potentially confounding age-related patterns. Second, it has not been considered a primary morphological variable in previous studies that established measurement standards for canids [29, 39–42]. Consequently, there is insufficient scientific evidence to justify the inclusion of neck circumference as a variable in age estimation discriminant functions. Nevertheless, given the critical importance of minimizing the impact of tracking devices on animal movement and physiological functions [26, 49–51], measuring neck circumference remains essential when evaluating external morphology for fitting tracking collars.

Most previous studies analyzing raccoon dog morphology have used data collected from deceased specimens [29–32]. Postmortem physiological changes, including rigor mortis, muscle and ligament contraction, and moisture loss, can lead to notable morphological differences compared to live individuals [52]. Directly applying external morphological data from carcasses to studies on live individuals may introduce errors and requires caution. In this context, our study, which collected external morphological data from live raccoon dogs, holds considerable significance. Nonetheless, because our study subjects were individuals undergoing rehabilitation, they may not be entirely representative of wild populations [53]. To mitigate differences from wild populations, body condition was assessed using radiographic examination and body condition was assessed using radiographic examination and body condition scores, with individuals showing signs of obesity or malnutrition excluded from the analyses. Future research should focus on free-ranging wild individuals and include comparative analyses between wild and rehabilitated populations to yield valuable insights into potential morphological differences between these groups.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our sample size relatively small and obtained from a restricted geographic area. All individuals originated from the Chungma region of South Korea, representing a geographically restricted and potentially homogenous populations. Morphological variation across different geographic populations of subspecies of racoon dogs may exist due to environmental adaptations, genetic differences or geographic clines. With a larger and more geographically diverse sample, some external morphological measurements may show overlap among age classes. In carnivorous mammals, rapid growth during early life stages can facilitate clearer discrimination between pups and newly weaned juveniles, whereas overlap among age classes tends to increase at later stages as growth rates decline [54]. Consequently, the high classification accuracy observed in this study may partially reflect age-specific growth patterns and population homogeneity rather than universally distinct morphological boundaries. Second, accurately evaluating the influence of such growth dynamics on age discrimination requires individuals of reliably known age, which were limited in our study. Third, even within the same species, body size can vary among regional populations [35], suggesting that our results may not be directly generalizable to raccoon dogs from all regions.

Future research should therefore focus on several key areas. Future studies should incorporate larger sample sizes and individuals of various age classes with confirmed aged information to more rigorously assess the reliability, limitations, and generalizability of external morphology-based age estimation methods. Most critically, validation of the discriminant functions using independent samples from different geographic regions is essential to assess their robustness and broader applicability. Future research should also include reliably aged individuals from multiple regional populations and develop population-specific discriminant functions based on the approach proposed here to improve the robustness and broader applicability of age estimation based on external morphology. Until such validation is conducted, we recommend that these discriminant functions be applied primarily to raccoon dogs from similar geographic regions or populations, with appropriate caution when extrapolating to other areas.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to propose sex-specific discriminant functions based on external morphometric traits for age estimation in raccoon dogs. The high classification accuracy achieved within our study population suggests that external morphology can serve as a potentially reliable basis for distinguishing adults from juveniles, pending validation across different populations. Importantly, because this approach relies on non-invasive measurements that are readily applicable in the field, it addresses key limitations of age estimation methods based on invasive procedures or postmortem data. From a broader perspective, the proposed method reduces physical burden on study animals while improving research efficiency, highlighting the feasibility of integrating animal welfare considerations into wildlife research.

Furthermore, our findings underscore the importance of carefully considering the potential burdens and adverse effects imposed on study animals, regardless of the intrinsic value or significance of wildlife research. This perspective aligns with growing societal expectations regarding research ethics and animal welfare. Minimizing stress and impacts on wild animals has been emphasized as a critical factor for achieving more accurate and sustainable research outcomes [26, 51]. In this context, our results support ongoing discussions on the development and application of research methodologies that balance scientific objectives with ethical and welfare considerations.

Conclusions

In this study, we quantitatively analyzed the external morphology of raccoon dogs and demonstrated that discriminant functions based on these traits can distinguish adults from juveniles with high accuracy within our study population. The proposed method is non-invasive and readily applicable in the field, offering a practical tool for age estimation in live animals. By minimizing physical burden on study animals while improving research efficiency, this approach supports the integration of animal welfare considerations into wildlife research and has the potential to facilitate more accurate and sustainable ecological studies. However, validation using independent samples from different geographic regions is recommended before broader application. This method may be particularly useful for wildlife management and conservation practices, such as population monitoring, field surveys, and assessing suitability for attaching tracking devices, in situations where rapid and non-invasive age assessment is required.

Significance statement

Despite the importance of age-related demographic data in wild mammals for understanding their ecology and management, practical and non-invasive methods for estimating age remain underdeveloped, particularly for medium-sized carnivores, such as the raccoon dog. Existing approaches often rely on invasive techniques or postmortem specimens, limiting their feasibility in field research and wildlife-monitoring programs. Furthermore, few studies have systematically characterized age-related morphological variation in live raccoon dog individuals, especially in the context of tracking device deployment and animal welfare. In this study, we investigated age- and sex-based variation in external morphology and developed discriminant functions that can classify raccoon dogs as juveniles or adults using body traits alone. The derived age estimation models achieved 100% classification accuracy within our study sample while using non-invasive measurements that are readily obtainable under field conditions. Our findings provide actionable tools for wildlife managers, researchers, and conservationists seeking to integrate ethical practices into ecological monitoring, though validation across different populations is recommended. This study contributes to the fields of wildlife biology, carnivore ecology, and animal welfare by offering scalable methods for field-based demographic inference.
