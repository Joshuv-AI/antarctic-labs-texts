Duodenal microbiome profiles exhibit sex-specific differences
Authors: Mohamad Rashid, Gabriela Leite, Gillian M. Barlow, Ava Hosseini, Daniel Brimberry, Dilara Flor, Gonzalo Parodi, Maritza Sanchez, Ignacio Rivera, Angel Trujillo, Maria Jesus Villanueva-Millan, Walter Morales, Stacy Weitsman, Mark Pimentel, Ruchi Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrior research using stool samples has shown that gut microbiome composition differs between males and females. As the small bowel microbiome plays key roles in host metabolic and immune health, we compared the duodenal lumenal microbiome in male and female subjects. The core duodenal microbiome differed significantly between sexes: genus Granulicatella was more prevalent in females, whereas Leptotrichia formed part of the core in males only. Microbial network analysis revealed differences in the number, composition, and interconnectivity of duodenal microbial communities in females vs. males, with tighter network interconnectivity in males, and higher network eccentricity in females that may reflect greater susceptibility to environmental fluctuations. A unique microbial community in females including Streptococcus, Granulicatella, Escherichia/Shigella, and Rothia was associated with differences in several predicted microbial metabolic pathways, including decreased bile acid deconjugation and fructan biosynthesis in females. Understanding these differences may help elucidate sex-specific differences in human health and disease.

Introduction

The gut microbiome, the complex ecosystem of microorganisms in the gastrointestinal tract, has emerged as a key determinant of human health and disease. Extensive research has shown that the gut microbiome plays multifaceted roles in digestion, metabolism, physiology, and immune function,1,2,3 and disturbances in both the composition and the diversity of commensal microbiota have been identified in numerous chronic health conditions, including inflammatory diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD),4 autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis,5 and metabolic disorders such as obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D).6 Consequently, targeting the microbiome using probiotics, prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) has been proposed for the treatment of a variety of conditions.7

Previous studies revealed sex-specific variations in the gut microbiome, referred to as the “microgenderome,”8,9 which have been shown to affect immune responses8,9,10,11 and susceptibility to disease.12,13 In mouse models, Org et al. found that the genera Allobaculum, Anaeroplasma, and Erwinia were more abundant in male mice when compared to females, but SMB53, Dorea, Coprococcus, and Ruminococcus were more abundant in females.14 Interestingly, when the authors compared results across 15 different mouse strains (all at one facility), they found a strong degree of consistency across mouse strains, but strain-specific sex-based differences were also observed,14 and results from studies performed at different facilities are variable.15 Our group previously found sex-specific variations in the rat gut microbiome, including lower relative abundances (RAs) of the genera Akkermansia (phylum Verrucomicrobia), Lactococcus (phylum Firmicutes), and Coprococcus (phylum Firmicutes) in males vs. females.16 In addition, we also found that both the response of the gut microbiome to broad-spectrum antibiotics, and the recovery of the microbiome following antibiotics cessation, differed significantly in male vs. female rats.16 Further, many diseases and conditions have different prevalences in women and men—autoimmune disorders are far more prevalent in women than in men,17 as is Alzheimer’s disease,18 whereas neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are more prevalent in men,19 as is Parkinson’s disease.20 While sex hormones, X chromosome inactivation and epigenetics are all likely contributing factors, some of these conditions have also been linked to gut microbiome alterations, including Parkinson’s20 and recently autoimmune hypothyroidism.21 Exploring the potential influence of sex-based differences in the gut microbiome in these diseases may affect therapeutic outcomes, and may also be a key component in the development of personalized therapies and interventions targeting the gut microbiome to enhance human health.

In any exploration of the gut microbiome, it is important to recognize the importance of looking beyond stool sampling. Stool is a useful surrogate for the colonic microbiome, but as conditions such as acidity, transit time, and microbial biomass differ significantly in the small and large intestines,22 the small bowel microbiome is significantly different from that of stool.23 Moreover, the small bowel plays key roles in metabolism and immune responses, and in maintaining gut barrier integrity.24,25,26 The REIMAGINE (Revealing the Entire Intestinal Microbiota and its Associations with the Genetic, Immunologic, and Neuroendocrine Ecosystem) study is an ongoing investigation aimed at understanding the role of the small intestinal microbiome in human health and disease, utilizing samples obtained during standard-of-care upper endoscopy, for which collection and sampling methods have been published and validated.23,27 In this study, samples from the REIMAGINE cohort were used to examine and compare the small intestinal (duodenal) lumenal microbiome in male and female subjects.

Results

Study subjects

REIMAGINE study subjects with available small bowel microbiome data were selected for analysis, matching for age (±3 years) and body mass index (BMI, ±3 kg/m2) to minimize variability. To reduce potential confounding effects on the microbiome, participants with recent antibiotic use (3 months), diabetes, metformin treatment, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), or a history of bariatric surgery were excluded. The resulting final cohort comprised a total of 266 subjects, including 133 females (mean age: 55.5 ± 14.9 years; mean BMI: 24.8 ± 5.0 kg/m2) and 133 males (mean age: 55.6 ± 14.7 years; mean BMI: 25.1 ± 4.5 kg/m2) (Table S1). The designation of subjects as male or female was based on self-report of sex at birth on the REIMAGINE study questionnaire. Reasons for endoscopy are shown in Table S2.

Metabolic parameters

Fasting serum levels of metabolic parameters, obtained on the day of the endoscopy procedure, were also compared between male and female subjects. Fasting glucose (GLU) levels were lower in females (94.16 ± 11.43 mg/dL) than in males (98.62 ± 16.48 mg/dL, p = 0.013), although mean values in both groups were within the normal range. Total protein (TP) levels were similar in females (6.67 ± 0.64 g/dL) compared to males (6.64 ± 0.63 g/dL, p = 0.668). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels were also similar in females (77.66 ± 35.1 U/L) compared to males (75.88 ± 52.52 U/L, p = 0.104). Alanine transaminase (ALT) levels were lower in females (17.95 ± 11.75 U/L) than in males (21.83 ± 14.45 U/L, p = 0.007), but the values in both groups were within the normal range. Lastly, aspartate transaminase (AST) levels were similar in females (28.62 ± 17.65 U/L) and in males (30.75 ± 23.08 U/L, p = 0.65) (Table S1).

Duodenal lumenal microbial diversity and microbiome analysis

Duodenal lumenal microbial alpha diversity was not significantly different between females and males (Mann-Whitney p value = 0.2, Figure S1A), which is consistent with the results of previous stool studies.28 However, beta diversity was significantly different between the two groups, indicating distinct duodenal lumenal microbial community compositions in females and males (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance [PERMANOVA] p value = 0.02, Figure S1B).

Taxonomic differences between the female and male duodenal microbiomes

There were no significant differences in duodenal lumenal microbiome composition between females and males at the phylum level (Table S3A). At the family level, 5 of the 10 families that were most significantly different between females and males had higher relative abundance (RA) in females, including Pseudomonadaceae (log2FC: 3.17, false discovery rate [FDR] p value = 3.84 × 10−6), Lactobacillaceae (log2FC: 1.50, FDR p value = 0.02), Beijerinckiaceae (log2FC: 1.44, FDR p value = 0.0017), Xanthobacteraceae (log2FC: 1.17, FDR p value = 0.0467), and Bacillaceae (log2FC: 1.09, FDR p value = 0.0378) (Table S3B). The remaining 5 had lower RA in females vs. males, including Flavobacteriaceae (log2FC: −1.88, FDR p value = 0.0012), Porphyromonadaceae (log2FC: −1.83, FDR p value = 0.0158), Rhizobiaceae (log2FC: −1.35, FDR p value = 0.0096), Mycoplasmataceae (log2FC: −1.18, FDR p value = 0.0107), and Synergistaceae (log2FC: −0.98, FDR p value = 0.0161) (Table S3B).

Differences between the female and male core duodenal lumenal microbiomes

Analysis of the duodenal lumenal core microbiome (defined as taxa present in 51% of all subjects analyzed29,30) identified significant differences between female and male subjects. For example, the genus Granulicatella was the 4th most prevalent in the duodenal microbiome of females but was less prevalent (8th most prevalent) in males, whereas the genus Leptotrichia was found in the core duodenal microbiome of males only, and not in the core duodenal microbiome of females (Figure 1). Duodenal lumenal microbial network analysis

Given the differences identified for core duodenal lumenal microbial populations, we explored potential differences between microbial networks in females and males. Microbial network analysis is a means of characterizing interactions between microbes that help drive function and stability of a given ecosystem.33,34 A network is comprised of nodes, which in microbial networks can represent genera, species or even strains, that are linked by edges, connections which represent interactions between nodes, such as signaling or metabolic exchange.33,35 Nodes are typically represented by circles, with larger circles denoting nodes with greater authority within a community. The authority of a node is derived both from the number of connections to that node and by the strength of those connections. In this study, microbial network analysis revealed significant differences in the number of communities, and in network interconnectivity, in the duodenal lumenal microbiomes in female subjects and male subjects. Five distinct microbial communities were identified in the duodenal microbial network in females, and six were identified in males. The most predominant communities can be seen in Figure 2 and are distinguished by color, with nodes representing individual genera. Each leader of a microbial community (i.e., the genus with the greatest authority within that community) in the duodenal microbial networks of female (Figure 2A) and male (Figure 2B) subjects was identified. In males, genus Catonella was the leader in one of the most important communities (purple community, Figure 2B), followed by Fusobacterium, Prevotella, and Neisseria. Leptotrichia was the leader in another predominant community in males (blue community, Figure 2B), followed by several other microbes, and genus Peptostreptococcus was the leader in a third community (green community Figure 2B). In females, genus Prevotella was the leader in the purple community (Figure 2A), followed by Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, and Campylobacter. Interestingly, the genus Catonella, which was the leader in the purple community in males and was also linked to Fusobacterium and Prevotella in that community, was not part of the purple community in females (Figure 2A). Peptostreptococcus was also the leader in the green microbial community in females, but Leptotrichia (the leader of the blue community in males) appeared to be a secondary authority in this community in females, followed by genus Lactobacillus (Figure 2A), which had no authority in the male network (Figure 2B). Of note, the duodenal microbial network in females included a microbial community primarily shaped by the genera Streptococcus, Granulicatella, Escherichia/Shigella, and Rothia (orange community, Figure 2A). Influences of differences in duodenal microbial networks on predicted metabolic functions

To evaluate whether the differences identified in predicted duodenal lumenal microbial metabolic functions between females and males (Table 2) could be explained by the differences in the microbial communities formed (Figure 2), reads of individual modules (microbial communities) were pulled down and analyzed separately to resolve the metabolic potential of specific microbial communities. Interestingly, the microbial community formed by the genera Streptococcus, Granulicatella, Escherichia/Shigella, and Rothia in females (orange community, Figure 2A), accounted for at least 5 of the different microbial metabolic pathways altered in females vs. males, including bile acid deconjugation and fructan biosynthesis (shown in bold font in Table 2). Deconjugation of bile acids is the process where bacterial enzymes, specifically bile salt hydrolases (BSH), remove the amino acid conjugates (glycine or taurine) from primary bile acids in the gut. This process transforms primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, and women may have higher levels of specific conjugated bile acids, particularly certain glycine and taurine conjugates, compared to men.38 Fructans are complex carbohydrates that are part of the FODMAP (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) group, which can cause digestive distress, including in conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS),39 which is more prevalent in women.40

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the composition of the duodenal lumenal microbiome differs significantly between females and males, with distinct microbial compositions and network structures between the sexes. While alpha diversity was comparable in females and males, beta diversity analysis indicated significant differences in duodenal lumenal microbial community composition. No significant differences were identified at the phylum level, but sex-specific differences in the RAs of multiple taxa were identified at the family and genus levels, with genera such as Faecalibacterium and Romboutsia being more prevalent in the female duodenal microbiome, whereas Sarcina and Lactococcus were more abundant in males. Core microbiome analysis also demonstrated sex-specific differences, with Granulicatella ranking among the most prevalent duodenal genera in females, while Leptotrichia only formed part of the core duodenal lumenal microbiome in males. Lastly, microbial network analysis revealed that duodenal lumenal microbial network structures also differed between females and males, with tighter network interconnectivity in males, and higher network eccentricity in females, which may be reflective of greater susceptibility to environmental fluctuations. These findings emphasize the role of biological sex in shaping the small bowel microbiome, which may have potential implications for metabolic health and disease susceptibility.

Research using stool samples has demonstrated significant sex-based differences in gut microbiota composition,41,42 which may contribute to the observed disparities in disease susceptibility, drug tolerability and efficacy, and metabolic function between males and females. For example, stool studies suggest lower RA of the genera Bacteroides and Bilophila in men than in women, and higher RA of Veillonella, Methanobrevibacter,43 and Escherichia.44 A study examining the effects of the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) indomethacin on duodenal mucosal permeability and the duodenal mucosal microbiome in men and women found that baseline duodenal mucosal alpha diversity was higher in healthy women than in healthy men, and that duodenal mucosal beta diversity decreased in both men and women after indomethacin administration, with decreases in abundance of specific higher-level taxa.45 While age, diet, and BMI influence these microbiome composition differences,41,43 sex hormones also appear to play a role. Both estrogen and testosterone levels have been associated with microbiota composition, with one study showing that higher estradiol levels in women correlating with increased RA of phylum Bacteroidetes and genus Bacteroides, and reduced RA of phylum Firmicutes and genera Slackia and Butyricimonas.44,46 Conversely, higher testosterone levels in men correlated with increased RA of Acinetobacter, Dorea, Megammonas, and Ruminococcus.46 That these changes directly result from changes in sex hormones is supported by mouse studies showing that the gut microbiome changes following gonadectomy, and that many of these changes can be restored through sex steroid replacement.14,47,48,49 There is also evidence from human studies: for example, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS, now polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome [PMOS]),50,51,52 which is a female-specific condition associated with hyperandrogenism, has been linked to gut microbiome alterations,53,54,55,56 and transplantation of gut microbes from women with PMOS into mice results in the development of PMOS-like phenotypes.55 Moreover, there are complex feedback mechanisms between microbiome composition and sex hormones41,43—gut microbes can metabolize sex hormones, including deglucuronidating estrogens and androgens,57,58,59 and sex hormones in turn modulate bacterial metabolism.60 These differences may impact host metabolic health, immune function, and the development of gastrointestinal disorders.

The small bowel microbiome plays a crucial role in maintaining human health, and is central to nutrient digestion and absorption, metabolic regulation, and endocrine and immune functions.6,61 Differentiated cell types perform specialized functions including but not limited to nutrient absorption, and the production and secretion of immunoglobulins, the mucus which lines and protects the gut epithelium,62 antimicrobial peptides such as defensins,63 and a variety of hormones and peptides.6,62 The necessity of studying small bowel microbiota directly, rather than relying on stool samples as surrogates for the entire gut, is becoming increasingly appreciated. To overcome the technical challenges associated with sampling this region,64 novel sampling procurement and processing techniques have been developed, including a novel double-lumen aspiration catheter that prevents sample contamination with material from the mouth, esophagus, and stomach,27 and the use of dithiothreitol (DTT) to combat the viscosity of small intestinal aspirates.27 These techniques allow researchers to examine how biological variables, including age,65 hormone therapy (HT),66 and now sex, impact the composition and function of lumenal microbial communities in the small bowel.

The core microbiome refers to microbial taxa that are consistently present across individuals within a specific population, despite variations in overall microbial composition. This foundational microbial community plays essential roles in maintaining the microbiome, which contributes to host health by contributing to critical processes such as nutrient metabolism, immune modulation, and barrier integrity, among many other functions.67 Other factors that potentially affect microbiome composition, including SIBO, diabetes, metformin use, and antibiotics, were excluded. Previously we defined the core microbiome in the duodenal lumen as including Rothia, Granulicatella, Streptococcus, Actinomyces, and Veillonella, findings which were independent of age and the aging process (a combination of chronologic age, number of concomitant diseases, and number of medications used).65 Here, we find many similarities in the core duodenal lumenal microbiome in males and females, which is consistent with findings for the core stool microbiome by Ma et al.68 However, we also found key differences between the core duodenal microbiota in male and female subjects. For example, the genus Granulicatella was significantly more prevalent in the duodenal lumenal microbiota of female subjects when compared to males. Granulicatella is also part of the core salivary microbiome in healthy females,69 and specific Granulicatella species were also found exclusively in the stool microbiota of female subjects but not in males.68 When we examine the associations between duodenal lumenal microbes, we find that Granulicatella is a leader of a unique duodenal microbial community found only in females, in which Granulicatella is associated with the genera Streptococcus, Escherichia/Shigella and Rothia. This is interesting as all of these genera have previously been associated with PMOS,53,54,55,56 and a co-abundance group containing Granulicatella, Streptococcus, and Rothia operational taxonomic units (OTUs) was previously identified in stool samples from women with PMOS, and appeared to be slightly elevated in women with PMOS and obesity.52 In addition, Escherichia/Shigella is a dominant genus associated with SIBO,70 which is more prevalent in women than in men.71 Lastly, we recently found that Rothia mucilaginosa correlated positively with serum testosterone levels particularly in premenopausal human subjects, and can produce testosterone in vitro (Parodi et al., unpublished data [https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvae163.2430]). These results may suggest that these genera may have unique interrelationships in the female duodenum which may reflect or even contribute to physiological, hormonal, or immunological differences between females and males, although further work will be needed to confirm this. This unique duodenal lumenal microbial community found only in females was also associated with changes in the functional metabolic potential of the duodenal microbiome in females, including a decreased ability to deconjugate bile acids, and decreased fructan biosynthesis. Although these are predictions, the former is consistent with the recent identification of higher levels of specific conjugated bile acids, particularly certain glycine and taurine conjugates, in women with Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) vs. men with MASLD.38 Fructans are typically thought not to be digested in the upper digestive tract but rather in the colon, and are thought to have beneficial prebiotic72 and immunomodulatory73 effects, but can also have deleterious effects on the digestive tracts of individuals who are sensitive to them. This includes individuals with conditions such as IBS, which is more prevalent in women, although it is not clear that women are more sensitive to fructans per se.

In contrast to these unique relationships in the female duodenal lumenal microbiome, the genus Leptotrichia was identified as part of the core duodenal lumenal microbiome of males only. Specific Leptotrichia species were previously found exclusively in the male stool and salivary microbiomes.32,74 Leptotrichia spp. are known to be normal components of the oral and stool microbiota and are involved in fermentative metabolism and lactic acid production,32,74 but more work will be needed to determine the potential relevance of sex-specific differences in the abundances of particular species. However, we did find that Leptotrichia is a leader of different duodenal microbial communities in males compared to females, and is an important authority in the overall duodenal lumenal network in males, which may suggest that Leptotrichia species may also influence physiological, hormonal, or immunological differences between males and females.

The duodenal lumenal networks in males and females also differed in terms of the numbers of communities present and in the interconnectivity of their duodenal microbial networks. The network in males exhibited tighter interconnectivity, meaning that different microbial species are more strongly linked and interact and influence each other more frequently.36,37 The genus Prevotella was central to the most interconnected communities and had a greater number of positive associations with other microbes in both males and females. The number of positive connections was slightly higher in males when compared to females, which may explain the slightly higher duodenal lumenal microbial diversity seen in males, as we have previously shown that greater RA of Prevotella is associated with greater duodenal lumenal microbial diversity.66 These findings suggest that the duodenal microbiome in males may be more resilient or stable and resistant to perturbations, and that Prevotella may be an important player in keeping the duodenal microbial network well connected. In contrast, the genera Pseudomonas and Serratia were identified as having the greatest number of negative associations with other microbes in males, suggesting them as potential disrupters within the microbial network. In females, Lactobacillus had the greatest number of negative associations, and we have consistently found that Lactobacillus plays a disruptive role within the duodenal lumenal microbiome.75,76

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the small bowel lumenal microbiome differs significantly between males and females, both in terms of microbial composition and of microbial network structure. Understanding these differences, and how they may contribute to sex-specific differences in health and disease, is essential for incorporating sex as a biological variable and the small bowel microbiome into personalized approaches to medicine.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. First, while we controlled for confounding variables such as antibiotic use, diabetes, BMI, and SIBO, other medical conditions and medications were not controlled for during the analysis, including smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, and hormonal medications. Additionally, our study population consisted of individuals undergoing standard-of-care upper endoscopy for various reasons, and as such the results may not fully represent the broader healthy population. We have previously analyzed the effects of menopause as well as the effects of taking HT on the small bowel microbiome,66 and found that while menopause does affect the duodenal microbiome, many of these effects are ameliorated in women taking HT. We also note that increasing age and declining hormone levels including testosterone in male subjects can also affect the duodenal microbiome. The effects of changing hormone levels, medications, and increasing age would be interesting to examine in a future study with a larger cohort. Lastly, while our microbial network analysis revealed significant sex-specific differences, further validation is necessary to generalize these findings and explore their clinical implications.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

Samples from a total of 266 male (N = 133) and female (N = 133) adult subjects who were recruited for the REIMAGINE (Revealing the Entire Intestinal Microbiota and its Associations with the Genetic, Immunologic, and Neuroendocrine Ecosystem) study27 were included in the analysis for this paper. For REIMAGINE, eligible participants included male and female patients aged 18 to 85 years undergoing standard-of-care upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy [EGD]) without colon preparation. The REIMAGINE study protocol was approved by the Cedars-Sinai Institutional Review Board (IRB #35192), and all participants provided informed written consent prior to enrollment.

Method details

Study subjects

For the present study, male and female REIMAGINE study subjects with available small bowel microbiome data were matched for age (±3 years) and body mass index (BMI, ±3 kg/m2). Subjects were excluded if they had taken antibiotics within 3 months preceding their EGD procedure. Additional exclusion criteria for the present study included the presence of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO, as defined as 103 colony-forming units per mL (CFU/mL)), diabetes, metformin treatment, or a history of gastric bypass surgery. These exclusions were to minimize potential confounding effects of these conditions on the small bowel microbiome.70,75,79 Of note, the designation of subjects as male or female was based on self-report of sex at birth on the REIMAGINE study questionnaire. Gender identities were not included on the REIMAGINE study questionnaire until more recently, and this information is not available for all subjects in the present study.

Questionnaire

Prior to their EGD procedure, all participants completed a comprehensive questionnaire that captured detailed information on demographics and medical history, including recent changes. To ensure accuracy, all self-reported medical details were verified through audits of participants’ medical records.

Blood sample collection and analysis

Prior to the EGD procedure, fasting serum samples were obtained from all subjects to measure levels of a series of serum metabolic and lipid markers. Analytes were measured on a Cobas Integra 400 plus (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis IN) per the manufacturer’s instructions, using reagent cassettes for alanine aminotransferase (ALTL, cassette# 20764957322), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, using the ALP IFCC Gen.2 Small [ALP2S] cassette# 03333752190), aspartate aminotransferase (ASTL, #20764949322), glucose (GLU, #04404483190), and total protein (TP2, #03183734190).

Duodenal aspirates

During the EGD procedure, luminal fluid samples were obtained from the duodenum using a novel custom-designed sterile double-lumen aspiration catheter (Hobbs Medical, Inc.).27 Fluid was aspirated from the second portion of the duodenum without performing any washes prior to inserting the aspiration catheter. To prevent contamination of samples with material from the upper gastrointestinal tract and stomach, the inner catheter was passed through a protective bone wax cap only after reaching the duodenum.27 A total of 1–2 mL of duodenal luminal fluid was collected for analysis.

Aspirate processing and microbial culture

Duodenal aspirates were immediately processed as described previously.27 Duodenal aspirates were treated with a 1:1 ratio of 1x DTT (6.5 mM dithiothreitol in 100 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0). Serial dilutions were then prepared and plated on MacConkey (MAC) and blood agar plates (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). MAC plates were incubated aerobically while the blood agar plates were incubated anaerobically for 16–18 h at 37°C. Negative controls (1xDTT) were also cultured on both MAC and blood agar plates. Colonies were electronically counted using the Scan 500 (Interscience, Paris, France). SIBO was identified as the presence of ≥103 colony-forming units (CFU) per mL of aspirate on MAC plates, per the North American Consensus.80 The remainder of each aspirate was centrifuged; microbial pellets were stored at −80°C under sterile AllProtect reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) prior to DNA isolation for sequencing.

DNA isolation and quantitation

Duodenal aspirate pellets were thawed on ice, and DNAs were isolated using MagAttract Powermicrobiome DNA/RNA KF Kits (Qiagen) on a KingFisher Flex System (ThermoFisher Scientific) as described previously,27 using 1xDTT aliquots as negative controls. The resulting DNAs were then quantified using Qubit dsDNA BR Assay kits (Invitrogen by Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen).

16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

16S libraries were prepared by amplifying the V3 and V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene as detailed previously,27 using gene-specific primers (S-D-Bact-0341-b-S-17 and S-D-Bact-0785-a-A-21). After confirming the purity and concentrations of the amplicon libraries, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina, San Diego, CA). 2 × 301 cycles of paired-end sequencing were performed using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (600-cycles) with 5%–10% PhiX (Illumina) as described previously.27

Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) clustering and taxonomic analysis was performed using CLC Genomics Workbench v.20.0.3 and CLC Microbial Genomics Module v.2.5 (Qiagen) as described previously.70 Briefly, sequences were trimmed, merged, and clustered into OTUs at 97% sequence similarity using the Amplicon-Based OTU clustering tool (Qiagen). Taxonomic levels were assigned using CLC Microbial Genomics default values, performing comparisons against the SILVA Database v.132, 2019 release.

Low depth samples (<5,000 sequences per sample) were removed, and alpha diversity indexes were calculated. The weighted Unifrac metric was used to calculate inter-sample diversity (beta diversity). The core microbiome29 was defined as the most widespread microbial components of the microbiome found in at least 51% of subjects.30

Network analysis

Microbial networks were constructed using the Integrated Network Analysis Pipeline (iNAP)77 and the Sparse correlations for compositional data (SparCC) approach to calculate potential associations between species. SparCC correlations and p-value calculations were performed on filtered OTU tables, and the following default parameters were used to calculate SparCC correlation values: 20 as the number of inference iterations, 10 for exclusion iterations, 0.1 as strength exclusion threshold, and 100 as the number of times shuffled (bootstrapping approach). Networks were built using a threshold of 0.2 for correlations and p < 0.01 for significance. Gephi software (Gephi.org©)78 was used for visualization and analysis of the networks. Different node colors were used to identify different taxonomic groups (based on the phylum level). Node diameters represented the average of each taxon’s relative abundance at the genus level. Blue edges denoted positive interactions between nodes, highlighting microbes that consistently appear together (co-abundance), and red edges denoted negative interactions between nodes, highlighting microbes that consistently appear apart (co-exclusion). Edge thickness denoted the strength of the interaction between nodes (thicker edges = stronger interactions). The number of triangles was estimated on Gephi using Matthieu Latapy’s algorithm.81

Leaders/authorities of each microbial community (modules) within the duodenal microbial networks of male and female subjects were identified considering the number of correlations (connections) and how strong these connections were.82 It did not matter if the correlation was positive or negative.

Prediction of duodenal microbial metabolic functions

To infer predicted microbial metabolic functions, OTU abundance tables from 16S sequencing were analyzed using the Infer Functional Profile (beta) tool available with the CLC Workbench Microbial module v.25.0.1 (Qiagen). In order to run this tool, a PICRUSt2 Multiplication Table and EC (Enzyme Commission Numbers) database were used. Default settings were applied. Pathways were built with the Identify Pathways tool, also available with the CLC Workbench Microbial module v.25.0.1 (Qiagen), which takes a functional abundance table with EC terms as input and translates these into pathway calls using the MetaCyc pathway database. To evaluate differences in the enrichment of these pathways between groups, a Negative Binomial GLM model was used to obtain maximum likelihood estimates for the fold change (FC) of a predicted metabolic microbial pathway between males and females, and the Wald test was used for determination of significance. False Discovery Rate (FDR) was performed to correct the p-values. This pipeline was applied to compare the overall predicted microbial functional potential between groups, taking in consideration the entire duodenal microbiome, and to the collection of reads assigned to microbial genera part of specific communities after network analysis.

Quantification and statistical analyses

Significant differentially abundant OTUs between female and male subjects were performed following published recommendations used when the average library size for each group is approximately equal and/or the fold difference between groups is low (>2-3x on average).

Multiple comparisons and statistical analyses for both 16S data was performed with CLC Genomics Workbench v.20.0.3/22.0.2 and CLC Microbial Genomics Module v.20.1.1/22.1 (Qiagen). A Negative Binomial GLM model was used to obtain maximum likelihood estimates for the FC of an OTU between males and females, and the Wald test was used for determination of significance. FDR was performed to correct the p-values. Two-tailed Spearman R correlations, Mann-Whitney tests and graph construction were carried out with rarefied OTU tables using GraphPad Prism 7.02 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).
