DTPA disrupts development of preantral ovarian follicles in vitro
Authors: James M Hester, Francisco J Diaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDietary zinc deficiency disrupts fertility in vivo by impairing oocyte and embryo development near ovulation. Acute treatment of newborn ovaries with a strong intracellular chelator (TPEN), which preferentially binds zinc, disrupts follicular development. However, the chronic effects of transition metal chelation on preantral follicle development are not known. In this study, the effect of the extracellular transition metal chelator, diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA), was used to examine more prolonged effects on preantral follicle development. Preantral granulosa cell–oocyte complexes from 14-day-old mice were cultured under control, chelated (DTPA), or rescue (DTPA + ZnSO4) conditions for up to 10 days. Preantral follicles cultured in DTPA alone showed impaired growth, disrupted nucleolar morphology, and impaired meiotic progression. The granulosa cells in DTPA-treated follicles underwent apoptosis at a higher rate than controls, had fewer physical connections to the oocyte, and reduced activation of pSMAD2 signaling. Moreover, Lhcgr and Ar transcripts were higher in cumulus cells, and Figla was lower in oocytes from DTPA-treated follicles. These data support a role for transition metals in general, and zinc in particular, in proper development of preantral ovarian follicles. The loss of somatic support cells explains some or all of the growth and developmental deficits seen in the DTPA-treated oocytes. DTPA preferentially binds zinc. Therefore, these results support growing evidence that a proper supply of transition metals, including zinc, is essential for optimal ovarian function.Lay summaryThe roles the mineral zinc plays in the ovary are not yet clear. The present study used follicles from mouse ovaries that were grown in the lab for up to 10 days. Follicles are round structures that contain a large egg cell at the center, surrounded by smaller cells called granulosa cells. Follicles were either grown with adequate zinc or insufficient zinc levels. The findings show that follicles require sufficient zinc to form connections between the egg and granulosa cells, which are essential for growth of both the egg and the granulosa cells. When there is insufficient zinc, loss of these connections leads to more granulosa cells dying and smaller follicles. These results show that zinc is important for growth of ovarian follicles, which could be important for treating infertility by supplying adequate levels of zinc in the diet.

Introduction

Infertility affects millions of women worldwide. Twelve percent of women in the US have sought infertility services (Warner et al. 2015). One contributing factor of reproductive dysfunction is zinc deficiency (Nriagu 2019, Garner et al. 2021), a condition estimated to affect 17% of the global population (Wessells & Brown 2012). Zinc levels in the follicular fluid of women undergoing IVF are lower than age-matched controls (Özkaya et al. 2011), pointing to zinc status as a likely contributor to subfertility in humans. The role of zinc in the oocyte has begun to be elucidated, particularly during meiotic resumption and fertilization. Total zinc content increases during oocyte maturation (Kim et al. 2010), with much of it stored in a labile pool of vesicles near the plasma membrane (Que et al. 2014). At fertilization, zinc-containing vesicles rapidly undergo exocytosis in a process termed the ‘zinc spark’, which has been shown to be a biomarker of egg activation in many species, including humans (Que et al. 2014, Duncan et al. 2016), and has proven to be a reliable indicator of embryo quality in rodent models (Zhang et al. 2016). Zinc deficiency prematurely initiates germinal vesicle breakdown while simultaneously interfering with progression through meiosis (Bernhardt et al. 2011, Kong et al. 2012, Tian & Diaz 2012). Preovulatory dietary zinc deficiency in female mice reduces the number of ovulations, fertilization rate, number of blastocysts, and litter size (Tian & Diaz 2013, Tian et al. 2014). Oocytes from female mice fed a zinc-deficient diet have reduced DNA and histone methylation, which is associated with subsequent neural tube defects in the developing embryo (Tian & Diaz 2013). These studies have established the critical role of zinc in the fully grown competent mammalian oocyte. Work from our lab and others (Hester et al. 2017, Mendoza et al. 2022) has also revealed defects in early oogenesis resulting from zinc deficiency in an invertebrate model. However, the effect of zinc deficiency on development of preantral follicles has not been examined in mammals.

In humans, development from a primordial follicle to a dominant follicle capable of ovulation takes almost a year, and over 99% of activated follicles undergo atresia at some point in development (Hirshfield 1991). Environmental, dietary, or disease factors that impair follicular development have a severe impact on fertility. One critical developmental milestone is the preantral to antral follicle transition, at which point rodent follicles are most vulnerable to atresia (McGee & Hsueh 2000). During the preantral to antral transition, the oocyte reaches its full size and undergoes nuclear maturation, adopting a transcriptionally silent chromatin configuration tightly wrapped around a large nucleolus (Zuccotti et al. 1998, 2005). Condensed chromatin surrounding the nucleolus (SN), as well as a large oocyte diameter, is an indicator of viability and meiotic potential (Johnson et al. 1995, Sun et al. 2001). Changes in the oocyte actin cytoskeleton also occur, preparing the cell for asymmetric division – the hallmark of oocyte meiosis (Schuh & Ellenberg 2008). Reorganization of the oocyte cortical actin, in particular, is required to allow the meiotic spindle to migrate to the cell periphery (Chaigne et al. 2013, 2015).

The somatic granulosa cells surrounding the oocyte also undergo a profound transition at the end of preantral development. The preantral granulosa cells differentiate into two distinct lineages: cumulus cells, which surround the oocyte, and mural granulosa cells, which line the wall of the antral follicle. Physiological function, as well as gene expression patterns, is distinct between the two cell types (Diaz et al. 2006, 2007a,b, Wigglesworth et al. 2015). The oocyte-derived factors (ODFs) GDF9 and BMP15 activate pSMAD signaling pathways in cells closest to the oocyte (Moore et al. 2003, Su et al. 2004). This induces a cumulus cell phenotype characterized by metabolic support of the growing oocyte. Granulosa cells farther from the oocyte differentiate into endocrine mural cells under the influence of circulating hormonal factors such as follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (Diaz et al. 2007a,b). Proper granulosa cell development is required for oocyte growth, preovulatory meiotic arrest, and sensitivity to the luteinizing hormone (LH) surge (Park et al. 2004, Norris et al. 2009, Zhang et al. 2010).

Previous studies have demonstrated that zinc deficiency interferes with follicular function in antral follicles. In cumulus cells, lack of zinc in the diet causes a loss of pSMAD2/3 signaling (Tian & Diaz 2012), while zinc chelation leads to a premature upregulation of progesterone (Tian et al. 2017). Reduced GDF9 transcript abundance likely contributes to these two effects in cumulus cells (Tian & Diaz 2013). Interestingly, movement of zinc into the oocyte is controlled by cumulus cells (Lisle et al. 2013). In antral follicles, zinc deficiency for 10 days leads to a complete block of follicle rupture and a lack of cumulus expansion (Tian & Diaz 2012). Apart from these studies in antral follicles, a recent study showed that zinc is far more abundant than copper or iron in preantral follicles, and zinc content increases as follicles progress from primordial to secondary follicles (Chen et al. 2023). Moreover, ovaries treated with the intracellular chelator TPEN for 24 h showed defective follicular development following an additional 6-day culture (Chen et al. 2023). In the present study, we used a milder extracellular chelator, DTPA, and show that treatment with this mild chelator during culture of preantral follicles compromises both oocyte and granulosa cell development. We evaluated in vitro treatment effects and identified defects in follicular and oocyte growth, increased granulosa cell apoptosis, disrupted somatic-germ cell connections, and altered granulosa cell signaling and transcript abundance. Many, but not all, effects of DTPA were blocked by supplemental zinc. Collectively, the findings add further evidence of the importance of transition metals, particularly zinc, during preantral follicular development.

Materials and methods

Preantral follicle collection and culture

CD-1 mice (Mus musculus) were commercially obtained from Charles River Laboratories (USA). Animal procedures, care, and handling were performed according to relevant guidelines and regulations and were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Penn State University. For each biological replicate, 8–12 14-day-old female mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation and cervical dislocation before ovary collection and digestion as described previously (O’Brien et al. 2003, Diaz et al. 2006, 2007a). All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) unless otherwise specified. Briefly, ovaries were placed in a digest media (MEM-alpha) containing 1 mg/mL collagenase II, 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, and 0.2% DNase I. Ovaries were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 and mixed by pipetting every 5 min until digestion was complete. This resulting preantral granulosa cell–oocyte complexes (PGOCs) were devoid of theca cells. PGOCs consisting of an oocyte and multiple layers of granulosa cells, with oocytes of 63 μm (SD = 9.12 μm), were washed three times in control culture media: MEMα supplemented with BSA (3 mg/mL), ITS (insulin, 5 μg/mL; transferrin, 5 μg/mL; selenium, 5 ng/mL), and epidermal growth factor (1 ng/mL). PGOCs were cultured for 4, 6, or 10 days on collagen membrane inserts (Transwell®-COL, Corning, USA), submerged in either control media, zinc-depleted media containing 10 μM DTPA, or rescue media containing 10 μM DTPA and 50 μM ZnSO4 in 12-well plates with 1.5 mL of media at 37°C in 5% CO2 incubator as described previously (O’Brien et al. 2003, Diaz et al. 2006, 2007a). Media was changed every 48 h by lifting inserts and placing them in a new 12-well plate with fresh treatment media. The concentration of DTPA was ∼50-fold higher than the concentration of zinc in the media, which was 23.21 nM as measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) at the Laboratory for Isotopes and Metals in the Environment (LIME) at the Pennsylvania State University. Importantly, the concentration of DTPA was much lower than that of calcium in the media according to the manufacturer (1.8 mM). This means that chelation of calcium is minimal under the culture conditions used. DTPA has been used extensively to chelate zinc in cell culture media (Chesters et al. 1989, Cinatl et al. 1996, Lefebvre et al. 1998, Cho et al. 2007), and induces apoptosis at a significantly lower rate than membrane-permeable chelators, such as (N,N,N′,N′-tetrakis(2-pyridinylmethyl)-1,2-ethanediamine (TPEN) (Nakatani et al. 2000). At the end of culture, PGOCs were separated from the collagen membrane by striking the outside of the culture dish sharply with a gloved hand. After dislodging from the collagen membrane, PGOCs were collected from the culture well using a mouth pipet and either flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA/protein analysis or moved to a fresh dish of media for further analysis.

Oocyte diameter calculation

After 4, 6, or 10 days of culture, PGOCs were dislodged from collagen membranes and collected. Complexes were moved to dishes containing fresh media and immediately imaged using a Nikon Eclipse TE200 inverted microscope fitted with an Olympus DP20 brightfield camera. Images were taken through a 4× objective. Oocyte diameter was measured from resulting images using DP2-BSW software from Olympus. Diameter was calculated for a minimum of 125 oocytes per time point and treatment. The experiment was repeated a minimum of three times with separate groups of follicles. Only the oocyte diameter was measured, excluding surrounding somatic cells. In the case of non-spherical oocytes, the longest axis was taken as the diameter. Datasets were generated by two independent researchers and averaged to calculate results.

PGOC total area calculation

The same brightfield images used to calculate oocyte diameter were used to measure the total area of PGOCs after culture and removal from the collagen membrane. The 2-dimensional area of PGOCs consisting of one oocyte and all attached granulosa cells was measured after 4 or 10 days of culture. The area of PGOCs was measured by tracing the perimeter of the free-floating structures using ImageJ analysis software (NIH). Analysis was performed on three biological replicates/time points, and a minimum of 75 PGOCs were included in each treatment/time point calculation. The experiment was repeated a minimum of three times with separate groups of follicles.

Zinc accumulation in the oocyte

To test the effect of treatment on zinc accumulation in the oocyte, PGOCs were cultured in control, DTPA, or rescue media for 4 days. PGOCs were collected as described previously and placed into fresh drops containing the corresponding treatment. Oocytes were mechanically denuded by gentle pipetting through a narrow-bore glass pipette, a technique previously shown to induce zinc accumulation by the oocyte (Lisle et al. 2013). Denuded oocytes were placed into culture media containing 4 μM of the amyl ester of the fluorophore, FluoZin-3AM (Invitrogen, USA; excitation 494 nm/emission 516 nm), which is an intracellular zinc indicator. After 30 min, oocytes were moved to a chamber slide with fresh media containing Prolong Live antifade reagent. After an additional 30-min culture period, cells were imaged using an inverted fluorescent microscope. To prevent signal bleaching, cells were imaged as a group. Fluorescence was measured in each oocyte with ImageJ and reported as corrected total cell fluorescence (McCloy et al. 2014). The experiment was repeated two times, and at least 50 cells were measured per treatment. Cells were kept in treatment (control, DTPA, rescue) through all steps of the imaging process.

Meiotic progression

To test the meiotic potential of cultured oocytes, PGOCs were cultured for 10 days. PGOCs were washed through three drops of control media and then cultured overnight in media containing 10 ng/mL EGF (maturation media) to stimulate cumulus expansion and maturation (Diaz et al. 2006, 2007a). Maturation media was the same for all groups and did not contain DTPA or exogenous zinc. After 20 h, oocytes were denuded and placed in a fresh drop of media. Meiotic progression was observed via brightfield microscopy. Oocytes containing a germinal vesicle were classified as GV-arrested oocytes. MII oocytes were identified by the presence of a polar body. Oocytes with neither a germinal vesicle nor polar body were classified as MI. Results are combined from five biological replicates per treatment. In total, at least 150 PGOCs from each treatment were matured.

Nuclear chromatin configuration

To observe the effect of treatment on chromatin configuration, PGOCs were collected and immediately fixed for 1 h in 4% PFA in PBS. Cells were washed in PBST, stained with Phalloidin-488 at 1:1,000, and mounted on ring slides with Prolong antifade plus DAPI (Fisher Scientific). Cells were imaged with a Zeiss Axiovert 200 fluorescent microscope through a 40× objective to observe chromatin arrangement (surrounding nucleolus/not surrounding nucleolus) and the number of nucleoli.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL assay)

Apoptotic cells were labeled in 4-day-cultured PGOCs using the DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TUNEL positive controls were generated by pretreating cells with DNase I for 10 min. Labeled complexes were mounted on etched ring slides with SlowFade antifade mounting media plus DAPI. Cells were imaged with a Zeiss Axiovert 200 fluorescent microscope through a 40× objective. The number of apoptotic granulosa cells/complex was calculated for individual PGOCs. Only complexes in which the oocyte was completely enclosed by granulosa cells were used for this analysis. The experiment was repeated three times, and at least 30 complexes were measured in each treatment group.

Trans-zonal projection (TZP) and cortical actin quantification

Connections between oocytes and the surrounding granulosa cells were examined in 4-day-cultured PGOCs as described (El-Hayek & Clarke 2015). Briefly, oocytes were collected and denuded of granulosa cells by passing through a narrow-bore glass pipette. Immediately after removal of surrounding cells, oocytes were fixed for 1 h in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature. Cells were washed three times in PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 and stained with Phalloidin-488 (Molecular Probes Cat# A-12379, USA) at 1:100 in PBST for 1 hour to label actin filaments. Cells were washed and mounted on slides with ProLong antifade mounting media plus DAPI. Cells were imaged with a confocal FV-10i microscope through a 60× objective. Fluorescence was measured in the perivitelline space, directly outside the plasma membrane and cortical actin ring, using ImageJ and corrected for background signal (McCloy et al. 2014). To eliminate bias during the analysis, the region of interest was selected automatically by the software based on changes in fluorometric intensity. Using this method, it is possible to select only the TZPs without including the bright cortical actin ring or the empty region outside the perivitelline space. Cortical actin width was also quantified by drawing a linear ROI across the cell membrane. The full width at half-max (FWHM) from the resulting emission spectra was calculated and used to compare groups. Cortical actin width was averaged from eight equally spaced measurements/oocyte. Measurements were combined from three biological replicates, and a minimum of 20 cells were included per treatment.

Western blot

SMAD2 activation was measured in PGOCs after 10 days of culture via Western blot as described previously (Tian & Diaz 2012). Briefly, complexes from three biological replicates were removed from the collagen membrane and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen in groups of 50. Pelleted cells were thawed in Laemmli buffer (Alfa Aesar, USA) containing beta-mercaptoethanol and boiled for 10 min. Proteins were separated on an 8–12% bis-tris PAGE gel using a mini-PAGE blot system and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The blot was cut in half at the 50 kDa marker, and the top half was probed for pSMAD2, while the bottom half was probed for β-ACTIN by incubating with specific antibodies overnight at 4°C according to standard immunoblotting procedures. Primary antibodies – rabbit anti-pSMAD2 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 3101, USA) 1:1,000, rabbit anti-βactin (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# A5060, USA) 1:10,000. Secondary antibody – mouse anti rabbit-IgG HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# 31464, USA) 1:15,000. Protein signal was quantified and background corrected with ImageJ.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Granulosa cells and oocytes were separated from each POGC cultured for 10 days and snap-frozen immediately after collection. RNA was isolated separately for each cell type. Total RNA was purified from 25 PGOC/replicate (n = 3 or 4) using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including column purification and removal of genomic DNA. Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the QuantiTect RT Kit (Qiagen). qPCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Cycler. Melt curves were evaluated for each gene product after the run, and amplification products were sequenced to validate primer specificity. Gene expression was calculated according to the ddCt method normalized to the stably expressed reference gene rpl19, as described previously (Livak & Schmittgen 2001). Primers used are listed in Supplemental Table S1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article).

Digital image manipulation

All fluorescence and densitometry measurements were made on raw, unaltered images. Representative images have been adjusted uniformly across treatment groups according to recommended best practices (Cromey 2010).

Statistical analysis

The rate of multi-nucleolar phenotype was analyzed via chi-square analysis. Meiotic progression after EGF treatment was compared at each meiotic stage using the Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA of ranked variables. Post-hoc comparison of treatments at each meiotic stage was performed using the Mann–Whitney test. For experimental outcomes, including zinc fluorescence, TZP fluorescence, pSMAD2 band intensity, and number of apoptotic cells/complex, data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. When a significant treatment effect was identified by ANOVA, Fisher’s LSD method was used for post-hoc comparison to maximize power to detect differences while maintaining an acceptable family-wise error rate, since there are only three treatment groups. For PGOC area and oocyte diameter, data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with day and treatment as independent variables. Separately, one-way ANOVA and Fisher’s LSD were used to analyze treatments within day. Statistical calculations were performed with Minitab version 18 or in R-Studio. P < 0.05 was considered significant in all cases. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Results

DTPA slows oocyte growth on day 4 and 6, but not day 10 of preantral granulosa cell-oocyte complex (PGOC) culture

Oocytes from PGOCs cultured in DTPA were smaller than control samples on day 4 (control: 60.77 ± 0.41 μM, DTPA: 58.40 ± 0.31 μM, rescue: 60.64 ± 0.40 μM; P < 0.001) and day 6 (control: 59.8 ± 0.33 μM, DTPA: 58.69 ± 0.28 μM, rescue: 59.4 ± 0.33 μM; P = 0.028), but not day 10 (control: 64.64 ± 0.52 μM, DTPA: 64.04 ± 0.28 μM, rescue: 63.44 ± 0.59 μM; P > 0.05) of cell culture (Fig. 1A). Oocytes cultured in rescue media were not statistically different from control at any time point. The clear effect of DTPA on days 4 and 6 shows that zinc availability is needed for optimal oocyte growth, but that oocytes can compensate for reduced zinc availability, as indicated by the catch-up growth of the oocyte by day 10. PGOC size is variable according to the age and size of the donor. However, careful attention was made to precisely determine the age of the donor mice by checking for new litters daily. Moreover, PGOCs from each collection were randomly distributed across treatment groups to ensure homogeneity. Finally, we measured the size of at least 50 PGOCs or 125 oocytes for growth determination, which is reflected in the small values for the standard error of the mean (SEM) reported in Fig. 1. Representative brightfield images are shown in Fig. S1, and data in Fig. 1 were reanalyzed across days and included in Fig. S2 to better show temporal changes in PGOC and oocyte size.

DTPA treatment reduces oocyte diameter and PGOC area. Oocyte diameter (A) and PGOC area (B) in control, DTPA, and DTPA plus zinc-treated PGOCs for 4, 6, or 10 days. Different superscripts indicate significant difference, P < 0.05. Values are mean ± SEM.

DTPA culture decreases PGOC size

To further assess the development of cells in culture, we measured the two-dimensional area of collected PGOCs on days 4, 6, and 10 of culture. This measurement includes both the oocyte and attached granulosa cells after removal from the collagen membrane. DTPA-treated PGOCs were significantly smaller than control at all time points examined (day 4 – control: 10,776 ± 599.8 μM2, DTPA: 7,505 ± 306.8 μM2, rescue: 11,114 ± 605.9 μM2, P < 0.001; day 6 – control: 7,918 ± 431.2 μM2, DTPA: 6,490 ± 200.5 μM2, rescue: 7,092 ± 308.6 μM2, P < 0.01; day 10 – control: 10,241 ± 390.2 μM2, DTPA: 7,368 ± 284.0 μM2, rescue: 9,313 ± 321.5 μM2, P < 0.001). On day 10 of culture, DTPA-treated PGOCs were still significantly smaller than controls (Fig. 1B). This indicates that, unlike oocytes, granulosa cell proliferation and hence overall growth of the PGOC does not have a compensatory mechanism to promote catch-up growth.

DTPA reduces the meiotic potential of cultured PGOCs

To assess the maturation capacity of DTPA-treated PGOCs, we transferred 10-day-cultured PGOCs to media containing 10 ng/mL EGF. All oocytes appeared healthy and were GV-arrested when placed into maturation media. Treatment had a significant effect on meiotic progression (Fig. 2). DTPA-treated oocytes were less likely to reach either the M1 (control: 40.2 ± 6.3%, DTPA: 10.2 ± 11%, rescue: 54.6 ± 5.1%; P < 0.01) or M2 (control: 16.4 ± 5.1%, DTPA: 3.4 ± 1.4%, rescue: 8.13 ± 1.3%; P < 0.05) stage of meiosis. Cells cultured in rescue media were not significantly different from control for any meiotic stage.

DTPA treatment interferes with meiotic resumption after 10 days of in vitro maturation. After 10 days of culture, PGOCs were removed from collagen membranes and placed in maturation media containing 10 ng/mL EGF. After 20 h, oocytes were denuded and assessed for meiotic progression by light microscopy. DTPA-treated cells did not progress through meiosis at the same rate as control or rescue cells. DTPA-treated oocytes were less likely to reach the M1 or M2 stage of meiosis (Kruskal–Wallis test P < 0.01 and P < 0.05, respectively). The majority of DTPA-treated cells did not undergo germinal vesicle breakdown (59.86%). Meiotic progression was not significantly different between control and rescue-treated cells.

DTPA culture disrupts zinc accumulation in the oocyte

To demonstrate that DTPA treatment impacts zinc homeostasis specifically, we examined the ability of treated oocytes to import zinc. After 4 days of culture, PGOCs were collected and mechanically denuded – a process previously shown to result in rapid zinc uptake by the oocyte (Lisle et al. 2013). Denuded oocytes from each treatment group were then loaded with the zinc-specific fluorophore, FluoZin-3AM, to visualize labile zinc within the oocytes. FluoZin-3AM fluorescent intensity was significantly lower in DTPA-treated cells compared to control or rescue oocytes (Fig. 3 control: 45,959 a.u., DTPA: 17,956 a.u., rescue: 47,956 a.u.; P < 0.001).

DTPA treatment reduces zinc accumulation in the oocyte. (A) Corrected total cell fluorescence for the zinc fluorophore, FluoZin-3AM, in oocytes from PGOCs treated with control medium, DTPA, or DTPA plus zinc for 4 days. (B, C, D) Representative fluorescent images. Scale bar = 100 μM. Different superscripts indicate significant difference, P < 0.05. Values are mean ± SEM.

DTPA treatment induces a multi-nucleolar phenotype in cultured PGOCs

Since DTPA-treated PGOCs are smaller and the oocytes less competent to complete maturation, the nuclear configuration was evaluated on days 6 and 10 of PGOC cell culture using DAPI staining (Fig. 4, Table 1). Cells were evaluated based on chromatin arrangement and categorized as having either a surrounded nucleolus (SN) or non-surrounded nucleolus (NSN) configuration. In addition, the number of nucleoli was counted. No differences were observed between treatment groups in the SN:NSN ratio. However, there were more oocytes with multiple nucleoli in DTPA-treated complexes on day 6 (Fig. 4), but not day 10 of culture (Table 1, P < 0.001). This observation is similar to the recovery in oocyte size between day 6 and day 10.

DTPA treatment increases apoptosis in granulosa cells

The smaller size of DTPA-treated PGOCs could be due to changes in cell survival. To determine the effect of DTPA on cell death, PGOCs cultured for 4 days with DTPA increased the number of TUNEL positive cells/PGOC compared with control or rescue groups (control: 13.6 ± 2.05, DTPA: 52.2 ± 4.37, rescue: 13.8 ± 2.34; P < 0.001). Apoptotic signaling was seen exclusively in the granulosa cells, not in oocytes (Fig. 5).

Representative images of TUNEL assay positive control (B), and control (C), DTPA (D), and rescue (E) treatment groups. Blue – DAPI staining; green – TUNEL labeling. Different superscripts indicate significant difference, P < 0.001. Values are mean ± SEM. Scale bar = 20 μm.

DTPA reduces trans-zonal projections between oocytes and granulosa cells

The reduction in cell survival, growth, and meiotic competence in DTPA-treated oocytes could be due to lack of somatic cell support. This was assessed by fluorescent labeling of actin filaments with Phalloidin-Alexa 488 in the perivitelline space to quantify TZP connections between granulosa cells and the oocyte (Fig. 6). DTPA-treated cells had lower fluorescent signal than control, indicating fewer connections between the oocyte and surrounding cumulus cells (P = 0.023). There was no significant difference in TZP fluorescence between control and rescue treatments. Cortical actin width was also quantified, but no significant difference was detected between treatments.

DTPA decreases SMAD2 activation in PGOCs

Lack of TZPs could result in lower oocyte-stimulated pSMAD2 phosphorylation in the surrounding granulosa cells. ODFs such as GDF9 induce phosphorylation of SMAD2 in cumulus granulosa cells surrounding the oocyte. Western blot analysis of 10-day-cultured PGOCs showed decreased levels of phosphorylated SMAD2 in DTPA-treated complexes compared with control (P < 0.05, Fig. 7). The pSMAD2:β-actin ratio demonstrates impaired signaling between the oocyte and surrounding somatic cells. Full-length blot images are shown in Fig. S3.

DTPA alters the gene expression profile of cultured granulosa cells

Changes in oocyte-granulosa cell communication are indicated by lower pSMAD2 levels and reduced TZPs. To test if these changes are associated with changes in transcript abundance of oocyte-regulated granulosa cell transcripts and oocyte-secreted factors, oocytes and somatic cells were separated before freezing in order to evaluate gene expression of each cell type separately. DTPA treatment lowered expression of the oocyte transcript Figla compared to rescue treatment (Fig. 8B P < 0.01). DTPA treatment disrupted gene expression in the granulosa cells, which showed higher expression of the mural cell marker Lhcgr, the cumulus cell markers Ar and Fosb, and the preantral granulosa cell Pcsk6 when compared with control granulosa cells (Fig. 8A, P < 0.05 for all).

Discussion

In order to investigate the effect of the transition metal chelator DTPA during the preantral to antral transition, we employed an in vitro system to grow preantral follicles in the presence of the metal chelator DTPA. This culture system has been employed for in vitro follicle growth (Eppig et al. 1992, O’Brien et al. 2003), and has been successfully used to study oocyte-granulosa cell interaction during preantral development (Vanderhyden et al. 1990, Diaz et al. 2007a, Sugiura et al. 2010, El-Hayek & Clarke 2015). Our results indicate that DTPA impairs preantral follicle growth, delays oocyte growth, interferes with nucleolar development, and decreases paracrine communication between somatic cells and the oocyte. Notably, zinc supplementation of DTPA media rescued these defects, suggesting that zinc is required for proper preantral follicular development.

We chose to employ the extracellular metal chelator DTPA rather than the intracellular chelator TPEN, as TPEN has been shown to induce cell death after culture (Nakatani et al. 2000). Extracellular chelation of transition metals rather than intracellular chelation is also a more physiologically relevant model, as it places the cell into a zinc-depleted environment akin to zinc-deficient serum/plasma. While previous studies have added DTPA to cell culture media in concentrations as high as 600 μM (Nakatani et al. 2000), pilot studies in our lab showed that DTPA levels above 100 μM were toxic to developing oocytes. 10 μM DTPA was therefore used for long-term preantral follicle cell culture. DTPA’s high affinity for zinc (Kd = 10−18.75) makes it a very selective chelator and preferentially binds zinc over other ions such as calcium at physiological pH (Arts et al. 2018). Moreover, the concentration of calcium in the media is 1.8 mM, which is almost 200X higher than the concentration of DTPA used for these studies. Therefore, the effects of DTPA are not likely due to reduced calcium availability. However, since DTPA can chelate metals other than zinc, the use of a rescue treatment combining DTPA and exogenous zinc was critical to establishing the role of zinc in preantral follicle development. To ensure that sufficient zinc rescue concentrations were added to block DTPA effects, we used 50 μM zinc to rescue DTPA effects while not inducing zinc toxicity. Indeed, the detectable free zinc signal was not different between the control and the rescue group (Fig. 3). The rescue zinc concentration (50 μM) was only marginally higher than the concentration of zinc in follicular fluid, which is estimated to be between 15 and 20 μM (Ng et al. 1987, Rodríguez-Díaz et al. 2023). However, it is important to point out that 50 μM zinc in the rescue group is higher than the 10 μM DTAP used and could introduce confounding effects of high zinc levels in the media independent of DTPA. The observation that there was no increase in cell death in the rescue group suggests that excess zinc did not cause acute toxicity. To evaluate changes in oocyte zinc status resulting from our treatment, we visualized zinc accumulation in the oocyte using the zinc fluorophore FluoZin-3AM (Fig. 3). Zinc-mediated fluorescence was reduced by approximately 50% in the DTPA-treated cells, indicating a reduction of labile zinc inside the DTPA-treated oocytes. Rescue treatment returned fluorescence to control levels, validating that zinc levels were significantly altered between our experimental groups.

Oocyte growth in the preantral and early antral stage is a key process in follicle development. While changes in oocyte diameter may be slight, the associated increase in cell volume is substantial (Eppig 2001), and increased cell size is correlated with the ability to reach the blastocyst stage after fertilization (Eppig et al. 1992). We observed growth restriction in DTPA-treated oocytes on day 4 of culture (Fig. 1). However, we observed no treatment effects on oocyte diameter on day 10. This may indicate a compensatory physiological mechanism for oocyte growth even under chelated conditions. The increased incidence of multiple nucleoli in DTPA-treated oocytes could indicate a compensatory increase in protein synthesis in these oocytes that resulted in catch-up growth. This is an appealing hypothesis that requires further experimental testing.

Oocyte maturation was impaired by DTPA. Interestingly, DTPA-treated oocytes were less likely than controls to undergo germinal vesicle breakdown and reach the MI or MII stage when placed into maturation media. All maturation media contained adequate and equal levels of zinc, indicating a zinc requirement during development in addition to the zinc requirement for meiotic resumption shown in previous studies (Kim et al. 2010, Bernhardt et al. 2011, Kong et al. 2012, Tian & Diaz 2012, 2013). The inability to resume and complete meiosis indicates a severe loss of fertility in those oocytes, as meiotic resumption and division are a prerequisite for proper embryo development after fertilization.

Chromatin rearrangement is an accepted marker of oocyte developmental potential. Preantral oocytes exhibit a diffuse DNA arrangement (non-surrounded nucleolus, NSN – Fig. 4A) during their growth phase. As oocytes gain the ability to resume meiosis, the nucleus undergoes a dramatic rearrangement characterized by chromatin condensation around the nucleolus (surrounded nucleolus, SN – Fig. 4B). This shift is concurrent with transcriptional silencing in the maturing oocyte (Christians et al. 1999, De La Fuente & Eppig 2001, Abe et al. 2010). While there was no significant treatment effect on the ratio of SN to NSN oocytes, we were surprised to encounter a multi-nucleolar phenotype in DTPA-treated oocytes on day 6 of culture (Table 1). Multiple nucleoli are common in the nucleus of growing oocytes, but this phenotype decreases as oocytes reach maturity (Zuccotti et al. 2005), and the nucleoli adopt a compact physiological arrangement called the nucleolar precursor body (NPB) (Kyogoku et al. 2014). The oocyte NPB is required for subsequent embryonic development (Kyogoku et al. 2014), and impaired nucleoli condensation may be a novel indicator of impaired nuclear maturation in zinc-deficient oocytes.

The temporal gene expression patterns of maturing oocytes and granulosa cells have been well characterized (Pan et al. 2005, Vigone et al. 2013, Wigglesworth et al. 2015). Under normal conditions, the maturing preantral oocyte is marked by elevated expression of oocyte markers Nobox, Figla, Sohlh1, Sohlh2, Zp3, as well as the secretory ODFs Gdf9 and Bmp15. GDF9 and BMP15 induce a cumulus cell phenotype in the surrounding preantral granulosa cells through phospho-SMAD signaling (Li et al. 2008, Sugiura et al. 2010). Cumulus cells surrounding the oocyte are characterized by increased expression of Slc38a3, Ar, Npr2, Amh (Eppig et al. 2005, Diaz et al. 2007b, Zhang et al. 2010) and the AP-1 protein transcripts Fos, Fosb, Jun, Junb (Wigglesworth et al. 2015). Mural granulosa cells upregulate expression of the LH receptor (Lhcgr) and Cyp19a1 under the influence of FSH. In our experimental model of preantral follicular development, we separated oocytes from somatic cells after 10 days of culture and processed each cell type separately (Fig. 8). DTPA treatment decreased expression of Figla in the oocyte, an effect noted previously during in vivo zinc restriction experiments in our lab (Tian & Diaz 2013). While the levels of Gdf9 and Bmp15 transcripts in the oocyte were not different between groups, DTPA-treated granulosa cells displayed significantly higher expression of Lhcgr (mural cell marker), Ar and Fosb (cumulus cell markers), and Pcsk6 (undifferentiated granulosa cell marker) compared with controls. These results indicate a dysregulation of granulosa cell differentiation in the DTPA-treated preantral follicle.

Disrupted oocyte-granulosa cell communication was evident after DTPA treatment. Most notably, there was a reduction of TZPs connecting DTPA-treated oocytes to the surrounding granulosa cells after 4 days of culture (Fig. 6). TZPs are actin-rich projections that originate from granulosa cells, pierce the zona pellucida, cross the perivitelline space, and contact the oocyte. TZPs terminate in gap junctions, through which cumulus cells provide the oocyte with growth factors, nutrients, and substrates for energy metabolism (Donahue & Stern 1968, Clarke 2018). We employed phalloidin staining to label TZPs and measured fluorescence in the perivitelline space to compare TZP connections between treatment groups. Paracrine communication between the oocyte and granulosa cells is critical for proper energy metabolism in both cell types (Sugiura et al. 2005, 2007, Diaz et al. 2007b). Compromised energy production, as well as reduced transfer of other products due to TZP reduction, may explain the growth disruption seen in our DTPA-treated samples. Whether DTPA treatment reduced cell-to-cell connections as a result of fewer granulosa cells surrounding the oocyte or interfered with TZP formation itself is not clear from this study and remains to be investigated.

DTPA disruption of SMAD2/3 signaling was another measure of impaired paracrine signaling between oocytes and cumulus cells. ODFs such as GDF9 and BMP15 lead to the phosphorylation and activation of SMAD2/3 pathways in cumulus cells (Kaivo-Oja et al. 2003, Li et al. 2008, Nomura et al. 2013). Unsurprisingly, pSMAD2 levels are therefore highest in the cumulus cells of large preantral follicles (Tian et al. 2010). Despite similar levels of GDF9 and BMP15 expression by oocytes in all treatments, SMAD2 activation was severely impaired by DTPA treatment (Fig. 7). Western blotting of oocyte-granulosa complexes on day 10 of culture showed a reduction of phosphorylated SMAD2. DTPA-treated cells displayed pSMAD2 levels similar to freshly collected preantral follicles, likely indicating either an inability of cumulus cells to respond to GDF9 or reduced secretion of GDF9 protein by the oocyte. These results, combined with the zinc rescue of gene expression profiling described above, suggest that the ability of oocytes to induce cumulus cell differentiation may be zinc-dependent during the preantral to antral transition.

PGOCs grown in DTPA-treated medium survived in culture but were associated with fewer granulosa cells (Fig. 1). Therefore, in order to explore oocyte-granulosa cell interactions and paracrine signaling more closely, we performed a second round of experiments on PGOCs after 4 days of culture. DTPA-treated oocytes at this stage were still surrounded by a complete layer of granulosa cells, but these granulosa cells displayed a much higher rate of apoptosis, possibly indicating lack of oocyte support (Fig. 5) and explaining the reduced somatic cell numbers in DTPA-treated samples on day 10 of culture. In addition, previous work demonstrated increased AkT activation in response to zinc (Chen et al. 2023). Signaling through Akt promotes granulosa cell proliferation and survival, and preantral follicle development (Johnson et al. 2001, Fan et al. 2008, Kawamura et al. 2013). Moreover, increased apoptosis in the cumulus cells of human IVF patients correlates with negative IVF outcomes (Lee et al. 2001). Increased apoptosis may be further evidence of impaired TGFβ signaling between the oocyte and granulosa cells, as oocyte-secreted factors protect the granulosa cells from apoptosis under ordinary conditions (Hussein et al. 2006).

In vivo dietary zinc deficiency during antral follicular development causes severe defects in ovulation and maturation of the oocyte (Tian & Diaz 2012, 2013, Tian et al. 2014). In this study, we have demonstrated that preantral follicle growth and development is impaired by treatment with the chelator DTPA and largely rescued by supplementing the chelated media with zinc. The granulosa cells surrounding the oocyte appear to have been the primary site of damage from DTPA treatment. Granulosa cells showed an increased rate of apoptosis, fewer TZPs to the oocyte, and an inability to express cumulus cell transcripts in response to oocyte-derived TGFβ signaling. Thus, loss of paracrine signaling and metabolic support between developing oocytes and surrounding granulosa cells represents a major phenotype resulting from DTPA treatment and is rescued by supplemental zinc. The findings show that zinc is an important nutrient for preantral follicle development, and, together with our previous studies showing dietary zinc deficiency causes ovarian dysfunction during the periovulatory period (Tian & Diaz 2012, 2013, Tian et al. 2014), establish adequate zinc availability as an important regulator for both somatic and germ cell development in the ovary. Thus, preconception supplementation with zinc, either in diet or during assisted reproduction in vitro, could enhance or help maintain fertility to improve reproductive health.
