Impact of Frailty Risk on Adverse Outcomes after Open Heart Valve Surgery in Older Patients: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Issei Kameda, Akio Shimizu, Kenta Ushida, Miho Shimizu, Kohei Ochiai, Yuki Kato, Ryo Momosaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study aimed to investigate the impact of preoperative frailty risk, assessed using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), on hospitalization-associated disability (HAD) and in-hospital complications in patients aged 65 years or older who underwent open heart valve surgery.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included patients in the JMDC database aged 65 years or older with valvular heart disease who underwent open heart valve surgery between April 2017 and December 2022. Patients were categorized into low (HFRS <5) and high frailty risk (HFRS ≥5) groups. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with HAD. The secondary outcomes included the proportion of patients who developed pneumonia, respiratory failure, and delirium during hospitalization. Mediation analysis was performed to examine whether preoperative frailty risk influenced the risk of HAD through postoperative complications.Results:Among 4658 patients (mean age 75.0 ± 5.5 years), 328 (7.0%) were classified as high frailty risk. The high risk group had a significantly higher proportion of HAD than the low risk group [odds ratio (OR), 3.49; 95% confidence interval (CI), 2.60–4.67; P <0.001]. Mediation analysis showed that high frailty risk was independently associated with HAD (direct effect: OR, 2.75; 95% CI, 2.01–3.76; P <0.001). The indirect effect mediated by postoperative complications was significant (OR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.11–1.43; P <0.05), accounting for 19% of the total effect.Conclusions:The preoperative frailty risk assessed by the HFRS may serve as a useful predictor of HAD and in-hospital complications in older patients undergoing open heart valve surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Frailty is theoretically defined as a clinically recognizable state of increased vulnerability resulting from aging-associated decline in reserve and function across multiple physiologic systems, such that the ability to cope with everyday or acute stressors is compromised.1) Among older hospitalized patients, frailty is associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes, including increased mortality, reduced mobility, and falls.2) Typically, frailty is assessed using a combination of functional tests, physical examination, and clinical interviews. However, in acute care settings, primary disease treatment often takes precedence, and comprehensive frailty assessments may not be systematically performed. Previous studies have reported that acute care settings present unique challenges to frailty measurement, resulting in variable implementation in routine practice.3) Recently, the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) has been proposed as a means of assessing frailty risk early during hospitalization using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10).4) The HFRS has demonstrated a predictive value for poor prognosis in several diseases,5,6,7,8) and it exhibits similar performance to other established frailty instruments, with superior predictive ability for early postoperative patient-centered outcomes and mortality in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.9) Therefore, in clinical practice, the early assessment of frailty using the HFRS is crucial for preventing poor outcomes and enabling early intervention in older patients.

Frailty is a particularly important prognostic factor following surgery in patients with valvular heart disease. Moreover, it is associated with increased postoperative mortality, increased risk of cardiovascular events, and increased hospital length of stay among patients undergoing cardiac valve surgery.10,11,12,13) Hemodynamic abnormalities associated with valvular heart disease can lead to a systemic inflammatory response, which in turn contributes to physical function deterioration. The decline in physical function is associated with increased frailty incidence and severity, which has been suggested to negatively affect postoperative outcomes and functional recovery.10) These findings suggest a strong association between frailty and outcomes in older patients with valvular heart disease, highlighting the need for appropriate frailty assessment and management in this population.

Hospitalization-associated disability (HAD) is an important factor influencing outcomes in older patients. HAD refers to new or worsening functional decline during hospitalization.14) Patients undergoing heart valve surgery are frequently subjected to physically invasive treatments, including open heart surgery, which can exacerbate functional decline. The incidence of HAD among older patients hospitalized with valvular heart disease is reported to be as high as 50%, with more than half failing to recover their prehospitalization functional capacity.15) Therefore, the early identification and intervention for risk of HAD may contribute to improved intermediate-term and long-term outcomes in this population. However, physical assessments may be difficult to perform in patients with severe valvular heart disease because of symptoms, including dyspnea. If the frailty risk assessed by HFRS can predict HAD incidence in older patients after heart valve surgery, it may contribute to the stratification of high-risk older patients. However, to the best of our knowledge, whether frailty risk assessment using HFRS can predict poor outcomes, including HAD occurrence, remains unclear.

The present study aimed to investigate the association between preoperative frailty risk assessed using the HFRS and hospitalization-associated disability in patients aged 65 years or older undergoing open heart valve surgery, using a nationwide Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) database. In addition, we examined whether in-hospital complications mediate the association between frailty risk and HAD using causal mediation analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Ethics

This retrospective cohort study used data from a hospital-based database developed by JMDC (Tokyo, Japan).16) The data provided by JMDC were de-identified and unlinked to personal information. Therefore, it was not necessary to obtain informed consent from patients. Because the JMDC database is publicly available, the ethics committee of Mie University determined that an ethical review was not required.

Data Source

The JMDC database used in this study comprises data collected from multiple health insurance associations since 2005, including DPC data. The DPC database is based on a medical payment system that was introduced in Japan in 2003. The JMDC DPC data include patient demographics, selected clinical information, admission and discharge status, diagnoses, surgeries and procedures performed, medications, and special reimbursements for specific diseases.17) The disease diagnoses were recorded by the attending physician using ICD-10 codes.

Participants

This study included patients aged 65 years or older who were diagnosed with valvular heart disease and underwent valve replacement (ICD-10 code: K555) or valvuloplasty (ICD-10 code: K554) between April 2017 and December 2022. To focus on preoperative frailty and postoperative outcomes in older patients with valvular heart disease, patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) patients who underwent surgery more than 8 days after hospital admission, (2) those who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting, and (3) those who died during hospitalization. Criterion (3) was used because HAD requires assessment of functional status at discharge, which cannot be evaluated in patients who did not survive.

Calculation of HFRS

The HFRS is a weighted score calculated by summing the points assigned to 109 ICD-10 codes.4) The score for code R50 (fever of unknown origin) is the lowest (0.1 points), whereas that for code F00 (Alzheimer’s disease) is the highest (7.1 points). In this study, the HFRS was calculated based on comorbidities at the time of admission. Gilbert et al.4) classified frailty risk based on the HFRS score into three categories: low (<5), medium (5–15), and high (>15). However, the number of patients classified as high risk was very small (n=8) when this classification was applied in this study. Therefore, the patients were categorized into two groups based on a previous study4,6,7,8,18): low frailty risk (HFRS <5) and high frailty risk (HFRS ≥5).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with HAD. In this study, the Barthel Index (BI) was used to assess activities of daily living (ADLs). The BI comprises ten items, including feeding, transfers, and toileting, and each item is scored 0, 5, 10, or 15 points based on the patient’s level of independence. The total BI score is classified into five categories: independent (80–100 points), minimally dependent (60–79 points), partially dependent (40–59 points), very dependent (20–39 points), and completely dependent (<20 points).19,20) In this study, HAD was assessed using the following formula: HAD = BI at admission − BI at discharge ≥ 5. In effect, we defined HAD as a decrease of at least 5 points on the BI score at discharge compared with the BI score in a stable condition before hospitalization, based on previous studies.21,22)

The secondary outcomes included the proportions of specific in-hospital complications: pneumonia (ICD-10 codes: J12–J18), respiratory failure (ICD-10 code: J96), and delirium (ICD-10 code: F05). These complications are commonly observed after cardiac surgery and have been reported in prior studies to be associated with impaired postoperative recovery and adverse functional outcomes.23,24)

In addition to these individual secondary outcomes, in-hospital complications were examined as potential mediators in the association between preoperative frailty and HAD. For the mediation analysis, postoperative complications were defined a priori as a composite outcome encompassing clinically relevant postoperative conditions associated with functional decline. This composite included pressure ulcers, pneumonia, aspiration pneumonia, postprocedural respiratory complications, respiratory failure, atelectasis, bowel obstruction, trauma, postoperative recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis, dysphagia, delirium, and heart failure. These conditions were identified using ICD-10 codes, and the presence of any of these complications during hospitalization was recorded for each patient.

Covariates

The covariates included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), number of hospital beds, year of admission, type of valvular heart disease, BI score at admission, and smoking index. Age was categorized according to the Japan Geriatrics Society classification as 65–74 years, 75–89 years, and ≥90 years.25) BMI was classified into four groups: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m2).26) The smoking index was calculated by multiplying the number of cigarettes smoked per day by the number of years smoked.27) The number of beds was categorized as 20–99, 100–199, 200–299, 300–499, or ≥500 beds. The year of admission (2017–2022) was included as a covariate to account for temporal changes in treatment strategies, clinical guidelines, and healthcare systems over the study period. Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics and outcomes were compared between the low and high frailty risk groups. Categorical variables were presented as count and percentage, and continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the t-test for continuous variables. Missing data were imputed using the missForest method based on random forest algorithms, which can flexibly handle datasets containing both continuous and categorical variables.28)

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between frailty risk, defined by the HFRS, and study outcomes. The models were adjusted for all covariates described above. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.

To examine whether postoperative complications mediated the association between preoperative frailty and HAD, a causal mediation analysis was performed using the Hayes PROCESS macro (Model 4).29) Bootstrap resampling with 5000 iterations was used to estimate bias-corrected 95% CIs for indirect effects. The same set of covariates was included in both the multivariable regression and mediation analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and R software (version 4.2.2; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P <0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 8091 patients aged 65 years or older who were hospitalized for valvular heart disease were identified from the JMDC database during the study period. Of these, 3433 patients were excluded for the following reasons: waiting time before surgery longer than 8 days (n = 2275), undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (n = 1049), or in-hospital death (n = 109). Consequently, 4658 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Based on the HFRS, 4330 patients (93.0%) were classified as the low frailty risk group (HFRS <5), whereas 328 patients (7.0%) were classified as the high frailty risk group (HFRS ≥5).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the association between frailty risk at admission, assessed using the HFRS, and postoperative outcomes in patients aged 65 years or older who underwent open heart valve surgery, as recorded in the DPC database. The results showed that the high frailty risk group had higher proportions of HAD and in-hospital complications than the low frailty risk group. Furthermore, the results of mediation analysis revealed that postoperative complications partially mediated the association between the HFRS and the proportion of HAD, accounting for 19% of the total effect.

Patients in the high frailty risk group at admission, as assessed by the HFRS, were independently associated with HAD occurrence following open heart valve surgery. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that preoperative frailty is a risk factor for HAD in older patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery.30) Chronic inflammation is one of the primary mechanisms of frailty, which reduces physiological reserve and impairs resistance to stressors.31,32) Chronic inflammation also delays metabolic recovery and tissue healing processes.33) Valve surgery typically involves invasive procedures, including open thoracotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, and often requires prolonged postoperative care in the intensive care unit (ICU). Frail patients may be more vulnerable to these surgical stresses, resulting in a higher risk of complications,34) prolonged bed rest, and delayed mobilization, all of which may lead to decreased ADLs and increased HAD risk. Previous studies have shown that perioperative interventions targeting frailty can improve postoperative outcomes.35,36) The present findings suggest the importance of preoperative frailty management in patients undergoing open heart valve surgery.

High frailty risk at admission was also independently associated with a greater proportion of postoperative pneumonia, respiratory failure, and delirium. These results are consistent with those of previous studies, indicating that frailty, as assessed by the HFRS, is associated with higher rates of postoperative complications.6,7,37) The HFRS is a scoring tool that quantifies frailty risk based on 109 comorbidities, including Alzheimer’s disease, gait abnormalities, and mobility disorders, which directly affect functional recovery.4) Comorbidities are known risk factors for physical decline, and preoperative impairment has been associated with postoperative infections and delirium.38,39) In addition, poor physical function is associated with poor oral hygiene, increasing pneumonia risk.40,41) Therefore, stratification of preoperative frailty risk using the HFRS may be useful in predicting postoperative complications.

The results of the mediation analysis further suggested that the preoperative frailty risk, as assessed by the HFRS, influences the risk of HAD through the occurrence of complications. This finding indicates that frail patients undergoing open heart valve surgery may be more likely to develop postoperative complications, leading to a decline in ADLs and an increased risk of HAD. Prior studies have also reported that frailty is associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications, and respiratory complications and delirium, in particular, are known to contribute to HAD.42,43) In the present study, high frailty risk was independently associated with pneumonia, respiratory failure, and delirium, supporting the notion that these complications contribute to the development of HAD. Respiratory complications may destabilize cardiorespiratory function and lead to prolonged bed rest, thereby increasing susceptibility to delirium, which is associated with cognitive decline and poor long-term prognosis, and ultimately exacerbating the risk of HAD.44,45)

These findings suggest that assessing the preoperative frailty risk using the HFRS may be effective for appropriate perioperative management. According to previous studies, preoperative nutritional interventions and rehabilitation can reduce the incidence of postoperative complications.35,36) Based on these findings, a tailored preoperative management strategy for patients with high frailty risk may help prevent HAD.

The strength of this study lies in its demonstration that preoperative assessment using the HFRS, a tool that enables automatic and rapid risk stratification based on existing ICD-10-coded data without requiring additional tests, is associated with adverse postoperative outcomes in older patients undergoing open heart valve surgery. However, this study has several limitations. First, the findings were based on the Japanese healthcare system and the DPC database; therefore, generalizability to other countries with different healthcare systems should be approached with caution. In addition, diagnoses recorded in the DPC database generally have high specificity but relatively low sensitivity, particularly for conditions that are not directly linked to reimbursement. As a result, the HFRS calculated in this study may have underestimated the true burden of preoperative frailty compared with scores derived from detailed clinical assessments or administrative data in other countries. However, the validity of administrative data based on ICD-10 codes has been supported by previous studies, suggesting that such bias was likely minimal.46) Second, some clinically important outcomes, such as in-hospital falls, were not included in this study. In the DPC database, in-hospital falls are known to be inconsistently reported and may be substantially underrecorded, making reliable identification difficult. To avoid potential misclassification bias, falls were not evaluated as an outcome. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the association between frailty and postoperative functional outcomes. Third, patients who died during hospitalization were excluded because the primary outcome, HAD, requires assessment of functional status at discharge and cannot be evaluated in non-survivors. Although this exclusion was methodologically necessary, it may have introduced selection bias and led to an underestimation of the overall impact of frailty on postoperative outcomes. Nevertheless, the number of excluded in-hospital deaths was relatively small (n = 109; approximately 2% of the cohort), and thus the influence on the main findings is likely to be limited. Finally, several important clinical data elements—such as cognitive function, mobility, muscle strength, frailty phenotype criteria, and intraoperative variables (operative time, cardiopulmonary bypass duration)—were not available in the DPC database. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that frailty assessment using the HFRS, which enables automatic and rapid risk stratification without requiring additional tests, may predict adverse postoperative outcomes, including an increased proportion of HAD and complications, in older patients undergoing open heart valve surgery. For future clinical implementation, prospective studies are needed to determine whether the frailty risk assessed by the HFRS in real-world settings can inform preoperative management and intervention planning.
