Potential outcomes of interpersonal violence for women’s artistic gymnasts: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Natalie Barker-Ruchti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify, map and provide insight into the potential outcomes former elite-level women’s gymnasts associate with their history of interpersonal violence (IV).MethodsThe study used a trauma-informed qualitative approach. The sample criterion was women’s gymnasts, who had spoken out about IV through social and/or traditional media since the release of the documentary Athlete A. Recruitment occurred purposively through social media; data were generated through online life-history interviews. The interview transcripts were analysed via a hybrid inductive/deductive thematic analysis.ResultsThe study sample included 18 former elite-level women’s artistic gymnasts and one former elite-level rhythmic gymnast, aged 20–53, from 12 countries across four continents. The former gymnasts recounted extensive polyvictimisation by their coaches during formative years, which they associated with 54 potential negative and 7 potential not categorically negative outcomes. Their descriptions of the experiences indicate extensive and complexly interconnected potential psychological, behavioural, physical and material/performance outcomes. The younger, recently retired former gymnasts described their experiences in acute terms, the older former gymnasts recounted recurring and new outcomes. The public discourse on IV in women’s gymnastics was acknowledged to have facilitated the development of not categorically negative outcomes.ConclusionThe study provides an overview of potential negative and not categorically negative outcomes of IV for women’s artistic gymnasts. The insights allow a nuanced understanding of the long-lasting impact of IV, which can raise awareness and inform education, research and care protocols for victims of IV in sport.Keywords: Disclosure, Gymnastics, Qualitative Research, Sociology, Women in sport

Introduction

The 2020 Netflix documentary Athlete A, which reported on the USA Gymnastics sex abuse scandal,1 prompted hundreds of current and former women’s artistic gymnasts to disclose experiences of psychological and physical abuse, sexual harassment and abuse and individual and organisational neglect. Their disclosures of what is today defined as interpersonal violence (IV) in sport2 shocked audiences worldwide.

A large body of social science research reveals women’s artistic gymnastics’ long history of IV: an unrealistic and harmful body ideal, leading to regular weighing, body shaming and forced and self-inflicted dietary restriction; 3–5 early specialisation, beginning as early as school entrance;6 early relocation to a regional/national training centre, often without family members; 7 8 authoritarian coach–gymnast relationships consisting of coach monitoring, gate-keeping, psychological manipulation and severe punishments; 9 10 and coaching and training methods that include forced stretching, training with pain and injuries and overtraining.11 12 Factors that have been identified to facilitate the IV are gymnasts’ young age and female gender, coaches’ afforded authority, gymnastics’ culture of intimidation and silence and organisations’ prioritisation of medals, financial gain and public image.13–18 Research also demonstrates that rhythmic gymnasts experience IV similar to that of women’s artistic gymnasts. 19

IV in sport has been identified to have significant long-lasting negative physical and mental health outcomes.2 20 21 This is particularly so for IV experienced during childhood and youth.22 Research also increasingly recognises that athletes experience polyvictimisation and that the exposure to multiple types of IV has cumulative outcomes. 23 The negative physical health outcomes have been found to include generalised negative physical health, psychosomatic symptoms/somatisation and sports injuries that restrict or inhibit movement capacity.23–25 The negative mental health outcomes include compulsive behaviours (eg, eating disorder; trichotillomania),26 lack of a positive sense of self,27 lower subjective well-being,28 maladaptive coping strategies,29 mental health problems (eg, anxiety; depression), post-traumatic and dissociative symptomatology,30 31 relational problems,32 substance abuse33 and suicide ideation.34 Experiences of IV have also been found to lead to outcomes that are not categorically negative, such as post-traumatic growth and meaning making.2 21 Recognising IV and being able to speak (out) about experiences of IV have been shown to facilitate the recovery process.35 36 Tuakli-Wosornu et al’s recent systematic scoping review on potential outcomes of IV in sport for athletes offers a seminal table of 62 potential negative psychological, behavioural, physical and material/performance outcomes and 4 potential not categorically negative psychological and behavioural outcomes.2 With regard to women’s artistic gymnastics, emerging research on the potential negative outcomes that gymnasts may experience because of IV while training in this sport includes unhealthy relationships with food, low self-worth, depression, negative body image, post-traumatic stress and self-harm.37–39 Not categorically negative outcomes have also been identified.40 However, no research has generated an overview of potential outcomes of IV in women’s artistic gymnastics.

The focus of this study was to create an overview of potential outcomes of IV for women’s artistic gymnasts. To achieve this, I drew on a body of qualitative data that was generated through a study that had given voice to former gymnasts with histories of IV. The data were analysed for the following objectives: (1) identify the potential outcomes that former elite-level gymnasts described to experience; (2) map the identified potential outcomes using Tuakli-Wosornu et al’s category model;2 and (3) provide insight into former gymnasts’ experiences of the potential outcomes.

Methods and analysis

The study was designed and conducted as a trauma-informed qualitative study.41 An extended description of the methods and analysis, following the COREQ (Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research) reporting criteria,42 is available in the online supplemental appendix.

Recruitment and data collection

Recruitment occurred purposively in early 2021 through a post on Twitter and international gymnastics blogs. 21 former gymnasts reached out, of which 19 consented to a semistructured online life-history interview using the Zoom application (table 1). The interviews were 90–240 min long, audio-recorded and hand transcribed.

Thematic analysis

I analysed the data using a hybrid inductive/deductive thematic analysis.43 The inductive analytic step involved identifying the interview transcript sections in which the former gymnasts described potential outcomes in the form of (1) self-reported medical diagnoses or (2) experiences and behaviours. The deductive analytic step involved the mapping of the identified transcript sections using Tuakli-Wosornu et al’s category model.2

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

The study prioritised women’s gymnastics because of the sport’s documented history of IV as well as the recent movement of current and former women’s gymnasts speaking out about IV. The purposive recruitment strategy did not prescribe demographic criteria, and the social media recruitment strategy, the online interview method and the option to hold the interview in the participants’ preferred language aimed for an inclusive research protocol. In the analysis, the vulnerability and precarity that research has reported to be commonplace in women’s artistic gymnastics were considered to identify potential outcomes.

Results

Sample

The sample includes 18 former women’s artistic gymnasts and one former rhythmic gymnast, aged 20–53 years (average 34.6 years), from 12 countries across Australasia (5), Europe (5), North America (6) and South America (3) and speaking five languages. They had each participated at the elite level of women’s artistic and rhythmic gymnastics (average 10.9 years) and had retired between 2 and 39 years ago (average 18.7 years ago). 16 of the 19 former gymnasts reported a complex picture of long-term polyvictimisation (table 2). Three also described sexual abuse, which resulted in the imprisonment of two coaches. One gymnast believed that she had suffered sexual abuse, but memory loss has prevented a police report. All 19 former gymnasts identified their coaches as the perpetrators of the IV. Nine also identified officials, other coaches and parents as neglectful or negligent (table 2). 13 former gymnasts had at the time of the study disclosed experiences of IV through social and/or traditional media and/or through available reporting mechanisms (eg, investigation; ombudsman; police). Six used the research interview to speak out for the first time.

Psychological outcomes

The cognitive outcomes entailed memory loss, dissociation, flashback, low self-confidence and difficulty concentrating. Memory loss was described to have occurred in response to stressful experiences, such as sexual abuse and severe neglect before reaching adulthood. Dissociation was described with metaphors such as ‘the lights going out’, ‘vanishing’ and ‘cancelling’. Some dissociation was active, through ‘trying to forget’, ‘blanking or cancelling out things’ and ‘repressing memories’ and was supported by behaviours such as throwing out gymnastics memorabilia, avoiding contact with the gymnastics community and not disclosing IV, even to close family (see behavioural outcomes). Dissociation was also described as ‘not being connected’, ‘numbed’ and ‘not being able to feel’. With the speaking out movement in gymnastics, the former gymnasts told of how they recollected memories, which others’ disclosures supported. This recollection was related to experiencing flashbacks.

Low self-confidence was noticeable in how the former gymnasts described themselves (eg, ‘as a nobody’), referred to their gymnastics achievements (‘I didn’t accomplish anything’) or by questioning the impact that they could have on changing gymnastics (‘why would they [gymnastics organisations] listen to me?’). The former gymnasts also associated low self-confidence with mental health problems (eg, anxiety; low mood) and mistrust.

The emotional outcomes related to the former gymnasts’ perceptions of gymnastics, negative emotional reactions and concerns regarding their speaking out about IV. Several of the former gymnasts described having no positive gymnastics memory and/or spoke of their disapproval of gymnastics. In connection with the former, the former gymnasts expressed feelings of betrayal, loss, failure because no one protected them and vulnerability as an athlete and when speaking (out) about their experiences of IV. Several also described guilt: for having quit gymnastics, for having invalidated other gymnasts’ disclosures when they had not yet recognised IV and not speaking out earlier to protect other gymnasts. Anger, irrationality and volatile mood state were also mentioned by several of the former gymnasts, especially when coaches and/or gymnastics organisations denied their experiences of IV.

The former gymnasts also outlined that they have, since the release of Athlete A, been dealing with emotional reactions regarding a possible disclosure. Most described that up until the documentary’s release, they had not known or were unsure that their experiences entailed IV. With the recognition that they were, the former gymnasts questioned the truthfulness and validity of their experiences and carefully considered whether to speak out. They described lengthy processes of verifying their memories and interpretations of what had happened to them (through fact-checking with others; reading old diary entries; writing down memories), and concerns that the speaking out could harm their mental health, not generate desired changes to gymnastics and result in gaslighting. Concern was also described regarding parents and others (eg, partners; children), and the coaches that had been violent towards them. Lastly, several former gymnasts revealed that they fear that a disclosure would negatively impact professional opportunities.

The mental health outcomes entailed depression, suicidality, anxiety and anxiety attacks, panic disorder, body image disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder. These potential outcomes, also in connection with cognitive outcomes, were described to have resulted in the need for support, such as through counselling, medication and therapy. One woman described that she had spent time in a trauma clinic and relied on a service dog; one was spending time at a clinic for eating disorders.

Behavioural outcomes

The most frequently described relational outcome was mistrust. The former gymnasts also spoke of disturbed relationships with parents and/or other family members because of the guilt their parents feel for not protecting their daughters, the former gymnasts’ disappointment that their parents had missed protecting them from IV and parental disappointment on their daughters’ deselection from elite-level or retirement from gymnastics. The former gymnasts also related the relational outcomes to avoiding the gymnastics community. Those who were mothers described fear that their children might experience IV in gymnastics/sport, which they linked to helicopter parenting and avoiding placing their children in gymnastics. One woman described ‘a really violent relationship with a partner’, which took several years to leave.

The most common non-relational outcome was eating disorder. Two former gymnasts (aged 23 and 25) revealed that they were still battling an eating disorder. One former gymnast recounted developing the disorder after retiring from gymnastics, and two described that a child of theirs had developed an eating disorder. In terms of the other non-relational outcomes, the former gymnasts indicated that they had experienced a general disorientation after retirement, which they related to unhealthy risk-taking, drive to control and perfection, peripatetic behaviour and conformity to violent gymnastics norms.

Physical outcomes

Chronic pain was the most common experience. Physical deformations, such as forced hyperextended knees (described by the former gymnasts to have been caused by coaches repetitively stretching their knee joints by sitting on their knees while their heels were placed on boxes), spondylolisthesis and arthritis/arthrosis (described by the former gymnasts to have been caused by harmful (too quick) skill progression, overtraining, training with injuries and insufficient medical treatment/rehabilitation of injuries), were mentioned as the main sources of the chronic pain. The pain, in turn, was associated by the former gymnasts with experiencing physical debilitation and joint/limb movement restriction and/or restricting physical activity. The former gymnasts also described several psychosomatic symptoms, including tachycardia, sleep disorder and chronic fatigue syndrome.

Material/performance outcomes

The former gymnasts described poor school/university performance, linking this outcome to feelings of betrayal, loss, failure and poor mental health. Two former gymnasts also described that they had found it difficult to choose a professional direction after retiring from gymnastics and as a result, had chosen a low-level/low-paid professional direction. Three former gymnasts outlined that they had lost gymnastics board/coaching/judging positions because of their disillusionment with gymnastics.

The emotional outcomes included a strong sense of right and wrong and post-traumatic growth. The former was described to have influenced the former gymnasts to stand up for the vulnerable. Post-traumatic growth was considered to have been possible because of support services (eg, therapy) and entailed learning about the self, gaining a brighter outlook on life and the future and influencing change in their national gymnastics context.

The relational outcome that the former gymnasts recounted was victim/survivor network. They spoke of how they had not had contact with their peers from their training days, and that they appreciated the reconnections that had become possible because of the speaking out movement. The former gymnasts connected this outcome with the non-relational outcome of liberation from silence through having voice and getting recognition and validation. Much of the awareness-raising/education and advocacy and activism that they described took place nationally and emerged from the victim/survivor networks and post-traumatic growth. In turn, these outcomes were described to enhance their networks and growth.

Discussion

The experiences described by this study’s sample of 19 former women’s gymnasts, who identified as victims of IV, generated 54 potential negative outcomes across the psychological, behavioural, physical and material/performance categories. Five emotional outcomes were specifically related by the former gymnasts to the recent movement of athletes (publicly) disclosing experiences of IV (last five emotional outcomes in table 3). The former gymnasts’ experiences also generated seven potential not categorically negative outcomes in the psychological, behavioural and material/performance categories.

The mapping of the 54 potential negative and not categorically negative outcomes substantiates just over a third of Tuakli-Wosornu et al’s potential outcomes of IV in sport for athletes. It also adds 17 potential negative outcomes: no positive gymnastics memory; disapproval of gymnastics; the outcomes relating to the speaking out movement; violent relationship; the outcomes relating to former gymnasts’ own children; peripatetic behaviour; conformity to violent gymnastics norms; (reconstructive) surgery; and the six material/performance outcomes. The former gymnasts’ descriptions also support two of Tuakli-Wosornu et al’s not categorically negative outcomes and add three such potential outcomes: strong sense of right and wrong; liberation from silence through having voice and getting recognition and validation; and the material/performance outcome. The qualitative approach to the study, the study’s timing during the first year of the #gymnastalliance movement, and the former gymnasts’ status as retired athletes may explain some of the additional potential outcomes to the Tuakli-Wosornu et al category model.

In terms of how the former gymnasts experience the potential outcomes, their descriptions reveal that the potential negative outcomes are extensive and complexly interconnected. The extent and complexity were recounted by all 19 former gymnasts, regardless of types and range of IV, geographical locations, gymnastics generations (ie, 1980s–2010s) and performance levels. This finding substantiates women’s artistic gymnastics’ history of IV documented by the available social science literature3–18 and reflects existing research on the cumulative impact that polyvictimisation, especially during formative years, may have for athletes.23 Lastly, the experiences shared by the former rhythmic gymnast support literature showing that IV occurs in similar ways to women’s artistic gymnastics.19

Where the former gymnasts’ recounted experiences differed is in terms of the potential outcomes’ temporality. The eight interviewees aged 20–26, whose retirement was 2–9 years ago, spoke of their potential outcomes in more acute terms, especially with regards to low self-confidence, mental health and eating disorders. A key consequence of this acuteness was described to require a greater need for support, such as through regular therapy, medication, admission to a trauma/eating disorder clinic and a service dog. The 11 older former gymnasts (32–53 years), who described greater distance from gymnastics because of alternative identities, meaningful professional lives, support beyond that of their parents and financial stability, spoke of the potential outcomes in recurring or new terms. New potential outcomes that they described related to being mothers and worsening physical health, the latter, which was described to include chronic pain, (reconstructive) surgery and arthritis/arthrosis, were related to increased medical and therapy cost and inability to work. The differences in temporality support research on the long-lasting impact that IV can have.2 20–22 It also indicates that the impact occurs on an evolving continuum of potential outcomes with IV acting as a ‘gift’ that keeps on giving. Lastly, the two former gymnasts’ descriptions of their children developing an eating disorder may indicate intergenerational impact.

The former gymnasts’ descriptions of the potential negative and not categorically negative outcomes also confirm that their ability to speak (out) about IV and potential outcomes is a result of them recognising themselves as victims.35 36 The former gymnasts noted that it was the recent public discourse surrounding IV in women’s artistic gymnastics, sparked by the USA Gymnastics sex abuse scandal, that led to this realisation. Although they acknowledged the public discourse as triggering, they described that hearing or reading other survivors’ testimonies was especially impactful. They also spoke of their recognition as a catalyst for finding voice and beginning the recovery process (see not categorically negative outcomes). This finding highlights the positive role that public conversations about IV in sport can play in supporting victims’ recognition and recovery, particularly if the discourse includes survivor testimonies.35 36

Finally, the trauma-informed qualitative interview study presented in this article demonstrates the contribution such research can make to understand the potential outcomes of IV for athletes. The descriptions of potential outcomes that the former gymnasts provided afford a nuanced understanding of what it might be like to live with the impact of IV. This insight into the lived experiences of potential outcomes is important in several ways: It raises awareness about the long-lasting impact that IV can have, complements the symptoms perspective of existing research, offers an educational resource and increases pressure on gymnastics and sport organisations to acknowledge and address IV. For those supporting victims of IV and those designing and implementing policies and actions to safeguard athletes, the findings confirm the need for trauma- and violence-informed care and practice, a multidisciplinary approach and the socioecological perspective that Tuakli-Wosornu et al advocate.2

Lastly, for future research, this study’s overview of potential negative and not categorically negative outcomes offers a valuable basis for studies into the long-lasting impact of IV in sport during childhood and youth. It can inform research into the experiences of other gymnast populations and athletes involved in aesthetic and early specialisation sports, the evolvement of potential outcomes over time, and the development of prevalence measurements, causality studies and evidence-based recovery frameworks. Future research should prioritise approaches that amplify the voices of former athletes, examine the continuum of evolving potential outcomes and capture what it means to live with its consequences. Such efforts are essential to deepen understanding of the lasting impact of IV and to guide the development of effective care protocols.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study that aimed to identify, map and provide insight into the potential outcomes former women’s gymnasts associate with their history of IV. Using quality criteria that applies to qualitative research,44 the strengths of this study include the research’s relevance and timeliness (worthy topic; significant contribution), the intercontinental and multigenerational sample and the trauma-informed approach giving voice to former gymnasts that identify as victims of IV (rich rigour; ethical; meaningful coherence); and the data’s volume, richness and depth, the hybrid inductive/deductive data analysis, which included expert review panels and the detailed description of the methods and analysis (credibility; resonance).

The study’s limitations are at least fourfold: the sample did not include gymnasts from Africa and Asia, nor from Eastern Europe, the latter two regions with important gymnastics populations, or current/former gymnasts, who do not yet identify as victims of IV and/or were not (yet) willing to speak (out) about their experiences. The open-ended nature of the life-history interview method did not specifically ask the former gymnasts to list and describe potential outcomes, which may have resulted in the former gymnasts not describing all potential outcomes. In addition, the self-reported medical diagnoses were not cross-checked with medical records and given that the study was not conducted from an expert clinical or psychological perspective, it is possible that potential outcomes were missed or that not everyone would agree with the presented mapping. Lastly, this study was conducted by a single researcher, which could introduce bias in the interpretation and analysis of the interviews. While the experience of this researcher and the consistency of the narratives shared by the former gymnasts provide some validation of the findings, future investigations could benefit from larger, multidisciplinary research teams.

Conclusions

The sampled former gymnasts described extensive polyvictimisation during formative years and complexly interconnected potential psychological, behavioural, physical and material/performance outcomes. The younger, recently retired former gymnasts described their experiences of potential outcomes in more acute terms than the older former gymnasts, who spoke of the impact in terms of recurring and new potential outcomes. The former gymnasts recounted recognising their history of IV because of the recent public discourse on IV in women’s gymnastics, which enabled them to speak (out) about their experiences and develop some not categorically negative outcomes. To ensure that former gymnasts can recognise experiences of and begin to recover from IV, it is crucial that researchers and carers maintain the public discourse on IV in gymnastics/sport. It is further crucial that gymnastics/sport organisations and carers develop, provide and extend trauma-informed treatment options.
