Does the Efficacy, Durability, and Safety of Faricimab for Diabetic Macular Edema in Randomized Trials Translate into Real-World Settings?
Authors: Tunde Peto, Stela Vujosevic, Veeral Sheth, Amanda Downey, Kara Gibson, Andreas Pollreisz
Published: 2025

Commentary

Introduction

Diabetic macular edema (DME) remains a major cause of visual impairment, with global prevalence expected to increase to around 24 million affected individuals by 2030 [1]. While intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monotherapies, such as aflibercept and ranibizumab, have transformed outcomes for patients with DME, the unmet need for these patients remains high given the need for frequent injections [1–3]. The high treatment burden of these anti-VEGF therapies on patients, carers, and healthcare systems can lead to suboptimal long-term outcomes and reduced quality of life; more efficacious and durable therapies are needed to help alleviate this burden [1–3].

Faricimab is a bispecific monoclonal antibody inhibiting both angiopoietin (Ang)-2 and VEGF-A which is currently approved for treating DME. In the phase 3 YOSEMITE and RHINE clinical trials, faricimab demonstrated non-inferior visual acuity gains, improved anatomical outcomes, and a comparable safety profile versus aflibercept 2 mg at 1 year, all of which were maintained to 2 years [4, 5]. The efficacy and safety of faricimab were assessed for a further 2 years in RHONE-X, the long-term extension of YOSEMITE and RHINE, with preliminary results demonstrating that visual and anatomical benefits were maintained and the safety profile remained consistent [6].

While clinical trials provide rigorous assessments of new therapies under controlled conditions, real-world data are essential to validate their findings in diverse patient populations with varying adherence and healthcare access. Previous studies on anti-‍VEGF monotherapies have shown that real-world outcomes in patients with DME often fall short of those seen in clinical trials [7, 8]. However, the real-world evidence collected to date on faricimab in DME has shown effectiveness and safety outcomes that are largely consistent with those reported in the phase 3 studies [9]. This commentary represents a sponsored expert opinion.

Role of Ang-2 in Disease Control

DME pathogenesis is multifactorial, whereby vascular leakage is synergistically driven by both Ang-2 and VEGF-A [10, 11]. While anti-VEGFs only inhibit processes mediated by the VEGF pathway, the additional inhibition of Ang-2 with faricimab may further reduce vascular destabilization and the associated leakage and inflammation [12–14]. Post hoc results from YOSEMITE and RHINE demonstrated greater resolution of macular leakage, a biomarker of vascular instability, with a smaller median leakage area (3.6 vs 7.6 mm2) and a greater proportion of eyes achieving resolution of leakage (28% vs 15%) at week 16 with faricimab than aflibercept 2 mg [15]. Eyes treated with faricimab versus aflibercept 2 mg also demonstrated greater decreases in mean hyperreflective foci (HRF) volumes, a biomarker of inflammation, to week 48 in YOSEMITE and RHINE [16]. Furthermore, recent preliminary conference communications of post hoc YOSEMITE and RHINE analyses have reported greater reductions with faricimab versus aflibercept 2 mg in generalized leakage and microaneurysm count at week 16, and greater decreases in mean hard exudate volume and risk of epiretinal membrane (ERM) formation to week 100, suggesting that faricimab may promote vascular stability and result in long-term reductions in vascular leakage and fibrosis [17–19]. Further validation of these findings is required given that conference communications are hypothesis-generating in nature.

Real-world studies investigating imaging biomarkers with combined Ang-‍2 and VEGF-A inhibition have also indicated improvements in macular leakage, hard exudates, and HRF volumes with faricimab, in both treatment-naïve eyes and eyes resistant to prior treatment [20, 21]. Recent case studies have further highlighted reductions in microaneurysms, a characteristic feature of DME associated with macular leakage, following four consecutive injections of faricimab [21, 22]. As greater macular leakage area and HRF volumes are correlated with higher retinal fluid volumes and worse functional outcomes, the observed reduction in macular leakage may indicate an improvement in overall disease control [23, 24]. These findings are limited by the small sample sizes inherent in case series, the absence of control groups, and potential biases introduced by the lack of central reading centers and standardized imaging protocols in these real-world studies. Despite these limitations, the alignment of early real-world findings with post hoc results from YOSEMITE and RHINE offers supportive evidence that dual Ang-2 and VEGF-A inhibition with faricimab not only improves anatomical and drying outcomes, but may also provide enhanced disease control compared with anti-VEGF monotherapy. Further validation in larger, controlled studies is warranted.

Faricimab’s dual mechanism of action may explain the favorable and consistent anatomical outcomes seen across clinical trials and real-world studies. Across YOSEMITE and RHINE, patients treated with faricimab 6 mg every 8 weeks (Q8W) or via a treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen demonstrated greater mean reductions in central subfield thickness (CST) over 1 year ( – 187.6 to – 206.6 µm across faricimab arms) compared to patients treated with aflibercept 2 mg Q8W ( – 170.1 to – 170.3 µm) [4]. Furthermore, in support of the role of combined Ang-2 and VEGF-A inhibition in improving drying outcomes beyond VEGF-A inhibition alone, a higher proportion of patients treated with faricimab achieved absence of intraretinal fluid (IRF) at 1 year (faricimab Q8W: 41–46%; faricimab T&E: 33–41%; aflibercept 2 mg: 22–27%) [25]. Although YOSEMITE and RHINE mostly recruited patients who were treatment-naïve, post hoc analyses indicate that CST and IRF reductions were comparable among patients who had received prior treatment [25].

Complementing these findings, there is a growing volume of real-world data on the anatomical and drying benefits of faricimab across both treatment-naïve patients and patients switching from anti-VEGFs. A recent presentation of 2-year results from FARETINA-DME, a large retrospective real-world study using data from the American Academy of Ophthalmologists’ IRIS® Registry, showed mean CST reductions from baseline of 33.2 µm and 38.9 µm in treatment-naïve and switch eyes with DME, respectively, following seven faricimab injections [26]. Similarly, early results from the multicenter prospective TAHOE study demonstrated a mean CST reduction of 45.3 µm across treatment-naïve and switch eyes after three faricimab injections, while initial data from the prospective VOYAGER study showed a mean CST reduction of 150.4 µm and increase in the proportion of eyes without IRF from 1.8% to 27.3% after 6 months of faricimab [27, 28]. In addition, there is emerging real-world evidence for the anatomical benefits of faricimab in patients who are difficult-to-treat, with a retrospective interventional case series of 51 patients with aflibercept-resistant DME reporting improvements in CST 4 months after switching to faricimab, which were maintained to 12 months (mean CST reduction: 59.6 µm) [29].

Alongside greater anatomical and drying benefits, the anti-Ang-2 activity of faricimab may lead to more rapid disease control. Post hoc analyses of YOSEMITE and RHINE revealed that median time to first absence of IRF was 40 weeks shorter and achieved with fewer injections in the faricimab arms versus the aflibercept 2 mg arm (median injections: faricimab Q8W, 9; faricimab T&E, 7; aflibercept 2 mg, 13) [25]. Similarly, in preliminary presentations of results from the real-world FARETINA-DME and TAHOE studies, improvements in CST were seen as early as after the first dose of faricimab [28, 30].

While promising, much of the available real-world data on anatomical and drying outcomes of faricimab treatment in DME are limited by relatively short follow-up durations (up to 1 year), along with heterogeneous methodologies, patient populations, treatment histories, and endpoints. Longer periods of follow-up from larger real-world studies, such as FARETINA-DME and VOYAGER, will be essential to determine whether these emerging trends remain consistent with findings from YOSEMITE and RHINE and are sustained over time across diverse patient subpopulations.

Durability of Faricimab

In addition to faricimab’s anatomical and drying benefits, results from YOSEMITE and RHINE demonstrated that dual Ang-2 and VEGF-A inhibition with faricimab may offer improved durability versus anti-VEGF monotherapies. To reflect real-world practice, faricimab T&E dosing was implemented as one of the investigational arms in YOSEMITE and RHINE, with interval extension and reduction criteria based on CST and best-corrected visual acuity [4]. At least 70% of patients in the faricimab T&E arm achieved dosing intervals of every 12 weeks (Q12W) or greater and at least 50% achieved intervals of every 16 weeks (Q16W) at 1 year, while maintaining comparable vision gains and greater anatomical improvements versus patients treated with aflibercept 2 mg [4]. The proportions of patients on extended faricimab dosing further increased at 2 years of treatment, with 78% achieving Q12W or greater dosing intervals and 62% achieving Q16W dosing intervals [5].

Faricimab’s durability is also supported by emerging real-world evidence. Recent results from the FARETINA-DME (IRIS® registry data) and FARWIDE-DME (UK Medisoft Electronic Medical Records System data) studies showed lower mean numbers of faricimab injections in months 7–12 compared with months 1–6 across large cohorts of both treatment-naïve patients and those who switched from prior anti-VEGF monotherapy; vision and anatomical gains were maintained to 12 months [26, 30]. Small-scale observational studies have also suggested that patients with difficult-to-treat DME may benefit from the durability of faricimab. In one retrospective case series, extended faricimab treatment intervals were seen across most switch patients who were previously treated with aflibercept 2 mg [31]. In addition, a separate case series reported that a clinically significant proportion (39%) of patients who were resistant to aflibercept 2 mg, and had previously received ≥ 8 aflibercept injections within 12 months, achieved ≥ Q8W dosing intervals at 12 months after switching to faricimab T&E [29]. Reporting of faricimab’s real-world durability beyond 1 year in large-scale studies, especially in treatment-naïve patients and those resistant to prior anti-VEGF treatment, is currently limited. Longer-term follow-up data from the FARETINA-DME and FARWIDE-DME studies are expected and will provide further insight into faricimab’s real-world durability in the future.

Importantly, early interval extensions with faricimab may be linked to the rapid disease control conferred by Ang-2 inhibition [25]. Post hoc analyses from YOSEMITE and RHINE demonstrated that more patients treated with faricimab with resolution of macular leakage at week 16 (i.e., after four monthly doses of faricimab) achieved extended dosing at 1 year versus patients with high macular leakage [15]. Moreover, resolution of macular leakage at week 16 coincided with better anatomical outcomes at 1 year, suggesting that early fluid resolution may lead to improved longer-term outcomes; these exploratory results require validation in further studies.

When considered alongside pre-specified durability outcomes from YOSEMITE and RHINE, the growing body of evidence from post hoc analyses and real-world studies collectively supports the potential of dual Ang-2 and VEGF-A inhibition with faricimab to confer extended treatment durability over anti-VEGF monotherapy. The durable efficacy seen with faricimab may offer the opportunity for early interval extensions and lower injection frequency for patients with DME in real-world clinical practice, which could help to reduce treatment burden and lead to better long-term outcomes compared with anti-VEGF monotherapy. With the increasing demand on health services as DME prevalence rises [1], the durability of faricimab could also help reduce healthcare resource usage and improve clinic capacity [32].

Safety of Faricimab

Faricimab has consistently demonstrated a favorable safety profile across clinical trials and heterogeneous real-world populations of patients with DME [5, 9]. In YOSEMITE and RHINE, ocular adverse events were mostly mild or moderate in severity and incidences were comparable between faricimab and aflibercept 2 mg treatment through 2 years [5]; real-world safety outcomes are consistent with these data [9]. In FARETINA-DME and FARWIDE-DME, rates of intraocular inflammation and endophthalmitis reported at 1 year were low and in line with those observed in clinical trials [26, 30]. Longer follow-up in large studies is required to capture any potential long-term or rare adverse outcomes in the real world.

Conclusions

With faricimab, consistent and favorable outcomes are being observed across clinical trials and the growing number of real-world studies. The totality of evidence from both clinical trials and emerging results from real-world studies provides robust support for the sustained disease control, extended treatment durability, and comparable safety of faricimab versus anti-VEGF monotherapy across heterogeneous populations of patients with DME. Dual Ang-2 and VEGF-A inhibition with faricimab therefore represents a promising treatment option for patients with DME with the potential to become standard of care, particularly if the emerging trends observed in real-world outcomes are validated as longer-term data become available.
