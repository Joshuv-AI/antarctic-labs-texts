Structural insights into single-pass transmembrane receptor GC-A activation by distinct antihypertensive antibodies
Authors: Shian Liu, Onorina Manzo, Jinan Wang, Lan Zhu, Fu Xiao, Yi-Chen Su, Devanshu Kurre, Wei Liu, Yinglong Miao, Annarita Di Lorenzo, Xin-Yun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe single-pass transmembrane receptor guanylyl cyclase A (GC-A), also known as natriuretic peptide receptor A (NPR-A) or NPR1, regulates blood pressure through vasodilation and natriuresis, making it a promising therapeutic target for hypertension and heart failure. We describe two monoclonal antibodies, XX16 and REGN5308, that differentially activate GC-A. Using cryo-electron microscopy and molecular dynamics simulations, we reveal that XX16 stabilizes GC-A in an active conformation even without its ligand ANP, whereas REGN5308 requires ANP to fully promote receptor activation. Both antibodies increase ANP binding affinity to GC-A and enhance GC-A-mediated cGMP signaling, although XX16 exerts a stronger stabilizing influence on ATP and GTP binding. In a mouse model of obesity-induced hypertension, XX16 treatment significantly reduces blood pressure, underscoring its therapeutic potential. These findings outline the structural and functional basis of GC-A activation by antibody positive allosteric modulators, offering strategies for durable antihypertensive therapies and improved management of cardiovascular diseases.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Antibody therapy, Peptide hormones, Heart failure, Structural biology

Introduction

Heart failure remains one of the most prevalent causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, driven in large part by chronic dysfunction of the heart’s ability to pump blood effectively1,2. Elevated intracardiac pressures and myocyte stretch in heart failure trigger the release of counter-regulatory natriuretic peptides, which activate GC-A to lower venous pressures, promote vasodilation, and enhance natriuresis and diuresis3–9. Although recombinant natriuretic peptide infusions were initially developed to harness these physiological benefits, their short circulating half-lives have limited broader clinical utility. Moreover, genetic studies have highlighted GC-A as a validated target for blood pressure (BP) modulation and potential heart failure therapies, supporting the rationale for developing long-lasting GC-A activators10–12.

Recent strategies to enhance or mimic natriuretic peptide signaling include the development of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that bind and activate GC-A11. These mAbs can bind membrane-bound receptors to induce conformational changes similar to (or augmenting) endogenous ligands, offering a durable pharmacological effect. Still, the mechanistic and structural details by which these antibodies engage and stabilize GC-A’s active conformation remain unclear. Characterizing these interactions is paramount for understanding how mAbs can fully or partially recapitulate the receptor’s native signaling pathways—potentially unlocking treatments for conditions such as heart failure, hypertension, and related cardiovascular disorders.

Here, we describe two monoclonal antibodies, XX16 and REGN5308, each with a distinct profile in modulating GC-A13,14. By applying complementary approaches—cellular signaling cGMP assays, cryo-EM, Gaussian accelerated Molecular Dynamics (GaMD), and an in vivo model of obesity-induced hypertension—we provide a detailed account of how these two antibodies differ in their molecular interactions with GC-A, the conformational states they induce, and their respective influences on the binding of ANP, ATP, and GTP to GC-A.

Our findings reveal that XX16 acts as a potent activator, capable of driving GC-A into an active conformation even in the absence of its endogenous ligand ANP. REGN5308, on the other hand, exerts a more moderate agonistic effect that is most pronounced when sub-saturating amounts of ANP are present. XX16 binds deeply within the extracellular domain (ECD) dimer interface of GC-A, stabilizing an active receptor conformation and slowing ANP dissociation to a greater extent than REGN5308. REGN5308, meanwhile, can adopt multiple conformations, requiring ANP to foster a fully active state. Functional studies demonstrate that XX16 robustly lowers BP in a mouse model of diet-induced obesity and hypertension, indicating a potential clinical application for antibody-based GC-A activators. Taken together, these results not only deepen our understanding of GC-A regulation but also suggest potential strategies for creating long-lasting therapeutics targeting this essential receptor for BP and fluid-volume homeostasis.

Results

Stimulation of GC-A–mediated cellular signaling by two distinct antibodies

We generated constructs encoding the Fab domains of XX16 and REGN5308—two anti-GC-A monoclonal antibodies—and purified the recombinant Fab fragments. These Fabs were then incubated with HEK293 cells stably expressing full-length human GC-A, and intracellular cGMP levels were measured to assess receptor activation (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Table 1). XX16 was able to activate GC-A at relatively low concentrations, whereas REGN5308 required higher concentrations to achieve comparable increases in cGMP (Fig. 1b). In the presence of sub-saturating concentrations of ANP, however, both XX16 and REGN5308 amplified cellular signaling to a similar extent (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1). The combination of the antibodies and sub-saturating concentrations of ANP produced synergistic effect (Fig. 1b). Collectively, these data demonstrate that both antibodies can effectively activate GC-A, albeit with distinct binding properties and potencies.

The receptor comprises an N-terminal extracellular domain (ECD), a juxtamembrane linker (JL), a single-pass transmembrane helix (TM), and an intracellular domain (ICD) that includes a protein kinase-homology domain (KHD), an amphipathic α-helical region (forming a parallel coiled-coil (CC) in a receptor dimer), and a C-terminal guanylyl cyclase catalytic domain (GCD). b Cellular cGMP assays for the activation of full-length human GC-A expressed in HEK293 cells by the two antibodies without or with sub-saturating concentrations (40 or 75 pM) of ANP. Data are represented as mean ± SD of three experiments.

Structural basis of activation of GC-A by antibody XX16

To investigate how XX16 activates GC-A, we purified the recombinant full-length wild-type human GC-A from insect cells as previously described in ref. 6. Recombinant Fab fragments of XX16 were also generated and purified from insect cells. We then assembled GC-A–XX16 complexes in the presence or absence of ANP (Supplementary Fig. 1) and subjected them to cryo-EM and single-particle analysis (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Both the GC-A–XX16 and GC-A–ANP–XX16 structures reached atomic resolution, enabling side-chain modeling for almost the entire ECD of GC-A and the variable domains of XX16 (Fig. 2a, b). No dissociation of XX16 from GC-A was observed in the cryo grids in either sample.

Structures of XX16-bound GC-A without and with ANP.

a, b cartoon representation of GC-A-XX16 in the absence (a) or presence (b) of ANP. c, d The front (c) and side (d) views of two complexes overlayed. e The key amino acid interactions between the CDRs from XX16 heavy chain and the top region of ECD dimer of GC-A. f Comparison of the active GC-A–XX16 complex conformation with the inactive state of GC-A.

Remarkably, both complexes exhibited nearly identical architectures, with XX16 binding at a 1:2 stoichiometry on the upper region of the GC-A ECD dimer (Fig. 2c, d). The two structures closely resemble the ANP-bound active state of GC-A6 (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In particular, XX16 alone is sufficient to stabilize GC-A in an active conformation (Fig. 2a), a notable contrast to GC-A alone, which adopts an inactive state in the absence of ANP6 (Fig. 2f). Thus, XX16 recapitulates the full activation normally conferred by ANP.

To understand this activating mechanism, we analyzed how XX16 engages the ECDs of GC-A. The complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of XX16—primarily from its heavy chain (HC) and, to a lesser extent, from its light chain (LC)—play a crucial role (Fig. 2e). Specifically, the CDR2 and CDR3 of the XX16 HC penetrate deeply into one ECD monomer’s central cleft and the GC-A dimer interface (Fig. 2e), while the LC of XX16 associates with the opposing ECD, orienting its CDRs toward the solvent (Fig. 2f). Binding by XX16 promotes both a compaction of the two ECD domains at the lower region of the ANP-binding pocket (Fig. 2f, front view) and a twisting motion of one ECD relative to the other (Fig. 2f, side and bottom views), same as the compaction-twist activation model of GC-A by ANP6.

The CDR2 of the XX16 HC, although short, features N56 and Y57 deeply embedded in the β-sheet core of the GC-A ECD (Fig. 2e), stabilizing the XX16–GC-A interaction. In contrast, the longer CDR3 of XX16 HC interacts more extensively: Y107 and S106 insert between two helices in one ECD of GC-A, whereas M103, I104, and Y101 shift the adjacent helix of the second GC-A ECD to occupy a cavity present only in the receptor’s active conformation (Fig. 2e). Consequently, W74 from both ECDs is stabilized to accommodate the HC CDR3 of XX16 (Fig. 2e). Consistent with the structural data, mutations of W74 and nearby residues E75 and H76 decreased XX16-induced cGMP signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Together, these interactions drive GC-A into the active state typically induced by ANP, thereby offering a structural explanation for XX16’s ability to activate GC-A.

Multiple conformations of the REGN5308–GC-A complex

At low concentrations, the monoclonal antibody REGN5308 did not activate GC-A, showing only marginal stimulation. However, at higher concentrations, it could significantly enhance GC-A signaling (Fig. 1b). When combined with sub-saturating concentrations of ANP, REGN5308 activated GC-A at levels similar to XX16 (Fig. 1b). To understand the structural underpinnings of these differences, we performed cryo-EM and single-particle analysis on full-length wild-type human GC-A in complex with the Fab fragment of REGN5308, both in the absence and presence of ANP (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Table 2).

Structures of REGN5308-bound GC-A without and with ANP.

a–c Cartoon representation of GC-A–REGN5308 in the absence (a) or presence (b, c) of ANP. d The key amino acid interactions between the CDRs from REGN5308 and the top region of a ECD monomer of GC-A. e and g Comparisons of the active GC-A–REGN5308 structures with the inactive state of GC-A. f and h Top views of GC-A–REGN5308 in the absence (f) or presence (h) of ANP reveal the mechanism of steric clash across the symmetry axis of ECDs.

Surprisingly, three distinct conformations emerged (Fig. 3a–c). We solved the structures of apo-GC-A bound to one REGN5308 (Fig. 3a), ANP-bound GC-A bound to one REGN5308 (Fig. 3b), and ANP-bound GC-A bound to two REGN5308 molecules (Fig. 3c), all at near-atomic resolution (Supplementary Table 2). In these complexes, REGN5308 engages the topmost region of the ECDs, with its LC oriented near the dimer interface (Fig. 3a–c). This positioning differs from the binding site observed with XX16 (Fig. 2). The structure of apo-GC-A bound to one REGN5308 resembles the inactive conformation of apo-GC-A6, whereas the ANP-bound complexes—whether with one or two REGN5308 antibodies—adopt an active, ANP-bound–like state of GC-A6 (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). These observations are consistent with REGN5308’s relatively modest ability to activate GC-A compared with XX16 in the absence of ANP.

Further inspection revealed that both the HC and LC of REGN5308 are involved in the binding, but neither interaction is specific to the active or inactive conformation of GC-A (Fig. 3d–h). Several tyrosine residues from the CDRs of REGN5308—Y32 and Y92 in the HC, and Y102, Y103, Y104, and Y105 in the LC—insert into the top cleft of the GC-A ECD (Fig. 3d). Notably, N2-linked glycosylation on GC-A contributes additional contacts with the REGN5308 HC (Fig. 3d). Unlike the relatively stable XX16-bound structures, the REGN5308–GC-A complexes displayed substantial conformational heterogeneity during cryo-EM processing. Also, consistent with the structural data, mutations of W44 and N77 of GC-A decreased REGN5308-induced cGMP signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2f).

In apo-GC-A bound to one REGN5308, the LC associates with one ECD, while the HC extends toward the second ECD, thereby creating steric clashes that prevent binding of a second REGN5308 (Fig. 3e). In the ANP-bound GC-A complexes, however, the upper region of the dimer interface shifts to accommodate a second REGN5308 molecule, with two LC domains packing against each other (Fig. 3g). Thus, only one conformation—featuring a single REGN5308—is observed in the absence of ANP, whereas two distinct conformations (one REGN5308 versus two REGN5308) appear in the presence of ANP. We propose that the REGN5308 binds independently of ANP, and the subsequent binding of a second REGN5308—made feasible by ANP-induced conformational changes—further stabilizes GC-A in its active state (Fig. 3e–h). Reversion to the inactive conformation would then require dissociation of REGN5308, offering a structural rationale for how REGN5308 acts as an GC-A stimulator.

The activating antibodies stabilize ANP, ATP, and GTP binding to GC-A

We employed the recently developed Ligand Gaussian accelerated Molecular Dynamics (LiGaMD3) to simulate ANP dissociation from GC-A in the absence and presence of activating antibodies15. Three independent 800 ns LiGaMD3 simulations were carried out for each system (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Without the antibodies, ANP dissociation was observed at around 350 ns, as indicated by an increase in the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) to over 30 Å (Fig. 4a). By contrast, in the presence of REGN5308, ANP dissociated more slowly in two of the three LiGaMD3 simulations (near ~500 ns in Simulation 2 (Sim2) and ~450 ns in Sim3) (Fig. 4b). With XX16, ANP dissociation was further delayed to ~650–750 ns in all three LiGaMD3 simulations (Fig. 4c). Representative intermediate conformational states of ANP during dissociation are shown in Fig. 4d–f. Consistent with these GaMD simulations, the ligand binding experiments showed that ANP bound GC-A with a dissociation constant of ~1.42 nM (similar to previous reported values) (Supplementary Fig. 5a)16. In the presence of 1 μM REGN5308 and XX16, the dissociation constants were ~0.12 and ~0.02 nM, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). Thus, both antibodies tend to retard ANP dissociation, although XX16 exhibits a stronger stabilizing effect.

Additional GaMD simulations were performed to assess the dynamic impact of the antibodies on ANP-bound GC-A: one for ANP-bound GC-A alone (Supplementary Fig. 6a), one with ANP and XX16 (Supplementary Fig. 6b), one with ANP and a single REGN5308 molecule (Supplementary Fig. 6c), and one with ANP and two REGN5308 molecules (Supplementary Fig. 6d)6,17. Compared with the reference ANP–GC-A system (Supplementary Fig. 6a), XX16 decreased conformational deviations in ANP’s position over time (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Moreover, XX16 significantly reduced the RMSD of one of the two ATP (labeled as ATP2 in Supplementary Fig. 6b) and GMPCPP (labeled as GTP2 in Supplementary Fig. 6b) relative to the initial cryo-EM structures, demonstrating a substantial stabilizing influence on the intracellular domain (ICD). In contrast, one or two REGN5308 molecules yielded higher RMSDs for ANP and ATP (labeled as ATP1 in Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). In addition, all ANP- and antibody-bound GC-As adopted active conformations, with the F425 (on one ECD)–F425 (on the second ECD) distances reduced to 20–30 Å (vs. 40–70 Å in apo-GC-A) (Supplementary Fig. 7, left columns), and the guanylyl cyclase domain (GCD) α4-α4 separations increased to >20 Å (vs. ~15 Å in inactive apo-GC-A), enabling GTP access (Supplementary Fig. 7, middle columns). In the ANP-XX16-bound GC-A, a stable π–π stacking interaction between W74 residues (Supplementary Fig. 8, the third row), together with Cl−-mediated hydrogen bonds involving W18, S53, G85, and C86 residues, further reinforced activation of GC-A by XX16 (Supplementary Fig. 9, the second row, Chain A of GC-A; Supplementary Fig. 10, the second row, Chain B of GC-A). Together, these findings indicate that XX16 exerts a stronger stabilizing effect than REGN5308 on the binding of ANP, ATP, and GTP to GC-A.

Antibody-induced activation and conformational changes

The antibody agonists activate GC-A comparably to ANP (Fig. 5). To define antibody-induced motions, we compared full-length human GC-A in its apo and XX16-bound models (Fig. 5). XX16 binding leaves the conformation of each ECD monomer essentially unchanged but drives relative motions between monomers—a seesaw-like compaction of the membrane-proximal subdomains and a twist of ~24° (Fig. 5). These compaction–twist movements propagate through the JL that connects the ECDs to the single-pass transmembrane helices (Fig. 5). In the intracellular region, the kinase-homology domains (KHDs) undergo a rotation that becomes more pronounced toward the GCD, where the α4 helices move farther apart, widening access for GTP to the active sites (Supplementary Fig. 7, middle columns). Consistent with our previously proposed compaction–twist activation model6, ANP or antibody agonists do not remodel individual GC-A monomers; rather, they reorient the dimer-modulating interdomain packing and relative angles to achieve activation.

Lowering blood pressure by the antibody activator

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of XX16 as an GC-A activator, we employed a mouse model of obesity-induced hypertension18 (Fig. 6). Because all the key XX16-interacting residues in GC-A are conserved between humans and mice—but not those interacting with REGN5308—we focused on testing the Fab fragment of XX16 only. Wild-type C57BL/6 male mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for six months, reaching an average body weight of 55.10 ± 1.25 g (n = 3) by the end of this period (Fig. 6a). Telemetry devices were implanted to monitor BP19, and following 30 min of baseline BP recording, the mice received a single injection of XX16 at 1 mg/kg (Fig. 6a, b). As shown in Fig. 6b–e, XX16 significantly lowered systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP, and mean BP compared to baseline levels. These findings demonstrate that antibody-mediated activation of GC-A can effectively reduce BP in an obesity-induced hypertensive mouse model.

Discussion

Our study provides important insights into how two distinct monoclonal antibodies, XX16 and REGN5308, modulate GC-A activity and downstream signaling relevant to cardiovascular homeostasis. Although GC-A is classically activated by endogenous ligands, our findings demonstrate that these antibodies can activate GC-A under various conditions, opening avenues for therapeutic interventions in hypertension and heart failure.

In cellular signaling assays, both XX16 and REGN5308 were able to increase intracellular cGMP in cells expressing GC-A. XX16 displayed robust agonist activity even at lower concentrations, whereas REGN5308 required higher concentrations to achieve comparable effects. Notably, under sub-saturating ANP conditions, both antibodies augmented the receptor’s response to similar levels. These results highlight that while both antibodies can activate GC-A, they differ in potency, with distinct binding characteristics and cooperativity.

The cryo-EM structures provide the structural basis for these differences. XX16 binds near the dimer interface of the GC-A ECDs in a 1:2 stoichiometry, with a conformation resembling that of the ANP-bound GC-A. This binding alone is sufficient to induce an active receptor even without ANP, underscoring XX16’s potent agonistic effect. Detailed examination of the XX16–GC-A interface revealed a deep insertion of heavy-chain CDRs into the ECD, stabilizing residues critical for the receptor’s active conformation. By contrast, REGN5308 associates with the topmost region of the GC-A ECD and displays multiple conformations. In the absence of ANP, one REGN5308 molecule binds GC-A, preserving an overall inactive conformation. Upon ANP binding, however, the receptor rearranges to accommodate a second REGN5308 molecule, thereby stabilizing the active state. This structural plasticity underpins the weaker agonistic response of REGN5308 when ANP is not present. Structures of the ECDs of GC-A bound with an activating antibody REGN5381 in the presence and absence of ANP were recently reported to contain two Fabs11. Comparing these structures, all the GC-A structures are in the active state when ANP was bound (Supplementary Fig. 11). In the absence of ANP, XX16-bound GC-A is in the active state (Supplementary Fig. 11a), while REGN5308-bound GC-A is in the inactive state (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Without ANP, REGN5381-bound GC-A was in a conformation different from the inactive and active states of full-length GC-A11. This difference is likely due to the ECD alone used in the study with REGN5381, as we discussed previously6.

Further mechanistic insights emerge from GaMD simulations. Both XX16 and REGN5308 slow ANP dissociation from GC-A, but XX16 exerts a notably stronger stabilizing effect, likely due to its deeper engagement of the receptor’s dimer interface. Moreover, XX16 confers enhanced stability to ATP and GTP bound to GC-A’s ICD, consistent with the observed potent agonist profile. In contrast, REGN5308 results in higher fluctuations and less overall stability in these regions.

Finally, we demonstrated the therapeutic potential of XX16 in a mouse model of obesity-induced hypertension. XX16 treatment significantly lowered systolic, diastolic, and mean BP, supporting its utility in reducing hypertensive states. The use of an HFD-fed mouse model more closely approximates clinical scenarios of obesity-related hypertension, underscoring XX16’s promise as a candidate for further preclinical and clinical investigations. Although we did not assess REGN5308 in vivo because of non-conserved binding residues between human and mouse GC-A, future models using humanized GC-A mice may further clarify its therapeutic profile.

Taken together, our findings elucidate the mechanisms by which two monoclonal antibodies, XX16 and REGN5308, differentially activate and stabilize GC-A. The two antibodies displayed comparable efficacy and produced partial responses with or without ANP; however, XX16 was more potent in the absence of ANP. Moreover, by decelerating the dissociation of ANP, these antibodies reinforce the catalytic capacity of GC-A. The observed BP–lowering effects of XX16 in a hypertensive mouse model highlight the potential of GC-A agonist antibodies in developing next-generation therapies for cardiovascular diseases. Further research should explore optimizing both antibodies for clinical applications, defining optimal dosing regimens, and evaluating potential synergistic effects with existing HF and hypertension treatments.

Methods

Ethical statement

All research was conducted in compliance with relevant ethical regulations and guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Animal study procedures (protocol number 2011-0091) were approved by the Weill Cornell Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Expression and purification of full-length wild-type human GC-A

The full-length human GC-A was cloned into pFastBac1 vector with N-terminus fused to the HA signal peptide and Flag tag, and C-terminus to the PreScission protease cleavage site, eGFP, and 10 × His tag6. Bacmids were transfected into Spodoptera frugiperda Sf9 insect cells (ATCC CRL-1711) grown in ESF 921 protein-free medium (Expression Systems) to produce viruses, which were used to infect Sf9 cells for expression as we previously described20,21. 48–72 h after infection, cells reached to 2–3 million per ml were harvested, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until use. For purification, cell pellets were thawed in TBS buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl supplemented with 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME, Sigma-Aldrich), 1% n-Dodecyl-β-D-Maltopyranoside (Anatrace), 0.2% Cholesteryl Hemisuccinate (Anatrace), 0.1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors (2 mg/ml leupeptin, 0.8 mM aprotinin, and 2 mM Pepstatin A, Goldbio). Insoluble particles were removed by ultracentrifugation at 142,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C, and the supernatant was mixed with GFP nanobody beads made in house. Beads was washed by TBS in 0.05% 2,2-didecylpropane-1,3-bis-β-D-maltopyranoside (LMNG, Anatrace) and 0.1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP, GoldBio). The full-length GC-A was eventually cleavage off GFP nanobody beads by incubating with PreScission protease over night at 4 °C. After concentration using Amicon Ultra filter (100 kDa cut-off, Millipore), GC-A was subject to further purification through size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using Superose 6 column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with TBS in 0.02% LMNG and 0.1 mM TCEP. The peak fractions containing the GC-A dimer were pooled and examined by SDS-PAGE before concentration to 1 mg/ml.

Generation and purification of the Fab fragments against human GC-A

The CDR regions from two therapeutic antibodies were engineered into a mouse Fab fragment backbone in the pFastBacDual vector to generate XX16 and REGN5308 constructs with a C-terminal PreScission protease cleavage site and 8 × His tag13,14. Expression was performed in a similarly fashion as hGC-A except that the media supernatant were harvested, instead. The supernatant containing secreted Fab fragments were adjusted to pH8 and supplemented with 2 mM CaCl2 before incubation with Ni-NTA beads (Qiagen) overnight. The Fab fragments were purified through Ni-NTA column and SEC in TBS and frozen at −80 °C.

Purification of the ANP-bound full-length GC-A complexed with Fab fragments

The holo hGC-A was incubated with 10 µM of ANP in advance; otherwise, it was subsequently prepared the same way as the apo hGC-A. The concentrated GC-A was mixed with either Fab fragment at 1:2 molar ratio for 1 h at 4 °C and re-purified through SEC. The formation of complex was confirmed by SDS-PAGE. Then, desired fractions were pooled and concentrated to 1 mg/ml and mixed with 1–2 mM adenosine triphosphate (ATP, Sigma-Aldrich)

Cryo-EM data collection

The cryo-sample preparation and data collection were done as previously described6. Briefly, protein was applied to 300- or 400- mesh gold Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools) and blotted by Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI) at 4 °C. Cryo-grids were stored in liquid nitrogen until use. Cryo-EM data were collected on different 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscopes (Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI) at super resolutions with a defocus range of −0.5 to −2 µm.

For the apo GC-A protein, thawed protein was mixed with 1–2 mM adenosine triphosphate (ATP, Sigma-Aldrich) and 2 mM MgCl2. Additional 1.5× ANP peptides were added to solve the active ECD structure. For GC-A complexed with ANP and Fab fragments, concentrated proteins were mixed with 1–2 mM ATP, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM Guanosine-5’-[(α,β)-methyleno]triphosphate (GMPCPP, Jena Bioscience). Glow-discharged 400 mesh gold Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools) were applied with 3.5 µl sample on the carbon side, blotted, and plunge-frozen by Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI). Cryo-grids were stored in liquid nitrogen. Cryo-EM data were collected on a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI) equipped with Gatan K3 direct electron detectors (Gatan, Inc.) and a Gatan Bioquantum energy filter that was used with a slit width of 20 eV, or a 200 kV Glacios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific/FEI) equipped with Falcon IV detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the K3 detector, the movies were automatically recorded using SerialEM22 or Leginon23 with beam-image shift of 5 × 5 at an 81,000× magnification; for the Falcon IV detector, movies were collected using EPU at a 100,000× magnification. The recording was performed in super-resolution mode in the defocus range of −0.5 to −2 µm.

Image processing, 3D reconstructions, modeling, and refinement

These procedures were performed in a similar fashion as our published work. In short, motion correction was performed by MotionCor in Relion3 or Relion4, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) and resolutions were estimated by CTFFIND4. The particles were picked by Laplacian-of-Gaussian (LoG) and extracted in Relion before transferring into cryoSPARC using PyEM for further analysis. The subsequent processing in cryoSPARC was detailed in the Supplemental figures to generate five structures of hGC-A-Fab complexes. Models were built in Coot and refined in PHENIX. Nearly all side chains were clearly identified and modeled in these high-resolution structures. Qualities of the maps and models were validated in wwPDB validation server. Models or maps illustrated in this manuscript were created using PyMOL and UCSF ChimeraX. Motion of dose fractionated movie stack was corrected by patch motion correction in cryoSPARC24. Sum motion-corrected frames with dose weighting and 2× binned were used for further processing. The ECD particles were picked by LoG in Relion at a smaller box size25,26. The Supplementary Figs. 1 and 3 detail the processing flow charts for all five structures27. Subsequent model building was carried out in Coot and refined in PHENIX28,29. At low-resolution regions, residue side chains were removed so that only the peptide backbone was fit to the density with secondary structure restraints; otherwise, side chains were adjusted according to the density. Qualities of the maps and structures were validated by wwPDB validation server and illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 3. For visualizations in this work, maps and models were created using PyMOL, UCSF Chimera, and ChimeraX30,31.

cGMP measurement

Cellular cGMP assays were performed similarly as we previously described32,33. HEK293 cells (ATCC CRL-1573) were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% fetal calf serum and transfected with wild-type full-length human GC-A. The cells were serum starved overnight, washed twice, and incubated in the buffer containing 0.5 mM IMBX (Sigma) for 30 min at room temperature20. The cells were subsequently treated with various concentrations of anti-GC-A antibodies without and with ANP as indicated for 5 min. The reaction was terminated by aspiration of medium and immediately treated with 0.1 M HCl for 10 min at room temperature. The samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for determination of cGMP concentration in triplicate with the Direct Cyclic GMP Enzyme Immunoassay kit (Enzo Life Sciences). The data are represented as mean ± SD of the three experiments. The analysis was done using the log(agonist) vs. response function of Prism 10 (GraphPad)20,21,34.

Ligand binding experiment

CHO-K1 cells (ATCC CCL-61) were cultured in F-12K medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and transfected with wild-type full-length human GC-A-GFP. The cells were serum starved for 6 h and washed twice. The cells were subsequently treated with various concentrations of ANP-Cy5 without and with 1 μM XX16 or REGN5308 as indicated in the dark for 30 min at 4 °C in the binding buffer (1 × HBSS + 0.1% BSA). The cells were washed three times with ice-cold wash buffer (the binding buffer without BSA) and the images were acquired. The GFP images represented GC-A receptors and the purple images represented the ligand ANP. The analysis was done using the one site—specific binding least square fit function of Prism 10 (GraphPad)20,21,34.

Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) simulations and simulation analysis

GaMD is a computational enhanced sampling technique which works by adding a harmonic boost potential to smooth biomolecular potential energy surface and reduce the system energy barriers17,35. GaMD does not require pre-defined collective variables or reaction coordinates and is thus advantageous for unconstrained enhanced sampling of complex biological systems such as GC-A. Since the GaMD boost potential exhibits a Gaussian distribution, the original biomolecular free energy profiles can be properly recovered through cumulant expansion to the second order17,35. GaMD has been successfully demonstrated on biomolecular simulations and revealed physical pathways and mechanisms of protein folding and ligand binding, which were consistent with experiments and long-timescale conventional MD simulations15,36,37. Recently, GaMD was also applied to successfully capture the activation of full-length GC-A6. Based on GaMD, Ligand GaMD 3 (LiGaMD3) has been developed to further enhance sampling dissociation and binding of both small-molecule ligand and flexible peptides15. In LiGaMD3, triple boost potentials are applied on three individual energy terms that play important roles in small-molecule/peptide dissociation, rebinding, and system conformational changes. LiGaMD3 has been shown to greatly improve the sampling efficiency of small-molecule/peptide interactions with target proteins. Therefore, we adopt the GaMD and LiGaMD3 methods for simulations of GC-A in this study.

Our cryo-EM structures of GC-A with bound antibodies were used for setting up the simulation systems for the full-length GC-As (Supplementary Fig. 4). The ANP-bound GC-A system was obtained from our previous study and the same protocol from our previous study was applied for GaMD simulations of the structures6. The disordered residues in the ICD of ANP-bound GC-A were added based on homology modeling using SWISS Model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) to obtain the complete structures of the full-length ANP-antibody-bound GC-As38. The chloride, ATP, GMPCPP, and glycan identified in the cryo-EM structures were maintained in the simulation models. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the full-length GC-A structure used in GaMD/LiGaMD3 simulations comprises N-terminal extracellular domain (ECD, with chloride ion and glycan), a juxtamembrane linker (JL), a single-pass transmembrane helix (TM), and an ICD that includes a ATP-bound protein KHD, a parallel coiled-coil (CC), and a GTP-bound C-terminal guanylyl cyclase catalytic domain (GCD, with GMPCPP and Mg²⁺).

Using CHARMM-GUI39, the ANP-bound GC-A, ANP-XX16-bound, ANP-REGN5308 (RGN)-bound and ANP-2X RGN-bound GC-As were inserted in POPC (palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) lipids to prepare the simulation systems (Supplementary Table 3). In each simulation system, the protein and lipid bilayer were solvated with TIP3P water molecules in a box of approximately 12.02 × 12.02 × 28.8–32.2 nm with the periodic boundary condition. The system charge was neutralized with 150 mM NaCl and 2 mM MgCl2. The CHARMM36m all-atom force field was used40. The simulation systems were first energy minimized for 5000 steps with constraints on the heavy atoms of the proteins and phosphor atoms of the lipids. The hydrogen-heavy atom bonds were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm and the simulation time step was set to 2.0 fs41. The particle mesh Ewald method was employed to compute the long-range electrostatic interactions and a cutoff value of 12.0 Å was applied to treat the non-bonded atomic interactions42. The temperature was controlled using the Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 1.0 ps−1. The system was then equilibrated using the constant number, volume, and temperature ensemble at 310 K for 250 ps and under the constant number, pressure, and temperature ensemble at 310 K and 1 bar for another 1 ns with constraints on the heavy atoms of the protein, followed by 10 ns short conventional MD without any constraint.

The GaMD module implemented in the GPU version of AMBER23 was then applied to perform the simulations17,35. GaMD simulations included a 16-ns short conventional MD run used to collect the potential statistics for calculating GaMD acceleration parameters, an 80-ns GaMD equilibration after adding the boost potential, and finally three independent 500-ns GaMD production simulations with randomized initial atomic velocities. The average and SD of the system potential energies were calculated every 1,600,000 steps (3.2 ns). All GaMD simulations were performed at the “dual boost” level by setting the reference energy to the lower bound. One boost potential was applied to the dihedral energetic term and the other to the total potential energetic term. The upper limit of the boost potential SD, σ0 was set to 6.0 kcal/mol for both the dihedral and the total potential energetic terms.

Additionally, Ligand GaMD 3 (LiGaMD3)15, which shows improved sampling of protein-ligand interactions was applied to simulate the ANP substrate dynamics in the ANP-bound, ANP-XX16-bound, and ANP-RGN-bound GC-As. The LiGaMD3 simulations proceeded included a 16-ns short conventional MD run used to collect the potential statistics for calculating LiGaMD3 acceleration parameters, an 80-ns LiGaMD3 equilibration after adding the boost potential, and finally three independent 800-ns LiGaMD3 production simulations with randomized initial atomic velocities. Three boost potentials were added to the essential non-bonded potential energy of the ANP substrate (first boost), the remaining non-bonded potential energy of the system (second boost), and the bonded potential energy of the system (third boost), respectively. The threshold energy for applying the boost potentials were set to the lower bound in the LiGaMD3 simulations. The (σ0P, σ0D, σ0B) parameters were set to (6.0 kcal/mol, 6.0 kcal/mol, 6.0 kcal/mol) for all the systems. All the other simulation parameters are kept the same as in the GaMD simulations.

For each simulation system, the three GaMD/LiGaMD3 production trajectories were used for calculating the root-mean-squared-derivations (RMSDs) of the ANP, ATP, and GTP, and structural clustering to identify the representative conformations with the hierarchical agglomerative algorithm using the CPPTRAJ module in AMBER23 package43.

Mouse model of obesity-induced hypertension

C57BL/6 male mice at 6–7 weeks of age were fed with HFD (60% fat; #D12492i, Research diet) for 6 months. The research animal facility housing the mice has a controlled 12-h light/12-h dark light cycle and temperature of 65–75 °F ( ~ 18–23 °C) with 40–60% humidity. Certified veterinarians in laboratory animal medicine and veterinary pathology oversee all aspects of animal care. All the studies were performed according to protocols approved by the Weill Cornell Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, as previously described in ref. 19.

Radiotelemetry BP measurements

SBP was measured in conscious WT male mice fed with HFD for 6 months as previously described in ref. 19. International (DSI) implantable radiotelemetry transmitters18. Anesthetized (isoflurane, to effect) mice were implanted with carotid artery catheters advanced to the aortic arch and radiotelemeter implants (model HD-X10) inserted in a subcutaneous pocket on the back. After 9–12 days of recovery, BP was monitored continuously, with values reported every 5 s. Basal BP was measured for about 30 min, then mice were injected i.p. with XX16 solubilized in PBS at the dose of 1 mg/Kg and BP was recorded for the following 3 h. At study endpoint, mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (150/15 mg/kg, i.p.) for intracardiac perfusion with PBS.
