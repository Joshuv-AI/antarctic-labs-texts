Tengchuan compound mixture ameliorates Mycoplasma Synoviae-induced synovitis: An integrated network pharmacology, metabolomics, and 16S rRNA sequencing study
Authors: Qibin Kan, Jie Luo, Ling Zhao, Gang Ye, Huaqi Zhang, Fei Shi, Hao Liang, Ze Zeng, Qinting Yang, Huaqiao Tang, Yinglun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycoplasma synoviae (MS) is a prevalent pathogen in poultry farming, often causing infectious synovitis and arthritis in poultry, resulting in joint swelling, lameness, and growth retardation, thereby significantly impacting production efficiency. Tengchuan compound mixture (TCM), a traditional Chinese medicine formulation designed to against MS infection effectively, but its active components and mechanisms isn’t elucidated. This study utilized liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to identify the active components in TCM and employed network pharmacology to predict key targets and pathways in against MS infection. A model of MS infection was established in specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens to assess the pharmacodynamic effects of TCM on parameters such as body weight, clinical manifestations, histopathology of tarsal joints, and serum inflammatory markers. Metabolomics analysis and 16S rDNA sequencing were conducted to elucidate the potential mechanism of TCM in preventing and treating MS infection. Western blot analysis confirmed the core targets identified by network pharmacology. TCM treatment effectively mitigated MS-induced joint lesions by suppressing the phosphorylation of proteins associated with the JAK2-STAT3 and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways. Pharmacokinetic analysis identified 18 major potential active ingredients. Network pharmacology identified 147 common targets of TCM against MS infection, including 13 core targets such as SRC, JAK2, PIK3CA, and STAT3. Metabolomics analysis revealed that MS infection disrupted amino acid and energy metabolism in chickens, which was partially restored by TCM treatment. Additionally, 16S rDNA sequencing indicated that TCM could recovery the gut microbiota disrupted by MS infection. In conclusion, this integrated multi-omics study provides mechanistic insights into TCM’s effects during MS infection, highlighting its role in ameliorating infection-induced inflammation and joint pathology while modulating host metabolic pathways and gut microbiota. These findings offer a comprehensive understanding of TCM’s therapeutic potential for managing Mycoplasma synoviae infection.Keywords: Mycoplasma synoviae, Chinese herbal compound mixture, Network pharmacology, Metabolomics, 16S rDNA sequencing

Introduction

Mycoplasma synoviae (MS) is a prevalent pathogen in poultry farming, primarily affecting chickens and turkeys, with horizontal and vertical transmission routes (Mugunthan, et al., 2023). MS infection manifests clinically as synovitis and arthritis, characterized by joint swelling, lameness, and locomotor dysfunction, often accompanied by respiratory tract lesions that progress to chronic airsacculitis and bronchitis (Sui, et al., 2022). Notably, during peak laying periods, MS infection exhibit significantly reduced egg production, increases eggshell breakage, and leads to quality defects (Cortés, et al., 2021). The resultant decline in productivity and elevated culling rates due to MS infection impose substantial economic burdens on farms.

MS can adhere to host cell extracellular matrix glycoproteins through surface lipoproteins, facilitating cell surface colonization, a critical early infection event (Klose, et al., 2022). Additionally,MS includes a diverse surface lipoprotein family that allows evasion of immune detection via antigenic variation, supporting sustained proliferation within the host (Si, et al., 2023). MS can alsoinduce stimulate the host to secreteproduce excessiveelevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, suchincluding as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, whichleading isto one of the main causes of synovial joint damage (Bolha, et al., 2013; Xu, et al., 2020). Presently, vaccination and antibiotic administration are the main strategies for preventing and managing MS infection (Yan, et al., 2023; Zhang, et al., 2023). However, both live and inactivated vaccines currently available are unable to completely eradicate MS colonization in the host, resulting in vaccinated chicken populations retaining MS and potentially transmitting it to non-vaccinated or recently introduced individuals(Wu, et al., 2024). Moreover, prolonged and excessive antibiotic usage not only fosters the development of antibiotic resistance in MS but also poses risks to the environment and human health (Puvača, et al., 2020; Qiao, et al., 2023).

In China, the efficacy of Chinese Herbal Medicine in preventing and treating avian infectious diseases has been demonstrated in recent years. such as, Baicalin, Matrine, Astragalus polysaccharides, Xiaochaihu Decoction, etc (Liang, et al., 2021; Song, et al., 2022; Wang, et al., 2021; Zan, et al., 2024). Research indicates that these herbal medicines contain active compounds like alkaloids, flavonoids, and organic acids, which can boost animal immunity and ameliorate infection-induced metabolic disorders(Gao, et al., 2022; Huang, et al., 2023). Moreover, gut microbiota can metabolize these compounds into active substances, enhancing their bioavailability and maintaining host gut microbiota homeostasis (Zhao, et al., 2024). Importantly, the presence of multiple components and targets in Chinese herbal medicines poses challenges for pathogens to develop drug resistance (Ren, et al., 2020). However, despite these benefits, challenges persist in effectively utilizing Chinese medicines for managing avian infectious diseases, particularly in the context of intensive farming. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms of action of these natural drugs.

In traditional Chinese medicine theory, joint lesions resulting from MS infection are commonly categorized as "Bi syndrome". Treatment of Bi syndrome in traditional Chinese medicine involves strategies such as enhancing blood circulation to alleviate stasis, relaxing tendons and activating collaterals, and dispelling wind and dampness to alleviate joint pain, reduce inflammation, and restore joint function. In line with this theory, a novel Compound Mixture, Tengchuan Compound Mixture (TCM), has been formulated for the clinical management and prevention of MS infection. TCM comprises five Chinese herbal medicines, including Sinomenium acutum, Spatholobus suberectus, Dioscorea nipponica, Radix Aconiti Preparata, and Cyathula officinalis, each with established efficacy and historical applications in traditional Chinese medicine. Nevertheless, the specific pharmacodynamic components and mechanisms of action of TCM in the prevention and treatment of MS infection remain to be elucidated.

Therefore, this study seeks to establish a network of interactions between active components of TCM and their targets through network pharmacology. In vivo experiments will validate the effectiveness of TCM against MS infection and elucidate mechanism of action. Additionally, gut microbiota analysis and metabolomics will be employed to elucidate the regulatory impact of TCM on metabolic pathways and the gut microecosystem. The ultimate goal is to offer empirical support and actionable insights for leveraging TCM in addressing MS infection.

Materials and methods

Materials and methods

Caulis Sinomenii, Caulis Spatholobi, Rhizoma Dioscoreae Nipponicae, Radix Aconiti Preparata, and Radix Cyathulae were provided by the Laboratory of Traditional Chinese Veterinary Medicine, Sichuan Agricultural University (Chengdu, China). Tylosin tartrate was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The ELISA kit was obtained from Jiangsu Enzyme-labeled Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, China). The Mycoplasma synoviae liquid medium was provided by Zhonghai Biotechnology Zaozhuang Co., Ltd. (Zaozhuang, China). Antibodies: GAPDH(60004-1-1g) was provided by Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China); JAK2(A19629), Phospho-JAK2(AP0531), STAT3(A19566), Phospho-STAT3(AP0715), PIK3CA(A16950), Phospho-PIK3(AP0427), Akt(A18675), Phospho-Akt(AP1453), Src(A19119), SOCS1(A7754), and PIAS3(A7060) were provided by ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Secondary Antibody and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) Secondary Antibody were provided by Thermo Fisher.

Phytochemical profiling of TCM

Proportionate Weighing of Five Medicinal Herbs, submerge the herbs in distilled water for 30 min, ensuring complete coverage, followed by boiling for 60 min. Filter the resulting medicinal liquid using gauze, repeat the boiling process twice with the same mass of distilled water, and combine the filtrates. Concentrate the combined liquid to 2 g/mL using a rotary evaporator, freeze-dry, and store at -20°C for future use.

Dissolve 50 mg of the lyophilized powder of the traditional Chinese medicine compound in a 100 μL solution of methanol and water (1:1, v/v). Following centrifugation at 20000g and 4°C for 15 min, filter the solution through a C18 membrane. The treated samples are subjected to chromatographic separation using a SHIMADZU-LC30 ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) from Waters (Milford, MA, USA). Each fractionated sample is analyzed in positive and negative ion modes via electrospray ionization (ESI), with mass spectrometry conducted on a QE Plus mass spectrometer. Ionization is performed using an HESI source under the specified conditions: Spray Voltage: 3.8 kV (+) and 3.2 kV (-); Capillary Temperature: 320°C; Sheath Gas: 30 arbitrary units; Auxiliary Gas: 5 arbitrary units; Probe Heater Temperature: 350°C; S-Lens RF Level: 50.

The raw data underwent peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction through MSDIAL software. Identification of TCM components involved accurate mass matching (mass tolerance < 10 ppm) and MS² spectrum matching (mass tolerance < 0.01 Da), with TCM components identified by querying the traditional Chinese medicine database.

Network pharmacology analysis

The 2D planar structures of components from TCM were retrieved from the PubChem database and assessed for pharmacokinetics via the SwissADME database. Potential pharmacodynamic agents within the TCM compound were identified by evaluating gastrointestinal absorption rates and drug-likeness criteria. Components exhibiting "High" gastrointestinal absorption and meeting three "Yes" criteria for drug-likeness were designated as potential pharmacodynamic agents within the TCM compound. Subsequently, the SwissTargetPrediction database was employed, with ‘Homo sapiens’ specified as the species, to predict the targets of these pharmacodynamic agents. To determine chicken-relevant targets, we obtained the reviewed Gallus gallus (TaxID: 9031) proteome from UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot and matched human gene symbols to gene names in the chicken dataset. Matched entries were treated as supported in chicken and were carried forward for subsequent analyses.

The Gene Cards and OMIM databases were queried using the keywords "Mycoplasma synoviae" and "Infectious synovitis" to identify target genes associated with MS. Additionally, a literature search was conducted to identify relevant reports on MS infection (Chen, et al., 2022b; Liu, et al., 2020; Liu, et al., 2021; Zhong, et al., 2023) to augment the pool of differentially expressed genes related to MS.

The Venny 2.1. tool was utilized to identify common targets between potential pharmacodynamic substance target genes of a traditional Chinese medicine compound and MS-related target genes. The identified intersection targets were input into the String database, specifying the species as "Gallus". A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed by setting a minimum interaction confidence score of .700 and filtering out proteins without interactions. The resulting PPI network data was exported, and subsequently, Cytoscape 3.9.1 was employed to generate the core target PPI network.

The overlapping targets of the traditional Chinese medicine compound and MS were analyzed using the DAVID database for Gene Ontology (GO) function and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) signaling pathway enrichment. The results were visualized using R 4.3.1 for further interpretation.

Animal experiments

All procedures involving animals in this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (approval No.20250095) and were conducted in strict accordance with relevant animal welfare guidelines.

Male specific pathogen-free (SPF) chickens aged 6 weeks were procured from Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Vital Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Following a one-week acclimatization period, the chickens were randomly allocated into six groups (n=8 per group): control group, model group, antibiotic group receiving Tylosin Tartrate (TYL) at a dosage of 0.2 g/kg/day, and three TCM treatment groups (TCM-L, TCM-M, TCM-H) receiving doses of 1, 2, and 4 g/kg/day, respectively. Each group of chickens was housed in individual cages, with the control group kept in a separate, isolated coop to prevent cross-infection, while all infected groups were maintained in the same barn. The facility was equipped with a ventilation system, and environmental conditions were standardized across all groups, with a temperature range of 22–25°C, relative humidity of 50%–60%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All chickens had free access to a standard layer grower diet and clean water ad libitum throughout the duration of the study, ensuring uniform nutrition and consistent husbandry practices across all experimental groups.

The MS-BJPG strain (obtained from Beijing Centre Biology Co., Ltd.) was utilized to induce a MS infection model. The lyophilized MS-BJPG strain was reconstituted in 2 mL of fresh MS medium. Upon observing a color change in the medium from red to orange-yellow, a portion of the culture was subcultured at a 1:9 ratio with fresh medium. This subculturing process was repeated three times to revive the viability of MS and establish its color change unit (CCU). The concentration of MS-BJPG was adjusted to 1 × 106 CCU/mL using fresh medium for subsequent experiments. The MS infection model was established as previously described by Yan(Yan, et al., 2023), with minor modifications. Briefly, all chickens in the model and TCM groups were challenged in a single session via intra-articular injection of 0.3 mL of this MS suspension into the right tarsal joint (inserting the needle ∼1 cm proximal to the joint). Control group chickens were injected at the same time and in an identical manner with 0.3 mL of sterile culture medium. This simultaneous challenge ensured that each group was exposed to infection under the same conditions and timeframe.

Drug treatments began on the third day post-infection. Chickens in the antibiotic group received TYL (0.2 g/kg/day orally), while those in the TCM-L, TCM-M, and TCM-H groups received the designated low, medium, or high doses of the TCM formulation (1, 2, or 4 g/kg/day, respectively) by oral gavage. All treatments were administered once daily at 8:00 a.m. for 7 consecutive days. During this period, the control and model groups were given an equivalent volume of normal saline orally on the same schedule to ensure that all groups underwent the same handling. After the 7-day treatment course, birds were observed for an additional one-week period without any treatment to monitor the progression or resolution of infection symptoms.

At the end of the observation period, all chickens were fasted for 12 h overnight to prepare for sampling. After venipuncture of the wing vein, each chicken was humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. Serum, tarsal joint tissues, and cecal contents were immediately collected for downstream analyses. To ensure procedural uniformity and minimize time-dependent variability in analytes, euthanasia and necropsy were conducted sequentially on a one-bird-at-a-time basis; all birds within a given group were processed within 30 min, and sample collection for all groups was completed within approximately 3 h.

Clinical symptoms

Body weight was recorded every other day from the onset of the experiment. On the 16th day, clinical symptoms such as mental state, movement, and feeding behavior of the chickens were assessed for scoring. Simultaneously, the tarsal joints were dissected to evaluate and score any pathological alterations.

Histopathological analyse

The joint tissue was fixed using a paraformaldehyde solution, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA. Subsequently, the decalcified joints were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with tartrate-based hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Microscopic examination revealed pathological alterations including inflammatory cell infiltration, synovial tissue hyperplasia, and cartilage damage in the tarsal joints.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, and IL-10 in the serum of each group were detected according to the instructions of the ELISA kit.

Serum metabolomics analysis

Transfer 100 μL of serum to an EP tube and combine with 400 μL of extraction solution (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1, v/v) containing an isotopically labeled internal standard mixture. Vortex the mixture for 30 s, followed by sonication in an ice-water bath for 10 min. Allow the solution to incubate at -40°C for 1 h. Subsequently, centrifuge the treated serum at 12000 rpm and 4°C for 15 min. Proceed with chromatographic separation of the resulting supernatant using a Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm).

The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was utilized to gather both primary and secondary mass spectrometry data. Specific parameters included a sheath gas flow rate of 50 Arb, Aux gas flow rate of 15 Arb, capillary temperature set at 320 °C, full MS resolution of 60000, MS/MS resolution of 15000, collision energy levels of SNCE 20/30/40, and spray voltages of 3.8 kV (positive) or -3.4 kV (negative). Subsequently, raw data were converted to mzXML format through ProteoWizard software, followed by peak identification, extraction, alignment, and integration using an R package (utilizing XCMS as its core). The data were then compared against an in-house secondary mass spectrometry database for substance annotation, with an algorithm scoring cutoff value established at 0.3.

16S rDNA sequencing

The genomic DNA (gDNA) from cecal contents was extracted using the MagBeads Fecal DNA Kit (Magen) and assessed for quality via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the V3-V4 variable regions were targeted, and specific barcoded primers were synthesized for amplification. The PCR products from the same sample were pooled and verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Gel extraction of the PCR products was carried out using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AXYGEN). Following preliminary quantification through electrophoresis, the PCR products were quantified using the QuantiFluor™ - ST Blue Fluorescence Quantification System (Promega) and combined in appropriate ratios based on sequencing volume requirements. The construction of Illumina PE250 libraries was performed using the Hieff NGS® Ultima DNA Library Prep Kit (Yeasen), with library quality assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 420 system. Subsequently, sequencing and analysis were conducted on the Novaseq 600 PE250 platform. The paired-end reads generated from sequencing were initially assembled based on their overlapping regions, followed by quality control and sequence filtering. Subsequent to sample differentiation, operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering analysis and taxonomic classification at the species level were performed. Utilizing the results from OTU clustering, diversity index analysis was conducted. Furthermore, statistical analysis of community structure was carried out at various taxonomic levels based on the taxonomic information obtained.

Western blot analysis

Articular synovial tissue proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, followed by protein concentration determination via the BCA method. Subsequently, protein loading buffer was added, and the samples were boiled for 10 min to modify protein properties. 40 μg of protein samples were separated by 4-20% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The Membranes were blocked in 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6; 150 mM NaCl; 0.1% Tween-20) at room temperature for 3 h，then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GAPDH (1:50,000), JAK2 (1:4,000), phospho-JAK2 (1:2,000), STAT3 (1:2,000), phospho-STAT3 (1:2,000), PIK3CA (1:2,000), phospho-PI3K (1:5,000), Akt (1:6,000), phospho-Akt (1:1,000), Src (1:2,000), SOCS1 (1:2,000), and PIAS3 (1:2,000). After three TBST washes (3×10 min), the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody(anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG-HRP, 1:20000) for 1 h at room temperature. Developed with ECL reagents, and imaged on a chemiluminescence system. Band intensities were quantified in Image J. GAPDH served as an internal control for assessing protein expression levels.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were reported as mean ± SEM, with statistical analysis conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.1. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group comparisons were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and pairwise comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) method. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Analysis of TCM components and prediction of targets

Upon analysis using UPLC-Q-Exactive Plus, the TCM extract yielded two distinct mass spectrometry files in positive and negative ion modes. The mass spectra for each ion mode are depicted in Fig. 1. A total of 637 compounds were identified within the TCM extract, categorized primarily as amino acids and derivatives, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenes, organic acids, and sugars. Among the top 80 compounds, 18 potential active ingredients were identified, with detailed information provided in Table S1. SwissTargetPrediction yielded 595 human targets, 459 of which aligned with reviewed Gallus gallus entries in UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot; these were subsequently analyzed as chicken-supported targets.

Component-target network construction and core target screening

Intersection targets were retrieved from the String database, specifying the species as "Gallus", a minimum interaction confidence threshold of 0.700. Proteins without interactions were excluded to construct a protein-protein interaction network. Analysis revealed 145 nodes and 442 edges among 118 genes, with an average node degree of 6.1. This data was imported into Cytoscape 3.9.1 for visualization (Fig.S1), where blue squares denote active components of the traditional Chinese medicine compound and red circles represent action targets. The "Cyto NCA" plugin in Cytoscape 3.9.1 and R language 4.3.1 were utilized to identify core targets based on specific criteria: Degree: 22; LAC: 13.82; Network: 15.06; Eigenvector: .15; Closeness: .59; Betweenness: 8.92. Thirteen core targets were identified: VEGFA, CASP3, PIK3CB, SRC, JAK2, MMP9, PIK3R1, CDC42, PIK3CA, STAT3, PIK3CD, EGFR, and KDR, as depicted in Fig. 2B.

GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG signaling pathway enrichment analysis

The intersection targets were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the DAVID database, encompassing Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). A total of 136 BPs, 45 MFs, and 28 CCs were identified. The top ten entries based on -log10(pvalue) in each category were selected and visualized using R version 4.3.1, as depicted in Fig. 3A. The analysis revealed that the intersection targets were primarily associated with processes such as phosphorylation, signal transduction, and protein phosphorylation in the BP category; ATP binding, metal ion binding, and zinc ion binding in the MF category; and enrichment in cellular components such as cytoplasm, membrane, and extracellular matrix. Additionally, an enrichment analysis of KEGG signaling pathways was conducted, uncovering significant enrichment in 32 pathways (p < 0.05). Visual inspection of the top 10 enriched signaling pathways was performed, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. The results highlighted significant enrichment of the intersection targets in pathways including the VEGF signaling pathway, ErbB signaling pathway, and FoxO signaling pathway, known for their crucial roles in angiogenesis, cell proliferation, immune inflammation, cell cycle regulation, and cell migration.

Pharmacodynamic evaluation of TCM against MS infection

The effectiveness of TCM against MS infection was assessed by monitoring various parameters in chickens, including body weight, mental and locomotor functions, and joint morphology. Throughout the experiment, the body weight of the control group exhibited a consistent increase, whereas the model group displayed a significantly lower rate of weight gain. Weight gain in the model group ceased on the 6th day, with a subsequent decline in body weight observed towards the later stages of the study. TCM treatment was found to ameliorate the weight loss induced by MS infection, with the TCM-H group demonstrating the most pronounced effect. The trajectory of body weight changes in the TCM-H group closely resembled that of the positive control group treated with TYL, as depicted in Fig. 4A. The condition of the chicken flocks in each group on the 16th day is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2. In the control group, each chicken exhibited a positive mental state, engaged actively in feeding, and demonstrated normal gait. Conversely, chickens in the model group displayed disheveled feathers, lethargy, and typical symptoms of MS infection such as lameness and a propensity for recumbency. TCM treatment effectively alleviated symptoms of lethargy and joint swelling induced by MS infection, with the TCM-H group yielding the most favorable outcomes, closely resembling the state of the chicken cohort in the TYL group. Clinical symptom scores were quantitatively assigned to each group based on a predefined scoring table (Table S2) to assess the efficacy of TCM against MS infection, as depicted in Fig. 4B. Both the TCM-M and TCM-H groups significantly reduced clinical symptom scores post MS infection, with no notable difference in scores between the TCM-H group and the TYL group (p > 0.05). These results highlight the significant improvement in clinical symptoms induced by MS infection with TCM treatment.

Autopsy examinations were conducted on the tarsal joints of each experimental group, as depicted in Fig. S3. The model group exhibited a substantial presence of grayish-white inflammatory exudate, with some forming yellow caseous substances. Treatment with TCM-H markedly attenuated the generation of inflammatory exudate. In the model group, a majority of chickens displayed joint swelling in both tarsal joints, along with footpad swelling and deformities, resulting in impaired mobility. Notably, the autopsy scores of the tarsal joints in the TCM-M and TCM-H groups exhibited a significant reduction (p <0.01). These findings underscore the substantial ameliorative effects of TCM on joint damage induced by MS infection.

To validate the protective efficacy of TCM on the tarsal joint, we assessed histopathological alterations in chicken tarsal joints (Fig. 5). The control group exhibited no discernible pathological changes. Conversely, the model group displayed extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in the synovial tissue and joint cavity, resulting in diffuse distribution, synovial tissue hyperplasia, and cartilage damage. Additionally, the joint ligament tissue exhibited disordered collagen fiber arrangement, along with inflammatory cell infiltration and fibroblast hyperplasia. Treatment with TYL notably mitigated joint lesions, manifesting a significant reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration. The cartilage and joint ligament structures remained intact, and synovial hyperplasia was markedly improved. TCM demonstrated a mitigating effect on joint lesions induced by MS infection, notably reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and synovial hyperplasia compared to the model group. MS infection induces synovitis in chickens' tarsal joints by eliciting an excessive release of inflammatory cytokines, leading to synovial damage. This research delved into the impact of TCM on pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels in chicken serum, as depicted in Fig. 4D-H. ELISA analysis revealed a significant increase in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels (p < 0.01) and a decrease in IL-4 and IL-10 levels (p < 0.01) in the serum of the model group compared to the control group. Conversely, all TCM doses tested notably reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in chicken serum (p < 0.01) while significantly restoring IL-4 and IL-10 levels (p < 0.01). These findings underscore the potent capacity of TCM to attenuate pro-inflammatory mediators and reinstate anti-inflammatory mediators in the serum of MS-infected chickens.

The influence of TCM on serum metabolomics

To assess the impact of MS infection on metabolic pathways, we performed serum non-targeted metabolomics analysis on three groups: the control group, model group, and TCM-M group. Methodological robustness was confirmed through the evaluation of overlapping QC samples using total ion chromatography (TIC), demonstrating excellent stability, repeatability, and data correlation Fig. S4. The identified metabolites predominantly comprised lipids and lipid-like molecules (36.62%), organic acids and their derivatives (21.56%), and organic heterocyclic compounds (13.57%) Fig. S5. Utilizing the OPLS-DA model, distinct separation was observed between the control and model groups (Fig. 6A), as well as between the model and TCM groups (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that MS infection induces notable alterations in metabolite levels, with TCM exhibiting the ability to counter MS infection by modulating metabolic pathways.

A total of 12,040 characteristic substances were detected in both positive and negative ion modes, leading to the identification of 681 metabolites. Compared to the control group, the model group showed 190 differential metabolites, with 57 up-regulated and 133 down-regulated (Fig. 6C). Details of 10 key differential metabolites are in Table S5. Furthermore, Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism also displayed notable enrichment post-TCM treatment, as depicted in Fig. 6F.

The influence of TCM on gut microbiota

Traditional Chinese medicine is recognized for its ability to modulate the gut microbiota, thereby influencing the pathogenesis of diseases (Chen, et al., 2024). To investigate the specific changes in gut microbiota caused by MS infection and the effects of TCM on this microbiota, we performed 16S rDNA gene sequencing on fecal samples. Alpha diversity analysis, measuring microbial community richness and diversity, showed a significant reduction in the chao1 and ace indices in the model group (p <0.01), indicating a decline in the richness and diversity of the cecal microbiota in roosters Table S8. Beta diversity analysis, used to compare microbial community structures across groups, revealed significant differences in gut microbiota composition among the three groups (Fig. 7A and Fig. 7B). These findings indicate that MS infection significantly altered the cecal microbiota in chickens, while TCM treatment improved the cecal microbial ecosystem.

PCA analysis and PCOA analysis of of microbial community composition. (A) Multiple samples PCA analysis. (B) Multiple samples PCOA analysis.

MS infection led to a significant decrease in the abundance of Firmicutes at the phylum level, while the abundances of Campylobacterota and Fusobacteriota increased significantly (Fig. 8A). Conversely, TCM treatment effectively reversed the changes in phylum structure caused by MS infection, significantly increasing the abundance of Firmicutes. Similarly, MS infection decreased the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, which was increased by TCM treatment (Fig. S6). At the genus level (Fig. 8B), MS infection significantly increased the abundances of Campylobacter and Fusobacterium, while decreasing the abundances of Faecalibacterium, Clostridia, and Lachnospiraceae_unclassified. TCM treatment not only reduced the abundances of Campylobacter and Fusobacterium, but also significantly increased the abundances of Faecalibacterium, Clostridia, and Butyricicoccus.

The regulatory effect of TCM on the cecal microbiota of chickens was further investigated using LEfSe analysis combined with linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to identify differential and dominant taxa among the groups. The analysis revealed a total of 45 species (LDA ≥ 2.5) as dominant taxa, with 22 in the control group, 10 in the model group, and 13 in the TCM group. The control group was characterized by relatively high proportions of unclassified Lachnospiraceae, Burkholderiales, Sutterellaceae, Parasutterella, and Ruminococcus. In the model group, the abundances of Campylobacteria, Gallibacterium, and Pasteurellaceae increased. The TCM group was primarily composed of Firmicutes, RF39_g_norank, and Lachnoclostridium (Figures. 9 and S8).

TCM inhibited JAK2-STAT3 and PI3K-AKT Signaling Pathway

The JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway, a well-characterized intracellular signal transduction route, influences cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses, playing a crucial role in various diseases. Guided by network pharmacology predictions, we assessed the expression of JAK2, p-JAK2, STAT3, and p-STAT3. Our findings revealed that MS infection significantly elevated the phosphorylation of Jak2 and Stat3 proteins in synovial tissues compared to the control group (p < 0.01). Treatment with a traditional Chinese medicine compound effectively reduced this phosphorylation (p < 0.01). Additionally, the compound markedly increased the expression levels of PIAS3 and SOCS1 proteins (Fig. 10).

Discussion

In recent years, the expanding scale of poultry breeding has led to a yearly increase in MS infection rates, emerging as a significant constraint on poultry farming efficiency (Wei, et al., 2023). While vaccination and antibiotic treatments can somewhat mitigate MS infection, prolonged use frequently results in heightened drug resistance and may lead to environmental and health issues, including drug residues(Beylefeld, et al., 2018). Exploring alternative prevention and control strategies is crucial. Traditional Chinese medicine offers a comprehensive approach to infectious disease prevention and treatment through its multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway effects. A Traditional Chinese medicine compound Mixture, based on traditional theory, includes Sinomenium acutum, Spatholobus suberectus, Dioscorea nipponica, Radix Aconiti Praeparata, and Cyathula officinalis. This formulation combines the unique properties of each component, exerting anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and immune-regulatory effects through synergistic interactions. Notably, Sinomenium acutum, rich in alkaloids, exhibits significant anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anti-allergic, and immunosuppressive properties​(Ding, et al., 2022). Spatholobus suberectus is abundant in polyphenols and flavonoids, exhibiting anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hematopoietic, and immune-regulatory properties (Zhang, et al., 2022). Dioscorea nipponica is primarily composed of steroidal saponins, with studies confirming its significant analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects (Wu, et al., 2023). Radix Aconiti Praeparata contains various alkaloids that alleviate central and peripheral pain and display anti-inflammatory activity (Chan, et al., 2021). Cyathula officinalis includes saponins and flavonoids, promoting blood circulation, clearing collaterals, and providing immune regulation and anti-inflammatory benefits (Huang, et al., 2019). The combination of these components yields a synergistic effect, enhancing anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and immune-regulatory activities, thereby improving the overall efficacy in preventing and treating MS infections.

This study employed LC-MC technology to analyze the components of TCM, identifying 637 compounds, with a notable presence of alkaloids, flavonoids, and organic acids. These components are known for their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immune-regulating properties(Shang, et al., 2024). Pharmacokinetic analysis highlighted 18 key active compounds, including sinomenine, magnoflorine, formononetin and menisperine. Contemporary research indicates that sinomenine mitigates rheumatoid arthritis by inhibiting synovial fibroblast secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reducing cell migration and invasion, and promoting apoptosis(Jiang, et al., 2023). Formononetin, an isoflavone, demonstrates significant therapeutic effects on immune diseases, exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative actions by inhibiting the interferon signaling pathway, with noted efficacy in psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis(Li, et al., 2024; Xu, et al., 2024).

MS infection frequently causes joint lesions in poultry by triggering an immune response that damages synovial tissue, resulting in arthritis and synovitis (Dusanic, et al., 2012). However, we acknowledge that this study did not explicitly confirm the MS burden in chickens. The primary focus of our study was on the clinical improvements associated with MS infection and inflammation, which were assessed based on clinical symptom scores and joint pathology observations. In the model group, clinical symptom scores and tarsal joint dissection scores were significantly higher than those in the control group, with substantial increases in IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels. The three TCM treatment groups showed improvements in the clinical symptoms and joint lesions caused by MS infection, alongside a reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in a dose-dependent manner, though the levels remained higher than those in the control group. These findings indicate that MS infection was successfully established and that TCM treatment can ameliorate joint lesions and alleviate inflammation. However, we cannot definitively conclude that TCM eliminates the MS infection itself.

This study identified 147 action targets related to MS infection through component-target network analysis. Subsequent PPI network analysis highlighted 13 core targets, offering insights into the molecular mechanisms by which TCM may counteract MS infection. Contemporary research indicates that targets such as VEGFA and EGFR facilitate pannus formation, leading to synovial hyperplasia and intensifying joint inflammation, making them critical targets for arthritis treatment(Chen, et al., 2022a; Kloesch, et al., 2013). Targets like PIK3CA, JAK2, and STAT3 exacerbate arthritis by enhancing inflammatory responses through cell proliferation and survival regulation (Tao, et al., 2024). Our study demonstrates that TCM effectively mitigates synovial inflammation and tissue damage caused by MS infection by modulating the JAK2-STAT3 and PI3K-AKT pathways. The JAK2-STAT3 pathway, a central component of the inflammatory response, becomes overactivated during MS infection, triggering a surge of pro-inflammatory factors. Inhibiting this pathway significantly reduces inflammatory cytokine production and alleviates joint inflammation (Yang, et al., 2024). Additionally, the PI3K-AKT pathway is linked to cell proliferation, synovial hyperplasia, and joint degeneration (Shen, et al., 2022). Importantly, TCM also suppresses the PI3K-AKT pathway, indicating a "dual-pathway synergy" that may disrupt the cycle of inflammation and tissue damage.

This study is the first to investigate the impact of MS infection on chicken metabolism and gut microbiota. Although the TCM-H group exhibited the most pronounced clinical effects, particularly in reducing lesions and improving clinical symptoms, the TCM-M group also demonstrated significant efficacy in clinical scoring and lesion reduction. Given that the therapeutic effects of TCM-M are sufficiently robust to be considered an effective intervention, we chose to prioritize the TCM-M group for further metabolomic and microbiome analysis in this study. Moreover, compared to TCM-H, TCM-M offers greater clinical applicability, particularly in terms of cost-effectiveness and practicality, striking a more favorable balance for clinical use. Consequently, to more accurately assess its effectiveness and feasibility in real-world applications, the TCM-M group was selected for more detailed investigation. Metabolomic analysis revealed that MS infection significantly disrupted amino acid and energy metabolism. TCM partially restored these metabolic levels. Concurrently, 16S rDNA sequencing showed that MS infection altered the gut microbiota, notably reducing the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria. TCM effectively mitigated this dysbiosis. The balance of gut microbiota is crucial for maintaining immunity and overall health, explaining the preventive and therapeutic effects of TCM.

The inhibitory effect of TCM on SRC kinase suggests it may regulate synovial fibroblast migration and invasion by disrupting the "SRC-PI3K-AKT" axis. Activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway is known to upregulate glycolysis-related enzymes, enhance aerobic glycolysis of glucose​(Burns and Manda, 2017), and mediate metabolic reprogramming, resulting in significant lactic acid production under aerobic conditions, akin to the Warburg effect (Pan, et al., 2022). This study observed increased serum pyruvate and lactic acid levels following MS infection, indicating a shift in cellular energy metabolism towards glycolysis. As a glycolysis product, pyruvate is the sole precursor for entry into the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and its level fluctuations directly impact mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation energy supply(Cao, et al., 2023). This metabolic pattern mirrors the condition in traditional Chinese medicine known as Bi syndrome, where "deficiency of healthy qi and blockage of meridians" impairs nutrient delivery, leading to inadequate energy supply and metabolite accumulation in joints. Conversely, active compounds like alkaloids and flavonoids in TCM have been shown to boost mitochondrial enzyme activity and restore oxidative phosphorylation(Sapian, et al., 2021). Research indicates that flavonoids such as apigenin can exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects by modulating immune cell metabolic pathways, including those of T cells (He, et al., 2021). These compounds enhance mitochondrial respiratory enzyme activity and support the TCA cycle, thereby reversing the Warburg metabolic pattern induced by MS infection and restoring efficient cellular energy utilization. This aligns with traditional Chinese medicine concept of "eliminating pathogenic factors and dredging meridians" to clear pathological products, enhance qi and blood flow, and restore meridian function, suggesting a novel approach for preventing and treating infectious diseases through the regulation of immune metabolism and mitochondrial function.

MS infection disrupts chicken gut microbiota, notably decreasing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio and increasing Campylobacter abundance. These alterations correlate with joint lesion severity. The F/B ratio is a key indicator of microbiota balance, with deviations signaling dysregulation(Stojanov, et al., 2020). Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are crucial for gut homeostasis; a high ratio often results in weight gain, while a low ratio is associated with gut inflammation (Stojanov, et al., 2020). Thus, the reduced F/B ratio from MS infection suggests diminished anti-inflammatory microbiota, potentially worsening gut and systemic inflammation. Additionally, increased Campylobacter abundance can trigger reactive arthritis by activating host immune responses, promoting synovial inflammation(Taneja, 2014).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis extends beyond the gut, impacting the immune environment of distant joints via the "gut-joint axis" (Longo, et al., 2024). Recent research indicates that alterations in gut microbiota can provoke systemic immune responses through mechanisms like endotoxin leakage, immune cell migration, and metabolite circulation, affecting joint inflammation (Omenetti and Pizarro, 2015). This study observed a significant increase in the phylum Firmicutes following TCM treatment. The phylum Firmicutes is abundant in probiotics that produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyric acid, which are crucial for gut barrier integrity and immune tolerance (Nan, et al., 2022). SCFAs have been shown to modulate the Th17/Treg balance via the "gut-joint axis," reducing excessive local pro-inflammatory factors in joints (Zhang, et al., 2025). Our study observed restoration of butyric acid metabolites like β-D-galactose and a significant increase in serum IL-10 following TCM treatment, highlighting the interplay between microbiota, immunity, and metabolism. Additionally, flavonoids such as formononetin in TCM may function through dual mechanisms: directly inhibiting synovial inflammation and serving as prebiotics to stimulate the growth of flavonoid-degrading bacteria. These bacteria convert flavonoids into anti-inflammatory small molecules, thereby enhancing systemic immune regulation(Braune and Blaut, 2016).

Metabolomic analysis indicates that MS infection significantly disrupts the glycine-serine-threonine metabolic axis, essential for articular cartilage repair and anti-inflammatory processes. Glycine and serine, key amino acids in collagen, are critical for articular cartilage repair, and their deficiency may impair this capacity ​(McCarty, et al., 2018). In vitro studies demonstrate that glycine supplementation markedly enhances type II collagen synthesis in chondrocytes (de Paz-Lugo, et al., 2018). Furthermore, glycine is a vital precursor for glutathione synthesis, crucial for maintaining glutathione levels and mitigating oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals, thereby protecting articular cartilage from oxidative damage (McCarty, et al., 2018). Threonine plays a crucial role in the synthesis of intestinal mucin, and disruptions in its metabolism can impair intestinal immune function and compromise the mucosal barrier, heightening the risk of secondary infections. Additionally,TCM influences the glyoxylate cycle, a key pathway in microbial energy metabolism. Restoring this pathway may help alleviate joint inflammation by modulating microbiota functions (Cong, et al., 2023). This intricate "metabolism-microbiota-immunity" regulatory network underpins TCM's multi-target effects. By modulating interactions among host, microbiota, and immune systems, TCM effectively addresses inflammation and joint damage, highlighting its systemic approach to treating complex diseases.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel traditional Chinese medicine compound mixture focus on MS infection, TCM. Using an MS infection model, we demonstrated the compound's significant efficacy in ameliorating MS-induced symptoms. Employing network pharmacology, metabolomics, and 16S rDNA sequencing, we explored the active ingredients and mechanisms underlying the TCM compound's ameliorative effects under MS challenge. Our findings indicate that the compound inhibits inflammatory factor release and mitigates tarsal joint damage by modulating the JAK2-STAT3 and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways. Metabolomic analysis revealed that MS infection disrupts amino acid and energy metabolism, with glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism identified as key pathways through which the TCM compound acts against MS. Additionally, the compound ameliorates MS-induced gut microbiota imbalance. These results elucidate the active ingredients and mechanisms of TCM against MS infection, providing a foundation for its clinical application in treatment and symptom management. Meanwhile, we emphasize that while TCM treatment alleviated symptoms, future studies incorporating pathogen quantification and TCM-only controls are needed to better delineate the therapeutic vs. preventative effects.
