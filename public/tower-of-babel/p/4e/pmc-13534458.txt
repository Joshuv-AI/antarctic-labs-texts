Intensity-correlation synthetic wavelength imaging in dynamic scattering media
Authors: Khaled Kassem, Areeba Fatima, Patrick Cornwall, Muralidhar Madabhushi Balaji, Daniele Faccio, Florian Willomitzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImaging through dynamic scattering media, such as biological tissue, presents a fundamental challenge due to light scattering and the formation of speckle patterns. These patterns not only degrade image quality but also decorrelate rapidly, limiting the effectiveness of conventional approaches, such as those based on transmission matrix measurements. Here, we introduce an imaging approach based on second-order correlations and synthetic wavelength holography to enable robust image reconstruction through thick and dynamic scattering media. By exploiting intensity speckle correlations and using short-exposure intensity images, our method computationally reconstructs images from a hologram without requiring phase stability or static speckles, making it inherently resilient to phase noise. Experimental results demonstrate high-resolution imaging in both static and dynamic scattering scenarios, offering a promising solution for biomedical imaging, remote sensing, and real-time imaging in complex environments.Subject terms: Imaging and sensing, Optical physics

Introduction

Imaging through scattering media-such as fog, biological tissue, or smoke-is essential for applications including medical diagnostics, autonomous vehicle navigation, and remote sensing in low-visibility or turbulent environments1–4.

However, multiple scattering in these media leads to chaotic speckle patterns, rendering traditional imaging methods (based e.g. on just a camera) ineffective in reconstructing the object. Various techniques have been developed to tackle the challenge of imaging in static complex media, including wavefront shaping5–9, transmission matrix (TM) methods10–13, and speckle-correlation techniques14–16. While these approaches have shown significant success, they provide limited performance through thick scatterers. More importantly, their effectiveness is often compromised in dynamic environments where the slightest movements lead to a complete decorrelation of the speckle patterns.To counteract these affects, speckle deblurring techniques have been explored, but they remain limited by speckle coherence and typically work only for simple objects17. Machine learning (ML)-based approaches have also been investigated, leveraging data-driven models to reconstruct images18–21. However, these methods often require extensive labelled datasets and lack physical priors, limiting their adaptability to highly dynamic scenarios where, e.g. both microscopic and macroscopic properties change in time. Quantum imaging techniques utilizing entangled photon pairs offer potential solutions for imaging through scattering22, yet their reliance on polarization entanglement makes them unsuitable for strongly scattering media where the polarization is changed.

In this context, synthetic wavelength holography (SWH)23,24 has emerged as a relatively recent technique for imaging through scattering media that exploits speckle correlations to mitigate the effect of scattering. Here, synthetic waves resulting from the beating between two coherent light waves at closely spaced wavelengths (see Fig. 1) traverse through the scattering media. The synthetic field assembled at the far end of the scattering medium has been shown to be the holographic representation of the hidden object placed in the media. These holograms preserve the phase information at scales exceeding the synthetic wavelengths, hence overcoming the deleterious effects of scattering.

However, this approach requires capturing complex-valued fields at closely spaced optical wavelengths for hologram generation, necessitating the use of holographic acquisition methods. Although it has been shown that this process can be performed in single-shot25,26, optical phase stability during the camera exposure is still required, which imposes space-time-bandwidth restrictions on current acquisition systems.

In this work, we introduce a paradigm that relaxes these constraints by exploiting the second-order correlation i.e., the g(2) correlations. As these g(2) correlations can be evaluated from intensity only measurements at different time instances, the need for holographic acquisition apparatus is no longer needed and the robustness of the system to phase fluctuations is significantly improved.

In general, the g(2) correlations have been studied for high-resolution, phase-robust microscopy in both quantum applications27 and classical approaches inspired by quantum sensing techniques, such as Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) interference with two beating wavelengths28,29. While intensity correlations based interferometers are known to be immune to time-dependent phase fluctuations, they still need spatial coherence, which is often absent in dynamic scattering environments30,31. Furthermore, other correlation-based holography techniques32 demand quick measurements to track rapidly evolving speckle patterns, a challenge in highly scattering and low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions. Spatial speckle correlations at two closely spaced wavelengths have been shown to extract information from scattering media33. However, the approach works best for sparse objects to get significant correlation signals, and require high spatial sampling density.

In our new approach, we reformulate the synthetic wavelength holography (SWH) principles under the framework of g(2) correlation formalism. We show that the mathematical implication developed therein lends itself well to the application of imaging through a dynamic scattering media such that the inherent fluctuation of the dynamic media can be harnessed for image reconstruction. The efficacy of our approach is shown across various scenarios, including traditional static two-planes of scatter settings, complex volume scattering, and dynamic scattering environments. To further enhance image reconstruction, we integrate a total variation (TV)-based denoising algorithm and iterative backpropagation. By combining both physical and computational approaches, our method not only provides a robust solution for imaging through dynamic scattering media but also shows how dynamic scattering can be utilized as an asset rather than a limitation.

We point out that the phase stepping phase difference is not to be confused with the randomized phase Δφ discussed earlier. They operate on different temporal and length scales and have different implications. The former is due to the relatively larger optical delay (on the order of the SWL Λ) introduced in one of the arms of the interferometer. It evolves over a longer timescale and is considered constant over all the elements of the ensemble. As such, it doesn’t average out to zero for the operation defined as 〈⋅〉t while calculating g(2) correlations. On the other hand, the phase φ is random phase ascribed to each of the elements of the ensemble. These are generated by introducing a very small delay in one of the interferometer arm, on the order of the optical wavelength, and therefore, are on sub micron-scale. They change over the timescale corresponding to each acquisition of the ensemble. Hence, it varies in the range of (0, 2π) over all the copies of the ensemble (and so averages out to zero, as discussed for Eq. (9)). Each acquisition in the ensemble must be captured with a camera exposure time shorter than the interferometer’s decorrelation time. This is essential to minimize noise, path length jitter, and scatterer movement. However, this requirement in no way limits our proposed method because performing the ensemble average over hundreds of frames compensates for these short acquisition times and effectively enhances the SNR. By increasing the number of acquired frames, we can mitigate the trade-off between exposure time and signal recovery, ensuring robust measurements. This is in stark contrast to traditional holography methods, which often require exposure times of milliseconds to seconds, which can be a limiting factor therein.

As discussed above, this technique is robust against phase noise owing to the time averaging requirement. This lends itself well to the case of imaging through dynamic scattering media. This is because as explained above phase randomization is necessary for the measurement of g(2) correlations. We can exploit the naturally induced phase noise from dynamic scattering to achieve the required random phases over different copies of the ensemble to carry out the averaging denoted by 〈⋅〉t of Eq. (8) which further simplifies the setup. One may ask whether the signal corresponds to a real-time intensity beat that could be directly resolved using a fast detector such as an indirect time-of-flight (iToF) camera. In the present implementation, the camera integrates the intensity over timescales much longer than the optical beat period. Consequently, the beat note is not directly resolved in real time. Instead, the relevant phase information is encoded in the interferometric delay variable. In principle, time-resolved imaging sensors could provide access to intensity modulations. However, in practice this is not feasible because corresponding beat frequencies for our used SWL span the range from tens of gigahertz up to the terahertz regime, which is far beyond the time resolving capabilities of existing iToF sensors. In regimes where very large synthetic wavelengths (e.g., hundreds of MHz) are sufficient, a direct detection with iToF sensors can be an option. The presented approach, however, avoids this requirement by transferring the beat resolution to the delay domain.

We point out that although the method is robust against phase noise, some degree of sensitivity to phase noise can still be present depending on the maximum path length variation induced by the scatterer and can affect the optimal choice of the SWL23,24. We discuss this in later sections.

Results

2-plane scattering

We begin by examining the simplest case of scattering at two distinct planes, implemented using optical diffusers in the experimental setup as shown in Fig. 1. Here, Scatterer A and Scatterer B are 220-grit and 120-grit optical diffusers, respectively. We investigate two measurement configurations: transmission, where the object is embedded between the two diffusers, and reflection, where the object is positioned behind a diffuser, with both illumination and imaging occurring from the same side, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

In both configurations, the incident light undergoes an initial scattering at the first diffuser before interacting with the object. The light is subsequently scattered again by the second diffuser, leading to approximately two distinct planes of scattering. The scattered light is subsequently imaged with a camera, where it interferes with a reference beam.

A 3D-printed letter “A” was used as the object, as shown in Fig. 1(c). The g(2) holograms E~(Λ) were generated using Eq. (11) by capturing 1000 frames at three optical delay phase steps. The resulting holograms were then computationally backpropagated using the angular spectrum method (ASM) (see Methods) which reveals the object information.

As discussed previously, phase randomization is necessary. Here, we investigate how the reconstruction changes when we apply phase randomization in two ways: first, by controlled phase randomization in the reference arm, as in standard g(2), achieved by randomly moving the piezo shown in Fig. 1. The second one, by utilizing the phase randomization from a dynamic scatterer. Volume scattering

We then demonstrate more challenging cases, specifically for volume scatterers for both transmission and reflection configurations. For the reflection configuration, we employed a scatterer with a thickness of 2.7 cm and a reduced scattering coefficient, μs′=0.22mm−1, corresponding to an average of 12 scattering events (see Supplementary Material for details). For the transmission configuration, we used two thinner yet stronger scattering elements to embed the object. The volume scatterer on the camera side was 1.5 cm thick with a reduced scattering coefficient of μs′=1.1mm−1, which is comparable to the human tissue. The scatterer on the laser side was 0.8 cm thick with a reduced scattering coefficient of μs′=1.1mm−1, leading to an average of 25 scattering events. Reflection measurements are demonstrated using the weaker scattering sample, as this configuration is inherently more challenging due to geometric constraints, strong surface backscattering, and the additional scattering occurring at the object upon backreflection.

Given the increased scattering in these cases, the choice of Λ becomes crucial. There is an inherent trade-off between resolution and SNR or reconstruction quality23. Stronger scattering requires a larger Λ in order to allow imaging at deeper depths (i.e. larger Λ are less affected by scattering), which, however, compromises resolution (i.e. the standard Rayleigh criterion applies and the smallest resolvable features scale with Λ).

However, this trade-off is essential for balancing the SNR and reconstruction accuracy. However, image quality is then improved by deploying iterative optimization algorithms and TV denoising techniques to converge to improved reconstructions of the object and also implement the fusion of images acquired at different wavelengths Λ thus merging low-resolution but higher SNR images from longer synthetic wavelengths with higher-resolution but lower SNR images from shorter synthetic wavelengths (see Supplementary Material for details).

In our experimental setup, we first investigated a static scatterer with a 3D-printed letter “G" object, in reflection. We performed measurements with two synthetic wavelengths, Λ = 3.08 mm and Λ = 5.33 mm. To generate these synthetic wavelengths the first optical wavelength was kept constant at λ1 = 854.31 nm, while the second was changed to λ2 = 854.07 nm and λ2 = 854.17 nm, respectively. The corresponding reconstruction results are shown in Fig. 3(a), where we present the back-propagated fields for each wavelength and the computationally fused result. As expected, the smaller Λ provides more details but exhibits more noise. The iterative algorithm aims to balance these effects, optimizing both SNR and resolution.

We note that SWH inherently supports depth-resolved and multi-plane imaging, as demonstrated in previous work23. In the present study, we intentionally restrict the experiments to single-depth scenes in order to isolate and analyze the robustness of the method under dynamic scattering and phase fluctuations. The extension to multi-depth objects follows directly from established SWL reconstruction principles and is therefore orthogonal to the focus of this work.

Discussion

In this work, we introduced a novel method for generating synthetic wavelength holograms for imaging through scattering media using g(2) correlations, circumventing the need for phase stability and effectively, leveraging the phase randomisation that is intrinsic to dynamically changing complex media. Our approach was demonstrated to effectively operate in challenging scenarios, including imaging through dynamic and thick diffusers. We highlighted the underlying technique by progressing through steps of increasing image-reconstruction difficulty, starting from thin diffusers in transmission or reflection mode, to a thick static diffuser, and finally to the dynamic thick diffuser.

Our findings highlight the importance of selecting an optimal synthetic wavelength to balance SNR and resolution, as well as the benefits of incorporating multiple synthetic wavelengths and computational techniques for improved image reconstruction. The intensity-correlation approach performs best when the scatterers are dynamically changing. This is particularly relevant in medical bioimaging settings.

In the following, we further discuss our approach by contrasting it to established approaches to image through scattering media.

Gated and confocal imaging

We point out that gated imaging and confocal approaches, when employed to solve the same problem, rely on temporal or spatial filtering to suppress multiply scattered light and primarily detect directly scattered (ballistic or quasi-ballistic) photons. These techniques are well established and can perform very effectively under appropriate conditions, but they typically require fast time-resolved detectors, scanning mechanisms, or precise spatial filtering, which increases hardware complexity and may introduce acquisition constraints. In contrast, the proposed synthetic wavelength intensity-correlation approach follows a conceptually different strategy. It does not rely on time-gating or spatial filtering. Instead, it reconstructs a synthetic wave using second-order intensity correlations acquired with a conventional CMOS camera. Importantly, since we perform full-field imaging, the method utilizes all detected photons, including multiply scattered ones, rather than rejecting them through filtering. This can result in SNR benefits for certain regimes. Moreover, our approach allows the use of comparatively simple hardware and avoids the need for ultrafast electronics or scanning architectures. We therefore view the proposed method as complementary to existing gated and confocal imaging approaches. It offers an alternative operating regime that prioritizes simplicity of implementation and compatibility with standard imaging sensors and may be particularly attractive in scenarios where conventional filtering-based approaches become impractical or overly complex.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

At a conceptual level, our approach acquires each synthetic field independently and eventually combines all fields digitally. This is different from OCT, where the signal is generated through a continuous or rapidly swept optical spectrum. In this sense, we share the coherence-gating principle of OCT, while differing substantially in implementation. In particular, unlike many OCT architectures, we operate in a full-field configuration. This full-field acquisition captures the complex field over the entire aperture simultaneously, enabling digital propagation and post-processing without raster scanning, A-scans, or B-scans. Secondly, the imaging depth is usually limited to millimetres range for OCT. For the proposed method of intensity correlated synthetic wavelength imaging, the depth in principle can be extended to centimetres to meters by increasing the synthetic wavelength. This is due to the fact that unlike OCT, synthetic fields can be assembled from completely scattered photons and do not rely on gating out ballistic photons, like the most common OCT approaches do. While the above points can be seen as advantages of SWI over OCT, there are also limitations: OCT has fewer restrictions on phase stability as it doesn’t require separate capturing of optical fields. Moreover, as OCT filters out ballistic photons, it can achieve correlated measurements at a much higher spectral bandwidth, which results in much better depth resolution.

Diffuse optical tomography (DOT)

Among other techniques, Diffuse Optical Tomography (DOT) is a common modality for imaging through scattering media; however, it involves solving a highly ill-posed inverse problem that relies on a specific forward model. To formulate this forward model, knowledge of the scatterer’s optical properties is required, which is often done by a pre-calibration process. In the scenarios considered in this work, we recover object information by backpropagating the field at the SWL recorded en face. Therefore, for the experiments shown in this paper, we do not need a pre-calibration process (as we would in DOT), which is a significant simplification and advantage and of our approach. However, we would like to emphasize that DOT literature demonstrates measurements at depths much greater than shown in our current work. For SWI measurements at these large depths, it is possible that similar calibration procedures might be required. Examining this effect will be part of our future work, as this is not within the scope of this paper.

In summary, we have demonstrated an imaging approach based on second-order correlations that enables image reconstruction through dynamic scattering media. Although this may have potential applications in the previously mentioned domains, such as medical diagnostics, autonomous vehicle navigation, and remote sensing, further development will be required before it can be implemented in practical systems. In future work, we aim to test this method for applications such as imaging through skin and other biological tissues.

Methods

Experimental setup

The experimental setup is based on a fiber-based Mach-Zehnder interferometer, as shown in Fig. 1. A Toptica DFB Pro 855 nm laser with two separate laser heads is directly coupled into a fiber beamsplitter (Thorlabs TW850R5A2) serving as the input beamsplitter. One output is collimated in free-space (Thorlabs F810APC collimator), forming the reference arm. The collimator is mounted on a piezo stage (Thorlabs NF15AP25/M) for phase randomization and is in turn mounted on a courser but longer tuning range motorized translation stage for Λ phase stepping. The reference mirror displacement is limited to 25 μm, corresponding to phase modulation over multiple 2π cycles. This modulation introduces phase diversity across frames, which is essential for correlation-based reconstruction. The other beamsplitter output is also collimated and directed onto the scattering samples. The object position depends on the measurement geometry: for a diffuser, the object is placed ~10 cm behind the imaged diffuser and an additional 10 cm from the other illuminated diffuser; for bulk scattering, the object is placed as close as possible for transmission measurements (sandwich configuration) or 1–2 cm from the imaged bulk scatterer for reflection measurements.

The interferometer outputs are combined using a 50:50 free-space beamsplitter (Thorlabs BS033). A single 50 mm lens images the scattering surface onto the camera in a finite-conjugate configuration, such that the scatterer plane and the sensor plane are conjugate. The lens-to-scatterer distance is roughly 80 cm. An iris is placed in front of the lens to ensure that the speckle size matches the camera pixel size when necessary. Measurements are recorded using a Basler acA720-520um CMOS camera operating at 500 Hz with a resolution of 720 × 540 pixels. The effective image size is 40 × 30 mm, corresponding to an effective pixel size of 55 μm (physical pixel size: 6.9 μm). Each camera frame captures an intensity-only image of the scatterer surface.

The exposure time is chosen to be shorter than the characteristic decorrelation time of the speckle pattern, typically on the order of tens to hundreds of microseconds. During experiments with active phase randomization, the reference mirror is driven at a frequency comparable to the camera frame rate, introducing phase variations between successive frames. In experiments with dynamic scattering, a vibration motor operating at 60 Hz induces random motion of the scattering medium. The exposure time is empirically adjusted to ensure that the speckle pattern remains quasi-static within each frame while decorrelating across successive frames.
