Dengue surveillance: complementary strategies for national monitoring
Authors: Diego Martín Flichman, Patricia Baré, Federico Alejandro Di Lello
Published: 2026

We thank the colleagues for their thoughtful appraisal of our study1,2 and agree that Argentina urgently needs a national dengue serosurveillance system integrating probability-based population surveys, haemovigilance monitoring, and transparent burden models.

The correspondents rightly observe that blood donors constitute a distinct subpopulation subject to selection biases. Nevertheless, our study was primarily designed to assess transfusion safety and provide sentinel evidence of dengue virus (DENV) circulation among blood donors in three Argentine regions, rather than to establish a definitive national denominator. As we acknowledged in the limitations section of our manuscript, blood donors differ demographically and socioeconomically from the general population, and our sampling frame does not constitute a probability-based national survey. We explicitly noted that these constraints limit generalizability and that our estimates should be interpreted with caution.1 In this sense, we agree that a multi-stage, probability-based household sampling design—such as the study cited in the City of Buenos Aires3—remains the gold standard for absolute population-level inference. However, nationwide, real-time household serosurveys during large-scale epidemics pose immense logistical and financial challenges, particularly in geographically expansive and economically constrained settings.

Rather than presenting a definitive census, our national infection estimate aimed to illustrate the massive underreporting inherent to passive surveillance. Beyond any potential selection biases, the dramatic shift from 23.1% to 50.6% seroprevalence across identical sampling sites between 2023 and 2025 undeniably demonstrates a widespread exposure. In this light, we acknowledge that confidence intervals around such estimates cannot convey the same level of population precision as those derived from calibrated, population-based designs.

Regarding the comparison with the City of Buenos Aires study, which enrolled 1493 adults,3 we agree that population-based studies provide valuable jurisdiction-specific estimates. Nevertheless, direct comparisons should be undertaken with caution. In addition to differences in geographic scope and sampling design, the studies employed distinct serological methodologies. Variability in assay performance may therefore contribute to differences in seroprevalence estimates, particularly given the well-documented variation in sensitivity between anti-DENV IgG ELISA and rapid immunochromatographic assays for detecting past dengue infection.4, 5, 6 Methodological heterogeneity should therefore be considered when interpreting differences in seroprevalence estimates.

In conclusion, we fully agree that Argentina requires an integrated national dengue serosurveillance strategy. Sentinel haemovigilance and probability-based population surveys should not be viewed as mutually exclusive or competing methodologies, but rather as complementary pillars. Our study contributes to this effort by documenting the sharp increase in exposure among blood donors and by underscoring the urgency of strengthening epidemic preparedness.
