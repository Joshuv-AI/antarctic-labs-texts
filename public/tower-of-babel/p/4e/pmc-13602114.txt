Heat exchange rates between adult sockeye salmon (Oncorhynchus nerka) and the environment
Authors: Avery Dextrase, David A Patterson, Eduardo G Martins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBody temperatures of salmon do not always match the temperatures of their immediate environment. We investigated the relationship between varying ambient water temperatures and body temperatures of adult sockeye salmon Oncorhynchus nerka using Newton's law of cooling and non‐linear mixed models. The median estimated coefficient of heat exchange k for O. nerka in this study was 0.09 min−1 with corresponding temperature change halftime and thermal time constant values of 7.7 and 11.1 min, respectively. There was little support for salmon condition factor and mass impacting k, or differences in k when the body was cooling or warming. Metabolically produced heat had a very small effect on body temperature. The bodies of the salmon did not instantaneously equilibrate with ambient temperatures. Model‐averaged predictions showed that it would take approximately 60 min for the core body temperature of an adult O. nerka to equilibrate with ambient temperature (i.e., rate of body temperature change <0.01°C min−1) after moving into water temperatures 10°C cooler or warmer than their initial body temperature. Greater differences in body and ambient temperatures were predicted when the salmon experienced rapid and larger changes in ambient temperature. The differences in body and ambient temperatures experienced by Babine Lake O. nerka in this study highlight a need to exhibit caution when equating water temperature to the body temperature of fish in thermally heterogeneous environments across short timescales. The estimated values of k and Tm from this study could be applied to other research requiring body temperature predictions for free‐ranging adult O. nerka or possibly other similarly sized and shaped salmonids without using internal temperature loggers.Keywords: Babine Lake, body temperature, heat transfer, metabolic heat, shuttle box, sockeye salmon

INTRODUCTION

Since most fishes are ectotherms, temperature significantly impacts their physiology, behaviour and fitness (Huey & Berrigan, 2001; Huey & Stevenson, 1979) and has long been regarded as their master environmental factor (Brett, 1971). Unsurprisingly, body temperatures experienced by fishes in the wild are commonly measured and analysed in ecological studies (e.g., Donaldson et al., 2009; Hight & Lowe, 2007; Nakamura et al., 2020; Raby et al., 2018). Accurately measuring the core body temperatures of free‐ranging fishes over time usually requires a temperature logger to be gastrically or surgically implanted into the body cavity, which can be costly, time‐consuming and may negatively impact the fish (Dick et al., 2020; Jepsen et al., 2015; Thorstad et al., 2013). Some researchers have avoided using internal temperature loggers and assumed that fish body temperature is equivalent to the water temperatures they occupy (e.g., Armstrong et al., 2016; Hein & Keirsted, 2012; Thums et al., 2013). While this assumption may be accurate for small fish like salmon fry, it fails for larger fish in thermally heterogenous environments since it takes time for their core body temperature to equilibrate with the water temperature (Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Spigarelli et al., 1977). Notable differences in fish body and water temperatures occur after a fish dives from the surface of a lake to below the thermocline or during brief forays into hot or cold areas for feeding (Meekan et al., 2015; Pépino et al., 2015). Differences may also be seen when salmon and other physostomous fishes approach the water's surface to regulate buoyancy. Failing to consider these differences could lead to incorrect assumptions about the thermal constraints on wild fish populations (Donaldson et al., 2008; Raby et al., 2025).

An alternative way to estimate body temperatures of fish in the wild without using internal temperature loggers is to use Newton's law of cooling (Pépino et al., 2015). It has been used to investigate the heat exchange between fish and ambient water (e.g., Azumaya & Ishida, 2005; Brill et al., 1994; Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Pépino et al., 2015; Stevens & Sutterlin, 1976). Newton's law of cooling states that an object's instantaneous rate of temperature change over time (t), dTbdt, is proportional to the difference between its temperature (Tb) and the ambient temperature (Ta) (Gaur et al., 2019; Gockenbach & Schmidtke, 2009). In the context of heat transfer between fish and water, Tb is core body temperature. The whole‐body heat exchange coefficient k (unit of time−1) represents the rate at which the core of a fish's body warms or cools to match the ambient water temperature (henceforth ambient temperature). Tm (unit of °C time−1) represents the rate of metabolic heat production of a fish (Brill et al., 1994; Pépino et al., 2015; Stevens & Sutterlin, 1976).

Coefficient k may vary depending on whether the fish is cooling or warming, especially for fish with countercurrent exchange systems, although this is not a consistent trend across all fish species and studies (Fechhelm & Neill, 1982; Holland et al., 1992; Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Pépino et al., 2015; Spigarelli et al., 1977; Stevens & Fry, 1974). For example, recent studies found non‐significant differences in heating and cooling coefficient k estimates for lake trout Salvelinus namaycush and brook trout Salvelinus fontinalis (Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Pépino et al., 2015). Previous studies have also shown that k is negatively related to body mass in a variety of fish species (e.g., Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Pépino et al., 2015; Spigarelli et al., 1977), indicating that heavier bodies exchange heat with the water at a slower rate than lighter bodies. This is because a fish with a larger mass has greater thermal inertia and lower body surface‐to‐volume ratio than a fish of lower mass but similar length and shape (Nakamura et al., 2020; Pépino et al., 2015). Mass is also associated with the metabolic rate of fish (Clarke & Johnston, 1999). Larger individuals typically have greater metabolic rates, however the metabolic rate per unit of body mass may be higher for smaller individuals (Chabot et al., 2016; Clarke & Johnston, 1999; Ohlberger et al., 2012). Therefore, Tm may be lower for larger fish (per unit of mass) than for smaller fish.

Despite the usefulness of Newton's law of cooling to estimate body temperatures of free‐ranging fish, the required estimates of k and Tm and their relationships with body size are limited to a smaller number of fishes, especially among the salmonids (e.g., S. fontinalis, Pépino et al., 2015; S. namaycush, Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; chum salmon Oncorhynchus keta, Azumaya & Ishida, 2005; brown trout Salmo trutta, Spigarelli et al., 1977). Given the importance of investigating fish responses to water temperature changes as the climate warms, increasing the number of species for which Newton's law of cooling can be applied could facilitate data collection on fish body temperatures in many scenarios. For example, the relationship could be applicable in studies where core body temperatures are required but external temperature logger tags are used instead of internal loggers to reduce handling time or avoid potential complications associated with anaesthetic use and implantation procedures (Cooke et al., 2011; Jepsen et al., 2015; Raby et al., 2017). Additionally, this approach could be used to predict the body temperatures of a fish when its location in a thermal gradient is known over time (e.g., Mathes et al., 2010). Furthermore, knowing how quickly the body temperature of a fish changes with ambient temperature can help researchers define individual‐specific ramping rates in critical thermal maximum (CTmax) trials to ensure that their body temperatures change at the same rates regardless of their size. This would avoid issues of comparability of CTmax estimates that often arise by subjecting fish of different masses to the same ramping rates (Desforges et al., 2023).

Here, we investigate the relationship between varying ambient temperatures and body temperatures of adult sockeye salmon Oncorhynchus nerka. Water and body temperatures were recorded every 1.5 min for the duration of 24‐h laboratory trials in a shuttle box system (Loligo® Systems, Denmark) where fish moved frequently between tanks with different and dynamic water temperatures. We used these data, Newton's law of cooling and non‐linear mixed models to estimate the whole‐body heat exchange coefficient k and the rate of metabolic heat production Tm of adult O. nerka. The relationship between k, fish body size and a heating versus cooling body, and between Tm and mass were explored. We illustrate the use of the parametrized models by predicting the body temperatures of free‐ranging O. nerka that were equipped with external temperature loggers while they passed through Babine Lake in British Columbia, Canada.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fish capture and data collection

Adult migrating female O. nerka were captured at the Babine River Counting Fence, a semi‐permanent structure located approximately 1 km downriver from Nilkitkwa Lake in British Columbia, Canada (Figure 1). Fish were dip‐netted from a fence trap box and immediately transferred to a fish trough (16 L) with a constant flow‐through of river water. For handling, fish were temporarily immobilized using electric fish handling gloves (Smith‐Root, Vancouver, USA) set to a current output of 5 mA (Reid et al., 2019). The fish were equipped with two identical temperature loggers (DST micro‐T, Star Oddi, Iceland) that recorded temperature every 1.5 min with an accuracy of 0.06°C and a resolution of 0.003°C. One of the loggers was coated in petroleum jelly and inserted gastrically (non‐surgically through the mouth) and the other was secured externally directly below the dorsal fin using Peterson disks and two pins. Fish were weighed in a rubber mesh weigh bag and fork length was measured.

The tagging location shown on the map is also the location of the Babine River counting fence and where the shuttle box trials were conducted. After sampling and tagging, the fish were placed in a shuttle box tank system. The system was filled with approximately 330 L of river water and consisted of two 1‐m diameter tanks (a warm and cool tank) connected by a 20 × 15 cm channel enabling the fish to move between tanks (Christensen et al., 2021). The shuttle box was used to conduct a 24‐h thermal preference trial on each of 20 fish as part of a separate behavioural thermoregulation study. As the shuttle box system constantly warms or cools the water in both tanks depending on the location of the fish (i.e., the system warms the water in both tanks if the fish is in the warm tank, and cools both tanks if the fish is in the cool tank), it creates various instances where the body temperature of the fish differs from that of the tanks by varying degrees, thus enabling collection of data to parameterize heat transfer models. The water in the shuttle box tank heated or cooled (depending on the location of the fish) at a rate of 2°C/hour. During each trial, the external and internal temperature loggers recorded the internal body temperatures and ambient temperatures experienced by each individual salmon every 1.5 min. After the trials were completed, the temperature loggers were removed for data download and the fish were released upriver from the fence to continue their migration. All trials were completed from mid‐August to mid‐October 2021.

To illustrate the application of the heat transfer model to predict body temperature of O. nerka, we used data that were collected for a separate study on migrating salmon. The data included the ambient temperatures experienced by O. nerka that were externally tagged with a radio tag (NTF‐5‐2, Lotek Wireless, Canada) and temperature/depth logger (LAT1100, Lotek Wireless, Canada; accuracy better than ±0.2°C and resolution of 0.05°C) programmed to record data every 1.5 min. These radio tagged fish (n = 60) were captured, handled and tagged between August 25 and September 3, 2021, using the same procedures described above for the shuttle box fish, but released right after tagging to resume their migration. The radio tagged fish did not have gastric temperature tags implanted (external only) and were generally similar in mass and condition factor to the shuttle box fish (Table 1). The temperature/depth logger was recovered from 23 individuals that were found by radio tracking on the Fulton River and Morrison River spawning grounds (approximately 80–90 km away from the tagging site, Figure 1) after passing through the thermally stratified waters of Babine Lake.

Data analyses

Equation (2) assumes the ambient temperature is constant within the time interval. However, in our study the ambient temperature changed by about 0.05°C within each 1.5 min data‐logging interval when the fish remained in a tank, but up to about 2°C when the fish moved between tanks within a given interval. Other studies have used similar equations to estimate k for free‐ranging fish under conditions in which ambient temperature likely varied between data‐logging intervals due to movement through heterogeneous environments (e.g., Kitagawa et al., 2001; Stoehr et al., 2018). To account for these changes in our analyses, we set Ta to the mean ambient temperature between the start (t−1) and end (t) of a 1.5 min data‐logging interval. The resulting models showed good fit to the data, with predicted body temperatures closely matching recorded body temperatures. Additionally, the most parsimonious model performed well in out‐of‐sample prediction when each individual time series was withheld in turn for cross‐validation (see Section 3). Therefore, we opted to use the simpler heat transfer model in Equation (2) with Ta representing the mean ambient temperature during an interval, rather than using a substantially more complex heat transfer model that would accommodate ambient temperature changes over the short time interval.

As the data contained multiple measures of Tb nested within individuals, non‐linear mixed models including individual random effects were used to estimate k and Tm (Oddi et al., 2019; Pépino et al., 2015; Pinheiro & Bates, 2006). Coefficient k was modelled as a function of a binary factor variable indicating whether the body heated or cooled between t−1 and t and the body size of the fish. Since there were very few instances (0.4% of the observations) where body temperatures did not heat or cool between temperature recordings, these data points were removed prior to the analysis. Due to uncertainty in which body size measurement most notably relates to k, two preliminary non‐linear mixed models were fit including either fish mass or Fulton's condition factor (an indicator of fish plumpness, higher value for more rotund fish; Richter et al., 2000) and the other fixed effects. These two models were compared using Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Burnham & Anderson, 2002, 2004). The body size measurement included in the model with the lowest AIC value was used in the subsequent model selection for k. The global model was fit to the data and model assumptions of residual homoscedasticity, normality and independence were checked graphically (Zuur et al., 2010). Assessments did not indicate major violations of residual homoscedasticity and normality, but residuals were temporally autocorrelated. To account for temporal autocorrelation, an autocorrelation structure of order one for the residuals was included. Additionally, a global model was fit with an autocorrelation structure accounting for the uneven time interval between observations caused by the removal of body temperature recordings that did not change over a 1.5 min interval (0.4% of the total observations). The coefficient estimates were nearly identical to the model with the original autocorrelation structure, but the model was much more computationally intensive (each model taking hours rather than minutes to fit). Therefore, the original autocorrelation structure assuming equally spaced observations was adopted to fit all subset models for model selection.

A total of eight candidate models were fit to the data using maximum likelihood, including an intercept‐only model and models containing all possible additive combinations of condition factor and warming/cooling variables as fixed effects on k, and mass as a fixed effect on Tm. To determine the most parsimonious model, AIC scores, ΔAIC values and AIC weights were calculated in R (Burnham & Anderson, 2002; R Core Team, 2021). The relative variable importance of each fixed effect was calculated by summing the AIC weights of the models containing the effect in the full candidate model set (Burnham & Anderson, 2002). Models were fit to the data using package nlme 3.1.164 (Pinheiro et al., 2021) in R (R Core Team, 2021).

We calculated the temperature change halftime (T1/2) and thermal time constant (TTC) of adult O. nerka based on the averaged‐model median estimated k value. These metrics quantify the rates of body temperature change and are expressed in units of time (Brill et al., 1994; Spigarelli et al., 1977). The T1/2 is calculated as the reciprocal of k multiplied by the natural log of 2 and similarly represents the time it takes the body temperature to change by 50% of the difference between Tb when t=0 and steady‐state Tb (Brill et al., 1994; Spigarelli et al., 1977). The TTC is the reciprocal of k and represents the time an individual takes to warm or cool to 63.2% of the difference between Tb when t=0 and steady state Tb (Brill et al., 1994).

Predicting body temperature of shuttle box and radio tagged fish

As no singular model had an AIC weight greater than 0.95, predictions generated from models with cumulative AIC weights adding to at least 0.95 were averaged (Burnham & Anderson, 2004; Symonds & Moussalli, 2011). Before averaging, the weights of these models were re‐scaled to sum to 1 and the models were re‐fit using restricted maximum likelihood. Model‐averaged predictions of body temperatures were first compared to the recorded body temperatures during the shuttle box trials to assess the fit of the models. To further evaluate model performance, cross‐validation was conducted using the model with the lowest AIC score. The model was re‐fit 20 times, each time leaving out the entire water and body temperature time series from a different shuttle box trial. Each model iteration was then used to predict O. nerka body temperatures for the omitted trial. We also used the re‐fitted models to make a second set of body temperature predictions, replacing the recorded Tbi,t−1 with the predicted body temperature for individual i at t−1. This approach was used to evaluate model performance in situations where only the initial body temperature in a time series is known, as is the case for the radio tagged salmon in our study. The absolute difference between predicted and recorded body temperatures was then calculated for each cross‐validation iteration and summarized as the median ± median absolute deviation (MAD).

To illustrate how quickly the body temperature of an O. nerka may respond to sudden changes in ambient temperature, the models were used to predict body temperatures over an approximately 1‐h period following instantaneous ambient temperature shifts from 4 to 17°C and from 17 to 4°C, reflecting the approximate coolest and warmest temperatures experienced by tagged O. nerka in Babine Lake in this study. Body temperature changes were also predicted for a fish that moved to water 2, 8 and 10°C cooler or warmer than its initial body temperature. The predictions were for a fish with a mass and condition factor equal to the mean mass and condition factor measured in the shuttle box fish. Next, the models were used to predict the body temperatures experienced by radio tagged fish as they migrated through Babine Lake. The median (±MAD) recorded ambient temperature, predicted body temperature and absolute difference between the ambient and predicted body temperatures (paired by fish and timestamp) while the fish underwent the shuttle box trials and were in Babine Lake were calculated and compared.

We used a general linear mixed model to investigate if greater and more rapid fluctuations in experienced ambient temperature resulted in greater discrepancies between ambient and predicted body temperatures. RESULTS

Heat transfer model

The preliminary k model with the lower AIC score included condition factor (− 70,869.39 vs. − 70,868.53 for mass), which was then used in subsequent model selection for k. Furthermore, Tm was included in all candidate models since the preliminary global model fit with Tm had a lower AIC score than the global model without Tm (− 70,869.39 vs. − 70,425.75). The most parsimonious heat transfer model had no fixed effects for k or Tm; however, all the fixed effects assessed appeared in three of the models comprising the 95% confidence set for the best models (Table 2). The relative importances of all the tested fixed effects were similar, with mass having the greatest relative importance (0.37 for Tm) followed by condition factor (0.33 for k) and heating/cooling (0.29 for k). Model‐averaged predictions of body temperatures closely resembled the recorded body temperatures of the shuttle box fish (median absolute difference: 0.02°C, MAD: 0.02°C, range: 0.00–0.42°C; Figure 2). The cross‐validation results indicated that the most parsimonious model had good out‐of‐sample predictive performance (Figure 2). The median absolute difference in the predicted and recorded body temperatures was 0.02°C for the predictions made with recorded Tbi,t−1 (MAD: 0.02°C, range: 0.00–0.42°C) and 0.08°C for the predictions made with the predicted Tbi,t−1 (MAD: 0.08°C, range: 0.00–0.81°C).

The estimates of the coefficient of heat exchange for O. nerka ranged from 0.08 to 0.09 min−1 (Table 1). Based on the averaged‐model median estimated k, the T1/2 was 7.7 min and the TTC was 11.1 min (Table 1). Coefficient k was virtually identical when the bodies of the fish were warming compared to when the bodies were cooling, as indicated by the model‐averaged predictions (Figure 3). Condition factor had a minor negative effect on k, with higher condition factors resulting in slightly slower heat exchange (Figure 3A). Similarly, estimates of Tm decreased slightly as fish mass increased (Figure 3B).

The model‐averaged predictions showed that an average‐sized female O. nerka with an initial core body temperature 10°C cooler or warmer than its steady ambient environment would take just under 1 h to equilibrate with the ambient temperature (i.e., rate of body temperature change <0.01°C min−1), and between 39 and 61.5 min to equilibrate after ambient temperature changes ranging from 2 to 13°C (Figure 4). Body temperature reached that of the ambient environment marginally faster when the body was heating (Figure 4). The rate of body temperature change is initially faster when there is a greater difference between body and ambient temperature, represented by the steeper slopes of the body temperature curves in Figure 4. Radio tagged fish body temperature predictions

Model‐averaged predictions of coefficients k and Tm were very similar between the shuttle box fish and the fish tagged with radio transmitters/loggers (Table 1). The radio tagged fish experienced slightly more variable and cooler ambient temperatures than the shuttle box fish (Table 1). Consequently, radio tagged fish predicted body temperatures were about 2°C cooler on average and more variable than those of shuttle box fish (Table 1). Although the median difference between ambient temperature and body temperature was similar between tagged and shuttle box fish, predictions of radio tagged fish body temperature were occasionally much cooler or warmer (Tb−Ta > 6°C) than the experienced ambient temperature (Figure 5), likely as a result of rapidly moving across the thermally stratified water column of the lake.

DISCUSSION

Our primary goal was to investigate how the core body temperatures of adult O. nerka respond to changes in ambient temperature, using Newton's law of cooling to estimate the coefficient of heat exchange, k, and rate of metabolic heat production, Tm, for these fish. Our results suggest that k may vary marginally depending on a salmon's condition factor and whether its body was heating or cooling. In addition, Tm appeared to have a small effect but was a necessary model term for predicting body temperatures of O. nerka, and there was little variation in Tm with the mass of the fish. The predicted body temperatures of salmon in the shuttle box trials closely mirrored the recorded body temperature. Additionally, the most parsimonious model had strong out‐of‐sample predictive performance, indicating that the modelled values of k and Tm were plausible.

The median estimated values of k for O. nerka in this study were similar to published k values for other members of the Salmonidae family. For example, the k of adult O. keta was estimated to be 0.09 min−1 (T1/2 of 7.7 min), the same as the model‐averaged median estimates for O. nerka in our study (Azumaya & Ishida, 2005). Negus and Bergstedt (2012) estimated that the k for S. namaycush ranged from 0.03 min−1 (T1/2 of 23.1 min) to 0.12 min−1 (T1/2 of 5.8 min). The k values estimated by Pépino et al. (2015) for S. fontinalis were higher, ranging from approximately 0.14 min−1 (T1/2 of 4.9 min) to 0.17 min−1 (T1/2 of 4.1 min). The faster body temperature changes may be explained by the size differences of the fishes studied. The S. fontinalis used in the Pépino et al. (2015) study ranged from about 200 to 600 g, notably smaller than the fish masses in our study (1000–2200 g) and the S. namaycush studied by Negus and Bergstedt (2012) (817–2230 g). The S. fontinalis were also considerably shorter (total lengths of 26–39 cm) than the O. keta studied by Azumaya and Ishida (2005) (fork lengths of 57–69 cm). It is important to note that this relationship does not hold true when comparing the heat exchange rates between fish species with and without countercurrent heat exchange systems. For example, the k of bigeye tuna Thunnus obesus were estimated to be as high as 0.6 min (T1/2 of 1.2 min) despite being significantly larger than the tested salmonids described above (2800–44,000 g; Malte et al., 2007).

Preliminary comparisons of models predicting k as a function of body size parameters indicated that condition factor was a slightly better predictor of k than those that included mass as a fixed effect. This contrasts with the results from Spigarelli et al. (1977) indicating that fish mass was more strongly related to the time it took for the bodies of eight Lake Michigan freshwater fish species, including four salmonid species, to heat or cool than other size parameters such as length, girth and condition factor. Additionally, other studies have found moderate or strong linear relationships between mass and k; however, they did not look at the relationship between other body size parameters and k (e.g., Fechhelm & Neill, 1982; Nakamura et al., 2020; Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Pépino et al., 2015). The relationship between mass and k can be explained by the fact that fishes with larger masses have slower rates of heat exchange due a thicker body wall (reduced thermal conductance) and a lower surface‐area‐to‐volume ratio (hence a greater thermal inertia) than lighter fishes with the same body shape (Nakamura et al., 2020; Pépino et al., 2015; Sidebotham, 2015).

It is possible that condition factor, in this case Fulton's condition factor, may be a better indicator of body wall thickness than mass alone if fish are all within a similar mass range. Fulton's condition factor considers the mass of the fish relative to its length, and higher factors have been associated with a plumper body (Akpesse et al., 2022; Matondo et al., 2021; Treer & Babačić, 2017). As an object's thermal resistance (the degree of resistance to heat flow) is usually directly related to its thickness (Çengal & Ghajar, 2010; Mishra et al., 2019), a fish with a lower condition factor and an elongated, skinny body would have a shorter distance (and lower thermal resistance) between the water and their isothermal core than a plumper or thicker fish of the same mass. Consequently, the core of the low‐condition factor fish could heat or cool at a faster rate than that of the high‐condition factor fish. Furthermore, a plumper fish could have a higher proportion of fat relative to water, corresponding with a slower rate of heat transfer due to fat having a lower thermal conductivity (Abbas et al., 2009; Mozsár et al., 2015; Radhakrishnan, 1997). Despite the slightly higher support of a model with condition factor over one with mass, subsequent model selection indicated limited support for models including condition factor as a predictor of k.

There was little difference between the estimated k value for a warming or cooling body, aligning with the findings of more recent research on k of other salmonid species (Azumaya & Ishida, 2005; Negus & Bergstedt, 2012; Pépino et al., 2015). Since salmonids do not have internal mechanisms of body temperature regulation (Brett, 1971), this result is not surprising. For some other fish species, such as bluegills Lepomis macrochirus (Fechhelm & Neill, 1982), whale sharks Rhincodon typus (Nakamura et al., 2020) and smallmouth bass Micropterus dolomieu (Weller et al., 1984), a greater k while the body is heating than while cooling has been reported. This has been attributed to factors such as elevated metabolic rates promoting faster heat exchange via the gills, or due to increased thermal conductivity of tissues at hotter temperatures (Abbas et al., 2009; Fechhelm & Neill, 1982; Nakamura et al., 2020; Nilsson et al., 2009). These effects could vary by species and may be minimal for salmonids; however, further research is needed to understand if and why k may differ during heating and cooling across fish taxa.

Although not directly evaluated in this study, it is important to note that the activity levels of a fish and how quickly water flows relative to the fish may impact k. Swimming speed could alter the heat exchange rate at the gills due to changes in respiration (Brett, 1964; Dean, 1976; Spigarelli et al., 1977). Greater movement of water across the surface of the body could result in more rapid heat exchange due to convection (Favilla & Costa, 2020). Thus, differences in the activity of the O. nerka in the shuttle box trials and the migrating radio tagged fish may have impacted the accuracy of the estimates of k and body temperature predictions for the latter group. The accuracy of these predictions could have also been affected by intraspecific variation of cardiorespiratory features, such as heart rate and gill area size. Although most of the heat exchange between fish and the environment takes place through the body wall and fins, a portion also occurs via gas exchange at the gills (Stevens & Sutterlin, 1976). Differences in cardiorespiratory features were not considered in this research but could have affected gas exchange (e.g., Pauly & Müller, 2025) and associated heat exchange rates.

Our results suggest that Tm had a small positive effect on the body temperatures of adult O. nerka. At steady‐state, Tb would be approximately 0.08–0.09°C higher than Ta based on the estimated median values of Tm and k (i.e., Tmk−1). The offset is slight due to the estimated k being about one order of magnitude larger than Tm. The small effect of Tm was unsurprising as O. nerka are ectotherms that lack a countercurrent heat exchange system (Brett, 1971). However, it was surprising that the global model including Tm had a much lower AIC score than the global model without Tm. Other studies on fishes lacking countercurrent heat exchange systems have found Tm to have an insignificant impact on body temperature or have left out Tm when using Newton's law of cooling to predict body temperature (e.g., Fechhelm & Neill, 1982; Negus & Bergstedt, 2012). These discrepancies could be due to differences in fish body shape (surface to volume ratio) and thermal inertia or due to measurement error. Estimates of Tm are small in comparison to the resolution of some temperature loggers, such as Lotek LTD1110 Series and iButton DS1921Z thermal loggers which have resolutions of 0.02 and 0.125°C, respectively (O'Donnell et al., 2024; Raby et al., 2018).

The predicted body temperatures experienced by tagged adult O. nerka were similar to ambient temperatures recorded while they were passing through Babine Lake. However, body temperatures were less variable than ambient temperatures, which is consistent with what has been found in other studies on fish (Nakamura et al., 2020; Pépino et al., 2015). Because the body‐ambient temperature differential increased with the rate of ambient temperature change, the greatest differences between body temperature and ambient temperature occurred after rapid movements along the water column of the stratified Babine Lake. These movements exposed fish to large ambient temperature changes across the metalimnion, which is typically located at a depth of approximately 11 m from May to October (Shortreed & Morton, 2000). An important implication of this relationship is that it would take an approximately 40–60 min for the core body temperature of adult O. nerka to equilibrate after migrating into ambient temperatures 2–13°C different from their initial body temperature. Due to the delay until body temperature stabilizes with ambient temperature, a very brief exposure to an extreme or unfavourable temperature would likely not be fully reflected by their core body temperature. On the opposite end, if a fish experienced an unfavourable temperature long enough for their body to stabilize with it, it would take more than a brief exposure to benign temperatures for their bodies to reach a more favourable temperature again. The time required for fish to equilibrate with ambient temperatures could help inform fisheries managers working on cold‐water refuge creation and enhancement to determine ideal refuge size and placement (e.g., Mejia et al., 2023). Optimally located and sized refuges could minimize stressful body temperatures experienced by thermally sensitive species while moving between them and allow fish to remain in the patches long enough to equilibrate with cooler conditions.

CONCLUSION

The differences in body and ambient water temperatures experienced by Babine Lake O. nerka in this study highlight a need to exhibit caution when equating ambient temperature to the body temperature of fish in thermally heterogeneous environments, especially over short timescales. Some fish may briefly tolerate extreme water temperatures during short incursions. However, this does not necessarily reflect their thermal tolerance or thermal limits since fish body temperatures take time to equilibrate with the surrounding environment. Body temperatures are important to accurately estimate since they impact physiology, behaviour and fitness (Huey & Berrigan, 2001; Huey & Stevenson, 1979). The estimated values of k and Tm from this study could be applied to other research requiring more precise body temperature predictions for free‐ranging adult O. nerka or possibly other similarly sized and shaped salmonids without using internal temperature loggers. The ambient temperatures salmon experience could also be predicted based on internally recorded body temperatures. If doing the latter, the investigator should proceed with caution, as Pépino et al. (2015) found that using k and known body temperatures of S. fontinalis to predict the experienced ambient temperatures was not entirely accurate due to different thermal experiences resulting in the same body temperatures. Overall, there is a need for estimates of k and Tm across fish taxa to understand broad scale relationships between these parameters and body mass and condition factor. Future research could also investigate how activity, water velocity and temperature influence heat transfer between fish and their ambient environment. Knowing how quickly a fish's body temperature warms and cools could help determine individualized ramping rates in CTmax trials to ensure uniform temperature rates of change (Desforges et al., 2023). Furthermore, estimated rates of fish body heat exchange could help inform assessments of thermal refuge utility for fish species, and aid watershed managers in prioritizing specific cool‐water patches for conservation and enhancement.
