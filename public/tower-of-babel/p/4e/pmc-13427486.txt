Dispatcher-assisted AED use in single-rescuer cardiac arrest: study protocol for a randomized simulation study comparing standard and optimized dispatcher-assisted CPR instructions
Authors: Barnabás Balassa, Henrietta Bánfai-Csonka, József Betlehem, Bernd W Böttiger, Bálint Bánfai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough automated external defibrillators (AEDs) improve outcomes in shockable rhythms, their use introduces interruptions in chest compressions. In single-rescuer scenarios, AED retrieval and application may further increase no-flow time. The effect of dispatcher instruction strategy on these interruptions remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate whether AED-optimized dispatcher-assisted CPR (DACPR) instructions reduce no-flow time compared with standard instructions in a simulated single-rescuer OHCA setting.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, controlled, parallel-group simulation trial will include adult laypersons and healthcare professionals. Participants will be allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either standard or AED-optimized DACPR instruction groups. All scenarios will be conducted in a controlled simulation environment with standardized AED accessibility (≤1 min retrieval distance) to isolate the effect of dispatcher communication on CPR performance. The primary outcome will be total no-flow time. Secondary outcomes include chest compression fraction, time to first compression, time to first shock, peri-shock pauses, and CPR quality metrics.DiscussionThis study investigates dispatcher instruction as a modifiable determinant of CPR interruption during AED use in single-rescuer scenarios. Findings will provide mechanistic insight into how communication strategies influence CPR quality under controlled conditions and may inform refinement of DACPR protocols.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the University of Pécs (PTE/87175-1/2025). Written informed consent will be obtained. Results will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publication and scientific conferences.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov: NCT07520877, prospectively registered on 8 April 2026.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Automated external defibrillator, Dispatcher-assisted CPR, Telephone CPR, Cardiac arrest

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a leading cause of death worldwide.1 Poor outcomes highlight the importance of early bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and defibrillation in the Chain of Survival.2, 3 Although bystander CPR improves outcomes, survival remains strongly dependent on minimizing interruptions in chest compressions and shortening time to first shock in shockable rhythms.4 Early defibrillation is particularly decisive: in shockable rhythms, survival may reach up to 90% when defibrillation occurs within the first minutes after collapse, while each additional minute of delay reduces the likelihood of meaningful survival. More recent evidence confirms that even a 6-min delay significantly decreases the probability of successful shock termination in ventricular fibrillation.5

The widespread dissemination of automated external defibrillators (AEDs) has enabled lay responders to deliver shocks before emergency medical services (EMS) arrival, shortening the call-to-shock interval and improving neurological outcomes.6, 7 Public access defibrillation programs shorten time to shock and improve neurological outcomes; however, AED deployment inevitably interrupts chest compressions because of rhythm analysis, pad placement, and shock delivery.8 These interruptions reduce cerebral and coronary perfusion pressures, which are the key determinants of successful resuscitation.9

In line with the ERC 2025 guidelines, special consideration must be given to the scenario of the single rescuer. For lone responders, AED use is justified when the device is immediately accessible (retrievable within 1 min).3 However, a lone rescuer cannot perform chest compressions while retrieving and preparing the AED, requiring a balance between early defibrillation and compression continuity.3, 10

In such situations, dispatcher instructions may play a critical role in determining whether CPR interruptions are minimized or amplified. Despite this, the optimal structure of DACPR instructions during AED use remains insufficiently defined.3 Whether dispatcher instructions can reduce these interruptions remains unknown.

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the effect of an AED-optimized DACPR protocol on no-flow time during simulated single-rescuer cardiac arrest scenario. We hypothesize that AED-optimized dispatcher instructions, specifically designed to prioritize compression continuity during AED handling, may significantly reduce no-flow time compared to standard DACPR instructions.

Methods

Study design

This study protocol was developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [11, Supplementary materials 1–2]. The study is designed as a prospective, randomized, controlled, single-center, parallel-group simulation trial. The study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number: NCT07520877, registration date: 8 April 2026).

Participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to one of two groups (Fig. 1):

The study aims to evaluate the effect of modified DACPR instructions on resuscitation performance during AED use when the first responder is alone, with particular focus on no-flow time.

Settings

The study will be conducted at the Human Patient Simulation Center of the Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Pécs, Hungary. The simulation environment provides standardized conditions for all participants, allowing controlled assessment of resuscitation performance.

Participants

Adult participants will be enrolled, including both laypersons and healthcare professionals (Fig. 1). Participation is possible regardless of whether someone has previously received CPR training or not. Including laypersons and healthcare professionals will allow exploratory subgroup analyses.

Inclusion criteria are age ≥18 years and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria include any physical limitation preventing the performance of chest compressions.

Participants will be recruited through convenience sampling using advertisements distributed via the University of Pécs, partner healthcare institutions, community organizations, and social media platforms. Recruitment will be open to both laypersons and healthcare professionals. Individuals expressing interest will receive written study information and will be screened for eligibility before providing informed consent.

Randomization and blinding

Participants will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio using a simple allocation method (e.g., physical or digital random number generation). Allocation concealment will be ensured by masking the meaning of the assigned numbers.

Participants will be informed of their group allocation after randomization but will remain blinded to the specific study hypothesis.

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of dispatchers is not feasible; however, dispatchers will follow standardized scripts and will not be informed about the study outcomes. Outcome assessors will be blinded to group allocation and study hypotheses.

Intervention

Participants will receive DACPR instructions via a simulated emergency call delivered by a trained member of the study team acting as dispatcher. Participants will receive one of two standardized, pre-scripted dispatcher instruction protocols according to group allocation (Table 1, Supplementary material 3). The standard protocol was developed based on the current Hungarian dispatcher-assisted CPR practice12 and adapted for a single-rescuer scenario. Consistent with the ERC 2025 Guidelines,3 AED use was incorporated into both study groups whenever the device was immediately accessible. The AED-optimized protocol was developed from this modified standard protocol by adding specific instructions aimed at minimizing interruptions in chest compressions during AED use. The AED-optimized script was designed to minimize hands-off time while remaining compatible with standard AED prompts and avoiding excessive dispatcher instructions. All scripts have been developed before study initiation, fully standardized, and delivered using identical wording across all simulation sessions.

Dispatchers will receive standardized training, and script adherence will be monitored by an independent assessor.

The simulation will be conducted in a controlled environment using an adult CPR manikin (Little Anne Q-CPR, Laerdal, Norway) capable of recording CPR quality metrics in real time. An AED trainer (Zoll AED Plus Trainer II, Zoll Medical, USA) will be used in all scenarios. The manikin will be dressed to enhance realism.

The physical environment will be identical for all participants, with a fixed room layout and standardized equipment placement. The AED will be positioned in a clearly visible location and will be accessible within approximately one minute in all scenarios. This design choice is intended to eliminate variability in AED accessibility and to isolate the effect of dispatcher instruction strategy on resuscitation performance.

All simulations will follow a predefined scenario. Participants will be informed prior to entry that they will encounter a collapsed person. Upon entering the room, participants will be expected to assess responsiveness, initiate an emergency call, and follow dispatcher guidance. The dispatcher will assist with breathing assessment and recognition of cardiac arrest. Once cardiac arrest is identified, the dispatcher will instruct AED retrieval. To ensure identical AED accessibility across all simulations, the dispatcher will provide the participant with the precise location of the AED using standardized wording. This approach simulates the use of a dispatch-linked AED registry while eliminating variability related to locating the device. Participants will then retrieve the AED and continue resuscitation according to dispatcher instructions and AED prompts.

The scenario will use a standardized shockable rhythm sequence and will be terminated after delivery of the third shock. The expected duration of each simulation will be approximately 6–7 min. To minimize additional sources of interruption and isolate the effect of dispatcher instruction on no-flow time, participants will perform compression-only CPR without rescue breaths throughout the simulation.

Standard DACPR group

Participants allocated to the standard DACPR group will receive dispatcher instructions reflecting current conventional practice.12 The protocol will emphasize rapid AED retrieval and connection prior to the initiation of chest compressions.

Dispatcher guidance will include:

•initiation of chest compressions following insertion of the AED (in accordance with AED prompts, keeping safety in mind)

No additional instructions will be provided to optimize compression continuity during AED use. Participants will be instructed to follow AED prompts without supplementary guidance regarding interruption minimization or prioritization of chest compressions (Table 1, Supplementary material 3).

AED-optimized DACPR group

Participants in the AED-optimized DACPR group will receive standard dispatcher instructions12 supplemented with specific guidance aimed at minimizing interruptions in chest compressions during AED-related tasks (Table 1, Supplementary material 3).

This protocol will explicitly prioritize compression continuity throughout the resuscitation process. Dispatcher instructions will emphasize:

•minimizing pauses during AED retrieval and preparation (e.g., turn AED on immediately as possible)

minimizing pauses during AED retrieval and preparation (e.g., turn AED on immediately as possible)

•continuation of chest compressions during AED setup phases where no direct hands-off interaction is required (e.g., during device preparation or charging, if applicable within the simulation scenario)

continuation of chest compressions during AED setup phases where no direct hands-off interaction is required (e.g., during device preparation or charging, if applicable within the simulation scenario)

•reduction of peri-shock pauses

reduction of peri-shock pauses

•immediate resumption of chest compressions following shock delivery

immediate resumption of chest compressions following shock delivery

Participants will also be encouraged to initiate or continue chest compressions even if AED prompts have not yet reached the corresponding step, provided this does not interfere with mandatory device operations (e.g., rhythm analysis or shock delivery).

Rationale and data collection

A central methodological consideration in this study is the interaction between AED accessibility and CPR interruption time. In real-world settings, variability in AED location and retrieval time represents a major confounder when evaluating resuscitation performance.

To isolate the effect of dispatcher instruction as a behavioral and cognitive determinant, AED accessibility will be deliberately standardized across all scenarios. All participants will be exposed to identical environmental conditions, with AED retrieval constrained to within approximately one minute.

This design does not aim to evaluate whether AEDs are available or whether they are used. Instead, it specifically examines how dispatcher instruction modifies CPR interruption patterns when AED use is feasible and operationally required.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation will be based on the primary outcome, total no-flow time. Previous simulation and experimental studies suggest that AED-related task switching introduces interruptions in the range of approximately 10–20 s, which are considered clinically relevant at the CPR process level due to their impact on cerebral and coronary perfusion pressures and resuscitation effectiveness. A minimum detectable difference of 15 s in total no-flow time between groups will therefore be considered clinically meaningful.14, 15

Assuming a standard deviation of approximately 25 s, sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1 for a two-tailed independent samples t-test. Given the simulation-based nature of the study, the calculation was based on detection of a moderate standardized effect size using the following assumptions:

•α = 0.05

α = 0.05

•Power (1 − β) = 0.80

Power (1 − β) = 0.80

•Effect size (Cohen’s d) ≈ 0.60

Effect size (Cohen’s d) ≈ 0.60

Under these assumptions, the required sample size is estimated to be approximately 45 participants per group (total n ≈ 90). To compensate for potential data loss related to technical recording issues or protocol deviations, an additional 5–10% will be added to the planned recruitment target.

Variability is expected to be reduced by the highly standardized simulation design, including identical environmental conditions, uniform AED accessibility, standardized dispatcher scripts, predefined shockable rhythm scenarios, and assessor-monitored protocol adherence across all simulations. Importantly, the selected effect size reflects a clinically meaningful process-level difference in CPR performance rather than a direct survival effect, which cannot be assessed in a simulation environment.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be conducted according to a predefined statistical analysis plan. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize baseline characteristics and outcome measures. Continuous variables will be reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), depending on distribution. Categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages. Normality of continuous variables will be assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of distributions.

For the primary outcome (no-flow time):

•Independent samples t-test will be used if normal distribution is confirmed

Independent samples t-test will be used if normal distribution is confirmed

•Mann–Whitney U test will be used if distributional assumptions are not met

Mann–Whitney U test will be used if distributional assumptions are not met

For secondary outcomes:

•Continuous variables will be analyzed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate

Continuous variables will be analyzed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate

•Categorical variables will be compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test

Categorical variables will be compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test

All tests will be two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

The primary analysis will follow an intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomized participants in their allocated groups. If relevant imbalances in baseline characteristics are observed, exploratory adjusted analyses (e.g., linear regression) may be performed.

Statistical analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0 or later).

Ethics and dissemination

This study will be conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulations. The study protocol has been approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the University of Pécs (approval number: PTE/87175-1/2025). All participants will receive detailed written and verbal information about the study prior to participation (Supplementary material 5). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before enrollment. Participation will be entirely voluntary. Participants will be informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Given the simulation-based nature of the study, risks to participants are expected to be minimal. The scenario involves performing chest compressions on a manikin in a controlled environment. No invasive procedures will be performed. Participants may experience mild physical fatigue during chest compressions; however, they will be allowed to stop at any time if needed.

All collected data will be anonymized prior to analysis. Data will be stored securely and will only be accessible to authorized members of the research team. Data will be used exclusively for scientific purposes. No follow-up measurements will be required after completion of the simulation.

The study results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentation at national and international scientific conferences. Authorship will follow standard scientific guidelines, and all contributors will be appropriately acknowledged.

Discussion

This study investigates whether DACPR instructions can influence CPR interruption patterns during AED use in a single-rescuer scenario.

Although early defibrillation improves survival, AED use inevitably interrupts chest compressions because of rhythm analysis, pad placement, and shock delivery.16, 17, 18 In single-rescuer scenarios, task switching may further prolong these interruptions, but the potential role of dispatcher communication remains insufficiently explored.2, 10, 19

By standardizing AED accessibility and simulation conditions, this study isolates dispatcher instruction as a modifiable determinant of CPR performance and evaluates whether emphasizing compression continuity reduces no-flow time.

If AED-optimized instructions reduce no-flow time, the findings may support refinement of DACPR protocols by explicitly prioritizing chest compression continuity during AED use. Conversely, if no meaningful difference is observed, communication strategies alone may be insufficient, indicating that further improvements require system-level interventions such as education, technology, or optimized public-access defibrillation systems.20, 21, 22 Although chest compression fraction (CCF) is recommended in the Utstein reporting guidelines,13 no-flow time was selected as the primary outcome because the intervention may influence both interruptions during CPR and the interval before the first chest compression, particularly when AED retrieval precedes CPR in a single-rescuer scenario. CCF will therefore be reported as a complementary secondary outcome.

The study evaluates not whether AED use is beneficial, but how it can be optimized in a single-rescuer scenario.

Potential limitations

This study has several important limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the study is conducted in a simulated environment, which inherently limits external validity. Simulation cannot fully reproduce the emotional stress, cognitive load, environmental unpredictability, and physical constraints of real OHCA, all of which may influence CPR quality and decision-making.

Second, AED accessibility was intentionally standardized, strengthening internal validity but limiting applicability to real-world variability in AED availability and retrieval time.

Third, the study focuses exclusively on single-rescuer scenarios and therefore does not capture the dynamics of multiple bystanders or variable dispatcher interactions.

In addition, the single-center design may limit generalizability but facilitates strict standardization of the simulation environment and outcome assessment.

Finally, the study evaluates process measures rather than clinical outcomes and is therefore not designed to assess survival or neurological recovery.

The findings should therefore be interpreted as mechanistic evidence requiring validation in observational or implementation studies. Recent evidence indicates that dispatcher-assisted public-access defibrillation programs improve AED retrieval and outcomes, while implementation studies highlight ongoing challenges related to dispatcher communication, AED accessibility, and bystander behaviour.23, 24 Accordingly, this study may inform future optimization of dispatcher-assisted resuscitation protocols but cannot directly predict patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This simulation study aims to evaluate whether AED-optimized DACPR instructions reduce no-flow time during single-rescuer OHCA with standardized AED accessibility. The findings may inform optimization of DACPR protocols and future clinical implementation studies.
