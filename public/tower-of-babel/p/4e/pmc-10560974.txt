Stage 1 hypertension defined by the 2017 ACC/AHA blood pressure guideline and cardiometabolic multimorbidity in Chinese adults
Authors: Xiaoru Qin, Chaolei Chen, Jiabin Wang, Zhiqiang Nie, Yanqiu Ou, Xiaofei Jiang, Yingqing Feng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe association of blood pressure (BP) classification defined by the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guideline with cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) remains unclear. The present study aimed to investigate this research gap in the Chinese adults. Cross‐sectional data were collected from a population‐based cohort conducted in Southern China. Participants were categorized as having normal BP, elevated BP, stage 1 hypertension, and stage 2 hypertension according to the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline. CMM was defined as having two or more of the following diseases: coronary heart disease, stroke, and diabetes. The relationship between the BP classifications and CMM was examined by multivariate logistic regression. A total of 95 649 participants (mean age: 54.3 ± 10.2 years, 60.7% were women) were enrolled in this study. Multivariable‐adjusted logistic regression models revealed that stage 1 hypertension (odds ratio [OR], 1.35; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–1.78) and stage 2 hypertension (OR, 3.53; 95% CI, 2.82–4.47) were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of CMM compared with normal BP. The association between stage 1 hypertension and CMM were profound in women (OR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.17–2.67) and in the middle‐aged group (OR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.02–2.35) compared with men and older individuals, respectively. Our study showed that among Chinese adults, stage 1 hypertension defined by the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline was already associated with higher odds of CMM compared with normal BP, particularly in women and middle‐aged participants. Managing stage 1 hypertension may be an important measure to prevent CMM in Chinese adults.Keywords: 2017 ACC/AHA guideline, blood pressure, cardiometabolic multimorbidity, stage 1 hypertension

INTRODUCTION

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), characterized by the simultaneous occurrence of two or more cardiometabolic diseases including coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and diabetes, represent a growing burden worldwide. 1 By 2019, there were more than 463 million recorded cases of diabetes, 197 million cases of CHD, and 12.2 million stroke survivors around the world. 2 , 3 As the global population continues to age, the prevalence of CMM is on the rise. 4 This trend has also been observed in China. 5 A number of previous studies have indicated that individuals with CMM were at significantly higher risk of morbidity and mortality than those with none or single cardiometabolic disease. 6 , 7 In light of these unfavorable figures, it is crucial that epidemiological and experimental investigations have a sharp focus on uncovering risk factors for CMM.

Hypertension is the most widespread cardiovascular disorder in the world, impacting 1.28 billion adults worldwide. 8 A significant body of evidence has demonstrated that elevated blood pressure (BP) increases the risk of cardiometabolic diseases in the general population. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 For example, in a recent observational study, involving more than one million adults aged 30 years and above, Rapsomaniki and colleagues found that higher BP levels were linked with an elevated risk of angina, myocardial infarction, and stroke. 13 Similar finding was found in a study focusing on people aged 20−39 years old. 14 In that study, compared with normal BP, stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension defined by the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) criteria were both associated with an increased risk of subsequent CHD and stroke. Another study involving 10 893 participants aged 35−54 years revealed that prehypertension and hypertension, as defined by the seventh report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC‐7) criteria, 15 were associated with greater risk of diabetes compared with individuals who has normal BP. 16 However, previous studies have mainly focused on the association of BP classifications with single cardiometabolic disease, the extent to which BP level contributes to increased risk of CMM is currently unknown, particularly in Asian populations. To address these gaps, we aimed to explore the association between BP classifications following the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline and CMM in a large‐scale and well‐designed cohort of Chinese population.

METHODS

Study population

The China Patient‐Centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events Million Persons Project (China PEACE MPP) is a nationwide screening program funded by the government, aiming to identify high‐risk populations for cardiovascular diseases (CVD). 17 The design and methods of China PEACE MPP have already been outlined in prior literature. 17 , 18 The present study was a sub‐cohort of the China PEACE MPP and data of participants enrolled in Guangdong province from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2020 were used. The study recruited 102 358 participants aged 35–75 years who were local residents for at least 6 months prior to the screening. We only excluded participants without low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) measurement (n = 6889), which was the only covariate with missing values, and made no more exclusion due to the completeness of other data. Finally, a total of 95 649 individuals were included in the final analysis. The study was approved by the Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital Ethics Committee (No. GDREC2016438H (R2)). Written informed consent were obtained from all participants.

BP measurements and classifications

Well‐trained researchers conducted BP measurements following standard procedures. Using the HEM‐7430 electronic monitor (Omron, Kyoto, Japan), BP was measured twice on the right upper arm of participants who had been instructed to sit in a relaxed position for 5 min. An average of the two BP readings was taken, and if a difference greater than 5 mm Hg was detected, an additional measurement was conducted, with the mean value of three readings being used. 17 In accordance with the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline, all participants were divided into distinct BP categories based on their BP measurements: normal BP (systolic BP [SBP] < 120 mm Hg and diastolic BP [DBP] < 80 mm Hg), elevated BP (SBP 120−129 mm Hg and DBP < 80 mm Hg), stage 1 hypertension (SBP 130−139 mm Hg and/or DBP 80−89 mm Hg), and stage 2 hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg, or use of antihypertensive drugs in the previous 2 weeks). 19 , 20

Definitions of outcomes

CMM was defined as the presence of at least two of the following cardiometabolic diseases: CHD, stroke, and diabetes. Participants were categorized as having CHD or stroke if they answered “Yes” to the question “Have you ever been told by your doctor that you have CHD or stroke?” in a structured questionnaire administered at enrollment. Also, a panel group of three cardiologists and neurologists identified participants' CHD or stroke‐related inpatient records from the Inpatients Registry before to recruitment using the Tenth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (I20–I25 for CHD and I61–I64 for stroke). 21 Diabetes was defined as the use of hypoglycemic agents, self‐reported history of doctor‐diagnosed diabetes, or having a fasting blood glucose (FBG) level of ≥7.0 mmol/L. 22

Covariates

Well‐trained staff administered standardized questionnaires, conducted physical measurements, and performed laboratory tests to collect covariates data. Socio‐demographic information included age, sex, occupation, residence, marriage, educational status, annual household income, and medical insurance. Lifestyle behaviors included current smoking and current drinking. Medical history included self‐reported doctor‐diagnosed dyslipidemia, and use of antidiabetic drugs, lipid‐lowering drugs, statins, and antiplatelet agents. Physical measurements were performed to examine height, weight, and waist circumference (WC), and body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Following the protocol of the China PEACE MPP project, 17 , 23 participants underwent lipid measurements including total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), LDL‐C, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), which were performed by a rapid lipid analyzer (CardioChek PA Analyzer; Polymer Technology Systems) using whole blood samples in their fasting state. FBG was measured using the BeneCheck BK6−20 M Multi‐Monitoring System, Suzhou Pu Chun Tang Biotechnology, China. Dyslipidemia was defined as TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, LDL‐C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, HDL‐C ≤ 1.0 mmol/L, TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, or lipid‐lowering agents were being used. 24

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented according to BP classifications and by the presence or absence of CMM. The continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations due to the normal distribution, and were compared using the ANOVA. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) and their comparison among groups was conducted using the Wilcoxon Mann–Whitney test, Kruskal–Wallis H‐test, or chi‐square tests, where appropriate. We created three logistic regression models to compute the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence interval (CI) of CMM for the BP categories. Model 1 was univariate. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was further adjusted for occupation, residence, marriage, educational status, household income, medical insurance, smoking, drinking, BMI, TC, TG, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, lipid‐lowering drugs, antiplatelet drugs, and statins. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analysis based on age (<60 or ≥60 years) and sex (men or women). We conducted all analyses using R statistical software version 4.2.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). A two side P value < .05 was considered to be statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

In total, 95 649 participants were included in the current study. Baseline characteristics by BP classifications and CMM status were listed in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. According to their BP measurements, participants were categorized as having normal BP (n = 26 363), elevated BP (n = 11 097), stage 1 hypertension (n = 20 092), and stage 2 hypertension (n = 37 917). CMM was observed in 0.3% of participants in normal BP, 0.5% in elevated BP, 0.7% in stage 1 hypertension, and 2.6% in stage 2 hypertension (Table 1). We detected significant differences between participants with or without CMM in all baseline variables, except for residence, medical insurance, and current drinking (P < .05). Participants with CMM had a higher likelihood of being farmers or current smokers, were older, had higher levels BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, TG and FBG, were more likely to be taking medications such as antihypertensive drugs, antidiabetic drugs, lipid‐lowering drugs, statins, and antiplatelet drugs, and had a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia compared with those without CMM (Table 2).

Association of BP classifications and CMM

Table 3 demonstrated the associations between BP classifications and CMM. In both univariable and multivariable models, higher levels of BP were associated with higher odds of CMM (P < .001). After adjusting for age, sex, occupation, residence, marriage, educational status, household income, medical insurance, smoking, drinking, BMI, TC, TG, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, lipid‐lowering drugs, antiplatelet drugs, and statins, we observed that individuals with stage 1 hypertension (OR, 1.35; 95% CI, 1.03–1.78) and stage 2 hypertension (OR, 3.53; 95% CI, 2.82–4.47) were associated with a higher prevalence of CMM compared with normal BP.

Subgroup analysis

Table 4 showed the association between BP categories and CMM in subgroups stratified by age and sex. According to the multivariable logistic regression analysis, the association of stage 2 hypertension with the prevalence of CMM was observed in all subgroups stratified by age or sex. Additionally, the multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that middle‐aged individuals with stage 1 hypertension (OR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.02–2.35) and women with stage 1 hypertension (OR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.17–2.67) had a significantly higher prevalence of CMM compared with normal BP. In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between stage 1 hypertension and CMM in the older participants and in men.

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the relationship between BP classifications according to the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline and CMM in Chinese adults aged 35–75 years. We found that stage 1 hypertension and stage 2 hypertension were both significantly associated with higher odds of CMM. Furthermore, for middle‐aged individuals and women with stage 1 hypertension, a similar association between stage 1 hypertension and CMM was observed. Moreover, compared to normal BP, the associations between stage 2 hypertension and high prevalence of CMM were consistent across all age and sex subgroups.

The outcomes from prior investigations that determined the association of hypertension (according to JNC‐7 definition) with CMM among adults were in line with those from this study. For example, a cross‐sectional study including 2288 participants aged 40 years and above suggested that hypertension was associated with the prevalence of CMM. 25 In addition, according to the UK Biobank study, there was a strong correlation between hypertension and cardiometabolic diseases. 26 However, few previous studies have investigated the relationship between hypertension and CMM in Asian populations, and the current study provides new evidence.

In 2017, the ACC/AHA released an updated BP management guideline which introduced a new definition for hypertension, starting with a BP level of 130/80 mm Hg. 19 Previously, hypertension was characterized by BP of 140/90 mm Hg or higher, while SBP ranging between 120−139 mm Hg and DBP between 80−89 mm Hg was classified as prehypertension. 15 The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline for BP management was established after numerous large‐scale studies, which demonstrated that individuals within the stage 1 hypertension BP range exhibited increased risk for CVD. 13 , 27 , 28 , 29 Moreover, several extensive cohort studies have revealed noteworthy correlations between BP within the stage 1 hypertension range and elevated risks of single cardiometabolic diseases. 13 , 30 , 31 Data from a cohort of 10 893 participants showed that stage 1 hypertension was a risk factor for diabetes and often preceded the development of diabetes in the White populations. 16 The results of our study have suggested that stage 1 hypertension was associated with the odds of CMM in Chinese adults. As such, these results further emphasize the significance of controlling stage 1 hypertension, which aligns with the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline's recommendations.

In our current research, subgroup analyses based on age and sex indicated that middle‐aged individuals and women with stage 1 hypertension were more closely associated with CMM than their counterparts. Previous studies that evaluated the correlation between BP and cardiometabolic disease among people < 60 years old were congruent with the findings of this study. For example, in a nationwide cohort study involving 2 488 101 Korean adults aged 20–39 years old, stage 1 hypertension (defined according to the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline) was found to be associated with a higher risk of cardiometabolic diseases compared to normal BP. 14 Similarly, a study involving 10 874 men aged 18–39 years reported that an increase of 15 mm Hg in SBP or 10 mm Hg in DBP was linked to mortality caused by single cardiometabolic disease. Furthermore, in the Northern Manhattan study, women exhibited a higher proportion of events attributable to hypertension than men (32% vs. 19%, P < .05). 32 However, the previous studies were limited to specific populations and did not take into account significant confounding factors such as education, marital status, or other CVD medications in addition to antihypertensive drugs. These factors could potentially confound the results. After addressing for these important aspects by including comprehensive drug use and demographic information in the models, our study found higher prevalence of CMM associated with higher BP levels starting from stage 1 hypertension, which enhanced the reliability of our results. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that the associations between stage 1 hypertension and CMM were profound in younger individuals and in women. This is noteworthy because the current approaches of cardiovascular risk quantification have suggested lower estimated risk in young adults, especially young women, who may be stratified as low risk even if they have markedly abnormal risk factor profile. 33 Hence, it may be necessary to consider stage 1 hypertension as possible risk factors for CMM, even in populations deemed to be at low‐risk for CVD. This suggests the importance of early detection and management of hypertension to prevent the progression of single cardiometabolic disease and future CMM.

Due to the higher incidence of CMM in individuals with stage 1 hypertension, it is imperative to develop an effective management strategy for stage 1 hypertension. While the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline proposes pharmacological intervention for patients in the stage 1 hypertension category and with a high estimated 10‐years CVD risk (≥10%), an effective therapeutic approach has yet to be established for those with stage 1 hypertension with low CVD risk. 19 Non‐pharmacological interventions, such as reducing salt intake and achieving healthy body weight, can be the first step towards managing stage 1 hypertension without the use of medication. In cases where non‐pharmacological interventions are not enough to manage stage 1 hypertension, the use of BP‐lowering medications should be considered as a viable option. To determine the optimal combination of non‐pharmacological and pharmacological interventions for stage 1 hypertension and elevated BP, randomized controlled trials may be necessary to determine the optimal combination of these interventions.

The study's large population offered significant strength, allowing for more precise analyses and increasing the study's generalizability. However, limitations should also be noted. First, the current study's cross‐sectional design restricts its capacity to establish causality, and therefore, future prospective cohort studies are needed to confirm any causative relationship suggested by the research. Second, it should be acknowledged that the study's findings are based on research conducted solely on Chinese adults and, therefore, may not be generalizable to other cultural or ethnic populations. Third, there were some uncollected CMM‐related indicators (such as physical exercise and dietary patterns) that were not adjusted in the models, and these indicators may lead to confounding bias and thus affect our results.

In summary, according to the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline, stage 1 hypertension was associated with a higher prevalence of CMM compared with individuals with normal BP in a large Chinese population. Our results suggest that the risk of CMM can be elevated in individuals with stage 1 hypertension, especially in those ≤60 years old and in women. There was growing evidence supporting the management of stage 1 hypertension, defined by the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline, as the preferred primary prevention strategy for CMM, while controlling for other major risk factors associated with CMM.
