The Prevalence of Cardiovascular Risk Factors Among Some Selected Workers at a Ghanaian University: A Quantitative Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Bernard Agyapong Tenkorang, Naab Justine Ti‐Baliana Martha, Marina Aferiba Tandoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a major cause of premature death globally, with the burden disproportionately higher in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). Many CVDs are driven by modifiable risk factors such as physical inactivity, alcohol use, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and obesity. Despite increasing awareness, limited data exist on these risk factors within workplace settings in Ghana, where early detection and prevention could have a substantial impact. This cross‐sectional study assessed the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors among the staff of Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) in Ghana.MethodsAnthropometric measurements were assessed together with physical activities, stress levels, and family history of CVD. Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23.ResultsAbout 60% and 40% of the study participants were non‐teaching and teaching staff, respectively. Overall, 32% of the participants reported having a CVD history, while 18% consumed alcohol, 14% and 5.8% reported “yes” and “sometimes” having depression, and 9.2% and 16% reported being easily and sometimes angered, respectively. Also, 19.2% of the participants had high blood pressure, while 32% had high cholesterol levels. Furthermore, 24.3% of the non‐teaching staff were obese compared to 32.6% of the teaching staff. Also, 9.46% of the non‐teaching staff had high blood glucose compared to 6.52% of the teaching staff. Additionally, 22% of the non‐teaching staff had high cholesterol levels compared to 48% of the teaching staff, while 24% of the non‐teaching staff compared to 6.5% of the teaching staff reported of alcohol consumption. Significant differences were seen in modifiable risk factors among the two groups: BMI (p = 0.0002), cholesterol level (p = 0.0136), and grip‐strength (0.0056). Significant associations were observed between alcohol consumption (p = 0.013), depression (p = 0.002), and staff status.ConclusionRegular health screening and workplace wellness programs are needed to support early detection and reduce future cardiovascular disease among staff.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a group of disorders of the heart and blood vessels. In 2012, CVDs accounted for 17.5 million deaths globally, making up 31% of total deaths. These numbers are projected to rise to 24.2 million by 2030 [1, 2].

The prevention of cardiovascular diseases relies on understanding its gradual development and addressing its related risk factors and lifestyle. Lifestyle factors are not the only focus of models like the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE) [3], the Framingham Risk Score, and the Cardiovascular Risk Score (QRISK2) [4], which prioritize medical risk indicators. The WHO's HEARTS report (2020) identifies seven risk factors, including poor diet, smoking, BMI > 30 kg/m2, physical inactivity, high blood pressure, higher blood glucose, and dyslipidemia. Increased exposure heightens the likelihood of atherosclerosis and CVD clinical manifestations like heart attack, brain attack, and angina pectoris. CVD encompasses various heart and blood vessel‐related illnesses like angina, brain attack, high blood pressure, and peripheral vascular disease (abnormalities of the circulatory system outside of the brain and heart), which develop gradually as fatty substances (atheroma) build up in arteries.

In sub‐Saharan Africa, individuals at high CVD risk are often in their most productive years, with work‐related stress and increased workloads linked to deteriorating health and higher CVD risk [5]. Psychosocial factors, including work‐related stress, exacerbate CVD risk [6]. This trend is evident in Ghana, where university employees face risks of sudden death and cardiac arrest due to factors such as chronic stress, unhealthy diets, and physical inactivity [7]. Efforts to manage non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) in Ghana, outlined in the 2011 NCD Policy, focus on early detection, lifestyle changes, and effective management.

A study by Kusi et al. [7] revealed that workers are often exposed to different hazards in their work environment, which can include physical, chemical, and psychosocial risks that contribute to disease and reduced quality of life. Occupational settings where workers spend long hours in sedentary roles or under high stress can increase vulnerability to cardiovascular risk factors. University employees, including teaching and non‐teaching staff, are not exempt from occupational risks. The rise in student enrollments each year has led to an overwhelming academic workload for university staff [7]. High stress levels and a student‐lecturer ratio of 67:1 [8] potentially contribute to work‐related stress.

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors among university personnel, which aligns with the sustainable development goals SDG 3.4 with an emphasis on reducing the incidence of Non‐Communicable Diseases (NCDs). It further highlights the need for comprehensive data to inform interventions and reduce CVD‐related mortality.

Methods

Study Design, Study Site, and Study Population

This study used a quantitative cross‐sectional approach to determine CVD risk among university workers (teaching and non‐teaching staff) aged 25–60 years working at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi‐Ashanti Region of Ghana, between September 2024 and March 2025.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

University staff (both teaching and non‐teaching), aged 25–60 years, were included in the study, while staff members who had already suffered from cardiac arrest and individuals who were below the age of 25 were excluded. Older adults have a higher likelihood of developing cardiovascular risk factors, so staff younger than 25 were excluded [10, 11, 12]. This age group typically shows a lower incidence of hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia, which could dilute the findings.

Sampling Procedure

Participants were recruited from different departments across the university. A call for participation was shared through the Departmental Heads. Simple random sampling (balloting) was used to select individuals who met the inclusion criteria.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance for this study was sought from the Committee of Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology for ethical validation (CHRPE/AP/219/22). To uphold ethical standards, an official letter requesting permission to initiate data collection was sent to the respective department heads of the university personnel. A comprehensive information sheet elucidating the study's purpose was provided to the participants. Furthermore, participants were engaged in a thorough discussion about the project and their consents were obtained before embarking on the data‐collection process.

Data Collection

A structured closed‐ended questionnaire was used to elicit information from the study participants. The participants' body weight and height data were assessed. Height was measured using a stadiometer (Ovcare, Height 200 cm No.26SM Japan) to the nearest 0.1 cm. Weight was measured using a Mechanical Personal Scale‐ GLC‐2017 to the nearest 0.1 kg, with participants wearing light clothing. Their BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms (kg) divided by the square of height in meters (m2).

A multi‐monitoring system (Accu‐Answer I saw: LBM‐01) was used to check for total cholesterol, and was recorded. A blood glucose monitoring system (On Call plus G113‐111) was used to test for blood glucose levels in mmol/L among all respondents, and these were recorded. A digital Omron blood pressure monitor (EC732) was used to measure systolic, diastolic, and pulse readings, which were recorded sequentially and averaged for accuracy. The readings were recorded after at least 5 min of rest in a seated position.

Data Interpretation

Body Mass Index (BMI)

BMI was computed as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Participants were classified based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC, 2023) criteria: underweight: < 18.5 kg/m2, normal weight: 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, overweight: 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, obesity: ≥ 30.0 kg/m2.

Blood Pressure (Hypertension Status)

Blood pressure was taken in a seated position and the average of two readings was recorded. Classification followed the Joint National Committee Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure guideline (2003):

Normal: systolic < 120 mmHg and diastolic < 80 mmHg, prehypertension: systolic 120–139 mmHg or Diastolic 80–89 mmHg, hypertension stage 1: systolic 140–159 mmHg or diastolic 90–99 mmHg, hypertension stage 2: ≥ 160 mmHg or ≥ 100 mmHg.

Fasting Blood Glucose

Fasting capillary glucose was interpreted following the American Diabetes Association criteria (2023): normal: ≤ 100 mg/dL (≤ 5.6 mmol/L), prediabetes: 100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L), diabetes: ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥ 7.0 mmol/L).

Grip Strength

Grip strength was measured using a calibrated hand dynamometer. Participants were asked to sit with their elbow at a 90° angle and squeeze the dynamometer as hard as possible for a few seconds. The measurement was repeated three times for each hand, and the highest value was recorded as the participant's maximum grip strength. Grip strength is a simple marker of overall muscle strength and has been shown to inversely correlate with cardiovascular risk. Lower grip strength has been associated with a higher prevalence of hypertension, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and increased risk of cardiovascular events.

Mental Health Assessment

Mental health was assessed using self‐reported screening questions. Participants were asked about symptoms related to mood, anxiety, and irritability. These questions were designed to provide a broad indication of mental health status within the study population.

Statistical Analysis

The data were coded, grouped, and analyzed utilizing the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. The outcomes of this analysis were presented as descriptive (frequencies and percentages), bivariate (χ 2 analysis), and Independent t‐test analyses (Welch test). All analyses were done at 95% confidence level with an α‐value of 0.05.

Distribution of Modifiable CVD Risk Factors

Table 2 describes modifiable cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors among the 120 participants. About one‐third (32.5%) had a history of CVD, though most engaged in physical activity (71.7%) and were non‐smokers (98.3%). Alcohol consumption was reported by 18.3%, while over half had high breakfast intake (52.5%), and 70% had moderate fruit intake. Nutritional Status and Cardio‐Metabolic Risk Factors Among the Teaching and Non‐Teaching Staff

Table 3 shows a comparison of nutritional and cardio‐metabolic risk factors between teaching (N = 46) and non‐teaching staff (N = 74). This revealed significant differences, particularly in body mass index (BMI), where non‐teaching staff had higher rates of normal weight (37.9%) than teaching staff (10.9%), while teaching staff showed greater proportions of overweight (47.8%) and first‐class obesity (8.7%) (p = 0.010). Blood glucose levels did not differ significantly (p = 0.427), although both groups had similar proportions within the normal range, 59.5% for non‐teaching and 73.9% for teaching staff, with small differences in prediabetes and high glucose risk. Despite differences in BMI, the overall cardio‐metabolic risk profiles of both groups appear similar, though teaching staff tend to exhibit slightly higher obesity rates.

Discussion

Cardiovascular risk is influenced by non‐modifiable factors like age and sex, as well as modifiable factors such as lifestyle, diet, stress, and blood pressure [13]. Moreover, a study by Chen et al. [14] proposed that exposure to psychological trauma or habitual stress may heighten the danger of several major diseases, including cardiovascular conditions. Chronic stress, prevalent among university staff due to workload and responsibilities, has been strongly linked to cardiovascular risk [14, 15]. This study examined some of these risk factors in a Ghanaian university setting.

The nutritional status of the study participants was determined based on BMI categories. Obesity has been identified as a significant factor in elevating the risk of CVDs. In line with findings from a study among university staff in the Western Cape, South Africa, our results show a substantial prevalence of obesity among staff. Specifically, 24% of non‐teaching staff and 33% of teaching staff in our study were classified as obese (BMI ≥ 30), reflecting a trend similar to that reported by Onagbiye et al. [16], where 35% of men and 45% of women were obese. Teaching staff showed higher obesity and cholesterol levels despite higher SES, likely due to occupational stress, sedentary work, and time‐constrained dietary choices [17, 18]. Evidence also suggests higher SES can paradoxically be associated with cardiometabolic risks through lower physical activity and calorie‐dense diets [19, 20, 21], highlighting the role of socio‐contextual factors in shaping health outcomes [22]. These findings highlight the persistent presence of excess body weight among university employees, underscoring the need for targeted interventions addressing diet, physical activity, and overall lifestyle to reduce cardiometabolic risk. Furthermore, 32% of the non‐teaching staff and 48% of the teaching staff were identified as overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9). The prevalence was comparatively higher than another study involving university staff at the University of Basrah in Bahrain, which recorded a prevalence of 7.3% [23]. However, the prevalence of overweight and obesity in this study is lower than the national average of 73%, in contrast to earlier studies within the country [24]. The factors contributing to obesity among the study participants remain unclear, but it is commonly linked to an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure. Contributing habits include high‐fat and sugar consumption, a sedentary lifestyle, and low fruit and vegetable intake.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends consuming at least 400 g or approximately five servings of fruits and vegetables (FV) daily to reduce the risk of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). However, family and cultural practices can influence dietary patterns [25]. In this study, 8.1% of the non‐teaching staff and 6.5% of the teaching staff were identified as having low fruit consumption (< 2 servings/day), potentially due to factors like limited fruit accessibility and socioeconomic status. Sixty‐six percent (66%) of the teaching and 76% of the non‐teaching staff reported moderate fruit consumption (2–4 servings/day), and 26% of the non‐teaching and 17% of the teaching staff reported high fruit consumption (≥ 5 servings/day). Similarly, a study conducted among 153 university workers at the University of Porto in Portugal, which aimed to assess how university workers ate at the workplace, found that 10.25% of them never ate fresh fruits at work, while 27.3% ate fresh fruits once a day, with 20.9% of them eating fresh fruits twice or more times daily. They also found that women ate fresh fruits more frequently than men (p < 0.001), while older workers (p = 0.044) and non‐teaching staff ate fruits more frequently compared to other professional workers [26].

Concerning breakfast consumption, some non‐teaching staff members, especially those in departments like security, cleaning, and maintenance, often skipped breakfast due to early work hours and socioeconomic factors such as educational background and income levels, which have a negative influence on their nutritional status. A similar trend was recorded in a study by Lee et al. [27], where better diet, good nutritional status and lower probability of obesity were found among adolescents as a result of higher socioeconomic characteristics of their parents. On the other hand, the majority of teaching staff (65%) with higher socioeconomic status consistently consumed main meals, especially breakfast, potentially leading to improved nutritional status and health outcomes [27].

Physical activity has been extensively correlated with improved well‐being and health status, with numerous researchers emphasizing its significance. Physical inactivity, conversely, is linked to the development of chronic ailments such as type 2 diabetes, cancer, and CVDs [28, 29]. The present study identified that 68% of the non‐teaching and 78% of the teaching staff engaged in various physical activities, such as jogging, skipping, gym workouts, and walking. This aligns with WHO recommendations for physical activity and reflects a higher participation rate [28, 30].

The study also explored tobacco use, reporting a notably low prevalence of 1.1% among study participants, which is comparatively lower than rates observed in Nigeria (10.4%) [31] and 11.3% in Burkina Faso [32]. This aligns with Ghana's efforts to curb tobacco use, supported by the Ghana Public Health Act (Act 851), which incorporates measures to safeguard individuals from tobacco‐related harm. The prevalence was particularly high among non‐teaching staff, which could be influenced by lower educational attainment. This supports prior research indicating that men in lower socioeconomic and educational strata are more likely to smoke [33].

Hypertension, characterized by systolic pressure at or above 140 mmHg over diastolic pressure at or above 90 mmHg, exhibited an overall prevalence of 48.33% among the teaching and non‐teaching staff. The non‐ teaching staff recorded the highest prevalence for hypertension (31.67%), with teaching staff accounting for the remainder. There may be inadequate health checks and preventive measures in place for non‐teaching staff, leading to a lack of awareness regarding their hypertensive status [34]. Non‐teaching staff may also encounter different day‐to‐day stressors and workplace dynamics, contributing to higher hypertension rates. Factors such as job security, workload, and sedentary behavior often differ between teaching and non‐teaching staff [34]. The participants whose systolic and diastolic blood pressure recorded within the hypertension category demonstrated an awareness of the condition, reflecting a positive trend in early detection and management. This prevalence is comparable to earlier studies in Ghana where most of the study respondents were aware of their hypertensive status. Among those individuals, some had received medical treatment and successfully managed their hypertension [35, 36, 37].

Elevated fasting blood glucose levels, indicative of potential diabetes, were observed in 9.46% of the non‐teaching and 6.52% of the teaching staff. Similarly, the risk of prediabetes was observed to be higher amongst the non‐teaching staff. This calls for significant concern as high blood glucose levels are associated with the risk of cardiovascular disease. Similarly, a study at the University of Ghana discovered fasting blood glucose levels at 4.2% among academic and nonacademic workers [38]. This indicates that blood glucose levels are rising; therefore, regular checks are necessary in our educational institutions.

High total cholesterol levels which is a recognized risk factor for coronary atherosclerosis were prevalent, with the teaching (48%) and non‐teaching staff (22%) exhibiting elevated levels. Gender and weight variations were also noted in cholesterol levels, consistent with the potential effects on blood vessel lumen and pressure [38].

The analysis of lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and dietary habits in relation to cardiovascular risk categories suggests a complex relationship where traditional behavioral modifications alone may not yield statistically significant changes in risk status. Most participants exhibited low smoking rates, minimal alcohol intake, and regular fruit consumption; however, none of these behaviors demonstrated significant associations with cardiovascular risk outcomes. While smoking status approached statistical significance, the low prevalence of smokers undermined the relevance of this association. Furthermore, similar findings were observed for physical activity and alcohol intake, indicating that these traditional risk factors alone may not capture the full spectrum of cardiovascular health determinants.

The next step in addressing these health risks is to implement comprehensive educational programs focused on lifestyle modifications. The WHO recommends regular consumption of fruits and vegetables, which was found lacking among the study participants, with a sizable portion of the non‐teaching staff consuming below the recommended servings [39]. Appropriate strategies could include workshops focused on nutrition and cooking classes, particularly aimed at increasing fruit and vegetable intake. Additionally, creating a supportive environment that promotes easy access to healthy food choices on campus is critical. Implementing regular health screenings, fitness challenges, and incentives for participation in physical activities could further encourage staff engagement and facilitate a reduction in obesity and hypertension rates. Furthermore, educational outreach should ensure that all staff, particularly those not engaged in teaching, are aware of their health status and understand the importance of routine health checks. This aligns with data showing that awareness and knowledge of CVD risk factors are critical for early interventions. Finally, a multi‐faceted approach combining nutrition education, physical activity enhancement, mental health support, and routine health screenings is imperative for reducing cardiovascular risk factors among university staff. Recognizing health as a priority in workplace policy and culture can lead to significant improvements in staff wellbeing.

Limitations

Uneven respondent distribution due to difficulties in recruiting teaching staff. Also, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of findings to the general population in Ghana.

Conclusions

The study assessed the nutritional status and the prevalence of some CVD risk factors among KNUST staff, identifying obesity, elevated cholesterol levels, hypertension, and fasting blood glucose as key challenges. This highlights the need for targeted health interventions among this population.
