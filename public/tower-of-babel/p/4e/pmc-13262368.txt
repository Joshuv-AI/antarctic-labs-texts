Nationwide survey of psychopathology and bullying in tic disorders with and without ADHD
Authors: Yu-Ju Lin, Fang-Ju Tsai, Susan Shur-Fen Gau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and tic disorder (TD) often co-occur. Given the well-established strong link between bullying and adverse psychiatric outcomes, understanding how TD and comorbid ADHD jointly interact to influence bullying involvement is crucial for informing clinical assessment and intervention. This study investigated psychopathology, bullying experiences, and their associations in children with TD, accounting for the influence ADHD, using data from a nationwide epidemiological survey.MethodsThe sample included 4816 children in grades 3, 5, and 7, randomly selected from 69 schools across Taiwan. Categorical psychiatric diagnoses were made through structured child interview using the Kiddie Epidemiologic Version of the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia, while dimensional emotional–behavioral problems were measured using the parent-reported Child Behavior Checklist. Bullying experiences were compared among four groups: TD-alone, ADHD-alone, TD+ADHD, and controls. Mediation analyses explored whether emotional–behavioral problems mediated the relationship between TD and bullying experiences.ResultsAmong participants, 102 (2%) had TD-alone, 388 (8%) had ADHD-alone, and 24 (0.5%) had comorbid TD+ADHD. TD was primarily related to obsessive-compulsive disorder [odds ratio (OR) = 7.35, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.93–20.68]. Children with TD exhibited elevated risks of both school (OR = 2.64, 95% CI = 1.47–4.75) and cyberbullying victimization (OR = 2.64, 95% CI = 1.47–4.74), whereas those with ADHD were more likely to be both perpetrators (OR = 2.64, 95% CI = 1.6–4.36) and victims (OR = 2.60, 95% CI = 1.95–3.47) of school bullying. After adjusting for ADHD, age, and sex, TD exerted a direct association with bullying victimization. Social problems and internalizing problems partially mediate the relationship between TD and school bullying victimization. Notably, thought problems had reduced involvement of cyberbullying among children with TD.ConclusionsTD exerted a direct association with school- and cyber-bullying victimization. Health professionals should routinely assess school-and cyber-bullying victimization, even when ADHD is not present.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13034-026-01080-x.Keywords: Tic disorders, Bullying, Cyber-bullying, ADHD, Psychopathology

Introduction

Tic disorders (TD), including Tourette syndrome and chronic tic disorders in this study, are childhood-onset neuropsychiatric conditions characterized by sudden, rapid, recurrent, and non-rhythmic motor movements (e.g., eye blinking or facial grimacing) and/or vocalizations (e.g., throat clearing or sniffing) that wax and wane in severity for more than one year. TD often co-occurs with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), a neurodevelopmental condition defined by a persistent pattern of inattention (e.g., difficulty sustaining focus) and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (e.g., excessive motor activity or acting without forethought) with symptom onset before age 12. In Taiwan, the lifetime prevalence of TD is approximately 2.6% [12], whereas ADHD affects 7.5–10.1% of the pediatric population [12]. ADHD and TD commonly co-occur: TD are seen in about 20% (14.7–20%) of children with ADHD [5, 53], and conversely, 30–54% of children with TD also meet the criteria for ADHD [27, 41, 57].

TD and ADHD share both overlapping and distinct psychiatric comorbidities. While both are associated with increased rates of oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder (CD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and mood disorders [25, 28, 29, 41, 46, 65], TD typically confers a greater risk for internalizing conditions such as obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and anxiety [22, 23, 36] compared with ADHD alone. Evidence regarding dimensional psychopathology remains mixed; some studies suggest an additive effect where the TD+ADHD subgroup faces the greatest behavioral burden [7, 52, 54], while others indicate that ADHD, rather than TD, is the primary cause of externalizing symptoms [59], social difficulties [36, 43], and functional impairments [46]. Given this complex interplay, research on TD should carefully account for comorbid ADHD to minimize potential confounding when evaluating associated impairments.

School (or traditional) bullying perpetration and victimization pose significant health concerns for children and adolescents [67]. In a survey, 7.1%, 4.8%, and 2.6% of children and adolescents reported having bullied other students (pure school bullying), been bullied by others (pure school victims), and having both bullied other students and been bullied by others, respectively [67]. ADHD is consistently identified as a risk factor for bullying involvement. In a Taiwanese adolescent sample, rates of pure perpetrators, pure victims, and both victim-perpetrators were 14.6%, 8.4%, and 5.6%, respectively, among those with ADHD [13]. Both the diagnosis and symptom severity of ADHD have been significantly associated with bullying perpetration and victimization in community studies [30, 71]. Findings on bullying experiences in TD were relatively inconsistent. One study reported that children and adolescents with TD were more likely to experience bullying victimization, and that victimization was positively associated with tic frequency, complexity, and severity [72]. In contrast, Chou et al. [13] reported that TD was not associated with school bullying victimization among adolescents with ADHD. Various definitions of TD, study populations, and whether psychiatric comorbidities were adjusted for likely contribute to these inconsistent results.

With increasing time spent on internet chatting and/or gaming among children and youth [64], global estimates of internet addiction are 7.02% [50]. ADHD-related symptoms and social adjustment difficulties are strongly correlated with more extended internet use [10]. Cyberbullying is likewise common: 8.4–10.3% of Taiwanese students reported being cyberbullied, and 5.8–8.1% reported cyberbullying others [8, 73]. Among adolescents with ADHD, rates are substantially higher, with cyberbullying victimization at 19.1% and cyberbullying perpetration at 14.3%, exceeding rates observed in the general adolescent population in Taiwan [69] and other countries [35, 63]. Evidence regarding cyberbullying in TD is limited, with one study reporting no association between TD and either cyberbullying or cyber-victimization [69], though further research is needed.

Overall, recent studies indicate that adolescents with ADHD are at increased risk for both cyberbullying and traditional bullying, whereas findings for TD remain sparse and inconsistent. Involvement in either form of bullying is linked to elevated rates of depression, suicidality [39], and broader adverse mental health outcomes [33, 45, 48]. The potential interplay among ADHD, TD, and bullying involvement in shaping psychiatric outcomes has not been fully delineated. This study examined the influence of TD and ADHD on psychiatric comorbidities, emotional–behavioral problems, and bullying involvement in children. We hypothesized that TD would increase vulnerability to internalizing problems and both school and cyberbullying victimization beyond the effects of ADHD. We further expected bullying involvement to associate with internalizing and externalizing problems and explored whether emotional and behavioral problems mediated the association between TD and bullying experiences.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study is a part of Taiwan’s National Epidemiological Study of Child Mental Disorders (TNESCMD). TNESCMD, a national school-based epidemiological survey, aims to estimate the prevalence of child mental disorders assessed by using the Epidemiological version of Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS-E) [11] according to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria [3] and to identify the possible risk and protective factors for these mental disorders [12]. TNESCMD used the stratified cluster sampling method, aiming to estimate the prevalence of common mental health problems, including bullying and mental disorders, and their possible risk factors. The TNESCMD sample was collected from 45 elementary and 24 junior high schools across 19 counties from June 1, 2015, to January 31, 2017. TNESCMD had complete data reported by both children and their caregivers. Detailed description of the study have been reported elsewhere [12, 37].

Participants

A total of 9560 students (4563 girls, 52.3%) in grades 3 (n = 2475), 5 (n = 2778), and 7 (n = 4307) were invited to participate in the study. Among them, 4816 (47.6%; 2520 boys, 52.3%) children, comprised by 1352 at grade 3 (28.1%; 693 boys, 51.2%), 1297 at grade 5 (n=, 26.9%; 666 boys, 51.4%) and 2167 at grade 7 (45.0%; 1161 boys, 53.6%) completed K-SADS-E and were included in the current study.

Interviewer training

Prior to the study, eight interviewers, all holding Bachelor’s or Master’s degrees in Psychology and with extensive clinical experience in child development, underwent six months of intensive training. The training covered childhood psychopathology and the standardized administration of the K-SADS-E and included a literature review, clinical observation, and supervised practice. To ensure diagnostic consistency, each interviewer independently evaluated ten clinical cases. Certification required achieving high inter-rater reliability, defined as at least 90% diagnostic agreement with gold-standard ratings provided by the corresponding author (a senior board-certified child psychiatrist).

Procedure for making a psychiatric diagnosis

Psychiatric diagnoses were established through interviews of children using the K-SADS-E, ensuring that all diagnoses met DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. The corresponding author reviewed any uncertain cases to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Participants were classified as having a psychiatric diagnosis if they had active symptoms within the six months preceding the interview.

The TD group included two diagnoses: Tourette syndrome, defined by the presence of multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic, and persistent (chronic) motor or vocal tic disorder, defined by either motor or vocal tics but not both. Symptom onset occurred before age 18, with tics persisting for more than 1 year and typically waxing and waning. Functional impairment is not required for the diagnosis of TD under DSM-5 criteria.

ADHD was diagnosed when children exhibited a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity that interfered with functioning or development, with at least six symptoms in either domain. Consistent with DSM-5 criteria, symptoms had to (1) begin before age 12, (2) occur in two or more settings (e.g., home and school), and (3) clearly interfere with or reduce the quality of social, academic, or occupational functioning.

The Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital approved this study (approval number: 201411056RINA) before study implementation. Written informed assent and consent were obtained from each study participant and their parents after an explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures and reassurance regarding confidentiality.

Measures

The Mandarin version of the kiddie-schedule for affective disorders and schizophrenia-epidemiological (K-SADS-E) version for DSM-5

The Mandarin K-SADS-E for DSM-5 is a semi-structured clinical interview designed to systematically assess mental disorders in children and adolescents according to DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and was developed with authorization by the American Psychiatric Association [3, 11, 66]. It partially builds upon the earlier Chinese version of K-SADS-E for DSM-IV [24], which was translated and culturally adapted by the Child Psychiatry Research Group in Taiwan using a rigorous two-way translation process and item modification to enhance cultural relevance [26]. Psychometric evaluation of the Mandarin version of the K-SADS-E for DSM-5 demonstrated excellent interrater reliability (prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa = 0.80–1.00) and strong convergent and divergent validity in our previous validation study [11].

Child behavior checklist (CBCL)-parent report form

The Chinese CBCL Parent Report Form is a parent-reported questionnaire completed for children aged 4–18 years. The 112-item Chinese CBCL-Parent Report Form, with a 3-point scale (0 = Not True, 1 = Somewhat or Sometimes True, 2 = Very True or Often True), was used to assess clinical behavioral/emotional problems [2]. The t-score for each behavioral/emotional problem on the Chinese CBCL was calculated using normative data from Taiwan [60, 68]. There are eight subscales, including withdrawal, somatic complaints, anxious/depressed, social problems, attention problems, aggressive behavior, and delinquent behaviors. The internalizing problems are the composite score of withdrawal, somatic complaints, and problems of anxiety and depression, while the externalizing problems are the composite score of aggressive and delinquent behaviors. The Chinese version of the CBCL was standardized in 2000, and the internal consistency (Cronbach α) and one-month test-retest reliability (intra-class correlation coefficient, ICC) for all subscales of the Chinese CBCL-Parent Report Form ranged from 0.72 to 0.90 and 0.51 to 0.74, respectively [68].

Chinese version of school bullying experience questionnaire (C-SBEQ)

The C-SBEQ was used to assess participants’ involvement in school bullying over the past year. The questionnaire consists of 16 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale: 0 (never), 1 (just a little), 2 (often), and 3 (all the time). It comprises four 4-item subscales assessing (1) victimization by passive bullying (Items 1–4; e.g., social exclusion, being called mean nicknames, and being spoken ill of), (2) victimization by active bullying (Items 5–8; e.g., being beaten up, being forced to do work, and having money, school supplies, or snacks taken away), (3) perpetration of passive bullying (Items 9–12), and (4) perpetration of active bullying (Items 13 −16). Higher scores indicate greater frequency of involvement in school bullying. For analytic purposes, participants who responded “often” or “all the time” (scores of 2 or 3) to any of Items 1–8 or Items 9–16 were classified as self-reported victims and perpetrators, respectively. The C-SBEQ has a stable four-factor structure, good test-retest reliability, and acceptable internal reliability among Taiwanese adolescents [70]. The psychometric properties (ICC = 0.74–0.81, Kappa coefficient = 0.42–0.55) of the C-SBEQ were good in Taiwan [70].

Chinese version of cyberbullying experiences questionnaire (C-CEQ)

The C-CEQ assessed participants’ involvement in cyberbullying over the past year. The six-item instrument uses a 4-point Likert scale (0 = never to 3 = all the time). It comprises two 3-item subscales: cyberbullying perpetration and victimization across various online platforms, including email, blogs, social media (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, Plurk), and images or video clips. The first three items assess experiences of posting mean or hurtful comments, sharing upsetting pictures, photos, or videos, and spreading rumors online. In contrast, the final three items assess corresponding experiences of being victimized by these same behaviors. Cronbach’s α coefficients for the perpetration and victimization subscales were 0.64 and 0.70, respectively. For analysis, responses were dichotomized (yes/no), and participants who endorsed any non-zero score on Items 1–3 or Items 4–6 were classified as self-reported cyberbullying perpetrators and victims, respectively [69].

Informant sources

The K-SADS-E, C-SBEQ, and C-CEQ were completed by participating students, whereas the CBCL was completed by their parents. A dual-assessment approach was used to evaluate psychiatric comorbidities. The K-SADS-E was administered to establish categorical DSM-5 diagnoses through structured child interviews, whereas the CBCL assessed dimensional severity and the range of emotional and behavioral symptoms via parent-report questionnaires. Due to constraints in survey time and resources, formal psychiatric interviews were conducted with the children, whereas parents’ observations were collected using standardized questionnaires. This approach enabled the assessment of both clinically diagnosed disorders and subthreshold emotional–behavioral symptoms that might not be captured using a single assessment tool.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All participating students were divided into four groups regarding the diagnostic status of ADHD and TD, respectively: (1) Control, (2) TD-alone, (3) ADHD-alone, and (4) TD+ADHD. The control group comprised children without TD or ADHD. Participants with other psychiatric conditions were not excluded to maintain a control group that reflects the broader clinical population. Sensitivity analyses excluding children with psychiatric conditions from the control group yielded results largely consistent with the primary analyses. To examine between-group differences while accounting for stratification and clustering, logistic regression models were estimated for categorical outcomes using PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC, and linear regression models were estimated for continuous outcomes using PROC SURVEYREG. Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc comparisons. Multiple analyses were conducted in the group comparison of bullying experience by adjusting for age, sex, and any psychiatric disorder. Group comparisons and interactions between group and bully experience for internalizing and externalizing problems were calculated after adjusting for age and sex. Given the low prevalence of certain psychiatric comorbidities, we applied Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression to address sparse data and reduce bias. Mediation analyses, developed by Preacher and Hayes [55], were used to calculate the mediation roles of emotional/behavioral problems in the relationship between TD and bullying experience to explore the potential mechanisms linking TD/ADHD to bullying involvement. Because variables were measured simultaneously, these indirect effects should be interpreted as statistical associations that suggest possible mechanisms for future longitudinal validation, rather than as confirmed causal relationships.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 4302 controls, 102 children with TD-alone, 388 with ADHD-alone, and 24 with TD+ADHD completed the questionnaires and interviews. Among children with TD, only 13 children had Tourette syndrome. Table 1 presents comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics across the four groups. There was no significant difference in age between any two groups. Males were more predominant in the TD+ADHD, ADHD-alone, and TD-alone groups than in controls. Children with TD+ADHD exhibited more current and past inattentive symptoms than those with ADHD-alone, followed by the TD and control groups. Children with ADHD-alone demonstrated more current and previous hyperactive/impulsive symptoms than those with TD and controls. No significant differences were observed among the four groups in parental education years.

Emotional-behavioral problems stratified by bullying experience

We further examined emotional and behavioral problems by dividing the four groups based on bullying experience, as shown in Figs. 1(a)-1(d) and Supplementary Table S3. We then calculated the statistical effects of group, bullying experience, age, and sex, and presented them in Supplementary Table S4. Generally speaking, both perpetrators and victims of school bullying had significantly more emotional and behavioral problems than their counterparts after controlling for group, age, and sex (all p < 0.05). Cyber-bullying perpetrators had significantly more attentional (F = 12.09, p < 0.001), externalizing (F = 10.39, p < 0.01) and internalizing (F = 5.78, p < 0.05) behavioral problems, excluding somatic complaints, while cyber-bullying victims also had significantly higher attentional (F = 8.44, p < 0.01), externalizing (F = 6.55, p < 0.05) and internalizing (F = 4.71, p < 0.05) problems, excluding somatic complaints and withdrawal than their counterparts. There was no interaction between diagnostic groups and bullying experience interactions on behavioral and emotional problems in cyber-bullying (Supplementary Table S5). Significant interactions between diagnostic groups and bullying experience in school bullying were attributable to higher levels of somatic complaints and aggressive behavior in non-perpetrators and non-victims compared with perpetrators and victims within the TD+ADHD group, a pattern opposite to that observed in the other three groups. The small sample size in the TD+ADHD group may have contributed to these conflicting results, thereby reducing their representativeness of the condition.

Emotional and behavioral problems of four groups stratified by bullying experience. Without stratifying bullying experience, children with ADHD alone had significantly more problems in all subscales of CBCL than controls, and more externalizing problems than children with TD alone. Both perpetrators (a) and victims (b) of school bullying and perpetrators (c) and victims (d) of cyber-bullying had significantly more emotional and behavioral problems than their counterparts after adjusting for group, age, and sex (all p < 0.05; please refer to supplementary Table S4). An exception occurred within the ADHD + TD group: both school bullying perpetrators (a) and victims (b) exhibited fewer internalizing and externalizing problems than their uninvolved peers. Similarly, cyber-bullying perpetrators (c) and victims (d) demonstrated fewer thought and attentional difficulties than their respective non-involved counterparts. There was no clinically meaningful interaction between diagnostic groups and bullying experience in emotional and behavioral problems (Supplementary Table S5). School bullying and cyberbullying

Table 4 presents group comparisons of school and cyberbullying involvement, both before and after adjustment for age, sex, and any psychiatric disorder. Children with ADHD alone [OR = 2.48 (95% CI: 1.72–3.58)] and TD alone [OR = 2.50 (1.38–4.52)] were more likely to be school bullying victims compared with controls (supplementary Table S7). Children with ADHD with or without TD were also more likely to be perpetrators of school bullying [OR = 5.67 (1.64–19.66) and 2.20 (1.30–3.71), respectively]. Those with TD+ADHD showed a markedly higher likelihood of cyberbullying perpetration [OR = 8.04 (2.36–27.44)], while children with TD alone were more likely to be cyberbullying victims [OR = 2.52 (1.39–4.59)]. Mediation role of emotional/behavioral problems in the association between TD and bullying experience

The mediation analysis was adjusted for age, sex, and ADHD. There was no direct association of TD with school bullying perpetration, although inattention significantly mediated this association (Fig. 2a). In contrast, TD showed significant direct association with school bullying victimization (Fig. 2b), cyberbullying perpetration (Fig. 2c), and cyberbullying victimization (Fig. 2d). Social problems and internalizing behavioral problems significantly mediated the relationship between TD and school bullying victimization. In contrast, thought problems attenuated the associations between TD and cyberbullying experiences, including both perpetration and victimization. Additionally, internalizing problems mediated the links between TD and both school and cyberbullying victimization. The estimates of the mediation model are presented in Supplementary S9.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this nationwide survey is among the few studies to simultaneously examine DSM-5 psychiatric disorders, emotional/behavioral problems, and bullying experiences in both school and online contexts among children with TD and ADHD. Our findings suggest that children with TD exhibit distinct psychiatric profiles and heightened victimization risks that persist even after accounting for the effects of ADHD.

Psychiatric comorbid conditions

Consistent with previous findings [23, 28], TD was independently associated with an increased risk of OCD, while ADHD exhibited a broader spectrum of comorbidities, including ASD, ODD, and mood/anxiety disorders [46]. This divergence aligns with genome-wide evidence identifying distinct shared genetic factors for TD and OCD versus the ADHD, ASD and MDD cluster [15]. While the co-existence of ADHD and TD may exert additive effects on dysthymic disorders [59], the small size of our TD+ADHD subgroup limits definitive conclusions.

Emotional-behavior problems

Dimensionally, children with ADHD showed significantly higher social, internalizing, and externalizing problems than controls, with greater attentional deficits than those with TD alone [7, 59]. Although an additive categorical pattern was noted for depressive disorders, the TD+ADHD group did not demonstrate significantly more severe internalizing problems, likely due to limited statistical power. Clinically, these results underscore that comorbid ADHD symptoms, rather than TD itself, are the primary drivers of social difficulties, withdrawal, and broader functional impairment in this population [31, 58].

School bullying

In our survey, we found that children with ADHD, regardless of TD status, were more likely than controls to engage in school bullying perpetration, whereas, children with TD and/or ADHD were at increased risk of school bullying victimization, consistent with previous studies [1, 16, 21]. In contrast, the association between TD and bullying perpetration becomes nonsignificant after accounting for comorbid conditions, particularly ADHD and related behavioral problems [9, 42]. This is further supported by our mediation analysis, which revealed no direct association between TD and school bullying perpetration. Instead, this relationship was fully mediated by attentional problems, providing empirical evidence that co-occurring ADHD symptoms play a more critical role in perpetration than tic pathology itself.

Children with TD also appear to be more vulnerable to bullying victimization [72]. Notably, some studies have reported that TD is independently associated with bullying victimization even after controlling for age, sex, and co-occurring mental, behavioral, and developmental disorders [9]. This is highly consistent with our findings: both group comparisons and mediation analyses demonstrated a significant direct association between TD and school bullying victimization. This relationship remained robust after controlling for age, sex, and comorbid ADHD, and was not fully mediated by other emotional/behavioral problems. Overall, our study indicates the core feature of TD itself might have unique association with bullying victimization.

We found social and internalizing problems partially mediated the correlation between TD and school bullying victimization, a pattern consistently observed in the general population [18, 32] and among individuals with psychiatric disorders [40]. Social anxiety has been reported to predict traditional bullying more than cyber-bullying [17], with reciprocal associations also noted [62]; our findings indicate that these relationships were also evident in children with TD.

Cyberbullying

Children with TD alone were significantly more likely to experience cyberbullying victimization than neurotypical controls. Our mediation analysis further confirms a direct association between TD and both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization, independent of ADHD. To our knowledge, there is currently little empirical evidence addressing the link between TD and cyberbullying; thus, our study provides novel insights into the digital social risks faced by this population. Further studies are warranted to validate these results and elucidate the underlying mechanisms, which will inform more effective prevention and intervention strategies.

There is growing evidence that cyberbullying victimization is significantly associated with depression and anxiety [34]. Our findings further indicate that internalizing problems mediate the association between TD and cyberbullying victimization. While some literature suggests that depression is more likely a consequence of bullying victimization [32, 34], the cross-sectional nature of our study precludes definitive causal inferences. Nevertheless, our data demonstrate a robust association between TD, internalizing problems, and victimization, but longitudinal research is required to clarify the bidirectional or temporal relationships between these variables.

Notably, children with TD who exhibited more thought problems were less likely to be involved in cyberbullying. Prominent thought problems, often characterized by withdrawal related to paranoid, eccentric, or idiosyncratic thinking, restricted interests, and excessive immersion in inner experiences, may limit social manipulation skills and reduce engagement in cyberbullying behaviors. While previous research indicates that vulnerability to thought problems increases the risk of traditional bullying victimization and may exacerbate cognitive distortions [61] , our findings suggest that these problems are associated with a lower likelihood of cyberbullying involvement (both as perpetrators and victims). This observed link likely reflects a reduced exposure to social media; individuals with high thought problem scores may be less active on digital platforms due to social withdrawal or a preference for non-social solitary activities. This interpretation aligns with evidence that individuals with psychotic [6] or autistic traits [44, 47], who often present thought problems, engage less frequently in social media activities than their neurotypical peers. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms and risk factors underlying bullying involvement among children with TD.

ADHD and cyberbullying

Previous studies have consistently shown that children with ADHD were at increased risk of both cyberbullying perpetration and victimization [19, 38, 49, 69]. Unexpectedly, in our survey, only children with TD+ADHD demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of cyberbullying perpetration, with a nonsignificant trend toward increased risk compared with children with TD alone and ADHD alone (supplementary Table S7, not surviving Bonferroni correction). One possible explanation is that the severity of ADHD symptoms and the complexity of co-occurring psychiatric conditions (also comorbidity of TD and ADHD) in our community sample were lower than those reported in clinical samples [38], thereby attenuating associations between ADHD and bullying experiences. Alternatively, the relatively small number of participants reporting bullying experiences may have limited statistical power to detect group differences. Accordingly, we conducted additional analyses using the whole sample, entering ADHD and TD simultaneously into multiple regression models rather than stratifying participants into four groups. After adjusting for age, sex, and any psychiatric comorbidity, ADHD was significantly associated with school bullying perpetration (Wald χ²=10.26, p = 0.001), victimization (Wald χ²=19.84, p < 0.001), cyberbullying perpetration (Wald χ²=5.67, p = 0.02), and victimization (Wald χ²=5.54, p = 0.02). TD was independently correlated to school bullying victimization (Wald χ²=6.08, p = 0.02), cyberbullying perpetration (Wald χ²=6.07, p = 0.02), and cyberbullying victimization (Wald χ²=8.00, p = 0.005), after controlling for ADHD.

Bullying and emotional/behavioral problems

Both traditional bullying and cyberbullying experiences, including perpetration and victimization, are associated with a wide range of emotional and behavioral problems [4, 20, 33, 38, 39, 48]. Our findings provide additional evidence that these associations of emotional/behavioral problems and bullying experience are independent of TD and ADHD diagnoses and not moderated by TD or ADHD status.

Bullying prevalence and cultural perspectives of TD and victimization

International literature indicates that bullying prevalence varies considerably across studies, ranging from 6.1% to 61.3% for traditional bullying and 3.3% to 74.6% for cyberbullying among adolescents [32]. A recent meta-analysis reported pooled global rates of 16% for perpetration and 25% for victimization [4]. While sociocultural factors, such as the collectivistic nature of Asian cultures, were previously thought to influence perceptions of bullying, recent evidence suggests no significant regional differences in prevalence between Asia and other regions [32].

The prevalence rates in our survey (6.94% for perpetration and 15.95% for victimization of school bullying, and 7.39% for perpetration and 12.75% for victimization of cyberbullying) were relatively low compared with global estimates. This discrepancy may partly reflect methodological factors. Specifically, some questionnaires were completed in classroom settings and collected by teachers, which may have discouraged students from fully disclosing bullying behaviors. Concerns about being labeled as ‘troublemakers’ or triggering teacher intervention may have led to under-reporting of both bullying perpetration and victimization.

Stigma and misconceptions about persistent tic disorders are pervasive globally, but the degree and nature of stigma can vary by cultural context [56]. Evidence suggests that collectivistic Asian cultures may exhibit a greater preference for maintaining social distance from individuals with mental health conditions compared to Western populations [14, 51]. Such cultural nuances may aggravate peer rejection and discrimination, specifically for children with visible tic symptoms, thereby increasing their susceptibility to bullying victimization, a relationship clearly evidenced in our Taiwanese sample.

Limitations

Our study has several methodological limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Specifically, results of mediation analysis represent statistical associations rather than causal mechanisms. Longitudinal studies are required to establish temporal precedence. Second, reliance on parent-reported information for some measures may have limited the detection of children’s internalizing problems. This may account for the discrepancy between the higher prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities (i.e., anxiety, OCD, sleep disorders) identified via child clinical interviews and the lack of significant differences in parent-reported emotional and behavioral symptoms, especially internalizing problems, in children with TD compared to controls. Third, several potentially important factors, such as parental cognitive function, parenting style, family functioning, and school environment, could influence experiences of school and cyberbullying. In addition, although we adopted inclusive criteria to reduce potential underreporting, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires means that social desirability bias cannot be entirely excluded, particularly for perpetration of bullying. Fourth, bullying was not differentiated into active and passive forms, nor were perpetrator-victim roles examined, owing to the small sample size of the TD+ADHD subgroup. Fifth, the relatively small sample size of the comorbid TD+ADHD subgroup may have limited our statistical power to detect subtle effects between these two conditions. It may lead to less stable estimates of the association between TD+ADHD and bullying perpetration. The co-occurrence of ADHD and TD in our survey is relatively low (19.05% of children with TD had ADHD, and 5.83% of those with ADHD had TD) compared to other studies. Our lower co-occurrence rates are likely due to the community-based sample, which captures milder, non-referred cases of isolated TD. Furthermore, our diagnosis was based on symptoms occurring within six months preceding the interview. Because more than a third of our sample was 13 years old, the data may reflect the natural remission of TD that typically follows early adolescence. Sixth, some participants were receiving psychotropic medication for their mental conditions at the time of assessment, which may have attenuated the severity of their emotional and behavioral problems. Seventh, a potential limitation of this study is the possibility of selection bias, as fewer than 50% of the invited students completed the K-SADS-E interview. Post-hoc comparisons indicated that parents who declined participation were slightly older than those who accepted (mean difference approximately 0.5 years, p < 0.01), and indigenous parents were somewhat more represented among participants (see Supplementary Table S10). However, parental education levels did not differ significantly between the groups. Given the minimal magnitude of these differences and the similarity in key socioeconomic indicators, the potential impact of non-response bias on the primary findings is likely limited. Despite these limitations, this study is the first nationwide survey in Taiwan to comprehensively assess a wide range of issues, including psychiatric diagnoses, emotional and behavioral problems, and both school and cyberbullying, in a large sample of children. Moreover, diagnoses were conducted by child psychiatrists and trained research assistants, rather than relying solely on questionnaire-based screening.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest a trend where children with TD may have increased involvement in school and cyberbullying victimization, independent of comorbid ADHD. Furthermore, the co-occurrence of TD and ADHD appears to be associated with a higher likelihood of bullying others, though this pattern warrants further investigation in larger clinical samples. TD had both direct association with bullying involvement and indirect association mediated by attentional (on perpetration), internalizing, and social problems (on victimization). Bullying experiences, per se, were also associated with broader emotional and behavioral difficulties. Our study could only provide cross-sectional characteristics and potential mechanisms of bullying experience among children of TD with or without ADHD. Further longitudinal clinical studies are warranted to clarify the causal relationship. Given their increased vulnerability to bullying involvement, children with TD should be monitored for bullying victimization and related emotional and social problems, even without ADHD. These findings highlight the need for integrated school and clinical interventions to enhance emotional regulation and social skills to prevent bullying among children with TD.
