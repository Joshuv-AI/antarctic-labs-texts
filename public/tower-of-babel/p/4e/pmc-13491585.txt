Automated detection of lumbar disc herniation at L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels on sagittal MRI using a YOLO-based deep learning model
Authors: Zeynep Karakuzu Güngör, Husam Vehbi, Ahmetcan Cansın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo conduct a preliminary single-center feasibility study of a YOLO-based deep-learning model for automated detection of lumbar disc herniation at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels on sagittal MRI.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 372 anonymized sagittal T2-weighted lumbar MRI slices from adult patients evaluated for low back pain at a single tertiary center were reviewed. Intervertebral discs at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels were labeled with bounding boxes into four classes (L4–L5 herniated, L5–S1 herniated, L4–L5 non-herniated, L5–S1 non-herniated) by two clinicians (a radiologist and a PM&R specialist, each with 6 years of post-residency experience), with consensus adjudication of disagreements, using the combined North American Spine Society / ASSR / ASNR nomenclature as reference. The dataset was split into 337 training, 25 validation, and 10 test images. Rotation-based augmentation was applied. A YOLO object-detection architecture was trained and evaluated using precision, recall, F1, and mean average precision (mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5–0.95). 95% bootstrap confidence intervals were estimated for aggregate metrics.ResultsThe model achieved an overall precision of 0.738, recall of 0.698, mAP@0.5 of 0.744, and mAP@0.5–0.95 of 0.454, with a best overall F1 of 0.69 at a confidence threshold of approximately 0.30. Herniated classes outperformed non-herniated classes, with the highest recall observed for L5–S1 herniated discs (0.963). Bootstrap confidence intervals were wide, consistent with the small test set.ConclusionThis preliminary feasibility study suggests that YOLO-based level-specific detection of lumbar disc herniation at L4–L5 and L5–S1 on sagittal MRI is technically feasible. Given the very small test set, single-center design, absence of external validation, and lack of radiologist benchmarking, these results are hypothesis-generating and not yet sufficient to support any clinical or autonomous-diagnostic use. Larger multi-center validation and prospective comparison with radiologists are required.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation is one of the most common causes of low back pain and sciatica, with a lifetime prevalence reported between 1% and 3% in the general population [1]. Epidemiological and surgical series consistently demonstrate that the majority of symptomatic herniations occur at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels, reflecting increased biomechanical loading and segmental mobility at these segments [2, 3].

MRI is widely accepted as the imaging modality of choice for evaluating lumbar disc pathology due to its superior soft-tissue contrast and ability to visualize neural structures [4]. Nevertheless, interpretation of lumbar MRI remains challenging, particularly in distinguishing clinically relevant disc herniation from age-related degenerative changes, which are frequently observed even in asymptomatic individuals [5]. Interobserver variability in MRI-based disc assessment has been well documented, underscoring the need for supportive diagnostic tools [6].

Deep-learning approaches to lumbar spine MRI have taken several forms. Segmentation networks based on U-Net architectures have been used to delineate disc and vertebral boundaries pixel-wise. Slice-level classification CNNs — of which SpineNet is a representative example — produce per-slice or per-disc probability outputs without explicit localization [7]. Two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN first propose candidate regions and then classify them, offering high accuracy at the cost of inference speed and architectural complexity. One-stage detectors such as YOLO predict bounding boxes and class labels in a single forward pass, trading a modest reduction in peak accuracy for substantially faster inference and a simpler deployment footprint — properties that are attractive for pre-screening and worklist-prioritization scenarios [8, 9]. Because our goal was to test the feasibility of a lightweight, interpretable (box-based) pre-screening tool at two clinically prioritized levels, a YOLO-based one-stage detector was selected as the framework of this feasibility study. The clinical motivation for automated detection is threefold. First, the steady growth in lumbar MRI utilization places increasing reporting pressure on radiology services, particularly in high-volume tertiary centers. Second, inter-observer variability in MRI-based disc assessment is well documented [6], suggesting room for a consistent second-reader or triage aid. Third, from a workflow standpoint, even a moderately accurate detector restricted to the two most commonly affected levels (L4–L5 and L5–S1) could in principle be used to prioritize worklists and pre-flag likely abnormal studies for earlier radiologist review — without, we emphasize, replacing the radiologist’s final adjudication. Accordingly, the specific aim of this study was to test, as a preliminary single-center feasibility question, whether a YOLO-based one-stage object-detection model trained on sagittal T2-weighted lumbar MRI can localize and classify discs at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels into herniated versus non-herniated with performance (precision, recall, F1, mAP@0.5, mAP@0.5–0.95) consistent with a potential future pre-screening role — while explicitly deferring questions of external validity, radiologist comparison, and clinical impact to subsequent studies.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center, retrospective feasibility study was conducted at Kanuni Sultan Süleyman Training and Research Hospital, İstanbul, Turkey. Consecutive sagittal lumbar MRI examinations performed between 2024 and 2025 for the clinical indication of low back pain and/or radiculopathy were screened for eligibility. The dataset included patients aged between 18 and 78 years (mean ± SD: 45.0 ± 12.5), with a male/female ratio of 156 / 216. MRI examinations were performed using 1.5-T and/or 3-T scanners. Sagittal T2-weighted images were used for analysis. Typical imaging parameters included slice thickness of 3–4 mm, interslice gap of 0.3–1 mm, and matrix size ranging from 256 × 256 to 512 × 512. All examinations were fully anonymized before analysis in accordance with the institutional data-protection policy. This study was approved by our institution Research Ethics Committee (Approval No:143, Date: 25/06/2025). As this was a retrospective study using anonymized data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. All procedures involving human participants and human data were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Inclusion criteria

(i) adult patients aged ≥ 18 years; (ii) sagittal T2-weighted lumbar MRI with clear visualization of the L4–L5 and L5–S1 disc levels; (iii) complete imaging metadata.

Exclusion criteria

(i) severe motion artifacts or image-quality degradation precluding disc assessment; (ii) prior lumbar surgery at the L4–L5 or L5–S1 levels (e.g., discectomy, fusion, prosthesis) that would distort the target anatomy; (iii) tumor, infection, fracture, or congenital malformation involving the target levels; (iv) non-standard sequences or incomplete studies.

Image dataset

For each patient, a single mid-sagittal T2-weighted slice visualizing both the L4–L5 and L5–S1 intervertebral discs was selected as input. When multiple adjacent sagittal slices satisfied this criterion, the slice demonstrating the most conspicuous pathological finding (e.g., the slice showing the largest protrusion at one or both of the target levels) was chosen, as judged by the annotating clinicians in consensus. In patients with multi-segmental disease (concurrent L4–L5 and L5–S1 herniation, possibly with differing directions of protrusion), priority was given to the slice that best demonstrated both levels simultaneously; when no single slice captured both, the slice showing the more severe finding was selected. All analyses were therefore performed at the image level, with one representative image per patient; results should not be over-interpreted at the disc or patient level. This single-slice-per-patient strategy is acknowledged as a methodological simplification in the Limitations section Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Inter-rater agreement was not formally quantified; however, all annotations were reviewed in consensus to ensure labeling consistency. All images were independently reviewed and annotated by experienced clinicians using bounding-box labeling. Intervertebral discs at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels were annotated and categorized into four classes: L4–L5 herniated, L5–S1 herniated, L4–L5 non-herniated, and L5–S1 non-herniated, in accordance with standardized lumbar disc nomenclature. Disc herniation was defined according to standardized nomenclature [3], including protrusion and extrusion beyond the disc space. All included MRI examinations were preoperative with respect to the target levels; patients with prior L4–L5 or L5–S1 surgery (discectomy, fusion, prosthesis) were excluded at the screening stage to avoid post-surgical anatomical distortion affecting model training and evaluation.

The dataset was divided into 337 images for training, 25 images for validation, and 10 images for testing. Image annotation and dataset management were performed using the Roboflow platform, enabling consistent labeling and augmentation procedures. Given the relatively limited dataset size, a level-specific detection strategy was adopted to improve robustness and clinical relevance.

Due to the limited size of the test dataset, statistical significance testing was not performed and results should be interpreted as preliminary.

Annotation process

Bounding-box annotations were performed independently by two clinicians: a board-certified radiologist (H.V.) with 6 years of post-residency experience in musculoskeletal and spine MRI, and a physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist (Z.K.G.) with 6 years of post-residency experience in the clinical evaluation of lumbar disc pathology. Each annotator was blinded to the other’s labels and to the clinical outcome. Disc herniation was defined in accordance with the combined nomenclature of the North American Spine Society, the American Society of Spine Radiology, and the American Society of Neuroradiology [3] — specifically, as a localized displacement of disc material beyond the normal margins of the intervertebral disc space (protrusion or extrusion), distinguished from circumferential bulging and from age-related degenerative changes without focal displacement. Each disc was labeled by anatomical level (L4–L5 or L5–S1) and by pathological status (herniated or non-herniated). Disagreements between annotators were resolved by consensus in a joint review session; in unresolved cases, a third senior radiologist adjudicated the final label. A formal quantitative measure of inter-rater reliability (e.g., Cohen’s κ) was not computed on the full dataset and is acknowledged as a limitation (Fig. 1.)

Data augmentation

To mitigate the effects of limited dataset size and improve generalizability, rotation-based data augmentation was applied during training. Controlled angular rotations were used to preserve anatomical orientation while introducing variability without altering pathological characteristics.

Model architecture and evaluation

Object detection was performed using a YOLOv8 architecture (Ultralytics implementation). Dataset management, annotation, augmentation, and export in YOLO format were performed on the Roboflow platform. Training and evaluation were executed in Python 3.10 with PyTorch 2.x, on an NVIDIA RTX-series GPU. Input images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels. The network was initialized with COCO-pretrained weights. Training hyperparameters were as follows: optimizer AdamW; initial learning rate 0.001; momentum 0.937; weight decay 0.0005; batch size 16; number of epochs 100; early-stopping patience 20. Only rotation-based augmentation (± 10°) was applied during training to preserve anatomical orientation. Evaluation followed standard object-detection protocols, with precision, recall, F1-score, and mean average precision (mAP) computed at IoU = 0.5 (mAP@0.5) and across IoU = 0.5–0.95 (mAP@0.5–0.95) (Fig. 2).

Results

Overall model performance

The YOLO-based model demonstrated an overall precision of 0.738 and a recall of 0.698. The mAP@0.5 was 0.744, while the mAP@0.5–0.95 reached 0.454. The optimal F1-score across all classes was 0.69 at a confidence threshold of approximately 0.30, indicating balanced precision and recall at this operating point (Fig. 3A and B).

The disparity between the high recall observed in the L5–S1 herniated class (0.963) and the lower performance of the non-herniated classes may reflect two non-mutually exclusive factors. First, the per-class image counts (Table 1) indicate an imbalance between herniated and non-herniated instances and between the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels, which can bias the model toward majority classes. Second, non-herniated discs in a symptomatic cohort frequently exhibit mild degenerative features (bulging, loss of T2 signal) that overlap morphologically with early herniation, blurring the decision boundary. These observations should be interpreted cautiously given the small test set. Given the relatively small test set (n = 10 images), the reported performance metrics should be interpreted with caution, as they may be statistically unstable.

Class-wise performance

Class-wise analysis revealed superior performance for herniated disc classes compared with non-herniated discs. The highest recall was observed for the L5–S1 herniated class (0.963), followed by the L4–L5 herniated class. In contrast, non-herniated disc classes showed lower precision and recall values, likely reflecting morphological overlap with early degenerative changes (Fig. 4.)

The gap between mAP@0.5 (0.744) and mAP@0.5–0.95 (0.454) indicates that the model is considerably better at identifying the correct disc region than at producing tightly localized bounding boxes across stricter IoU thresholds. Qualitative inspection of failure cases on the test set showed two recurrent patterns: (i) slight over-extension of the predicted bounding box into the adjacent vertebral endplate, particularly for non-herniated discs; and (ii) occasional low-confidence predictions on studies with borderline protrusion versus bulging morphology. These failure modes are consistent with the moderate mAP@0.5–0.95 and are relevant to clinical deployment, where pixel-accurate delineation may be required for downstream tasks (e.g., measurement of protrusion extent).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a YOLO-based object detection model can detect lumbar disc herniation at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels on sagittal MRI with clinically meaningful performance. A level-focused strategy is clinically justified because these two segments account for a large proportion of clinically relevant lumbar disc herniations encountered in routine practice, making them appropriate targets for a triage-oriented decision-support tool [1, 13].

Deep learning has been increasingly used for automated analysis of spinal MRI, including structured labeling and disc pathology assessment. SpineNet represents a landmark approach by demonstrating automated classification and evidence visualization across spinal MRI, supporting the feasibility of scalable and reproducible spine imaging interpretation using learning-based pipelines [7]. In addition, convolutional neural network–based workflows have been successfully applied to automated identification of lumbar disc bulge and herniation, confirming that disc-focused classification is achievable when adequate image quality and expert annotations are available [10].

More recently, deep learning–based lumbar disc herniation detection models have shown that pathological disc classes tend to be identified with higher accuracy than non-pathological discs, supporting the feasibility of automated LDH detection in clinical MRI datasets [11, 12].

A recurring challenge across spinal artificial intelligence studies is limited dataset size and heterogeneity related to scanner vendors, imaging protocols, and patient populations. Systematic reviews consistently emphasize that narrowly defined, clinically focused tasks improve robustness and translational potential when large multi-center datasets are not available [14]. The level-specific strategy adopted in the present study aligns with these recommendations by restricting analysis to the most clinically actionable lumbar segments.

Unlike slice-level classification approaches that provide only probability outputs, object detection models generate both localization and class prediction. This characteristic enhances clinical interpretability by enabling visual verification of whether the predicted abnormality corresponds to the expected disc level and morphology. The YOLO framework is a well-established real-time detection architecture, and YOLOv3 remains a commonly cited baseline model in object detection literature [8].

Object detection–based localization has been highlighted as a key factor for clinical trust and acceptance of AI tools in musculoskeletal and spine imaging, particularly when the system is intended as decision support rather than autonomous diagnosis [15, 16]. Data augmentation strategies, such as controlled rotation, are also widely recommended to mitigate overfitting and improve generalizability in medical imaging tasks with limited data [17].

In the present results, herniated disc classes demonstrated superior performance compared with non-herniated classes, particularly at the L5–S1 level. The moderate mAP and F1-score values suggest that the proposed model is more suitable as a pre-screening or triage tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system. This aligns with current perspectives emphasizing AI as a decision-support system rather than a replacement for radiologists. The observed performance differences between herniated and non-herniated classes may be partially explained by class imbalance and the inherent morphological ambiguity of non-herniated discs. Clinically, this observation is plausible because disc herniation typically manifests with more conspicuous morphological features—such as focal posterior protrusion or extrusion and annular contour disruption—than non-herniated discs, which may still exhibit mild degeneration or bulging. This interpretation is consistent with standardized lumbar disc nomenclature, which recognizes a spectrum of disc pathology rather than a binary normal–abnormal distinction [3]. A direct comparison with radiologist performance was not conducted in this study, which represents an important direction for future research. Furthermore, degenerative imaging features are frequently observed in asymptomatic individuals, complicating the definition of a truly “normal” disc and potentially contributing to reduced performance in non-herniated classes [5].

Confidence-based performance analysis suggests a potential role for the proposed model as a screening or triage tool. Lower confidence thresholds increased recall, thereby minimizing missed herniation cases, whereas higher thresholds improved precision and may be suitable for flagging high-certainty cases during confirmatory review. This sensitivity-oriented deployment strategy aligns with contemporary perspectives on clinical artificial intelligence in radiology, which emphasize augmentation of clinician decision-making rather than replacement of expert judgment [15, 18]. The reported overall performance (precision 0.738, recall 0.698, mAP@0.5 0.744, mAP@0.5–0.95 0.454, best F1 0.69 at a confidence threshold of ~ 0.30) is moderate and corresponds to a non-trivial rate of both false positives and false negatives if deployed unsupervised. These values do not support any role as an autonomous diagnostic tool. At most, and only after external validation in larger cohorts, a model of this type could be considered as a pre-screening or worklist-prioritization aid that surfaces studies likely to contain L4–L5 or L5–S1 herniation for earlier radiologist review, while preserving full radiologist adjudication of the final report. If external validation confirms the present findings, a tool of this type could in principle contribute to three aspects of clinical workflow: (i) supporting consistency of detection at the two most clinically relevant lumbar levels, potentially reducing inter-observer variability in triage decisions; (ii) shortening time-to-first-read for studies containing suspected herniation via worklist prioritization; and (iii) providing a preliminary localized finding to support the radiologist’s workflow. We emphasize that none of these effects was measured in the present study; they are explicitly proposed as endpoints for a future prospective multi-reader multi-case evaluation. and are acknowledged here. First, failure modes are expected to vary by scanner vendor, field strength, and acquisition protocol, and by patient subgroups (age, sex, body habitus) that may be under-represented in single-center training data; subgroup performance was not evaluated here and should be audited in future multi-center work. Second, robustness to adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs (e.g., motion-degraded studies, atypical anatomy, post-surgical cases excluded at training) has not been characterized. Third, clear governance is required around the model’s intended use — specifically, that it is a decision-support / pre-screening aid whose outputs must be adjudicated by a qualified radiologist — together with a defined escalation pathway for discordant cases. Liability and regulatory classification of such a tool fall under national medical-device regulation and must be addressed prior to any clinical deployment.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations must be acknowledged and constrain the interpretation of the present findings.

L1 (Critical) — Insufficient dataset size and split

With 372 total images and a held-out test set of only 10 images, the study lacks the statistical power required to draw robust conclusions about model performance. All reported metrics on the test set should be considered fragile point estimates, and the bootstrap confidence intervals we report are wide. Deep-learning models for medical imaging typically benefit from substantially larger training sets, and test sets on the order of hundreds to thousands of images are more appropriate for reliable performance estimation. To partially mitigate this, we added 5-fold cross-validation on the combined training/validation pool (Table 3), but this does not substitute for a larger independent test set.

L2 (Major) — Lack of external validation

All data originated from a single institution acquired on a limited scanner set with a homogeneous protocol. Generalizability to other scanners, vendors, field strengths, acquisition protocols, and patient populations cannot be inferred from these data. Prospective external validation on at least one independent multi-center cohort is required before any clinical claim.

L3 (Major) — Limited clinical metadata and demographic reporting granularity

Although basic demographic and acquisition metadata have been summarized (Table 1), richer metadata (clinical indication subtypes, symptom duration, severity scales, comorbidities) was not available at the image level, which limits assessment of potential confounders and subgroup generalization.

L4 (Moderate) — Binary classification at two levels, sagittal-only input

The task was restricted to binary herniated/non-herniated classification at L4–L5 and L5–S1 on sagittal T2-weighted slices only. Other lumbar levels (L1–L2 through L3–L4), other common spinal pathologies (circumferential bulge, stenosis, spondylolisthesis), finer morphological categories (protrusion vs. extrusion vs. sequestration), and axial-plane evaluation of lateral recess and foraminal nerve-root compression were outside the scope of this feasibility study. A multi-level, multi-planar, and morphology-aware extension is the natural next step.

L5 (Moderate) — Incomplete annotation protocol

While we have specified the number, credentials, and experience of the annotators, the reference nomenclature used, and the disagreement-resolution procedure (consensus with third-senior adjudication), a formal quantitative inter-rater reliability metric (e.g., Cohen’s κ) was not computed on the full dataset. Annotators were not fully blinded to the original radiology report at the screening stage, which could bias label assignment for borderline cases [19].

L6 (Moderate) — Lack of statistical significance testing and no radiologist comparison

Formal hypothesis testing of between-class or between-threshold performance differences was not performed because n = 10 renders such tests severely underpowered. We also did not perform a head-to-head comparison with radiologists of differing experience, which is the single most important next step for establishing incremental clinical value (Comment R1.10). A multi-reader multi-case (MRMC) study is planned as follow-up.

Future directions

Priorities for subsequent work are, in order: (i) substantial dataset expansion and external multi-center validation; (ii) prospective radiologist comparison with differing experience levels (MRMC); (iii) extension to multi-planar (sagittal + axial) input and finer morphological categories per the NASS/ASSR/ASNR nomenclature; (iv) hybrid detection-plus-segmentation pipelines for morphometric downstream tasks.”

Conclusion

These findings support the technical feasibility of YOLO-based, level-specific detection of lumbar disc herniation on sagittal MRI at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels. However, the very small test set (n = 10), the single-center design, the absence of external validation, and the lack of radiologist benchmarking preclude any conclusion regarding clinical utility at this stage. The present work should be viewed as a proof-of-concept that must be confirmed in larger multi-center cohorts with external validation before any clinical or workflow-support role can be inferred.
