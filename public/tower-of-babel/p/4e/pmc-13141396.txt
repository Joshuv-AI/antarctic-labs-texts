Ultra-deep metagenomic sequencing enables reconstruction of diverse, high quality microbial genomes from human urine samples
Authors: Yadong Liu, Hongli Sun, Xinyu Tan, Kexin Li, Zilong He, Songnian Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) represent a major global health challenge, necessitating the precise identification of causative pathogens for effective diagnosis and treatment. While metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a powerful diagnostic tool, its clinical application has been limited by the lack of high-quality microbial genomes from urine samples.ResultsWe reconstructed 223 reliable quality MAGs spanning bacterial, fungal, mycoplasmal, and viral, including the first demonstration of multiple Escherichia coli subpopulations within individual urine samples. The collection comprised six fungal genomes, ten mycoplasma genomes, and eight completed viral genomes. Comparative analysis showed 72% concordance with culture while detecting additional pathogens in 30% of cases, including fastidious organisms missed by conventional methods.ConclusionsThis study establishes an optimized mNGS framework that overcomes current diagnostic limitations in UTIs through high-depth sequencing and minimal host contamination, enabling unprecedented resolution of the urinary microbiota, including the first identification of intra-sample E. coli subpopulations. The comprehensive MAG collection provides a valuable resource for advancing UTI diagnostics, mechanistic research, and personalized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04826-x.Keywords: Urine, mNGS, Metagenomic binning, High-quality MAGs, Diagnosis

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a common infectious disease caused by infection and invasion of the urinary system by various pathogenic microorganisms (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Candida albicans, etc.) [1]. Severe cases may lead to life-threatening complications, including septic shock. UTI exists globally and affects more than 250 million people every year [2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 (GBD 2019), UTI cases and associated deaths increased by 60.4% and 140.2%, respectively, between 1990 and 2019 [3], highlighting worsening epidemiological trends. A major concern in UTI management is widespread empirical antibiotic therapy, which contributes to rising antimicrobial resistance and increased healthcare costs [4]. Currently, traditional microbial culturing, which remains constrained by low sensitivity and an inability to detect fastidious or unculturable pathogens [5–8], underscores the pressing need for more comprehensive and precise pathogen detection methods in urine.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is an unbiased, high-throughput method capable of comprehensively profiling microbial communities in clinical samples, including rare, fastidious, and co-infecting pathogens that evade traditional methods [9]. Its diagnostic utility has been validated across diverse infections. For example, Ramachandran et al. [10] reported that the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) mNGS data improved diagnostic accuracy by 22% compared to traditional culture, with eight cases identified exclusively by mNGS directly influencing clinical decision-making. Similarly, Huang et al. [11] demonstrated mNGS’s superior sensitivity (88.30% vs 25.73%) for pulmonary infection pathogens. Notably, Jia et al. [12] highlighted mNGS’s advantages in UTIs, detecting 26 species (vs 5 by culture) with 87.9% sensitivity (vs 30.3%, P < 0.001), achieving 100% clinical concordance and guiding effective targeted antibiotic therapy.

Despite its potential, routine mNGS data faces two critical limitations: low sequencing depth and high host DNA contamination. In the study by Gu et al. [13], the data volume of mNGS data after removal of human genomic reads was only in the range of 3.2 Kb to 429.4 Mb. This limitation, compounded by database incompleteness, often results in insufficient genomic coverage and prevents conventional mNGS from achieving the high-quality, complete pathogen genomes necessary for precise diagnostics. To address these challenges, we employed ultra-deep mNGS sequencing coupled with metagenomic binning on clinical urine specimens, and successfully reconstructed high-quality metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) spanning bacteria, fungi, viruses, and mycoplasmas. Our aim is to overcome the sensitivity and specificity challenges of conventional mNGS by generating a wealth of high-quality microbial MAGs, thereby enabling more precise and comprehensive detection of urinary tract pathogens. This endeavor seeks to provide novel approaches and insights for improving the diagnosis of UTIs.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction and metagenome sequencing

A total of 67 urine samples were collected from 67 patients (aged 24–92 years) at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between June and October 2022 (Table S1). All samples were collected in accordance with standardized aseptic operating procedures. Immediately after collection, samples underwent routine clinical microbiological analysis following guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Briefly, 10μL of urine were inoculated onto blood agar plates (Bio Mérieux, France) and incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 16–24 h. Once microbes have grown, we continued to use MALDI-TOF MS (Bio Mérieux, France) to identify them and determine the final results of the urine culture. Residual samples remaining after routine testing were packaged into 1.5 mL sterile centrifuge tubes, stored at –80 °C, and transferred to the sequencing laboratory within 24 h. This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration and the Rules of Good Clinical Practice. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital in accordance with the ethical review of biomedical research involving human beings (I-23PJ1724), and the samples were taken from informed and consenting individuals. Data on the demographic characteristics and clinical laboratory findings of all patients were extracted from the patients’ medical records.

The urine specimens were immediately aliquoted into sterile 1.5 mL tubes and stored at −80 °C to maintain nucleic acid integrity until processing. DNA was extracted by the TIANMicrobe Magnetic Patho-DNA/RNA Kit (NG550) (TIANGEN BIOTECH Co., Ltd., China), a pathogen-focused nucleic acid extraction system designed to significantly reduce host background and enrich microbial DNA. Libraries were prepared with the MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Prep Set (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., China). The concentration and fragment size were assessed by the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, UK) and Qsep100 DNA Analyzer (BiOptic Inc., China), respectively. Only those with a total yield > 5 ng passed quality control. Pooled libraries were sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., China) (PE 2*150 bp) at the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Data preprocessing, taxonomic profiling and genome assembly

Quality filtering of the raw paired-end reads was performed via Trim-Galore (v0.6.10) [14] (parameters: –quality 20, –stringency 10, and -length 75). Human-derived reads were removed by mapping to the GRCh38 reference genome using Minimap2 (v2.26-r1175) [15] with default parameters, followed by extraction of unmapped read pairs (SAMtools v1.6 [16], –require-flags 12). Taxonomic classification of the sequencing reads was performed using Kraken2 (v2.1.2) [17] and Bracken (v2.8) [18] with a custom Kraken2 database which included RefSeq complete genomes/proteins (bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses, downloaded 2023–02–23). Clean reads were assembled using the “metaSPAdes” pipeline of SPAdes (v3.15.5) [19] (k-mer sizes: 21, 45, 69, 93, and 117). Assembly was assessed via QUAST (v5.2.0) [20], retaining only scaffolds exceeding 1,000 bp in length for further analysis.

Metagenomic binning, plasmid prediction and genome annotation

Metagenomic binning was performed using MetaWRAP pipeline (v1.3.2) [21] with three tools: CONCOCT (v1.0.0) [22], Maxbin2 (v2.2.6) [23] and MetaBAT2 (v2.12.1) [24]. To maximize microbial recovery in this low-biomass environment, we retained all non-redundant bins (removing only bins with completely same sequences). Taxonomic classification of all bins was assigned using Kraken2 (v2.1.2). Plasmid sequences (≥ 5 Kb) were predicted from all bacterial bins using Plasmer (v0.1 20,220,816) [25]. Complete circular plasmids were verified by terminal sequence overlaps. Antibiotic resistance genes were identified using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database-Resistance Gene Identifier (CARD-RGI, v6.0.3) [26] with plasmid-specific parameters (–data plasmid, –alignment_tool BLAST) and Strict significance based on CARD curated bitscore cutoffs. Bacterial MAGs (bMAGs) and mycoplasma MAGs (mycMAGs) were evaluated using CheckM v1.0.12 [27] and BUSCO (v5.5.0) [28]. Only medium-quality MAGs (CheckM completeness > 70%, CheckM contamination < 5%, CheckM score (completeness-5 × contamination × 100) > 60 and BUSCO completeness > 50%), which exceed the MIMAG criteria for medium-quality genomes [29], were retained for further analysis. Fungal MAGs (fMAGs) were assessed with EukCC (v2.1.0) [30] and BUSCO, and those meeting quality thresholds (EukCC completeness > 60%, contamination < 5%, score (completeness-5 × contamination × 100) > 50; BUSCO completeness > 60%) were retained as reliable quality genomes. High quality viral MAGs (vMAGs) were validated using QUAST and online BLAST. All retained MAGs were annotated via Prokka (v1.14.6; bMAGs/mycMAGs/vMAGs) [31] and Funannotate (v1.8.11; fMAGs) [32].

Identification of antibiotic resistance genes, virulence factors and subpopulations in bMAGs

Antibiotic resistance genes in medium-quality bMAGs were predicted using CARD-RGI. Virulence factors were identified by aligning protein sequences against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [33] using DIAMOND (v2.1.11.165) [34] with stringent parameters (–id 90, –evalue 1e-6, –subject-cover 90, –query-cover 90). For strain-level resolution of ultra-deep metagenomic sequencing, we selected bMAGs assigned to the same species from each sample, ensuring high completeness and low contamination (CheckM completeness > 90%, contamination < 1%, score > 90; BUSCO completeness > 90%). The core genes of conspecific bMAGs were identified using Roary (v3.13.0) [35] and aligned with the “-e” parameter, which invokes PRANK (v170427) [36]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed from core gene alignments using FastTree (v2.1.11) [37]. Strain typing on conspecific bMAGs was performed through core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) using chewBBACA (v3.3.10) [38] with minimum spanning trees generated using GrapeTree (v1.5.0) [39] based on cgMLST allelic profiles.

High-quality genome analysis of fungi, viruses and mycoplasma

To improve the assembly continuity of the highest-quality fMAG, we performed BLAST alignment against the Candida albicans SC5314 reference genome (GCF_000182965.3, https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/000/182/965/GCF_000182965.3_ASM18296v3/) using stringent criteria (≥ 90% identity, E-value ≤ 1e-10). Scaffolds were manually ordered and linked based on syntenic regions meeting these criteria, resulting in a pseudochromosome-level assembly. Collinearity analysis between the pseudochromosome assembly and SC5314 using Mauve (v20150226) [40] and TBtools (v2.001) [41]. High‑quality mycMAGs and vMAGs were further polished through manual curation: viral contigs were validated via BLAST against NCBI nt/nr and self‑BLAST to remove terminal redundancies after circularization, while mycoplasma scaffolds were aligned to reference genomes, manually joined, and visually corrected.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Clean data volumes were quantified using SI-standard: (i) uncompressed FASTQ file sizes in gigabytes (GB = 109 bytes), and (ii) sequence yields in gigabases (Gb = 109 bases). Species abundance rarefaction analysis was performed using the vegan package (v2.6–4) [42] in R (v4.2.2), excluding low-abundance species (≤ 0.1% relative abundance) to minimize potential contaminants and sequencing artifacts [43]. Species composition was visualized with “ggplot2” (v3.4.1) [44]. All circular maps were generated by the Proksee web server (https://proksee.ca/) [45]. Comparative genomic analyses included collinearity plots constructed with Mauve and TBtools. All heatmaps were constructed using TBtools with hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance and the complete linkage method.

Results

Clinical sample collection and basic analysis of metagenomic data

We analyzed 67 urine samples (54 females and 13 males) including 27 confirmed urinary tract infection (UTI) cases and 40 cases without specific UTI diagnosis (Table S1). After host‑DNA removal and quality control, we obtained 533 Gb of high‑quality microbial data from an initial sequencing output of 812 Gb (range 3.37–22.42 Gb per sample; 63 samples > 5 Gb). The corresponding read counts decreased from approximately 5.41 billion raw reads to 3.57 billion microbial reads post‑processing, with 64 samples yielding > 1 Gb of microbial data (maximum 18.65 Gb) (Table S2). Over 70% of samples (n = 48) showed < 50% host contamination, including 15 samples with < 1% contamination. Species rarefaction curves with clear saturation trend demonstrated adequate sequencing depth (Figure S1), with > 99% classified rate in 43 samples (Table S2). We identified 326 species spanning 13 phyla in 67 samples (Fig. 1). Among them, Escherichia coli dominated 29 samples (> 90% reads), while Enterococcus faecalis (> 95% in U33/U35) and Candida albicans (> 95% in U64) were other prevalent opportunistic pathogens. Metagenomic assembly yielded 303,873 scaffolds (range: 69–28,607/sample). The average size of the assemblies was 22.76 Mb (range: 3.16–105.38 Mb). GC content varied from 37.47% to 65.40%, with N50 values ranging from 2,146 bp to 690,246 bp (Table S3).

Metagenomic binning of urine samples

Single-sample binning, taxonomy classification and gene prediction

Metagenomic binning yielded a total of 4,323 bins (average 65 bins/sample) (Table S4). The final bin set spanned a broad size range (1 Kb–33.73 Mb) with scaffold counts varying from 1 to 17,916. Taxonomic classification, performed on bins in which ≥ 90% of sequences aligned uniquely to a single species reference genome, revealed 236 species across 17 phyla. This inclusive approach applied no quality cutoffs during initial classification to maximize sensitivity for capturing the full spectrum of urinary microbiota.

From this dataset, we recovered 223 reliable quality MAGs for downstream analysis (Table S5). A subset of 19 high‑quality MAGs, selected for detailed examination in subsequent sections, are highlighted in Table 1 to show their genomic features and quality. They represented diverse taxonomic groups (47 species across 35 genera), comprising 199 bacterial, 10 mycoplasma, 6 fungal, and 8 viral genomes. Quality thresholds were strictly maintained: prokaryotic MAGs met CheckM completeness > 70%, contamination < 5%, score > 60 and BUSCO completeness > 50%, while eukaryotic MAGs met EukCC completeness > 60%, contamination < 5%, score > 50 and BUSCO completeness > 60%. Gene prediction analysis identified 712,669 coding sequences (CDSs) across the 223 reliable quality MAGs. Among the bMAGs, 671,953 CDSs were predicted, with an average of 3,377 CDS per MAG. Among the fMAGs, 30,029 CDSs were predicted, with an average of 5,005 CDSs per MAG. Among the mycMAGs, 10,603 CDSs were predicted, with an average of 1,060 CDSs per MAG. 84 CDSs were predicted in the vMAGs. One viral MAG carried 49 CDSs, while the remaining MAGs carried only 5 CDSs each.

Comparative analysis with culture results

Using traditional urine culture results (Table S1) as a reference, we performed comparison with the curated set of 223 reliable quality MAGs. In culture‑positive samples, at least one high‑quality MAG matched the cultured organism at the species level in 48 of 63 samples (76.2%). Especially, 41.7% of concordant cases (20/48) identified additional pathogenic MAGs not detected by culture, suggesting potential polymicrobial infections. Notably, all four culture-negative samples (U2, U4, U9 and U10) yielded high-quality pathogenic MAGs, including fastidious mycoplasmas. Nine samples (U40, U52, U76, U80, U89, U94, U96, U97 and U98) showed discordance between culture organism and high‑quality MAGs. Among these, the cultured species remained dominant in the read‑based abundance profile (Fig. 1, Table S2) in U80 and U96. In U76, the high‑quality Candida orthopsilosis MAG is phylogenetically closely related to the cultured Candida parapsilosis, a pair that is often misidentified in routine culture. For the remaining discordant samples, the high‑quality MAGs represent organisms not detected by culture. Critically, we detected multiple antibiotic-resistant ESKAPE [46] pathogens and other high-risk species missed by culture: the intrinsically multidrug-resistant Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (U4, U10, U30), anaerobic sepsis-associated Bacteroides fragilis (U20, U40), carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae (U56, U89 and U91), and opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa (U29, U30 and U60).

Identification of antibiotic-resistant plasmid sequences

A total of 2,038 plasmid sequences (5,001–146,780 bp) were identified across all bacterial bins (Table S6). Among these, 177 plasmids carried antibiotic resistance genes (Figure S2), including four complete closed circular plasmid sequences (Figure S3). The plasmid-borne antibiotic resistance genes were primarily associated with penems, aminoglycosides, and cephalosporins, with resistance mechanisms involving antimicrobial inactivation (e.g., CMY−2 carbapenemase, CTX-M-195/197 ESBLs), efflux pumps (e.g., oqxA/B, mef (B)), and target modification (e.g., vanA cluster genes, rmtB 16S methylase). Notably, high-risk antibiotic resistance genes associated with last-resort antibiotics were detected, including the vanA-mediated vancomycin resistance (vanR/S/H/X/Y/Z), plasmid-encoded mcr-1 variants (MCR−1.4/1.8/1.11) for colistin resistance, and the bifunctional aminoglycoside resistance gene AAC(6')-Ie-APH(2'')-Ia.

Genomic features of bacterial MAGs

Analysis of 199 medium-quality bMAGs revealed substantial genomic diversity (Table S5). These bMAGs contained 30,975 scaffolds in total, with individual MAGs averaging 156 scaffolds (range: 3–1,523 scaffolds). Notably, more than half of the bMAGs (n = 114) contained < 100 scaffolds. The bMAGs exhibited wide variation in genome size (1.14–6.50 Mb) and GC content (28.29%–73.24%). Taxonomically, Escherichia coli (80 bMAGs, 40.2%) dominated the collection, followed by Enterococcus faecalis (19, 9.5%) and Porphyromonas asaccharolytica (11, 5.5%), reflecting clinically relevant urinary pathogens.

Antibiotic resistance genes and virulence factor prediction of bMAGs

A total of 5,695 antibiotic resistance genes covering 265 gene types were identified from 199 medium-quality bMAGs, with 206 genes located on plasmids (Figure S4). Notably, 467 of these resistance genes harbored SNPs relative to their best-matched ARO sequences. The maximum number of resistance genes carried by individual bMAG was 72. These genes primarily mediated resistance through antibiotic efflux (4,442; 78.0%) and target alteration (1,341; 23.5%) mechanisms. The antibiotic drug categories mainly associated with these genes were fluoroquinolone antibiotics (2,556; 44.9%). Parallel virulence factor analysis revealed 19,937 virulence genes spanning 773 types (Figure S5). The maximum number of genes predicted in individual bMAG was 340. Based on VFDB classification, these virulence factors were predominantly concentrated in five functional categories: nutritional/metabolic factors (7,255; 23.9%), adherence (5,797; 19.1%), motility (5,404; 17.8%), effector delivery systems (4,730; 15.6%) and immune modulation (3,447; 11.4%), collectively accounting for 88% of all virulence factors.

Potential strain-level heterogeneity in high-quality bMAGs

Applying stringent quality thresholds (CheckM completeness > 90%, contamination < 1%, score > 90; BUSCO completeness > 90%), we obtained 72 high-quality bMAGs from 28 samples, with at least two conspecific bMAGs per sample (Table S7). These bMAGs represented eight species, predominantly Escherichia coli (n = 50), along with urinary tract pathogens (Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Proteus mirabilis), opportunistic pathogens (Enterococcus faecalis, Citrobacter koseri, and Morganella morganii), and commensal species (Lactobacillus crispatus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii).

Phylogenetic analysis based on core genes was performed separately for bMAGs of different species. The results (Fig. 2A) revealed that Escherichia coli bMAGs from samples U43 and U44 formed a distinct clade separate from other samples, indicating close relatedness. Notably, in this clade, U43.concoct.10 clustered with U43.metabat2.2, while U44.concoct.9 clustered with U44.metabat2.1. However, U43.maxbin2.0 and U44.maxbin2.0 each formed independent branches with 100% bootstrap support, suggesting potential divergence in their core genomes compared to other conspecific bMAGs within their respective samples. cgMLST analysis using a 7,601 loci wgMLST scheme (from Chewie-NS [47]; https://chewbbaca.online/species/5) with 95% loci presence threshold (validated via ExtractCgMLST module) yielded a 2,713 loci scheme. Minimum spanning tree (Fig. 2B) based on cgMLST allelic profiles further demonstrated that U44 Escherichia coli bMAGs did not form monophyletic clusters, suggesting potential strain-level variation. U44.concoct.9 clustered with sample U53's bMAGs, whereas U44.maxbin2.0 grouped with U43's bMAGs.

Functional analyses revealed marked heterogeneity in antimicrobial resistance (Fig. 2C) and virulence factor distributions (Fig. 2D). Regarding antibiotic resistance patterns, U43.maxbin2.0 exhibited a unique profile distinct from other bMAGs, while U43.concoct.10 and U44.maxbin2.0 shared similar patterns. Notably, U43.metabat2.2 and U44.metabat2.1 possessed identical resistance gene repertoires. Virulence factors composition showed that U44.maxbin2.0's virulence profile closely resembled those of U43.concoct.10 and U43.maxbin2.0, whereas U43.metabat2.2 aligned more closely with U44.concoct.9 and U44.metabat2.1 in terms of virulence factor composition.

These findings demonstrate substantial phylogenetic and functional divergence among the six high-quality Escherichia coli bMAGs from U43/U44, particularly through strain-specific antibiotic resistance and virulence determinants, suggesting coexisting genomic lineages within individual samples.

Bioinformatic analysis of fungal MAGs

We further investigated six reliable quality fMAGs from three samples (Table S5). These fMAGs comprised 6,067 scaffolds, with individual MAGs averaging 1,011 scaffolds. The four Candida albicans MAGs averaged 11.47 Mb in genome size (average GC content: 33.68%), with two showing exceptional completeness (EukCC completeness > 95%). The other two Candida orthopsilosis MAGs were comparatively smaller (average 8.58 Mb) with higher GC content (average 37.33%).

We selected “U64_concoct.37” with the highest assembly quality (Candida albicans; EukCC completeness = 97.1%, contamination = 0.41%, score = 95.05; BUSCO completeness = 91.7%) for further analysis. This 12.98 Mb MAG (GC content: 33.59%) contains 565 scaffolds (N50 = 41,929 bp), with the largest scaffold reaching 297,546 bp. Its pseudochromosome assembly contains eight sequences and 5,866 predicted CDSs. Comparative analysis with the C. albicans reference genome (SC5314) revealed remarkable genomic conservation and minimal differences (Table 2). The collinearity circle (Fig. 3) confirmed a strong collinearity relationship between the corresponding chromosomes of the two genomes, while block alignment (Figure S6) also showed essentially consistent gene order and orientation across all eight chromosomes. These results establish their high similarity and consistency.

Genomics analysis of mycoplasma and viral MAGs

Mycoplasma pseudochromosome-level assemblies

Ten high-quality mycMAGs from six samples were classified into two clinically relevant species: Metamycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma parvum (Table S5). These MAGs spanned 533 to 730 Kb with characteristic average genome size and GC content (M. hominis: 636 Kb, 26.88%; U. parvum: 620 Kb, 25.64%).

Three MAGs reached pseudochromosome-level quality (Fig. 4), including a near-complete U. parvum assembly (U94.maxbin2.1: CheckM completeness = 99.05%, contamination = 0.14%, score = 98.35; BUSCO completeness = 98.3%) and two near-complete M. hominis assemblies (U94.metabat2.13 and U10.metabat2.11: CheckM/BUSCO completeness = 100%, contamination = 0.38%). The pseudochromosome assemblies maintained species-specific genomic features. The M. hominis assemblies averaged 672 Kb (1,141 CDSs) with 26.78% GC content, while the single U. parvum assembly measured 731 Kb (1,243 CDSs) with 25.30% GC content.

Discussion

UTIs represent a significant global health burden, affecting approximately 250 million individuals annually worldwide [2]. Traditional diagnostic approaches, particularly culture-based methods, remain hampered by several critical limitations. As the current gold standard, pathogenic culture demonstrates low positivity rates, frequently failing to detect polymicrobial infections and fastidious or low-abundance pathogens [12, 48]. This inaccurate and incomplete detection of pathogens often resulting in prolonged diagnostic odysseys and empirical treatment failures [49]. Furthermore, while antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) through minimal inhibitory concentration determination provides essential resistance profiles, it cannot reliably predict emerging resistance phenotypes or detect cryptic resistance mechanisms. These diagnostic shortcomings underscore the urgent need for more comprehensive pathogen detection methodologies to guide precise therapeutic interventions [1].

In recent years, mNGS has emerged as a transformative tool in pathogen detection through its unbiased and high-throughput sequencing approach [50]. It can achieve accurate quantitative or semi-quantitative determination of pathogen concentrations [51]. Consistent with previous findings (Fu et al.) [48], which reported that mNGS identified 24 additional pathogens and increased clinical diagnostic rates by 73.0% compared to traditional methods, our study similarly demonstrates the sensitivity of mNGS in detecting urinary pathogens. Our comparative analysis revealed 71.6% concordance between high-quality MAG taxonomy and culture results, with mNGS detecting potential supplementary pathogens in 41.7% of concordant cases, indicating frequent polymicrobial infections overlooked by standard diagnostics. Noteworthy was the consistent detection of high-risk, often multidrug-resistant pathogens that evaded culture-based methods – including Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Bacteroides fragilis, ESKAPE pathogens (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa) – across multiple samples. These clinically significant omissions from conventional diagnostics underscore a critical limitation of culture-dependent approaches, especially for fastidious organisms and those requiring specialized growth conditions. Most notably, mNGS successfully recovered pathogenic mycoplasma MAGs from all the culture-negative samples, overcoming a major diagnostic blind spot for fastidious organisms. The observed discrepancies between methods provided valuable insights: the cases where read-based analysis confirmed cultured organisms as dominant taxa despite MAG discordance underscore current limitations in genome recovery from complex microbial communities, particularly when dealing with closely related strains. Conversely, the differentiation of phylogenetically proximate species (e.g., C. parapsilosis vs. C. orthopsilosis) demonstrates mNGS's potential to rectify taxonomic misassignments inherent to phenotypic identification methods. These findings reveal new dimensions of diagnostic refinement. Beyond taxonomic identification, our MAG collection enabled comprehensive genomic characterization of resistance determinants and virulence factors, substantially expanding the clinical utility of mNGS data. This molecular profiling capability, combined with unbiased detection, positions mNGS as a powerful tool for both diagnostic and mechanistic studies of UTIs. Although standardization challenges persist, our optimized protocols demonstrate that mNGS can overcome traditional urobiome analysis limitations while maintaining diagnostic concordance for dominant pathogens. The reconstructed MAGs provide unprecedented insights into the urinary microbiota, particularly regarding antibiotic-resistant strains that evade conventional detection, offering a more complete understanding of urinary microbial communities and their clinical implications.

While mNGS has been widely applied to fecal, blood, and CSF samples [52], studies of the urinary microbiome remain limited [53]. Due to imperfect extraction and sequencing technologies, human-derived data (excluding feces) often contain significant host contamination, with typically less than 1% of data being non-human [54]. Therefore, the detection sensitivity of mNGS largely depends on the sample type, which further limits its clinical application. The urobiome typically contains minimal biomass, making it highly susceptible to contaminants during the sampling and sequencing process [55]. Compared to the conventional level (typically > 99% [54]), the host contamination rate of our urine samples was significantly reduced (mean host rate = 32%, host rate of 15 samples < 1%). Additionally, the clean mNGS data for each of the 43 urine samples exceeded 5 Gb. These technical advances provide a framework for high-resolution characterization of individual urinary microbiota, moving beyond population-level profiling to enable strain-specific analysis of uropathogens.

For bacteria, multiple-strain infections have been known to reach considerable frequencies (6.1%–52.9%) for a variety of human pathogens [56]. Strain-level awareness is imperative for accurate clinical management, given that traits like antibiotic resistance and virulence potential can vary substantially between strains of the same species [56]. However, conventional mNGS-based bacterial identification remains limited to genus or species-level resolution, failing to capture strain-level heterogeneity. This limitation stems from two key constraints: moderate sequencing depth (clean FASTQ size range: 1–1.5 GB) obscures low-abundance strain signals, and excessive host DNA contamination (> 99%) generating prohibitive background noise. By integrating ultra-deep sequencing with optimized experimental protocols, we demonstrated the detection of potential Escherichia coli subpopulations within individual urine samples. Phylogenetic reconstruction, strain-specific markers, and functional gene distribution analyses provided concordant evidence of strain-level variation. Our results demonstrate that ultra-deep sequencing with effective host depletion enables strain-level resolution in clinical mNGS. For fungal pathogens, we achieved unprecedented genomic resolution, reconstructing six high-quality fungal MAGs – a notable advancement given the historical challenges posed by large fungal genomes and low urinary abundances. The pseudochromosome-level assembly of the best Candida albicans fMAG showing strong collinearity with the reference SC5314 strain demonstrates the method's capacity for high-fidelity fungal genome recovery. Under specific conditions, viruses and mycoplasmas have the potential to induce severe UTIs [57, 58]. Our successful reconstruction of eight complete viral genomes and three near-complete mycoplasma draft genomes overcomes previous limitations in detecting these fastidious urinary pathogens [59]. This demonstrates that optimized sequencing depth and analytical pipelines can also improve the detection and identification of viral and mycoplasma genomes.

As a retrospective study using clinically collected samples, we lacked dedicated negative controls to systematically assess potential contamination from environmental or laboratory sources. To minimize the negative impact of this issue as much as possible, we implemented abundance‑based filtering to remove low‑abundance signals likely to represent background contamination. Additionally, the reliance on short-read sequencing limits comprehensive characterization of complex genomic regions. Nevertheless, the short-read mNGS and metagenomic binning strategies employed in this study demonstrate strong clinical practicality. Future studies coordinating these methods with parallel bacterial isolation, increased sequencing depth, and optimized experimental conditions could further enhance diagnostic services while enabling applications like bacterial subpopulation analysis.
