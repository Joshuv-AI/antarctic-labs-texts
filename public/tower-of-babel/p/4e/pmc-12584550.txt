Unveiling splicing disruptions due to common somatic variants in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Mireya Morote-Faubel, María Guaita-Céspedes, Beatriz Fernández-Blanco, Cristina Martínez-Valiente, Mariam Ibáñez, Marta Santiago-Balsera, Esperanza Such, Javier de la Rubia, Alessandro Liquori, José Cervera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) is a genetically and clonally heterogeneous blood malignancy characterized by somatic gene mutations influencing disease biology. Despite advances in genomic analyses, splicing alterations associated with AML mutations remain underexplored. In this study, we systematically analyzed 914 single nucleotide variants previously found in 1540 AML patients and integrated DNA and RNA sequencing data from an additional 639 cases obtained from the BeatAML and TCGA-LAML cohorts. We identified 125 somatic variants in 32 genes with predicted splicing impact, including traditionally categorized missense/nonsense mutations. Among the 36 variants tested experimentally, 17 were validated through RNA-seq and/or minigene assays, with the latter providing support when RNA-seq evidence was limited. Validated splicing-associated variants (SAVs) frequently induced exon skipping or cryptic splice site activation in AML-associated genes such as EZH2, FLT3, ASXL1, IDH1, and U2AF1. In several cases, these splicing changes altered conserved protein domains or were predicted to trigger nonsense-mediated decay. Our findings reveal a previously underappreciated contribution of cis-acting SAVs to AML pathogenesis, highlighting the need for integrative prediction–validation strategies to uncover their functional and potential therapeutic relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00842-6.

Background

Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) is a malignant disorder of hematopoietic stem cells characterized by the clonal expansion of undifferentiated myeloid lineage blasts. It constitutes a genetically and clonally heterogeneous blood cancer, involving recurrent somatic mutations and complex patterns of clonal evolution. These mutations influence disease biology and phenotype, therapy response, and relapse risk [1]. Recently, the widespread adoption of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has advanced our understanding of AML genetics, enabling risk stratification through combined cytogenetic and genomic analysis [2, 3]. Despite an average of only five recurrent mutations per AML exome, at least one mutation is commonly categorized as a driver in 96% of patients, informing the classification of AML subtypes [2–4]. The current AML molecular landscape identifies patterns of comutations in 32 genes, classifying patients into 16 clinically and biologically distinct subgroups [3]. However, 8% of patients still lack detectable driver mutations or class-defining lesions [3]. Therefore, exploring alternative gene disruption or activation mechanisms beyond conventional perspectives is necessary. For instance, splicing alterations observed in commonly mutated AML genes, such as EZH2, ZRSR2, and DHX34, can mimic genetic mutations [5]. While the mechanisms behind these later events are often unknown, mis-splicing is frequently linked to mutations in genes encoding splicing factors that control multiple targets. Yet, mutations in these genes (e.g., U2AF1 and ZRSR2) account for only ~ 10% of variants in de novo AML [2, 3], suggesting the need to explore further mechanisms, including cis-regulatory effects.

To date, AML molecular studies have primarily examined the direct impact of somatic variants on protein function, particularly missense changes altering amino acid sequences and truncating variants (e.g., nonsense, canonical splice site, frameshift indels) [2–4]. While pan-cancer studies reveal that certain somatic variants influence protein function by altering splicing, this type of alteration is often inaccurately annotated by common NGS analysis pipelines [6]. These mutations, referred to as splicing-associated variants (SAVs), lead to splice-site disruption/creation, intron retention, and exon-skipping events [7–13], potentially introducing unexplored functional implications for AML pathobiology.

Here, we systematically assessed the potential splicing-related functional impact of 914 single nucleotide variants (SNVs), all previously classified as oncogenic or possibly oncogenic, using targeted DNA sequencing data from 1540 AML patients enrolled in multi-center clinical trials of the German-Austrian AML Study Group [2]. Additionally, we integrated DNA and RNA sequencing data with in silico predictions across 639 patients from the BeatAML and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets, identifying shared AML-related mutations inducing mis-splicing in these three cohorts [4, 14]. In addition, we utilized combined in silico and in vitro validation to evaluate rare somatic variants within the same cohorts. Through this approach, we comprehensively uncovered a set of 125 AML-related somatic variants with the potential to disrupt splicing, and experimentally confirmed the effect in 17 out of 36 randomly tested variants. As a result, this study reshapes the molecular profile of frequently mutated genes associated with leukemia.

Methods

Dataset description

This study analyzed SNVs from a cohort of 1,540 AML patients enrolled in clinical trials conducted by the German-Austrian AML Study Group, focusing on the coding regions of 111 genes [2]. Out of 3,847 recurrent driver mutations identified, we retained 914 unique SNVs (Suppl Table S1), previously categorized as oncogenic (n = 701) or possibly oncogenic (n = 213) based on literature reports, clustering with somatic variants, functional locus location, or identification of truncating variants in loss-of-function-sensitive genes [2]. Variants were annotated with Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) and classified as missense (n = 625), nonsense (n = 231), or splice site (n = 55) mutations, with smaller categories for synonymous (n = 2) and start loss (n = 1) mutations (Fig. 1).

Workflow designed for filtering, prioritization, and validation of candidate splicing-altering variants

Splicing predictions

We evaluated the potential impact on splicing for all annotated SNVs using MaxEntScan and SpliceAI prediction tools [15–17] via the MobiDetails platform [18]. Variants were flagged as splice-associated if they met specific thresholds: MaxEntScan scores above 3 with a 10% score change between wild-type and mutant sequences [16, 19], and SpliceAI scores above 0.2 [15]. Predictions were classified into low, moderate, and high impact based on established thresholds [15, 16].

Extended genomic and transcriptomic datasets

Genomic and transcriptomic data were retrieved from TCGA-LAML (150 AML patients, phs000178.v11.p8) and BeatAML (489 AML patients, phs001657.v1.p1), accessible from the Genomic Data Commons repository v32.0 (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [4, 14]. We analyzed a total of 535 exomes using variant calling files (VCFs) from workflows MuTect2, VarScan2, SomaticSniper, and MuSE. Additionally, 607 matched RNA-Seq files provided transcriptomic data, where variants were called using RNAmut [20] for a virtual panel of 30 genes (Suppl Table S2), aiding functional validation.

Functional validation by RNA-seq data

Coordinates of potential SAVs were converted from the GRCh37 (hg19) to the GRCh38 (hg38) genome, and STAR-counts RNA-Seq files provided read counts for splice junctions nearest to each variant. For each splicing event, we calculated the percent splice-in (PSI) as the ratio of canonical junction read counts to the sum of alternative and canonical counts [21]. DeltaPSI (ΔPSI) values were derived as the difference between the PSI for alternative events and the mean PSI of non-mutated AML samples. Sashimi plots were generated using RNA-Seq data from AML patients with and without the variants of interest, supported by manual review in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).

Statistical analysis

We assessed the significance of ΔPSI comparisons between mutated and non-mutated groups. The choice of statistical analysis depends on the number of mutated samples available. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used when comparing more than two groups (i.e., IDH1), Mann–Whitney U tests for two-group comparisons when the data were non-normally distributed (i.e., NRAS, TP53 p.Y220C), and Welch’s t-tests for two-group comparisons when the data approximated normality and had unequal variances (i.e., U2AF1). For variants presented in a single mutated sample, ΔPSI values were analyzed via the Empirical Cumulative Distribution Function (ECDF), which assesses whether a single observation lies within the extreme tail of the ΔPSI distribution from wild-type samples (Suppl Fig. 1). For the comparative analysis between predicted SAVs and non-SAV mutations across biological and functional features extracted from VEP, continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test, given the non-normal distributions observed.

Functional validation by splicing reporter minigenes

When a variant with a likely impact on splicing was not identified in the extended AML cohorts, we proceeded to assess its functional effect using splicing reporter minigenes. Briefly, we amplified a DNA fragment of approximately 500 base pairs (bp) from the genomic region surrounding the variant. This amplification was carried out using the High-Fidelity Phusion polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) sourced from a healthy donor. The amplified DNA was then cloned into the pSPL3 exon-trapping vector. Following this, site-directed mutagenesis was conducted using the QuickChange® Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies) to introduce the specific change of interest. HEK293 cells, plated in six-well dishes, were transfected, and after a 24-hour incubation period, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and subsequently reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara). The resulting cDNA was amplified using the pSPL3-specific SD6 forward and SA2 reverse primers. The amplified PCR products were visualized using the QIAxcel ScreenGel Software (Qiagen) and were further subjected to Sanger sequencing using the ABIPRISM 3130 DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). All the experiments were performed in triplicate.

Protein predictions

Validated SAVs were analyzed using ExPASy (https://web.expasy.org/translate/) for translated protein sequences and the NCBI Conserved Domains Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi?) for functional domain alterations. NMDEscPredictor (https://nmdprediction.shinyapps.io/nmdescpredictor/) assessed transcript degradation potential via the Nonsense-mediated Decay (NMD) pathway.

Further methodological details and the code repository are available in the Supplementary section and at https://github.com/JVCervera/SplicingVariantsAML.

Results

Identification of somatic point variants with a likely effect on splicing

A total of 914 high-quality somatic point variants were gathered from 1540 AML patients [2]. Integrating predictions from MaxEntScan and SpliceAI tools [15, 17], we identified 125 variants (13.7%) potentially altering splicing, including 61 oncogenic and 64 likely oncogenic changes (Figs. 1 and 2, and Suppl Table S1). Among these, predictors concurred on a probable cis-acting effect in 75 (60.5%) cases, with MaxEntScan and SpliceAI independently predicting an additional 32 and 18 variants, respectively (Suppl Fig. 2).

Focusing on variants in canonical (± 1, ± 2) and + 5 donor splice positions, 54 variants across 14 genes were found, with TET2, RAD21, PHF6, STAG2, and DNMT3A carrying more than 5 variants each (Fig. 2). Within this set of variants, encompassing 86 predicted events, acceptor loss (n = 28) and donor loss (n = 26) were the most prevalent, followed by acceptor gain (n = 24) and donor gain (n = 8).

Furthermore, potential splicing effects were identified in 71 variants, classified as missense (n = 48) or nonsense (n = 23) (Fig. 2). Notably, this group included well-known variants commonly associated with AML, such as IDH1 p.R132C/S and U2AF1 p.Q157R, distributed across 26 genes. Among these, DNMT3A (n = 13), TET2 (n = 12), and KMT2D (n = 5) were the most impacted genes. These alterations have the potential to induce various aberrant splicing events, including donor gain (n = 43), donor loss (n = 15), acceptor gain (n = 8), and acceptor loss (n = 3).

Comparative analysis of predicted SAVs versus non-SAV mutations

To further characterize the biological and clinical relevance of the predicted SAVs, we compared their Variant Effect Predictor features with those of the remaining non-SAV mutations in the 914-variant dataset. SAVs were significantly more distal in the transcript than non-SAVs, with higher median positions in both cDNA (p = 0.011) and protein coordinates (p = 0.015), suggesting a greater likelihood of impacting functional domains. Consistently, SAVs showed higher CADD_PHRED scores (p = 0.001), but no significant differences in REVEL (p = 0.59) or BLOSUM62 (p = 0.28). In categorical comparisons, SAVs and non-SAVs differed markedly in impact levels (p = 4.8 × 10− 9), with a higher proportion of high-impact variants in SAVs (52.8%) compared to non-SAVs (26.5%), the latter being predominantly of moderate impact. At the amino acid change level, SAVs more frequently harbored truncating substitutions such as Q/* (12.9% vs. 9%) and R/* (8.6% vs. 6.6%), whereas non-SAVs more often involved substitutions like R/H (2.6% vs. 0%) and C/Y (2.8% vs. 0%). SAVs also displayed significantly different exon-level distributions (p = 0.0075), including enriched representation in exons such as TET2 exons 10 and 11 (8.6% vs. 3.3%), while non-SAVs more often affected TET2 exons 3 (4% vs. 0%), and CBL exon 8 (2.2% vs. 0%).

Identification of recurrent AML-associated mutations leading to aberrant splicing through RNA sequencing

To assess the impact of the identified 125 potential SAVs on splicing, we analyzed extensive datasets incorporating both DNA and RNA sequencing data. This analysis covered a total of 639 AML patients from the BeatAML and TCGA-LAML cohorts, detecting 31 recurrent variants, of which 27 could be finally validated based on RNA data availability (Suppl Table S3, Suppl Fig. 1, and Suppl Fig. 3).

Focusing on canonical and + 5 donor splice positions, we observed that all analyzed changes (n = 4) led to the activation of cryptic sites compared to non-mutated samples (Table 1, Suppl Tables 4, and Suppl Fig. 4). Notably, variants such as CBL c.1096–2 A >T (p = 0.0066; ΔPSI = 0.25) and c.1096-1G >C (p = 0.0133; ΔPSI = 0.15) triggered the activation of two unexpected cryptic sites downstream in exon 8, while TET2 c.3954 + 5G >A (p = 0.0022; ΔPSI = 0.30) activated a cryptic donor site in exon 7. All of these splicing alterations were independently corroborated using data from public datasets [22–25].

Within the subset of 23 nonsense and missense variants, five were confirmed to significantly impact splicing based on RNA sequencing data, independently of the genetic background, including the presence of mutations in splicing factor genes (Table 1 and Suppl Fig. 3). Four of these variants activated cryptic donor splice sites, including PTPN11 p.G503V (p = 0.0043; ΔPSI = 0.04) and U2AF1 p.Q157R (p = 0.02102; ΔPSI = 0.017), with activation concurring with an increase in exon 5 inclusion (Fig. 3a and Suppl Fig. 5). IDH1 p.R132C (p = 0.044; ΔPSI = 0.019) and p.R132S (p = 8.94E-16; ΔPSI = 0.064) variants activated a cryptic donor splice site 34 nucleotides upstream (Fig. 3b), as confirmed in the TCGA-LAML cohort (Suppl Fig. 6). In contrast, TP53 p.K132R activated a cryptic acceptor splice site in exon 5 in one of the patients harboring this variant (p = 0.0021; ΔPSI = 0.46, Suppl Fig. 7).

Furthermore, four variants displayed subtle but not statistically significant increases in aberrant effects (beyond the 80th percentile in ECDF), linked to exon skipping or splice gain events (Suppl Fig. 8–11). These include FLT3 p.N841K (p = 0.1667; ΔPSI = 0.007), showing a faint increase in exon 20 skipping, as confirmed in the TCGA-LAML cohort (p = 0.0866; ΔPSI = 0.009); KDM6A p.Q506* (p = 0.1211/0.1431; ΔPSI = 0.028), which activated a donor splice site 18 nucleotides upstream; and WT1 p.S386* (p = 0.1616; ΔPSI = 0.008), predicted to activate a donor site 3 nucleotides downstream. As these events were observed in only one or two mutated samples and did not meet conventional RNA-seq validation thresholds [21], they were subsequently assessed in minigene assays. Finally, the aberrant effects of 12 variants, including hotspots like K/NRAS p.G13D, and IDH1 p.R132G, could not be confirmed (Suppl Table S4).

Identification of rare AML-associated mutations leading to aberrant splicing through minigene reporter assays

Through our exploration of variants in the BeatAML and TCGA-LAML databases, we validated 27 (21.6%) out of the 125 variants predicted to potentially alter splicing. To maximize the impact of our analysis, we focused on the missense and nonsense variants (n = 71) and randomly selected 16 variants with one or two significant predictions for functional testing using minigene reporter assays. Additionally, we included 7 variants with no predicted splicing impact as negative controls.

Among the 16 variants, 10 (63%) induced abnormal splicing compared to the wild-type minigene (Fig. 3). Effects included exon skipping (n = 6) and the activation or creation of donor splice sites (n = 6). Notably, EZH2 p.M121I, TET2 p.R1167K, and ASXL1 p.K85R changes led to complete skipping of their respective exon 4 (Fig. 4a). Exon skipping was also observed due to FLT3 p.N841K and DNMT3A p.E817V variants, although in both cases a faint product corresponding to wild-type transcript was still detected (Fig. 4b).

Three variants, WT1 p.S386*, STAG2 p.L182*, and PTPN11 p.G503V, triggered the activation of cryptic donor splice sites (Fig. 4c). These sites were located 3 nucleotides downstream, 3 nucleotides upstream, and 6 bases upstream of the variant, respectively. Consequently, major aberrant transcripts were identified for these variants, each containing smaller exons. Additionally, in mutated minigenes, slight normal transcripts corresponding to the inclusion of specific exons were observed.

The remaining two variants, RAD21 p.D230N and KDM6A p.Q506*, each induced two aberrant events (Fig. 4d). RAD21 p.D230N caused the activation of two donor splice sites, while KDM6A p.Q506* produced a major transcript with exon 15 skipped and a minor transcript resulting from the activation of a donor splice site. In both cases, a faint normal transcript was observed in the mutated minigene, indicating partial preservation of the wild-type splicing pattern.

Minigene assays for the remaining six variants, despite scores at or slightly above the thresholds of one (TP53 p.K132R, DNMT3A p.N838D, and ASXL1 p.A530V) or both predictors (FLT3 p.N781K, DNMT3A p.F868V, and EZH2 p.D664V), did not reveal aberrant transcripts (Suppl Fig. 12). In addition, as expected, none of the variants lacking significant predictions (WT1 p.G351R, FLT3 p.I548V, ASXL1 p.R606Q, DNMT3A p.G550R, SF3B1 p.D799G, KIT p.P627T, and PHF6 p.G213R) exhibited aberrant splicing in the mutated minigene (Suppl Fig. 13).

Prediction of the effect of validated splicing-altering variants on protein structure

We examined the potential of the 17 previously confirmed SAVs from RNA-seq and/or minigene assays to induce NMD and/or alter protein structure (Table 1). Employing in silico simulations of splicing-related changes, we generated primary transcript sequences and predicted NMD activation. Subsequently, we analyzed the derived primary protein sequences to identify potential structural modifications arising from the loss and/or gain of conserved protein domains. Given that each variant may lead to multiple mis-splicing events, our analysis considered the impact of each variant based on the various effects it could induce.

Aberrant splicing events, resulting in reading frame shifts (n = 12), except for exon 4 skipping in ASXL1 and donor gain resulting from KDM6A p.Q506*, demonstrated the potential to trigger NMD. In contrast, as anticipated, in-frame alterations (n = 12) were not predicted to activate NMD (Table 1).

Concerning protein structure, 17 mis-splicing events caused by 14 variants led to significant changes in conserved domain composition (Table 1). In 11 cases, a distinct domain was partially lost, while in the remaining 7 cases, the loss was complete. No instances of gaining new functional domains were observed. Nine variants (ASXL1 p.K85R, CBL c.1096–2 A > T and c.1096-1G > C, IDH1 p.R132C/S, PTPN11 p.G503V, STAG2 p.L182*, TET2 p.R1167K and c.3954 + 5G > A) resulted in the partial loss of specific domains (Suppl Figs. 14–17). Consequently, the remaining portion of the domain did not allow the identification of it in the mutated protein, instead indicating an association with a broader superfamily of proteins. For example, the isocitrate dehydrogenase domain (PTZ00435) recognized in wild-type IDH1 protein would transform into an isocitrate/isopropyl malate dehydrogenase superfamily domain (cl00445) due to the deletion of 281 amino acids in the IDH1 protein.

Additionally, five variants led to the complete loss of a functional domain (Suppl Figs. 17–19). This was evident in TP53 c.376–2 A > G, U2AF1 p.Q157R, and WT1 p.S386* variants, leading to the absence of the P53 DNA-binding domain and P53 tetramerization motif, one of the Zinc-Finger CCCH domains, and a general Zn-finger, respectively. Similarly, RAD21 p.D230N and KDM6A p.Q506* variants caused the loss of the Rad21/Rec8 middle HEAT-repeat and the Rad21/Rec8-like protein domains, as well as two JmjC domains, respectively. This complete loss was observed exclusively for the donor gain events, while exon skipping events for these variants did not alter the domain composition compared to the wild-type protein.

Strikingly, the mis-splicing observed as a result of the remaining three variants, DNMT3A p.G817V, EZH2 p.M121I, and FLT3 p.N841K, did not appear to affect the composition of conserved domains, despite the deletion of 25, 39, and 40 amino acids, respectively. However, in the instance of FLT3 p.N841K, the skipping of exon 20 resulted in the loss of the ATP binding pocket within the tyrosine kinase domain (Suppl Fig. 20).

Discussion

In this study, we employed a DNA–RNA integrated approach to systematically analyze the correlation between somatic mutations present in a large cohort of AML patients [2] and alterations in splicing. Our analysis identified variants associated with splicing changes across multiple types of single nucleotide mutations, independently of the broader mutational context. While previous studies have focused on splice-altering events in several cancers [7–13], our work expands the understanding of cis-acting splicing-associated variants (SAVs) in AML. We found 125 (13.7%) out of 914 AML-related somatic mutations with predicted splice-altering potential, including 71 traditionally classified as missense or nonsense. Several key AML-associated genes, such as DNMT3A, TET2, RAD21, and STAG2, harbor these mutations. Functional analysis by RNA sequencing and/or minigene reporter assays confirmed splicing alterations in 17 (47.2%) out of the 36 tested variants. These findings emphasize that a substantial fraction of mutations traditionally classified as coding may exert dual effects, simultaneously impacting protein structure and RNA processing.

Our in silico approach, combining MaxEntScan [17] and SpliceAI [15], was particularly reliable for variants in splice-site regions, including those in CBL, TET2, and TP53, which were validated in RNA-seq in this study and confirmed in previous works [23–25]. It also allowed us to reclassify KDM5A c.3930 + 2 C >T as potentially non-spliceogenic [26], a rare event in which a noncanonical donor GC splice site, present in only ~ 1% of the human genome, changes to a GT canonical site. This modification likely increases U1 snRNA recognition, preserving the ability to generate normal transcripts [27].

An important outcome of this approach is the identification of SAVs that would not be recognized as splice-altering using conventional RNA-seq thresholds (ΔPSI ≥ 10% and FDR/q < 0.05) [21]. These cutoffs, while practical, do not always capture the biological complexity or functional consequences of specific mutation–disease relationships. As highlighted by the ClinGen SVI Splicing Subgroup, these criteria should be adapted to consider orthogonal evidence, reproducibility, tissue context, and gene dosage sensitivity [28]. A broader role for missense or nonsense mutations in regulating splicing, beyond their canonical protein-coding effects, has been examined in only a few cases [6]. Our findings contribute to this emerging view by providing several examples in which a single point mutation simultaneously alters the amino acid sequence and RNA processing.

Two variants in our study exemplify this concept. U2AF1 p.Q157R activates an alternative 5′ donor site one nucleotide downstream, producing an in-frame deletion within the second CCCH zinc-finger domain, consistent with findings by Herdt et al. [29]. This deletion (p.Q157Rdel) alters protein functionality while preserving zinc coordination and retains RNA-binding capacity, thereby regulating a distinct set of target exons compared with canonical p.Q157R [29]. Although p.Q157Rdel accounts for only ~ 2% of all U2AF1 reads at that position, it is reported to influence the overall splicing pattern in p.Q157R-positive patients, supporting a role for this isoform in modulating splicing regulation [29]. Such isoform-specific effects may contribute to the distinct co-mutation patterns (ASXL1, TP53) and poorer prognosis observed for p.Q157R compared with p.S34 variants [30].

For the first time, we also report aberrant splicing in IDH1 p.R132C/S, which activates a cryptic donor site 34 nucleotides upstream, generating an in-frame deletion within exon 4. This deletion is predicted to partially perturb the isocitrate dehydrogenase domain near p.R132, potentially altering substrate/cofactor binding or dimer geometry [31]. While the major oncogenic effect of p.R132 mutations is the neomorphic production of 2-HG, this cis-splicing event could further modulate enzyme function [31]. The same isoform was also detected in lower-grade glioma and prostate adenocarcinoma datasets [12], indicating it is not AML-specific. Notably, IDH1 p.R132C/S variants have been associated with AML subgroups resembling secondary AML and with poorer outcomes than p.R132H, raising the possibility that this isoform is functionally relevant and may contribute to the clinical phenotype [32].

In other cases, RNA-seq validation was limited by low variant allele frequency or few mutated samples. FLT3 p.N841K, KDM6A p.Q506*, and WT1 p.S386* showed modest ΔPSI values (~ 3% or less) and, in some instances, were detected only in RNA-seq and not at the exome level. Nonetheless, these variants were also found in pan-cancer datasets (e.g., KDM6A p.Q506* in TCGA thyroid carcinoma [24]) and exhibited reproducible mis-splicing patterns in our minigene assays.

Across our minigene assays, exon skipping was the most prevalent aberrant event, either in isolation (EZH2 p.M121I, TET2 p.R1167K, ASXL1 p.K85R) or accompanied by residual normal transcripts (FLT3 p.N841K, DNMT3A p.E817V). Cryptic donor site activation was the second most frequent alteration (WT1 p.S386*, STAG2 p.L182*, PTPN11 p.G503V, RAD21 p.D230N, KDM6A p.Q506*). Notably, predicted acceptor site gains (DNMT3A p.F868V, TP53 p.K132R) were not confirmed experimentally; in the case of TP53 p.K132R, the apparent RNA-seq effect observed in one of the patients was explained by co-occurrence of TP53 c.376–2 A > G (Suppl Fig. 21), underscoring the value of integrating multiple evidence sources.

Splicing-associated variants can have direct structural and therapeutic implications. Loss of the STAG2 pfam08514 domain (p.L182*) may impair cohesin–RAD21 interactions [33], (Suppl Fig. 22), affecting mitotic cohesion and genome organization [34]. EZH2 p.M121I-mediated exon 4 skipping may promote isoform switching and influence cell proliferation and differentiation [35, 36]. In FLT3 p.N841K, increased exon 20 skipping observed in minigene assays but not significantly at RNA-seq level is likely due to a baseline level of exon 20 skipping in normal individuals [36], with the mutation enhancing this event. Given that exon 20 encodes part of the ATP-binding pocket (Suppl Fig. 20), such alterations may influence sensitivity to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as gilteritinib and quizartinib, which target the ATP-binding site and adjacent side pockets [37].

Conclusions

Our data show that SAVs in AML include many missense and nonsense mutations not traditionally linked to splicing. Even modest ΔPSI changes can produce reproducible isoforms with distinct biochemical properties, be observed in other cancers, and associate with specific clinical subgroups. Integrating RNA-seq data with orthogonal validation is essential to capture the full range of splice-associated variants and understand their biological and clinical impact in AML.
