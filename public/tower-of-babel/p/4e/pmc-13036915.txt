Characterization of soy albumin based water-in-water emulsion and its application for entrapment of quercetin from onion peel waste
Authors: A. D. Srikanth Tangirala, Prakyath Shetty, Karthimol Anilkumar, C. K. Sunil, R. Jagan Mohan, Ashish Rawson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study characterizes water-in-water (W/W) emulsions, consisting of two immiscible water phases formulated using soy albumin with locust bean gum, guar gum, or xanthan gum as sustainable alternatives to traditional emulsions reliant on synthetic stabilizers. The emulsions were tested at varying protein–polysaccharide ratios, the 60:40 ratio of soy albumin and LBG (SL-6) exhibited optimal properties, including the smallest particle size (0.250 ± 0.015 μm), lowest interfacial tension (1.531 ± 0.061 mN/m), and superior rheological behavior. Stability was observed over 0–52 h, with SL-6 showing the lowest decay rate constant (k = 15.120 s⁻¹). The Power Law model was applied to characterize the emulsions’ non-Newtonian flow behavior, analyzing the consistency index (K) and flow behavior index (n). Emulsions exhibited higher R² values confirmed the model’s accuracy in describing rheological properties. Microstructural analysis was conducted using confocal laser scanning microscopy. Further in this study, SL-6 was applied as a green solvent for entrapping quercetin from onion peel waste, showing equivalent entrapment and better bioavailability compared to conventional solvent—Ethanol. Introduction

Emulsions are mixtures of two immiscible liquids stabilized by surfactants. They are widely used in food processing due to their ability to enhance texture, stability, and shelf life. Conventionally food processing and extraction, extensively utilizes synthetic stabilizers and energy-intensive toxic organic solvents which has raised concerns about environmental sustainability and health & safety. The demand for greener, healthier alternatives has spurred interest in biopolymer-based systems. One such innovation is water-in-water (W/W) emulsions, W/W emulsions are an innovative class of emulsions that consist of two immiscible aqueous phases, such as different biopolymer solutions, which are dispersed within each other [1]. The specialty of W/W emulsions lies in their ultralow interfacial tensions and substantial interface thickness, which differentiate them from conventional emulsions and allow for stabilization through gelation rather than molecular surfactants. This unique stabilization mechanism is crucial for maintaining the integrity of food products [1, 2].

Water-in-water emulsions have a multitude of applications in both food processing and pharmaceuticals. In food processing, it can be used for encapsulation of bioactive compounds, flavors, and nutrients, protecting them from degradation and controlling their release, thus enhancing stability and bioavailability [3, 4]. These emulsions also improve the texture and stability of food products, providing a creamy mouthfeel without the need for high-fat content, which is especially beneficial in low-calorie and health-oriented food products [5].

In the pharmaceutical field, water-in-water emulsions are employed for controlled and sustained drug release, enhancing the pharmacokinetic properties and bioavailability of active pharmaceutical ingredients, which is particularly useful for drugs requiring precise dosing and prolonged release [6]. Additionally, these emulsions can be engineered for targeted delivery, improving treatment efficacy and reducing side effects by modifying the emulsion’s composition to interact with specific biological environments [3]. Lastly, their ability to encapsulate and protect sensitive biological molecules makes them suitable as vaccine adjuvants, enhancing the immune response without compromising the stability of vaccine components [3].

Polyethylene glycol and dextran are commonly used phase components to formulate W/W emulsions, though alternative combinations have also been explored to optimize specific properties or address particular needs. For instance, researchers have investigated alternative biopolymer pairings, such as dextran and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) [7], chitosan and κ-carrageenan [8], and gelatin with carboxymethylcellulose [9]. These studies highlight the flexibility of W/W emulsions in accommodating diverse phase combinations for tailored applications.

Similarly for formulating food-grade W/W emulsions biopolymers such as albumin fraction of proteins which are water soluble, and water soluble polysaccharides viz., gums–gum arabica, guar gum, xanthan gum, etc. can be considered as potential phase components.

Soy albumin was selected as a protein source due to its exceptional ability to form stable interfaces and enhance the overall stability of emulsions. It improves the rheological properties of high-protein emulsions, ensuring that they maintain their structure and texture under various conditions [10, 11]. Additionally, soy albumin excels in encapsulating and delivering hydrophobic bioactive compounds, thereby enhancing their bioavailability [10, 11]. Its versatility makes it particularly valuable for formulating plant-based and protein-rich food products [12]. These advantages make soy albumin an ideal choice for developing high-quality, stable emulsions.

W/W emulsions in food technology has vast application, ranging from serving as micro-reactors for synthesizing high-value particles, to, acting as carriers for labile molecules and smart particles for encapsulating lipophilic as well as lipophobic components [13]. These emulsions are biocompatable and have the ability to encapsulate and deliver bioactive ingredients, making them suitable for creating healthier and more functional food products [14]. One particularly promising application of W/W emulsions is their use as green solvents for the extraction of bioactive compounds.Quercetin, a flavonoid with therapeutic and antioxidant properties, is traditionally extracted using methods that rely on its polarity and the use of solvents such as methanol, ethanol, and acetone. However, these methods are associated with significant environmental drawbacks, including the large-scale use of organic solvents, their toxicity, and the emission of volatile organic compounds. The bioactivity of flavonoids can also be compromised due to prolonged exposure to high temperatures and solvents [15].

The objective of the present study was to develop and characterize a W/W emulsion with the specific aim of utilizing its inherent phase properties to entrap quercetin as a model bioactive compound. The study further explores the potential of this emulsion as a green solvent by demonstrating its ability to stabilize quercetin while significantly reducing environmental impact.

Experimental

Materials

All the materials: Soy protein isolate (SPI)—99% purity, 300,000 to 600,000 kDa, Locust bean gum (LBG)—99.9% purity, 310 kDa, Guar gum (GG)—99.9% purity, 220 kDa, Xanthan gum (XG)—99.0% purity, 2000 kDa, Methanol (HPLC grade), Ethanol (HPLC grade), Aluminum chloride, Potassium acetate, Pepsin enzyme solution (4.8 mg/mL), Pancreatin enzyme solution (8 mg/mL), Hydrochloric acid (HCl)—1 M, 3 M, and 6 M solutions, 1 M solution of Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)—1 M, 3 M, and 6 M solutions were all analytical grade and procured from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (Bengaluru, Karnataka, India).

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) dye, Rhodamine B dye, Quercetin standard, procured were all analytical grade and procured from Merck chemicals (Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India), and de-ionized water was used for this research study. Onion peels (Pusa Red variety) were procured from a local farmer—Thanjavur, India.

Methods

Bulk solution preparation

Bulk solutions were prepared following the method suggested by Tangirala et al. [16]. SPI (10%), LBG (1.0%), GG (1.0%), and XG (1.0%) were prepared by adding each component (all as powder form) to deionized water at pH 6.5. The mixtures were then mixed at specific temperatures: GG and SPI at 25 °C, XG at 75 °C [17], and LBG at 85 °C.Labscale hand heldHomogenizerUltra-Turrax® T 25, IKA manufacturer, USA, was used for mixing the bulk solutions at 6000 rpm for 10 min.

Soy albumin preparation

Soy albumin was extracted from SPI as described by Galbas et al. [18] with slight modifications as described—Following the mixing, the SPI solutions were subjected to centrifugation at 3350×g (4500 rpm, Rotor—221.55 V20, Model—Z 326 K, Hermle Co. centrifuge, Germany) at 10 °C for 20 min. This centrifugation step was performed to separate the sediments and extract the Soy Albumin (SA)—water-soluble protein fraction. The collected water-soluble protein fraction was then used for further preparation of the emulsions.

Emulsion preparation

Ternary solutions were prepared by mixing specific amounts of SA and LBG (SL), SA and GG (SG), and SA and XG (SX) solutions to obtain the required proportions with slight modifications as described [19]. The polysaccharides used for the emulsion preparation were selected due to their steric hindrance effect.The protein and polysaccharide solutions were combined in ratios of 10:90, 20:80, 30:70, 40:60, 50:50, 60:40, 70:30, 80:20, and 90:10 to create each emulsion. The formulated emulsions were transferred to screw-capped glass vials and stored in a refrigerated chamber at 4 °C.

Phase diagram and its determination

The phase diagrams (binodals) for biopolymer systems at pH 6.5 and 20 °C were established following the phase-volume ratio method [20]. Nine experimental sets were conducted for each phase diagram to obtain binodal points by analyzing the relationship between biopolymer concentrations and phase-volume ratios in binary solutions. The phase-volume ratios were plotted against weight fractions, yielding two binodal points and one point on the rectilinear diameter (representing equal volume fractions). This procedure provided a robust fit for phase-separated systems, and the study was conducted at pH 6.5 and 20 °C.

These diagrams illustrate the regions where biopolymer mixtures either remain mixed or separate into two phases [21]. Each point on the diagram represents the concentrations of the two biopolymers and the solvent [22]. The binodal curve distinguishes the one-phase region from the two-phase region and includes theoretical points such as the critical point and threshold point. Tie-lines indicate how biopolymers distribute between phases with the phase separation influenced by factors like concentration, temperature, molecular weight, and interactions [23].

The measurements were taken at intervals of 4 h, ranging from 0 to 52 h. 5 mL of emulsion sample was taken in cuvette, the emulsion was left undisturbed, and measurement of absorbance was done. The obtained data was fitted in the Exponential decay model to explain the emulsion stability behaviour.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) measurements were done with a Zeiss LSM 710 CLSM microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany) utilizing an HC × PL APO 25× objective with slight modification [26]. A few drops of the prepared emulsion were placed on a glass slide, mounted with a cover slip, and examined. The protein part of the emulsion was labelled with Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), while the polysaccharide part of the emulsion was labelled with Rhodamine B dye. The emission and excitation ranges of FITC were 494 nm and 521 nm, respectively, and for Rhodamine B were 546 nm and 567 nm, respectively.

Rheological analysis

Rheological description of the W/W emulsions was conducted using MCR 302 (Anton Paar) shear rheometer. The experiment utilized cone-and-plate geometry (CP50-2, cone diameter 50 mm, cone angle 2°, 1 mm gap) at shear rates ranging from 0.1 to 1000 s−1 [28].The shear rate range of 0.1 to 1000 s⁻¹ was selected to comprehensively analyze the emulsion’s behavior under varying conditions, from low shear rates simulating storage to high shear rates representing application or processing.

Onion peel preparation

Onion peels of Pusa red variety were selected for this study and sample preparation was carried out as described by by Sagar et al. [30]. The onion peels were rinsed with distilled water and placed on a porous tray for 10 min to drain excess water. To minimize nutrient loss, peels were subjected to freeze-drying using a Lyophilizer LYO 10 T (Borg Scientific, Chennai) for a duration of 24 h at − 50 °C and 0.040 mbar pressure. The freeze-dried peels were then milled using a Preethi Blue Leaf Platinum Mixer grinder (750 W, 18,500 rpm). The resulting powder was sieved using an INSIF sieve analyzer (Sieve No. 18). The sieved onion peel powder, with a particle size of 1 mm, was stored at − 30 °C in airtight plastic containers for subsequent use.

Quercetin entrapment

This study employed conventional solvent extraction method using, Ethanol, and SL-6 for the extraction of quercetin. The choice of Ethanol was based on an extraction optimization study which considered various ethanol concentrations. A 59% ethanol solution was ultimately utilized, as recommended by Jang et al. [31].

Conventional Solvent extraction

In this extraction method, 1 g of onion peel powder was mixed with 50 mL of the solvents (59% ethanol). The mixture was then left undisturbed at room temperature for a duration of 3 h [31]. This extended period was allocated to facilitate the efficient extraction of quercetin into the emulsion.

Sample preparation

Sample preparation was done following the procedure suggested by Jang et al. [31]. After conventional solvent extraction treatment, the sample was subjected to centrifugation of the sample at 4500 rpm for 15 min at 5 °C using a refrigerated centrifuge (4500 rpm, Rotor—221.55 V20, Model—Z 326 K, Hermle Co. centrifuge). The supernatant was then filtered using 0.45 μm pore size PVDF filters. The filtered supernatant was collected in capped tubes and stored at − 30 °C for further use.

Quercetin estimation

Quercetin was estimated by using Spectrophotometer (DS-11 Series DeNovix Spectrophotometer, USA make)according to the method described by Alva-Ensastegui et al. [32]. 1.25 mL of the sample was mixed with 5 mL of methanol, vortexed for 5 min, and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 10 min, Relative Fluorescence Units (RFU) for each sample were measured at an excitation wavelength range of 350–360 nm and an emission wavelength range of 430–450 nm.The estimation process involves adding quercetin to an aqueous solution. The fluorescence emission spectra are recorded after each addition, allowing for the creation of calibration plots. These plots show a linear relationship between fluorescence intensity and quercetin concentration.

The use of a range of excitation (350–360 nm) and emission (430–450 nm) wavelengths in quercetin estimation was chosen to ensure optimal detection of quercetin’s fluorescence properties. Quercetin exhibits variable fluorescence characteristics depending on its concentration, solvent, and molecular interactions, which can lead to slight shifts in its excitation and emission maxima. Using a range rather than a single wavelength provides more reliable and reproducible results by accommodating these variations and maximizing sensitivity.

This approach aligns with the method described by Alva-Ensastegui et al. [23], which also utilizes wavelength ranges to enhance the accuracy of fluorescence measurements. Furthermore, employing a range allows for better signal integration and reduces the risk of missing peak fluorescence intensity, especially in complex matrices where quercetin may interact with other components. This ensures a comprehensive and robust quantification of quercetin in the samples.

Invitro digestion and bioavailability study of quercetin

Bioavailability study of quercetin was conducted according to the method of Mathews et al. [33]. The typical simulationofin-vitro digestion protocol entails two sequential phases—Gastric and Intestinal digestion phases performed within a single static bioreactor.

Sample digestion and quercetin analysis

A 5 mL aliquot of the extracted sample supernatant was subjected to an in-vitro digestion process at 37 °C. During the gastric digestion stage, the pH was adjusted to 1.2 with the addition of 0.63 mL of pepsin solution. Subsequently, the intestinal digestion stage was performed by adjusting the pH to 4.0 and adding an intestinal enzyme solution.

The bioavailability of quercetin was estimated both Before Digestion (BD) and After Digestion (AD), while the antioxidant activity (AA) was evaluated after digestion. Results and discussion

Emulsion stability study

Food emulsions need to maintain stability to achieve their intended functionality, which is crucial for their overall performance and efficacy. In this study, the emulsion stability and the duration before phase separation were evaluated by observing all formulated emulsions over 52 h at 25 °C. Absorbance measurements were recorded every four hours, and the resulting data were analyzed using the Exponential Decay Model, which provided the parameters ‘A’ (amplitude) and ‘k’ (rate constant) as indicators of emulsion stability and separation rate.

The results presented in Table 2 indicate that SL emulsions exhibited the highest R² and adjusted R² values, reflecting a strong fit between the model and the observed data. The R² value, also known as the coefficient of determination, measures how well the model explains data variability, with values closer to 1 indicating a better fit. Adjusted R² accounts for the number of variables in the model, preventing overestimation of its explanatory power when multiple predictors are used.

For the SL emulsions, the high R² and adjusted R² values confirm that the Exponential Decay Model reliably describes the relationship between absorbance and time. This supports the validity of the ‘A’ and ‘k’ parameters in assessing emulsion stability and phase separation behavior. The close alignment of R² and adjusted R² values further confirms that the model is well-balanced—neither overfitted nor underfitted—reinforcing its suitability for this analysis. Among the tested phase combinations, SL emulsions demonstrated superior stability, evidenced by lower k values, suggesting a slower separation rate. This enhanced performance highlights the importance of polysaccharide selection in emulsion design and emphasizes the robustness of SL emulsions for extended shelf-life and functionality.

The greater stability observed in LBG-based emulsions compared to those containing GG or XG can be attributed to the unique structural attributes of LBG. As a galactomannan, LBG contains a mannose backbone with galactose side chains and is characterized by irregular substitution patterns. Its lower solubility and fewer galactose branches compared to GG lead to higher viscosity upon heating and cooling, contributing to improved emulsion stability [34]. Thus, LBG’s chemical composition and physicochemical behavior play a key role in enhancing emulsion robustness.

In contrast, SX emulsions showed higher initial absorbance values (A), indicating better initial dispersion. However, they consistently exhibited higher decay rates, resulting in faster phase separation.

The rapid separation seen in SX, relative to SL and SG emulsions, may be linked to the strong net negative charge of XG, while LBG and GG carry weaker negative charges (Table 1). This charge disparity induces electrostatic repulsion among XG molecules, accelerating separation. Furthermore, GG is non-ionic. The resulting separation is likely due to segregative phase separation, a process driven by electrostatic or steric repulsion between two biopolymers that leads each to exclude the other from its domain, prompting phase segregation [35].

In emulsions comprising SP with XG, GG, or their blends, weak electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding can form loose networks, allowing soluble complexes to retain water. This contributes to increased internal friction and higher viscosity, which may explain the decreased stability observed in the SX and SG emulsions (Table 1). The interactions between SP and LBG are more complex—at lower LBG concentrations, weak electrostatic complexes form, reducing SP solubility and charge. At higher LBG concentrations, hydrogen bonding dominates, forming strong networks with substantial water-holding capacity, resulting in higher-than-expected viscosity [36].

Combining soy protein and LBG in a 60:40 ratio significantly enhances emulsion stability, as confirmed by previous studies. This ratio promotes optimal interaction between soy protein and LBG, leading to improved rheological behavior, structural integrity, and functional performance. For example, appropriate LBG concentrations increase the viscosity and elasticity of emulsions, aiding in resistance against phase separation [37]. Moreover, higher LBG content reduces droplet size and promotes a uniform dispersion, further stabilizing the emulsion [38].

Additionally, the 60:40 ratio allows for the development of a cohesive emulsion network, balancing the hydrophobic interactions of soy albumin with the thickening properties of LBG. The resulting interfacial layer minimizes droplet coalescence and improves long-term stability [37]. The present findings align with this body of evidence, emphasizing the significance of precise formulation ratios in achieving stable emulsions. The current study’s observation of lower rate constants and extended phase separation times for SL emulsions affirms that the 60:40 soy albumin–LBG combination is highly effective, offering valuable insights for the design of stable emulsions in diverse food applications.

Interfacial tension

Table 2 summarizes the interfacial tension values for emulsions prepared using different protein–polysaccharide combinations. Water-in-water (W/W) emulsions are known for their exceptionally low interfacial tensions [1]. As shown in Table 2, SL emulsions consistently demonstrated particularly low interfacial tension values, ranging from 1.53 ± 0.061 to 3.22 ± 0.129 mN/m. Generally, emulsions with higher interfacial tension are more prone to phase separation or droplet coalescence, as the system tends to minimize interfacial area to reduce energy. Lower interfacial tension values, therefore, reflect enhanced interfacial stability, suggesting greater emulsion longevity with reduced risks of droplet merging and phase separation [39].

Evaluating interfacial tension across different protein–polysaccharide systems helps identify combinations that offer greater stability against destabilizing factors. This insight is vital for designing improved food formulations that offer longer shelf life and desirable textural qualities [40]. Interfacial tension is a key parameter for understanding phase separation behavior, as it measures the energy required to increase the interfacial area between immiscible liquid phases and provides information about the dynamics at the interface.

The strong synergy between soy albumin and LBG in SL emulsions can be attributed to robust intermolecular interactions and effective interfacial coverage. When XG or GG is incorporated into the soluble protein formulations, weaker electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding occur, forming a network structure that enhances water retention. At lower LBG concentrations, the reduced net negative charge leads to lower solubility and viscosity. In contrast, higher LBG concentrations intensify intermolecular hydrogen bonding, resulting in greater water absorption and increased viscosity, which exceed values predicted from the individual components [36].

Phase diagrams serve as critical tools for analyzing the behavior of biopolymer mixtures in solution. They provide essential insights into the conditions under which phase separation occurs, which is particularly relevant in food science, biotechnology, and materials engineering. The interpretation of phase diagrams involves examining biopolymer–solvent interactions, leading to the formation of separate phases with different component concentrations. Protein–polysaccharide mixtures often exhibit thermodynamic incompatibility due to disparities in molecular size, charge density, and hydrophilicity, which typically results in phase separation. Additionally, external factors such as pH, ionic strength, and temperature play a pivotal role in modulating this behavior [41].

The asymmetrical shape of a binodal curve often reflects inherent polymer properties, with the curve generally shifting toward the polymer that has lower molecular weight or greater hydrophilicity [41]. As seen in Fig. 2, the binodal curve for the SL formulation lies closest to the y-axis, indicating improved phase stability. This proximity implies the formation of a well-defined biphasic system, wherein the materials are efficiently partitioned between the two phases at equilibrium. A smaller distance from the y-axis suggests better phase stability, whereas a larger displacement from the x-axis denotes skewed material distribution, where one phase sequesters a greater proportion of the components [28].

Complex phase behavior can also emerge in composite or ternary biopolymer systems, influenced by both polymer structure and solvent interactions. For example, polydispersity in biopolymers like dextran can affect miscibility and lead to more intricate phase diagrams [42]. In ternary systems (two polymers and a solvent), the Flory–Huggins theory is often applied to predict component interactions, while tie line analysis provides qualitative insight into phase ratios and overall system stability [43]. These theoretical and analytical tools help deepen our understanding of W/W emulsion behavior.

The superior phase stability observed in SL emulsions is attributed to effective interactions between soy albumin and LBG. These interactions, help form a robust network that supports emulsion stability. LBG, a galactomannan polysaccharide, contributes by binding at the interface and enhancing the emulsion’s water retention and structural integrity.

The Slope of the Tie Line (STL) for all formulations was determined, as illustrated in Fig. 1A–C, which show the equilibrium compositions of the two phases within the biphasic region. A tie line connects these compositions and its slope represents the ratio of compositional and volumetric differences between phases. STL is a valuable metric for evaluating phase proportions, compositional characteristics, and stability [20]. A rectilinear point on the tie line denotes a specific equilibrium state between the two phases. Analyzing binodal curves and tie lines thus provides a comprehensive understanding of emulsion phase behavior and assists in optimizing formulations for enhanced stability.Comparable studies, such as those investigating the phase behavior of water–glycinin–pectin systems, have been reported by Dickinson [44].

Among the SL group, SL-6 showed the smallest particle size, making it highly desirable for producing a stable emulsion. These observations underscore the critical influence of phase composition on both droplet size and overall emulsion stability. Similar patterns have been reported in emulsions formulated using xanthan gum [46], locust bean gum [47], and guar gum [48], supporting the idea that polysaccharide selection significantly affects emulsion characteristics.

When comparing data across interfacial tension and particle size analysis, a strong correlation emerges: emulsions with lower interfacial tension—particularly SL—consistently demonstrate smaller particle sizes and improved stability. This is because lower interfacial tension reduces the energy barrier for droplet formation, enabling the creation of finer droplets. These smaller droplets exhibit higher surface charge densities, which enhance electrostatic repulsion and minimize coalescence. Thus, SL emulsions, characterized by low interfacial tension and reduced droplet size, exhibit superior physical stability and longevity.

Rheological analysis

A rheological investigation was conducted for the SL-6, SG-6, and SX-6 emulsion formulations. The variations in apparent viscosity, storage modulus (G′), and loss modulus (G″) in response to shear stress are illustrated in Fig. 3A–C. All three emulsions exhibited dilatant (shear-thickening) behavior, where changes in shear stress led to observable transitions in flow properties. This dilatant nature is particularly important, as it enhances the emulsion’s interfacial rheological strength, thereby increasing its resistance to destabilizing processes such as coalescence and Ostwald ripening, ultimately contributing to improved shelf life and performance. Such characteristics are especially beneficial for food and pharmaceutical applications, where natural stability is crucial [49].

Among the three systems, SL emulsions consistently outperformed SG and SX formulations across all rheological parameters measured under varying shear stress. These rheological properties are useful indicators of how the emulsions behave under mechanical forces. A higher storage modulus (G′) reflects a material’s ability to store energy elastically and resist deformation, while a higher loss modulus (G″) indicates a greater dissipation of energy during deformation.

As shown in Fig. 3A, the SL-6 emulsion displayed the highest apparent viscosity across the full range of shear stress, implying greater structural stability. This aligns with results from particle size distribution, interfacial tension, and emulsion stability measurements, further validating the superior stability of the SL emulsion [50]. The unique behavior of LBG in solution promotes the formation of supramolecular aggregates, which significantly enhance the system’s viscosity. In contrast, XG in the SX emulsion may contribute less effectively, and although GG increases the zero-shear viscosity in protein systems, its influence appears to be less impactful compared to LBG. These observations are consistent with findings reported by Xiao et al. [51].

From Fig. 3B, it can be noted that the storage modulus (G′) of SL-6 is relatively lower compared to SG-6 and SX-6. An increasing storage modulus with rising shear stress typically suggests a transition from a more structured, elastic state to a less organized, more fluid-like one. This behavior is important for evaluating the mechanical robustness of emulsions under stress. In this case, the more gradual increase in SL-6’s storage modulus implies that it maintains better structural integrity over a range of applied stresses, making it a preferable formulation for stability. Similar rheological studies also report enhanced biphasic structural advantages in SL emulsions, particularly in terms of moduli behavior [52].

According to Fig. 3C, SL-6 exhibited a higher loss modulus (G″) compared to SG-6 and SX-6. An increase in G″ with shear stress indicates a greater viscous response, signifying that the emulsion dissipates more energy as heat rather than storing it elastically. In this study, the elevated loss modulus of the SL emulsion suggests that it becomes more fluid-like under shear, but still retains structural resilience, which is advantageous in applications involving high mechanical stress. Moreover, SL emulsions offer benefits such as enhanced viscoelastic tunability, improved stability, and better solubility and bioavailability for poorly water-soluble compounds [50, 53, 54].

Together, these rheological parameters provide critical insights into the emulsions’ mechanical behavior under deformation. SL emulsions consistently demonstrated higher apparent viscosity and loss modulus, underscoring their superior structural and functional performance under varying shear conditions. These advantages are attributed to the synergistic interactions between proteins and LBG, particularly LBG’s ability to form organized, supramolecular networks that reinforce the emulsion structure.

Power law model

The Power Law model, characterized by the consistency index (K) and flow behavior index (n), serves as a robust framework for evaluating the flow behavior of non-Newtonian fluids, such as emulsions. The consistency index (K) reflects the fluid’s apparent viscosity at a unit shear rate, providing insights into its resistance to flow, while the flow behavior index (n) indicates the degree to which the fluid deviates from Newtonian behavior. A value of n < 1 denotes shear-thinning behavior, where viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate, whereas n > 1 signifies shear-thickening behavior, where viscosity increases as shear rate increases.

From Table 4, it is evident that all emulsions—SL, SG, and SX—exhibit shear-thickening behavior, as indicated by n values greater than 1. Among them, Sample SL displays moderate viscosity with the lowest consistency index, yet shows a distinct shear-thickening profile. Sample SG, on the other hand, exhibits a higher consistency index and a more pronounced shear-thickening effect, as reflected by its higher flow behavior index (n). The coefficient of determination (R²) values, which are close to 1 for all three samples, suggest that the Power Law model accurately captures the relationship between apparent viscosity and shear rate in these emulsion systems.

The inclusion of gums in emulsions has been shown to enhance both apparent viscosity and shear-thickening behavior. This effect is primarily driven by specific interactions between gum molecules and other components in the emulsion matrix. For example, emulsions containing locust bean gum (LBG) exhibit shear-thickening characteristics, which are attributed to interactions between LBG and proteins like sodium caseinate, as well as external conditions such as pH and ionic strength [55]. Likewise, xanthan gum contributes to increased viscosity under shear, due to its ability to form entangled network structures, which resist deformation and can exhibit shear-thickening behavior under certain conditions. These observations offer valuable insight into the rheological characteristics of the emulsions, highlighting the critical role of polymer–protein interactions in modulating flow properties. Comprehensive evaluation of physicochemical properties and stability of SL-6 formulation

A broad understanding of physicochemical properties related to the W/W emulsions requires an integrated assessment of phase behavior, interfacial tension, particle size distribution, rheological characteristics, and overall emulsion stability. In this present study, these multifactorial analyses clearly indicate that SL-6 exhibited the superior performance.

The phase diagram depicts key information about the regions of biphasic separation and tie-line compositions. Using this information, SL-6 formulation was chosen that has a stable biphasic region, indicating favorable thermodynamic immiscibility and effective polymer–polymer interactions conducive to phase separation and droplet formation. The orientation and length of tie-lines within the SL system when compared to the other polysaccharide phase further suggested stronger partitioning potential, with locust bean gum (LBG) contributing to well-structured continuous phases.

Interfacial tension analysis revealed that SL emulsions exhibited the lowest interfacial tension values among all tested formulations. Low interfacial tension reduces the energy required to disperse one phase within another, enabling the generation of smaller droplets during emulsification. This was directly confirmed by particle size analysis, which showed that SL-6 had the smallest mean droplet diameter (0.250 ± 0.015 μm). Smaller droplets inherently provide a greater interfacial area, enhancing both steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion—two critical factors that reduce coalescence and enhance kinetic stability.

These structural advantages were corroborated by emulsion stability studies, where SL emulsions displayed the lowest destabilization rate constants (k) and highest R² values under the exponential decay model, indicating slower separation kinetics and superior long-term stability. The coalescence resistance afforded by smaller droplets and the increased viscosity from LBG interactions likely contribute to this enhanced performance.

To further characterize these systems, rheological evaluations were performed on SL-6, SG-6, and SX-6 emulsions. Across all formulations, dilatant (shear-thickening) behavior was observed, consistent with Power Law model outputs showing flow behavior indices (n > 1). Among the emulsions, SL-6 consistently exhibited the highest apparent viscosity across all shear stress levels (Fig. 3A). This enhanced viscosity is attributed to the supramolecular aggregates formed by LBG in aqueous media, which increase resistance to flow and augment mechanical stability—both important for applications requiring high structural integrity under processing conditions.

Further, viscoelastic parameters reinforced the superior rheological behavior of SL-6. Although the storage modulus (G′) of SL-6 was lower than that of SG-6 and SX-6, its loss modulus (G′′) was notably higher (Fig. 3C). A higher loss modulus indicates a more viscous and dissipative system, suggesting that SL-6 can absorb and dissipate energy more effectively under stress, thereby reducing the likelihood of structural collapse. The modest increase in storage modulus with shear stress also indicated that SL emulsions retained their integrity without becoming overly elastic, supporting better adaptability under shear.

The Power Law model fitted to the viscosity data yielded further mechanistic insights. SL emulsions had a lower consistency index (K), indicating moderate initial viscosity, and a higher flow behavior index (n), confirming pronounced shear-thickening behavior. These rheological features, coupled with superior structural resilience, enhance the SL emulsion’s applicability in formulations subjected to dynamic mechanical stresses, such as in food, pharmaceutical, and nutraceutical systems.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate a strong interrelationship among the phase diagram, interfacial tension, particle size, rheology, and emulsion stability. The SL-6 formulation, selected through rational phase diagram-based design, displayed optimized physicochemical properties across all evaluated dimensions. Its low interfacial tension facilitated fine droplet formation, small particle size conferred enhanced steric and electrostatic stabilization, rheological robustness provided mechanical resilience, and stability analysis confirmed superior long-term performance. These synergistic characteristics positiones SL-based emulsions as promising candidate for high-performance delivery systems and structured liquids in food and pharmaceutical applications.

Quercetin entrapment study

Further in the study, quercetin was extracted from the onion peel waste by conventional solvent extraction process using the solvents—59%-Ethanol [31] and its extraction was compared with the entrapment bySL-6. The total extractable quercetin from the onion peel powder was found to be 92.71 ± 0.61 mg/kg, closely aligning with the values reported by Sagar et al. [30]. The quercetin yields (mg/kg dw) obtained by the extraction process from onion peel powder using the Ethanol was—74.01 ± 1.97 mg/kg, and SL-6 was—73.71 ± 1.98 mg/kg. A notable aspect of this research is despite quercetin’s limited solubility in water, the use of SL-6 lead to improved extraction of quercetin and the yield was comparable with that of 59% ethanol.

The solubility of quercetin in ethanol can be attributed to ethanol’s ability to disrupt the intermolecular hydrogen bonds in quercetin, thereby facilitating its dissolution. This process is more efficient in ethanol than in water due to ethanol’s lower polarity, making it a superior solvent for quercetin, a relatively non-polar compound. Despite quercetin’s limited solubility in water, quercetin’s solubility in SL-6 is due to the non-covalent interactions between quercetin and proteins, which have been shown to alter and enhance the protein’s structural and functional characteristics, crucial for developing functional food ingredients. Parolia et al. [58] reported that quercetin’s binding ability with proteins is due to the interaction between the lysine side chains of proteins and with the active phenolic hydroxyl groups and catechol moiety (two adjacent (ortho) aromatic hydroxyl groups) present on the B-ring of Quercetin (as depicted in Fig. 5). Given these findings, it is plausible that the interaction between SL-6 and quercetin followed a similar pattern, with SL-6 potentially enhancing the solubility and bioavailability of quercetin.

Conclusion

W/W emulsions represent an innovative and sustainable alternative with wide-ranging applications andthe phase diagrams provided valuable insights into the emulsions’ distinct behaviors, highlighting the critical interactions between proteins and polysaccharides. Among the studied emulsions SL emulsions containing SA and LBG demonstrated exceptional stability, characterized by low interfacial tension, smaller particle sizes, and slower phase separation. The application of the exponential decay model further validated the prolonged stability of SL emulsions, with lower decay rates reflecting enhanced stability.

The Power Law model, further confirmed shear-thickening behavior (n > 1) across all samples. SL exhibited moderate viscosity with the lowest consistency index, while SG and SX demonstrated stronger shear-thickening effects. CLSM analysis provided a deeper understanding of the microstructural interactions and mechanisms underlying phase separation.

Additionally, the study highlighted the effectiveness of SL-6 as a green solvent for quercetin entrapment. SL-6 achieved comparable yields to ethanol while offering superior bioavailability and antioxidant activity, aligning with green chemistry approach. These findings underscore the potential of SL-6 as a sustainable solvent and emphasize the interplay of structural, rheological, and functional properties in advancing stable W/W emulsions.

Although the entrapment of quercetin—a water-insoluble phytochemical—was successfully demonstrated in this study, the investigation was limited exclusively to quercetin. This presents a research gap and highlights the need for further studies involving other water-insoluble & soluble phytochemicals present in onion peel. Secondly, while SL-6 exhibited promising performance as a green solvent at the laboratory scale, the study did not assess its scalability or industrial applicability.

Future research should aim to broaden the scope by investigating a wider range of bioactive compounds and evaluating the feasibility of large-scale applications of SL-6 in functional food systems.
