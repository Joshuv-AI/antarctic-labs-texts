Gut microbiome changes in a captive giant panda with cardiovascular disease
Authors: Xueying WANG, Ryoji UMEMOTO, Mizuki KATO, Takashi HAYAKAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interaction between gut bacteria and their host is vital for the early diagnosis and treatment of disease in captive animals. Here, we report a 16S rRNA sequencing-based bacterial profile during the progression of cardiovascular disease and drug treatment in a captive giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca). We observed changes in the composition of bacteria associated with inflammation and regulating nutrient metabolism. Predicted metabolic functions also exhibited alterations. The remarkably reduced gut microbiome diversity, along with the imbalance in community interaction networks, indicated possible gut dysfunction. Our findings represent the first description of gut bacterial changes in a giant panda with cardiovascular disease. Maintaining symbiotic bacteria diversity is crucial for preventing health issues in captive animals and advancing wildlife conservation efforts.

According to the IUCN Red List Category, the giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) population is currently classified as vulnerable [21]. As the captive population of giant pandas exceeds one-quarter of the total population, the management of their health, genetic diversity, and reintroduction into the wild has become a critical concern for conservation of this species [43].Giant pandas have a carnivore-like gastrointestinal tract, characterized by a simple structure, a degenerated cecum, a short, straight colon, and a short digestion time. Yet they rely almost exclusively on bamboo, a low-nutrient, low-energy, and high-fiber diet, resulting in poor energy utilization [31, 41, 44].

The gut microbiome and its host animal are often described as a symbiotic relationship [9, 10]. The gut bacteria provide additional metabolic capacity to their hosts [3]. Symbiotic microbes in the gut provide mammals with additional digestive capacity [5, 33, 35]. The unsaturated fats from dietary sources and the host metabolic patterns influence atherosclerosis in captive animals, with the frequency of coronary arteriosclerosis being much higher in omnivorous and herbivorous animals than among carnivorous animals [38]. For example, cardiovascular disease is a major cause of mortality in captive great apes [32]. A study of zoo-housed and wild western lowland gorilla (Gorilla gorilla gorilla) cohorts shows remarkable disease-related differences in gut microbiome composition [13, 23]. Although biomarkers within the gut microbiome of gorillas have been reported as potentially useful in identifying cardiovascular disease, there remains insufficient data to elucidate the relationship between cardiovascular disease and gut bacteria in captive bears [20].

The leading cause of death among giant pandas is parasitic infections. In particular, infections such as Baylisascaris schroederi often leads to malnutrition and potential multiple organ failure [7, 24, 39]. Other prevalent diseases include viral infections, as well as other respiratory and intestinal infections [28]. Considering the specialized diet and simple digestive system of giant pandas, the composition of their gut microbiome exhibits extremely low diversity [16, 31, 44]. Such individuals may be at a disadvantage due to increased susceptibility to pathogenic infections. Studying the symbiotic microbiota of giant pandas could have remarkable implications for preserving their immune barriers, managing their overall health, and conserving the species. Cardiovascular conditions are rare in giant pandas, but instances of myocardial inflammation, heart failure, and hypertension brought on by chronic kidney disease have been noted in deceased captive giant pandas. Due to the high disease tolerance of large wild animals, diseased animals often lack obvious clinical symptoms, which complicates immediate and effective treatment efforts. Consequently, they are frequently susceptible to medical complications or even sudden death [40]. Understanding changes in gut bacteria in giant pandas during the development of cardiovascular disease can aid in the early detection of the condition in large captive wild animals.

In this study, we conducted gut bacterial microbiome analysis in a captive female adult giant panda diagnosed with cardiovascular disease. During the 3 years (March 2021 to March 2024) of its illness, we treated it with various cardiovascular disease medications. We continuously recorded its physiological activities, including diet intake, activity level, heart rate, and blood pressure. We present a comprehensive analysis of the changes in the composition and functional profiles of the animal’s gut bacteria during disease progression and explore whether any bacterial biomarkers are associated with the onset and development of cardiovascular disease. This study did not involve animal experimentation. Fecal samples were noninvasively collected after the giant panda defecated naturally. We complied with the Code of Ethics and Welfare of the Japanese Association of Zoos and Aquariums. This study was approved as a collaborative study between Kobe Oji Zoo and Hokkaido University under the agreement on Japan-China joint research (issued on April 21st, 2021).

A captive female adult giant panda (“TanTan”) in Kobe Oji Zoo (Hyogo, Japan) was monitored in this study. Between May 2021 and August 2022, a total of 152 fecal samples were collected weekly during routine husbandry. Fresh fecal matter was collected, promptly frozen at −20°C, and transported to Hokkaido University. Prior to DNA extraction, fecal samples were pretreated to separate the microorganism from the plant residues by phosphate-buffered saline resuspension and high-speed centrifugation [42, 44]. Fecal DNA was extracted following a slightly modified protocol based on Hayakawa et al. (2018) [18], by using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN N.V., Hilden, Germany) with bead-beating and enzymatic digestion. The extracted DNA concentrations were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using bacterial universal primers (515F/806R), followed by dual-index library preparation, purification, and sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform using the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (2 × 301-bp), according to the Illumina (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) 16S metagenomic sequencing protocol and previous studies [22, 25]. We have deposited short-read sequences in the DNA Data Bank of Japan database with accession number PRJDB37503. Metadata of the samples are available in Supplementary Table 1.

In the bioinformatics procedures, bcl2fastq version 2.20.0.422 (Illumina, Inc.), Claident version 0.9.2022.03.23 [36], FastQC version 0.12.1 [2], QIIME2 version 2025.4 [4], and R version 4.4.1 software were used. Sequenced reads were demultiplexed and quality checked, and sequences with low-quality scores were filtered out before merging. Sequences were denoised following the DADA2 workflow, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were summarized using feature tables. Taxonomic classification was annotated using the pre-trained classifier Greengenes [14, 29, 30, 15]. The feature table of ASV abundances and assigned taxonomy is available in Github (https://github.com/hayatak/Gut-microbiome-in-a-giant-panda-with-cardiovascular-disease). Alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted based on a rooted phylogenetic tree constructed with the align-to-tree-mafft-fasttree pipeline. Differential abundance analysis was conducted using ANCOM-BC2 [26, 27], while functional profiles were predicted using the q2-picrust2 pipeline, based on the Enzyme Commission, KEGG Orthology, and MetaCyc Pathway databases [6]. Additionally, the microbial interaction networks were inferred using the Sparse Correlations for Compositional data (SparCC) algorithm and visualized using igraph version 2.1.2 in R [11, 12, 15, 34].

During meals in 2021, the patient remained awake, while sleeping at other times, demonstrating normal behavior. During summer, her weight increased by approximately 10 kg above her baseline due to fluid retention such as ascites, accompanied by decreased physical activity, shorter eating periods, and longer sleep. By late September, as ascites worsened, her weight had increased by approximately 20 kg above baseline, accompanied by reduced nighttime food intake, indicative of decreased appetite. Since the initiation of ascites drainage procedures in 2022, the individual’s body weight has progressively normalized, accompanied by an improvement in appetite and increased activity levels. By March 2022, however, the animal’s appetite and activity levels declined once more, its sleep duration increased, and it consumed bamboo only a few times a month. In 2023, activity time increased slightly. From 2024 onward, aside from taking medication, cleaning the room, and undergoing morning health checks, it predominantly slept throughout the day and did not consume bamboo at all. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

A total of 152 fecal samples collected between May 2021 and March 2024 were evaluated, two samples (221022 and 231104) with fewer than 10,000 reads were excluded from further analysis (Supplementary Table 2). The temporal data series was divided into three approximately equal time-based stages, specifically stage 1 (May 2021 to March 2022), stage 2 (April 2022 to March 2023), and stage 3 (April 2023 to March 2024). We performed between-group comparisons of gut bacterial composition across the three stages to identify changes in gut bacterial composition over time and in relation to disease.

Microbial diversity decreased progressively over time. The quantity of gut bacteria (at the genus level) in TanTan remarkably decreased from stage 2; however, few new bacteria (at the genus level) were introduced during stages 2 and 3 (Fig. 1). Bacterial genera constituting less than 1% of the samples were excluded, and the counts of the remaining bacterial genera are depicted in the Venn diagram (Fig. 1B) [19]. We identified 64 bacterial genera specific to stage 1 that were lost in stage 2; however, five and six specific bacterial genera occurred in stages 2 and 3, respectively. Additionally, we considered bacterial genera occurring in more than 50% of the stages to be common bacterial genera. The numbers of these common bacterial genera detected across all three stages are depicted in the Venn diagram (Fig. 1C). Eight bacterial genera were found in all three stages. Additionally, 25 bacterial genera were present in over half of the samples from Stage 1, whereas one bacterial genus was detected in over half of the samples from Stage 3.

Alpha diversity indices, including observed features, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, Shannon diversity, and Pielou’s evenness, showed a remarkable decrease from stage 1 to stage 3, as indicated by the pairwise Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni correction (Supplementary Fig. 2). Between stage 1 and stage 2, a discernible decreasing trend in bacterial richness was observed, while no remarkable variation was detected in evenness. However, from stages 2 to 3, bacterial species richness decreased slightly, accompanied by a pronounced loss of evenness (Supplementary Fig. 2D).

The principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Jaccard and unweighted UniFrac distance exhibits a horizontal distribution from early (red) to late (blue) disease stages along axis 1 (Supplementary Fig. 3A and 3C). Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac distances showed similar patterns. Samples collected prior to 2023 (red and yellow) demonstrate a broader geographic dispersion, indicating greater variability in species composition and abundance among fecal samples from that earlier period. Conversely, samples from 2023 to 2024 (green and blue), are clustered on the right side of axis 1, indicating reduced species and abundance in the later period. When these samples are grouped by the stages, the four beta diversity indices show statistically significant differences between each pair of stages (P<0.001, pairwise test of permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA)) (Supplementary Tables 3–6).

The composition of gut microbiota is diverse, with sequencing reads derived from samples produced from amplicons containing hundreds or thousands of distinct features. Compared with stage 1, stage 3 experienced a remarkable reduction, losing 10 taxonomic groups (Fig. 2D): the genus Acidaminococcus, the family Pasteurellaceae, the genus Lardizabala, the genus Dialister, the genus Lachnospira, the order Lactobacillales, the family Clostridiaceae, the genus Turicibacter, the genus SMB53, and the order Streptophyta. These bacterial groups include strains that have been reported to be involved in immune regulation, anti-inflammatory responses, and the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [17, 37], whereas others are involved in fat accumulation [1, 8]. They also include probiotics that contribute to gut environment balance.

We employed the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) to predict the functional abundance of gut bacterial functions in this individual giant panda, utilizing microbiome marker gene sequences to elucidate the evolutionary changes associated with disease progression. A total of 421 metabolic pathways were inferred across all fecal samples, with 100 pathways showing notable variations in relative abundance over the study period (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 7, 8). The increasing pathways associated with cardiovascular diseases, such as atherosclerosis and heart failure, include the following: l-carnitine degradation I (CARNMET-PWY), methylglyoxal degradation (METHGLYUT-PWY), 2-methylcitrate cycle II (PWY-5747), 2-methylcitrate cycle I (PWY0-42), and succinate fermentation to butanoate (PWY-5677). Conversely, decreasing pathways linked to cardiovascular diseases encompass l-lysine fermentation to acetate and butanoate (P163-PWY), the super pathway of phenylethylamine degradation (PWY-6071), and phenylacetate degradation (PWY-0-321) (Supplementary Table 9).

The interactions among gut bacterial taxonomic groups across the three developmental stages exhibit notable alterations (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 10). Twenty bacterial taxa were consistently identified throughout all stages, including the family Clostridiaceae, the genus SMB53, the genus Caloramator, the genus Actinomyces, the order Streptophyta, the genus Lardizabala, the kingdom Bacteria, the genus Psychrobacter, the class Bacilli, the family Lachnospiraceae, the genus Clostridium, and the genus Epulopiscium. These 12 taxa may represent the core constituents of the gut microbial community in giant pandas.

As the disease progressed, the number of nodes in the gut bacterial network decreased significantly, with 65, 29, and 24 bacterial taxa observed across the three stages. Several taxa experienced temporary disappearance in stage 2 and were recovered in stage 3: the genus Escherichia, the genus Turicibacter, the family Lactobacillaceae, the class Betaproteobacteria, and the genus Brachymonas. In addition, stage 2 also saw a brief appearance of six groups: Rhodococcus, Sinomonas, Cohnella, Arcanobacterium, Microbacterium, and Paenibacillus.

However, the network structure also became less connected over time (Supplementary Fig. 5). From stage 1 to stage 2, with the disruptions among following taxa: the phylum Firmicutes, the family Lactobacillaceae, the class Betaproteobacteria; the genus Klebsiella, the genus Aggregatibacter, the genus Turicibacter; the family Clostridiaceae, the genus SMB53, the genus Actinomyces; the order Actinomycetales, the kingdom Bacteria, the family Rhizobiaceae, the order Rhizobiales, the connection between the taxa, the genus Kaistobacter, and the genus Hymenobacter, resulting in the fragmentation of the gut bacterial community in stages 2 and 3, lacking the ability for comprehensive information transmission, compared with the relatively integrated network structure observed in stage 1.

The gastrointestinal drugs A and B and the cardiovascular drugs B, C, E, and F primarily were predominatly negatively associated with bacterial abundance, with the most substantial impacts on bacterial abundance occurring at 0 days, 1 day, 3 days, 3 days, 1 day, and 2 days following drug ingestion, respectively. Cardiovascular drug A and mineral supplement B predominantly enhance the proliferation of gut bacteria, with the most remarkable impact observed on the day of administration. Conversely, cardiovascular drug D and mineral supplement A exert a marginal effect on the bacterial population, reaching their peak influence at 2 days and on the day of treatment, respectively.

In this study, we monitored the gut bacterial dynamics of a captive giant panda with cardiovascular disease over 3 consecutive years. Our findings showed that gut microbial diversity decreased markedly with the progression of cardiovascular disease. Several bacterial compositions changed during the disease progression may be associated with cardiovascular disease, while some may also be related to medication administration.

However, this study focused on a single individual with cardiovascular disease but lacked a healthy control group within the same captive setting. Although the results provide preliminary insight into trends in gut bacterial changes during the disease state, they are not representative or applicable because of the limited sample size. Because the detailed composition of the drugs is not publicly accessible, this study cannot examine the causal relationships and mechanisms responsible for the microbial changes. Functional inference from 16S rRNA data is difficult to reliably infer specific metabolites. Therefore, the functional interpretations in this study should be considered indicative trends in potential metabolic capacity rather than direct evidence of metabolic activity.

Future studies should increase the sample size by including more diseased animals from the Ursidae family and healthy controls to improve statistical significance. Integrating drug information with gut microbiome metabolomics data obtained from forthcoming pharmacological reports may clarify the microbiome’s biological response to medical intervention. While functional predictions should be complemented by experimental validation such as metabolomic analyses. Although ex situ conservation is having a positive impact on the preservation and rehabilitation of many wildlife populations, more attention should be given to health issues such as high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease, which are prone to developing in captivity. Consequently, protecting microbial diversity in captive animals will be equally crucial as safeguarding species’ numbers and genetic diversity. Consistently maintaining the gut microbiome’s diversity and metabolic functions similar to those of wild populations can reduce unhealthy conditions in captivity and enhance the reintroduction of captive animals into their native habitats.
