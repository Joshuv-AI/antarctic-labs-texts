Promoting reconciliation through research
Authors: Pamela H Orr
Published: 2026

Key points

Researchers who partner with Indigenous communities have a duty to promote reconciliation through the grounding of research in acknowledgement of past and current harms, and through action to repair harm, suffering, and inequity.

In this issue of CMAJ, Geboe and colleagues present the results of a qualitative community-based participatory study of Inuit experiences of the ongoing epidemic of tuberculosis in Nunavik, Quebec.1 The voices in this study are powerful, clear, and compelling. They speak to changes that must be made to current health programs in Canada, based on 4 themes that emerged from participant interviews, and touch the heart of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada’s (TRC’s) calls to action in health and health care. In Geboe and colleagues’ work, the “medium” (methodology) by which this research was conducted is also the message. Non-Indigenous researchers, of which I am one, can learn from their conduct in several important ways.

Canada’s Tri-Council federal research agencies — the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, and the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council — have published a framework for ethical research involving Indigenous Peoples (https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html). This policy statement provides core principles for non-Indigenous researchers who are contemplating research with Indigenous Peoples. But researchers must reach deeper into the specific ways of seeing and being among the Indigenous Peoples with whom they wish to partner. The Tri-Council states its framework does not “override or replace” the ethical guidance and principles offered by Indigenous groups and communities. Inuit, First Nations, and Métis Peoples have published their own research guidelines, and among these groups, particular communities have their own ways of doing what they have always done: asking questions and looking for answers.2–4

To reach deeper, researchers must begin with relationships. The academic researchers in the related study heard and responded to Nunavik individuals, families, and communities who have suffered from endemic and epidemic tuberculosis for more than a century. Their understanding, empathy, and ability to respond was nurtured and supported by their pre-existing relationships of research and health care delivery in communities over many years.5 The establishment of relationship comes first, prior to research.6 What is meant by “relationship” may vary according to community. Inuit have used various descriptors, including listening, respecting, contributing, and “offering a piece of ourselves.”7

Relationships emerge within an Ethical Space. Willie Ermine, a scholar and Elder from the Sturgeon Lake First Nation in Saskatchewan, described Ethical Space, in which societies with disparate worldviews may engage in a respectful relationship.8 The concept of Ethical Space is applicable wherever and whenever there is engagement between people who have different ways of seeing and being. This space is not entered with the intent to persuade or to create consensus or agreement; consensus may or may not emerge as a consequence. The purpose of engagement within the Ethical Space is the creation of respectful relationships.

Scholar and researcher Shawn Wilson, from the Opaskwayak Cree Nation in northern Manitoba, described research as a ceremony.9 Rather than seeing research as an endeavour designed to arrive at an answer, Wilson describes research as an act, a ceremony, of relationship with others and all the worlds around us. In this view, the act of research is to apprehend reality not as an object but as relationship.9

Research must also be grounded in Indigenous community values. Geboe and colleagues embedded their research in Kirkness and Barnhardt’s 4 Rs concept of respect, relevance, reciprocity, and responsibility, originally described in the context of Indigenous education.10 However, their methods also speak specifically and directly to values expressed in the Inuit “great laws” (Maligarjuat) — respectful relationships, prioritization of community well-being, and the duty to plan for the future.11 The researchers maintained allegiance to Inuit values through a steering committee of Inuit experienced in community or health leadership. The committee was devised at project inception and imbued with authority. Validity of interpretation was fostered through reflexive analysis of results through the lenses of team, community members, and leaders.

Whether research occurs at all and, if so, what priorities are chosen, depends on a true understanding of community values and worldviews. Communities may question the appropriateness of doing health research at all when they experience little or no action based on solutions already known to have the potential to improve health and well-being. Within the Ethical Space, values may or may not align; something extraordinary may or may not occur. Based on a shared reverence for the well-being of Indigenous children and shared recognition of systemic harm they have suffered, Sinclair and colleagues at the University of British Columbia formed research partnerships with northern Indigenous communities to investigate the role of carnitine palmitoyl-transferase 1A metabolic gene variants in child health.12

Beyond the “what” of research priorities lies the “how” of methodology. Values determine how we arrive at answers that hold truth, and, as First Nations scholar Margaret Kovach notes, the validity we ascribe to our methods.13,14 Speaking to different ways to search for, and recognize, truth, a Dene man once asked me whether a person had to believe in the methods of science in order for the results to be true. Within an Ethical Space, there is respect for, and opportunity to learn from, systems of knowledge that challenge us all to see in new ways.15

When working with Indigenous partners, non-Indigenous researchers have a specific duty to do the work of reconciliation. Geboe and colleagues do this through engaging in mutual relationship with community, bringing awareness to past and present harms in the context of epidemic tuberculosis in Nunavik, and outlining the steps they have taken to try to achieve policy change through their research findings. They move from acknowledgement to action in the work of reconciliation.

In alignment with the TRC’s calls to action, the authors of the related research identify 4 themes from participant interviews: a strong desire for healthy communities in the setting of chronically underresourced health services; undue hardship experienced by some Inuit during tuberculosis care; the negative effects of information and knowledge gaps in communities; and the recommendation for implementation of a new effective tuberculosis program of prevention and care based on Inuit principles of sovereignty, culture, and well-being. Non-Indigenous researchers, health and health care workers, administrators, and governments can take steps toward reconciliation by listening to — and acting on — these messages.
