Population trends in nutritional status and socioeconomic divergence in extreme obesity in Peru: geospatial dynamics and intergenerational risk
Authors: Jhosmer Ballena-Caicedo, Víctor Juan Vera-Ponce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeru is undergoing a sustained nutrition transition; however, evidence on the simultaneous behavior of extreme phenotypes — geriatric underweight and class III obesity — and their generational determinants remains limited. We aimed to analyze the temporal, geospatial, socioeconomic, and intergenerational dynamics of population malnutrition over the last decade.MethodsThis was a repeated cross-sectional, population-based analytical trend study with age–period–cohort (APC) analysis, using data from the Peruvian Demographic and Family Health Survey (DHS) 2014–2024 (excluding 2020), including 257,264 individuals aged ≥15 years. Nutritional status was assessed using body mass index (BMI) and life-stage–specific cut-offs (adolescents: 2007 World Health Organization BMI-for-age Z-scores; adults: World Health Organization BMI criteria; older adults: Pan American Health Organization/Salud, Bienestar y Envejecimiento and Peruvian Ministry of Health criteria). Temporal trends were estimated using average annual percent change (AAPC) from weighted log-linear regressions. Economic inequality was quantified with the Erreygers Concentration Index (ECI). Generational risk was estimated through an APC model restricted to adults aged 20–80 years for class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2).ResultsAmong adults, normal weight prevalence fell markedly (AAPC −3.4%/year; p < 0.001), while class III obesity showed the largest proportional increase (+8.9%/year; p < 0.001). Underweight remained close to 20% in older adults by 2024, reflecting a persistent geriatric double burden. ECI showed that extreme obesity, initially concentrated in wealthier quintiles, spread toward lower and middle strata, though the pro-rich gradient persisted (ECI 2014: 0.008; ECI 2024: 0.011; both p = 0.002). Cohorts born in the 1990s and 2000s showed substantially higher relative rates of class III obesity compared with the 1970 reference cohort at equivalent biological age, reflecting a marked unfavorable generational shift.ConclusionsPeru exhibits a divergent double burden of malnutrition. The rise in class III obesity spans multiple socioeconomic strata, disproportionately affects recent cohorts, and coexists with persistently high underweight in the geriatric population.Keywords: Obesity, Body mass index, Nutritional transition, Socioeconomic inequality, Peru

Introduction

The double burden of malnutrition—defined as the coexistence of undernutrition and excess weight within the same population—has become a hallmark of the nutrition transition in low- and middle-income countries. Globally, between 1990 and 2022, increases in the combined burden of underweight and obesity were driven mainly by the expansion of obesity, while underweight persists in socially and territorially vulnerable subgroups [1,2].

Peru provides a particularly relevant setting to study this process. In recent decades, the country has undergone demographic, urban, and epidemiological changes that have gradually shifted its health profile toward noncommunicable diseases. In parallel, national evidence shows that the double burden and excess weight are not evenly distributed but vary markedly by urbanization, socioeconomic status, and territory [[3], [4], [5]].

However, important gaps remain for epidemiological surveillance. The available regional and Peruvian literature has focused mainly on overall obesity or abdominal adiposity, limiting the identification of clinically complex phenotypes such as class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2) [6,7]. This category is associated with substantially higher mortality and with management needs that exceed conventional care for excess weight [8,9]. In addition, among older adults BMI does not carry the same clinical meaning as in younger adults, and malnutrition remains a common and clinically relevant problem in geriatrics; therefore, the lower end of the nutritional spectrum requires a specific assessment [10].

Against this background, we aimed to analyze temporal trends in the nutritional status spectrum by life stage among the Peruvian population aged ≥15 years during 2014–2019 and 2021–2024. Peru represents a relevant sentinel case for this inquiry: it is among the few low- and middle-income countries with a decade-long series of nationally representative surveys using objectively measured anthropometry, making it well-suited to document patterns that are likely emerging — or already present — across comparable settings in Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa, and South and Southeast Asia. The analytical framework applied here — combining temporal trend estimation, socioeconomic inequality indices, and age–period–cohort (APC) modeling — is directly transferable to other countries with Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data, providing a replicable model for surveillance of the nutritional double burden.

Methods

Study design

This was a repeated cross-sectional, population-based analytical trend study using APC analysis, based on publicly available secondary data from the Peruvian DHS. The analysis included ten annual rounds from 2014 to 2019 and 2021–2024; the year 2020 was excluded a priori because the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted standardized in-person collection of anthropometric measurements, compromising comparability across rounds. This manuscript is reported in accordance with STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) [11], whose checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Data source

We used publicly available microdata from the Peruvian DHS, conducted by Peru's National Institute of Statistics and Informatics (INEI). DHS is a cross-sectional survey with a complex, two-stage, probabilistic, stratified sampling design, with inference at the national level and by urban/rural area and department (24 departments and the Constitutional Province of Callao).

In the first stage, clusters are selected with probability proportional to size; in the second stage, households are selected from the listing, following the operational scheme described by INEI.

For this study, we used the questionnaire/module administered to the selected household member aged ≥15 years, which includes anthropometric measurements (weight and height) collected in the field by trained personnel using digital scales and stadiometers, following standardized procedures and quality control. The datasets are publicly available through INEI's microdata portal.

Population and sample

The source population comprised individuals aged ≥15 years selected for the DHS health questionnaire in private households in Peru, in the 2014–2019 and 2021–2024 survey rounds.

We included participants with valid weight and height measurements to compute body mass index (BMI). We excluded (a) women who were pregnant at the time of the survey, because BMI is not interpretable during pregnancy; (b) individuals with biologically implausible anthropometric values (BMI <10 or >65 kg/m2), thresholds used as a data-cleaning rule to minimize measurement or data-entry errors; and (c) participants with missing data in key stratification covariates (area of residence, natural region, or wealth).

Variables

Dependent variable: nutritional status spectrum

The primary dependent variable was the nutritional status spectrum, assessed using BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Because the clinical and epidemiological meaning of BMI varies across the life course, we applied life-stage–specific cut-offs based on international guidelines and normative recommendations, using constant classification rules over time to enable valid temporal comparisons within each age group. Accordingly, categories are interpreted primarily within each life stage and are not assumed to be strictly comparable across stages.

In adolescents (15–19 years), we used BMI-for-age Z-scores (zBMI) from the World Health Organization (WHO) 2007 reference, specific to exact age in months and sex [12]. To describe the spectrum and assess severity trends within the WHO framework, we defined six categories: underweight (Z < −2 SD), normal weight (−2 ≤ Z ≤ +1 SD), overweight (+1 < Z ≤ +2 SD), and three severity strata within obesity based on zBMI: obesity (+2 < Z ≤ +3 SD), severe obesity (+3 < Z ≤ +4 SD), and extremely severe obesity (Z > +4 SD). These strata represent operational BMI-for-age severity subcategories and were used for descriptive and trend purposes (to characterize the shift toward the right tail); therefore, they should not be interpreted as direct clinical equivalents of the absolute BMI-based obesity classes used in adults.

Among adults (20–59 years), we used the standard WHO BMI criteria [13], defining six categories: underweight (BMI <18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), obesity class I (30.0–34.9), obesity class II (35.0–39.9), and obesity class III (extreme) (BMI ≥40.0 kg/m2). This classification was kept constant across all annual rounds included in the temporal analysis, ensuring longitudinal comparability within the study period.

In older adults (≥60 years), we applied normative anthropometric nutritional assessment criteria recommended for this life stage, because using cut-offs for younger adults may underestimate the risk associated with low body weight in senescence [10,14]. We acknowledge that BMI cut-offs for older adults are not yet universally standardized; however, the use of higher underweight thresholds in this age group is supported by regional evidence from the Pan American Health Organization/Salud, Bienestar y Envejecimiento (PAHO/SABE) studies across Latin America and is consistent with the clinical position of the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) practical guideline, which recognizes that standard World Health Organization (WHO) adult cut-offs may not adequately capture malnutrition risk in older populations [10]. The cut-offs applied here follow the normative technical guide issued jointly by Peru's Ministry of Health and National Institute of Health [14] and are interpreted descriptively within this life stage rather than as an assertion of physiological equivalence with adult WHO criteria. We defined: underweight (BMI ≤23.0), normal weight (BMI >23.0 to <28.0), overweight (BMI ≥28.0 to <32.0), and (geriatric) obesity (BMI ≥32.0). To maintain a six-category spectrum and describe severity at the upper extreme using absolute thresholds of high clinical relevance, the geriatric obesity category (BMI ≥32.0) was further stratified into: obesity 32.0–34.9, severe obesity 35.0–39.9, and extreme obesity (BMI ≥40.0 kg/m2). This sub-stratification was implemented for descriptive and operational purposes and should be interpreted as severity stratification based on absolute thresholds, without assuming full physiological equivalence across life stages.

Independent variables and stratification

Stratification variables included: age group (adolescents, adults, and older adults), sex (men/women), area of residence (urban/rural), natural region (Lima Metropolitan Area, rest of the Coast, Highlands, and Jungle), cluster altitude (<2500 and ≥ 2500 m above sea level), educational attainment (primary or less, secondary, and higher), and household wealth index (quintiles Q1–Q5). The wealth index corresponds to the DHS/INEI indicator constructed using principal component analysis of household assets and characteristics. In this study, we used the annual version of the wealth quintile (cut-offs based on each round's distribution); therefore, it should be interpreted as a relative ranking within each survey year rather than an absolute measure directly comparable across years [15].

Operational definition of “extremes” for socioeconomic inequality

To quantify socioeconomic inequality at the extremes of the nutritional spectrum, we defined two binary outcomes using fixed life-stage–specific criteria. Extreme deficit (underweight) was defined according to each age group's cut-off: in adolescents, Z < −2 SD; in adults, BMI <18.5; and in older adults, BMI ≤23.0.

Severe excess adiposity was defined differently by life stage to ensure interpretability and estimation stability. In adults and older adults, we used the BMI-based definition of extreme obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2). In adolescents, because the most extreme zBMI strata were rare (particularly Z > +4 SD), the inequality analysis used WHO-defined obesity (Z > +2 SD) as the outcome, which yields more robust estimates of the concentration index. Accordingly, adolescent severity strata (Z > +2 to ≤ +3; > +3 to ≤ +4; > +4) are reported and analyzed separately within the spectrum, whereas the inequality analysis focuses on a stable binary outcome and is explicitly interpreted as a life-stage–specific construct, without assuming mathematical or physiological equivalence with BMI ≥40 kg/m2 in adults.

Across all rounds included in the temporal analysis (2014–2019 and 2021–2024), these classification rules and operational definitions were applied identically, without year-specific modifications, ensuring longitudinal comparability within each life stage and consistency across descriptive, trend, inequality, and generational analyses.

Statistical analysis

All estimates explicitly incorporated DHS’ complex sampling design using sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units (PSUs; clusters). Variances and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated using Taylor series linearization. In strata with a single PSU, the singleunit (centered) option was applied to preserve variance estimation under the complex design.

In each annual round, we estimated the weighted prevalence of the outcome pˆt and its standard error SE(pˆt), accounting for the survey's complex design. Counts are reported as unweighted absolute frequencies, and proportions as weighted prevalences with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Time was defined as the observed calendar year (2014–2019 and 2021–2024); the year 2020 was excluded a priori and the missing point was not interpolated.

To assess whether observed changes could be attributed to shifts in age structure, we conducted a sensitivity analysis among adults aged 20–59 years by estimating crude and age-standardized prevalences using the direct method, with the WHO World Standard Population (2000–2025) in 5-year age groups [16]. Interpretation focused on whether standardization materially changed the magnitude or direction of the observed changes.

Socioeconomic inequality in the nutritional extremes was assessed using the Erreygers Concentration Index (ECI) for binary outcomes [18]. Positive values indicate pro-rich concentration and negative values indicate pro-poor concentration. The ECI was estimated annually incorporating survey weights and design-based variances, and interpreted alongside quintile-specific prevalences. Because the DHS wealth index is a relative ranking constructed within each annual round, the ECI reflects the concentration pattern along that year's socioeconomic gradient rather than absolute wealth levels comparable across years.

To characterize generational patterns of extreme excess adiposity, we fitted an APC model restricted to individuals aged 20–80 years and the outcome of class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2), following Carstensen's framework for Lexis diagrams [19]. This age range was selected to ensure diagnostic homogeneity — as it applies a single uniform adult BMI criterion throughout — and adequate weighted cell counts across age × period strata. Adolescents were excluded because their nutritional classification uses WHO 2007 BMI-for-age z-scores, which are not comparable with the absolute BMI ≥40 kg/m2 threshold; individuals over 80 years were excluded due to sparse data and potential survivorship bias from weight loss associated with terminal chronic disease, which would distort cohort effect estimates. To address the inherent identifiability problem in APC models, we used Carstensen's parameterization, separating the shared linear component (drift) from the non-linear curvatures. Results were interpreted as structural prevalence patterns rather than individual-level causal inference.

Geospatial dynamics were described for adults aged 20–80 years using annual department-level prevalence estimates of class III obesity, interpreted as territorial distribution patterns rather than point comparisons between departments in a single year. Given the low frequency of the outcome in several subnational cells, interpretation emphasized temporal consistency of the pattern over isolated year-to-year fluctuations. Consistent with the a priori analytic exclusion, the year 2020 was omitted from the departmental visualization.

Data cleaning, prevalence estimation under the complex survey design, and the main analytical processing were performed in Stata v17, whereas APC estimation, APC modeling (Epi package), and visualizations were conducted in R v4.3. A two-sided significance threshold of p<0.05 was used, while also prioritizing effect size, internal consistency, and epidemiological plausibility, particularly for rare outcomes and subnational analyses.

Ethical considerations

This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available DHS microdata accessible through INEI's microdata system. The files used do not contain direct personal identifiers, and analyses were performed and reported exclusively in aggregate form. Under Peruvian regulations and international guidelines, this analysis does not constitute human subjects research with risk and does not require institutional ethics committee approval.

Results

Participant selection

Of 280,391 records from DHS rounds 2014–2019 and 2021–2024, 7898 were excluded due to missing anthropometric data or out-of-range values, 5240 due to age <15 years, and 9989 due to pregnancy, yielding a final analytical sample of 257,264 observations of individuals aged ≥15 years with valid anthropometry (Fig. S1).

Sample characteristics and demographic transition

Comparing the first and last round of the period (2014 vs 2024), sociodemographic characteristics were consistent with a demographic transition. The urban population proportion increased from 75.1% (95% CI 73.4–76.8) in 2014 to 82.6% (95% CI 81.4–83.8) in 2024. The age composition showed population ageing, with the proportion of older adults increasing from 17.2% (16.4–17.9) to 19.3% (18.4–20.2). With respect to education, the proportion with higher education increased from 29.4% (28.1–30.6) to 35.0% (33.7–36.2).

Additionally, there was a relative increase in population concentration in Lima Metropolitan Area (31.4% [29.2–33.5] in 2014 vs 37.4% [35.2–39.6] in 2024) and a reduction in the proportion residing in the Highlands (30.4% [28.4–32.5] vs 23.9% [22.4–25.5]), along with a decrease in the proportion living at altitudes ≥2500 m above sea level (23.4% [21.5–25.4] vs 20.2% [18.8–21.6]) (Table 1).

Structural changes in nutritional status

Because nutritional status categories were defined using life-stage–specific cut-offs, changes in the spectrum were interpreted within each age group. Among adolescents (15–19 years), normal weight decreased slightly from 62.7% (95% CI 60.1–65.4) to 60.8% (58.1–63.6); overweight remained stable (23.8% at both time points), whereas severe obesity increased from 1.3% (0.8–1.8) to 2.4% (1.4–3.4). Extremely severe obesity remained rare (0.5%–0.4%).

Among adults (20–59 years), normal weight decreased from 36.3% (35.2–37.4) to 27.6% (26.6–28.6), while higher-severity categories increased stepwise: obesity class I from 16.3% (15.5–17.1) to 21.5% (20.5–22.5), obesity class II from 3.9% (3.4–4.3) to 5.6% (5.1–6.2), and obesity class III from 1.1% (0.9–1.4) to 2.1% (1.7–2.4). Overweight showed little change (41.2%–41.9%).

Among older adults (≥60 years), underweight decreased from 22.6% (20.9–24.3) to 18.2% (16.1–20.4), while the upper categories increased: geriatric obesity from 7.3% (6.1–8.5) to 9.6% (8.0–11.2), severe obesity from 4.9% (3.8–6.1) to 6.3% (5.0–7.6), and extreme obesity from 1.0% (0.6–1.3) to 1.3% (0.7–2.0). Normal weight remained relatively stable (39.6%–40.7%) (Table 1).

This shift toward higher BMI values was consistent with the density visualization (ridge plot), which showed an expansion of the right tail of the distribution, particularly among adult and older adult women (Fig. S2).

In the demographic sensitivity analysis restricted to adults (20–59 years), crude and age-standardized prevalences were virtually identical for both underweight and class III obesity in 2014 and 2024. For underweight, estimates were 1.26% versus 1.27% in 2014 and 1.28% versus 1.27% in 2024; for class III obesity, 1.12% versus 1.10% in 2014 and 2.05% versus 2.03% in 2024. Overall, these results suggest that the magnitude and direction of the observed changes in adults are not materially altered after age standardization (Table S4).

Temporal rate of change and life-stage dynamics

Temporal trajectories of the nutritional spectrum (2014–2019 and 2021–2024) differed by life stage (Fig. 1). The rate of change was summarized using annual percent change (APC) estimated with inverse-variance–weighted log-linear regression (Table S2).

Trends across the full nutritional status spectrum by life stage (Peruvian Demographic and Family Health Survey [DHS], 2014–2019 and 2021–2024). Points show annual weighted prevalence estimates and shaded bands represent 95% confidence intervals. Nutritional categories were defined using life-stage–specific cut-offs. The line connecting 2019 and 2021 is for visual purposes only and does not represent probabilistic interpolation of the excluded year 2020.

Among adults (20–59 years), normal weight declined steadily (APC −3.4% per year; 95% CI −4.4 to −2.4). In contrast, increases were proportional to the severity of excess weight: obesity class I (APC +3.3% per year; 95% CI + 2.0 to +4.5), class II (APC +4.8% per year; 95% CI + 2.7 to +6.9), and class III (APC +8.9% per year; 95% CI + 6.2 to +11.7), representing the fastest growth rate in the spectrum for this life stage (Table S2).

Among older adults (≥60 years), underweight decreased significantly (APC −3.9% per year; 95% CI −6.4 to −1.4; p = 0.008) but continued to affect a substantial fraction by 2024 (Fig. 1). Simultaneously, obesity class II increased significantly (APC +4.0% per year; 95% CI + 0.9 to +7.1; p = 0.016) and obesity class III showed an upward trend (APC +6.3% per year; 95% CI −0.3 to +13.2; p = 0.058) (Table S2).

Among adolescents (15–19 years), normal weight showed a moderate but significant decline (APC −1.3% per year; 95% CI −2.5 to −0.1; p = 0.040). Obesity severity categories defined by zBMI (WHO 2007) showed positive directions of change, although with wide intervals and without conclusive statistical evidence for the most extreme strata, consistent with their low absolute frequency (Table S2; Fig. 1).

Sex and territorial disparities in excess weight

Sex-stratified analyses showed a female predominance at the upper end of the spectrum. Among adults, the prevalence of class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2) was higher in women than in men in 2014 (1.4% [95% CI 1.1–1.8] vs 0.8% [0.4–1.1]) and in 2024 (3.0% [2.3–3.6] vs 1.1% [0.8–1.4]). Among older adults, prevalence was also higher in women (2024: 2.0% [0.9–3.1]) than in men (0.5% [0.0–1.1]). Among adolescents, where the upper extreme corresponds to WHO 2007 obesity (zBMI > +2 SD), women had higher prevalences than men at both time points (2014: 16.1% [13.0–19.1] vs 5.7% [3.9–7.5]; 2024: 15.1% [12.3–17.8] vs 9.1% [6.5–11.7]) (Table S1; Fig. S3).

Territorially, urban areas maintained higher absolute levels at the upper extreme, particularly among adults (class III obesity: 1.3% [1.0–1.6] in 2014 and 2.3% [1.8–2.7] in 2024). However, class III obesity among adults also increased in rural areas (0.5% [0.3–0.7] to 0.9% [0.5–1.2]). Among adults, we also observed a territorial gradient consistent with higher prevalence in Lima Metropolitan Area and the coastal belt (2024: 2.9% [2.0–3.7] in Lima Metropolitan Area and 2.2% [1.7–2.7] in the rest of the Coast) compared with the Highlands (1.0% [0.6–1.4]) (Table S1; Fig. S4).

Shift in socioeconomic inequalities

Socioeconomic inequalities at the nutritional extremes were described by wealth index quintiles using equiplots (Fig. 2) and quantified annually using the ECI (Table S3). Consistent with the study design, the ECI is reported for 2014–2019 and 2021–2024. Underweight among older adults remained strongly concentrated in the lowest wealth strata. In 2014, the poorest quintile had 50.5% [95% CI 47.3–53.8] versus 11.3% [8.1–14.5] in the richest quintile; in 2024, although prevalence decreased, a marked gap persisted (34.8% [31.6–38.0] vs 11.6% [6.5–16.8]) (Fig. 2; Table S1). Consistently, the ECI for geriatric underweight was negative and statistically significant in all years (p < 0.001), confirming persistent pro-poor concentration (Table S3).

In contrast, class III obesity among adults showed a pattern consistent with diffusion toward lower and middle quintiles. In 2014, prevalence was 0.4% [0.1–0.8] in Q1 and 0.8% [0.4–1.1] in Q2, with higher values in upper quintiles (e.g., Q4: 1.6% [0.9–2.3]). In 2024, increases were observed in Q1–Q3 (Q1: 0.6% [0.4–0.9]; Q2: 1.7% [1.1–2.3]; Q3: 2.5% [1.7–3.3]) and a peak in Q4 (3.5% [2.2–4.8]), with a less monotonic gradient (Q5: 1.5% [0.8–2.2]) (Fig. 2; Table S1). Overall, the ECI remained positive and statistically significant in 2014 (0.008; p = 0.002) and 2024 (0.011; p = 0.002), indicating a small but non-zero pro-rich concentration; however, the quintile redistribution suggests that the phenomenon has expanded beyond the highest strata (Table S3).

Among adolescents, the “extreme excess” outcome corresponds to WHO 2007 obesity (zBMI > +2 SD). Prevalences were higher in middle-to-upper wealth quintiles at both time points (2024: Q4 17.1% [11.5–22.8] vs Q1 5.7% [3.8–7.6]), and the ECI was positive in most years (e.g., 2014: 0.050; p = 0.006; 2024: 0.069; p < 0.001), consistent with pro-rich concentration (Table S1; Table S3).

Intergenerational risk analysis (Age–period–cohort model, APC)

To characterize generational patterns of class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2) and disentangle components associated with age, period, and birth cohort, we fitted an APC model restricted to ages 20–80 years using observed DHS rounds (2014–2019 and 2021–2024) (Fig. 3).

Age–period–cohort (APC) model for class III obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥40.0 kg/m2) among adults aged 20–80 years (Peruvian Demographic and Family Health Survey [DHS], 2014–2019 and 2021–2024). Panel A shows the model-based age-specific prevalence, expressed as cases per 100,000 population. Panels B and C show relative rates by period and birth cohort with 95% confidence bands, using 2014 and the 1970 birth cohort as the reference categories.

The age effect (Fig. 3, Panel A) showed the expected increase in prevalence with advancing age, followed by relative stabilization at older ages. The period effect (Panel B) varied around the baseline level without a sustained monotonic pattern across observed rounds. Finally, the cohort effect (Panel C) showed a sustained increase in relative risk in more recent generations. Specifically, those born from the 1990s onward showed substantially higher relative rates of class III obesity than the reference cohort (born in 1970) at equivalent biological age, reflecting a sustained and unfavorable generational shift toward extreme excess adiposity in more recent generations.

Geographic expansion

The department-level analysis of class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2) among adults (20–59 years), displayed as a departmental heat map, showed a heterogeneous spatial distribution across the observed period. Descriptively, the highest prevalences were persistently concentrated in departments along the coastal belt (including Lima/Callao and other coastal departments), whereas several high-Andean departments showed comparatively low prevalences throughout the series (Fig. S5).

The visualization is presented for the 2014–2019 and 2021–2024 rounds (2020 excluded a priori). Because class III obesity is a low-frequency outcome in several subnational units, interpretation prioritizes temporal consistency of the pattern over isolated year-to-year fluctuations (Fig. S5).

Discussion

Main findings

First, over the past decade we observed a population shift in the nutritional spectrum toward phenotypes with greater excess weight. This shift was driven primarily by adults, among whom the proportion with normal weight dropped sharply, while class III obesity nearly doubled and exhibited the fastest temporal growth rate across the entire spectrum analyzed. In parallel, although geriatric weight deficit declined significantly, it continued to affect nearly one-fifth of older adults.

Second, we showed that these extremes operate along divergent socioeconomic and territorial gradients. Geriatric nutritional vulnerability remained strongly anchored in poverty. In stark contrast, extreme obesity among adults showed clear social diffusion: although it retained an overall pro-rich concentration, its prevalence expanded rapidly into middle and lower socioeconomic strata. Moreover, this extreme excess remained strongly sex-dimorphic to the disadvantage of women and showed a clear urbanization gradient.

Comparison with other studies

Our findings are consistent with the global and regional literature describing the double burden of malnutrition as a dynamic process in which excess weight increases more rapidly than forms of undernutrition, without the latter disappearing completely. Within this framework, the pattern observed in Peru suggests a more advanced transition toward the upper end of the spectrum in the adult population. Although previous studies had already shown that the double burden persisted in the country and remained structured by social inequality, most of that evidence focused on mother–child dyads or on aggregated obesity. For example, Santos et al. [4] documented persistent social inequities in the double burden in Peru, and Pomati et al. [20] reported that, although the prevalence of the double burden in mother–child dyads declined, the estimated number of cases remained above 200,000 and children were increasingly likely to live with mothers who were overweight or obese. In contrast, our results show that the current transition affects not only broad categories of excess weight but also its clinically most severe extreme: among adults aged 20–59 years, class III obesity nearly doubled and was the fastest-growing category, while among older adults underweight decreased from 22.6% to 18.2% but persisted alongside simultaneous increases in geriatric obesity categories. Taken together, this suggests that nutritional polarization in Peru is now more complex than that described in previous studies focused on childhood undernutrition or on non-disaggregated obesity [1,2,4,20].

The socioeconomic inequality pattern we found also aligns closely with Latin American evidence, while adding important nuances. In our study, underweight among older adults remained strongly concentrated in the poorest wealth quintiles, whereas class III obesity among adults expanded into lower and middle quintiles while retaining an overall pro-rich concentration. This configuration is compatible with the “social transition of obesity” described by Jiwani et al. in Latin America and the Caribbean, where the obesity burden progressively shifted toward less advantaged socioeconomic groups without a complete and uniform reversal of the gradient across countries. Our results refine this interpretation for Peru: rather than a total reversal, we observed partial diffusion of the upper extreme of the spectrum into lower and middle strata, while geriatric underweight remains anchored in poverty. This combination suggests that the two extremes of malnutrition do not share the same social dynamics. In addition, comparison with the study by Farro-Maldonado et al. [7] on abdominal obesity among Peruvian adults—which reported a prevalence of 73.8%, higher in women than in men (85.1% vs 61.1%)—reinforces the idea that inequalities by sex and socioeconomic position are no longer expressed only in central adiposity or overall obesity, but also in categories of greater clinical severity [6,7].

The territorial and sex distribution observed in our study is also consistent with previous work in the country. The predominance of the upper extreme of the spectrum among adult and older adult women, as well as its higher frequency in urban areas, Lima Metropolitan Area, and the coast, aligns with the Peruvian literature on adiposity and urbanization. In the PERU MIGRANT cohort, Carrillo-Larco et al. [21] showed that rural-to-urban migrants and urban residents had obesity incidence 8.19 and 9.51 times higher, respectively, than the rural population, suggesting that urban exposure substantially modifies adiposity risk in the country. Similarly, Woolcott et al. [22] found an inverse relationship between altitude and body fat–defined obesity among Peruvian adults. Our results are consistent with both studies but add two nuances: first, that this gradient is already visible specifically for class III obesity, not only for overall or abdominal obesity; and second, that the increase in rural areas—although smaller than in urban areas—indicates that the phenomenon has begun to diffuse beyond the settings where it historically concentrated. In other words, prior evidence on urbanization, migration, and altitude helps contextualize the pattern we observed, but our data suggest that the expansion of severe excess weight can no longer be understood as an exclusively urban problem [7,21,22].

The persistence of underweight among older adults, even in a context of increasing obesity in the upper categories, is consistent with recent reviews that continue to recognize malnutrition as a highly prevalent condition with a substantial burden on health and functional status in older populations. In this sense, our findings suggest that Peru faces a geriatric double burden in which deficit and excess processes coexist within the same historical period, underscoring the need to avoid linear interpretations of the nutrition transition. Finally, although comparable APC studies of severe obesity are scarce in Latin America, analyses in other settings have shown that more recent birth cohorts tend to have higher prevalences or odds of obesity than earlier cohorts. This observation is directionally consistent with the unfavorable generational shift we found for class III obesity among adults; however, in our case this interpretation should be framed as a population-level prevalence pattern rather than individual-level causal inference [23,24].

Implications for public health and clinical practice

A first clinical implication of our findings is that class III obesity should no longer be treated as a quantitative extension of general obesity and should instead be approached as a phenotype with high care complexity. In our study, among adults aged 20–59 years, class III obesity increased and exhibited the fastest growth rate in the spectrum. From a clinical practice perspective, this suggests the need to strengthen pathways for detection, risk stratification, and referral for severe obesity, including appropriate equipment, protocolized management of comorbidities, timely access to intensive therapies, and—in selected subgroups—metabolic/bariatric surgery within shared decision-making frameworks [9].

Beyond Peru, these findings carry broader implications for countries undergoing comparable stages of the nutrition transition. The simultaneous rise of class III obesity and the persistence of geriatric underweight within the same population challenges linear models of nutritional progress and suggests that health systems in low- and middle-income settings must prepare concurrently for the care demands of both extremes — rather than treating the decline of undernutrition and the rise of obesity as sequential phases. The generational concentration of extreme obesity risk in cohorts born after 1990 is particularly relevant globally: if similar birth-cohort patterns are present in other transitioning economies, the full burden of class III obesity in these populations has not yet materialized, and early regulatory and clinical action would have disproportionate preventive value. In this sense, the Peruvian data serve not only as a national baseline but as an early signal with direct relevance to global nutrition surveillance and policy.

From a public health perspective, the expansion of the upper extreme of the spectrum into lower and middle wealth quintiles and its diffusion beyond historically more advantaged settings suggest that interventions focused solely on individual education will be insufficient. In addition, severe excess weight was higher in urban than rural areas, although it also increased in rural settings, and showed higher prevalence in Lima Metropolitan Area and the coast than in the highlands. This pattern supports a double-duty actions agenda aimed at addressing shared determinants of multiple forms of malnutrition—particularly the food environment, early-life exposure, and economic barriers to accessing healthy diets. In practice, this supports integrated regulatory packages: in Chile, the first phase of the food labeling, marketing, and school sales law was associated with reductions in calories, sodium, and sugar purchased in “high-in” products; complementarily, a systematic review and meta-analysis of sugar-sweetened beverage taxes found a mean 15% reduction in sales of taxed beverages [[25], [26], [27]].

At the other extreme of the spectrum, our results suggest that the health system response cannot be limited to cardiometabolic control. Among older adults, underweight declined from 22.6% to 18.2% but remained concentrated among the poorest groups. This has direct implications for clinical practice and service organization: geriatric underweight should trigger systematic malnutrition screening, formal nutritional assessment, and individualized support, regardless of apparent body size or the simultaneous presence of chronic disease.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include the use of a series of national surveys with measured anthropometry, broad temporal coverage, and an explicit life-stage approach, which enabled differentiation of patterns that are often obscured when excess weight is reported in aggregated categories. In addition, analytic triangulation across annual prevalences, annual percent change, the ECI, and the APC model — together with the use of sampling weights and complex-design correction — strengthened the internal consistency of findings regarding the shift toward the upper extreme of the spectrum, persistence of geriatric underweight, and socioeconomic reconfiguration. Finally, the a priori exclusion of 2020 improved temporal comparability in the main analysis by avoiding inclusion of a methodologically atypical round: COVID-19 restrictions prevented standardized in-person anthropometric data collection that year, as physical distancing protocols meant that weight and height were frequently not collected following usual field procedures; any pandemic-related nutritional changes that persisted beyond the acute phase are captured in the 2021–2024 rounds included in the analysis.

Regarding limitations, first, repeated cross-sectional surveys allow description of population changes but cannot establish individual causality or exposure–outcome temporality; therefore, proposed mechanisms should be interpreted as hypotheses rather than demonstrated causal relationships. Second, although BMI is useful for population surveillance, it does not distinguish body composition or fat distribution and may underestimate sarcopenia and malnutrition in older adults; alternative measures such as waist circumference were not used because their cut-offs remain highly contested — abdominal obesity prevalence in Peruvian adults varies substantially depending on whether Adult Treatment Panel III or International Diabetes Federation criteria — introducing criterion-dependent instability into temporal comparisons. Nonetheless, at extreme values such as BMI ≥40 kg/m2 it remains a pragmatic proxy for severe excess adiposity. Third, the lack of dietary and physical activity data precludes attributing observed changes to specific exposures. Fourth, the wealth index is relative to each annual round, such that inequality changes reflect shifts along each year's socioeconomic gradient rather than absolute wealth differences between rounds. Fifth, some subnational estimates for rare outcomes such as class III obesity may be unstable; territorial visualization was therefore interpreted descriptively and with caution. Sixth, APC was estimated using a single temporal segment and was not designed to formally detect change points. Finally, the absence of biochemical or clinical data is inherent to the data source and limits the study to population-level nutritional surveillance rather than assessment of metabolic consequences.

Conclusions and recommendations

In conclusion, over the past decade the Peruvian population has experienced a profound reconfiguration of its nutritional spectrum, producing a divergent double burden. On one hand, there is an accelerated expansion and clear social diffusion of extreme obesity (class III) among adults, which has rapidly permeated into middle and lower socioeconomic strata. Simultaneously, a structural vulnerability persists among older adults, in whom weight deficit and malnutrition risk remain strongly anchored in extreme poverty.

On this basis, recommendations can be summarized into three axes. First, strengthen structural prevention through double-duty actions targeting the food environment, the price and access to healthy foods, and the protection of childhood, given that this approach can simultaneously address different forms of malnutrition and that integrated regulatory packages and taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages have been associated with reductions in purchases of critical nutrients and in sales of taxed products. Second, prepare the health system for the care of severe obesity by strengthening pathways for detection, referral, and specialized management, with appropriate equipment and access to intensive therapies and, when indicated, metabolic/bariatric surgery, which has shown greater benefits for weight and diabetes than non-surgical management. Third, consolidate an integrated geriatric response by institutionalizing routine malnutrition screening and functional assessment, and incorporating sarcopenia case-finding to prevent underweight and muscle loss from going unnoticed in clinical practice. These recommendations should be interpreted as implications guided by the combination of our findings and the available external evidence, and not as effects demonstrated directly by the present study.

•Class III obesity is the fastest-growing nutritional category among Peruvian adults and should be managed as a high-complexity phenotype requiring structured detection pathways, specialist referral, and — when indicated — access to metabolic/bariatric surgery.
