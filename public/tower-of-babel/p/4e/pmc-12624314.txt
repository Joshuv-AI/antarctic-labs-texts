Azobenzene‐Based Glycomacrocycles: Synthesis, Photoisomerization, and Photomodulation of Liquid Crystal Helical Pitch with Helix Inversion
Authors: Gaoyu Li, Reo Hatano, Stéphane Maisonneuve, Ken‐ichi Iimura, Yuna Kim, Juan Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNovel azobenzene‐embedded glycomacrolactones bearing different substituents on the glucopyranoside ring have been synthesized and characterized by NMR spectroscopy, UV/vis spectroscopy, and circular dichroism (CD), then applied for cholesteric liquid crystal photomodulation. All the macrocycles displayed good photochromic properties, fatigue resistance, thermal bistability, and chiroptical activity. As chiral dopants for nematic liquid crystal (NLC), an amplification of helix upon E to Z photoisomerization and enforcement of helical twisting power (HTP) at Z‐rich state have been observed. We also demonstrated the critical influence of sugar‐ring functionalization in governing chirality transfer and photoswitching behavior in cholesteric liquid crystals (CLCs). Two of the synthesized glycomacrocycles uniquely achieved photoinduced helix inversion—marking the first such example for this class of compounds.Keywords: cholesteric liquid crystal, glycomacrocyclic azobenzene, helical twisting power, photoinduced helix inversion, photoisomerization

Introduction

Molecular photoswitches are attracting increasing interest in chemistry, biology, pharmaceuticals, dynamic functional soft materials, energy storage, and so on.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] Chiral photoswitchable molecules enlarge greatly the application fields, from optical displays, color filters, data storage, sensors, anti‐counterfeiting tags, and tunable lasers as well as chiral analysis.[ 1 , 5 , 6 ] However, most molecular photoswitches are either achiral (like azobenzenes, indigoids, diarylethenes, hydrazones, and fulgides) or synthesized as racemic mixtures (as spiropyrans and over‐crowded alkenes). To obtain enantiomerically pure photoswitches, chiral resolution is often employed.[ 7 , 8 ] Other types of chiral molecular photoswitches are usually synthesized by attaching a chiral component on the photoswitching skeleton. As optically transparent and biodegradable chiral compounds, carbohydrates represent a nonextinguishable natural resource to make chiral photoswitches for various applications. Up to date, main efforts have been focused on the development of glycosyl azobenzenes[ 9 , 10 ] and diarylethenes.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] Thanks to the facile synthesis of azobenzenes, good photoswitching properties and significant geometrical, conformational, and polarity changes between E and Z‐azobenzene isomers, glycosyl azobenzenes have been largely developed, with applications various from photoresponsive gelators[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] surfactants or detergents,[ 18 , 19 , 20 ] glycolipids,[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] photocontrol of carbohydrate‐lectin interaction,[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 ] photoswitching of enzyme inhibition,[ 29 , 30 ] to liquid crystals (LCs),[ 31 ] or reworkable adhesives.[ 32 ] Embedding one or several molecular photoswitches in a cyclic or macrocyclic ring has allowed to modulate further their photochemical and photophysical properties, and consequently enlarged their applications.[ 33 , 34 ] Since 2017, glycomacrocyclic azobenzenes have been reported by G. Despras,[ 35 , 36 ] J. Xie,[ 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 ] S. Jarosz,[ 43 ] and coworkers.[ 44 ] Efficient photocontrol of molecular shapes,[ 35 , 36 ] solubility,[ 36 ] cation complexation[ 43 ] as well as chirality transfer from sugar to the azobenzene moiety[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 ] has been achieved through E⇄Z photoisomerization. Furthermore, the chiroptical activities of two azobenzene‐embedded glycomacrolactones (A and B, Figure 1a) have been successfully used as chiral dopants for photoresponsive cholesteric liquid crystals (CLCs), which exhibited large conformational changes of CLCs, providing dynamic control of helical superstructures in response to a light stimulus.[ 41 ]

a) Reported azobenzene glycomacrolactones; b) Structure of macrolactones reported in this work; c) Molecular structures of utilized nematic LCs.

In CLCs, the helical twisting power (HTP, β) is a key parameter that quantifies the ability of a chiral dopant to induce a helical superstructure when blended with an achiral nematic host. It is defined as β = 1/(c∙P), where c is the dopant concentration and P is the helical pitch—the distance required for the director to complete a full 360° rotation. A high HTP allows the formation of tightly wound helices at low dopant loading, minimizing disturbance to host properties such as viscosity and clearing point. Moreover, the capacity to modulate HTP through external stimuli such as light makes it central to the design of responsive soft photonic systems.[ 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 ]

Considering the abundant resource of carbohydrates and the availability of different configurations of various natural sugars, there are enormous possibilities to develop photoswitchable glycomacrocycles for LC applications. In our previous work, glycomacrocycles are synthesized from the phenyl α‐D‐thiomannopyranoside (for A and C, Figure 1a) and phenyl β‐D‐thioglucopyranoside (for B and D, Figure 1a), with the o,o‐di‐O‐substituted azobenzene linked on the 4,6‐ (for A and D) or 2,3‐position (for B and C) of the sugar moiety through two ester linkages. The free hydroxyl groups are protected as benzyl ether. As a continuing interest in the development of photoswitchable glycomacrocycles to achieve enhanced HTP values and photoinduced helix inversion through sugar side chain modulation, we have synthesized four new glycomacrolactones, with o,o‐di‐O‐substituted azobenzene linked on the 4,6‐position of phenyl β‐D‐thioglucopyranoside (1,2, Figure 1b) or methyl α‐D‐glucopyranoside (3,4, Figure 1b) as an analogue of the glucomacrolactone D. The hydroxyl functions on the 2‐ and 3‐positions are functionalized either with a hexyl group (for 1 and 3) or a p‐cyanobiphenyloxyhexyl (6‐OCB) substituent (for 2 and 4) so as to investigate their efficiency as chiral dopants through their potential interaction with nematic LCs such as 5CB, DON‐103, and ZLI‐1132.[ 49 ] Molecular structures of these nematic hosts are provided in Figure 1c. We have also changed the phenyl thioglucosides (1,2) to methyl glucosides (3,4) to evaluate the influence of the substituent at the anomeric position. These macrocycles can be readily photoswitched between E and Z isomers under UV‐vis irradiation, with high fatigue resistance and chiroptical activities. Structural modifications at the sugar moiety significantly influence the chirality transfer and photoswitching behavior of glycomacrocyclic azobenzene dopants in CLCs. In particular, variations at the anomeric position and the 2,3‐positions of the sugar ring were found to modulate both the magnitude and directional sense of HTP changes upon E→Z photoisomerization. Among these, dopants bearing a methyl glucoside (─OMe) substituent exhibited particularly enhanced responsiveness, with one example achieving a switching ratio of up to +470 %, including reversible helix inversion behavior. This performance is comparable to that of a previously reported smaller‐ring analogue (compound B, +500 %) and represents the first observation of photoinduced helix inversion in a glycomacrocyclic azobenzene. System incorporating mesogenic side chains in combination with ─OMe also exhibited large HTP modulation (up to +335 %) with inversion, whereas earlier macrocycles with ─SPh substitution (e.g., compound A) showed more limited switching (up to +90 %). These findings underscore the importance of sugar‐side functionalization and side‐chain design in enabling precise, light‐driven helicity control in soft chiral materials.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

Preparation of the target compounds is achieved through the O‐alkylation of the corresponding di‐O‐bromoacetyl glucosides with 2,2′‐dihydroxyazobenzene (Schemes 1, 2), strategy employed for the synthesis of the glycomacrocycles A and B.[ 37 , 38 ] Scheme 1 depicted the synthesis of the glycomacrocycles 1 and 2 starting from the phenyl 4,6‐O‐benzylidene‐β‐D‐thioglucopyranoside 5,[ 50 ] by first introducing hexyl or 6‐OCB group on the 2‐ and 3‐positions through O‐alkylation reaction, followed by removal of the benzylidene group on the 4,6‐positions and functionalization with bromoacetyl group on it, leading to the corresponding key intermediates 8 and 14. 6‐OCBBr 11 was prepared in 67 % yield by reaction of 4′‐hydroxy‐(1,1′‐biphenyl)‐4‐carbonitrile 10 with 1,6‐dibromohexane in the presence of 1.5 equiv. of K2CO3 and 0.1 equiv. of KI at 60 °C in butanone for two days. We then used the optimized conditions for the synthesis of the macrocycle D,[ 38 ] that is, reaction of 8 or 14 with 1 equiv. of o,o‐di‐hydroxyazobenzene 10 in the presence of 2 equiv. of K2CO3/0.5 equiv. of 18‐crown‐6 in DMF at room temperature to afford the glycomacrocycles 1 and 2 (E/Z = 10/3 for 1, and E/Z = 20/7 for 2) in 28 % and 36 % yields, respectively. In summary, through a four‐step reaction sequence, compounds 1 and 2 have been obtained in 19 % and 20 % total yields.

Photochromic Properties

We then investigated the photochromic properties of the glycomacrolactones 1–4 in acetonitrile. O‐Hexyl substituted compounds 1 and 3 displayed two relatively strong π→π* transitions between 308–350 nm and a weaker forbidden n→π* transition around 450 nm of azobenzene moiety (Figure 2a, c black line, Table 2). Their absorption spectra are similar to that of the reported compound D.[ 38 ] The O‐(6‐OCB)‐substituted derivatives 2 and 4 showed the expected UV/vis spectral features of both the p‐cyanobiphenyl of 6‐OCB and azo moieties (Figure 2b, d black line, Table 2). The absorption of the 6‐OCB part is much stronger than the azobenzene, with the λ max blue‐shifted to 295–296 nm. A slightly blue‐shifted n→π* absorption band is observed at 420–460 nm characteristic of the azobenzene moiety.

Upon irradiation at 365 nm, a strong decrease of the bands at 308–350 nm, 450 nm and the appearance of a new band at 420 nm are observed for 1 and 3, revealing the E to Z isomerization of the macrocycle (Figure 2a, c blue line). Two isosbestic points can also be observed at 401 and 442 nm. In the case of compounds 2 and 4, UV irradiation led to a significant decrease of the absorption bands at 356, 385 nm, and only a slight decrease of the band near 295 nm (Figure 2b, d blue line). Two isosbestic points can also be observed at 401 and 440 nm. Varying E:Z ratios at the photostationary states (PSSs) were determined either by 1H NMR spectroscopy or by the molar absorption coefficient at a specific wavelength after the required irradiation time to reach the PSS (Figures S6–S11). The molar absorption coefficient (ε) was obtained from the plot of the absorbance at a specific wavelength versus concentration (Figures S2–S5) and taking into account the proportion of Z isomer in solution (Figures S6–S11, eq S5, Tables S2 and 3). For all four macrolactones, E→Z photoconversion under UV irradiation is more efficient (87–100 % of Z‐isomers) than the Z→E back photoisomerization (60–70 % of E‐isomers) (Table 2), which is due to the comparable level of molar absorption coefficient of both E and Z isomers above 400 nm (Figure 2). All the macrocycles are photostable, thus displaying high fatigue resistance since photoswitching cycles have been repeated more than 10 times without any noticeable degradation (Figure 2).

Chiroptical Activity

Previously, we have observed the chiroptical activities of both E and Z glycomacrolactones A‐D, with a chirality transfer from the sugar to the azobenzene unit.[ 37 , 38 ] The circular dichroism (CD) spectra of 1–4 show similar spectral changes, displaying a negative Cotton effect near 458 nm and a positive band near 294 nm corresponding, respectively, to n→π* and π→π* transitions of the E‐azobenzene in acetonitrile (Figure 3, red line). E→Z photoisomerization with 365 nm light leads to a blue shift of all CD bands, without sign reversal (Figure 3, blue line). Compared to the O‐benzyl analogue D, a significant increase in intensity is observed in compounds 1–4 (Δε value changes before and after UV irradiation, Table 2), while the 6‐OCB derivative Z‐3 displays the highest Δε value (‐6.2 M−1 cm−1). The negative Cotton effects on the CD spectrum indicate that all the E‐ and Z‐isomers adopt preferentially a P helical conformation, and the nature of substituents on the sugar ring did not induce any helical structure change.[ 37 , 38 ]

Cholesteric Liquid Crystal Photomodulation

The chirality transfer behavior of dopants 1–4 in CLCs was investigated using the Cano wedge method.[ 52 ] In this technique, doping an achiral nematic liquid crystal (NLC) with a chiral molecule induces a cholesteric (N*) phase via molecular chirality transfer. The efficiency of this transfer is quantified by the HTP, as outlined in the introduction. This parameter serves as a critical metric for evaluating dopant performance in tuning the supramolecular helicity of CLCs under photoirradiation.[ 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 ]

Three nematic LC hosts (Figure 1) —were selected to bracket practical ranges of host parameters known to affect chirality transfer and HTP, namely dielectric anisotropy (Δε), viscosity, and elastic constants (with emphasis on the twist constant K22). 5CB serves as a single‐component cyanobiphenyl benchmark with a well‐characterized positive Δε.[ 53 ] ZLI‐1132 is a multicomponent positive‐Δε mixture that is widely used as a host; lower K22 and reduced elastic anisotropy relative to 5CB have been reported under comparable conditions.[ 54 , 55 ] DON‐103 is a commercial ester‐rich nematic mixture whose polarity/viscosity profile differs from cyanobiphenyl‐dominated hosts; ester linkages introduce larger transverse dipole components relative to biaryl systems.[ 56 , 57 ] This bracketing enables us to test whether HTP‐modulation trends persist across chemically distinct matrices.

To ensure homogeneous cholesteric phases, dopant concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 wt% were prepared. After thorough mixing, each mixture was injected into a Grandjean‐Cano wedge cell via capillary action at room temperature (see Experimental Section for details). All prepared wedge cells exhibited clear and distinct vertical Cano lines under polarized optical microscopy (Figures 4, 5, and S16–S20), confirming the formation of stable cholesteric ordering. Illumination of the wedge cell with UV (365 nm) and visible (430 nm) light to each PSS brought a dynamic shift of the Cano lines. The width between the Cano lines (b) was used to calculate the helical pitch lengths (P). Ps and corresponding HTPs (βs) of dopants 1–4 before and after UV and visible light irradiation at each PSS are summarized in Table 3. HTP values were calculated based on dopant concentration in mol%; for comparison, HTPs determined using weight percent concentrations are provided in Table S4. The twist sense of the induced cholesteric phase was determined using the color‐shift method under polarized light microscopy (see Experimental Section for details).[ 7 , 58 ] Overall in their initial and light‐irradiated states their left (−)‐handed cholesteric helices are maintained, and reversible shortening and lengthening of the distance between the Cano lines occurred upon the irradiation with UV and visible light, respectively (Figures 4 and S1–6‐20). It indicates the shortening of P–amplification of helix – upon E to Z photoisomerization of dopants. This behavior was consistent with our previous study with CLCs doped with A and B, exhibiting enforcement of HTP at Z‐rich state (Table S5).[ 39 ]

Interestingly, some CLCs containing dopant 3 (in 5CB and ZLI‐1132) and 4 (in DON‐103) displayed right (+)‐handed initial helix directional sense. In addition, in these CLCs, a decrease in HTP (increasing P) upon UV irradiation was observed, which is opposite to other CLCs. More surprisingly, upon constant UV exposure within 1 minute, the Cano lines completely vanished via shifting and their gap widening, as featured in Figure 5b,c (e.g., 3 in 5CB), indicating the destruction of the initial cholesteric phase. An induced achiral nematic phase was observed in the wedge cell, with the P changing to infinity (Figure 5c). Continuous UV irradiation for around an additional 20 seconds resulted in the dramatic appearance of a dense oily streak texture from the thicker area expanding to the thinner area. The oily streak gradually coalesces into a typical Cano lines texture in the wedge cell (Figure 5d‐g) within 1 minute of further irradiation (e.g., calculated P of 11.8 µm, Figure 5g), suggesting that the helical reorganization of LC molecules occurred with well‐aligned helices. CLCs containing dopant 3 in ZLI‐1132 (Figure S19c) and 4 in DON‐103 (Figure S20c) also exhibited the UV‐induced achiral nematic phase with infinite Ps, followed by reemergence of cholesteric phase (Figures S19d and S20d, respectively), finally stabilized forming several Cano lines (Figures S19f and S20e). This whole “cholesteric − nematic − cholesteric” process indicates that the transition is related to handedness inversion via an achiral nematic phase. In CLC containing 3 in 5CB, the HTP in the initial state was +2.4 µm−1, which eventually transitioned to ‐8.5 µm−1 at PSS365. Much amplified helix in the opposite left (−)‐ sense was consistent for the other two CLCs exhibiting a maximum HTP switching ratio of 470 % (3 in ZLI‐1132). Subsequent visible light irradiation for 60–120 seconds triggered the redisappearance of Cano lines, resulting in the emergence of achiral nematic phase again (Figure 5h,i). Then, the emergence of oily streak texture (N* phase) from the thicker area and expanding to the thinner area, eventually forming Cano lines (Figure 5j–l) were observed exhibiting HTP of +4.2 µm−1 at PSS430 with right‐handedness. Hence, helix reinversion via nematic phase upon subsequent visible irradiation was confirmed accompanying comparable HTPs to their initial ones. This reversible helix inversion was consistent for the other two CLCs (Figures S19g–j and S20f–i), which represents a rare and valuable example of light‐driven handedness switching in CLCs based on macrocyclic azobenzene dopants.[ 56 , 59 ]

Figure 6 summarizes the initial HTPs (Figure 6a) and photoinduced HTP switching ratios (Figure 6b) of glycomacrocyclic azobenzene dopants featuring various R1‐R2 substituent combinations, evaluated in three nematic hosts. Here, R1 represents the substituent at the 2,3‐position on the sugar unit, either a linear alkyl group (C6H13) or a mesogenic group (6‐OCB). R2 corresponds to the anomeric substituent, being either phenyl‐thio (─SPh) or methyl‐glucoside (─OMe). Initially, dopant 2 in ZLI‐1132 exhibited the highest HTP value (−29.3 µm−1). Generally, dopants demonstrated superior compatibility with 5CB and ZLI‐1132 compared to DON‐103, reflected by higher initial HTPs. This compatibility likely arises from better molecular interactions, possibly due to structural compatibility between the dopants and the respective host matrices. Interestingly, the R2 substituents ─SPh and ─OMe significantly influenced the initial HTP values more prominently than R1 substituents (linear alkyl C6H13 and mesogenic 6‐OCB). Specifically, ─SPh substituents in 1 and 2 generally led to higher initial HTPs (β) across all nematic hosts, which can be attributed to stronger intermolecular interactions arising from π–π stacking interactions and favorable van der Waals forces with the nematic host molecules.

Upon UV irradiation, distinct differences in HTP switching behavior were observed depending on the nature of the sugar substituents. Notably, dopant 4, bearing a methyl‐glucoside (─OMe) and a mesogenic 6‐OCB group, exhibited large photoswitching ratios in 5CB and ZLI‐1132—reaching up to +335 %—without inducing helix inversion. In contrast, dopant 3, which combines the ─OMe group with a flexible linear alkyl chain, exhibited pronounced photoinduced helix inversion accompanied by substantial amplification of chirality, with a maximum switching ratio of +470 % in ZLI‐1132. Meanwhile, the relatively modest switching observed for dopants 1 and 2, both bearing ─SPh groups, suggests restricted conformational reorganization and weaker dopant‐host interactions upon photoisomerization.

These observations highlight the pivotal role of the ─OMe group in enabling dynamic modulation of HTP. Compared to the bulkier ─SPh substituent, the ─OMe unit offers lower steric hindrance, greater conformational flexibility, and improved miscibility with nematic hosts—facilitating more effective chirality transfer. Moreover, when paired with a linear alkyl chain, as in dopant 3, the ─OMe group promotes a synergistic structural rearrangement upon E→Z isomerization, enabling reversible helix inversion through enhanced chiral asymmetry and efficient alignment with the LC matrix.

This unusual photoswitching behavior is further supported by mechanistic insights from previous DFT and spectroscopic studies.[ 39 ] For the previously reported glycomacrolactones A and B, E→Z photoisomerization led to a more compact molecular conformation and increased internal ring strain, particularly in compound B, which facilitated enhanced alignment with the nematic host and a higher degree of helical induction in the Z‐rich state. Consistently, CD spectroscopy revealed stronger Cotton effects for Z‐isomers, supporting their superior chiral transfer capabilities.

Compared to these earlier macrocycles, the present series (1–4) exhibits further advancements in both initial HTP values and photoswitching performance. For example, compounds 1–4 consistently outperformed compound A—despite sharing the same 17‐membered ring size—due to optimized sugar substitution patterns. While compound B, bearing a smaller 16‐membered ring, previously exhibited a maximum switching ratio of +500 %, dopant 3 in this study achieved a comparable + 470 % via photoinduced helix inversion, representing the first such observation for a glycomacrocyclic azobenzene system in CLCs. These findings highlight the critical role of sugar‐side functionalization in modulating chirality transfer and helicity, with the ─OMe substituent emerging as a key enabler of both large switching amplitudes and helix sense reversibility. Furthermore, these effects are sensitively modulated by the nature of the nematic host, emphasizing the interplay between dopant structure and mesophase environment in governing chiral amplification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have designed and synthesized four azobenzene‐embedded 17‐membered glycomacrolactones with hexyl (compounds 1 and 3) or mesogenic mimicking substituent 6‐OCB (2 and 4) on the 2‐ and 3‐positions of methyl α‐D‐glucoside (3 and 4) or phenyl β‐D‐thioglucoside (1 and 2). The four glycomacrocycles studied in CH3CN displayed good photoswitching properties under UV‐vis irradiation, good photostability, and high thermostability for the Z‐1–4 (t1/2 > 25 days at room temperature). All the E‐glycomacrocycles 1–4 displayed chiroptical activity, with a negative Cotton effect near 458 nm. E→Z photoisomerization leads to a blue shift of all CD bands without sign reversal, with the highest Δε value for the 6‐OCB derivative Z‐3. We demonstrated that compounds 1–4 exhibit as chiral dopants for nematic LC hosts 5CB, DON‐103, and ZLI‐1132, with an amplification of helix upon E to Z photoisomerization and enforcement of HTP at Z‐rich state. The largest photoswitching ratios (from +263 % to +470 %) have been observed for the methyl α‐D‐glucoside derivatives 3 and 4 in 5CB and ZLI‐1132.

These findings highlight the critical influence of sugar‐side functionalization in governing chirality transfer and photoswitching behavior in cholesteric LCs. Compared to previously reported macrocycles, the present glycomacrocyclic azobenzene dopants exhibit enhanced HTP values and switching efficiencies, with dopants 3 and 4 uniquely achieving photoinduced helix inversion—marking the first such example for this class of compounds. The ─OMe substituent, in particular, plays a pivotal role in enabling both large HTP modulation and dynamic helicity control, with the effects strongly modulated by the nature of the nematic host.

Experimental Section

General Information

Reagent‐grade chemicals were obtained from diverse commercial suppliers and used as received. The reactions carried out under anhydrous conditions are performed under argon in glassware previously dried in an oven. CH2Cl2 was previously dried through an alumina cartridge using a solvent purifier MBRAUN SPS‐800. All the reactions with azobenzene‐containing substrates were carried out in the dark. Reactions were monitored by TLC on Silica Gel 60F‐254 plates with detection by UV (254 nm or 365 nm) or by spraying with 10 % H2SO4 in EtOH and heating for about 30 seconds at 400–600 °C. Column chromatography purification was performed on CombiFlash Rf+ and RediSep Rf or Rf Gold normal phase silica columns (with UV detection at 254 and 350 nm for all azobenzene‐derivatives). 1H and 13C‐NMR spectra were recorded on a JEOL ECS‐400 or a JNM ECZL‐400‐S spectrometer. Structural assignments were made with additional information from gCOSY and gHMQC experiments. High‐resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were performed on a Q‐TOF mass spectrometer by the SALSA platform from the ICOA laboratory. Optical rotations [α]D25 were measured using a JASCO P‐2000 polarimeter at 589 nm where no absorption occurred for all compounds. Photochromic reactions were induced in situ by a continuous irradiation Hg/Xe lamp (Hamamatsu, LC6‐ or LC8‐Lightningcure, 200 W) equipped with narrow band interference filters of appropriate wavelength from Semrock. Quartz cells of 10 mm path length have been used for solution measurements. More details are given in Supporting Information.

Helical Pitch Length and Helical Twisting Power Determination

Grandjean‐Cano wedge cell method was used to determine helical P and HTP of the photoresponsive CLCs. CLC mixture containing homogeneously dissolved 1∼4 in nematic LCs (5CB, DON‐103, and ZLI‐1132): 5CB was purchased from Kanto Chemical Co. Inc. (Japan); DON‐103 and ZLI‐1132 were obtained from DIC Corporation (Japan) and Merck, respectively. Prepared CLCs were injected into a wedge‐shaped Cano wedge cell (EHC Corporation, KCRK‐07, tan θ = 0.0196 ± 5 %) using a Pasteur pipette under capillary force. When Cano lines became visible and well‐oriented after stabilization for a few hours in the dark room, the width between the Cano lines (b) was measured and the helical P was calculated from the equation, “P = 2b tanθ”. Every measurement was conducted at room temperature. Photoisomerization was conducted using LED light sources at 365 nm (70 mW) and 430 nm (20 mW) (CL‐1503, Asahi Spectra Co., Japan). Microscopic analyses were performed using a polarizing optical microscope (MT9000 series, Meiji Techno Co., Japan) equipped with a Moticam1080 camera (Shimadzu Rika Co., Japan). For the observation of Cano wedge cells exhibiting photo‐induced helix inversion (Figures 5 and S19, S20), cut‐off filters were applied to the polarizer to prevent unintended photoisomerization and stabilize the Cano texture.

Helix Handedness Determination

In Cano wedge cells, the direction of color shift across the wedge upon clockwise rotation of the analyzer indicated the handedness of the helix: a shift toward the thinner side corresponds to a left‐handed (negative) helix, while a shift toward the thicker side corresponds to a right‐handed (positive) helix, and all the helix senses before and after photoirradiation were also confirmed using S‐811 (a standard left‐handed dopant) as a reference.

General procedure I (GP‐I) for the 2,3‐di‐O‐alkylation of glucoside

To a solution of sugar derivative 5 or 15 in dry DMF (0.13 M), was added NaH (60 % in mineral oil) at 0 °C under Ar. The mixture was stirred for 10 minutes at 0 °C. Then bromohexane or 6‐OCB‐Br was added and the reaction mixture was heated to 60 °C for 2 hours and stirred under Ar. After the completion of this reaction, the mixture was cooled down to rt and quenched with MeOH (5 mL/mM substrate) by stirring for 10 minutes. After evaporation of the solvents under reduced pressure, the residue was diluted with CH2Cl2, washed with water and brine, dried over MgSO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by CombiFlash Rf+.

General procedure II (GP‐II) for the benzylidene acetal deprotection

To a solution of benzylidene acetals in MeOH (0.14 M), was added CSA. The mixture was stirred 16 hours at rt. After the completion of this reaction, Et3N was added until pH ≥ 7. The solvent was then removed under reduced pressure and the residue was diluted with EtOAc and washed with H2O and brine, dried over MgSO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by CombiFlash Rf+.

General procedure III (GP‐III) for the 4,6‐di‐O‐bromoacetylation of glucoside

To a solution of 2,3‐di‐O‐alkylated‐D‐glucopyranoside (1 eq) in dry CH2Cl2 (0.5 M), was added dry pyridine (5 eq) and bromoacetyl bromide (4 eq) at 0 °C. The mixture was stirred at 0 °C under Ar for 10 minutes. After the completion of this reaction, MeOH (0.4 mL/mM substrate) was added. The reaction mixture was diluted with DCM, washed with sat. aqueous NH4Cl and brine, dried over Na2SO4, and the solvents were removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by CombiFlash Rf+.

General procedure IV (GP‐IV) for the macrolactonization

To a solution of 2,2′‐dihydroxyazobenzene 9 (1 eq) in dry DMF (7.24 mL/0.05 mmol) were added K2CO3 (2 eq) and 18‐crown‐6 (0.5 eq). The mixture was stirred at rt for 15 minutes. After that, a solution of 4,6‐di‐O‐bromoacetyl‐D‐glucopyranoside (1 eq) in dry DMF (3.22 mL/0.05 mmol sugar) was added slowly under Ar and the reaction mixture was stirred at rt for 1.5 hours. After the completion of the reaction, the solvent was removed and the residue was diluted with EtOAc. The solution was then washed with water and brine. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4 and the solvents were removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by CombiFlash Rf+.
