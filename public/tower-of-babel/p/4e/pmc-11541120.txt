Clinical Outcome of an All Arthroscopic ‘Whole Layer’ Rotator Cuff Repair Technique with Simultaneous Biceps Tenodesis
Authors: Chiu‐Yu Shih, Pei‐Ru Lin, Hui‐Chen Huang, Cheng‐Pu Hsieh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo retrospectively evaluate clinical outcomes, including function and pain, of patients after our all arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique with simultaneous biceps tenodesis procedure; factors influencing results were also evaluated. Given the frequent association of rotator cuff tear with long head of biceps lesion and the need for effective combined treatment strategies, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy of our technique and compare it with established methods. We hypothesized that our technique would significantly improve function and reduce pain in patients with rotator cuff tears and biceps pathology.MethodsThis is a retrospective study that included patients older than 20 years who underwent all arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique with simultaneous biceps tenodesis procedures for concomitant rotator cuff tear and long head biceps pathology, from 2016 to 2020. Patients were evaluated preoperatively and at a minimum of 2 years of follow‐up using the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) and visual analogue scale (VAS) scores paired t‐tests were used for analysis and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05(two‐tailed). The satisfaction rate and complications were also evaluated.ResultsAfter an average follow‐up of 2.3 years, 118 patients demonstrated significant improvement in both the ASES score (from 36.13 to 95.01, p < 0.001) and VAS score (from 6.81 to 0.89, p < 0.001). Ninety‐four percent of the patients reported satisfaction with the surgical outcome. No complications related to Popeye deformity, biceps cramping pain, or ipsilateral shoulder reoperation were observed. Factors such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes did not influence the results. Patients showed significant improvement in both the ASES and VAS scores (p < 0.001).ConclusionAt a mean follow‐up time of 2.3 years, the all arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique with simultaneous biceps tenodesis is a therapeutic and efficient procedure. The procedure revealed a satisfactory functional outcome, reduced pain, and minimal complications and reoperations.

Introduction

Arthroscopic repair of the rotator cuff is a commonly performed procedure with positive outcomes. Techniques include single‐row, double‐row, modified Mason–Allen, suture bridge, and transosseous repair. All techniques compress the tendon to the bone to ensure tendon healing. 1 , 2 , 3 However, limitations exist with each of these conventional repair methods. Single‐row repairs may exhibit higher retear and lower healing rates. 4 , 5 Double‐row and suture bridge techniques, while effective, can be technically demanding and require more anchors, increasing cost. 6 The modified Mason–Allen repair may not be suitable for large or complex tears because of potential limitations in footprint restoration. 7 Transosseous repairs carry the risk of fracture with drill holes in the bone. 8 We propose a novel approach to rotator cuff repair, termed the “whole layer” technique, which involves augmenting the entire thickness and large area of the musculotendinous sling of the rotator cuff to address the torn site with a posterior‐to‐anterior suture vector. The technique offers a potentially simpler, more versatile, and cost‐effective alternative to existing methods with minimal complications.

During arthroscopic repair of the rotator cuff, the pathology of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHB) should be observed because LHB lesions are commonly associated with rotator cuff tears. 9 , 10 A good strategy to treat LHB lesions is essential to avoid postoperative anterior shoulder pain, flexion dysfunction, and to improve functional outcome. 1 , 11 Pathology of the long head of the biceps tendon is associated with superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) tear, partial biceps tear, tendinitis or instability. 12 , 13 Treatment of the injured long head of the biceps usually involves tenotomy or tenodesis. The simplest method is by tenotomy of the biceps at the supraglenoid tubercle. However, the belly of the biceps muscle may drop distally and cause prominence over the arm (Popeye deformity). 14 , 15 , 16 Tenodesis, compared with tenotomy, preserves tension and decreases the incidence of cosmetic deformity. 17 , 18 Both arthroscopic suprapectoral tenodesis and open subpectoral tenodesis showed similar clinical results and pain relief. 19 , 20 However, the open technique may result in more complications due to its invasiveness. 21

When performing both arthroscopic rotator cuff repair and biceps tenodesis, a separate incision or an additional surgical implant is usually necessary. 22 , 23 Our all arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique with simultaneous biceps tenodesis offers a cost‐effective approach by using the same suture anchor for both procedures. Although biceps tenodesis with concomitant rotator cuff repair improves the outcome, the optimal tenodesis method with rotator cuff repair remains to be determined. 24 This study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes, including function and pain, of patients undergoing our all‐arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair with simultaneous biceps tenodesis. We hypothesized that this novel technique will lead to significant improvements in function, range of motion, pain reduction and potentially offer versatility to existing methods.

Methods

Patient Selection

The inclusion criteria included patients who presented with partial or small to large full‐thickness rotator cuff tears, along with a long head of biceps lesion, manifesting as SLAP tear, partial tear, tendinitis, or instability. These patients underwent an all‐arthroscopic “whole‐layer” rotator cuff repair technique with biceps tenodesis performed by the senior author between June 2016 and June 2020. This study excluded patients with prior ipsilateral shoulder surgery, greater tuberosity fracture, irreparable massive rotator cuff tears, subscapularis tear, and concomitant Bankart lesions.

Clinical Evaluation

The senior author performed all preoperative history and physical examinations, including Jobe, Hawkins, and Neer tests for rotator cuff tear and impingement. Pain during palpation in the bicipital groove, positive O'Brien and speed tests indicated LHB pathology. The presence of both the rotator cuff tear and the LHB lesion was confirmed by preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and during arthroscopic surgery. Age and pathological morphology were considered to determine whether LHB tenodesis or rotator cuff repair was performed. For young adults, debridement or superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) repair is typically preferred for SLAP lesion. In the event of massive rotator cuff tear, either superior capsular reconstruction or in space balloon insertion was performed. Patients who fell into these specific groups were excluded from our study.

Surgical Procedure

Partial Thickness Rotator Cuff Tear/Small Whole‐layer Rotator Cuff Tear

The arthroscope is inserted into a standard posterior portal to perform a comprehensive joint assessment. The anterior portal was established using an outside‐in method in the rotator interval. To facilitate surgical visualization and alleviate postoperative pain, any inflamed synovitis is cauterized. A spinal needle is percutaneously inserted approximately 1 cm lateral to the anterior portal, passing through the LHB tendon proximal to the bicipital groove (Figure 1). This technique enables subsequent positioning of the transtendinous approach to the humeral head, establishing a pathway for suture anchor placement and biceps tenodesis.

The arthroscope is advanced into the subacromial space. Subacromial bursa debridement is then performed to remove any debris or inflammation. The anterior and lateral margins of the acromion are identified, and an outside‐in technique is used to create a lateral portal approximately 1 to 2 cm lateral to the acromion's lateral tip. Using a burr, the subacromial spurs susceptible to impingement syndrome were cleared. Then, the arthroscope is inserted through the lateral portal to assess the undersurface of the acromion. Any residual bone spurs are removed through the anterior portal. Rotator cuff cauterization is performed through the anterior portal, using the spinal needle as a reference point (Figure 2). The needle is avoided by approximately 1 cm to prevent accidental injury to the LHB, which could retract distally and disappear. A transtendinous hole is created through the supraspinatus, and the insertion site for the double‐loaded suture anchor is made below the LHB tendon proximal to the bicipital groove. The anterolateral double‐loaded suture anchor is inserted. Next, an anterolateral portal (accessory lateral portal) is created using the outside‐in technique. The grasper forceps are then inserted through the anterolateral portal beside the suture anchor for retrieval of the 1‐0 PDS through the spinal needle (Figure 3). Then the arthroscope is reinserted into the posterior portal. One of the suture strands is shuttled through the LHB using 1‐0 PDS and then manipulated to encircle the biceps tendon. The suture limb is then pulled through the anterior portal (Figure 4). Approximately 80% of the cross‐sectional area of the LHB between the needle penetration site and the origin of the biceps is cauterized. Then, the arthroscope is placed back into the lateral portal. The other suture limb is pulled through the anterolateral portal using a loop grasper. A Samsung Medical Center (SMC) sliding knot is tied down to complete biceps tenodesis.

Medium/Large Full‐thickness Rotator Cuff Tear

The main steps for the medium/large full‐thickness rotator cuff tear are similar with some modification. Acromioplasty is performed before spinal needle LHB penetration to ensure clear visualization of the humeral head and the extra‐articular LHB under the acromion. The spinal needle is used primarily for suture passage for biceps tenodesis rather than acting as a guide to create a route for anchor placement. The suture anchor can be inserted through the tear site of the rotator cuff.

If the tear site is in the middle of the crescent tissue, the lateral row knotless suture anchor can be inserted more posteriorly to cover the defect (Figure 9).

Illustration of the configuration of the “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique for a small full‐thickness rotator cuff tear in the middle of crescent tissue.

If there is a medium full thickness rotator cuff tear, an additional suture anchor can be inserted posterior to the anterolateral double‐loaded suture anchor. The suture limbs of the anterolateral suture anchor can be passed medially through the torn tendon using the suture bridge technique. The suture limbs of the additional posterior suture anchor are loaded into the lateral row knotless suture anchor in the “whole layer” manner and tensioned. (Figure 10).

Illustration of the configuration of the “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique for the median full thickness rotator cuff tear incorporating two medial row suture anchors and one lateral row suture anchors.

For large full‐thickness rotator cuff tears, two medial row suture anchors and two lateral row knotless suture anchors are used. Suture bridge double row configuration is adopted with additional strengthening of the entire structure with the “whole layer” repair technique. (Figure 11).

Rehabilitation Protocol

The patient is instructed to wear an arm sling postoperatively. During the first 2 weeks of recovery, the patients should perform pendulum exercises with limited shoulder range of motion while avoiding any strenuous activity that involves the operated shoulder. They are encouraged to actively move their elbow, wrist, and fingers on the operated arm. After 2 weeks, they may begin to exercise the active range of motion for the operated shoulder. Strengthening exercises can begin after 3 months, and the patient should gradually increase his level of activity over time. A full return to sports activities is expected approximately 6 months after surgery.

Outcome Measures

To assess the effectiveness of the procedure, the senior author routinely collected preoperative and postoperative data for a minimum follow‐up period of 2 years. Patient‐reported outcomes included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score and a visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain, measured on a scale of 0 to 10 (0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating extreme pain). Furthermore, data on reoperation, biceps cramping pain, and Popeye deformity were collected postoperatively for a minimum of 2 years. At the end of the follow‐up period, patient satisfaction was assessed using a straightforward yes‐no question regarding their satisfaction with the surgery.

Statistical Analyses

To compare the pre‐ and post‐ASES or VAS scores, data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), and paired t‐tests were performed. Subgroup analyses to evaluate pre‐ and post‐ASES scores or VAS scores were conducted, stratified by diabetes status, hypertension, age, sex, BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption. All statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and the two‐tailed p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Operative Findings and Implant Selections

Among the 118 patients analyzed, 82 (69.5%) underwent a 1 + 1 configuration (one medial and one lateral suture anchor) for partial thickness, small and median rotator cuff tears, while 25 patients (21.2%) received a 2 + 1 configuration (two medial and one lateral suture anchor) for median tears. Additionally, 11 patients (9.3%) underwent a 2 + 2 configuration for large tears. The choice of implant configuration was not solely based on tear size, but also on considerations such as tear configuration, tissue quality, patient age and activity levels, tailored to individual clinical needs. Subacromial decompression and acromioplasty were standard procedures for all patients.

Outcomes

There was a significant improvement from preoperative to postoperative ASES scores (mean score ± SD = 36.13 ± 7.02 to 95.01 ± 5.89, p values < 0.001). There was also a significant reduction in pain from the preoperative period to the time of postoperative follow‐up (mean VAS score ± SD = 6.81 ± 1.52 to 0.89 ± 1.12, p values < 0.001). Discussion

The main finding of this study was that our all‐arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair with simultaneous biceps tenodesis procedure produced satisfactory clinical results with restored function and pain relief. There were no clinically apparent complications associated with failed biceps tenodesis. No patient required reoperation of the ipsilateral shoulder. We reached a satisfaction rate of 94%.

Comparison to Traditional Approach

Compared to the traditional open approach, the all‐arthroscopic biceps tenodesis avoids potential neurovascular injury, infection, hematoma/seroma formation and cosmetic drawbacks associated with a distal bicipital groove incision. 21 , 25 The anterolateral double‐loaded suture anchor functions efficiently with biceps tenodesis and repair of rotator cuff tear. This reduces the possible disadvantage of inserting additional implants such as osteolysis, additional surgical approach and saves cost. 26 , 27

Comparison to Other Arthroscopic Techniques

Several other studies have also combined biceps tenodesis with arthroscopic repair of the rotator cuff. 22 , 23 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Our technique avoids the need to externalize the LHB, a technique described by Levy. 22 Meghpara et al. described an all arthroscopic technique using the anterolateral anchor of the double row rotator cuff repair to perform biceps tenodesis. 23 However, their technique requires a tenotomy prior to rotator cuff repair, increasing the risk of slippage of the biceps tendon and misjudging the tension of LHB. In addition, the LHB is attached to the anchor with a double‐row rotator cuff repair above it. This may interfere with direct contact between the LHB and the humeral head, thus decreasing the potential for bone‐to‐tendon healing. They reported significant improvements in ASES shoulder scores from 7.1 preoperatively to 94.1 postoperatively (p < 0.001) and in VAS scores from 6.4 preoperatively to 0.5 postoperatively (p < 0.001).

Veenstra et al. published a case series study that incorporates proximal biceps tenodesis into supraspinatus repair. 32 Their technique uses a single suture anchor to fix the LHB and tie the rotator cuff with a simple or mattress suture. Our technique has the advantage of an additional LHB fixation because another loop is performed around the biceps tendon, and piercing the LHB. Furthermore, rotator cuff repair is restricted to the area between the passage of the anchor suture through the supraspinatus and the suture anchor spot in their technique. They reported improved patient‐reported ASES scores from 45.9 preoperatively to 91.6 at the 2‐year follow‐up (p < 0.001) and pain VAS scores from 5.2 preoperatively to 0.7 at the 2‐year follow‐up (p < 0.001). In contrast, the whole‐layer rotator cuff repair technique has a larger area of rotator cuff repair and compression. However, we do require a lateral row knotless suture anchor to tension and pull the cuff more anteriorly and inferiorly to achieve full coverage of the anatomical footprint.

Unique Features of the Whole‐layer Rotator Cuff Repair Technique

Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair is a well‐studied procedure with advantages over the open approach in terms of infection and morbidity. Single row, double row, and Suture Bridge are the main rotator cuff repair techniques. There are no significant differences in the outcome observed between these procedures, as shown in the latest literature. 33 , 34 However, the suture bridge technique appears to be the optimal treatment option to maximize the tension and area of the bone tendon interface to improve tendon healing and lower retear rate. 5 , 35 , 36 With the same concept as the suture bridge technique, the whole‐layer rotator cuff repair technique was developed to optimize healing biology with tight contact of bone tendon interface, decrease gap formation, and restore anatomical footprint with manual tensioning of the suture limbs after deploying the lateral row knotless suture anchor. Unlike the suture bridge technique with medial suture anchors that anchor to the lateral row in a crossed or straight fashion, our construct utilizes a compression vector from posterior to anterior suture fixation with some degree of obliquity toward laterally to augment the weakest anterior portion of supraspinatus insertion. For partial thickness rotator cuff tears, the procedure obviates the need to convert the tears into full‐thickness defect. Instead, the transtendinous defect is created by referencing the spinal needle piercing the LHB, which typically corresponds to the site of the rotator cuff injury. Tensioning both suture limbs increases the pressurized contact area, restores the footprint and effectively covers the transtendinous defect created iatrogenically. Furthermore, in both suture bridge and double row rotator cuff repair techniques. A tension load was created between the suture anchors of the medial and lateral row. The medial row anchor bears a high stress concentration to reduce and fixate the torn tendon. 37 In our technique, we used the intact portion of the supraspinatus enthesis as an additional anchor point to create tension because our suture limb vector is horizontal to the enthesis. In addition, the whole‐layer rotator cuff technique protects the medial anchor with a strong reduction force in moderate to severe tears to prevent re‐tear.

Technical Challenges and Potential Complications

Our procedure requires experience to minimize potential complications. The transtendon approach to the humeral head to pass the suture anchor requires skillful cauterization to prevent accidental tenotomy. Additionally, mastering the insertion of the spinal needle through the posterior portal to the hole created in the supraspinatus requires some experience due to the possible impingement of the scapula spine and variations in the morphology of the humeral head. Bending the spinal needle may be necessary to navigate these anatomical complexities.

Limitations

While effective, our procedure has certain limitations. The transtendon approach compromises a portion of the intact bursal layer that can cause stiffness and residual pain in some reports. 38 , 39 Furthermore, with all arthroscopic suprapectoral biceps tenodesis, distal biceps pathology within zone two could not be fully addressed. Finally, suture strands that cut through the humeral head could occur if the lateral row knotless suture anchor is overtensioned in osteoporotic bone. This alters the stability of the lateral row knotless suture anchor and tension of the suture strands.

While our study demonstrated satisfactory clinical results with restored function and pain relief following the procedure, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the absence of a control group, such as a traditional rotator cuff repair cohort or an open biceps tenodesis group, limits our ability to directly compare outcomes and assess the relative efficacy of our technique. Second, the lack of biomechanical data precludes a detailed understanding of the mechanical advantages offered by our approach compared with alternative procedures. Third, the absence of subgroup analyses, including variations in tear site and size, suture anchor placement configurations, and the functional status of patients, may overlook potential nuances in treatment outcomes. In addition, the retrospective nature of our study may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Factors such as loss of follow‐up and potential differences between included and excluded patients could affect our results. Furthermore, the reliance on patient‐reported outcomes without objective measures and the absence of postoperative imaging to confirm rotator cuff integrity represent additional limitations.

Prospects of Clinical Applications

Our procedure could simultaneously treat rotator cuff tears, impingement, and LHB pathology such as SLAP lesion, tendinitis, and subluxation. This versatility makes it a valuable option for managing a wide range of shoulder conditions. Moreover, the procedure does not require specialized surgical instruments such as suture hooks and suture passers, potentially expanding its applicability in different clinical settings.

Conclusion

The all arthroscopic “whole layer” rotator cuff repair technique with simultaneous biceps tenodesis is a therapeutic and efficient procedure. Both the rotator cuff tear and the biceps pathology, which usually occur together, were treated. This technique has a wide application, from partial thickness rotator cuff tear to large whole‐layer rotator cuff tear with certain modifications and the application of additional implants. This simple and reproducible procedure resulted in a satisfactory functional result and greatly reduced pain with minimal complications and reoperation.
