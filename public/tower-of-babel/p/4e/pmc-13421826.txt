High burden of histology-confirmed placental malaria and associated adverse birth outcomes among primigravidae in Northwestern Tanzania: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gilman Buhaga, Fridolin Mujuni, Subira Matiku, Richard Kiritta, Dismas Matovelo, Edgar Ndaboine, Humphrey D Mazigo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health problem in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Tanzania, contributing significantly to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Placental malaria [PM] is linked to adverse fetal outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age infants, congenital malaria, and intrauterine death. Primigravidae are particularly vulnerable due to limited malaria-specific immunity and socio-economic barriers. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of placental malaria, fetal outcomes, and associated factors among primigravidae women delivering at Sengerema Designated District Hospital [DDH], Mwanza, Tanzania.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study enrolled 422 primigravidae women from October 2024 to April 2025. Structured questionnaires and reviews of antenatal care [ANC] cards captured socio-demographic, obstetric, and medical information. Maternal peripheral, cord, and placental blood were screened for malaria using rapid diagnostic tests [RDT], microscopy, and placental histology. Neonatal outcomes recorded included birth weight, gestational age, survival, and growth for gestational age.ResultsAmong 422 primigravidae, malaria prevalence was higher by microscopy than mRDT in maternal 19.7% [95% CI 15.9–23.5) and 6.9% (95% CI 4.5–9.3], respectively Histopathology showed 39.8% prevalence. Adolescents had higher infection rates. Active, active-chronic, and past chronic infections were 15.4%, 13.0%, and 11.4%, respectively. The study showed histologically confirmed PM prevalence of 39.8% [95% CI 35.1–44.5], and 23.7% [95% CI 19.6–27.8] by microscopy. The prevalence of maternal peripheral malaria and congenital malaria by microscopy was found to be 19.7% [95% CI 15.9–23.5 and 6.9% (95% CI 4.5–9.3], respectively. Significant predictors of PM included fewer than four ANC visits [AOR = 1.75, p = 0.04] and moderate anemia [AOR = 1.84, p = 0.04]. PM was significantly associated with low birth weight [p = 0.01], preterm birth (p = 0.001), and small-for-gestational-age neonates [p = 0.048].ConclusionPlacental malaria is highly prevalent among primigravidae women attending Sengerema DDH. Early ANC attendance, supervised uptake of IPTp, and improved diagnostic detection are critical to reducing malaria-related maternal and neonatal complications.LimitationCross-sectional design limits causality and generalizability. Use of LNMP and lack of PCR may have caused misclassification and under-detection. Exclusion of HIV-positive women and incomplete ITN/IPTp assessment may affect applicability.Keywords: Placental malaria, Adverse birth outcomes, Primigravidae, Tanzania

Introduction

Malaria, caused by Plasmodium species, remains highly prevalent in tropical regions. Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax are the most clinically significant species infecting humans, followed by P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi [1], and more recently, P. cynomolgi [2]. In 2024, an estimated 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 deaths occurred globally, with Africa accounting for 94% of cases [1, 3] Tanzania is among 11 countries with high malaria prevalence, contributing approximately 4% of global malaria deaths [3]. Tanzania has a heterogeneous malaria burden, with some regions experiencing high and stable transmission, while others have low transmission intensity. Chacha G et al. showed marked regional variation, ranging from 21.6% in Tanga to 44.4% in Kagera [4, 5]. Immunity to malaria gradually develops in adults through repeated exposure, leading to milder infections [6, 7]. However, pregnant women and children under five remain particularly vulnerable [3].

Malaria transmission occurs through the bite of an infected Anopheles mosquito, as well as via contaminated sharp objects, blood transfusion, or from mother to child, the latter resulting in congenital malaria [8–10]. Due to physiological changes during pregnancy, including reduced immunity, pregnant women are more susceptible to the adverse outcome of malaria compared to their non-pregnant counterparts. In pregnant women, Plasmodium falciparum has unique features due to its effects on the placenta. The Plasmodium falciparum-infected erythrocytes [IE] express the surface variant antigens, including P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 [PfEMP-1]. The unique variant of PfEMPI that mediates placental sequestration is called VAR2CSA, which binds to chondroitin sulphate A [CSA] in syncytiotrophoblasts of the placenta [11–13]. The expression of PfEMP-1-VAR2CSA and adherence of IE in the placenta make the parasite escape spleen clearance and cause pregnant women to experience the relative absence of peripheral parasite and be asymptomatic [12, 13].

While this sequestration offers survival advantages to the parasite, it causes placental damage. Histopathological features include the presence of parasites and leukocytes in intervillous spaces, hemozoin-laden macrophages, fibrin deposits, trophoblast proliferation, cytotrophoblastic hyperplasia, and thickening of the trophoblastic basement membrane [14, 15]. These changes impair placental exchange and contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as low birth weight, preterm birth, intrauterine fetal death, small-for-gestational-age infants, and congenital malaria [12, 13]. Primigravidae women are at the highest risk due to a lack of pre-existing immunity to VAR2CSA, as variant surface antigen type PfEMEP1-VARCSA2 is sequestered and adhered in the placenta, triggering a pro-inflammatory immune response, which is protective and reduces the severity of malaria illness in subsequent infection [16–18]. Congenital malaria, the vertical transmission of Plasmodium from mother to infant, may lead to neonatal anemia, jaundice, coagulopathy, shock, or death if untreated [10, 19].

Research consistently shows higher rates of placental and congenital malaria among primigravidae. In Nigeria, 19.0% of pregnant women are reported to have placental malaria, with prevalence significantly higher in primigravidae [28.4%] compared to multigravida [13.8%] [20]. In the Republic of Congo, 13.98% of newborns have congenital malaria, more than half [53.85%] born to primigravidae mothers [21]. In Tanzania, placental malaria prevalence ranges from 10 to 37.6% [22, 23].

To reduce malaria in pregnancy, the WHO recommends interventions such as Intermittent Preventive Treatment in pregnancy [IPTp], use of insecticide-treated nets [ITNs], and prompt malaria treatment [24]. However, the uptake of these preventive measures remains suboptimal in many low- and middle-income countries. For example, only 62.2% of pregnant women in Ghana attend at least four antenatal care [ANC] visits [25] While in Tanzania, the figure is even lower at 51% [26] As a result, many women receive inadequate malaria education and preventive services. Furthermore, only 32% of Tanzanian pregnant women receive the recommended three doses of IPTp-SP, 28.3% in rural areas, and 33.1% in the Lake Zone [23]. Uptake is particularly low among primigravidae, many of whom are adolescents with limited decision-making autonomy and significant socioeconomic barriers to accessing ANC [27].

Sengerema district, located along the shore of Lake Victoria, has a tropical climate that provides a favorable breeding environment for mosquitoes. Poverty, low literacy levels, and limited access to healthcare services increase the malaria burden in the area. Additionally, livelihoods such as fishing, livestock keeping, and farming expose primigravidae to higher mosquito contact, increasing their risk of infection and subsequent placental malaria. Given these challenges, this study aims to assess current placental malaria infection rates and associated pathological changes, evaluate adherence to malaria preventive measures, and determine fetal outcomes among primigravidae attending Sengerema DDH.

Methodology

Study design and study area

The study used a hospital-based cross-sectional design conducted from October 2024 to April 2025 at Sengerema Designated District Hospital in Mwanza, Tanzania. Sengerema District has a population of 425,415, a tropical climate with 930–1200 mm of annual rainfall and temperatures between 11 and 28 °C, and economic activities mainly involving fishing, livestock keeping, and small-scale business. The district has one hospital and several lower-level health facilities providing antenatal and gynecological services. As a malaria-endemic area, pregnant women and children are at high risk, and the district aims to reduce malaria prevalence from 25 to 18% by June 2024.

Study population

The study included primigravidae women with 28 weeks of gestation and above who were admitted for delivery at Sengerema DDH. As a high-risk group lacking prior malaria immunity, they were screened for malaria, and their newborns were assessed to determine the prevalence of congenital malaria and related adverse outcomes. Gestational age was primarily estimated using the last normal menstrual period [LNMP]. Where available, this was corroborated with ultrasound findings from antenatal records.

Selection criteria

The study included primigravidae women at ≥ 28 weeks’ gestation admitted for labor or elective delivery, excluding those with multiple pregnancies or conditions such as preeclampsia, renal disease, HIV/AIDS, or sickle cell disease.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using the Kish–Leslie formula [1965] assuming a 50% prevalence, 5% margin of error, and 95% confidence level, yielding 384 participants. After adding a 10% non-response rate, the minimum required sample size increased to 422.

Sample collection

A total of 422 primigravidae women were enrolled in the study using a probability sampling technique, specifically systematic random sampling. Eligible participants were identified from the antenatal and labour ward registers, and every kth woman meeting the inclusion criteria was selected until the required sample size was achieved.

Data were collected via structured questionnaires on socio-demographics, obstetric history, and fetal outcomes. Maternal blood samples were taken before delivery for malaria testing and hemoglobin measurement, while cord and placental blood samples were collected after birth for malaria screening. Congenital malaria was defined as the presence of malaria parasites in the umbilical cord within 30 min after delivery, as determined by a rapid diagnostic test [RDT] or microscopic examination. Additionally, placental tissue biopsies were collected and sent to Bugando Medical Centre for pathological analysis.

Sample processing procedures

Hemoglobin was measured pre-delivery using a HemoCue photometer, with anemia classified as mild, moderate, or severe [< 11 g/dL]. Maternal malaria was diagnosed using rapid diagnostic tests targeting histidine-rich protein 2 [HRP2] and parasite lactate dehydrogenase [pLDH] antigens, confirmed by Giemsa-stained blood smear microscopy.

Placental tissue preparation for histopathology evaluation

Placental tissue was collected immediately after delivery, and a 2 cm full-thickness biopsy was obtained from the maternal surface by a trained principal investigator, taken midway between the placental edge and the cord insertion site after rinsing with normal saline. The specimen, including both maternal and fetal surfaces, was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and stored at 4 °C for a minimum of one week in a labeled container corresponding to the participant’s questionnaire number, before transportation to the Pathology Department at Bugando Medical Centre (BMC), where tissue processing was done using in-house SOPs. Two tissue blocks were made from each specimen. Following paraffin embedding, 2 Sects. (4 µm thick) were made from each block. From each block, 1 slide was stained with H&E and 1 with Giemsa. Histopathological evaluation was done by two independent pathologists, and in cases of ambiguity, the case was discussed by both pathologists. The assessed features of placental malaria were the presence of Plasmodium parasites and associated lesions such as syncytial knotting, syncytial rupture, increased placental barrier thickness, villous necrosis, expanded intervillous spaces, inflammatory infiltrates (mononuclear and polymorphonuclear cells), parasitized erythrocytes, and hemozoin pigment. Placental malaria was finally classified according to Rogerson criteria as acute infection (parasites without pigment), chronic infection (parasites with pigment in monocytes and/or fibrin), past infection (pigment only without parasites), or no infection (absence of both parasites and pigment) [28].

Statistical data analysis

Study data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2010 and analyzed in STATA version 15. Categorical variables were summarized using proportions, tables, and histograms. Associations between independent and outcome variables were assessed using chi-square, odds ratios, and Fisher’s exact tests, with logistic regression applied for independent variables. Variables with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis and biologically plausible factors were included in multivariate logistic regression, in which a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and variables showing significance were further stratified.

Results

Sociodemographic

Out of 587 primigravidae women, 422 were enrolled, mostly self-referrals [92.7%]. The mean age was 19.8 ± 2.8 years, with 55.5% under 20. Most were married [79.4%] and had basic education [82.2%], while 70.6% were unemployed. About 66.8% had four or more antenatal contacts, predominantly attended by nurses [96.5%]. Only 21.5% booked in the first trimester, while 70.6% booked in the second. Of those using IPTp, 97.1% received it at ANC clinics, but only 85.1% had direct-observed therapy. [Table 1].

Prevalence of congenital malaria

The prevalence of cord blood malaria was 1.4% by mRDT and 6.9% by microscopy. Among microscopy-positive cases, 46.4% were born to mothers aged < 20 years and 53.6% to those ≥ 20 years. For mRDT-positive cases, 33.3% occurred in mothers < 20 years and 66.7% in those ≥ 20 years. [Fig. 1].

Prevalence of peripheral, placental and congenital malaria

Prevalence of peripheral malaria

Among 422 participants, the overall prevalence of maternal peripheral malaria was 19.7% by microscopy and 3.1% by rapid diagnostic test [mRDT] [Fig. 1]. By age, microscopy-positive cases included 44 [54.3%] women under 20 years and 37 [45.7%] aged 20 years or older. Similarly, mRDT-positive cases comprised 7 [53.8%] under 20 years and 6 [46.2%] aged 20 years and above. These results indicate a slightly higher prevalence of peripheral malaria among adolescent primigravidae compared to older women. [Fig. 2].

Prevalence of peripheral, placental, and congenital malaria according to age

Prevalence of placental malaria

The number of malaria-positive cases varied notably by diagnostic method and age group. The prevalence of placental malaria was 4.7% based on mRDT, 23.7% on microscopy, and 39.8% on histology (Fig. 1). Among microscopy-positive placental cases, 58 [58%] were in women below 20 years of age and 42 [42%] in those aged 20 years and above. For mRDT-positive placental cases, the distribution was equal, with 10 cases [50%] in each age group. Among participants below 20 years, histology detected 91 cases [54.2%] while 71 cases [45.8%] were observed in the group aged 20 years and above (Fig. 2). Based on Rogerson's criteria, the prevalence of histopathological findings was 15.4% classified as active infection, 13.0% active-chronic, and 11.4% as past chronic (Table 2) (Fig. 3).

Factors associated with placental malaria based on histological infection status

A total of 422 primigravidae women were analyzed for factors associated with placental malaria [PM] based on histological changes [Table 3]. In univariate analysis, booking in the second trimester [cOR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.01–2.79, p = 0.044], having fewer than four antenatal visits [cOR = 1.88, 95% CI 1.24–2.84, p = 0.003], taking 1–2 doses of IPTp-SP versus ≥ 3 doses [cOR = 1.88, 95% CI 1.22–2.90, p = 0.005], and moderate anemia [hemoglobin 7.0–9.9 g/dL, cOR = 1.69, 95% CI 1.0–2.80, p = 0.049] were significantly associated with PM. In multivariate analysis, only having fewer than four ANC visits [AOR = 1.75, 95% CI 1.02–2.98, p = 0.041] and moderate anemia [AOR = 1.84, 95% CI 1.04–3.28, p = 0.038] remained significantly associated with placental malaria.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a high burden of malaria among primigravidae women delivering at Sengerema DDH, with prevalences of 19.7% for peripheral malaria, 39.8% for histology-confirmed placental malaria [PM], and 6.9% for congenital malaria. Placental malaria was significantly associated with adverse birth outcomes, including low birth weight [LBW], preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age [SGA] neonates, and moderate maternal anemia. These findings highlight the persistent impact of malaria in pregnancy in high-transmission rural settings despite ongoing preventive interventions.

Peripheral malaria prevalence was higher when detected by microscopy than by rapid diagnostic tests [RDTs], underscoring the limited sensitivity of RDTs in pregnancy. This discrepancy likely reflects low-density parasitemia, placental sequestration of infected erythrocytes, HRP2/3 gene deletions, antigen persistence, and partial immunity in endemic areas. [29, 30]. Adolescents and younger primigravidae accounted for a greater proportion of infections, consistent with reduced malaria-specific immunity, delayed ANC attendance, and inconsistent use of preventive measures. The observed prevalence was lower than PCR-based estimates from Tanzania but higher than reports from the Democratic Republic of Congo [DRC], reflecting differences in diagnostic methods and transmission intensity [31, 32].

Congenital malaria was detected in 6.9% of newborns, all of whom were asymptomatic at birth, suggesting a protective effect from maternal antibodies and fetal hemoglobin. Although more cases were observed in mothers aged ≥ 20 years, the difference between age groups was minimal, suggesting that maternal age was not a strong determinant of congenital malaria in this population. This relatively low prevalence was compared with a study in Nigeria and Sudan. [9, 33]. Comparable findings were reported in Guinea [7.3%] and Uganda [6.1%) [19, 34]. This may reflect partial protection from trans placentally acquired maternal antibodies and fetal hemoglobin [35], as well as moderate uptake of IPTp-SP and ITN use. Nevertheless, the finding confirms ongoing vertical transmission and the need for improved detection, as RDTs performed poorly in identifying neonatal infections.

Placental malaria prevalence varied markedly by diagnostic approach, with histology detecting substantially more infections than microscopy or RDTs. Histopathology remains the gold standard for identifying both active and past infections and characterizing placental damage [12]. Using Rogerson’s criteria, over one-third of infections showed active or active–chronic pathology, indicating ongoing placental inflammation at delivery. This prevalence is higher than that of Mlugu et al., who reported 9.4% histopathological PM in Tanzania. [36], but comparable to high-transmission settings in Nigeria [38.4%] [37] and studies by Wilson and Natallie et al., with 41.05 and 45% respectively [38, 39]. The higher prevalence observed compared with other studies in Tanzania may be explained by the exclusive inclusion of primigravidae and the high malaria transmission intensity in the Lake Zone.

Several factors were associated with placental malaria. In multivariate analysis, fewer than four ANC visits and moderate maternal anemia remained independent predictors. Late ANC booking and suboptimal IPTp-SP uptake were significant in univariate analysis; IPTp-SP was not statistically significant in the multivariate analysis, likely due to high coverage that reduced variability, incomplete adherence to recommended doses, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as malaria exposure and health-seeking behavior, which may have reduced its observed effect after adjustment. Although two-thirds of participants attended at least four ANC visits, only one-fifth booked in the first trimester, limiting the timely delivery of preventive interventions consistent with several previous studies [20, 40–42]. These findings reinforce the importance of early and regular ANC attendance, consistent with WHO recommendations [24].

Insecticide-treated nets coverage in this study was very high at 91.2%. This may be because mosquito nets are provided free to every pregnant woman during antenatal care, and this was higher than a national estimate of 51% [43]. Despite a suggested protective effect against placental malaria, ITN use was not significantly associated with reduced risk, differing from reports in DRC and Nigeria. [44, 45]. The extremely high reported ITN coverage, which reduces statistical contrast between users and non-users, may account for the lack of a statistically significant protective association between ITN use and placental malaria in this study. Another possible reason may be due to limited variability, self-report bias, and unmeasured factors such as adherence and environmental exposure, which may have weakened its effect after adjustment. Furthermore, the effectiveness of ITN may be diminished by widespread pyrethroid resistance in malaria vectors, and self-reported net use may overestimate consistent nightly protection. Studies on malaria in pregnancy conducted throughout sub-Saharan Africa have revealed similar findings. [46, 47].

Despite the relatively high uptake of IPTp-SP in this study, only 71.6% of women received three or more doses, falling short of the 80% national target. [43]. A combination of late prenatal care initiation, insufficient visit frequency to enable monthly SP administration, and health-system obstacles like SP stock-outs and incomplete directly observed therapy are probably responsible for this shortfall. [26]. Behavioral factors may also play a role, such as a lack of awareness about the advantages of IPTp and worries about adverse drug reactions. Stronger early ANC engagement, supply-chain dependability, and supervised drug administration may enhance malaria prevention during pregnancy, as comparable issues impacting IPTp delivery and adherence have been extensively documented in malaria-endemic settings [48, 49].

In this study, moderate anemia was strongly associated with placental malaria, likely reflecting chronic inflammation, increased erythrocyte destruction, impaired erythropoiesis, and splenic clearance resulting from placental sequestration of parasites. The high overall anemia prevalence suggests an interaction between malaria, nutritional deficiencies, and limited access to effective hematinic supplementation, as reported in other malaria-endemic African settings [41, 50].

Sociodemographic factors such as education, marital status, and employment were not significantly associated with placental malaria, possibly due to the homogeneity of the study population, which consisted largely of adolescent, unemployed women with similar socioeconomic backgrounds. Similar findings were reported in Uganda, contrasting with studies in Nigeria, where education significantly influenced malaria prevention behaviors [22, 51, 52]. In contrast to some urban or more heterogeneous settings, structural and health system factors, particularly ANC access and quality, appear to play a more dominant role in this context.

Additionally, placental malaria in this study was significantly associated with low birth weight [LBW], preterm birth, and small-for-gestational-age [SGA] infants, findings that are consistent with extensive evidence from malaria-endemic regions. Sequestration of Plasmodium falciparum–infected erythrocytes within the intervillous space triggers inflammatory cell infiltration, fibrin deposition, and thickening of the placental barrier, which impair uteroplacental blood flow and nutrient transfer, thereby contributing to fetal growth restriction and earlier delivery [53–56]. Similar associations between placental malaria and LBW, SGA, and prematurity have been reported in studies from sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, where histologically confirmed infection is strongly linked to adverse fetal outcomes [57–59].

The observation that mean birth weight was lowest among women with past chronic infection suggests that even resolved infections may leave residual placental damage, such as pigment deposition and vascular remodeling, which continue to affect placental efficiency and fetal growth, a pattern previously described in placental histology studies [60, 61] No significant association with stillbirth was observed in this cohort, most likely due to the small number of intrauterine fetal deaths, as larger pooled analyses have demonstrated an increased stillbirth risk in malaria-exposed pregnancies [60, 61]. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of preventing placental infection throughout pregnancy to reduce the burden of fetal growth restriction and prematurity in malaria-endemic settings.

Strength and limitations

The use of placental histopathology and the large sample size strengthen the validity of the findings. Despite these strengths, this study has several limitations. First, its hospital-based cross-sectional design limits generalizability to the wider population and precludes causal inference. Second, gestational age estimation relied partly on the last normal menstrual period [LNMP], which may have introduced misclassification. Third, the absence of molecular diagnostic methods such as PCR may have led to under-detection of submicroscopic or low-density infections. In addition, pfhrp2/pfhrp3 gene deletions were not assessed, limiting interpretation of potential false-negative HRP2-based RDT results. The study also did not fully capture adherence, timing, and correct utilization of ITNs and IPTp-SP, which may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, the exclusion of HIV-positive women limits the applicability of findings to all pregnant populations, particularly in high HIV-prevalence settings.

The study did not stratify adverse birth outcomes by histopathological classifications of placental malaria [active, active-chronic, and past infection], which may have obscured differences in risk between these groups.

Anemia

Anemia

Anemia in pregnancy was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) as a hemoglobin level <11.0 g/dL, with severity classified as mild (10.0–10.9 g/dL), moderate (7.0–9.9 g/dL), and severe (<7.0 g/dL).

Conclusion

Placental malaria remains highly prevalent among primigravidae delivering at Sengerema DDH and is strongly associated with low birth weight, preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age neonates, and moderate maternal anemia. The burden is higher among women with delayed antenatal booking and suboptimal IPTp-SP uptake. These findings indicate that malaria in pregnancy continues to contribute substantially to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, particularly among first-time mothers in high-transmission settings.
