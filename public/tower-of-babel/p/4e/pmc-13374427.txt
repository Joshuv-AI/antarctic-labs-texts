Incidence and predictors of treatment default among children aged 6–59 months with severe acute malnutrition in stabilisation centres in western Ethiopia: a prospective follow-up study
Authors: Diriba Bekele Geleta, Lencho Kajela Solbana, Lalesa Tadese Chuko, Ibse Jirata Biri, Gutu Endalew Teina, Yonas Abebe Tarefa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTreatment default from inpatient care for severe acute malnutrition (SAM) leaves children at high risk of mortality, relapse and incomplete recovery. Identifying factors associated with treatment default is essential to improve programme performance and reduce preventable deaths.MethodsWe conducted a hospital-based prospective follow-up study among children aged 6–59 months admitted with SAM to inpatient stabilisation centres of tertiary hospitals in western Ethiopia between 10 January and 15 August 2025. Eligible children were consecutively enrolled at admission and followed until treatment default, recovery, death, transfer or end of follow-up. Time-to-event data were analysed using Kaplan-Meier methods and Gompertz regression to estimate adjusted HRs (AHRs) with 95% CIs.ResultThree hundred children contributed 3182 child-days of observation. The cumulative incidence of treatment default was 17.7% (95% CI 13.0 to 22.2), with a median time to default of 18 days. Independent predictors of higher hazard of treatment default included rural residence (AHR 4.25; 95% CI 1.49 to 6.57), previous SAM treatment (AHR 2.47; 95% CI 1.06 to 5.76), female-headed household (AHR 3.14; 95% CI 2.32 to 7.45), greater distance to a health facility (AHR 3.58; 95% CI 1.98 to 5.12) and caregiver illiteracy (AHR 6.17; 95% CI 4.12 to 8.34).ConclusionThe default rate exceeded Sphere standards for inpatient SAM care. Socioeconomic and geographic barriers were key predictors of treatment default, indicating the need for targeted multisectoral interventions to improve retention in inpatient treatment for complicated SAM.

Introduction

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in children is characterised by severe wasting and/or nutritional oedema and is diagnosed using anthropometric criteria based on the WHO growth chart reference median.1 According to WHO guidelines, SAM is defined as a weight-for-height (WFH)/length Z-score <−3, a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) <11.5 cm and/or the presence of bilateral pitting oedema.2 SAM is classified as ‘complicated’ when accompanied by clinical signs of infection, metabolic disturbance, severe appetite loss or other medical complications requiring inpatient care; in the absence of these features, it is considered ‘uncomplicated’ and can be managed on an outpatient basis.3

In low-income and lower middle-income countries experiencing the double burden of malnutrition, undernutrition remains a particular concern.4 According to the 2018 joint child malnutrition estimates by UNICEF, WHO and the World Bank, sub-Saharan African countries collectively have about 2.2 million cases of SAM.5 Among these countries, Ethiopia has the second highest burden of malnutrition despite implementing global and national nutrition commitments since 2009.6 In Ethiopia, SAM is a major public health and economic problem and is the primary diagnosis in approximately 20% of paediatric hospital admissions,7 with SAM-related mortality rates ranging from 3.8% to 12.5%.8

In Ethiopia, children with complicated SAM are managed according to standard national and WHO-aligned protocols in facility-based settings, which include admission to inpatient stabilisation centres and provision of comprehensive clinical and nutritional treatment.9 However, previous work has shown that approximately 20%–36% of children self-discharge after treatment has begun, contributing to readmission rates of 1.2% to 10.4%.1012 Moreover, the country has the proportion of treatment default and non-recovery rates of 1.67% to 25.2% and 2% to 61.13%, respectively.13

Default from treatment is a common challenge that undermines recovery, increases the risk of complications and contributes to preventable deaths.14 When children discontinue care before achieving nutritional recovery, they face an elevated risk of mortality and relapse, as their vulnerability to infection persists due to impaired immune function and incomplete rehabilitation.13 Untreated or partially treated cases also hinder normal growth, delay cognitive development and contribute to long-term deficits in educational attainment and economic productivity.15 16 At the household level, treatment default imposes additional financial strain and caregiving demands, while at programme level it undermines the effectiveness of nutritional rehabilitation services, leads to wastage of scarce therapeutic resources and hinders attainment of Sphere Handbook guidelines (Humanitarian Charter and Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response) performance standards.17 At the broader community level, persistent treatment discontinuation perpetuates the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition, poverty and poor child survival, posing a major barrier to global nutrition and child health targets.1

Living in rural areas, readmission of SAM cases and caregivers without formal education have been identified as important reasons for inpatient treatment default among children with SAM.18 According to SPHERE standards for the management of SAM, the treatment default rate in well-functioning programmes should remain below 15%.19 Evidence on treatment default in complicated SAM remains limited, often relying on single-facility retrospective reviews with scant household socioeconomic and geographic access data. This multicentre prospective study addresses this by capturing clinical factors, travel time and household wealth via time-to-event analysis to characterise default timing during admission.

We aimed to assess treatment default incidence and predictors among children aged 6–59 months starting inpatient treatment for complicated SAM in western Ethiopia stabilisation centres.

Method and materials

Study setting and period

This study was conducted in four tertiary hospitals in western Ethiopia: Wollega University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (WUCSH), Metu Karl Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (MKCSH), Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC) and Ambo University Referral Hospital (AURH). These facilities serve large catchment populations across Oromia and neighbouring regions and provide comprehensive paediatric services, including dedicated units for the inpatient management of complicated SAM. WUCSH, JUMC and MKCSH have specialised SAM/stabilisation units equipped for clinical care, therapeutic feeding and psychomotor stimulation, while AURH functions as a major referral hospital with full paediatric inpatient and outpatient services.

The study was conducted from 10 January to 15 August 2025 among 6–59 months children admitted with SAM to WUCSH, MKCSH, JUMC and AURH.

Study design

Prospective follow-up study was conducted among 6–59-month-old children admitted to stabilisation centres of WUCSH, MKCSH, JUMC and AURH with SAM from 10 January to 15 August 2025.

Population

Source population

The source population was all children aged 6–59 months who were admitted to stabilisation centre of tertiary hospitals in western Ethiopia with SAM.

Study Population

All consecutively enrolled 6–59 months old children admitted to stabilisation centre of WUCSH, MKCSH, JUMC and AURH with SAM.

Target population

All children aged 6–59 months admitted to stabilisation centres at tertiary hospitals (WUCSH, MKCSH, JUMC and AURH) in western Ethiopia for SAM treatment.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

All children aged 6–59 months with SAM admitted to the stabilisation centre of the selected hospitals during the study period were included.

Exclusion criteria

Children with unavailable primary caregivers, prior enrolees readmitted after default or caregiver refusal of consent.

Sample size determination, sampling technique and procedure

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using STATA V.17 (Using STATA command power cox, failprob () wdprob() effect(hratio)), incorporating significant variables from a similar study. Assumptions included a 10% withdrawal probability, 95% CI, 80% power, a default proportion of 0.097 and an SD of 0.5. The adjusted HR (AHR) of 2.99 was derived from a study conducted in Northwest Ethiopia, yielding a final sample size of 300 children.

Sampling technique and procedure

This study was conducted at four tertiary hospitals in western: WUCSH, MKCSH, JUMC and AURH. We employed a simple random sampling technique to select the hospitals, utilising a sampling frame derived from the six tertiary hospitals in Western Ethiopia. Screening of all new admissions was conducted continuously (days, nights and weekends) to minimise time-of-day and day-of-week bias. Eligibility was assessed within 24 hours of admission and documented in a screening log that recorded all admissions, eligibility status, enrolment status and reasons for non-enrolment to monitor potential selection bias. Caregivers of eligible children were approached for informed consent, and enrolled children were assigned a unique study ID linking the ward register number, hospital and admission date. Using consecutive sampling, 92 eligible children were enrolled from JUMC, 71 from WUCSH, 68 from MKCSH and 69 from AURH, proportional to their respective patient flows, for a total sample of 300. Baseline sociodemographic and household data were collected from caregivers, and participants were prospectively followed until the occurrence of the outcome of interest (treatment default) or censoring.

Data collection technique and procedure

A structured questionnaire, originally adapted in English from the study conducted in Northwest Ethiopia and subsequently modified based on a review of relevant literature, was employed for data collection. The content validity of the tool was assessed by subject matter experts, who evaluated each item for its relevance and representativeness relative to the underlying construct. To ensure cultural and linguistic accessibility for the children’s caregivers, the tool was translated into the local language (Afaan Oromoo) by two language experts, with a back-translation process conducted to verify accuracy and equivalence. Following a pretest on 5% of the sample size (15 children), the tool was refined by rearranging sections and incorporating additional important variables. For data collection, two nurses were recruited at each hospital, along with one MSc-qualified supervisor per site. Prior to the commencement of data collection, the principal investigator (PI) provided training to all data collectors and supervisors covering ethical considerations, questionnaire administration and data handling procedures. Data collection was performed 24 hours after admission, giving more time for screening for eligibility. Baseline demographic and household characteristics were collected from caregivers of the children. Clinical management data were systematically gathered throughout the follow-up period from medical records and through consultation with assigned nursing staff. This included documentation of prescribed medications, feeding protocols and nutritional interventions administered during hospitalisation.

At admission, measurements (age, weight, height, MUAC) were recorded to calculate anthropometric indicators WFH, weight-for-age and height-for-age (HFA) establishing baseline malnutrition severity. These were treated as fixed covariates in the time-to-event models. While nutritional status may fluctuate, the brief 28-day follow-up period justifies using baseline measurements as a proxy for severity, as formal modelling of longitudinal changes was not feasible. During admission, children were prospectively followed on a daily basis by data collectors using a structured follow-up form. For each participant, we documented the date of admission, daily clinical progress, date and type of treatment outcome and any transfers between wards or facilities. The primary event of interest was treatment default, defined as absence from the inpatient ward for 2 consecutive days after initiation of treatment. Children were censored at the earliest occurrence if: (i) stabilisation and discharge according to the national SAM protocol; (2) in-hospital death; (3) transfer to another health facility; (4) referred or (5) end of the study period without treatment default. Time at risk was calculated in days from the date of admission to the date of treatment default or censoring. Throughout the process, the collected data were reviewed by the trained supervisors and subsequently by the PI to verify completeness, consistency, accuracy of entries and to identify any logical errors or outliers.

Study variables

For this study, the outcome variable was treatment default, coded as 1 for defaulted and 0 for censored. Censored cases included children who died, recovered, were referred, transferred out or completed follow-up without defaulting.

Operational and term definition

Default (from inpatient SC)

Was declared for unplanned absence from stabilisation centre care defined as no return after 2 consecutive days without notification or permission during inpatient management of complicated SAM.20 The child was tracked from treatment initiation at the stabilisation centre until discharge to outpatient therapeutic programme (OTP).

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)

WHO defines SAM in children aged 6–59 months as the presence of WFH/length < −3 Z-scores, MUAC<115 mm, or bilateral pitting oedema.21

Time to default

Was determined by calculating the difference (in days) from the start of treatment for SAM (F-75 initiation) until event of interest (default).

Event

Treatment discontinued prematurely, before the patient met the inpatient-to-OTP discharge criteria.20

Censored

Children were censored if they did not default by study end or if follow-up ended due to referral, death, stabilisation or transfer.20

Transfer out

Referral to another facility or relocation to a different stabilisation centre (SC) for further care.

Follow-up time

Days from SAM treatment initiation to default, censoring (referral, death, transfer or stabilisation), or 28 days whichever came first.22

Incidence of default

The proportion of children who experience treatment default out of all children enrolled in the stabilisation centre during the study period or follow-up time.

Stabilisation centre (SC)

Specialised inpatient units designed for initial medical management of complicated SAM, providing therapeutic feeding, treatment of medical complications and stabilisation before OTP transition.

Family size

Small: ≤4 members, medium: 5–6 members, large: ≥7 members23

The wealth index

It was constructed via principal component analysis of household assets, following Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey methodology. Households were grouped into terciles: poor (lowest 40%), medium (middle 20%) and rich (highest 40%).

Data processing and analysis

Data were collected electronically using KoBo Collect and exported to Stata V.17 (StataCorp) for management and analysis. The dataset was checked for completeness and internal consistency, cleaned and coded prior to analysis. The extent of missing data was assessed; because missingness for key variables was less than 3%, analyses were conducted on a complete-case basis without imputation. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ characteristics: means (with SD) or medians (with IQRs) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. For the survival analysis, the outcome variable was defined as time (in days) from admission to treatment default. The event indicator was coded as 1 for children who defaulted from treatment and 0 for those who were censored.

Survival functions were estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods, and differences in time to treatment default between categories of explanatory variables were assessed with log rank tests.

To identify independent predictors of treatment default, we first fitted a Cox proportional hazards model. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals (global and covariate-specific tests) and inspection of log (−log) survival plots. We then fitted alternative parametric survival models (exponential, Weibull, log logistic, log normal and Gompertz) and compared model fit using Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Potential confounders were identified a priori from the literature and biological plausibility; variables with a p value ≤0.25 in bivariable analysis, together with clinically important covariates, were entered into the multivariable model to obtain effect estimates adjusted for confounding. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors and no evidence of problematic collinearity was found. The Gompertz model showed the lowest AIC and BIC and was, therefore, selected as the final model, as it provided the best fit and allowed flexible modelling of a monotonic baseline hazard. Multivariable Gompertz regression models were estimated using maximum likelihood, and results are presented as AHRs with 95% CIs. All analyses were conducted within a frequentist framework; no Bayesian methods were applied.

Data quality management

The adapted questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the target sample at Gimbi Hospital to assess feasibility, clarity and logical flow, and minor wording adjustments were made based on the findings. Prior to data collection, the PI provided structured training for data collectors and supervisors on the study protocol, use of the electronic tool and standardised procedures for interviewing caregivers and extracting data from records. Throughout the data collection period, supervisors and the PI conducted daily reviews of completed forms to check for completeness, internal consistency and plausibility; any discrepancies or missing information were corrected promptly through verification with source documents.

Ethical consideration

The consent process began with a clear oral explanation of the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality measures and the right to withdraw at any time. Following this, written informed consent was obtained using standardised consent forms, which were signed by the caregiver in the presence of a witness, in accordance with ethical guidelines for research involving human participants. For caregivers with limited literacy, the consent form was read aloud in their preferred local language, and an impartial witness confirmed that the information was accurately communicated and understood. To safeguard participant confidentiality, responses were coded with unique random identifiers, ensuring that no individual could be linked to the information provided at any stage of the study.

The study revealed several key findings regarding the participants’ health status on admission. Anthropometric measurement

During the study, over half of the participants, 170 (56.67%), were admitted with edematous SAM. A significant number of these individuals, 116 (69.41%), displayed grade III bilateral pitting oedema. Further analysis of the participants’ nutritional status revealed that more than 66% were classified as severely wasted based on their WFH ratio. Similarly, 194 (64.67%) were categorised as severely wasted using the MUAC measurement. The data also showed that a majority of the study group, 182 individuals (60.67%), were severely underweight, and 94 (31.33%) exhibited severe stunting. Regarding the specific diagnoses, over 45% of the participants were admitted with kwashiorkor. Additionally, 118 (39.33%) had marasmus, and 46 (15.33%) presented with Marasmic-kwashiorkor. Treatments and routine medications

The medical management of the study’s participants involved several key interventions, though some were more common than others. Only a minority of individuals received supportive care like blood transfusions (22%) or intravenous infusions (20.67%), and approximately 20% required feeding through NG tube. Treatment outcome

Based on the study’s findings, 53 children (17.67%) experienced a default event. Of the total children observed, 11 (3.67%) were transferred to another stabilisation centre, 18 (6.0%) died and 12 (4.0%) were referred. The majority, 206 (68.67%), were stabilised and transferred to an OTP.

Incidence and time of default

This study enrolled 300 children, who were followed for a cumulative period of 3182 children-days of observation. The median time to an event of default was 18 days (95% CI 17 to 19). The overall incidence of treatment default during the observation period was found to be 16.97 per 1000 children-days (95% CI 12.99 to 22.15). From the total treatment default, nearly half (48.14%) of the respondents were defaulted between 5 and 10 days. An estimated cumulative survival probability at 10, 15 and 20 days were 0.89, 0.81 and 0.39, respectively (figure 1).

Overall Kaplan-Meir failure estimate. The Kaplan-Meier failure curve shows a steady increase in the cumulative probability of treatment default over time. By 10 days after admission, an estimated 10% of children had defaulted, rising to 16% by day 15 of inpatient care. This pattern indicates that the first 2 weeks of inpatient care represent a critical period during which the risk of treatment default is highest.

Goodness-of-fit for the Gompertz regression model was assessed using Cox–Snell residuals. The resulting curve closely tracked the 45° reference line, indicating satisfactory overall model fit and no major lack of specification (figure 2).

Comparison of incidence of default among categories of covariates

We used log-rank tests to compare time-to-default and the corresponding cumulative incidence across categories of key covariates. Statistically significant differences were observed across categories of residence, prior treatment history, comorbidity status, family size, caregiver type, head-of-household status, travel time to the nearest health facility and caregiver educational attainment. In particular, children with a history of treatment for SAM had different default times than those without such a history (Log rank, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 2); children living ≥2 hours from a health facility differed from those living <2 hours away (Log rank, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 1) and default time and cumulative incidence differed between children whose caregivers were illiterate and those with formal education (Log rank, p=0.001) (online supplemental figure 3). The observed separation of stratified survival curves across these factors indicates meaningful variation in both the risk and timing of default, underscoring the salience of access-related and caregiver-related determinants. These unadjusted comparisons motivate inclusion of these variables in subsequent multivariable time-to-event models to account for potential confounding.

Proportional hazard assumption

Proportional hazard assumption was satisfied based on Schoenfeld residuals (global p=0.67) and log (-log) survival plots.

Model comparison

Four survival model specifications were compared using AIC and BIC; the Gompertz model had the lowest AIC (147) and BIC (228.5) and was selected as the best-fitting, most parsimonious model before including covariates.

Predictors of default

Following the initial bivariate analyses, covariates meeting a liberal screening criterion (p<0.25), including age category, place of residence, treatment history, comorbidity, wealth index, head of household, family size, distance to the nearest healthcare facility, media exposure, type of caregiver, HFA and caregiver educational attainment, were carried forward into a multivariable Gompertz regression model. Statistical significance in the final model was set at p<0.05.

In the multivariable Gompertz regression model, five variables were independently associated with time to treatment default. Children from rural areas had more than a fourfold higher hazard of default compared with those from urban settings (AHR 4.25; 95% CI 1.49 to 6.57). A history of previous treatment for SAM was associated with approximately a two-and-a-half-fold increase in the hazard of default (AHR 2.47; 95% CI 1.06 to 5.76). Living in a female-headed household was associated with a more than threefold higher hazard of default (AHR 3.14; 95% CI 2.32 to 7.45). Travel time to the health facility exceeding 2 hours also substantially increased the hazard of default (AHR 3.58; 95% CI 1.98 to 5.12). The strongest association was observed for caregiver illiteracy, which was linked to more than a sixfold higher hazard of treatment default (AHR 6.17; 95% CI 4.12 to 8.34). These findings highlight the importance of geographic accessibility, household structure, prior treatment exposure and caregiver education in determining retention in inpatient SAM care (table 3).

Discussion

This study estimated the incidence of treatment default and identified its predictors among children aged 6–59 months admitted with SAM in western Ethiopia, with the aim of informing programme design and quality improvement. Default is a critical programme outcome in SAM care because it compromises treatment effectiveness, increases the risk of relapse and mortality and can bias monitoring indicators if not systematically captured.19 21 24 Our findings add to the evidence on context-specific drivers of disengagement from SAM services and identify practical targets for intervention in line with global guidance.19 24 By prospectively collecting both clinical and household-level socioeconomic data across multiple tertiary hospitals, this study provides programme-relevant estimates that are less susceptible to underascertainment of default than routine record reviews.

The incidence of treatment default was 17.67% (95% CI 13.3 to 22.0), exceeding the SPHERE performance threshold of <15% and indicating suboptimal retention in inpatient care.19 The Kaplan-Meier failure function showed that the cumulative probability of default rose rapidly during the first 2 weeks after admission, suggesting that early barriers such as caregiving and economic pressures to interfere continuation of treatment. Survival modelling using the Gompertz distribution further indicated an increasing baseline hazard of default over time on treatment, conditional on covariates, consistent with a pattern in which the pressures of prolonged hospitalisation accumulate and at some point outweigh perceived benefits. Taken together, these findings underscore the benefit of the prospective design and survival approach for identifying high-risk time windows and tailoring support such as counselling, logistical support and close monitoring especially during this initial period. Our estimate is similar to the 16.5% default reported in northwest Ethiopia25 and to findings from other low-resource settings where default rates often cluster near or above the SPHERE threshold in the presence of access barriers and supply constraints.24 26 Conversely, lower default proportions have been reported in some Ethiopian studies (2.2% and 9.7%),9 10 27 whereas a study from Nigeria documented a higher rate (24.1%).2 Such variation likely reflects differences in programme modality (inpatient vs outpatient), operational definitions of default, follow-up intensity and the handling of competing outcomes such as death and transfer.19 21 Our prospective design, which followed children to a clearly defined outcome and incorporated socioeconomic covariates, provides a more robust and policy-relevant estimate than retrospective analyses that may miss unrecorded defaults.10

Several factors emerged as independent predictors of treatment default. Children from rural areas had a substantially higher hazard of default than those from urban settings, consistent with findings from northwest Ethiopia.10 This likely reflects cumulative access constraints, including longer travel distances, seasonal road inaccessibility, transport costs and time away from farming or income-generating activities. Rural households are more likely to experience chronic poverty, food insecurity and limited access to formal employment, making prolonged hospital stays particularly disruptive to livelihoods.28 29 They may also have fewer social networks to share caregiving responsibilities, lower exposure to health information and less familiarity with hospital systems, which can reduce confidence in, and perceived necessity of, completing treatment. In addition, the concentration of higher level SAM services in urban tertiary hospitals means that rural families often navigate multiple referral steps and facilities, increasing complexity and creating more decision points at which they may opt out of care.28 30 Longer travel time (>2 hours) further increased the hazard of default, corroborating studies from Uganda, a multicountry review and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which highlight distance and travel time as major barriers to continuity of care.13 31 32 Travel time of more than 2 hours likely operates as a direct barrier to continuity of care through both financial and opportunity costs. This might be due to long journeys to hospital mean caregivers must pay for transport, forgo income and arrange care for other children. These costs grow with each extra day of admission or follow-up and can lead caregivers to leave once a child appears visibly improved, before full nutritional recovery. This supports interventions to reduce indirect costs of inpatient care such as transport support, flexible visiting and, when clinically appropriate, earlier transition to ambulatory or outpatient care and to prioritise closer follow-up for children from distant rural areas.

A history of previous SAM treatment was also associated with increased default, in line with Ethiopian10 and Ugandan evidence.32 Previous exposure to SAM treatment may further interact with these pressures: caregivers, who have already experienced a long or burdensome treatment episode, or a relapse, may become discouraged about the value of remaining in care. This highlights the need for enhanced counselling and psychosocial support for families with previous SAM episodes, including clear communication about relapse risk, the expected duration of treatment and tailored problem-solving around barriers to staying in care. Children from female-headed households had a higher risk of default, possibly reflecting constrained financial and social support; this is consistent with broader evidence that paternal involvement is associated with better adherence and child outcomes in acute and chronic paediatric conditions.33 Finally, caregiver illiteracy was strongly associated with treatment default, in agreement with Ethiopian and Ugandan studies showing that low education is linked to poorer understanding of health messages, less favourable perceptions of care and reduced adherence.10 32 34 Together, these findings underscore the need for strategies that address geographic and economic barriers (eg, transport support, early transition to ambulatory care, community follow-up), strengthen family and particularly paternal engagement and enhance the clarity and accessibility of health education for caregivers with limited literacy. All these pathways likely operate alongside unmeasured health-system factors such as perceived quality of care, staff–caregiver communication, waiting times and the degree of psychosocial support offered to caregivers, which were not fully captured in our data but may either mitigate or exacerbate the impact of socioeconomic barriers.

The shared frailty model indicated significant unobserved heterogeneity between hospitals (frailty variance θ=3.42; p=0.032), justifying the inclusion of a random effect. The positive Gompertz shape parameter (γ=0.316) suggests that, conditional on covariates, the baseline hazard of default increased over time on treatment. Default incidence differed markedly between facilities: Wollega University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital had the highest default proportion (18.2%), whereas Jimma University Medical Center had the lowest (6.45%). This nearly threefold difference likely reflects differences in catchment populations and contextual conditions; for example, Wollega University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital serves children from conflict-affected and highly insecure areas, including internally displaced families facing severe economic hardship and limited access to transport. These findings indicate that default from SAM treatment is shaped not only by individual and household characteristics but also by broader contextual and humanitarian factors, underscoring the need for targeted support such as integration of SAM services into humanitarian responses, transport subsidies, community outreach and defaulter tracing, and social or financial assistance for children from conflict-affected and displaced populations.

This study utilised a prospective follow-up design to examine the household and socioeconomic factors linked to default. While the study has its strengths, it is not without limitations. In our analysis, recovery, transfer to another facility and in-hospital death were treated as censoring events, although they are in fact competing outcomes that preclude the occurrence of treatment default. Standard Cox and parametric survival models, including the Gompertz model used here, do not explicitly account for such competing risks and assume that censored individuals would have had the same hazard of default as those remaining under observation. If the probabilities of recovery or death differ across exposure groups, this may bias estimates of the hazard and cumulative incidence of default. Future studies should consider applying competing-risks methods (eg, cause-specific or subdistribution hazard models) to more accurately characterise the relationship between default, recovery and death. Notably, the study was unable to evaluate hospital-related factors, such as the quality of care provided, encompassing both the hospital’s infrastructure and the expertise of its healthcare providers. Further limitation relates to the fact that nutritional status was modelled using baseline anthropometry as fixed covariates rather than time-varying exposures. Given that children’s status can fluctuate rapidly during treatment, our findings reflect the prognostic association of admission severity with treatment default, rather than the causal effects of sustained malnutrition. The inability to account for these longitudinal changes may have introduced exposure misclassification, potentially biasing the observed associations.

Conclusion

The study found that the default rate from inpatient treatment for complicated SAM in western Ethiopia exceeded Sphere Handbook benchmarks for SAM programmes. Rural residence, previous SAM treatment, greater distance to the health facility, female-headed households and caregiver illiteracy were independent predictors of treatment discontinuation. These findings apply specifically to children receiving inpatient care for complicated SAM in similar hospital settings and should not be generalised to outpatient or community-based management of uncomplicated malnutrition.

Recommendation

Healthcare interventions should prioritise targeted support for vulnerable populations, particularly rural families and female-headed households. Strategies should include improving healthcare accessibility, providing transportation assistance, developing literacy-based health education programmes and creating specialised support mechanisms for families with complex treatment histories. Comprehensive, context-specific approaches are essential to reduce treatment default rates and improve overall paediatric malnutrition management.
