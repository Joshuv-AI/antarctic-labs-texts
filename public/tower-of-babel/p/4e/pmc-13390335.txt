Factors influencing vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns in ICU practice: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chengxu Duan, Hui Li, Xinqi Zhuang, Guangjin Min, Hui Ma, Haoyan Tian, Wenke Han, Xiaohan Hua, Wei Zhao, Yenong Zhou, Manhua Zhao, Yin-Ping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ICU is a high-stress environment characterized by frequent exposure to patient suffering and traumatic events, making vicarious trauma a significant occupational hazard for nurses. Generation Z nursing interns face unique challenges during ICU rotations due to insufficient skills and limited resilience. However, their current status regarding vicarious trauma and the factors influencing it still remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate the levels of vicarious trauma and its key influencing factors within this cohort.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Zhengzhou, China, from July to September 2025, recruiting 240 Generation Z nursing interns through convenience sampling. The instruments used included a demographic characteristics questionnaire, the Vicarious Trauma Questionnaire for Disaster Rescuer (VTQ), the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ), the Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students (PISNS), and the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS). Data were analyzed using independent t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, Pearson correlation, and multiple stepwise linear regression.ResultsThe mean VTQ score for Generation Z nursing interns was 80.50 ± 26.49, with one-fifth exhibiting vicarious trauma reactions. Significant associations were observed between VTQ and negative coping styles, PISNS, and PSSS (r = -0.195 to -0.216, p < 0.01), as well as between VTQ and positive coping styles (r = 0.250, p < 0.01). The coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.443) indicated that the ICU rotation sequence, daily working hours, night shift frequency, patient death exposure, perceived workload, sleep quality, coping styles, and perceived social support together explained 44.3% of the variance in vicarious trauma.ConclusionThis study offers a comprehensive understanding of vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns by identifying multiple influencing factors, including the clinical environment, work arrangements, individual psychology, and social support. To address these issues, the nursing education system should implement measures such as optimizing the clinical environment, adjusting internship arrangements, and enhancing educational guidance to support the mental health of Generation Z nursing interns and reduce the risk of vicarious trauma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04804-8.

Background

The intensive care unit (ICU) is one of the most demanding environments within the healthcare system. Due to the high-intensity and high-stress nature of their work, ICU nurses are constantly exposed to life-and-death situations, severe patient suffering, and profound family grief [1–3]. Such repeated traumatic exposure can cause significant psychological distress, leading to vicarious trauma among nurses [4]. Vicarious trauma, defined as a negative psychological response resulting from indirect exposure to traumatic stimuli through empathetic engagement with patients or witnessing distressing events, has become a critical occupational hazard for nursing professionals [5–7]. Unlike compassion fatigue, which arises from prolonged empathic engagement with suffering and manifests as emotional exhaustion, vicarious trauma stems from repeated exposure to others’ traumatic experiences and involves profound disruptions to an individual’s cognitive schemas, worldviews, and sense of self. This traumatic reaction manifests as a range of physical and psychological symptoms, including anxiety, depression, emotional numbing, and reduced empathy [6, 8]. Furthermore, vicarious trauma impairs care quality and efficiency, increases the risk of medical errors, and contributes to higher occupational risk and staff attrition, thereby exacerbating the global nursing workforce shortage [9].

Unlike registered nurses who have accumulated clinical experience and established psychological adjustment capabilities, nursing interns, especially those from Generation Z (born 1995–2009) [3, 10], face unique challenges during their ICU rotations. Having grown up in the digital age, this cohort exhibits distinctive cognitive, behavioral, and psychological characteristics that may affect their adaptation to ICU practice [11–13]. Clinical internships represent a critical transition stage from student to nurse, during which nursing interns may not yet have fully developed clinical skills or sufficient psychological preparedness for high-stress clinical environments [2, 14]. In China’s nursing education system, all nursing students are required to complete at least one ICU or specialty ICU rotation. This clinical training typically lasts from several weeks to several months and constitutes an essential component of clinical practice education. While pedagogically necessary, exposure to high-acuity clinical environments may provoke distinct psychological stress responses [14]. As this cohort is aged 18 to 23 years, a stage characterized by immature psychological coping development and ongoing professional identity formation, their first confrontation with the fragility of life and the limits of medical intervention in the ICU may exacerbate psychological burden. This vulnerability is potentially linked to age, professional stage, and generational background [15]. Unlike those in other internship departments, Generation Z nursing interns in the ICU face a significant mismatch between their developing competencies and the demanding clinical environment [2, 16]. Short-term, high-intensity exposure to the harsh realities of the ICU internship may further increase the likelihood of physiological and psychological distress in this cohort [17].

Existing studies have demonstrated that vicarious trauma is influenced by multiple factors. Environmental and occupational stressors, such as frequent exposure to patient death, heavy workload, and long working hours, have been identified as critical factors that exacerbate the risk of vicarious trauma. This can lead to persistent negative emotions, cognitive biases, and physiological stress responses in nursing interns, thereby inducing and aggravating vicarious trauma [18–20]. Regarding individual psychological and behavioral characteristics, factors including personality, health status, sleep quality, psychological preparedness, and coping styles have also been identified as key determinants of vicarious trauma [9, 20, 21]. Several studies [9, 22, 23] have demonstrated that support from family, friends, or organizations is negatively correlated with vicarious trauma, which can alleviate the psychological impact of stressors, provide emotional and resource assistance to nursing interns, and thus reduce the severity of vicarious trauma. Meanwhile, exposure to negative online medical information, such as reports on medical disputes, healthcare worker burnout, and traumatic clinical cases, may interact with real-world clinical experiences and potentially further compromise psychological adaptation among young nursing workforce [24, 25]. As a cohort growing up in an era of pervasive digital immersion and rapid technological advancement, Generation Z nursing interns face compounded psychological challenges during ICU practice. These nursing interns are required to rapidly master clinical skills, maintain vigilance and emotional engagement with patients and their grieving families, while navigating a complex clinical environment as they develop professional competence [14, 16]. During this period, Generation Z nursing interns are continually exposed to traumatic events, including painful patient conditions, treatment failures, and ethical dilemmas, which may be associated with increased self-doubt, reduced professional commitment, and heightened psychological distress [26, 27].

Despite well-documented influencing factors among registered nurses [9, 18, 19, 23, 28, 29], current research on vicarious trauma remains limited to formally employed nursing staff, leaving several critical gaps unaddressed. First, as a core group within modern healthcare systems and the future nursing workforce, no studies have specifically explored vicarious trauma among nursing interns. Second, no research has examined vicarious trauma in the uniquely high-stress context of compulsory ICU internships in China. Third, existing studies have yet to conduct a comprehensive investigation of the factors influencing vicarious trauma, lacking an integrated analysis of clinical environmental characteristics, work arrangement factors, individual psychological traits, and social support dimensions. Given their crucial stage of professional role transition and occupational concept formation, the occupational health of Generation Z nursing interns warrants equal attention and prioritization [27, 30]. Neglecting the vicarious trauma experienced by Generation Z nursing interns could undermine the stability of future nursing teams and impede the long-term development of the nursing profession [14, 31]. Addressing their occupational health and conducting a comprehensive survey aligns not only with the requirements outlined in the “Healthy China 2030” Planning Outline for investigating and intervening in occupational health risks in high-risk industries [32], but also with calls to address the occupational health of the new generation of nurses during their critical phase of professional role transition and identity formation [27, 30]. Therefore, investigating the current status of vicarious trauma and analyzing its influencing factors among Generation Z nursing interns during ICU practice holds significant theoretical and practical value. This research can enhance understanding of occupational health challenges faced by nursing interns and help optimize psychological support systems tailored to this specific cohort in the ICU setting.

Theoretical framework

When exploring factors related to vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns in ICU practice, no single overarching theory fully explains its occurrence and mechanisms. However, several theoretical perspectives and frameworks provide a foundation for understanding this phenomenon. The Theory of Psychological Stress System proposes that psychological stress responses arise from the interaction of multiple factors, offering a comprehensive framework for analyzing the influences on vicarious trauma [33–35]. Based on a literature review and research objectives, we integrated the eight concepts of the Theory of Psychological Stress System into three core dimensions affecting vicarious trauma according to their inherent attributes. This integration guided the selection and classification of variables for this cross-sectional study on the current status and influencing factors of vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns during ICU practice (Fig. 1). The three dimensions are defined as follows: (1) The individual factors dimension integrates six personal concepts from the Theory of Psychological Stress System: cognitive appraisal, coping styles, personality traits, physical and mental responses, physical fitness, and demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age). It encompasses the main internal individual attributes examined in this study, including professional identity, coping styles, sleep quality, and relevant demographic characteristics. (2) The external stressors dimension, corresponding to the concept of life events, includes external environmental stress stimuli such as ICU rotation sequence, daily working hours, night shift frequency, patient death exposure, and perceived workload. (3) The social support resources dimension, corresponding to the concept of social support, covers external support resources available to individuals, including perceived social support from family, friends, and supervisors or colleagues. This systematic theoretical framework provides a solid foundation for analyzing the factors influencing vicarious trauma in this cohort.

Aims

This cross-sectional study aims to (1) describe the current status of vicarious trauma, demographic characteristics, clinical practice-related factors, individual psychological traits, and social support among Generation Z nursing interns in ICU practice; (2) explore the correlations between vicarious trauma and these multidimensional factors based on the Theory of Psychological Stress System; and (3) identify the key factors influencing vicarious trauma in this cohort. The findings will address the research gap concerning vicarious trauma in this specific group of nursing interns, enrich theoretical research on occupational health among Generation Z nursing interns, and provide insights for targeted psychological interventions and clinical education improvements to safeguard the mental health of the future nursing workforce.

Methods

Study setting and sampling

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit nursing interns who met the study criteria in Zhengzhou, China, from July to September 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nursing students who had just completed their internship in the ICU; (2) nursing students born between January 1, 1995, and December 31, 2009; and (3) voluntary participation in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nursing students had a history of psychiatric disorders or chronic mental health conditions; (2) nursing students were exposed to major life events within the preceding 3 months (e.g., severe illness, family adversity, or bereavement); and (3) nursing students were unable to comprehend survey items or demonstrations of patterned responses. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

The G*Power 3.1.9 program was used to calculate the sample size. The parameter settings are as follows: F tests - Linear multiple regression: fixed model, R2 deviation from zero, Effect size f2 = 0.15, significance level at α = 0.05, number of predictors = 36, and power = 0.80. The minimum sample size required for conducting multiple linear regression analysis was calculated to be 203. Considering a 20% nonresponse rate, a total of 247 questionnaires were distributed, and 240 valid questionnaires were returned, with a validity rate of 97.17%.

Measurements

General characteristics

Based on the research aims and literature reviews, the self-developed general demographic characteristics questionnaire included questions concerning participants’ age, gender, education level, residence, attitude toward nursing major, motivation for choosing nursing major, post–graduation career intention, prior training in trauma coping, experience of receiving psychological assistance, level of internship hospital, ICU type, sequential stage of the ICU rotation within the clinical internship program, daily working hours in ICU, duration of ICU internship, night shift frequency during ICU internship, number of patient deaths witnessed during ICU internship, satisfaction with ICU internship, perceived workload in ICU, self–rated sleep quality during recent ICU internship, frequency of associating online medical/nursing information (e.g., medical disputes, medical malpractice incidents, reports on healthcare worker burnout) with personal ICU experiences, frequency of persistent intrusive thoughts or prolonged rumination after exposure to negative/traumatic online content. The self-developed general demographic characteristics questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 1).

Assessment of vicarious trauma

The Vicarious Trauma Questionnaire for Disaster Rescuer (VTQ) was developed by Han [36]. This instrument has been extensively applied in assessing vicarious trauma levels among Chinese nursing cohorts, including pre-hospital emergency nurses, emergency department nurses, and clinical nurses [19, 37, 38]. The questionnaire contains five dimensions and 38 items, which are emotional response (9 items), behavioral response (7 items), cognitive response (5 items), life beliefs (6 items), and physiological response (11 items). Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher total scores indicating greater vicarious trauma severity. The original study demonstrated that the total Cronbach’s α is 0.93 and that the subscales range from 0.73 to 0.92 [36]. Participants whose total VTQ score or any dimension score exceeded the corresponding theoretical median were defined as exhibiting a vicarious trauma reaction. Theoretical median thresholds were established as follows: 114 (total score), 27 (emotional response), 21 (behavioral response), 15 (cognitive response), 18 (life beliefs), and 33 (physiological response) [38]. These theoretical median thresholds function as statistical stratification criteria rather than clinically validated diagnostic thresholds. The total Cronbach’s α was 0.975, and the Cronbach’s α of the dimensions ranged from 0.829 to 0.955 in this study.

Assessment of coping styles

The Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ) was developed by Xie [39]. The questionnaire contains two dimensions and 20 items, which are positive coping (12 items) and negative coping (8 items). Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never adopted) to 3 (frequently adopted). The original study demonstrated that the total Cronbach’s α is 0.90 and that the subscales range from 0.78 to 0.89 [39]. Higher positive coping dimension scores signify a predominant use of positive strategies, whereas elevated negative coping dimension scores indicate maladaptive behaviors. The total Cronbach’s α was 0.916, and the Cronbach’s α of the dimensions ranged from 0.849 to 0.940 in this study.

Assessment of professional identity

The Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students (PISNS) was developed by Hao [40]. This psychometric instrument has been extensively validated in China for assessing professional identity levels among nursing students. The scale contains five dimensions and 17 items, which are professional self-image (6 items), benefit of retention and risk of turnover (4 items), social comparison and self-reflection (3 items), independence of career choice (2 items), and social modeling (2 items), with item 12 reverse-scored. The original study demonstrated that the total Cronbach’s α is 0.830 and that the subscales range from 0.462 to 0.831. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher total scores indicating stronger professional identity [40]. The total Cronbach’s α was 0.968, and the Cronbach’s α of the dimensions ranged from 0.669 to 0.949 in this study.

Assessment of social support

The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) was developed by Jiang [41]. As one of the most extensively validated instruments in Chinese psychological research, it dominates social support evaluation in clinical populations. The scale contains three dimensions and 12 items, which are family support (4 items), friends support (4 items) and significant other support (4 items). The original study demonstrated that the total Cronbach’s α is 0.90 and that the subscales range from 0.82 to 0.90 [41]. Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with higher total scores indicating elevated perceived social support. The total Cronbach’s α was 0.964, and the Cronbach’s α of the dimensions ranged from 0.896 to 0.948 in this study.

All these Chinese scales are well-established, non-commercial indigenous tools developed by Chinese scholars and published in open academic literature. They have been widely adopted in previous studies and have demonstrated strong reliability and validity. In the present study, all scales were applied strictly in accordance with their original published instructions.

Data collection

With approval from the nursing education departments of multiple hospitals in Zhengzhou, Generation Z nursing interns rotating in ICU (including specialty ICUs) during the current academic year were recruited. The participating hospitals were selected basis of the following criteria: (1) hospital had major clinical training bases at various levels in Zhengzhou, including Grade-A and Grade-B tertiary hospitals; (2) the hospital provided a structured ICU rotation program for nursing interns, with a minimum duration of 2 weeks; and (3) hospital expressed commitment from hospital management to support and facilitate research procedures during the study period. Upon completion of their ICU rotation, each cohort of Generation Z nursing interns completed the questionnaires after the exit examination. Prior to questionnaire completion, researchers clearly explained the survey objectives and significance to the participating nursing interns. With informed consent, Generation Z nursing interns voluntarily and independently completed anonymous questionnaires. All questionnaires were collected immediately upon completion, with quality checks conducted by researchers to ensure data authenticity and reliability.

Statistical analysis

All collected data were verified and screened in Excel to ensure accuracy. The qualified datasets were imported into the SPSS Statistics 27.0 software program for analysis. For large-sample data, when the absolute values of the kurtosis index are < 10 and the skew index are < 3 after statistical testing, the data are considered approximately normally distributed [42]. Descriptive statistics were expressed as the means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to assess intergroup differences in demographic characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the associations between vicarious trauma, coping styles, professional identity, and social support among nursing interns. A multiple linear regression model was constructed with the total VTQ score among the nursing interns as the dependent variable. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in univariate and correlation analyses were designated as predictors, followed by a stepwise regression procedure with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 10 indicating no multicollinearity.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU1AF2025LSYY-012). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants voluntarily completed the questionnaire after providing their consent to participate. All participants were fully informed of the research purpose, the principle of anonymous data processing, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time. Each participant signed an informed consent form prior to data collection.

Results

Characteristics of the sample

A total of 240 Generation Z nursing interns with ICU internship experience were recruited for this study. The participants ranged in age from 18 to 23 years, with an average age of 20.56 ± 1.19 years. The majority of participants were female (76.25%), junior college students (68.33%), originated from rural area (51.25%), and their internship hospitals were Grade A tertiary hospitals (92.50%). With respect to professional attitudes, more than half of the participants held a neutral attitude toward the nursing major (53.33%), whereas most chose nursing due to parental or others’ recommendations (62.50%) and intended to employ in nursing after graduation (60.42%). Three-fourths of the participants reported no prior trauma coping training (75.00%), and 90.00% had no experience of receiving psychological assistance.

In terms of ICU rotations, nearly half of the participants were satisfied with their ICU internship (44.58%). Most participants worked 8–12 h per day in the ICU (71.67%), with a rotation duration of 29–35 days (57.08%). Approximately half of the participants reported that the ICU was the second department of their clinical internship program (47.08%), with 48.75% working 2 night shifts/week. For trauma exposure, more than half of the participants indicated having witnessed at least one patient death (54.17%), and more than three-fourths perceived workload in ICU as neutral (75.83%). Analysis of factors influencing vicarious trauma

According to Table 1, the univariate analysis revealed that 12 factors significantly affected the total VTQ score among Generation Z nursing interns. The total VTQ score was used as the dependent variable, and 16 variables with statistical significance identified via univariate and correlation analyses were included as independent variables. The results demonstrated that the sequential stage of the ICU rotation, daily working hours, night shift frequency, number of patient deaths witnessed, perceived workload in ICU, sleep quality, coping styles, and perceived social support significantly influenced vicarious trauma for Generation Z nursing interns (p < 0.05). Discussion

This cross-sectional survey explored the levels of vicarious trauma and identified influencing factors among nursing interns practicing in the ICU, with a particular focus on the Generation Z cohort. Although several studies have investigated vicarious trauma among registered nurses in China, this is the first study specifically targeting nursing interns exposed to ICU practice. The findings indicate that Generation Z nursing interns reported higher levels of vicarious trauma than both frontline nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic and medical teams deployed in Hubei Province [37, 38, 43], yet lower levels than pre-hospital emergency nurses and registered ICU nurses [4, 19]. This phenomenon may originate from nursing interns’ insufficient clinical experience and stress adaptation ability, compounded by insufficient preparedness for traumatic exposure [44, 45]. Exposure to ICU clinical environments during the initial internship period induces intense multidimensional shocks across sensory, affective, and cognitive domains [17]. Nurses who worked during the pandemic were able to mitigate stress responses through the support from well-established psychological support systems [18]. However, current internship programs lack systematic mental health assistance frameworks for nursing interns, further exacerbating the psychological burden on Generation Z nursing interns. Meanwhile, registered nurses in emergency departments endure prolonged trauma exposure lasting months to years [19, 46]. This cumulative effect of long-term trauma exposure may be associated with significant adverse impacts on their psychological health and professional value systems [47, 48], which could help explain why their levels of vicarious trauma remain higher than those of nursing interns with only short-term clinical practice.

Behavioral responses refer to avoidance behaviors directed toward trauma-related stimuli, such as news reports, articles, or graphic images [36]. The highest mean score on behavioral response items among Generation Z nursing interns in this study suggests several possible explanations. First, previous studies [49, 50] have indicated that Generation Z nursing students possess relatively strong altruistic motivation, which may drive them to actively fulfill nursing duties during their internships. When confronted with ICU scenarios characterized by resource constraints and therapeutic futility, such altruistic motivation may be frustrated, leading to psychological distress and avoidance behaviors [51]. Second, according to Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, individuals tend to maintain and protect psychological resources when facing stress [52, 53]. Repeated exposure to clinical contexts involving treatment failure and patient death may deplete the emotional reserves of nursing interns [54]. These avoidance responses to trauma-related stimuli may help limit emotional investment and conserve the diminishing psychological resources of Generation Z nursing interns.

In our study, Generation Z nursing interns who undertook ICU practice as their third rotation department presented a higher level of vicarious trauma. Similar to previous findings [55–57], abrupt environmental changes and exposure to high-trauma, high-pressure clinical settings can lead to negative outcomes, including acute stress reactions and occupational burnout. During the transition to ICU practice, Generation Z nursing interns may encounter significant increases in patient severity, work demands, and a comparatively restrictive work environment, all of which may hinder their adaptation [58]. Additionally, Generation Z nursing interns may place a high value on job stability and prefer relatively more flexible and free work environments [11]. As a result, the initial excitement, enthusiasm, and professional expectations developed during their first two rotations in non-emergency departments may gradually diminish [59], potentially giving way to negative psychological states such as anxiety, depression, and reduced empathy, thereby increasing the risk of vicarious trauma [60–62]. Furthermore, the third rotation typically occurs early in the clinical internship program in China, a critical period for nursing interns to develop their clinical cognitive frameworks [63]. The stark contrast between the demanding ICU environment and the nursing interns’ idealized professional expectations may provoke self-doubt and weaken professional commitment, which could further exacerbate vicarious trauma [14].

Interestingly, our findings demonstrated that, compared to other shift frequencies, Generation Z nursing interns working two night shifts per week exhibited lower levels of vicarious trauma. This phenomenon aligns with the view that appropriately scheduled night shifts can provide valuable opportunities for informal communication with preceptors and foster a deeper understanding of nursing culture [64]. During night shifts, Generation Z nursing interns may receive constructive feedback from experienced nurses through mentoring and guidance [65], which promotes their holistic development and helps mitigate vicarious trauma. Conversely, overloaded day shifts and single night shifts hinder meaningful bonding with preceptors due to excessive clinical demands or insufficient contact time, resulting in inadequate professional and psychological support. Based on previous research [64] and the findings of the present study, the night shift experiences of Generation Z nursing interns may be improved through several feasible strategies. These may include optimizing the frequency of night shifts, encouraging informal communication among nursing interns, and enhancing proactive support and positive feedback from ICU preceptors to nursing interns.

One significant finding was that greater vicarious trauma was observed in those who frequently witnessed patient deaths, with potential explanations outlined below. First, ICU practice often represents nursing interns’ initial direct exposure to death at close range [66], which may serve as a significant external stressor contributing to the development of vicarious trauma. As novices in clinical training, they often lack sufficient clinical experience and specialized skills to effectively process the negative emotional responses triggered by mortality-related events [45, 67]. Repeated exposure to patient deaths in the ICU further prompts these nursing interns to empathize deeply by placing themselves in the roles of patients or family members, eliciting intense emotional reactions such as profound grief, persistent guilt, and a decline in professional self-efficacy [68, 69]. Furthermore, although death education curricula have gradually been integrated into nursing education in China, their effectiveness is undermined by cultural taboos surrounding death and the marginalization of such content within academic programs [26, 27]. Despite the inclusion of hospice care and death coping education in nursing schools, nursing interns may still lack adequate preparation to deal with patient deaths in real ICU settings due to insufficient hands-on practice during internships [30, 45].

This study further revealed that working more than 12h per day and perceiving a relatively heavy workload were associated with significantly higher levels of vicarious trauma, consistent with several previous investigations [19, 37]. This finding can be explained by the Job Demand-Control-Support model, which posits that high job demands induce psychological strain and impair mental health, particularly when individuals lack sufficient job control and external support [70, 71]. For Generation Z nursing interns practicing in the ICU, extended working hours and overwhelming workloads represent typical high job demands that continuously drain their emotional and cognitive energy. Meanwhile, as nursing interns, they have limited autonomy to adjust their work schedules, task arrangements, or coping pace in traumatic situations [58]. A heavy workload further reduces their opportunities to communicate with preceptors, receive peer support, or adequately process negative emotions, leading to insufficient support under high work pressure [59, 72]. Collectively, these findings indicate that extended working hours and heavy workloads are prominent work-related factors linked to increased vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns during ICU practice.

This study found that Generation Z nursing interns who reported better sleep quality exhibited significantly lower severity of vicarious trauma. Several factors may explain this relationship. Nursing interns working in the ICU face multiple stressors, including high-intensity work, alarm fatigue, and frequent exposure to traumatic events, all of which contribute to poor sleep quality [58, 73, 74]. Previous studies [75–77] have shown that poor sleep quality leads to persistent physical fatigue and exacerbates emotional anxiety, thereby increasing psychological vulnerability to negative clinical experiences. Impaired sleep quality also diminishes work efficiency and cognitive regulation during nursing practice [78, 79], intensifies the psychological impact of traumatic events, and may accelerate the accumulation of vicarious trauma. Conversely, adequate sleep promotes cognitive recovery, stabilizes mental health, and enhances the psychological resilience of nursing interns when facing traumatic clinical situations [80, 81]. Despite the distinct adverse effects of poor sleep on nursing interns’ mental health, clinical performance, and trauma tolerance, sleep problems among nursing students remain underestimated [79, 82]. Nursing curricula lack targeted education on sleep health, insufficient attention is given to sleep-related risks in clinical practicum management, and intervention efforts addressing this critical issue are limited [73, 82].

Coping styles refer to specific behaviors adopted by individuals to alleviate stress when facing stressful events, encompassing both positive and negative coping styles [39]. The findings of this study demonstrated that coping styles significantly influence vicarious trauma levels among Generation Z nursing interns. Consistent with previous research [34, 83], coping styles serve as a crucial individual factor that can either buffer or amplify the adverse effects of external stressors. Positive coping styles include problem-solving, seeking support, proactive self-regulation, and other strategies [4, 39]. These methods help Generation Z nursing interns effectively establish psychological buffers when confronting intense psychological impacts, thereby facilitating cognitive restructuring and post-traumatic growth [59, 84]. In contrast, negative coping styles (e.g., avoidance, deliberate detachment, fantasy, emotional suppression, and tendencies toward substance abuse) may provide short-term relief from distress, but hinder the effective processing and resolution of adverse effects resulting from traumatic experiences among Generation Z nursing interns [20]. Accordingly, these findings underscore that coping styles are closely associated with vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns in ICU practice, and the appropriate use of positive coping strategies is essential for their psychological adaptation to traumatic clinical environments.

Perceived social support refers to an individual’s subjective feelings and experiences of social support, encompassing their perception and evaluation of the understanding, respect, and support provided by various social groups, such as peers, family, and supervisors [23]. This study found that Generation Z nursing interns with higher levels of perceived social support reported lower levels of vicarious trauma. Consistent with previous research [85], social support is a pivotal protective factor for nurses against traumatic stress and is essential for maintaining their well-being, professional ethics, and ongoing practice. This may partly account for our observation, as Generation Z nursing interns often place a high value on psychological safety and tend to seek recognition and respect in clinical settings [11]. During ICU practice, which is characterized by uncertainty and intense emotional labor, Generation Z nursing interns may be more likely to express greater needs for professional respect, emotional belonging, and self-actualization [17, 86]. According to the psychological stress system framework, social support represents a critical external resource that offsets pressure from external stressors and replenishes depleted psychological resources [33]. Strong social support networks, such as peer support, family comfort, supervisor recognition, and organizational safeguards, can promptly restore the essential psychological resources of Generation Z nursing interns that are depleted by traumatic experiences. This support may fulfill their fundamental needs for security, recognition, and belonging during ICU practice, thereby reducing their risk of vicarious trauma [3, 87].

Recommendations for education and practice

Based on the framework of the Theory of Psychological Stress System and the findings of this study, the following targeted recommendations are proposed. First, to optimize the management of external stressors, a phased clinical rotation plan should be implemented to appropriately postpone ICU internship arrangements, ensuring that nursing interns master basic nursing skills and gain adequate clinical adaptability. Meanwhile, daily working hours and night shift frequency for Generation Z nursing interns should be strictly limited. ICU departments should clearly define nursing interns’ responsibilities and dynamically adjust the difficulty and volume of assigned tasks according to each nursing intern’s competence and stage of internship. Night shifts are recommended to be designed as learning-oriented shifts supervised by experienced preceptors. Second, to strengthen individual stress regulation, nursing schools and internship hospitals should collaboratively provide systematic death education and targeted guidance on positive coping styles for Generation Z nursing interns. A dedicated sleep hygiene module, covering practical sleep management techniques, should be incorporated into ICU orientation. Furthermore, mindfulness-based stress reduction and proactive coping skills should be taught in ICU pre-job training to enhance interns’ psychological stress adaptation. Finally, to improve the social support system, nursing schools and internship hospitals need to collaborate to establish a low-threshold, comprehensive mental health support system for nursing interns during ICU practice. This system should integrate specialized psychological counseling, vicarious trauma intervention strategies, and peer support groups. Additionally, continuous psychological monitoring tailored to the ICU internship should be implemented, covering pre-ICU preparation, support during the ICU internship, and post-ICU follow-up. These comprehensive strategies are expected to collectively safeguard the psychological health and sustainable professional development of Generation Z nursing interns, thereby supporting the stability and high-quality advancement of the future nursing workforce.

Limitations

Several additional limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design collects data at a single time point, which restricts definitive causal inferences between predictive factors and vicarious trauma. Meanwhile, reverse causality cannot be ruled out, which is an inherent constraint of this cross-sectional design. Second, the use of multiple stepwise linear regression presents methodological limitations. As a data-driven variable selection approach, it carries inherent risks of model overfitting and Type I error inflation, and the selection process is not fully theory-driven, which may reduce the stability and generalizability of the regression model. Third, we employed convenience sampling and recruited participants solely from Zhengzhou, where high-level medical resources are relatively concentrated. This sampling method, combined with the limited geographic scope, may restrict the broader applicability of our findings to nursing interns in other regions and practice settings. Fourth, all data were collected via self-report questionnaires, introducing potential recall bias, response bias, and social desirability bias. Influenced by Chinese face-saving culture and the spirit of selfless dedication, nursing interns might feel ashamed of psychological problems and be reluctant to report negative psychological conditions, potentially leading to an underestimation of their level of vicarious trauma. Additionally, the widely used scale in this study demonstrated an extremely high Cronbach’s α coefficient, indicating excellent reliability but possibly suggesting item redundancy. Another limitation is the absence of a comparison group (e.g., Millennials or older nursing interns), which prevents us from determining whether the observed patterns are unique to Generation Z or reflect broader age-related effects. Finally, data were collected only during the early phase of the internship, limiting insights into the longitudinal psychological development of nursing interns. Future studies should develop a theoretical framework based on generational background and further explore how Generation Z characteristics influence the occurrence and progression of vicarious trauma. To enhance methodological rigor and generalizability, multi-method research designs integrating self-report measures with physiological or behavioral assessments are recommended, alongside cross-generational, multi-center sampling and more robust analytical approaches, including cross-validation and structural equation modeling, to address these limitations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study addresses a critical research gap by examining vicarious trauma among Generation Z nursing interns during their ICU practice. By identifying key influencing factors encompassing clinical rotation arrangements, work-related conditions, individual psychological traits, and social support, this research enhances the theoretical understanding of vicarious trauma in emerging nursing professionals with distinct generational characteristics. These findings provide valuable insights for developing psychological protection strategies for nursing interns in high-stress clinical environments and contribute empirical evidence to occupational mental health research within the nursing field. Ultimately, the results of this study provide empirical support for safeguarding the mental well-being and sustainable professional development of Generation Z nursing interns, thereby promoting a stable and high-quality nursing workforce in China.
