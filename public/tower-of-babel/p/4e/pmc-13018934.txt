Effectiveness of a school-based obesity control program among adolescents in Makkah: A pretest–posttest intervention study
Authors: Muath A. Aldomini, Baraa S. Quronfulah, Mohamed O. Nour, Tassnym H. Sinky, Mohammed A. Alharbi, Abdullah A. Alghamdi, Ahmad S. Alkathiri, Saja S. AlHazmi, Ruba M. Bukhari, Joud G. Alharthi, Shahd A. Almehmadih, Ruba A. Alnaffaee, Hibah A. Bahammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the impacts of a structured school-based educational intervention on obesity-related knowledge, binge eating behaviors, and anthropometric measures among intermediate school students in Makkah City.MethodsA one-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design was used. In total, 255 intermediate school students participated in a two-week obesity control program incorporating nutrition education, promotion of healthy lifestyle habits, awareness of binge eating disorder (BED), and physical activity guidance. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire to assess sociodemographic and anthropometric measures (body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference), obesity-related knowledge, and binge eating behavior using the Binge Eating Scale (BES). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS to assess pre–post changes and associations.ResultsThe intervention led to a significant improvement in obesity-related knowledge, with median scores increasing from 11 (interquartile range (IQR): 8–14) pre-intervention to 15 (IQR: 12–18) post-intervention (p < 0.001). Knowledge scores were significantly higher for female students than males at both pre- and post-test (p < 0.001). In addition, students with lower knowledge were more likely to report moderate-to-severe binge eating behaviors both before and after the program (p = 0.017 and p = 0.004, respectively). BES scores had positive correlations with BMI, weight, and waist circumference, and negative correlations with knowledge scores. Logistic regression identified low pre-test knowledge as an independent predictor of moderate-to-severe binge eating symptoms (odds ratio = 0.871; 95 % confidence interval: 0.796–0.953, p = 0.003).ConclusionThe educational program effectively enhanced knowledge of obesity and eating behaviors among students. These findings highlight the link between psychological eating patterns and obesity risk, emphasizing the need to integrate emotional and behavioral components into school-based health education. Incorporating these multidimensional strategies may contribute to early prevention of disordered eating and long-term obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is a major global public health concern associated with a wide range of chronic conditions, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers.1 In addition to its physical consequences, obesity is linked to adverse psychological outcomes, such as anxiety, depression, and reduced self-esteem, which may contribute to maladaptive coping strategies and the pursuit of invasive weight-loss interventions in severe cases.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7

In KSA, obesity affects individuals in all age groups, but the prevalence among children and adolescents has been increasing markedly over recent decades. National surveys indicate that 11.2 % of children and adolescents are overweight and 9.4 % are obese.8,9 These levels impose a substantial long-term burden on the health care system through increased demand for clinical services and rising economic costs. Excess weight during adolescence is a strong predictor of adult obesity and related chronic diseases, highlighting the importance of early preventive interventions.10

Globally, the prevalence of childhood obesity increased from approximately 4 % in 1975 to more than 18 % by 2019, affecting over 330 million children and adolescents.11 In KSA, rapid urbanization has been accompanied by lifestyle changes characterized by reduced physical activity and increased consumption of energy-dense foods.12 These trends are further exacerbated by structural barriers to physical activity, including limited access to green spaces and reliance on private transportation.13 Cultural practices related to food consumption, such as large portion sizes and late-night eating, may also contribute to unhealthy dietary patterns.14,15 In addition, parental factors, including obesity status, irregular family meals, and limited dietary supervision, play significant roles in shaping the health behaviors and obesity risk of adolescents.16,17

The psychological dimensions of obesity warrant particular attention during adolescence. Obese adolescents are at increased risk of mood disorders, emotional eating, and disordered eating behaviors.18 School environments may intensify these vulnerabilities through experiences of stigmatization, peer pressure, and social exclusion.19 KSA has responded to these challenges by implementing several national initiatives aimed at obesity prevention, including taxation of sugar-sweetened beverages and energy drinks, mandatory calorie labeling policies,20 and the “Rashaka” initiative, which targets a 5 % reduction in childhood obesity over five years.21

International studies support the effectiveness of school-based interventions at improving obesity-related outcomes among adolescents. In Spain, combined educational and physical activity programs resulted in improved dietary behaviors and modest but significant reductions in body mass index (BMI) z-scores and obesity prevalence.22 Findings obtained from the “HEalth In Adolescents” (HEIA) trial in Norway further demonstrated that a 20-month multicomponent intervention increased physical activity levels and reduced sedentary behavior.23 Similarly, a non-randomized trial in China involving more than 900 students significantly reduced BMI, skinfold thickness, and fasting glucose following a 12-week intervention.24 A recent systematic review of 33 school-based health education programs found that nearly half resulted in reductions in BMI or BMI z-scores, and a modest but statistically significant pooled effect was confirmed by meta-analysis.25 Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of comprehensive, multicomponent school-based interventions for promoting healthier weight trajectories during adolescence.

Evidence from KSA further supports the role of school-based health education in improving obesity-related behaviors. In particular, a randomized posttest-only study among fifth- and sixth-grade girls in Riyadh demonstrated significant improvements in nutrition knowledge, dietary self-efficacy, and healthy eating practices, with sustained effects at follow-up, and self-efficacy emerged as a key predictor of healthier behaviors and lower BMI.26 A study in Al-Baha involving 725 primary school students found that less than 60 % had received nutrition education, despite widespread demand for such programs, and it was noted that the absence of structured education was associated with no observable differences in dietary behaviors or BMI.27 In agreement with these findings, a national review of eight school-based interventions found that 75 % resulted in improvements in obesity-related behaviors or BMI/BMI z-scores, particularly those that employed multicomponent or theory-based approaches and parental engagement.28 Together, these studies highlight both the effectiveness of school-based interventions and the persistent unmet need for culturally tailored programs in KSA, particularly in regions such as Makkah where sociocultural contexts may differ.

Binge eating disorder (BED) has been increasingly reported among adolescents and frequently occurs with obesity. BED is characterized by recurrent episodes of excessive food intake, often triggered by emotional distress and associated with anxiety and depression.29 In Al-Jouf, 6.2 % and 5.5 % of adolescents were reported to have moderate and severe binge eating behaviors, respectively, and the observed prevalence was higher among females.30

Given the rising prevalence of adolescent obesity in KSA and its associated psychosocial and health consequences, targeted and context-specific interventions are required, particularly in culturally distinct regions such as Makkah. Studies throughout the world have examined the interplay between obesity and sociocultural factors, but regional evidence remains limited and insufficient to capture local determinants of adolescent health behaviors in KSA.31,32 This study aimed to address this deficiency by evaluating the impacts of a structured school-based educational intervention on obesity-related knowledge, binge eating behaviors, and anthropometric measures among intermediate school students in Makkah City.

Materials and Methods

Study design

A quasi-experimental, one-group pretest–posttest design was employed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured, school-based educational intervention that targeted obesity prevention among adolescents in Makkah City, KSA.

Study setting

The intervention was implemented during the 2024–2025 academic year across male and female government intermediate schools in five administrative regions of Makkah: Eastern, Western, Northern, Southern, and Central.

Study participants and eligibility

Participants were male and female students aged 11–17 years enrolled in government intermediate schools in Makkah City. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 11–17 years; (2) enrollment in a participating school during the study period; and (3) provision of written informed consent from parents/guardians and verbal assent from the student. Exclusion criteria included students from private or non-regular schools, and those with diagnosed chronic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, endocrine disorders affecting growth or weight (e.g., hypothyroidism), chronic respiratory diseases requiring long-term medication (e.g., asthma), or cardiovascular conditions or physical or mental disabilities that could influence dietary intake, physical activity, or participation in the intervention.

Sampling strategy

The intermediate regular government education stage in Makka during the 2024–2025 academic year included 237 schools (118 for boys and 119 for girls), 2938 classes (1546 for boys and 1392 for girls), and a total of 86350 students (44600 boys and 41750 girls).33

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G∗Power 3.1 for a paired-samples t-test, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.5), consistent with established guidelines for behavioral and educational interventions that reflect a clinically meaningful difference in the outcomes while maintaining study feasibility,34 α = 0.05, and 95 % power. The minimum required sample was determined as 135. To enhance precision, statistical power, and generalizability, while also accounting for attrition and allowing for robust within-subject and between-group comparisons, 255 students were ultimately enrolled.

Intervention description

A structured two-week health education program was delivered in the selected schools by trained health educators, who received a 6-h preparatory session and were monitored for adherence to content delivery using a structured checklist.

All sessions were delivered in the same order across schools, with two sessions per week covering the four topics (nutrition education, healthy habits, binge eating awareness, and physical activity). The learning objectives were general and aimed to establish knowledge and behavioral skills applicable to all students, rather than being personalized to individual participants. The program included four interactive 45-min lectures, incorporating didactic content, group discussions, and behavior change techniques focused on:•Nutrition Education: understanding food groups, balanced meals, and caloric needs.•Healthy Habits: establishing sustainable behaviors in terms of diet, sleep, and physical activity.•Binge Eating Awareness: recognizing emotional triggers and coping mechanisms.•Physical Activity: promoting regular exercise and overcoming barriers.

Each session had measurable learning objectives, and ≥70 % of students were expected to meet predefined performance criteria (e.g., identifying food groups and setting personal health goals).

Standardized teaching materials were used to ensure fidelity to the intervention, and arrangements and logistics were coordinated with school administrators, including scheduling, audiovisual support, measurement tools, space availability, consent processes, and questionnaire distribution and collection (completion in about 15 min).

Measurements and instruments

Sociodemographic data

A brief survey collected data regarding age, sex, academic year, and parental education levels. All measurements were conducted by trained personnel using calibrated electronic weight scales, stadiometers, and measuring tapes by following World Health Organization (WHO) protocols to ensure accuracy and reliability. Only the students’ knowledge section was re-administered after the intervention. Anthropometric measurements and binge eating behavior data were not reassessed post-intervention to minimize disrupting academic schedules, maintain high participation rates, and due to logistical constraints on repeated physical measures.

Anthropometric measurements

•BMI was first calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2) and then classified using age- and sex-specific cut-offs defined by the International Obesity Task Force (IOTF) in line with WHO growth references. Participants were categorized as underweight (mild to moderate thinness, BMI 16–18.5), overweight (BMI 23–25), or obese (BMI ≥27). This internationally recognized approach ensured the accurate assessment of weight status by accounting for differences in growth and development, without the need to calculate BMI-for-age z-scores.35•Waist circumference was measured using a non-stretchable tape at the midpoint between the lowest rib and iliac crest following the WHO protocol to assess central obesity, as recommended by Al-Hazzaa (2025).36 WHO guidance was preferred because it provides validated adolescent cut-offs for central adiposity, which may indicate cardiometabolic risk independently of BMI and facilitate cross-study comparisons.

Binge eating severity

Binge eating behavior was assessed using the Arabic version of the Binge Eating Scale (BES),37 a validated 16-item self-report tool developed by Gormally et al.38 for evaluating cognitive, emotional, and behavioral indicators of binge eating. Each item presented three to four response options, which were scored from zero to three, with total scores ranging from 0 to 46. Severity was classified as: ≤17 indicating minimal or no concern, 18–26 indicating moderate severity, and ≥27 suggesting severe concern that may warrant clinical attention. The Arabic BES version used in this study was previously validated among Arabic-speaking adolescents and exhibited strong psychometric properties (Cronbach's α = 0.86).

Obesity-related knowledge

A structured, custom-developed 21-item multiple-choice questionnaire was administered pre- and post-intervention. Items were aligned with session objectives and reviewed to assess content validity by a panel of experts in health education and related fields. The questionnaire was translated into Arabic, piloted, and exhibited acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.76). Knowledge scores were computed as the percentage of correct responses (scored as: 1 = correct answer and 0 = incorrect answer), with a maximum possible score of 21, and categorized as poor (<50 %), fair (50–74 %), and good (≥75 %).39

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at our institution (Approval No. HAPO-02-K-012-2025-03-2586). Parental informed consent and verbal student assent were obtained prior to participation. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study. Participation was voluntary, and students could withdraw at any time without penalty.

Data analysis

Data were coded, entered, and analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested to confirm normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Primary outcome variables included BMI, BES scores, and overall knowledge scores for educational sessions. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed to assess pre- and post-intervention changes in knowledge levels. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests to examine differences in categorical variables. BES scores were analyzed as both continuous and categorical variables, depending on the analytical objective. BES scores were treated as continuous for correlation analyses with anthropometric measures, obesity-related knowledge scores, and age by calculating Spearman's correlation coefficients, and categorized into severity groups using established cut-off values for descriptive and comparative analyses. Subgroup analyses explored sex-based differences (male vs. female) in intervention outcomes by conducting independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. In addition, binary logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with the risk of binge eating behavior among adolescents. The goodness of fit was assessed for the logistic regression model using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Obesity-related knowledge

The obesity-related knowledge of participants improved significantly following the educational intervention, where the median scores increased from 11 (IQR: 8–14) at pre-test to 15 (IQR: 12–18) at post-test (p < 0.001), and females scored higher than males at both pre- (13 vs. 10, p < 0.001) and post-test (18 vs. 14, p < 0.001) (Figure 2). Discussion

This study evaluated the impacts of a structured, school-based educational intervention on obesity-related knowledge and binge eating behavior among adolescents in Makkah City. The results showed that even a brief, two-week program significantly improved the understanding of healthy behaviors related to nutrition, physical activity, and emotional eating among students. These findings confirm that short-term, classroom-based health education can effectively increase health literacy in adolescents, agreeing with previous interventional studies that reported improved knowledge of macronutrients, body image, and weight management among students.40, 41, 42, 43

The behavioral improvements observed in our study align with evidence obtained from previous school-based interventions conducted both globally and in KSA. Multicomponent programs implemented in Spain,22 Norway,23 and China24 resulted in knowledge gains as well as measurable improvements in dietary behaviors, physical activity, and BMI, demonstrating the capacity of school settings for influencing health-related behaviors. Similar patterns have been documented in KSA, where interventions in Riyadh improved dietary practices and self-efficacy among students,26 and a national review found that most school-based programs obtained positive changes in obesity-related behaviors or BMI.27 These findings suggest that the effects of culturally adapted school-based education can extend beyond knowledge acquisition to meaningful behavioral improvements.

The median knowledge scores increased significantly post-intervention. The proportion of students with poor knowledge decreased substantially, whereas the proportion of those with fair to good knowledge increased. Gains were consistent across sexes, demonstrating the program's broad applicability. However, subgroup analysis revealed that the knowledge scores were significantly higher for female students than male students both before and after the intervention. This suggests that female adolescents may be more receptive to health education messages, potentially due to greater health consciousness or higher academic engagement with health-related topics, as also indicated in previous studies conducted in KSA and elsewhere.44, 45, 46 These findings highlight the need for sex-sensitive tailoring of future interventions to ensure equitable impact, particularly among male students who may require different motivational or pedagogical strategies.

The intervention led to significant improvements in obesity-related knowledge and immediate post-test reductions in binge eating severity, but the study did not include a long-term follow-up to determine whether these behavioral and anthropometric changes were sustained. In addition, the absence of a control group limits causal inference because the observed changes cannot be attributed solely to the intervention. External factors such as seasonal influences, parallel school-based activities, or exposure to broader public health messaging may have contributed to the observed outcomes. Nonetheless, the observed knowledge gains may provide a foundation for future behavioral change in accordance with the theory of planned behavior and other health promotion frameworks.47

Importantly, students who retained poor knowledge after the intervention were more likely to report moderate-to-severe binge eating. Logistic regression confirmed that low baseline knowledge independently predicted a higher risk of binge eating. These findings highlight the importance of cognitive awareness for shaping psychological eating patterns, as also shown by previous studies that linked low health literacy with emotional and disordered eating.30,48

Nearly one-third of the participants were classified as overweight or obese, which is consistent with previous reports of increasing obesity rates among Saudi adolescents, particularly in urban areas.49,50 The prevalence of moderate–severe binge eating was relatively low in this study (22.7 %), but this behavior was associated with higher BMI and waist circumference. The median BMI and prevalence of obesity were higher among students with severe binge eating, highlighting the link between emotional eating and physiological risk.51,52

Interestingly, no significant associations were observed between the severity of binge eating with sex, age, academic year, or parental education. By contrast, some studies conducted in other countries suggested a higher prevalence of binge eating among females and older adolescents.53,54 However, the observed trend toward greater severity in the 2nd intermediate year and among students with obese BMI profiles may reflect transitional psychosocial stressors or body dissatisfaction during mid-adolescence, warranting further qualitative investigation.

Utilization of the BES facilitated objective assessment of emotional eating patterns that are often overlooked in traditional school-based interventions. The association between lower knowledge scores and increased BES severity persisted even after adjusting for other factors, and logistic regression confirmed that low pre-test knowledge was an independent predictor of binge eating behavior. These results reinforce the importance of promoting healthy eating but also addressing the emotional and behavioral underpinnings of food choices in adolescents.55,56

The intervention was designed using an ecological model by focusing on intrapersonal and interpersonal levels. Despite time and logistical limitations, knowledge outcomes improved and the school social environment was indirectly influenced, as noted in feedback from teachers and administrators. Broader community and policy-level engagement may enhance sustainability, but focused level-specific interventions remain effective in resource-constrained settings.57,58

The inclusion of binge eating awareness distinguishes the program in the present study from previous interventions that focused primarily on diet and physical activity. The intervention addressed cognitive and emotional aspects of eating behaviors by teaching students to recognize emotional triggers and develop coping strategies. This holistic approach is critical because adolescents may experience stress and body image pressures that contribute to maladaptive eating. Integrating mental health components into obesity prevention programs is increasingly advocated in contemporary adolescent health promotion models.7,51,59

The intervention was designed to promote positive health behaviors and self-awareness but potential unintended consequences warrant careful consideration. Evidence suggests that obesity prevention efforts and school practices that focus predominantly on body weight or weight loss rather than holistic health behaviors can inadvertently reinforce weight stigma and related negative psychosocial outcomes. Indeed, studies indicate that weight-focused campaigns may increase body dissatisfaction, dieting behaviors, and preoccupation with weight, particularly among adolescents.60,61 Thus, it is important to frame interventions in a weight-neutral, health-focused manner. Future programs should incorporate explicit stigma-reduction components, promote body positivity, and ensure that discussions around obesity and eating behaviors emphasize overall well-being rather than appearance or weight as evidence indicates that such approaches can lessen negative attitudes and improve psychological outcomes among adolescents.62,63 Engaging school counselors and parents, as well as including psychoeducational modules targeting weight bias, could further mitigate potential negative psychological impacts and foster the development of a more supportive, inclusive environment for all students.

According to the findings obtained in the present study, structured school-based health education programs that integrate obesity prevention and binge eating awareness should be systematically incorporated into intermediate and secondary curricula. Programs should cover emotional regulation, stress management, and coping strategies for maladaptive eating behaviors. Regular screening using validated tools such as the BES may help to identify at-risk students for timely referral to counselors or health care providers.

At the school level, teacher training and interactive, student-centered learning can enhance engagement, particularly among males. Strengthening parental involvement through workshops and educational materials may reinforce healthy behaviors outside the classroom. Policy-level collaboration between the Ministry of Education and Ministry of Health in KSA can support the scale-up of evidence-based interventions, improve school food environments, and align obesity prevention with national public health strategies. Collectively, these measures may contribute to sustainable improvements in adolescent health literacy and behaviors, and long-term obesity prevention.

Study limitations

The main strengths of the present study include using a well-defined sampling strategy across diverse geographic regions, employing a culturally adapted and psychometrically validated BES tool, and a high participant response rate. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed a one-group pretest–posttest design without random assignment or a control group, which limited its internal validity and reduced the ability to attribute observed changes exclusively to the intervention. The absence of a control group was ethically and logistically justified given the educational nature of the intervention and constraints within the school system, but it was still difficult to separate the effects of the intervention from concurrent influences such as general health awareness or peer learning. Therefore, future studies should employ randomized controlled trials or cluster-based quasi-experimental designs incorporating matched control schools to strengthen causal inference and facilitate more robust evaluations of behavioral and anthropometric outcomes over time. Second, the short duration of the intervention may have been insufficient to monitor long-term changes in behavior and BMI. Third, this study relied on self-reported tools, such as the BES, which may have led to response bias or social desirability effects. Fourth, the post-intervention assessments were limited to knowledge outcomes. The lack of follow-up anthropometric and behavioral data, including BES, restricted the amount of insight that could be gained into sustained physiological or psychological changes. Fifth, the findings may not be generalizable to adolescents in private schools or regions outside Makkah given potential differences in curricula, socioeconomic background, and school infrastructure. In addition, sampling only one class per school may have introduced cluster effects, where class-level dynamics or instructor influence could have confounded the intervention's outcomes. Moreover, it is unclear whether short-term gains in knowledge translated into meaningful behavioral changes in diet or physical activity without longitudinal tracking. Finally, the study coincided with a period of high academic pressure. Schools were focused on completing the curriculum, resulting in limited class time for the intervention. The lecture sessions often overlapped with exam periods, thereby affecting the ability of students to engage with and fully participate in the program.

Conclusions

The results obtained in this study demonstrated the effectiveness of a short-term, structured, school-based educational intervention for improving knowledge of obesity and related behaviors, including binge eating, among adolescents. The observed association between lower obesity-related knowledge and higher binge eating severity highlights the value of integrating psychological and behavioral dimensions into intervention design. The program was brief but it yielded measurable improvements in health literacy and awareness of disordered eating behaviors, supporting the integration of targeted health education into school curricula to prevent obesity and unhealthy eating patterns.

However, the outcome measures were assessed only immediately post-intervention, so the sustainability of these improvements over time could not be determined. Future studies should adopt a comprehensive ecological model that engages students but also families, school systems, and community stakeholders. Integrating trained health educators, conducting longitudinal follow-ups, and incorporating randomized or matched control groups will be essential to determine whether short-term knowledge gains translate into sustained behavioral and physiological outcomes. Replicating this model in diverse settings will further enhance its generalizability across KSA and in similar regions.
