Rapid cycle deliberate practice: a complement to clinical exposure in critical care education
Authors: Bruno Manetti Previero, Ana Spataru, Ruxandra Pinto, Dominique Piquette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid cycle deliberate practice (RCDP) is an emerging simulation technique that differs from traditional simulation by providing opportunities for shorter and more frequent cycles of practice and feedback.ObjectivesTo explore whether RCDP-based weekly simulations during a 4-week Intensive Care Unit (ICU) rotation helped at least 80% of residents and medical students rotating in the intensive care unit achieve a minimum of 80% of key clinical tasks expected from physicians initially caring for acutely deteriorating inpatients.MethodsThis prospective, observational study consisted of delivering 3 weekly RCDP-based small-group simulation sessions, followed by one individual formative simulation-based assessment, repeated monthly. Learner performance during the formative assessment was measured with a checklist containing 22 key clinical tasks previously practiced during the RCDP-based simulations. The primary outcome was learner performance score expressed as the proportion of successfully completed clinical tasks during the assessment simulation. Secondary outcomes included attendance, RCDP acceptability, and perceived psychological safety.ResultsThe average attendance to the RCDP sessions was 77% among available learners (residents and medical students). Ten out of the 23 learners (43%; 95% CI, 23-64) assessed at the end of the rotation performed more than 80% of the clinical behaviors. The overall mean performance score of all learners was 77% (95% CI, 73-80). Most of the learners perceived RCPD as educationally valuable and psychologically safe.ConclusionsAlthough the targeted performance outcome was not achieved, learners who participated in RCDP-based simulations demonstrated, during their assessment, important skills not consistently practiced during short ICU clinical rotations at the study institution. RCDP-based simulations identified areas for curriculum improvements and were also perceived as valuable and safe educational tools by learners.Keywords: medical education, critical care, simulation training, educational technique, formative feedback

Introduction

Simulation-based medical education (SBME) is commonly used during residency to complement clinical experiences and optimize learning.1 When adequately designed, simulation is an effective tool to learn clinical skills relevant to critical care, like managing patients in shock or respiratory failure.2 Two features of simulation effectiveness are for learners to receive feedback on their performance and to deliberately practice their skills based on the feedback received.3,4 Deliberate practice is based on three principles: (1) maximizing the time spent by learners actively engaging in purposeful practice (trying to “do it right or better”); (2) providing specific evidence-based or expert solutions to practice challenges; and (3) fostering psychological safety so that learners feel comfortable making mistakes and receiving feedback.5 Rapid cycle deliberate practice (RCDP) aligns with the principles of deliberate practice but is characterized by shorter cycles of practice—feedback—“scenario rewind”—practice again (see Appendix S1). Compared to traditional manikin-based simulation, RCDP-based simulation immediately addresses gaps in performance instead of waiting until the end of the scenario to provide feedback. It also gives learners opportunities to redo a task in the moment, based on the feedback received. In practice, RCDP simulation scenarios are interrupted by multiple pauses to allow for brief, task-oriented feedback and repeated practice of the same section of a scenario until performance is adequate.5,6 Highly ­relevant, timely feedback and additional time spent refining performance are therefore theoretical advantages of RCDP-based simulation compared to traditional manikin-based simulation.

The evidence supporting RCDP-based simulation as an effective learning strategy in acute care contexts is evolving. Two pediatric studies on airway training showed that participants exposed to RCDP improved their airway skills post-training.6,7 In addition, learners exposed to RCDP-based cardiopulmonary arrest simulations achieved quicker pulse checks and displayed lower stress levels than those trained with traditional simulation.8 However, not all studies comparing traditional and RCDP-based simulations have demonstrated greater skill development, higher learner satisfaction, and gains in self-confidence with RCDP.9–11 A recent meta-analysis highlighted similar inconsistencies in learning outcomes resulting from RCDP-based simulations.12 We therefore ignore if the theoretical benefits of RCDP-based over traditional simulation translate into greater learning and better future performance in practice.

Clinical training in the critical care environment is challenging. Case unpredictability, patient safety concerns, fast pace, and heavy workload often limit medical learners’ learning opportunities.13 Medical students and residents may complete mandatory or elective ICU rotations without being exposed to key clinical cases and skills that are important for their future practice. Opportunities for high-fidelity, manikin-based, simulation-based learning are also limited in many training programs due to high costs, limited access to simulation facility, lack of facilitators, etc. However, low-fidelity, fellow-facilitated, RCDP-based simulations could rep­resent a pragmatic strategy to provide standardized learning opportunities to learners rotating in ICU and mitigate inconsistencies in clinical experiences.

The aim of this study was therefore to explore whether RCDP-based weekly simulations, combined with clinical experience during a 4-week ICU rotation, result in at least 80% of rotating residents and medical students achieving a minimum of 80% of key clinical tasks expected from physicians initially caring for acutely deteriorating inpatients. We hypothesized that RCPD-based, low-fidelity, fellow-facilitated simulations would be feasible and help most ICU learners master key clinical tasks related to the care of acutely deteriorating patients, regardless of their level of clinical experience and special background.

Methods

This prospective, observational educational initiative consisted of delivering, every month, the same four weekly simulation sessions. The first three 60-minute sessions were small-group RCDP-based simulations (training sessions). The fourth 30-minute session was an individual formative traditional simulation-based assessment (assessment session). This simulation curriculum was provided in addition to usual departmental educational activities (eg, monthly orientation, daily small-group teaching sessions, departmental rounds, etc.). Ethics approval for this project was waived by our institutional research ethics board.

Study participants and setting

All non-critical care learners (specialty residents, medical students) completing an adult critical care medicine rotation at an urban, Canadian tertiary academic hospital between October 2022 and June 2023 were eligible to participate in this project. On average, five to eleven non-critical care residents from various medical and surgical speciality programs complete a 4-week ICU rotation at this institution every month. In addition, the ICU accepts annually 3 to 6 medical students for a 2 to 3-week rotation. When present, medical students are included in all clinical and teaching activities offered to the residents.

RCDP-based simulation curriculum design and facilitator training

Two of the authors (or core project team), a critical care fellow (B.M.P.) and a critical care faculty (D.P.) with extensive critical care clinical and education experience, thoroughly reviewed the learning objectives and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) of the Canadian core specialty residency programs (internal medicine, general surgery, and anesthesia) with mandatory critical care rotations. They also reviewed local data on the typical reasons for consultation of the local rapid response (outreach) team about clinically deteriorating ward patients. Three clinical problems encompassed the key learning objectives common across residency programs and the most frequent reasons for rapid response calls: (1) circulatory shock, (2) hypoxemic respiratory failure, and (3) altered level of consciousness. This preliminary work informed the development of the curriculum learning objectives, the four simulation scenarios, and the formative assessment checklist.

The core team (B.M.P. and D.P.) then created four realistic simulation scenarios that each included the three clinical problems above, although in variable clinical contexts and order (see Appendix S2 to S6 for the scenarios). The four scenarios were a hemorrhagic shock, a status epilepticus, an opioid intoxication, and a mixed respiratory failure. At some point in each of these scenarios, learners had to address the hemodynamic instability, hypoxia, and decreased level of consciousness of the simulated patient. The goal was to ensure that learners who could only participate in one or two learning simulation sessions during their critical care rotation would nonetheless be exposed to all key tasks to be learned. Scenarios were further refined based on the feedback of three local critical care faculty with extensive experience in patient care and clinical teaching.

Once finalized, the first three scenarios (RCDP training scenarios) were divided into three sections, allowing for up to three learners to lead care during a section of the scenario. These transitions represented key opportunities for mini debriefing on the performance gaps observed by the facilitator(s) during the preceding section. Feedback was followed by repeated practice of challenging tasks as needed. Other learners were assigned complementary clinical roles (eg, ICU nurse, referring resident physician, etc.) during the scenarios.

One to three critical care fellows facilitated each RCDP-based simulation session. Prior to facilitating their first simulation, each fellow participated in a 60-minute interactive workshop co-delivered by the core ICU fellow and faculty, who explained the scenarios, the debriefing approach, and the role of the facilitator. Additionally, fellow facilitators were paired with the core ICU fellow (B.M.P.) for their first simulation session. Standardization of debriefing was further facilitated by a debriefing script following an ABCDE (see Appendix S7). In addition to the debriefing provided during transitions between scenario sections to change the scenario lead, facilitators were trained to conduct safety-focused quick debriefings for critical errors that presented immediate harm to the simulated patient at any point during the scenarios.

Simulation-based formative assessment

The fourth scenario (assessment scenario) was designed as an uninterrupted scenario to assess formatively the learners’ performance at the end of their ICU rotation. RCDP was not used for this session, although end-of-scenario feedback was provided for educational purposes. To assess learner performance, the core team created a checklist containing 22 key clinical tasks (investigations and management) aligned with the targeted learning objectives and the immediate routine care provided by critical care teams in response to the common medical problems included in the learning scenarios. The checklist items were also reviewed by the three external critical care physicians who provided feedback on the scenarios. The core team facilitator (B.M.P.) was solely responsible for confirming completion of the checklist items by learners during the week-4 assessment sessions. A second fellow facilitator led the assessment simulation and post-scenario debriefing. The assessment was confidential and for the benefit of the learners only; it was not shared with local ICU faculty or training program directors.

Curriculum delivery

The simulation sessions were scheduled and delivered on a weekly basis (on the same weekday afternoon) based on a 4-week cycle, repeated over a year. Any resident or medical student rotating in the ICU who was not post-call, on vacation, or off-site for their program-specific half-day teaching was expected to attend the four simulation sessions. Learners who attended the simulation training sessions completed anonymously a psychological safety survey after each session. Learners who attended the assessment simulation sessions completed a demographic survey and evaluated the acceptability and impact of previous RCDP-based simulations.

Simulations relied on a basic, portable head-neck-chest manikin with airway management and CPR training capability that the local team of critical care allied professional educators already used for training. To increase realism, additional pre-recorded physiological sounds were played during the scenarios, as needed (eg, gasping or obstructive breathing). A free app-based monitor controlled by the facilitator also displayed values and tracings of non-invasive blood pressure, oxygen saturation, capnography, and ECG, which provided dynamic, real-time clinical information to the learners. The manikin was simply positioned on a regular table of the critical care teaching/meeting room. Basic resuscitation equipment was available upon request (eg, airway devices, oxygen delivery devices, etc, mostly unused or expired material from the clinical units).

Outcomes

The educational objective of this project was to provide hands-on learning opportunities that complemented clinical training to improve the quality of care delivered by specialty residents and medical students to acutely deteriorating patients. The primary outcome was therefore the learners’ performance score on the checklists completed during the assessment scenario on week #4. To be considered successful, this educational initiative was meant to achieve the following pre-determined outcome: ≥80% of learners completing the assessment scenario and demonstrating ≥80% of the key clinical behaviors targeted during the assessment simulations. This threshold was selected as an arbitrary but reasonable indicator of high performance for this group of physicians who practice, in real life, under the supervision of senior physicians. Secondary outcomes included (1) learner participation, based on the proportion of eligible learners who attended the formative assessment and the RCDP-based weekly simulations; (2) acceptability and perceived impact of the RCDP method (paper-based surveys on week #4); and (3) psychological safety of RCDP (paper-based survey completed post-RCDP sessions on week #1 to 3) using the Edmondson’s psychological safety questionnaire.14

Statistical analysis

We summarized categorical data as frequencies and percentages and continuous data as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). We used Fisher’s exact test to compare differences in percentages between groups. Scores between groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Results

Learner demographics and attendance at week #4 assessment

The average attendance to the RCDP sessions was 77% among available learners. Twenty-three (23) learners completed the assessment scenario on week #4, representing 27% (23/85) of the overall cohort of learners who rotated in our ICU during the study period. The characteristics of the learners who completed the assessments and those of all eligible learners who rotated in the ICU during the study period are presented in Table 1. There was no difference in the level of training between the earners who completed the assessment scenario and those who rotated in the ICU during the study period but did not attend week #4 session (P = 0.98). There was, however, a significant difference between groups when comparing base specialties, as shown in Table 1.

Learner performance during assessment simulation

Ten of 23 learners (43.5%; 95% CI, 23.2–63.7) displayed ≥80% of the targeted key clinical behaviors during the assessment scenario on week #4 (Figure 1). There was no difference in the proportion of learners who achieved a performance score of ≥80% based on speciality (P = .54), training level (P = .70), or number of previous RCDP simulations attended (P = .56). The overall mean score for all learners was 77% (95% CI 73–80) (Figure 1). There was no difference is the mean performance scores between different specialities, training levels, or number of RCPD sessions attended (Appendix S8).

Distribution of key clinical tasks performance scores.

Thirty-two percent (32%; 7 out of 22) of the targeted key clinical tasks were completed by ≥80% of the learners during the assessment simulation. Oxygen titration, fluid administration, and requesting appropriate bloodwork were the key clinical tasks achieved by all participants. In contrast, reviewing the medication list, calling for help, elevating the head of the bed, and requesting troponin were the clinical tasks with the lowest rate of completion among the participants (Figure 2).

Acceptability and perceived impact of the curriculum

All 23 participants who completed the assessment scenario on week #4 completed the RCPD acceptability survey. Respondents indicated a high level of acceptability of RCDP-based training. Most learners reported a positive learning impact on their ability to complete the targeted clinical tasks (91.3%), to achieve their critical care rotation objectives (100%), and to increase their comfort level in managing acutely deteriorating patients (95.7%; Figure 3A).

Acceptability (A) and psychological safety (B) of the RCPD technique.

Psychological safety of RCDP simulation sessions

A total of 71 anonymous psychological safety surveys were completed after the weekly RCPD simulations over the study period. Participants almost universally perceived the RCPD sessions as being psychologically safe (Figure 3B). This finding was not significantly affected by the number of facilitators present during the sessions. (P = 1.00).

Discussion

We explored whether RCDP-based weekly simulations during a 4-week critical care rotation, when combined with clinical experience, helped learners complete key clinical tasks expected from any physician initially caring for acutely deteriorating inpatients. Our results showed that almost half of the participants who completed the assessment completed ≥80% of targeted clinical tasks after a 4-week ICU rotation, which included the opportunity to participate in up to three RCDP-based scenarios. In addition, most of the learners rated the RCDP method as a positive experience that did not compromise their psychological safety.

Although the targeted objective (scores ≥80% for ≥80% learners) was not achieved, we were encouraged by the fact that most of the targeted key tasks were completed by ≥70% of the learners during the assessment simulation. The initial target was probably ambitious, and the results indicate a reasonable level of mastery of skills seldom practiced by junior learners in typical ICU settings; for example, insertion of an oral airway was performed correctly in over 90% of cases, whereas adequate bag-mask ventilation was recorded in over 75% of scenarios. This finding is favorable given that most of the study learners were from a medicine or surgical background, with limited prior airway exposure. Acute, unpredictable, high-stakes clinical environments often limit learners’ opportunity to practice high-risk tasks like airway management on real patients. High completion rates of these tasks may therefore indicate a learning effect of the simulation sessions. One of the least well-performed key clinical behaviors, “calling for help,” was achieved in <60% of cases. We suspect, based on existing literature and clinical experience, that help-seeking behaviors were affected by a desire for autonomy and learners’ assumption that help would not come in a simulation context.15 Completion of other key clinical tasks may have been constrained by certain low-fidelity aspects of the simulation. For example, we did not use reclinable hospital beds or stretchers for any of the scenarios, which may have affected the occurrence of “raising the head of the bed” behavior. These results provided important insights on when and how learning may be improved by either increasing the realism of the simulation or ensuring adequate educational exposure in the clinical setting.

The lack of effect of prior experience or exposure to RCDP sessions is an interesting finding that aligns with our hypotheses and design. We designed this intervention to ensure that all learners (regardless of their baseline specialty programs) have, by the end of their 4-week ICU rotation, the ability to complete basic clinical tasks involved in early resuscitation. RCPD sessions were also purposefully focused on the same learning objectives so that attending one session combined with usual clinical experience could suffice to master the targeted basic skills. Trainees with prior experience with airways (eg, anesthesia residents) may not have necessarily gained much from the simulation training to learn how to use an oral airway but may have learned to send cultures before giving antibiotics, for example. Internal medicine residents may have improved other aspects of their performance. A successful training would mean bringing all learners to the same level for the tasks included in the simulations.

Most participants rated positively multiple aspects of the RCPD-based simulation sessions. These findings align with recent studies showing high levels of learner satisfaction with RCPD-based training.7,8,16,17 Many participants saw value in breaking down scenarios into short sequences interspaced by mini debriefings, a finding also reported by Chancey et al.17 In addition, learners’ perception of high psychological safety of RCPD sessions was described in previous studies.17,18 Potential contributors to the learners’ psychological safety during this study included: a pre-existing positive relationship between learners and facilitators developed through joint clinical activities; a structured pre-briefing reminding learners of the pure educational intent of the simulations; the confidentiality of the individual performance scores, and the lack of involvement of any faculty in any of the sessions. Therefore, this fellow-led initiative appeared to have created a positive learning climate, lowering the stakes of suboptimal performance. This is important, as the effectiveness of simulation-based education depends, in part, on learners’ psychological safety.19 While we did not conduct direct comparisons between RCPD simulations and other forms of teaching, knowing that our participants felt comfortable speaking up, making suggestions, and bringing up challenges suggests that the RCPD-based technique can be a valuable added tool to simulation-based training in the critical care setting.

The main strength of this project was its pragmatic nature. Scheduling weekly one-hour sessions during regular clinical daytime activities for any available learners was feasible and achieved good outreach. Fellows were generally more available than faculty as facilitators, contributed to psychological safety, and gained educational experience. We used a low-fidelity portable manikin, expired or existing training material, and a room already available for training at our institution to minimize costs. Designing different scenarios that addressed the same key learning objectives optimized the chances for rotating learners to be exposed to essential content. It also avoided repetitions for those able to attend multiple weekly sessions. Finally, to our knowledge, no published work has described the use of RCPD in adult critical care education, as previous studies were conducted in pediatrics and neonatal resuscitation, anesthesia, internal medicine, and emergency medicine settings.5–10

The interpretation of our findings is limited by the small number of participants who were able to complete multiple RCDP sessions and the final formative assessment. Learners’ attendance was certainly limited at times by heavy clinical workload and frequent periods away from the bedside, challenges inherent to any clinical-based teaching activity. In addition, we did not conduct a formal assessment of the validity of the checklist. However, we believe that the tool development conferred content validity to the tool. Moreover, given the formative goal of the assessment, its value rests mostly in informing learners and educators about educational next steps and less so in rigorously predicting or assessing clinical competence. It was also impossible for us to determine how much of the learners’ performance during the formative assessment was directly attributable to the previous RCDP sessions, to their clinical exposure during the ICU rotation, to concurrent formal teaching activities, or to previously acquired competencies. No pre-test was performed prior to training, and we did not compare RCDP with traditional simulation. However, years of local rotation evaluations and available evidence20,21 indicate that clinical exposure and opportunities to practice resuscitation skills are often limited for junior learners, even during ICU rotations. In that context, creating new practice opportunities in a safe, realistic learning environment is likely to improve clinical performance. Finally, as multiple facilitators conducted the debriefing, learners may have been exposed to variable debriefing techniques and levels of psychological safety. We mitigated this problem by training and directly supervising fellow facilitators.

Conclusion

Repeated weekly RCPD-based simulations combined with clinical exposure during 4-week ICU rotations were successfully implemented to teach medical learners key clinical tasks required for caring for acutely deteriorating patients. Although our targeted performance score was not achieved in the desired proportion of learners, RCPD proved to be a valuable tool to support learners’ mastery of skills not routinely practiced during clinical activities, to identify opportunities for curricular improvements, and to highlight the challenges of providing consistent learning experiences during short ICU rotations. Deemed psychologically safe and well-perceived by our learners, RCDP requires further effectiveness and comparative research in the field of adult critical care medicine to fully understand its role and limitations.
