Association between conventional cigarette smoking behavior and peri-implantitis: a survey-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Hyeok-Jun Yang, Yeon-Tae Kim, Jae-Hong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough smoking is a well-recognized risk indicator for peri-implantitis (PI), the association between the intensity of current smoking and PI remains insufficiently characterized. This study investigated the association between smoking exposure, categorized according to daily cigarette consumption, and PI in a survey-based cross-sectional study.MethodsA total of 1,021 patients with at least one functional dental implant were included. Peri-implant conditions were classified at the patient level according to the implant site showing the poorest peri-implant condition in each participant, based on standardized clinical and radiographic criteria. Smoking exposure was assessed using a self-administered questionnaire and categorized as non-smoker, former smoker, or current smoker, with current smokers further stratified as light smokers (1–9 cigarettes/day), moderate smokers (10–19 cigarettes/day), or heavy smokers (≥ 20 cigarettes/day). Logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios for PI after sequential adjustment for age, sex, periodontitis stage, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension.ResultsPI was identified in 150 participants (14.7%). Moderate smokers exhibited significantly higher odds of PI (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 2.01; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.13–3.59), and heavy smokers showed the highest odds (AOR = 3.57; 95% CI, 2.00–6.38). In contrast, the estimates for former smokers and light smokers were not statistically significant and should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of PI events and wide CIs.ConclusionHigher daily cigarette consumption was associated with higher odds of PI, with significant associations observed in individuals smoking 10 or more cigarettes per day. These findings support the clinical relevance of assessing daily cigarette consumption during peri-implant risk evaluation. However, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm temporality and to further refine smoking-related risk assessment in peri-implant care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09355-7.Keywords: Cross-sectional studies, Peri-implantitis, Risk factors, Smoking, Surveys and questionnaires

Background

Peri-implantitis (PI) is defined as an inflammatory reaction in the tissues surrounding dental implants, accompanied by progressive loss of supporting alveolar bone [1, 2]. Continuous loss of peri-implant alveolar bone is a major clinical concern and a leading cause of long-term implant failure [3]. With the widespread adoption of dental implants for the rehabilitation of fully and partially edentulous patients, the growing incidence of biological complications, such as PI has become a major concern, prompting extensive research into their underlying etiologies and associated risk indicators and factors [4–6].

Among various risk indicators and factors, smoking has been consistently identified as a major risk indicator for PI [7, 8]. Smoking impairs the vasculature of periodontal tissues and the function of immune cells such as neutrophils, thereby weakening the host’s ability to defend against infections [9]. Tobacco consumption may also disturb bone remodeling by impairing osteoblast activity, promoting osteoclast-mediated resorption, and increasing oxidative stress and inflammation. These effects may compromise peri-implant bone stability and osseointegration, thereby providing a biological basis for smoking-related peri-implant tissue breakdown [10, 11]. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that smoking significantly alters the composition and structure of the peri-implant microbiome even in clinically healthy states; pathogenic species, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Treponema denticola, are more abundant in smokers compared with non-smokers [12, 13]. Therefore, smokers harbor a dysbiotic, pathogen-rich peri-implant microbial community that may lead to reduced treatment responsiveness and accelerated progression of PI [14, 15].

Despite this background, the way smoking exposure is operationalized in PI research remains limited [16, 17]. Many previous studies have relied on a simple dichotomous classification of smoking status, potentially obscuring important differences between former smokers and current smokers, as well as between lower and higher levels of daily cigarette consumption [16–18]. Moreover, heterogeneous study designs and inconsistent diagnostic criteria have made it difficult to define how smoking exposure should be interpreted in everyday clinical risk assessment. This is clinically relevant because risk communication in implant dentistry increasingly requires more than identifying whether a patient smokes; it also requires explaining how the magnitude of smoking exposure may influence PI risk and future treatment needs.

In clinical peri-implant care, smoking-related risk information is commonly used to guide patient education, maintenance planning, and behavioral counseling. However, risk communication based only on a binary distinction between smokers and non-smokers may be insufficient, because patients with different levels of cigarette consumption may not share the same risk profile. A more detailed characterization of smoking exposure may therefore help clinicians communicate risk more specifically and tailor preventive or supportive care strategies according to individual risk. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between conventional cigarette smoking behavior, categorized according to daily cigarette consumption, and PI in a survey-based cross-sectional population using standardized clinical and radiographic criteria.

Materials and methods

Ethics

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Daejeon Dental Hospital, Wonkwang University College of Dentistry (approval number: W2208/004 − 001). It was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [19]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before participation.

Participants

This study included patients who attended the Department of Periodontology, Daejeon Dental Hospital, Wonkwang University, between January and December 2021. During this period, patients were consecutively screened at routine clinical visits, and those who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 20 years; (2) presence of at least one dental implant that had been functionally loaded for at least 1 year; and (3) availability of both clinical and peri-apical radiographic records. Individuals who declined to complete the smoking questionnaire or reported e-cigarette use were excluded to ensure a standardized assessment of conventional combustible cigarette exposure. The questionnaire was designed to assess conventional cigarette smoking; therefore, other tobacco products, including waterpipes, cigars, smokeless tobacco, or the combined use of combustible cigarettes and smokeless tobacco, were not analyzed as separate exposure categories. Antibiotic consumption was not considered an exclusion criterion, and recent or ongoing antibiotic use was not systematically recorded in the present dataset. No additional exclusion criteria were applied based on systemic conditions. Systemic conditions, including diabetes mellitus and hypertension, were recorded as baseline characteristics and included as covariates in the adjusted regression models. Existing clinical and radiographic records were reviewed to confirm eligibility, including implant function time, and to obtain background information. Peri-implant conditions were classified at the study visit on the basis of standardized clinical and radiographic findings.

Periodontal assessment of the remaining natural dentition

The periodontitis stage of the remaining natural dentition was determined according to the 2017 World Workshop classification based on the severity and complexity of periodontal destruction [20, 21]. Staging was assigned at the participant level by a calibrated periodontist using available clinical periodontal records and radiographic findings obtained during routine periodontal examination. No exclusion criterion was applied according to the periodontal treatment status of the natural teeth; therefore, patients with treated or untreated periodontitis were eligible for inclusion if they met the implant-related eligibility criteria. Periodontitis stage was recorded as a participant-level baseline variable and was included as a covariate in the adjusted regression models.

Classification of PI

Peri-implant conditions were categorized into three groups according to standardized clinical and radiographic criteria [22]. (1) Peri-implant health was defined as the absence of clinical signs of inflammation, with no bleeding or suppuration on gentle probing, and no evidence of bone loss beyond the expected initial remodeling. (2) Peri-implant mucositis was diagnosed when bleeding or suppuration occurred on gentle probing, without radiographic evidence of bone loss beyond the crestal changes associated with initial remodeling. (3) PI was diagnosed when bleeding or suppuration on probing was accompanied by a marginal bone level ≥ 3 mm apical to the most coronal portion of the intraosseous implant or probing depth ≥ 6 mm. The severity of PI was further stratified as follows: slight (bone loss of 3–4 mm or < 25% of implant length), moderate (bone loss of 4–6 mm or 25–50% of implant length), and advanced (bone loss > 6 mm or > 50% of implant length) [23].

Clinical and radiographic assessment of peri-implant conditions

Peri-implant conditions were assessed at the patient level according to the implant site showing the poorest peri-implant condition in each participant [22]. Radiographic assessment was performed using intraoral periapical radiographs obtained as part of routine clinical examination. When available, the earliest post-loading radiograph in the clinical record was used as the baseline reference for evaluating marginal bone-level changes. For implants without an identifiable baseline radiograph, peri-implant status was classified using standardized cross-sectional criteria based on the current radiograph and clinical findings. In these cases, PI was diagnosed when bleeding or suppuration on probing was accompanied by a marginal bone level ≥ 3 mm apical to the most coronal portion of the intraosseous implant or probing depth ≥ 6 mm. Marginal bone levels were measured by identifying the fixed reference point, such as implant shoulder, and the most apical level of peri-implant bone loss. Clinical peri-implant examination was performed using a periodontal probe (CP-15 UNC, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA), and probing depth, bleeding on probing, and suppuration on probing were assessed at six sites per implant: mesiobuccal, mid-buccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual/palatal, mid-lingual/palatal, and distolingual/palatal sites. Based on the combined clinical and radiographic findings, the final diagnosis of peri-implant health, peri-implant mucositis, or PI was made by a single calibrated periodontist (JHL). Because all diagnoses were made by one examiner, inter-examiner agreement was not applicable. However, intra-examiner reliability for radiographic marginal bone-level measurements was confirmed by repeated measurements of periapical radiographs on two separate occasions, showing excellent agreement with an intraclass correlation coefficient greater than 0.90.

Smoking status

Smoking behavior was assessed using a standardized self-administered questionnaire completed directly by the participants during their clinical visit to the Department of Periodontology. This questionnaire was used to obtain patient-reported information on conventional cigarette smoking status and daily cigarette consumption in a consistent format across all participants. The smoking questionnaire was created for this study and was originally administered in Korean. Supplementary Material 1 provides a content-equivalent English translation, and the daily cigarette consumption categories were based on previously reported consumption bands [24]. Based on self-reported smoking history, individuals were classified into five categories. Non-smokers were defined as those who had never smoked. Former smokers were defined as individuals who had completely ceased smoking for at least 1 year prior to the time of assessment. Patients who had ceased smoking less than 1 year before the assessment were classified as current smokers. For participants actively smoking at the time of assessment, daily cigarette consumption was recorded as the current average number of cigarettes smoked per day. For participants who had quit smoking less than 1 year before assessment, daily cigarette consumption was recorded according to their most recent habitual daily consumption before cessation, and these participants were classified as current smokers. Current smokers were further subdivided according to their daily cigarette consumption as follows: light smokers, 1–9 cigarettes/day; moderate smokers, 10–19 cigarettes/day; and heavy smokers, ≥ 20 cigarettes/day (Questionnaire in Supplementary material 1).

Sample-size consideration

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this study used a consecutive clinical sample recruited over a fixed study period. All eligible patients who attended the Department of Periodontology between January and December 2021 and met the inclusion criteria were included. To assess sample-size adequacy, the number of PI events and the statistical power for the main smoking-group comparisons were evaluated post hoc. The final sample included 1,021 participants and 150 peri-implantitis events, corresponding to approximately 13.6 events per variable in the most fully adjusted logistic regression model when the categorical smoking and periodontitis-stage variables were considered as dummy variables. In an approximate two-sided comparison with non-smokers, using α = 0.05, the observed sample size provided 87.6% power for moderate smokers and 99.6% power for heavy smokers. However, the power was lower for former smokers and light smokers, and these subgroup findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared among smoking groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables. For descriptive analyses, smoking status was presented as non-smoker, former smoker, and current smoker; for regression analyses, current smokers were further stratified according to daily cigarette consumption as light smokers (1–9 cigarettes/day), moderate smokers (10–19 cigarettes/day), and heavy smokers (≥ 20 cigarettes/day). For the primary regression analysis, peri-implant health and peri-implant mucositis were combined into a single reference category, because the main objective was to compare PI with peri-implant conditions without established radiographic bone loss at the patient level. Univariable logistic regression was first performed to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs), followed by multivariable logistic regression analyses to estimate adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1, adjusted for age and sex; Model 2, additionally adjusted for periodontitis stage; and Model 3, further adjusted for diabetes mellitus and hypertension. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To formally assess the exposure-response trend, daily cigarette consumption was entered as a continuous variable using category midpoints of 0, 5, 15, and 25 cigarettes/day for non-smokers, light smokers, moderate smokers, and heavy smokers, respectively. Because the proportion of men varied markedly among the smoking groups, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting the sample to male participants. In this male-only analysis, smoking exposure was categorized as non-smoker, former smoker, and current smoker, with all current smokers grouped together owing to the reduced subgroup size after restricting the analysis to men. Logistic regression models were adjusted for age, periodontitis stage, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and JMP version 17.1.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,021 participants were included in this study, comprising 536 men (52.5%) and 485 women (47.5%). Among them, 107 (13.1%) had diabetes mellitus and 225 (22.0%) had hypertension. Notably, the prevalence of hypertension was lower among former smokers (3/68, 4.4%) than among non-smokers (156/693, 22.5%) and current smokers (66/260, 25.4%). Because the former smoker subgroup was relatively small and included only three participants with hypertension, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect statistical imprecision of the subgroup estimate rather than a biologically meaningful difference. Regarding smoking status, 693 (67.9%) were non-smokers, 68 (6.7%) were former smokers, and the remaining 260 (25.4%) were current smokers. In terms of peri-implant conditions, peri-implant health or peri-implant mucositis was observed in 871 patients (85.3%) and PI in 150 patients (14.7%). Regarding the anatomical distribution of the implants, 161 (15.8%) implants were in the maxillary anterior region, 95 (9.3%) in the maxillary premolar region, 252 (24.7%) in the maxillary molar region, 49 (4.8%) in the mandibular anterior region, 65 (6.4%) in the mandibular premolar region, and 399 (39.1%) in the mandibular molar region. Smoking severity and PI

Figure 1 presents the observed prevalence of PI and the adjusted odds ratios according to smoking category. Compared with non-smokers, former smokers demonstrated an imprecise and statistically non-significant estimate for PI (AOR-3 = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.40–1.89). Light smokers also demonstrated a non-significant estimate with a wide CI (AOR-3 = 1.38; 95% CI, 0.66–2.92). In contrast, moderate smoking (10–19 cigarettes/day) was associated with significantly higher odds of PI (AOR-3 = 2.01; 95% CI, 1.13–3.59), and heavy smoking (≥ 20 cigarettes/day) demonstrated the strongest association (AOR-3 = 3.57; 95% CI, 2.00–6.38) (Table 2).

Exposure-response trend analysis

A formal trend analysis supported an exposure-response association between conventional daily cigarette consumption and PI. Former smokers were excluded from this analysis because they did not represent current daily cigarette consumption. When cigarette consumption was entered as a continuous variable using category midpoints, each 10-cigarettes/day increment was associated with significantly higher odds of PI after adjustment for age, sex, periodontitis stage, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension (AOR per 10 cigarettes/day = 1.78; 95% CI, 1.47–2.14; P for trend < 0.001).

Male-only sensitivity analysis

Because the proportion of men varied markedly among the smoking groups, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed in male participants only, with current smokers grouped together as a single category. In this analysis, current smokers exhibited significantly higher odds of PI than non-smokers after adjustment for age, periodontitis stage, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension (AOR = 3.03; 95% CI, 2.00–4.59; P < 0.001). Former smokers did not show significantly higher odds of PI than non-smokers (AOR = 1.36; 95% CI, 0.70–2.61; P = 0.363).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, higher daily cigarette consumption was associated with increased odds of PI after adjustment for sociodemographic characteristics, periodontitis stage, and systemic comorbidities. Moderate and heavy smokers showed significantly higher odds of PI than non-smokers, whereas former smokers and light smokers did not. The categorical analysis showed progressively higher odds of PI across increasing levels of daily cigarette consumption, and this pattern was supported by a formal trend analysis. These findings strengthen the interpretation that higher conventional cigarette consumption is associated with a greater likelihood of established PI. Furthermore, the male-only sensitivity analysis showed that current smokers remained significantly associated with higher odds of PI, suggesting that the observed association was not explained solely by the marked imbalance in sex distribution among smoking groups.

The periodontitis stage of the remaining natural dentition differed significantly among the smoking groups, with current smokers showing a higher proportion of stage IV periodontitis and a lower proportion of stage I periodontitis. This pattern is biologically plausible because smoking is a major modifiable risk factor for periodontitis and may share inflammatory, microbial, and behavioral pathways with PI [8, 25]. Accordingly, periodontitis stage was included as a clinically relevant covariate in the adjusted models. However, its role should be interpreted cautiously, as it may act not only as a confounder but also as a potential mediator between smoking exposure and PI. In the sequential models, adjustment for periodontitis stage resulted in only minimal changes in the smoking-related estimates, suggesting that measured periodontitis severity did not substantially explain the smoking–PI association in this dataset. Nevertheless, the absence of marked attenuation does not exclude mediation, because collinearity, measurement imprecision, or partial cancellation of adjustment effects may also have contributed to this finding. Therefore, the adjusted models should be interpreted as conditional association models rather than formal mediation or causal direct-effect models. Longitudinal studies with temporally ordered periodontal and peri-implant assessments are warranted to clarify these pathways.

The findings of this study are consistent with those of previous studies. Multiple cross-sectional studies have demonstrated that current smokers are at a significantly higher risk of PI, with reported ORs of 2.6–2.7 [26, 27]. Rinke et al. reported a PI prevalence of 47% among smokers, corresponding to an OR of 31.58 compared with non-smokers [28]. Furthermore, a long-term follow-up study confirmed an increased incidence of peri-implant lesions in smokers (OR = 4.6), which reinforces the adverse impact of smoking on peri-implant health [29]. Similarly, a meta-analysis and the joint consensus report of the Academy of Osseointegration and the American Academy of Periodontology demonstrated that smoking is significantly associated with an increased risk of PI, with pooled estimates indicating approximately a two-fold higher susceptibility (risk ratio [RR] = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.34–3.29, P = 0.001; OR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.68–3.06, I² = 65.2%, Egger test p = 0.4827) [8, 16].

However, the association between smoking and PI risk remains a matter of debate, as some epidemiologic studies have suggested that the apparent impact may be confounded by other dominant clinical variables. A retrospective epidemiologic study indicated that the effect of smoking may be overshadowed by strong confounders such as a history of periodontitis (OR = 19, 95% CI: 10.62–33.92, P < 0.001) [30]. Similarly, Rinke et al. reported a high OR of 31.58 for smokers, but 88% of the smokers also had a history of periodontitis [28]. Reis et al. emphasized in their systematic review that many studies dichotomized smoking status as “yes/no,” without accounting for consumption levels, thereby limiting interpretations [17]. Moreover, they highlighted that earlier meta-analyses mixed cross-sectional and retrospective studies, leading to potentially biased results owing to the heterogeneous data. Only when the analysis was restricted to prospective cohort studies did a consistent two-fold increase in PI risk at the implant level (RR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.46–2.85) and patient level (RR: 2.79, 95% CI: 1.42–5.50) among smokers become evident, underscoring the need for standardized methodologies and reporting of smoking exposure in future research [17].

In the present study, the crude and fully adjusted estimates were generally similar, indicating that adjustment for the measured covariates included in the sequential models did not materially change the estimated association between smoking exposure and PI. This finding suggests that the observed association was not substantially explained by age, sex, periodontitis stage of the remaining natural dentition, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension in the present dataset. However, because this was an observational cross-sectional study, the similarity between crude and adjusted estimates should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal effect or as proof that confounding was absent. Residual confounding by unmeasured or incompletely measured implant-related, prosthetic, behavioral, hygiene-related, periodontal, systemic, or tobacco-exposure factors may still remain.

The stronger association observed among moderate and heavy smokers is biologically plausible in light of documented vascular, cellular, bone-remodeling, and microbial alterations associated with tobacco exposure [18, 31]. At the vascular level, nicotine and other tobacco-derived compounds have been linked to vasoconstriction, tissue hypoxia, oxidative stress, and impaired angiogenesis, which may adversely affect peri-implant wound healing and tissue homeostasis [31]. At the cellular level, smoking has been associated with impaired neutrophil function, fibroblast proliferation, collagen metabolism, and osteoblast activity, as well as enhanced osteoclast-mediated bone resorption through inflammatory and bone-remodeling pathways such as the RANKL/OPG axis [31–33]. These alterations may contribute to an imbalance between bone formation and resorption and may be consistent with reduced osseointegration and greater peri-implant marginal bone loss [31–33]. In addition, smoking has been shown to alter the peri-implant microbial community toward a more dysbiotic and pathogen-enriched profile, which may increase inflammatory burden and reduce responsiveness to peri-implant therapy [14, 15]. Taken together, these findings provide a plausible biological context for the observed association between higher cigarette consumption and PI; however, causal or temporal relationships cannot be inferred from the present cross-sectional data.

The association between daily cigarette consumption and odds of PI observed in this study carries important biological and clinical implications. The higher odds observed among moderate and heavy smokers are biologically plausible and may be consistent with the cumulative adverse effects of greater tobacco exposure on peri-implant tissues. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of tailoring patient education, risk assessment, and maintenance protocols according to smoking intensity rather than status alone, enabling earlier intervention and improved long-term outcomes [34]. Importantly, tobacco smoking should not be regarded as an absolute contraindication for implant therapy. Rather, recent evidence indicates that smoking has detrimental effects on peri-implant tissues and is associated with reduced implant survival, increased crestal bone loss, and a higher incidence of PI [18]. Therefore, smoking should be considered a modifiable risk indicator requiring individualized risk assessment, smoking cessation counseling, and close supportive peri-implant maintenance, rather than a categorical reason to deny implant treatment.

This study has several limitations. First, because of its cross-sectional design, causal and temporal relationships between smoking exposure and PI cannot be established. In addition, baseline radiographs were unavailable for some implants, warranting classification based on cross-sectional radiographic criteria and potentially limiting discrimination between physiological early remodeling and pathological peri-implant bone loss. Second, smoking exposure was self-reported and limited to daily cigarette consumption; therefore, recall bias, social desirability bias, and exposure misclassification cannot be excluded. In addition, although cumulative smoking exposure, smoking duration, pack-years, and time since smoking cessation were not evaluated, these variables are relevant for interpreting smoking-related peri-implantitis risk [26]. Third, although the regression models adjusted for several important covariates, residual confounding remains possible because implant-related, prosthetic, and hygiene-related factors were not fully controlled. In particular, although plaque index, full-mouth plaque score, peri-implant plaque accumulation, oral hygiene practices, and maintenance compliance were not systematically recorded, these factors are closely related to peri-implant inflammation and may differ according to smoking behavior. Fourth, the role of periodontitis stage requires cautious interpretation. Although the periodontitis stage of the remaining natural dentition was included as an adjustment variable, periodontitis may act not only as a confounder but also as a potential mediator linking smoking to PI. Adjustment for periodontitis stage may therefore have partially attenuated the smoking-related estimate; however, the odds ratios changed only minimally across sequential models, suggesting that measured periodontitis stage did not substantially explain the observed association in this dataset. Nevertheless, residual confounding, mediation, and potential misclassification of periodontitis stage, particularly in patients with reduced dentition, cannot be excluded. Fifth, all participants were recruited from a single dental hospital, which may limit generalizability. Sixth, peri-implant health and peri-implant mucositis were combined into a single reference category in the primary regression model. Although this approach allowed comparison of PI with conditions without established radiographic bone loss, it may have reduced discrimination between a healthy peri-implant state and reversible inflammatory disease. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the association between smoking behavior and established PI, rather than the full spectrum of peri-implant disease progression. Seventh, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was a consecutive clinical sample. Although the study was adequately powered for the main comparisons involving moderate and heavy smokers, the former and light smoker subgroups had few PI events and wide confidence intervals; therefore, their non-significant results should not be interpreted as evidence of no association. Eighth, cigar use and other tobacco products were not separately quantified, and recent or ongoing antibiotic consumption was not systematically recorded. These unmeasured factors may have contributed to exposure misclassification or residual confounding. Finally, the patient-level analytical approach should also be acknowledged as a limitation. Although this approach minimized statistical dependence among multiple implants within the same participant, patients with more implants were more likely to present with PI because classification was based on the most affected implant site. Therefore, implant count may represent a potential source of residual confounding if not fully accounted for. Future implant-level or multilevel studies incorporating implant count and failed implants are warranted.

Conclusion

Higher conventional daily cigarette consumption was associated with higher odds of PI, with the clearest associations observed among moderate and heavy smokers. The non-significant estimates for former and light smokers should be interpreted cautiously because of limited subgroup events and wide CIs. Longitudinal studies with larger subgroup samples are needed to confirm temporality and further refine smoking-related risk assessment in peri-implant care.
