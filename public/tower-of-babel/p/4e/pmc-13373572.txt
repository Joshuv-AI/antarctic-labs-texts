Story-based interventions for chronic health conditions in youth: a systematic review and narrative synthesis
Authors: Emily L Rappolt, Eunji Cho, Molly L Tanenbaum, Allison Myers, Shelagh A Mulvaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically review the literature on the implementation and effectiveness of story-based interventions for youth with chronic conditions to improve health-related outcomes.MethodsThe protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO: CRD42024550328. A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, CINHAL, EMBASE, and Web of Science. Databases were searched from inception to June 2024. Studies were included if the intervention was story-based, the sample had a mean or median age between 11 and 25, and participants had a chronic condition. Studies were limited to quantitative and mixed methods. Three reviewers independently screened abstracts and full text for eligibility. Risk of bias was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Effect sizes were extracted or calculated. Reported qualitative findings were narratively synthesized.ResultsOf the 3,797 articles screened, 7 articles met the inclusion criteria. Considerable variation existed across studies in intervention goals, components, and outcomes assessed. Notable impacts were related to improvement in distress and depressive symptoms. Intervention strategies ranged from autobiographical writing to digital formats such as video narratives and online participatory community sites. Interventions demonstrated mixed impact in improving health-related outcomes with effect sizes ranging from −0.54 to 5.50.ConclusionStory-based interventions focused on youth with chronic illness provide promising means to elicit relevant experiences and impact health outcomes. The knowledge base is in a formative stage and characterized by multicomponent interventions with research designs that do not support strong causal inference. Further research is warranted to replicate outcomes and understand active intervention components.

In 2021, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that more than 40% of children and adolescents in the United States have at least one chronic health condition (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024b). The CDC defines a chronic disease as a condition that lasts one year or more, and negatively impacts daily living or functioning (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a). Children and young adults face challenges adapting to chronic health conditions, including learning to manage their chronic condition, coping with disease symptoms, navigating peer relationships and stigma, as well as developing self-identity during a period of dynamic psychological and social development (Lerch & Thrane, 2019).

Storytelling, the use of personal narratives to convey lived experiences (Lee et al., 2016), and its variants have gained popularity as a health intervention to improve outcomes for individuals with chronic conditions (Gucciardi et al., 2016; West et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2015). While stories have been used in a variety of ways, for the purpose of this review, storytelling interventions (hereafter referred to as story-based interventions) are defined as the creation and/or sharing of first-person narratives.

Storytelling through the creation and/or sharing of first-person narratives capitalizes on intrinsic human needs for “sense-making” and to organize thoughts and emotions about life events. As an inherent human process, storytelling can be used to describe, express, and make connections between one’s identity, experiences, and the broader environment (Bury, 2001). Recounting past experiences through a story or narrative may allow individuals to enhance self-awareness through reflection, organize complex or long-term experiences into meaningful episodes, and improve social support through shared common experiences (Haigh & Hardy, 2011; Laing et al., 2017). Furthermore, story-based interventions have been utilized as a method for individuals to improve self-reported knowledge about their chronic condition through listening to the experiences of others and have been embedded into self-management programs (Gucciardi et al., 2016).

The process of sense-making that takes place through storytelling has led story-based interventions to become increasingly prevalent in health research due to their ability to inform, educate, and facilitate the sharing of health-related emotional coping and behavioral strategies (Steiner, 2005). First-person stories created in storytelling interventions can take various forms, including oral or written narratives, visual narratives using photos or images such as “photovoice” (Gucciardi et al., 2016), and digital narratives created with multimedia tools (West et al., 2022). In health research, story-based interventions have been designed to help individuals share their lived disease-relevant experiences in order to enhance personal awareness, relatedness with others, learn relevant coping and problem-solving skills, and identify resources for support (Gucciardi et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2016; West et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2015). In chronic illness specifically, the types of experiences that may be the focus of stories include the time period around diagnosis, daily struggles with adherence, social, or communication challenges including stigma, or coping with the loss of function or even mortality risks. These approaches have been utilized across a range of disease populations such as oncology (Laing et al., 2017), diabetes (Mulvaney et al., 2011), human immunodeficiency virus (West et al., 2022), and obesity (Maldonato et al., 2010).

Due to the increased use of story-based interventions, understanding how these interventions are implemented, measured, and their quantitative impact is vital to understand their potential in healthcare. Most recently, West et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review focused on the use of digital storytelling in health research for both adult and pediatric conditions (West et al., 2022). Their review concluded that digital storytelling is a promising intervention in healthcare that fosters empowerment, honors cultural backgrounds, and promotes change. West et al. (2022) sought to understand the utilization of digital storytelling in health research broadly and therefore did not focus on a specific age group, population, or health condition. Another prior review focused on the use of digital storytelling specifically in pediatric oncology (Wilson et al., 2015). These reviews suggested that the use of a story-based format may provide good alignment with ongoing and daily challenges in living with chronic conditions. However, prior reviews of story-based interventions have provided only limited insights regarding the use of story-based interventions for youth (pre-teens, adolescents, and young adults) with a variety of chronic health conditions (Gucciardi et al., 2016; West et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2015). Prior reviews have not included broad samples of modalities of story creation used in healthcare, have not focused broadly on chronic health conditions in the pediatric and young adult population, or have not focused specifically on the quantitative outcomes associated with their use. Inclusion of multiple conditions and types of intervention modalities could provide a more generalizable set of insights for future research. Thus, while prior reviews of story-based research have addressed adult populations or specific illnesses, there is a gap in our understanding specific to pre-teens, adolescents, and young adults with chronic illness.

The objectives of this systematic review were to (1) describe intervention components, structure, and implementation of story-based interventions for youth 11–25 years of age with chronic conditions; (2) identify outcomes used to evaluate the impact of the interventions; and (3) summarize the quantitative impact and qualitative results associated with relevant health outcomes. Through summarizing the effectiveness of story-based interventions for youth with chronic conditions, we seek to inform and improve the development of interventions; inform research designs and measures; and point to promising mechanisms of impact.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted by following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009). The final PRISMA checklist is available in Supplementary file S1. This review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO: CRD42024550328. Minor amendments can be found in the PROSPERO record. Amendments did not change prespecified methods, analyses, or outcomes. Relevant search terms and keywords were selected by the authors following the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) (Aromataris & Riitano, 2014). Database searches were conducted in PubMed, CINHAL, EMBASE, and Web of Science. The search strategy was originally developed for PubMed and was adapted for CINHAL, EMBASE, and Web of Science in collaboration with a biomedical librarian. The complete search strategy for this review can be found in Supplementary file S2. Databases were searched from the date of inception to June 2024. The search was not limited by publication date to capture both early and recent developments in the field. The search strategy resulted in articles published between the years 1961 and 2024. Unpublished studies and gray literature were not included in this review. Only published, peer-reviewed literature was included to ensure that the evidence reviewed reflects research that has undergone rigorous scholarly evaluation and dissemination.

Selection criteria

Articles were reviewed if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) the reported mean or median age of participants ranged between 11 and 25 years of age; (2) diagnosis of any chronic condition; (3) the main study intervention involved stories or narratives; (4) the stories or narratives were first-person accounts created by pre-teens, adolescents, or young adults; (5) the study was written, or translated, into English; and (6) the study utilized quantitative or mixed methods. Articles were excluded if (1) the study sample had serious intellectual disabilities that could hinder an individual from participating in the benefit of creating or interpreting stories, (2) pre-teens, adolescents, or young adults with chronic conditions were not the primary population of interest (e.g., studies focused primarily on caregivers or siblings), (3) the intervention targeted prevention in individuals without a chronic condition, (4) the study was a case report, (5) the study did not include an intervention (e.g., the study provided only a descriptive analysis of themes), or (6) no quantitative outcomes were reported. Qualitative studies were not included in this review to ensure studies included measurable outcomes that could inform the evaluation of intervention effectiveness.

Data extraction and synthesis

Articles were uploaded to Covidence for initial title/abstract and full-text screening. Three authors (E.R., S.M., E.C.) participated in the screening and review processes. Each article was reviewed by two of the three authors. Reviewers were blinded to each other’s decisions. Discrepancies between the first two authors were resolved by the remaining author (E.R., S.M., or E.C.), who did not review the article, and/or through discussion with all authors if needed. Data extraction was completed using an Excel template created based on the study’s objectives through collaboration among the reviewers. Two authors (S.M., E.C.) independently conducted data extraction in separate spreadsheets to remain blinded to each other’s decisions. The reviews were compared for discrepancies, which were resolved through a third reviewer (E.R.), or through group discussion.

Data extracted from studies included title, publication date, study design and purpose, participant demographics (study setting, geographic location, chronic illness diagnosis), guiding theory/framework, and study conclusions. Specific intervention components were extracted from each article. These components included the type of storytelling (digital stories vs. written narratives, etc.), the format (individual vs group), the delivery mode (in-person vs. virtual), and the duration and frequency of the session(s). The measures used in each study, frequency of measurement, stated study outcomes, and quantitative and qualitative results were also recorded. Quantitative outcomes were converted to Cohen’s d effect sizes when sufficient information was provided. Study characteristics and outcome data were tabulated in summary tables after data extraction to visually display key features and results.

A narrative synthesis approach was utilized for this review to comprehensively address the research questions and provide a more accurate summary of the evidence. A meta-analysis was not conducted due to the current state of research on story-based interventions, which is highly heterogenous in methodology, implementation, and outcome measures. Narrative synthesis allows for a more nuanced understanding of quantitative and qualitative findings, especially when studies are heterogeneous or have methodological limitations (Siddaway et al., 2019).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used for quality appraisal on all included articles (Hong et al., 2018). The MMAT allows for systematic appraisal of qualitative, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), non-randomized studies, quantitative descriptive studies, and mixed-methods studies (Hong et al., 2018). For this review, only the RCT and non-randomized studies checklists were used. The mixed-methods checklist of the MMAT was not used, as no mixed-methods studies met the criteria for inclusion in this review. Each checklist comprises two standard screening questions and five design (domain)-specific quality questions. The screening questions are rated as “yes” or “no,” whereas the design-specific checklist items are rated as “yes,”, “no,” or “can’t tell” (Hong et al., 2018). Calculating overall quality scores for articles using the MMAT is discouraged, and therefore, overall quality ratings of articles were not completed (Hong et al., 2018). Studies were not excluded from the review based on their quality appraisal score to allow for a broader understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of quantitative studies that use story-based interventions.

Results

Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flow diagram. The database search initially resulted in a total of 3,797 articles. A total of 1,270 duplicates were identified and removed. After removal of duplicates, title and abstract screening was conducted on 2,527 articles. Of these articles, 2,391 did not meet the inclusion criteria during abstract review and 136 articles were included for full-text review. Ultimately, seven articles met the inclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria for this review were specific to include only interventions using first-person narratives to improve health outcomes for youth with chronic conditions. Our criteria excluded studies that may have used a storytelling component but did not include the creation and/or sharing of a first-person narrative. For example, Zarifsaniey et al. (2022) used a digital storytelling game for adolescents with type 1 diabetes. Their intervention used a fictional character and game to teach disease-related knowledge and skills to participants, such as healthy eating and insulin injection (Zarifsaniey et al., 2022). The storyline of Zarifsaniey et al.’s (2022) intervention is fictional and does not include sharing or reflecting on disease-specific experiences, resulting in exclusion from the review.

Characteristics of studies

The seven studies that met the inclusion criteria included two RCTs and five quasi-experimental studies. One RCT and three quasi-experimental studies also incorporated qualitative data collection (see Table 1). None of the studies were categorized as “mixed methods” due to (1) the authors not reporting the study as mixed methods and (2) the lack of systematic integration of both qualitative and quantitative methods to address the study’s stated research question(s). Because the age range of participants within single studies was often broad (e.g., spanned adolescent-adult or pre-teen to adolescent), we included studies if a mean or median age between 11 and 25 years was reported. The youngest mean age of participants in a study was 11 years (Akard et al., 2015), while the oldest mean age was 20.2 years (Aardoom et al., 2014) (see Table 1).

Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used to assess the methodological quality of the studies (Hong et al., 2018). Assessment showed that overall, no article satisfied all the criteria on the MMAT within their respective study design checklist (see Tables 2 and 3). Included studies had clear research questions, collected data to address their stated research question(s), and assessed adherence/exposure to the intervention. No RCTs included blinding. In the quasi-experimental studies, the primary weakness was the use of analyses that did not account for possible confounding variables (see Table 3).

Use of theories or frameworks

In the studies reviewed, theories or theoretical frameworks were inconsistently reported as relevant to the interventions. Three studies did not explicitly describe any theory or framework to guide the research (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006). Theories, theoretical frameworks, or a specific core construct were used by four of the seven studies (Aardoom et al., 2014; Akard et al., 2015; Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Rizou et al., 2017). To develop a digital storytelling intervention for children with cancer, Akard et al. (2015) created a conceptual framework that combined prior research with the dignity model of palliative care. Two studies used pre-existing theories and explicitly described how the theories influenced the selection of constructs, measures, and/or the intervention approach. In a study of digital storytelling for adolescent-athletes with special education needs, Ofoegbu et al. (2021) used the Theory of Rational-Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT), which focuses on decreasing dysfunctional beliefs, unhealthy emotions, and behaviors through teaching rational beliefs. Rizou et al. (2017) utilized Leventhal’s Self-Regulation Theory in a psycho-educational intervention development for children and adolescents with epilepsy, which focuses on the effects of illness perceptions and beliefs on behaviors and illness symptoms. An additional study by Aardoom et al. (2014) utilized the construct of empowerment to outline the processes by which individuals take control over their lives and disease management, using an interactive pro-recovery website and online community as the intervention.

Intervention characteristics: components, modalities, and implementation

The studies varied widely in the intervention components, structure, and implementation methods (see Table 1). Five of the studies were pilot or feasibility studies, with no larger studies identified in subsequent literature (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022; Rich et al., 2006; Rizou et al., 2017). Interventional components and activities included: creation of online personal digital stories (Akard et al., 2015); autobiographical/creative writing in order to reflect on personal experiences (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010); camera-recorded illness narratives (photovoice) (Rich et al., 2006); and viewing or listening to stories/narratives created by similar youth to better understand effective disease management strategies, struggles and emotional coping strategies, and commonalities in lived experiences (Aardoom et al., 2014; Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Rizou et al., 2017).

Interventions were implemented in a variety of settings and used a variety of methods to elicit stories or perceptions of the stories provided. One story-based intervention was implemented through an online platform with little human interaction (Aardoom et al., 2014), four were completed in-person within community settings such as in personal homes, summer camps, or community centers (Akard et al., 2015; Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006), one was completed in real time in a group setting via videoconferencing (Lanis et al., 2022), and one took place in an outpatient clinic (Akard et al., 2015; Rizou et al., 2017). Across studies, stories were created individually, developed in groups, or presented to participants as pre-created stories. Stories were created independently by participants in three of the studies (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006), and in one study, participants received assistance creating their story from a researcher (Akard et al., 2015). Two studies included pre-created stories that were viewed or listened to by participants in a group setting (Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Rizou et al., 2017), and one study allowed participants to independently create a story as well as view pre-created stories by pre-teen, adolescents, or young adults with the same diagnosis (Aardoom et al., 2014). Overall, more than half of the story-based interventions were completed by participants in group settings (Lanis et al., 2022; Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Piana et al., 2010; Rizou et al., 2017).

Story-based interventions were implemented over widely different time durations (see Table 1). Two studies implemented their story-based interventions as structured, multi-session programs conducted over days to weeks (Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Piana et al., 2010). An additional three studies implemented their story-based interventions over the course of 1 day, with sessions lasting 1–4 hr in length (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022; Rizou et al., 2017). The final two studies did not have standardized timelines for participants to complete their intervention (Aardoom et al., 2014; Rich et al., 2006).

Sharing stories created by participants was a major component of several interventions. In total, five articles involved a method of story sharing (Aardoom et al., 2014; Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006). Stories were shared in group settings (live over video call or in-person) in two studies (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010). In Lanis et al.’s (2022) study, participants took part in a creative writing session that was completed in real time in a group setting through a videoconferencing platform. At the end of the session, participants were given the opportunity to share their written story and receive feedback from their peers. Similarly, participants in Piana et al.’s (2010) study completed a 9-day summer camp for type 1 diabetes. Participants completed multiple autobiographical writing prompts each day and were given the opportunity to share their stories and listen to the stories of their peers during some sessions (Piana et al., 2010). In Akard et al.’s, (2015) study, pediatric cancer patients created video-based legacy stories. Participants were given a link after completing the study to share their story with others (Akard et al., 2015). Aardoom et al. (2014) assessed the impact of a completely virtual online platform, Proud2Bme. On the platform, children and adolescents with disordered eating had the ability to share personal stories related to their illness and receive positive feedback from peers through blogs and chats on the website (Aardoom et al., 2014). In Rich et al.’s (2006) study, participants recorded their day-to-day lives with asthma for 4–6 weeks using a camcorder. After completion, the videotape was shared with participants’ medical providers to improve disease-related education and to guide treatment (Rich et al., 2006).

Although sharing personal stories was a component of many story-based interventions, only Aardoom et al.’s (2014) study quantitatively documented the impacts of sharing. In this study, sharing was measured as a component of a larger questionnaire assessing empowerment processes (Aardoom et al., 2014). Additionally, only one study documented how many participants took part in sharing. Akard et al.’s (2015) study used a follow-up self-report survey to document how many families shared the videos created about their child’s experience. Two studies conducted qualitative follow-up surveys that allowed parents or participants to express how the intervention influenced them; however, the surveys did not specifically address the benefit of story sharing (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022).

Outcome measures, attrition, and missing data

The most frequently assessed outcomes across studies were quality of life and psychological distress or depression (both were included in three of the seven studies). Quality of life was measured most frequently using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022). However, quality of life measures specific to the sample conditions were also used, such as the Pediatric Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (PAQLQ) (Rich et al., 2006). Measures used to assess psychological distress or depression included the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Lanis et al., 2022), Pediatric Symptoms Checklist-17 (PSC-17) (Lanis et al., 2022), Beck Depression Inventory, version II (BDI-II) (Ofoegbu et al., 2021), and the Revised Children’s Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS) (Rizou et al., 2017). Two studies measured illness perception, one utilized the Child Attitude Toward Illness Scale (CATIS) (Lanis et al., 2022), while the other used the Brief Illness Perceptions Questionnaire (BIPQ) (Rizou et al., 2017). Additional outcomes assessed included irrational beliefs through the Child and Adolescent Scale of Irrationality (CASI) (Ofoegbu et al., 2021) and chronic condition-specific outcomes, such as sleep problems and somatic complaints measured using the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) and somatization scale of the Symptoms Checklist 90-R (Rizou et al., 2017). General empowerment and empowerment processes/outcomes were measured by Aardoom et al. (2014) using the Dutch Empowerment Questionnaire and the Patient Empowerment in Online Support Groups Measure (Aardoom et al., 2014; van Uden-Kraan et al., 2009). The Dutch Empowerment Questionnaire measures an individual’s overall sense of empowerment at a specific point in time, whereas the Patient Empowerment in Online Support Groups Measure assesses the extent to which a patient feels empowered by participation in online support group(s) (Aardoom et al., 2014; van Uden-Kraan et al., 2009). Examples of empowerment processes include sharing experiences and exchanging information, while examples of empowerment outcomes include feeling better informed and improved acceptance of illness (Aardoom et al., 2014; van Uden-Kraan et al., 2009).

All studies utilized only participant self-report measures. Three studies completed pre- and post-intervention measures (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022; Rizou et al., 2017), two studies used single, cross-sectional surveys/questionnaires after their intervention (Aardoom et al., 2014; Piana et al., 2010), one study conducted two pre-tests and a single post-test (Rich et al., 2006), and one study measured outcomes pre-intervention, post-intervention, and at 6-month follow-up (Ofoegbu et al., 2021). All but two studies reported adequate metrics related to validity and reliability for the measures used. Reliability and validity were not reported for the questionnaire created by Piana et al. (2010) to measure the feasibility and acceptability of their intervention. Rizou et al. (2017) stated that all measures used (RCADS, AIS, and Symptoms Checklist 90-R) were validated; however, no metrics related to reliability or validity were reported.

Engagement, missing data, and attrition were inconsistently reported across studies. Four of seven studies documented engagement through follow-up survey completion, session attendance, or intervention usage (Aardoom et al., 2014; Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010). Only one study provided a description of how missing data were handled in data analysis (Ofoegbu et al., 2021). Attrition ranged from 0% to 47% (Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Piana et al., 2010; Rizou et al., 2017). Two of the seven studies did not explicitly mention or provide information about attrition (Aardoom et al., 2014; Rich et al., 2006).

Intervention impact

When sufficient information was provided, effect sizes were extracted, calculated, or converted to Cohen’s d (Table 4). Overall, effect sizes ranged from −0.54 to 5.50, with depression and distress outcomes generally demonstrating the largest effect sizes.

Quality of life outcomes

Akard et al. (2015) found no statistically significant effect of their legacy story intervention on quality of life for children with cancer. Although not statistically significant, Akard et al. (2015) did report a slightly improved effect size for the PedsQL subscales of emotional, physical, and school functioning for the story intervention group compared to controls (see Table 4). Lanis et al.’s (2022) creative writing intervention demonstrated a statistically significant improvement on the PedsQL physical health (p < .02, insufficient information for effect size calculation) for patients with chronic rheumatologic conditions. Similarly, Rich et al.’s (2006) camera-recorded illness narratives intervention found statistically significant improvements in overall-asthma-specific quality of life scores (p = .04, insufficient information for effect size calculation).

Depression and distress outcomes

Two out of three studies that assessed psychological distress or depression reported significant improvements. Ofoegbu et al.’s (2021) study demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in depressive symptoms among special needs athletes in the digital story intervention group compared to the oral storytelling control group at post-test and 3-month follow-up (Cohen’s d = 5.50 and 4.24, respectively). Similarly, Rizou et al. (2017) found a significant decrease in psychological distress in participants with epilepsy in the story intervention group compared to the control group (Cohen’s d = 1.35). However, Lanis et al. (2022) found no statistically significant changes in depressive symptoms (PHQ-9) or psychosocial functioning (PSC-17) after their story-based intervention (see Table 4).

Perceptions and beliefs outcomes

The two studies that assessed illness perceptions, or attitude toward illness, demonstrated mixed findings. Lanis et al. (2022) found no significant improvement in attitudes toward illness following their story-based intervention. In contrast, Rizou et al. (2017) observed significant improvements in coherence (i.e., perceived understanding of illness) and sleep problems among participants in the intervention group compared to the control group (Coherence: Cohen’s d = 1.06; Sleep problems: Cohen’s d = 1.44). Aardoom et al. (2014) found an inverse relationship between baseline empowerment and empowerment processes reported after the intervention. Specifically, individuals with disordered eating who started with lower levels of general empowerment reported higher levels of empowering process, such as sharing experiences, feeling entertained, and helping others after engaging in the intervention (Cohen’s d = −0.49, −0.52, −0.72 respectively) (Aardoom et al., 2014).

Qualitative findings

Four studies incorporated post-intervention interviews (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006). Interview data were utilized to elucidate participant satisfaction with the interventions, as well as to document participant experiences. Across studies, the interventions were found to be highly enjoyable among participants, with participants often expressing interest in future sessions (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006). Using interviews, Rich et al. (2006) reported that 84% of participants found the intervention to be helpful in improving their health status, asthma awareness, and coping self-confidence. Common interview themes included increased disease awareness (Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006), improved confidence in self-management (Piana et al., 2010; Rich et al., 2006), improved relatedness to others (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010), improved disease coping (Akard et al., 2015; Lanis et al., 2022), and enjoyment in listening to the stories of others (Lanis et al., 2022; Piana et al., 2010).

Discussion

The purpose of this systematic review was to document existing knowledge related to the implementation and impact of story-based interventions for the pre-teen, teen, and young adult populations with chronic conditions. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to document quantitative outcomes of story-based interventions specifically targeting those groups, providing measurable, comparable evidence on their effectiveness where such data have been lacking. In total, seven articles met the inclusion criteria for this review. The small number of articles was primarily due to the limited use of quantitative outcomes for interventions. For example, many studies utilizing photovoice were found in the literature. Photovoice is a methodology that uses a combination of photography and narrative to share lived experiences (Wang & Burris, 1997). However, none of the photovoice studies included quantitative outcomes and instead focused on describing or understanding lived experiences.

This review found that story-based interventions for youth have been implemented internationally and across a variety of chronic health conditions. To date, story-based interventions have been variably conceptualized, structured, and implemented. The ability of story-based interventions to be implemented through a variety of modalities, formats, and durations allows these interventions to be customizable to the needs of different populations and settings. Each study included in this review used unique story-based strategies and assessed a variety of health outcomes. Depressive symptoms and distress were often found to be positively impacted by story-based interventions (Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Rizou et al., 2017). Results also showed promise for other outcomes such as improving disease coping, self-awareness, insomnia, illness perceptions, and quality of life (Lanis et al., 2022; Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Rich et al., 2006; Rizou et al., 2017).

Similarly, the duration and intensity of interventions varied across studies, ranging from a single one-hour session to multi-week programs. Based on the findings of this review, it is possible that interventions conducted over longer time periods may lead to greater improvements in outcomes. For example, Ofoegbu et al.’s (2021) study was implemented over the longest duration (12 weeks) and demonstrated the largest effect size among articles included in this review, with Cohen’s d values of 5.50 and 4.24 for changes in depression at post-test and 3 months, respectively. However, studies that implemented story-based interventions over 1 (Lanis et al., 2022) and 4 hr (Rizou et al., 2017) also reported small to moderate effect sizes and showed statistically significant improvements in some outcomes, such as in illness perceptions (Cohen’s d = 1.06) (Rizou et al., 2017) and quality of life (p < .02; insufficient information to calculate an effect size) (Lanis et al., 2022) (see Table 4). Additional studies using variable models for implementation, or doses of an intervention, are needed to understand how intervention intensity and duration (number of sessions and session length) influence the effectiveness of story-based interventions among young populations with chronic health challenges.

Story-based interventions often involved various types of interactions, both direct and indirect, between participants and others. Our review found the interventions were delivered in diverse formats, such as group or individual settings and digital or in-person methods, which resulted in different types of interaction. However, the benefits, challenges, and comparisons of these various forms of interaction were not well discussed. The nature of these interactions also varied across interventions: ranging from participants creating written or digital stories shared only with the research team, to sharing with online communities, caregivers, or others of their choosing; and to viewing stories created by others of a similar age and diagnosis. Across studies, these sharing activities were found to be an important component of story-based interventions, but only one study in this review directly measured the impact of sharing. Aardoom et al. (2014) found that participants with lower overall empowerment at the start of the study reported higher levels of empowerment through the process of sharing experiences with others.

The benefits of sharing first-person stories among individuals with chronic illness have been described generally, but mechanisms for optimizing sharing did not figure prominently in these interventions. Proudfoot et al. (2012) found that peer support and sharing health-related information increased participants’ self-esteem, self-awareness, and engagement in their own health. The processes of sharing and peer support influence change through normative social comparison (Proudfoot et al., 2012), which may be a particular benefit to group-based story interventions. In individual settings, the process of creating stories and then sharing those stories with others has altruistic benefits for the sharer. Studies by Vaala et al. (2018) and Rini et al. (2014) found that individuals who share health-related information often do so with the hope of helping others with similar chronic conditions, which can be therapeutic for the individual sharing (Rini et al., 2014; Vaala et al., 2018). In another RCT of young adult cancer survivors, story-based writing was shown to enhance psychological well-being and perceived social support (Haydon et al., 2025). These related studies demonstrate the unique power of sharing stories not only for self-reflection but also for fostering connections and a sense of meaning when stories are shared to benefit others. Future studies should further explore methods to use sharing to improve engagement, relatedness, social support, and leverage altruistic motivations.

This review provides insights into possible mechanisms of change. Many components of story-based interventions draw on concepts from social cognitive theory such as vicarious learning, role modeling, and the enhancement of self-efficacy (Bandura, 2004). It is also plausible that health outcomes impacted by story-based interventions, such as disease perception and psychological distress, are mediated by processes such as sharing experiences and self-reflection. These mechanisms may help individuals improve self-awareness of barriers to self-management, develop problem-solving and coping skills, and strengthen self-efficacy. Further research should aim to quantify the role of processes such as sharing and self-reflection on outcomes related to story-based interventions.

Although quantitative research related to story-based interventions is in a formative stage, this review highlights promising clinical implications. The findings from this review suggest that story-based interventions may offer a promising avenue to influence disease perceptions (Rizou et al., 2017) and reduce psychological distress (Ofoegbu et al., 2021; Rizou et al., 2017). With this in mind, story-based interventions can be flexibly adapted for both group and individual psychoeducation sessions for youth with chronic conditions to foster disease coping and psychological outcomes.

Finally, it is important to note that of the seven studies included in this review, five were pilot or feasibility studies with no subsequent studies published in the available literature. To improve the quality of research utilizing story-based interventions, future studies should prioritize robust sample sizes to enhance reliability and generalizability. Comparative effectiveness designs, or RCTs, should be considered to evaluate different methods of story-based interventions and their impact on this population.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. Most notably, there was a limited number of studies that utilized quantitative outcomes with the populations of interest. The studies included here focus on the development or sharing of first-person experiences, which excludes stories created based on fictional characters. Although the review provides valuable and unique insights into the nature and impact of story-based interventions, there were too few studies using similar formats or approaches to make strong conclusions regarding specific approaches or outcomes. Additionally, because we included studies based on a mean or median participant age of 11–25 years, some studies may have been excluded from our review even if they included some participants within the desired age range. Lastly, this study only included studies that were written or translated into English.

Conclusion

This review offers insights into the existing evidence and potential of story-based interventions for youth with chronic health conditions, which may motivate and help guide future research and the development of more tailored, practical, and effective interventions for this population. Story-based interventions provide a promising and adaptable approach for supporting youth with a variety of chronic health conditions. The processes of creating and sharing first-person experiences through stories were identified in our review as a potential means to improve psychosocial outcomes in pre-teen, adolescent, and young adult populations. Studies using story-based interventions were found to be engaging by participants and were associated with decreased symptoms of depression and distress. However, current literature lacks hypothesis testing, replication, or standardization in implementation. To strengthen the reliability and comparability of findings, future research should prioritize the use of rigorous research design, integration of objective quantitative outcomes, and clear documentation of sample characteristics and intervention processes. Future research in this area is needed to promote replicability and reproducibility of findings and to identify intervention mechanisms that drive changes in health outcomes for youth with chronic conditions.
