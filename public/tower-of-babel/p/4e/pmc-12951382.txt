Proteomic Signatures of Cellular Reprogramming in Bladder Cancer: Insights into the Acquisition of Cancer Stem-like States and Phenotypic Plasticity
Authors: BENGI SU RUMEYSA BARLAK, BANU ISKENDER
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimReprogramming somatic cells to an embryonic state opens a transformative pathway to convert cancer cells into benign ones. By delving into the changes that occur during this process, we can enhance our understanding of tumor development and unlock groundbreaking therapeutic strategies. In this study, we successfully reprogrammed the bladder cancer cell line using Yamanaka factors and conducted a stage-specific, comprehensive proteomic analysis of the resulting molecular changes.Materials and MethodsThe bladder cancer cell line HTB-4 was reprogrammed and cultured on vitronectin-coated surfaces following Sendai virus reprogramming, enabling a thorough evaluation of pluripotent marker expression. Both parental and reprogrammed cells were tested for proliferation, migration, invasion, and colony formation. nLC-MS/MS analysis was performed to identify molecular differences between parental bladder cancer cells and reprogrammed cells across initial passages.ResultsReprogrammed HTB-4 cells retain their ability to adhere and exhibit significant expression of pluripotency-associated proteins, forming colony-like structures. Stage-specific proteomic analyses reveal notable differences between reprogrammed cells and progenitor cells, particularly in pathways related to epithelial-mesenchymal transition, stem cell maintenance, and differentiation.ConclusionWe developed an in vitro model of bladder cancer reprogramming that identifies biomarkers associated with the induction of stem-like states and cellular plasticity. Our findings reveal significant stage-specific proteomic changes offering insights into the hierarchical organization of bladder cancer and the molecular mechanisms underlying the cancer stem cell phenotype. These results facilitate the development of more precise, patient-specific in vitro models for studying tumor recurrence and treatment resistance. However, further mechanistic studies are needed to translate effectively potential biomarkers into clinical practice.

Introduction

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are a promising advancement created by introducing specific genes into differentiated cells. This process effectively reverses cell fate and activates pluripotency genes, opening new avenues for research and potential therapies (1, 2). The ability to transform induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) renders them immensely valuable for regenerative therapies and the study of disease mechanisms and development (1, 2). Despite advances in therapeutic options for advanced urothelial carcinoma, patient outcomes remain highly variable. Recent work underscores the value of stage-specific biomarkers for predicting responses to chemotherapeutics, yet also highlights that resistance remains a major barrier and is still poorly understood at the molecular level (3, 4). In this context, cancer cell reprogramming provides a complementary approach to probe tumor plasticity and resistance-associated state transitions where iPSCs can serve as a platform to study tumorigenesis (5). Early molecular changes during carcinogenesis can be pinpointed by comparing cancer cells with normal healthy cells. This opens a window of opportunity to identify cancer-specific biomarkers, epigenetic rewiring and gene expression patterns (6). Current research in cancer cell reprogramming indicates that manipulating cellular identity can effectively mirror the phenotypic plasticity of tumors. This process holds the potential for malignant-to-benign conversion, thereby highlighting a significant avenue for novel therapeutic strategies (5, 7).

Cancer arises from disrupted control mechanisms governing cell growth, division and differentiation due to a high burden of genetic and epigenetic alterations that usually develop through a multistep process and result in the acquisition of cancer-specific traits (8). The characteristics commonly referred to as the ‘hallmarks of cancer’ encompass sustained proliferation, resistance to cell death, immortality, angiogenesis, the ability to invade and metastasize, dysregulated metabolism, immune evasion, genomic instability, and alterations in the tumor microenvironment (8, 9). Tumor formation, malignant transformation and therapeutic response rely on cellular plasticity that could be seen as dedifferentiation, blocked differentiation and trans-differentiation (9). Thus, cellular plasticity leads to evasion from terminal differentiation, which is fundamental for carcinogenesis (10). Cellular plasticity should not be limited to changes in cell identity or phenotype, but should encompass all cellular responses to physiological stressors, including inflammation, injury, senescence, or carcinogenic transformation (11). Therefore, any changes induced in cancer cells, along with the careful interpretation of their response to these stimuli, may provide insight into cancer cell development and the cancer cell’s strategy to resist therapy.

Cancer develops through mutations, but before mutations become evident, epigenetic alterations occur via epigenetic mediators, modifiers, and modulators. Epigenetic modifiers contribute to the stemness by inhibiting the differentiation phenotype (12). This induces dedifferentiation, which manifests itself as tumor cell plasticity and a phenotypically flexible stem-cell-like state similar to the early embryonic development (13, 14). Understanding the mechanisms that drive or inhibit the initial malignant transformation of urothelial cells is vital for developing preventative and reversal strategies. For instance, recent in vitro evidence suggests that certain molecules can inhibit chemical carcinogen-mediated transformation, providing insights into the plasticity of urothelial cells during the early stages of carcinogenesis (15). This inherent plasticity suggests that the boundary between a malignant state and a pluripotent-like state is fluid, governed by shared molecular networks that allow cancer cells to be potentially redirected toward a less aggressive or more differentiated phenotype. Pluripotency factors such as NANOG, OCT4 and WNT signaling components are believed to act as epigenetic modifiers that contribute to tumor development and impairment of differentiation (12). Many studies have associated the dysregulated expression of pluripotency factors with malignant behavior (13, 14). Nevertheless, emerging evidence indicates that these factors have a more complex function. A dose-dependent, time-limited activation of pluripotency factors can attenuate malignancy and promote phenotypic reversion toward a more benign state (5, 15, 16). However, cancer cells are resistant to complete reprogramming, resulting in lower reprogramming efficiency than in healthy cells and leading to incomplete reprogramming outcomes (16, 17). Cancer-associated epigenetic and chromosomal changes impede resetting to a pluripotent-like state, and the majority of cells remain undifferentiated, indicating partial rather than complete reprogramming (18).

Therefore, leveraging the concept of cellular plasticity and the potential for phenotypic reversal in vitro, we aimed to reprogram bladder cancer cells by introducing the Yamanaka factors (OCT3/4, SOX2, KLF4 and c-MYC-OSKM) to demonstrate the fate-reversal or induction of normalization capacity of reprogramming in cancer cells. We hypothesize that when full dedifferentiation is avoided, the cancer cell phenotype will be dominated by the pluripotent state for a period of time upon reprogramming, which then will be left to spontaneous differentiation and reactivation of the cancer phenotype. Proteomic analysis of these checkpoints is expected to reveal physiologically relevant biomarker changes.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture and SeV-based reprogramming. HTB-4 (ATCC HTB-4; RRID: CVCL_0554) and SV-HUC-1 (ATCC CRL-9520; RRID: CVCL_3798) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The cell lines were certified mycoplasma-free (Certificate of Analysis for HTB-4 cells, Lot Number 70027312; Certificate of Analysis for SV-HUC-1 cells, Lot Number 70045213) and had been authenticated within the last 3 years. HTB-4 cells were grown in DMEM F12K (ATCC), supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Capricorn Scientific, Frankfurt, Germany), and 1% L-Glutamine (Capricorn Scientific). SV-HUC-1 cells were grown in Advanced RPMI 1640 Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Scientific), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Capricorn Scientific), 1% L-Glutamine (Capricorn Scientific). Cell cultures were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2. After reprogramming, Essential 8™ Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 1% FBS and 4 ng/ml basic FGF was used to culture reprogrammed HTB-4 cells.

Non-integrating Sendai virus carrying reprogramming factors (OSKM) were used to reprogram HTB-4 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at the Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) we previously determined (19). MOI 5:5:4 was used for polycistronic KLF-4-OCT4-SOX2, monocistronic c-MYC and monocistronic KLF-4, respectively. Before reprogramming 1×105 HTB-4 cells were plated, grown for 2 days and transfected with the predetermined MOI. When wells became confluent, they were transferred onto vitronectin-coated plates. The medium was shifted to Essential 8™ and the media were changed daily while monitoring the morphology and colony formation in the plates.

Immunofluorescence. The expression and location of proteins in the reprogrammed cells, including surface markers TRA-1-81 (cat. no.: #83321), SSEA4 (MC813) (cat. no.: #43782), TRA-1-60(S) (cat.no.: #61220) and nuclear markers NANOG (D73G4) (cat. no.: #99399), SOX2 (D6D9) (cat. no.: #34516), and OCT-4A (C30A3) (cat. no.: #94310) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) were detected by immunofluorescence. Cells were seeded onto vitronectin-coated chamber slides (Ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany), grown until 70-80% confluency, and fixed using 4% formaldehyde for 20 min. After triple washing with PBS+ containing Ca++/Mg++ (PBS+), primary antibodies prepared in PBS+ containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 1% BSA were applied +4°C overnight. The primary antibodies were washed off, and the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h on a shaking platform. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and the cells were photographed using a fluorescence microscope.

Wound-healing assay. Wound-healing assays were initially performed to compare the baseline migratory capacity of control HTB-4 and reprogrammed HTB-4 cell lines. Subsequently, the assay was conducted to evaluate the effects of paclitaxel treatment on cell migration. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured until they reached a confluent monolayer. A scratch wound was then created in the center of each well using a sterile 200 µl pipette tip. The cells were incubated in medium supplemented with 1% FBS, either in the absence (control) or presence of increasing concentrations of paclitaxel (5, 10, 25, and 100 nM). Cellular migration into the cell-free area was monitored for 72 h, with representative images captured using a phase-contrast microscope. The wound gap area was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and statistical analysis was performed to determine significant differences in closure rates between groups.

Clonogenic assay. The colony-forming ability of parental HTB-4 and reprogrammed HTB-4 cell lines was initially compared under baseline conditions. Subsequently, the assay was performed to evaluate the dose-dependent effects of paclitaxel on clonogenicity. Cells were seeded at a density of 6×103 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To assess drug sensitivity, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of paclitaxel (1, 2.5, 5, 10, and 25 nM) and allowed to proliferate for 10–15 days. Following the incubation period, colonies were fixed, stained with 1% crystal violet, and photographed. For semi-quantification, the crystal violet was dissolved, and the relative intensity was measured spectrophotometrically at 560 nm. All experiments were performed in duplicate.

Protein isolation. Protein isolation was carried out using a scraper to disrupt cell attachment, followed by enzymatic lysis on ice for 30 min with RIPA lysis buffer with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were further disrupted using stainless steel beads of 0.2 mm and 0.5 mm sizes in a bullet blender homogenizer. Samples were mechanically disrupted for 3 min, centrifuged at 16,000×g at 4°C for 15 min. After centrifugation, the protein concentrations were quantified using the Bradford method (Bio-Rad, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Proteomic analysis using LC-MS/MS. Enzymatic digestion of proteins from parental HTB-4 cells, rep HTB-4 cells and SV-HUC-1 cells was performed using the Expedon FASP (Filter-Aided Sample Preparation) kit. 200 µg of protein extracts were mixed with 8 M urea prepared in Tris-HCl buffer to denature the proteins. After denaturation, the sample-urea mixture was transferred to filters with a 30 kDa pore size and centrifuged at 15,000×g. An additional 200 µl of an 8 M urea solution was added to the filters twice. Iodoacetamide buffer containing urea was added to the filters in the dark for 20 min to prevent disulfide bond formation. 50 mM NH4HCO3 was added, centrifuged, and a trypsin solution prepared in 50 mM NH4HCO3 was added to the samples, which were incubated at 37°C overnight. The resulting peptides were collected in a fresh tube, concentrated, and stored at -80°C.

The Qubit Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to measure peptide concentrations, which were analyzed using an Ultimate 3000 RSLC nano system (Dionex, Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HPLC separation was performed using two mobile phases: A (0.1% formic acid) and B (80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Truncated peptides were concentrated and desalted using a trap column. The peptides were then transferred to an Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 analytical column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated by chromatography. Analyses were conducted for 120 min at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. Full-scan MS1 spectra were obtained at a resolution of 70,000, with a scan range of 400-2000 m/z, a target AGC of 3xE6, a maximum injection time of 60 ms, and a spray voltage of 2.3 kV. MS/MS analysis used data-dependent acquisition on the first precursor ion. MS2 analysis with collision-induced dissociation (HCD) was performed at a resolution of 17,500, an AGC target of 1E6, a maximum injection time of 100 ms, and a normalized collision energy of 27. The instrument was calibrated before each analysis using the LTQ Velos ESI Positive Ion Calibration Solution (Pierce Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis. Raw data analysis and protein identification were performed using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The instrument was operated with a mass accuracy of 2 ppm. For the database search, the peptide mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and the MS/MS tolerance was 0.2 Da. We required a minimum peptide length of 6 amino acids and at least 2 unique peptides for confident protein identification. Fixed modification included cysteine carbamidomethylation, while methionine oxidation and asparagine deamination were set as dynamic modifications. The reproducibility of the experiments was evaluated by comparing the chromatograms obtained from reprogrammed HTB-4 cell passages, parental HTB-4 cells, and SV-HUC-1 protein extracts. We set a minimum threshold of 2 unique peptides identified per protein. The data obtained were then cross-referenced with information from the UniProt and Gene Ontology (GO) databases. The human protein database (Proteome ID UP0005640) was obtained from UniProt in FASTA format. The proteomes downloaded and assembled from the GO database are as follows: cellular developmental process (GO:0048869), cell adhesion (GO:0007155), epithelial cell differentiation (GO:0030855), and cell motility (GO:0048870). Proteome analysis was performed using the Proteome Discoverer program, first on the entire proteome and then on specific proteomes from the Gene Ontology (GO) database. The results were analyzed with Venn diagrams and graphs using VENNY 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) and STRING (https://string-db.org) to assess protein locations, mass, numerical comparisons, and pathways that differ between control and reprogrammed cell lines.

Results

SeV-mediated delivery of Yamanaka factors resulted in reprogramming of HTB-4 cells. The experimental timeline for bladder cancer cell reprogramming is illustrated in Figure 1A. Following SeV-mediated delivery of reprogramming factors, parental HTB-4 bladder carcinoma cells underwent progressive morphological and phenotypic changes. In the initial phases of reprogramming, cells retained the mesenchymal-like morphology of parental HTB-4 cells. By day 7, cells displaying high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios and distinctive nucleoli started to form small, compact colonies indicative of early pluripotent reorganization. Still, some cells failed to colonize suggesting that not all transduced cells achieved complete reprogramming. The colonies expanded in both size and number but exhibited loose organization rather than being tightly packed. After reprogramming, cultures exhibited heterogeneous colonies resulting in a spectrum of cell states from induced pluripotent-like cells to partially reprogrammed cells (Figure 1B).

Reprogramming of HTB-4 bladder cancer cells using SeV-mediated delivery. (A) An illustration of the reprogramming timeline indicates the establishment of iPSC-like cell culture by day 30. (B) Monitoring of the reprogrammed bladder cancer cell line revealed epithelioid morphology and colony-like formations from day 7 onward. After transfer onto vitronectin under feeder-free conditions, the rep HTB-4 cells continue to form bigger stem-cell-like colonies. Colonies were formed by cells that transformed from a mesenchymal to a more epithelial morphology, similar to induced pluripotent stem cells (indicated by red circles). (C) Validation of pluripotent-like cells from bladder cancer cell line HTB-4 by immunofluorescence. Reprogrammed HTB-4 cells exhibited positive staining for pluripotency nuclear markers NANOG, OCT4 and SOX2 and cell surface markers TRA-1-60, SSEA-4 and TRA-1-81, while parental HTB-4 cells failed to express these markers—cell nuclei stained with DAPI. Scale bars represent 100 μm.

Immunofluorescence staining confirmed expression of nuclear and cell surface markers associated with pluripotency. Colonies showed intense positive staining for cell surface markers TRA-1-81, SSEA-4, and TRA-1-60, while nuclear expression of OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG supported the activation of transcriptional networks of induced pluripotent cells (Figure 1C). However, cells at the periphery of the colonies exhibited weaker or heterogeneous expression of pluripotency markers, consistent with incomplete reprogramming. The parental bladder cancer cells also exhibited weaker and single-cell-based positivity for the pluripotency-associated factors. The presence of compact and loose colonies shows that a subset of cells successfully transitioned to a pluripotent-like state. These partially reprogrammed populations are hereafter referred to as rep HTB-4 cells.

Reprogramming induces behavioral alterations in bladder cancer cells. After reprogramming, parental HTB-4 cells and rep HTB-4 cells demonstrated distinct behavioral patterns in terms of cell migration, clonogenicity and drug response. To assess the impact of reprogramming on the migratory ability of bladder cancer cells, we used a wound-healing assay. In this assay, parental HTB-4 cells rapidly migrated to close the scratch within 24-36 h, whereas reprogrammed HTB-4 cells exhibited significantly slower migration and incomplete wound closure even after 48 h (Figure 2A).

Colony-forming assays were performed to assess the cells’ clonogenic potential. Parental HTB-4 cells formed numerous large, irregular colonies. In contrast, reprogrammed HTB-4 cells produced fewer and smaller colonies, indicating reduced proliferative capacity (p<0.05). The colonies formed by reprogrammed cells were compact, with smooth edges, consistent with epithelial differentiation rather than retention of a mesenchymal phenotype (Figure 2B).

Together, these results demonstrate that SeV-mediated reprogramming suppresses both the migration and colony-forming ability of bladder cancer cells. The reduced motility in conjunction with the emergence of compact, non-invasive colonies supports the reprogramming-induced normalization of bladder cancer cells. To evaluate the effects of paclitaxel treatment on the migratory ability of parental and reprogrammed HTB-4 cells, a wound-healing experiment with increasing doses of paclitaxel (5, 10, 25, 100 nM) was used. In parental HTB-4 cells, wound closure occurred after 24h, while treatment with paclitaxel inhibited migration in a dose-dependent manner. At higher doses, wound closure was inhibited, and the cell-free area remained largely open, reflecting antimigratory and cytotoxic effects, with many cells showing morphological evidence of cytotoxicity and detachment. Doses above 25nM resulted in significant cytotoxicity in parental HTB-4 cells, preventing accurate measurement of wound closure. Therefore, statistical analysis for migration was performed only on sub-lethal concentrations. At 48 h, the control, 5 nM, and 10 nM groups exhibited complete wound closure. In contrast, higher doses of treatment significantly prevented closure. The remaining wound area for the 25 nM group was 92.01±22.46, while the 100 nM group showed the highest level of inhibition with an area of 165.50±17.36. Post hoc analysis with Tukey HSD confirmed that both the 25 nM and 100 nM groups were significantly different from the control and the low-dose groups (p<0.001). In contrast, reprogrammed HTB-4 cells displayed delayed wound closure even in the absence of paclitaxel. At the highest dose (100 nM), virtually no wound healing was observed. Statistical analysis of the wound-healing assay revealed a highly significant, dose-dependent effect of the treatment on the measured wound area (p<0.001). Post hoc comparisons using the Tukey HSD test demonstrated that low concentrations (5 nm and 10 nm) did not significantly alter wound closure compared to the control group (p>0.05). In contrast, treatments at 25 nm and 100 nm at 72h resulted in a dramatic increase in the remaining wound area (164.32±20.34 and 237.10±12.72, respectively) compared with the control (51.30±37.20, p<0.001). Quantitative image analysis confirmed that migration inhibition was dose-dependent in both cell types, but the inhibitory effect was more pronounced in reprogrammed cells. These findings suggest that reprogramming enhances the paclitaxel drug response (Figure 2C, D).

Paclitaxel treatment markedly reduced the clonogenic potential of parental HTB-4 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Under control conditions, parental HTB-4 cells formed numerous, large colonies, whereas at higher doses, colony number declined. Post hoc analysis of the parental cells indicated no significant reduction in relative intensity compared to the control up to 5 nM, with significant inhibition appearing at 10 nM (p<0.001). Conversely, reprogrammed cells showed a lower sensitivity threshold, with a significant decrease in intensity starting at 2.5 nM (p<0.001) compared to the control. Further declines were observed at 5 nM and 10 nM (p<0.001), with maximum suppression at 25 nM (p<0.001). Paclitaxel treatment further decreased colony formation in a similar dose-dependent trend. Reprogrammed cells formed small, compact, and well-defined borders rather than the aggressive, spreading morphology, and were more sensitive to paclitaxel inhibition (Figure 2E).

Together, these results demonstrate that paclitaxel treatment suppresses both colony formation and migration in parental and reprogrammed HTB-4 cells. The reprogrammed HTB-4 cells showed increased sensitivity to paclitaxel at all tested concentrations. These results suggest that cellular reprogramming enhances the response to paclitaxel treatment.

Proteomic changes signpost reversal of bladder cancer cell fate upon reprogramming. Hierarchical clustering of the global proteome data revealed distinct segregation among the parental HTB-4 bladder cancer cells, normal urothelial cells and the reprogrammed HTB-4 derivatives p1, p4 and p7. A clear difference in protein abundance profiles across cell types was evident based on row Z-scores. The reprogrammed HTB-4 derivatives, rep HTB-4 p1 and p4, formed a cluster with the parental HTB-4 bladder cancer cells, indicating a limited reprogramming at the early stage. By passage p7, reprogrammed cells exhibited clustering patterns closely resembling those of SV-HUC-1 normal epithelial cells (Figure 3A). This reflects a gradual convergence of global protein expression profiles and a shift toward a more normalized state upon reprogramming. This gradual normalization across increasing passage numbers (from p1 to p7) suggests continuous reprogramming favors protein expression patterns of a non-tumorigenic phenotype and a possible restoration of main networks regulating differentiation, EMT process and vesicle-mediated transport (20).

After realizing the passage-dependent proteomic shifts between reprogrammed HTB-4 cells and the gradual normalization of the global proteome, we investigated the biological basis of this transition by analyzing the protein groups associated with epithelial cell differentiation, cellular developmental process and cell motility. For epithelial cell differentiation-related proteins, the parental HTB-4 cells displayed a distinct expression pattern indicative of disrupted epithelial cell identity. In contrast, the reprogrammed HTB-4 cells gradually reactivated epithelial proteins as passage number increased. Rep HTB-4 p4 and p7 cells clustered close to the control SV-HUC-1 cells, implying the restoration of epithelial identity during reprogramming (Figure 3B).

Similarly, proteins involved in the cellular developmental process displayed extensive remodeling across cell groups. HTB-4 bladder cancer cells and rep HTB-4 p1 clustered together, while rep HTB-4 and epithelial control SV-HUC-1 clustered closely, and rep HTB-4 p4 occupied an intermediate position but close to the HTB-4-rep HTB-4 p1 cluster, which flags a clear, passage-dependent proteomic re-organization during reprogramming (Figure 3C). This progressive clustering among the passages suggests substantial remodeling of developmental pathways toward a normal urothelial-like state. This trend supports the previous literature on the induction of normalization of cancer cells upon epigenetic and transcriptional reprogramming (7, 19).

Hierarchical clustering of proteins involved in cell motility and cell adhesion reflects functionally coherent distinctions between the cell groups (Figure 3D and E). The parental HTB-4 cells and rep HTB-4 p1 cells exhibited similar expression profiles with elevated levels of motility-associated proteins. Reprogrammed HTB-4 p4 cells displayed an intermediary state between malignant and non-malignant clusters. In the later passages of reprogramming, rep HTB-4 p7 cells clustered closely with the normal urothelial cell line SV-HUC-1, indicating that prolonged reprogramming suppressed migratory behavior and promoted partial restoration of regular expression profiles. This data was also supported by cell migration experiments, in which rep HTB-4 cells exhibited slower migratory dynamics than parental HTB-4 cells. The proteins involved in cell adhesion exhibited a similar trend consistent with a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET) where the SV-HUC-1 cells formed a distinct branch while the reprogrammed HTB-4 cells (p1, p4 and p7) and the parental HTB-4 cells clustered together. Rep HTB-4 p1 cells clustered closely with the parental HTB-4 cells, while rep HTB-4 p4 and p7 cells clustered on separate sub-branches, which were closer to SV-HUC-1 cells. This may suggest that remodeling of adhesion-associated proteins and re-establishment of epithelial adhesion systems is progressive. Rep HTB-4 p4 cells showed partial recovery, and the proximity of the reprogrammed cluster to control cells signs the partial restoration of adhesion-related components. This pattern is consistent with earlier findings that, despite partial normalization of adhesion signaling, certain tumor-associated features persist in partially reprogrammed or hybrid cells (21).

We then compared the protein content of rep HTB-4 p1, p4 and p7 and analyzed the commonly upregulated and downregulated proteins at each stage of reprogramming to elucidate the proteome snapshots of the initial phases (Figure 4A). The enriched biological processes (BPs), molecular functions (MFs), and cellular components (CCs) were consistent with cancer cell reprogramming, including rewiring of RNA processing and chromatin regulation, a metabolic shift, enhanced antioxidant and proteasomal capacity, increased vesicle secretion, EMT, and adhesion changes (Figure 4B, C). Metabolic shift to glycolysis, increased proteostasis, and strong antioxidant capacity are classic cancer cell traits that are also observed during early iPSC induction. The commonly downregulated proteins reflect pluripotent-like differentiation with reorganization and destabilization of nuclear architecture, the loss of differentiation-specific structures including desmosomes and cornified envelope. Pluripotency depends on active chromatin state and upregulation of RNA-processing and spliceosomal genes. The downregulation of gene ontology (GO) terms related to chromatin binding, heterochromatin, and the spliceosome indicates a flexible cell state rather than stable pluripotency (Figure 4D, E). Research shows that reprogramming occurs in distinct stages with mixed chromatin states. Initially, cells may exhibit abnormal heterochromatin, which can create barriers to reprogramming (22, 23). A fully open embryonic stem cell-like chromatin landscape is achieved only after these barriers are resolved. Therefore, downregulation of chromatin-associated genes at earlier passages of bladder cancer reprogramming could indicate a transitional epigenetic state and partial reprogramming.

Protein-protein interaction provides molecular insights for similar molecular mechanisms modulated during reprogramming and bladder carcinogenesis. We analyzed the proteins that are regulated at different stages that might directly or indirectly contribute to the reprogramming of bladder cancer cells. Integrative analysis demonstrated that extracellular matrix signaling, cytoskeletal dynamics and metabolic rewiring are the primary functional hubs that support cellular plasticity. At passage 1 of reprogramming, the ECM-integrin signaling cluster, involving VTN, ITGA5, FERMT1, and RAB21, dominates cell adhesion and integrin trafficking engaged in MET during reprogramming (Figure 5A). Vitronectin (VTN) is known to support human pluripotent stem cell self-renewal and stable culture as a coating substrate, while integrin α5β1 (ITGA5) links cells to vitronectin (24). A mechanotransduction hub involving caveolin-1 (CAV1) and cavin-1 (CAVIN1) modulates self-renewal, migration, and proliferation of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) (25). Downregulation of AHNAK increases iPSC generation by c-MYC upregulation and confers 3-factor (Oct4, Sox2 and Klf4) reprogramming, a plausible option, while its upregulation induces embryoid body formation (26).

At passage 4 of reprogramming, the protein-protein interaction network yielded 3 major functional clusters, including translational, cytoskeletal-adhesion and stress response (Figure 5B). The core translational network comprises ribosomal subunits, eukaryotic initiation factors, and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, which are known to be crucial for both pluripotent and cancer stem cell maintenance. Lower global translational rates and a balanced proteostasis process, from protein synthesis to degradation, support the immortal and undifferentiated state of pluripotent cells. In contrast, disruption of proteome integrity and loss of controlled protein synthesis result in differentiation and ageing (27, 28). Reprogramming to a pluripotent state involves the remodeling of transcriptional programs, metabolic shifts, and the rewiring of the proteome. Protein quality control and proteostasis are crucial for resetting the somatic proteome, maintaining stemness, and facilitating a successful transition to the pluripotent state (29). Similar to the p1 reprogrammed bladder cancer cells, p4 cells also maintained the increased regulation of integrin α5β1, which mediated adhesion to fibronectin or vitronectin in feeder-free cultures of human PSCs (30). It is not surprising that p4 cells exclusively regulate cytoskeletal and adhesion components since a successful remodeling that favors MET is an early hallmark of reprogramming. Moreover, mechanical cues, mediated by integrins and caveolae, also contribute to the self-renewal phenotype of cancer stem cells. Another cluster enriched in p4 reprogrammed bladder cancer cells is centered on RNA-binding and stress-response networks. RNA-binding proteins that regulate the alternative splicing of stemness regulators, such as HNRNPs and DDXs, modulate ribosome heterogeneity, enabling the selective translation of mRNAs (31). ARS2/SRRT is known to preserve pluripotency by inhibiting premature transcription termination, and ARS2 depletion leads to loss of pluripotent identity and differentiation (32). Thus, during reprogramming, bladder cancer cells undergo phases including selective transcription induced by reprogramming factors and stress adaptation during changes in cell identity. What we inferred from the protein-protein interaction network involved in regulating translation, proteostasis, cell adhesion and posttranslational protein hubs is that the resulting cell population exhibits hallmarks of pluripotency or tumor cell differentiation into cancer stem cells.

By passage 7 of the reprogramming, several key biological processes were enriched within the protein interaction networks. Specifically, we identified significant activity in pathways related to cadherin and actin binding, translation, and metabolism, including fatty acid catabolism and polyamine biosynthesis (Figure 5C). A metabolic reset toward glycolysis and controlled mitochondrial activity is necessary for maintaining pluripotency. Nodes representing the metabolic enzymes (PFKM, IDH1, ACO2, and UQCRFS1) are involved in metabolic rewiring for stabilization of stemness and cancer stem cell properties (33, 34). Amino acid, nucleotide, and peptide biosynthesis sustain the rapid cell cycling characteristics of pluripotent and cancer stem cells. Enrichment of fatty acid catabolism and monocarboxylic acid catabolic pathways reflects increased metabolic flexibility in stem cells, maintaining energy balance and redox control. Together, these enriched terms provide evidence for the regulation of biosynthesis and catabolic metabolism, which are essential for pluripotency and also sustain the stem-like features that contribute to cancer progression in a context-dependent manner. Since p7-reprogrammed bladder cancer cells express pluripotency-associated markers, we believe that metabolic adaptability supports pluripotency rather than cancer progression. In either case, this in vitro model system based on reprogrammed bladder cancer cells provides a platform to study cell transitions and precancerous stages that mimic cancer stem cell states.

Discussion

Reprogramming cancer cells raises unanswered questions that overshadow the potential of this technique for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Here, we employed label-free quantitative proteomics to compare the protein content of parental and reprogrammed HTB-4 bladder cancer cells, aiming to identify proteomic signatures that may help understand the caveats of cancer cell reprogramming. Previously, a sequential proteome analysis of somatic cells revealed a two-step global proteome rearrangement at the initial and final phases of reprogramming (20). However, in cancer cell reprogramming, the situation of intermediate cell states or partially reprogrammed cells has not been extensively studied. Since the reprogramming outcome is known to be influenced by dynamic epigenetic and genetic regulations, as well as microenvironmental factors, such as feeder-free culture systems that induce spontaneous differentiation (35), we believe that identifying protein changes during cancer cell reprogramming may enhance our understanding of cellular plasticity.

Reprogrammed cancer cells can effectively model cancer progression and cellular plasticity in vitro. Cancer cell reprogramming cannot be fully optimized due to the increased mutational burden and cancer-specific epigenome; consequently, conventional techniques for somatic cell reprogramming often fail to achieve complete reprogramming in heterogeneous cancer cell pools. The partially reprogrammed cells do not reach full pluripotency but remain in an intermediate stage that exhibits stem-like and malignant features. Still, these cells offer a valuable tool for studying carcinogenesis, as they retain cancer-associated mutations while also gaining the ability to self-renew and differentiate, similar to those of pluripotent stem cells. Earlier examples of modeling oncogenic transitions and cellular identity shifts were observed in melanoma and colorectal carcinoma, where OSKM-induced cancer cell clones did not achieve complete reprogramming; instead, they adopted hybrid EMT/MET phenotypes and displayed elevated invasive potential (18, 36). These intercellular states represent a more plastic, invasive, and therapy-resistant population that can offer a platform for studying the early stages of cancer development.

Here, we report the successful reprogramming of bladder cancer cells, as validated by the expression of nuclear and cell surface markers associated with pluripotency. This work is also the first comprehensive and sequential proteomic characterization of cancer cell reprogramming, using SeV-induced bladder cancer cells as a model. By analyzing sequential passages (p1, p4 and p7), we revealed time-dependent molecular changes and the dynamic remodeling of the proteome during reprogramming (Figure 6). The gradual transformation from a highly proliferative and metabolic phenotype at p1 to a more plastic and stem-like state at p7 was marked by the enrichment of pathways related to translation and amino acid biosynthesis at p1 and those associated with pluripotency, energy metabolism, and stress resistance at p7, respectively. Quantitative MS-time course experiments also showed that the first 48h of OSKM induction is dominated by c-MYC-associated programs, including translation, ribosome biogenesis and metabolism and the later reprogramming wave is characterized by pluripotency-associated protein networks (20, 37). A progressive convergence between reprogrammed bladder cancer cells and the normal urothelial cell line SV-HUC-1 in the expression of epithelial markers, including E-cadherin, cytokeratin-8, and cytokeratin-18, indicates a partial restoration of epithelial identity and a reduction of mesenchymal traits in our study. Our clustering data showing MET trends are consistent with reprogramming and pluripotency-associated membrane protein and adhesion changes reported in the previous literature (38). The decline in migratory ability coincided with morphological and proteomic evidence of MET in reprogrammed cells. Our findings suggest that reprogramming reverses the EMT-associated phenotype of parental bladder cancer cells, leading to the re-gaining of epithelial adhesion proteins and reduced motility, similar to the cancer cell reprogramming studies reporting reduced migration and metastatic potential in different cancer models (5, 39).

An in-depth study of hESCs, iPSCs, and their parental fibroblast cells revealed a high proteomic similarity between hESCs and iPSCs. However, a small percentage of proteins (2.2%), primarily involved in metabolism, cell adhesion, and antigen processing, were differentially expressed. This suggests a failure of complete epigenome resetting during reprogramming, arising from differences in the starting cell population, and depends on whether a cell is permissive to transcriptional regulation (40, 41). Cancer cells are known to be less permissive to reprogramming, and various attempts result in incomplete or partial reprogramming (17, 39). Taken together, the integration of clustering and PPI analyses suggests that OSKM-induced reprogramming drives bladder cancer cells toward a phenotype that lies between differentiation and stemness but not to a complete pluripotent state. In hematopoietic and solid tumors, a drug-resistant subset of cells, namely cancer cells, retains the ability to initiate tumorigenesis that shares similarities with normal stem cells (42). Tumorigenesis is believed to arise from either a de-differentiated tumor cell or a genetically altered tumor cell that acquires stem cell characteristics (43). Reprogramming is a powerful tool for creating cancer stem cell models, essential for studying the early phases of cancer due to the similarities with induced pluripotent stem cells. The relationship between cancer and pluripotency is reflected in the intersection of the pathways governing pluripotency, differentiation and oncogenic transformation (44). The increased plasticity, differentiation ability, altered morphology, drug sensitivity, and changes in protein expression in our study suggest a possible production of a cancer stem cell population, but further molecular characterization and functional assays are necessary before drawing definitive conclusions.

In conclusion, our data indicate that bladder cancer cells can be partially reprogrammed, express pluripotency factors, modify parental cell characteristics, regain differentiation potential, and alter the cancer-associated proteome. Although we have provided evidence for the feasibility of reprogramming the HTB-4 bladder cancer cell line in both our current and previous studies, our research still has limitations (19). First, we attempted to reprogram two bladder cancer cell lines; however, one failed to reprogram, possibly due to distinct genomic and epigenomic fingerprints. UM-UC-3 cells initially grew as semi-suspension cultures, exhibiting a heterogeneous mixture of adherent and suspended cells, suggesting premature differentiation towards another lineage and hindering further validation of pluripotency (Supplementary Figure 1). Second, we refer to the reprogrammed cell line as partially reprogrammed because in vivo validation, such as teratoma formation, is lacking. Since we were primarily interested in disrupting the course of bladder carcinogenesis and inducing a reversal of malignant cell behavior, stable pluripotent-like cultures were not vital, and spontaneous differentiation after reprogramming was acceptable for testing our hypothesis. Third, cancer stem cells and pluripotent stem cells share overlapping features; it would be ideal to conduct experiments to determine the actual cellular state and demonstrate the in vivo tumorigenic potential of the reprogrammed bladder cancer cells. Fourth, we acknowledge that the distinct culture environments, specifically the use of serum-supplemented medium for parental cells versus chemically defined medium and a substrate for reprogrammed cells, represent a potential confounding variable. The transition to a serum-free, growth-factor-enriched medium was a biological necessity to prevent spontaneous differentiation and maintain the induced pluripotent state. While these environmental differences may contribute to variations in markers of EMT, proliferation, and migration, the observed shifts remain consistent with the fundamental biological rewiring associated with cellular reprogramming. Fifth, we utilize SV-HUC-1 cells as a non-malignant comparator; however, we recognize that as an SV40-immortalized cell line they necessitate distinct culture conditions and they do not represent an unaltered primary urothelial state. Despite this, SV-HUC-1 remains a validated and standard model in urothelial research, providing a stable genetic background necessary for the high-throughput proteomic and functional comparisons conducted in this study.

Conclusion

Cancer cell reprogramming remains a promising field of research with potential applications in patient-specific disease modelling, diagnostics, and therapies. We present an in vitro model that can be tailored to individual patients to mirror the dynamic transitions during bladder carcinogenesis, support biomarker development, and enable toxicity testing. Furthermore, our data provide a comprehensive framework for clarifying the mechanisms underlying cancer cell reprogramming and cancer stem cell research. We believe that, despite its challenges, cancer cell reprogramming can enhance our understanding of mechanisms and reverse cell fate for therapy.
