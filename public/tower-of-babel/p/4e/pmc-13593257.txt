Evaluation of vaccination coverage in Brazilian regions from 2000 to 2022
Authors: Monique Léia Aragão de Lira, Ketyllem Tayanne da Silva Costa, Thiffany Nayara Bento de Morais, Angelo Giuseppe Roncalli da Costa Oliveira, Fábia Barbosa de Andrade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In recent years, there has been a drop in vaccination coverage, which favors the reintroduction of vaccine-preventable diseases that are already being eliminated. The objective of this study is to evaluate vaccination coverage in Brazil from 2000 to 2022. This is an ecological time-trend study based on secondary data of individuals aged 0 to over 60 years in Brazil and its regions. Joinpoint regression was used to identify significant changes in trends over time. The dependent variable was general vaccination coverage, and the independent variables included geographic region and year, enabling analysis of temporal and spatial patterns. In the initial years of the study, the results showed global vaccination coverage parameters in Brazil that were lower than those recommended by the Ministry of Health, estimated at values greater than or equal to 90%. The findings highlight the need for strengthening vaccination strategies and public health policies to restore trust in immunization programs. Future research should explore barriers to coverage and assess the long-term effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on vaccination trends in Brazil.

1. Introduction

Vaccination is the safest and most effective cost-effective public health intervention ever implemented in the elimination and control of communicable diseases, in addition to being decisive in reducing morbidity and mortality, its technology enabled the eradication of serious diseases, such as smallpox in 1980.[1,2]

Faced with a smallpox epidemic, after years of experiments, the vaccine was discovered in 1796, by Edward Jenner, and arrived in the national territory in 1804 and only in 1832 was legislation establishing mandatory vaccinations. The Ministry of Health of Brazil determined the creation of the National Immunization Program (NIP) only in 1973, which was institutionalized in 1975.[3]

The epidemiological profile of the Brazilian population in 1980 was characterized by high incidence and mortality from communicable diseases, with the majority of cases and deaths occurring in the population under 5 years of age.[4] The average incidence of these diseases in the 1980s was 68.2/100,000 inhabitants, showing a decrease of 99.1%, reaching 0.6/100,000 inhabitants in 2010. Similarly to the decline in morbidity due to vaccine-preventable diseases, proportional mortality from communicable diseases in 1980 corresponded to 12% of total deaths in Brazil and in 2015 it was 3 times lower (4%).[5]

The immunization strategy and increased vaccination coverage (VC) play a fundamental role in disease prevention, as it has already contributed to the eradication of polio, elimination of measles and rubella, control of meningitis, among others.[6] The reduction in incidence and mortality from vaccine-preventable diseases, notably in the first years of life, has increased life expectancy and reduced hospitalizations. However, in recent years, there has been a drop in vaccination coverage, which favors the reintroduction of vaccine-preventable diseases that are already being eliminated.[6,7]

The mission of the National Immunization Program is based on the control, eradication and elimination of vaccine-preventable diseases and, to this end, the Ministry of Health establishes some indicators that support the program’s analysis: vaccination coverage; dropout rate; and homogeneity rate.[8] Brazil has one of the most comprehensive health systems in the world, the Unified Health System, which plays a central role in the free distribution of vaccines to the population.[9]

The NIP is considered an efficient public policy, recognized nationally and internationally. The universality of care is guaranteed by the free offer of 47 immunobiologicals to all selected groups, in vaccination rooms distributed throughout the country.[10]

Over the decades, Brazil has made significant advances in promoting and making vaccines available, resulting in the eradication or effective control of several diseases that previously posed serious risks to the population. However, despite the successes, vaccination coverage in Brazil still faces challenges that require continued attention, the drop in collective immunity could lead to the resurgence of some previously overcome diseases.[11]

Given the Brazilian scenario, the objective of this study is to evaluate vaccination coverage in Brazil in relation to Brazilian regions in the period from 2000 to 2022.

2. Methods

This is an ecological study of the temporal trend in general vaccination coverage per 100,000 inhabitants based on secondary data from people aged 0 to 60 years in Brazil and its regions for better presentation and evaluation of the data. All cases registered in the NIP from 2000 to 2022 were studied.

For general coverage of immunobiologicals in the National Vaccination Calendar for people aged 0 to over 60 years, the following vaccines were used: Bacillus Calmette-Guérin, Hepatitis B, Pentavalent (Diphtheria, Tetanus, Pertussis, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Polio), Oral Poliomyelitis, Inactivated Poliomyelitis, Human Rotavirus, Pneumococcal 10, Meningococcal C, Yellow fever, Triple Viral, Tetra Viral, Hepatitis A, Triple Bacterial, Double Adult (Diphtheria, Tetanus), Human Papillomavirus, Pneumococcal 23, Chickenpox, Meningococcal ACWY, Double Adult Acellular, Influenza (campaign). Due to the unavailability of public data, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccine was not included in the study.

The immunobiological data and the projection of the population of the Federation Units according to the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics for each region of Brazil were obtained from February to May 2023, through the Information Technology Department of the Unified Health System, Platform of the Ministry of Health. Accessed https://datasus.saude.gov.br/informacoes-de-saude-tabnet/. These were the only data analyzed for the development of this research, which is based on secondary data.

The dependent variable was general vaccination coverage in the age group from 0 to over 60 years old. The 5 Brazilian regions (North, Northeast, Midwest, South, and Southeast), states and the years of the time series from 2000 to 2022 were used as independent variables.

After data collection, it was necessary to clean the dataset using Microsoft Excel, which was also used to create Figure 1 through a boxplot and column chart to facilitate the understanding of vaccination coverage distribution. In addition, for a more robust statistical analysis, the Joinpoint software, version 4.9.0.0 (Surveillance Research Program, National Cancer Institute), was used. This software is freely and publicly available through the National Cancer Institute of the United States (http://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/).

Vaccination coverage in Brazil and the average for the years 2000 to 2022 in the states. Brazil, 2023.

Joinpoint performs segmented linear regression to identify significant trend changes over time, organizing the results into 2 main outputs: Annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change, which indicate statistically significant variations in the analyzed rates. The Monte Carlo Permutation method was applied to test the significance of the models. Finally, the results are presented as epidemiological curves, offering a clearer visualization of vaccination coverage trends in Brazil and its respective regions.

In this study, approval from the Research Ethics Committee was not necessary, as it was secondary data, in the public domain.

3. Results

Between 2000 and 2022, a total of 1381,586,427 vaccine doses were administered in Brazil, covering 100% of the population aged 0 to 60 years. Figure 1A presents a box plot illustrating national VC over the study period, revealing a percentage range from 77.06% to 78.18%, with a median coverage of 62.5%.

In the early years of the analysis, Brazil’s VC consistently fell below the threshold recommended by the Ministry of Health (≥90%), though it remained relatively stable through 2014. The highest recorded coverage occurred in 2015, peaking at 93.12%. This was followed by a sharp decline in 2016 (52.34%) and again in 2021 (61.66%). A modest recovery was observed between 2018 and 2019; however, coverage declined in the years that followed. By 2022, there was a slight upward trend (67.24%) compared to 2021, yet VC remained below the nationally recommended benchmark (Fig. 1B)

When examining the average vaccination coverage across Brazilian states over the entire period (2000–2022), the highest averages were observed in the Federal District (88.09), followed by Mato Grosso do Sul (82.26)—both located in the Central-West region—and Roraima (81.97) in the North. In contrast, the lowest averages were recorded in Acre (69.97), Rio Grande do Norte (70.68), and Rio de Janeiro (70.15), representing the North, Northeast, and Southeast regions, respectively (Fig. 1C).

Based on the results of this study, the segmented regression of the country and its regions presents equivalent straight line interruption points in the periods 2013 to 2016, 2016 to 2019 and 2019 to 2022, following a descending, increasing and descending order, respectively (Fig. 2).

In the early years of the historical series (2000–2010), vaccination rates in Brazil remained stable, followed by a growth trend from 2010 to 2013. However, a significant decline occurred in 2016, with an APC of –7.25. This was followed by a slight recovery in 2017 (APC: 4.00), which gradually diminished through 2022 (Fig. 2A). Linear regression analysis reveals that the Northeast region mirrored the national trend, reaching its peak coverage during the same period (Fig. 2B).

In contrast, the North region (Fig. 2C) initially showed an upward trend between 2000 and 2004, followed by 2 consecutive periods of decline (2004–2013 and 2013–2016), the latter marked by a significant APC of –8.51. A subsequent increase from 2016 to 2019 was again followed by a decline from 2019 to 2022, reflecting a pattern similar to the national trajectory. The Midwest region (Fig. 2D) displayed a comparable trend to that of the North, with the distinction of exhibiting stability during the 2004 to 2013 interval.

The South (Fig. 2E) and Southeast (Fig. 2F) regions are presented in an analogous way in the increasing and decreasing data lines, except when we consider the time of the initial analyses, where for the first interval of the line segment the South region shows the time of fall in 2000 to 2008 and the Southeast region has this period more spaced out (2000–2009), following which they show an increase, respectively, in the intervals of 2008 to 2013 and 2009 to 2013. In subsequent years, the segments showed a decrease in the vaccination rate (2013–2016), followed by an increase (2016–2019) and returning to a declining situation in the period 2019 to 2022, in both regions.

In all regions and in Brazil, in the year 2022 a growing trend can be seen compared to the year 2021, but still below the range of the parameters (Fig. 1).

According to the results of the study, in the period from 2000 to 2022, the linear regression analysis shows that in Brazil there were 4 moments of annual percentage variation in vaccination coverage—APCs: in the period from 2000 to 2010 of 0.02% per year, from 2010 to 2013 where there was an increase in the rate with APC of 2.45% per year, from 2013 to 2016 with a reduction in APC (–7.25%), from 2016 to 2019, it is observed an improvement in the rate with an APC of 4.00% per year, followed by a decrease from 2019 to 2022 with an APC of −5.87%, which was statistically significant (P = .0125), Table 1.

Compared with Brazil, the variation has similar behavior in the Northeast region (2010, 2013, 2016, and 2019) (Fig. 1B). The North and Central-West regions (Fig. 1C, D) present a similar pattern in the years 2004, 2013, 2016, and 2019, and in the Southeast and South regions, different patterns are initially observed in 2008 and 2009, respectively, and equally the other patterns of change in 2013, 2016, and 2019 (Fig. 1E, F), with all regions showing the same statistical significance as in Brazil.

4. Discussion

Throughout the history of Brazil’s NIP, its role in expanding vaccination coverage and investing in information systems capable of revealing both the program’s strengths and challenges has become increasingly prominent.[12] The NIP establishes vaccination coverage targets for the vaccines included in the national immunization schedule, aiming for coverage rates between 90% and 95% as the minimum threshold necessary for the control, elimination, and eradication of vaccine-preventable diseases.[13] In the present study, nationwide vaccination coverage goals were only achieved in 2015, a pattern similarly observed across the Northeast, Midwest, South, and Southeast regions. These achievements were followed by marked declines in coverage in 2016 and again in 2021, both nationally and regionally.[14] In 2015, Brazil was officially certified as having eliminated rubella, following the last confirmed case in 2009. Measles was declared eliminated in 2016; however, vaccination coverage had already begun to show a downward trend that year.[15] A similar study analyzing low vaccination coverage and its potential impacts on the health of the Brazilian population between 2014 and 2020 documented a substantial decline in immunization rates over time. According to Sato, “[...] the emergence of new measles cases in the country from 2018 onwards is an immediate consequence of low vaccination coverage [...].” She further notes that since 2016, coverage has declined by approximately 10% to 20% points annually—a trend accompanied by rising infant and maternal mortality rates, raising significant concerns for public health.[16]

Between 2000 and 2015, Brazil consistently met the Ministry of Health’s recommended targets, maintaining relatively stable coverage levels. As a result, many vaccine-preventable diseases became rare or unfamiliar, potentially diminishing public perception of their severity and increasing the risk of reintroduction or resurgence of diseases that had been previously controlled or eradicated. Starting around mid-2016, a decline in vaccination coverage began to emerge—not only in Brazil, but globally-signaling a broader failure to meet established immunization goals.[17]

The results presented in the graphical trends demonstrate a relatively homogeneous pattern of vaccination coverage across certain regions and at the national level over the last 7 years of the time series. These patterns include segment limits with 4 inflection points and an overall downward trend. While the early years of the series indicated a steady, though suboptimal, increase in coverage, this was not sustained.

Similar findings were reported in a study conducted in Brazil’s Northeast region from 2010 to 2021, where a consistent downward trend in vaccination coverage was observed.[13] A descriptive study in the North region between 2015 and 2019 similarly found that coverage levels failed to exceed 80% following 2015. These results align with national trends and reinforce the observed decline in vaccination coverage in recent years.[7]

One study attributed the persistence of low vaccination rates to the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted healthcare systems globally and posed a major challenge to school reopenings, largely due to reduced in-person attendance at health services-including childhood immunizations.[18]

Multiple factors may have contributed to this decline. These include systemic issues within the Brazilian Unified Health System, technical challenges such as the implementation of a new immunization information system, potential data loss, inconsistencies in data accuracy and completeness, as well as sociocultural influences. Public perception of vaccination may have been further affected by growing vaccine hesitancy, anti-vaccination movements fueled by misinformation and fake news, and the broader impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic.[12]

Recent studies suggest that, in the Brazilian context, the decline in vaccination coverage is associated with structural factors as well as the lasting effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. Municipalities with lower fiscal capacity, largely dependent on federal and state funding, tend to show lower rates of childhood vaccination coverage.[19] Furthermore, the COVID-19 period exacerbated a preexisting downward trend, with only limited recovery in the subsequent years.[20] Therefore, this context may have significantly impacted vaccination coverage; however, this reality can vary depending on the circumstances of each country.

Therefore, these findings suggest the need for investment in the restructuring of public immunization policies, with the aim of restoring public trust through educational campaigns, improvements in information systems, and the fight against misinformation.

The limitations of the study are the use of secondary data and the reduced number of research related to quantitative data on the general coverage of vaccines in the country in recent years regarding the topic addressed.

5. Conclusion

From the results obtained in this study, it was possible to verify a general percentage vaccination coverage of the Brazilian population above 80% in the years 2001, 2004, 2011, 2014 and 2015 and a considerable decline from 2016 onwards, reaching even lower numbers than 55%, accentuated by the COVID-19 pandemic, despite the annual multi-vaccination campaigns implemented by the National Immunization Program. In the temporal analysis, states in the North, Northeast and Southeast regions have the lowest rates in the general average of vaccination coverage in the federated units.

The linear regression analysis of the country and its regions identified 4 temporal variations (2013–2016, 2016–2019, and 2019–2022), establishing a pattern of decline, recovery, and subsequent downturn. Particular emphasis is given to the North region, which showed the most significant negative variation between 2019 and 2022. In 2022, Brazil demonstrated an improvement compared to 2021; however, vaccination coverage remained below the target set by the Ministry of Health.

These findings suggest a likely increased risk of reintroducing vaccine-preventable diseases, potentially compromising the progress achieved in public health in Brazil. Therefore, it is crucial to strengthen mass vaccination strategies nationwide, with a focus on equity and the reduction of structural and social barriers. Furthermore, the results highlight the need for more assertive and robust public policies, particularly to restore public trust in the immunization system. Finally, this study has limitations due to its reliance on secondary data and the scarcity of specific data, especially regarding COVID-19 vaccination.
