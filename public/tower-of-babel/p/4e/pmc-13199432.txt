Tear fluid as a novel specimen for detection of alpha-synuclein seeding activity in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Matteo Costanzo, Anna Ladogana, Marco Sbriccoli, Francesco Marchet, Flavia Porreca, Clara Salciccia, Michele Equestre, Gabriele Moracci, Maria Ilenia De Bartolo, Giulia Ruocco, Filippo Carlo Carlà, Antonella Conte, Giovanni Fabbrini, Daniele Belvisi, Anna Poleggi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a clinically heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorder characterized by intraneuronal accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein. Real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) detects seeding-competent αSyn species but commonly relies on invasive matrices. We evaluated tear fluid (TF) as a non-invasive biofluid for αSyn RT-QuIC in 44 PD patients and 32 matched healthy controls (HCs). ROC analyses were performed on participant-level readouts (mean of four technical replicates), and optimal cut-offs were selected using the Youden index before being applied to each technical replicate. At the subject level, the best trade-offs were observed at maximum fluorescence (MaxRFU) ≥ 2/4 (sensitivity 59.1%; specificity 65.6%), MedianRFU ≥ 3/4 (sensitivity 40.9%; specificity 78.1%), and area under fluorescence curve (AUFC) ≥ 3/4 (sensitivity 47.7%; specificity 71.9%). In a sensitivity analysis excluding RT-QuIC positive HCs with potential prodromal/risk features, specificity increased to 75% (MaxRFU), 86.2% (MedianRFU), and 79.3% (AUFC). Assay-characterization analyses, performed including all technical replicates, showed significantly higher fluorescence signals throughout the RT-QuIC amplification curve (p < 0.0001) with shorter lag phase, higher MedianRFU and MaxRFU, and larger AUFC values in PD than in HCs (all p < 0.05). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested possible phenotype-related variability in test response. Our findings suggest that αSyn seeding activity may be detected in TF. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder traditionally defined by clinical criteria, with definitive diagnosis requiring post-mortem evidence of α-synuclein(αSyn) pathology together with neuronal loss in the substantia nigra pars compacta1–3. The misfolding and aggregation of αSyn are considered central to PD pathophysiology, defining it as a prototypical α-synucleinopathy4–6. Growing recognition of this mechanism has driven efforts to develop biomarkers capable of detecting pathogenic αSyn conformers in vivo6–11.

Real-Time Quaking-Induced Conversion (RT-QuIC) has emerged as a particularly promising technique, enabling the detection of minute amounts of seeding-competent αSyn through a cyclic amplification process6,12. The assay exploits the prion-like behaviour of misfolded αSyn, using cycles of incubation and shaking to promote aggregates formation, which is monitored through fluorescence6. While initial evidence for the utility of RT-QuIC in the ante-mortem diagnosis of PD was obtained using cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples, its application has been extended to more easily accessible biological fluids and tissues, including skin, olfactory mucosa (OM), blood and saliva8–11,13–15. Each of these substrates presents specific advantages and limitations. Lumbar puncture and skin biopsies may be considered invasive limiting their use in large-scale screening, routine confirmatory testing, or patient selection for clinical trials. The OM may represent an early site of synucleinopathic involvement in specific PD subtypes16,17. However, its sampling requires technical expertise and the sensitivity of RT-QuIC appears to be region-dependent, with higher diagnostic accuracy when targeting the agger nasi13. Blood represents an attractive substrate given its accessibility, but RT-QuIC application is challenging, often requiring the isolation of neuron-derived extracellular vesicles or immunoprecipitation-based protocols8,18–20. Saliva demands rigorous pre-analytical procedures21.

Among alternative biofluids, tear fluid (TF) represents an attractive candidate because its collection is non-invasive, rapid, and can be performed in an outpatient setting with high patient acceptability, enabling straightforward repeat sampling over time. These features make TF particularly suitable for longitudinal assessments, patient stratification and potentially large-scale screening and clinical trial settings, where feasibility and tolerability are pivotal. In addition, TF may hold potential pathophysiological relevance. Indeed, it is secreted by the lacrimal gland, which shares with salivary glands the innervation pattern22–25. One previous study reported elevated levels of αSyn in the TF of PD patients22 but evidence regarding αSyn seeding activity detection in TF remain scarce. Only recently has αSyn-based RT-QuIC been explored in this matrix and independent studies evaluating feasibility and diagnostic performance are still warranted26.

Addressing this gap may contribute to the development of fully non-invasive, αSyn-specific diagnostic tools suitable for early-stage detection, patient stratification, and large-scale screening. In this proof-of-concept study we aimed to assess the feasibility of applying RT-QuIC to TF samples in patients with clinically diagnosed PD. We also evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of TF-based RT-QuIC for distinguishing PD from healthy controls (HCs). Finally, we explored potential relationship between RT-QuIC kinetic parameters and clinical measures of disease severity.

Methods and materials

Subjects

Patients with clinically established diagnosis of PD1 were consecutively enrolled at the Department of Human Neuroscience, Sapienza University of Rome. Exclusion criteria included the presence of overt dementia and psychiatric conditions that could interfere with the ability to provide informed consent. Patients with known ocular diseases were excluded. Age and sex-matched HCs were recruited among non-consanguineous volunteers and caregivers. Data and sample collection was carried out during a single morning session and lasted approximately 1–1.5 h. All evaluations were conducted in the OFF state, after an overnight withdrawal of antiparkinsonian drugs. This study was approved by the national ethics committee (n. 0059845) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. All patients were informed of the purpose of the study and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Demographic and clinical assessment

Clinical assessment was performed by a single neurologist specialized in movement disorders to minimize inter-rater variability. Patients with PD were screened to exclude other neurological or movement disorders. Demographic data (age, age at onset, sex and disease duration) were collected. Disease stage and motor symptom were assessed using the Hoehn and Yahr scale (H&Y) and the Movement Disorder Society-sponsored Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) part III, respectively27,28. Non-motor symptoms were evaluated using the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS)29, while global cognitive function was evaluated with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)30 and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)31. Executive functioning was assessed using the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB)32, while personality and behavioral changes were assessed by the Italian Version of the Frontal Behavioral Inventory33. The presence and severity of REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD) symptoms were evaluated through the RBD Screening Questionnaire34. Finally, MDS-UPDRS parts I, II, and IV were used to assess non-motor experiences of daily living, motor experiences of daily living, and motor complications, respectively28. HCs underwent a general neurological examination to exclude the presence of neurological signs. They were clinically screened for prodromal symptoms potentially associated with synucleinopathies, including REM sleep behavior disorder, hyposmia, autonomic dysfunction, urinary disfunctions and constipation. The presence of RBD was assessed using the RBD Questionnaire34. Hyposmia, autonomic symptoms, urinary dysfunction, and constipation were evaluated using the corresponding items from the NMSS: items 1 and 2 for autonomic symptoms, item 21 for constipation, items 22, 23, and 24 for urinary dysfunction, and item 28 for hyposmia29. A cumulative score greater than 1 in the relevant items was considered indicative of the presence of the respective symptom. The presence of potential prodromal symptoms was recorded for descriptive and exploratory analyses.

Sample collection and storage

TF samples were collected from both eyes using Schirmer test strips (Contacare olphtalmics and diagnostics, India), without topical anesthesia22,35. Strips measuring 5 millimeters in width and 35 millimeters in length were placed at the inferior eyelid margin, in the medial region near the lacrimal sac, for 10 min35. Tear secretion was measured by recording the wetting length on the millimeter-graded paper strip. Immediately after collection, strips were placed in low-binding polypropylene tubes on dry ice and transferred to − 80 °C within 30 min for storage until further analysis35. After the procedure, participants were asked to rate their level of discomfort using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (no discomfort) to 10 (maximum discomfort).

Sample processing

For the extraction of TF from the strips we followed the protocol by Schmitz et al., with modifications35. An extraction buffer was prepared to a final concentration of 40mM phosphate buffer (PB) pH 8.0, 170 mM NaCl, and 0.0015% SDS. Each frozen strip was thawed on ice, and the wet part was cut into ~5 mm pieces using sterile single-use scissors to prevent contamination and transferred into 1.5mL Eppendorf tubes. Cut pieces from the two eyes of each subject were combined to yield a single extract per case. Based on wetting length, the required extraction buffer volume was predetermined (e.g., 35 mm corresponds to 100µL of TF extraction buffer) to target comparable dilution across cases35,36. This approach was supported by a preliminary optimization step in two HCs, in which NanoDrop spectrophotometric measurements (A280-based; Thermo Fisher Scientific) showed an increase in TF protein concentration with increasing Schirmer strip wetting length (Supplementary Fig. 1). Afterwards, the tubes were briefly spun (5 s), vortexed for 30 s and incubated on a shaker at 20 °C for 30 min. After incubation, each 1.5mL tube was placed inside a 2mL Eppendorf tube (with the cap removed), pierced at the bottom using a sterile needle, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C to recover the extracted fluid. TF recovered from each sample was then pooled into a 0.5mL Eppendorf tube and kept on ice until further processing.

RT QuIC assay

For RT-QuIC assays, we used commercially available recombinant human αSyn (AlexoTech). Each vial was reconstituted in 2 mL of 40 mM PB, to obtain a final concentration of 0.50 mg/mL. The content was filtered using a centrifugal filter unit of 0.22 μm pore size (Nanosep) at 4 °C for 30 min at 3,000 × g and subsequently pooled. Protein concentration was then determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop device by measuring absorbance at 280 nm, applying an extinction coefficient of 0.36(mg/mL) −1·cm− 1. Based on this measurement, the αSyn stock concentration was adjusted and used at a final concentration of approximately 0.1 mg/mL in each reaction well.

For RT-QuIC experiments, a black 96-well plate (Sigma-Aldrich) was preloaded with six 0.8 mm-diameter silica beads (OPS Diagnostics) per well. 85µL of reaction buffer (0.1 M PB pH8, 170mM NaCl, 10µM Thioflavin T, 0.0015% SDS, and 0.1 mg/mL recombinant αSyn) were added in each well and seeded with 15µL of TF extracted sample for a final volume of 100uL per well. Reactions were performed in quadruplicate. To monitor spontaneous aggregation, each plate included quadruplicate unseeded control wells containing 85µL of reaction mix (including recombinant human αSyn) and 15µL of Milli-Q water (in place of patient sample), yielding the same final volume per wells as seeded reactions. The plates were sealed with a sealing film (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated in a FLUOstar OMEGA (BMG Labtech) plate reader at 55 °C for 100 h under cyclic shaking conditions (1 min shaking at 600 rpm followed by 1 min’s rest). Thioflavin T fluorescence was measured every 15 min using excitation and emission wavelengths of 435 nm and 485 nm, respectively. Kinetic parameters were extracted using MARS Analysis software (BMG Labtech). The reaction conditions (temperature, shaking cycles, SDS concentration, recombinant αSyn concentration and inclusion of silica beads) were selected based on preliminary optimization experiments starting from a CSF-based protocol37, with the goal of maximizing assay sensitivity and minimizing background signal (See Assay optimization for TF-based RT-QuIC and Supplementary materials). Regarding RT-QuIC data processing, background correction was performed by subtracting the mean signal of the first 10 readings from each replicate.

Assay optimization for TF-based RT-QuIC

Preliminary optimization experiments were performed to adapt a CSF-based αSyn RT-QuIC protocol to TF37. Optimization was conducted on a pilot set of TF samples (patients with clinically established PD, n = 3; HCs, n = 3) collected and extracted using the same procedures described above. Pilot optimization samples were used exclusively to select reaction conditions and were not included in the main study cohort. We evaluated reaction conditions including temperature (42 °C vs. 55 °C), shaking speed (400, 600, and 700 rpm), the presence/absence of silica beads, recombinant αSyn concentration (0.005 mg/mL, 0.01 mg/mL, and 0.02 mg/mL) and SDS concentration (0.0010%, 0.0015%), while keeping the remaining reaction components and readout settings constant. Conditions were selected by prioritizing (i) amplification in PD samples (i.e., a higher proportion of positive wells and higher fluorescence kinetics), (ii) minimal background signal and delayed/absent amplification in HCs, and (iii) absence of spontaneous aggregation in unseeded wells across runs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism®v10.1.1 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as absolute counts and percentages. Age and sex were compared between HCs and PD patients using Welch’s t-test and the chi-square test, respectively. Given that TF represents a previously unexplored matrix for αSyn RT-QuIC, the first step of the statistical analysis was to identify candidate positivity thresholds for RT-QuIC kinetic readouts and to explore their diagnostic utility at the participant level. Since four technical replicates were obtained from the same TF extract, replicate-level observations were not treated as independent. For each background-corrected replicate, kinetic parameters were extracted, including median fluorescence (MedianRFU), maximum fluorescence (MaxRFU) and area under the fluorescence curve (AUFC). Replicate-level values were then averaged across the four replicates to obtain one value per participant for each parameter, in line with previous studies38–40. Receiver operating curve (ROC) analyses were then conducted using these participant-level parameters to estimate discriminatory ability (area under the curve, AUC) and to identify candidate cut-offs via the Youden index41–43. ROC-derived cut-offs were subsequently applied to individual technical replicates to classify replicate positivity. Subject-level RT-QuIC status was then defined based on the number of positive replicates (≥ 1, ≥ 2, ≥3, or 4/4) and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were reported for each threshold. Balanced accuracy was derived as the mean of sensitivity and specificity for each threshold. We also tested a recently proposed “two cut-offs” approach, previously adopted in several clinical studies on CSF αSyn RT-QuIC44,45, classifying subjects as clearly negative (0/4), intermediate (1–3/4), or clearly positive (4/4). Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated excluding intermediate cases and computing sensitivity/specificity only for 0/4 vs. 4/4. Additionally, as exploratory assay-characterization we compared kinetic parameters extracted at the replicate level (lag phase, MedianRFU, MaxRFU, time to half-maximum fluorescence (T50), and AUFC) between PD and HCs, using the Mann–Whitney U test. Potential associations among demographic, clinical and RT-QuIC kinetic parameters were evaluated with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. False discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons was applied as appropriate. Finally, we compared demographic and clinical features between RT-QuIC–positive and RT-QuIC–negative PD patients. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p < 0.05.

The TF collection procedure was well tolerated by all participants, with no significant between-groups differences in VAS discomfort scores (U = 698; p = 0.94). Optimization of TF-based RT-QuIC conditions

We optimized the TF-based αSyn RT-QuIC assay by sequentially modifying temperature, shaking speed, bead inclusion, SDS concentration, and recombinant αSyn concentration in a pilot set tested across repeated runs, with unseeded wells included as controls. For one PD pilot patient, we also collected a CSF sample.

In run 1 (2 PD and 2 HCs; Conditions: 42 °C, 400 rpm, 0.0015% SDS, recombinant αSyn 0.01 mg/mL, with and without six silica beads), no relevant amplification was observed, except for one PD sample tested with six beads showing fluorescence increase in 1/4 replicates (Supplementary Fig. 2A). In run 2 (2 PD and 2 HCs; Conditions: 42 °C, 600 rpm, 0.0015% SDS, recombinant αSyn 0.01 mg/mL, six silica beads), no amplification was detected in any replicate (Supplementary Fig. 2B). In run 3 (2 PD and 1 HCs; Conditions: 55 °C, 400 rpm, 0.0015% SDS, recombinant αSyn 0.01 mg/mL, six silica beads), only a weak signal increase was observed in one PD sample (2/4 replicates) (Supplementary Fig. 2C). In run 4 (3 PD and 3 HCs; Conditions: 55 °C, 700 rpm, 0.0015% SDS, recombinant αSyn 0.01 mg/mL, six silica beads), amplification became more evident in PD samples, but specificity decreased because one HC sample showed clear fluorescence increase (4/4 replicates), whereas the other two HCs and unseeded wells remained negative (Supplementary Fig. 2D). In run 5 (1 PD and 1 reactive HC; Conditions: 55 °C, 700 rpm, recombinant αSyn 0.01 mg/mL, six silica beads), lowering SDS from 0.0015% to 0.0010% increased fluorescence reactivity but also increased non-specific amplification in the HC sample; therefore, 0.0015% SDS was retained (Supplementary Fig. 2E). In run 6 (2 PD and 2 HCs; Conditions: 55 °C, 600 rpm, 0.0015% SDS, recombinant αSyn 0.01, six silica beads), amplification was preserved in PD samples and absent in HC and unseeded wells (Supplementary Fig. 2F). In run 7 (1 PD and 3 HCs; Conditions: 55 °C, 600 rpm, 0.0015% SDS; six silica beads), testing recombinant αSyn concentrations of 0.005 mg/mL, 0.01 mg/mL, and 0.02 mg/mL showed stronger but less specific signals at 0.005 mg/mL and weaker overall amplification at 0.02 mg/mL; therefore, 0.01 mg/mL was selected as the best signal-to-background compromise (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Based on these findings, the final conditions selected for the study were 55 °C, 600 rpm, 0.0015% SDS, 0.01 mg/mL of recombinant αSyn and six silica beads per well.

Under these conditions, as an illustrative comparison, TF and paired CSF sample from one PD pilot patient were both tested, supporting the biological plausibility of TF seeding detection (Supplementary Fig. 2H).

ROC–derived cut-offs for RT-QuIC positivity thresholds

ROC analyses based on participant-level readouts (i.e., values averaged across four technical replicates) were performed for MedianRFU, MaxRFU and AUFC, yielding AUCs of 0.592 (95%CI = 0.463–0.721), 0.586 (95%CI = 0.458–0.715) and 0.587 (95%CI = 0.458–0.716) respectively. For each parameter, the optimal cut-off was determined using the Youden index. For MedianRFU, the best cut-off was > 14,913 RFU (YI = 0.17; sensitivity = 62.5%, specificity = 54.6%), while for MaxRFU, the cut-off was > 58,439 RFU (YI = 0.19; sensitivity = 62.5%, specificity = 56.8%). For the AUFC, the optimal cut-off was > 4,120,283 RFU/hours (YI = 0.21; sensitivity = 53.1%, specificity = 68.2%).

Subject-level RT-QuIC classification based on the number of positive replicates

By applying ROC/Youden index-derived cut-off values for MaxRFU (> 58,439 RFU), MedianRFU (> 14,913 RFU) and AUFC (> 4,120,283 RFU/hours) to each technical replicate, we assessed participant-level diagnostic performance (Table 2). Using the MaxRFU parameter, the best sensitivity/specificity trade-off was observed at the threshold of at least two positive replicates (sensitivity = 59.1%; specificity = 65.6%). Using the MedianRFU fluorescence parameter, the optimal balance was achieved with at least three positive replicates (sensitivity = 40.9%; specificity = 78.1%). Based on the AUFC, the most favorable compromise between sensitivity and specificity was obtained at the threshold of at least three positive replicates (sensitivity = 47.7%, specificity = 71.9%). In an exploratory “two cut-offs” sensitivity analysis (0/4 vs. 4/4 positive replicates), MaxRFU showed sensitivity of 46.2% and specificity of 78.9% (included cases = 26 PD and 19 HCs; excluded intermediate cases = 18 PD and 13 HCs) while MedianRFU showed sensitivity of 45.8% and specificity of 78.9% (included cases = 24 PD and 19 HCs; excluded intermediate cases = 20 PD and 13 HCs). For AUFC, sensitivity was 50.0% and specificity 73.7% (included = 26 PD 19 HCs; excluded intermediate cases = 18 PD and 13 HCs) (See Supplementary materials). Exploratory characterization of TF-based αSyn seeding activity and RT-QuIC kinetics in PD patients and HCs

As an exploratory assay-characterization analysis, considering all individual technical replicates across the dataset, fluorescence values from PD-derived samples were significantly higher than those from HCs throughout the amplification curve (U = 42604; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). Replicates from PD patients showed significantly higher MedianRFU (U = 9534; p = 0.02) (Fig. 3A), greater MaxRFU (U = 9627; p = 0.03) (Fig. 3B), shorter lag phase (U = 2462; p = 0.018) (Fig. 3C), and larger AUFC (U = 9411; p = 0.014) (Fig. 3D) compared to replicates from HCs. Conversely, no significant differences were observed in T50 values between PD patients and HCs (U = 2645; p = 0.10). Unseeded control wells showed no detectable amplification throughout the RT-QuIC assays.

Relationship between TF-based RT-QuIC kinetic parameters and clinical features in PD patients

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis demonstrated no significant associations between TF-based RT-QuIC kinetic parameters and demographic variables. Only weak, non-significant trends were observed between AUFC and age (r = − 0.295, p = 0.052, q = 0.158) and between MaxRFU and sex (r = 0.294, p = 0.052, q = 0.158).

Regarding clinical features, after FDR correction, the FBI total score was the only measure significantly associated with TF RT-QuIC kinetic parameters, showing inverse correlations with medianRFU (r = − 0.371, p = 0.0142, q = 0.031), maxRFU (r = − 0.362, p = 0.0170, q = 0.032) and AUFC (r = − 0.377, p = 0.0127, q = 0.028). NMSS total score showed a weak trend toward a negative association with AUC (r = − 0.299, p = 0.0292, q = 0.051), but this did not survive correction for multiple comparison.

Exploratory analysis of demographic and clinical features in subjects with discordant results

To explore potential factors which may influence the outcomes of RT-QuIC testing, we examined demographics, family history and potential premorbid features of HCs with positive RT-QuIC results (i.e., false positives) and compared the clinical profiles of PD patients with negative versus positive RT-QuIC results (i.e., false negative vs. true positives). For these analyses, we used the participant-level classification based on the MaxRFU parameter with a threshold of ≥ 2 positive replicates, as this rule showed the best trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, with a balanced accuracy of 62.4% (vs. 59.5% for the optimal MedianRFU-based rule and 59.8% for the optimal AUFC-based rule). Among the RT-QuIC positive HCs, one reported a family history of PD, one had constipation, one had autonomic dysfunction and another had both autonomic dysfunction and RBD. Notably, when excluding these four subjects, the specificity of the assay increased from 65.6% to 75%.

In PD patients, we compared demographic and clinical variables between true positives (n = 26) and false negatives (n = 18). Sex distribution did not differ significantly between groups (χ²=2.946, p = 0.0861). No statistically significant differences were observed in the other demographic variables examined (age, age at onset, disease duration) after correction for multiple comparisons (all q > 0.05).

Regarding clinical measures, at the uncorrected level, the false negative group showed non-significant trends toward higher ranks for MDS-UPDRS part I (U = 159.5, p = 0.076, q = 0.288), MDS-UPDRS part II (U = 154.5, p = 0.057, q = 0.288), NMSS total score (U = 161.0, p = 0.082, q = 0.288), and FBI total score (U = 144.5, p = 0.055, q = 0.288). No differences were observed in the remaining clinical variables (MDS-UPDRS parts III-IV, MMSE, MoCA, and FAB; all q > 0.05). The prevalence of hyposmia, constipation, autonomic symptoms, and RBD did not differ significantly between groups, as assessed using the chi-square test (all p > 0.05). Conversely, urinary problems were more frequent in the false negative group than in the true positive group (χ²=6.246, p = 0.0124). Among the patients who underwent DAT scan (n = 20), 14 were from the true positive group and 6 from the false negative group.

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to unequivocal PD cases (4/4 vs. 0/4), findings were directionally consistent with the main analysis. False negatives showed non-significant trends at the uncorrected level toward higher ranks for NMSS total score (U = 46.5, p = 0.0540, q = 0.252) and FBI total score (U = 43.0, p = 0.0515, q = 0.252). Urinary and autonomic symptoms were more frequent in false negatives than in true positives (χ²= 6.003, p = 0.0143, same distribution across groups for both variables).

Discussion

A key contribution of our study is to provide independent proof-of-concept evidence that αSyn-based RT-QuIC can be applied to TF in patients with PD.

Previous studies have explored several matrices, including CSF46,47, OM13,48, skin9,10, blood8,18,20, and saliva11,14. The main advantages of TF in this context are its ease of collection, good tolerability and non-invasive sampling, without the need for specialized instrumentation. In our cohort, low VAS discomfort scores and a mean TF volume exceeding 200 µL in both groups confirmed the feasibility of TF sampling.

Beyond collection, PD samples showed higher fluorescence signals and significantly different RT-QuIC kinetic parameters than HCs in the exploratory assay-characterization analysis, supporting a detectable group-level seeding-related signal. Importantly, a recently published study also detected αSyn seeding activity in TF from PD patients using RT-QuIC26. Taken together, findings from two near-contemporaneous independent studies in distinct cohorts provide converging support for TF as a promising non-invasive matrix, while highlighting the need for further optimization and multicenter validation.

The presence of seeding-competent αSyn in TF is biologically plausible. Elevated soluble αSyn levels in TF from PD patients have been reported using an ultra-sensitive single-molecule array platform, suggesting that ocular fluids may reflect ongoing synucleinopathic processes22. Lacrimal glands share autonomic innervation with salivary glands, which show Lewy body pathology even in the early PD stages24,49. Although the pathways of αSyn spread to the lacrimal system remain unclear, experimental studies in primary mouse neurons suggested that preformed oligomeric αSyn aggregates can undergo both anterograde and retrograde axonal transport50–52, supporting potential propagation from central autonomic structures to peripheral targets such as lacrimal glands. Moreover, TF-based RT-QuIC has shown promising results in prion diseases, the prototype of protein misfolding disorders that include synucleinopathies35,36.

A major limitation of our study is the suboptimal sensitivity of the assay. Similarly, Canaslan et al., detected αSyn seeding activity in TF in 67% of PD cases, with specificity of 76% versus controls26. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in cohort size, recombinant αSyn preparation (commercial vs. home-made), and reaction conditions, the partially convergent sensitivity range across independent studies supports the view that limited sensitivity is an expected challenge at this stage of TF assay development, consistent with early feasibility studies adapting seeding assays to new matrices15,48,53.

The reduced sensitivity in TF may be explained by both matrix-related factors and assay-optimization issues. Compared with CSF, blood, or saliva, TF-based seeding amplification assay relies on significantly smaller sample volumes and a complex matrix that may dilute αSyn seeds and affect amplification kinetics. TF has a high water content, potentially lowering seed concentration, and a relatively high protein concentration (~ 8 µg/µL), including proteases and protease inhibitors, which may interfere with assay performance54. Although NanoDrop-based protein measurements increased with Schirmer strip wetting length in our preliminary tests, this may not reflect αSyn seeding activity, and possible matrix-related inhibitory effects at higher sample volumes should be specifically addressed in future studies.

Our pilot experiments further highlighted the need for reaction optimization. Recombinant αSyn concentration had a non-linear impact on assay performance and even small changes in SDS concentration markedly influenced kinetics. These observations are consistent with RT-QuIC optimization studies in prion diseases, where multiple reaction parameters markedly influence amplification kinetics55,56. Beyond reaction optimization, pre-analytical strategies not assessed here, such as protease inhibition or immunoprecipitation-based enrichment of pathological αSyn species before amplification, may further improve diagnostic performance, as reported for other matrices8,21,57. Future studies should also integrate qualitative seeding readouts with quantitative αSyn measurements in TF to clarify whether weak or negative RT-QuIC signals reflect a low seed burden and to establish rational sample dilution criteria based on αSyn content. In parallel, implementing a prespecified workflow for indeterminate samples, such as repeating the assay under an alternative validated reaction condition (e.g., a different recombinant αSyn concentration), may improve classification confidence. Overall, these findings support the need for harmonization of reaction conditions to ensure analytical validation, transferability, and cross-laboratory comparability58.

Importantly, across Canaslan et al.26 and the present study, TF-based RT-QuIC specificity at the optimal classification threshold ranged between 70 and 80%. This level of specificity suggests that, if appropriately optimized, TF-based RT-QuIC could serve as an easily accessible confirmatory tool in clinically suggestive PD cases or support patient selection in trials. Notably, when current MDS diagnostic criteria are correctly applied, sensitivity and specificity reach 94.5% and 88.5%, respectively1,59. Thus, the value of an RT-QuIC biomarker may lie less in replacing clinical criteria and more in supporting diagnosis in ambiguous cases, detecting disease at prodromal stages, or enabling biological stratification in trials.

In our study, some RT-QuIC–positive HCs reported potential prodromal features. Although classified as controls in the absence of clinical signs, preclinical stages of disease cannot be excluded. Future studies should therefore evaluate TF-based RT-QuIC in prodromal populations and in differential diagnosis versus atypical parkinsonian syndromes.

Finally, although correlations between TF RT-QuIC kinetic parameters and clinical/demographic variables were overall limited, exploratory analyses suggested that patient-related factors may contribute to variability in assay response, in line with previous studies on OM15,60,61. In particular, false negative PD patients showed a trend toward higher NMSS scores and significantly greater autonomic and urinary burden than true-positive patients. These features are classically associated with a body-first subtype62–64. Although exploratory and requiring replication in larger cohorts, these findings raise the possibility that phenotypic heterogeneity affects the detectability of αSyn seeding activity in TF, supporting a stratified approach to biomarker development and validation.

We recognize several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power to detect associations between RT-QuIC results and clinical features. Second, controls were limited to healthy individuals, precluding conclusions about specificity versus other neurodegenerative disorders. Third, we did not perform a head-to-head comparison of TF-based RT-QuIC with established matrices within the same participants. Finally, diagnostic thresholds were defined post hoc and require external validation. Future studies should include disease controls and direct within-subject comparisons across matrices to better define diagnostic performance.

Despite its proof-of-concept nature, this study provides evidence that RT-QuIC can be applied to TF to detect αSyn seeding activity in PD. TF emerges as a feasible, well-tolerated, and fully non-invasive matrix for biomarker development. These findings provide a rationale for further optimization and analytical validation of TF-based RT-QuIC, warranting confirmation in larger, multicenter studies.
