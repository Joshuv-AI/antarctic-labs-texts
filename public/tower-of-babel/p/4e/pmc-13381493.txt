Molecular Mechanisms of Hippocampal Synaptic Plasticity Disruption Induced by Chronic Methamphetamine Exposure: A Narrative Review
Authors: Mahdiyeh Hedayati-Moghadam, Fateme Razazpour, Zohreh Hakemi, Fateme Khani, Yousef Baghcheghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic methamphetamine (Meth) abuse represents a significant global public health crisis, characterized by profound and often persistent cognitive deficits, particularly in hippocampal-dependent memory and learning. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on the molecular mechanisms through which chronic Meth exposure disrupts hippocampal synaptic plasticity, ultimately driving cognitive impairment. We establish that the initiating event involves Meth-induced dysregulation of dopaminergic signaling, primarily through dopamine transporter (DAT) inhibition, leading to sustained extracellular dopamine surges. This dopamine excess triggers a pathogenic cascade dominated by three interconnected processes: (1) pronounced oxidative stress via dopamine auto-oxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage synaptic components; (2) mitochondrial apoptotic activation through p53-Bax signaling, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3-mediated cleavage of synaptic scaffolding proteins (e.g., PSD-95, ARC); and (3) suppression of neurotrophic support via BDNF-TrkB-PI3K/Akt pathway impairment, compounded by glutamate receptor dysregulation (NMDAR internalization, AMPAR trafficking defects). These mechanisms converge to induce structural synaptic pathology—including dendritic spine loss (notably mature mushroom spines in dentate gyrus), presynaptic vesicle depletion, and postsynaptic density disintegration—and functional deficits in LTP and LTD. Critically, these processes are amplified by neuroinflammation (microglial TNF-α/IL-1β release) and epigenetic dysregulation (HDAC2 upregulation, BDNF promoter methylation), creating a self-sustaining cycle of synaptic injury. Preclinical and clinical evidence consistently links these molecular disruptions to measurable cognitive decline, including impaired spatial navigation, pattern separation, and declarative memory. Therapeutic strategies targeting key nodes of this cascade show significant promise: dopamine stabilizers (e.g., aripiprazole) normalize D1/D2 receptor imbalance; anti-apoptotics (e.g., minocycline, resveratrol) inhibit caspase-3 and p53; neurotrophic agents (TrkB agonists, BDNF mimetics) restore synaptic protein synthesis; and antioxidants (e.g., N-acetylcysteine) counteract ROS. Future research must address critical gaps in understanding sex-specific vulnerabilities, circuit-selective susceptibility (e.g., hippocampal-VTA loops), and the role of stable epigenetic modifications in sustaining cognitive deficits. Advancing multi-target therapeutic approaches aligned with the temporal progression of Meth neurotoxicity offers the best hope for reversing synaptic dysfunction and mitigating the enduring cognitive burden of Meth addiction.Graphical Abstract Chronic methamphetamine exposure initiates hippocampal synaptic disruption through dopamine transporter dysfunction, causing sustained extracellular dopamine elevation that triggers oxidative stress, mitochondrial apoptosis (p53/Bax/caspase-3), and neuroinflammation (microglial TNF-α/IL-1β). Introduction

Chronic methamphetamine (Meth) abuse is a global public health crisis, leading to severe and often persistent cognitive deficits, with hippocampal-dependent memory and learning being particularly vulnerable (Drugs UNOo 2024, Rogers et al. 2023). The hippocampus is essential for declarative memory and spatial navigation, functions that rely fundamentally on synaptic plasticity—the activity-dependent modification of synaptic strength (Tartt et al. 2022). Long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) are the principal forms of synaptic plasticity, serving as key cellular substrates for information encoding and storage (Hagena and Manahan-Vaughan 2024). It is crucial to note that synaptic plasticity encompasses both presynaptic and postsynaptic mechanisms; however, this review will focus primarily on the postsynaptic machinery that is centrally disrupted by chronic Meth exposure.

The induction of N-Methyl-D-Aspartate Receptors (NMDAR)-dependent LTP at synapses such as the CA3-CA1 Schaffer collateral pathway requires coincident presynaptic glutamate release and postsynaptic depolarization to relieve the Mg²⁺ block from NMDARs, permitting Ca²⁺ influx (Lau et al. 2009). This calcium signal activates kinases like CaMKII and initiates downstream cascades that lead to AMPAR trafficking and structural reinforcement of dendritic spines (Giese 2021). Neurotrophins, particularly brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), are critical modulators of this process. BDNF is released in an activity- and calcium-dependent manner from both pre- and postsynaptic sites and signals through its receptor, tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), to enhance glutamate release, promote receptor trafficking, stimulate dendritic protein synthesis, and support spine maturation (Li et al. 2010, Peregud et al. 2023). Disruption of this finely tuned molecular machinery compromises hippocampal function. Importantly, impairments in synaptic plasticity are strongly implicated in the cognitive deficits observed in neuropsychiatric disorders, including addiction (Castillo et al. 2023, Kohno et al. 2022).

Notably, the molecular hallmarks of synaptic dysfunction and cognitive impairment—including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, apoptotic signaling, and neurotrophic deficit—are not unique to Meth neurotoxicity. Converging evidence from studies on other neurotoxic substances reveals shared pathogenic pathways. For instance, chronic ethanol exposure impairs memory and induces depressive-like behaviors through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, disrupted neurotrophic support (particularly BDNF), and neurotransmitter system dysregulation (Hedayati-Moghadam et al. 2024, Mashayekhi-Sardoo et al. 2025). Similarly, toxic cannabinoid exposure can trigger hippocampal apoptosis via calcium dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the induction of ER stress (Mashayekhi-Sardoo et al. 2025). This congruence across different substance classes underscores the fundamental vulnerability of hippocampal plasticity to insults that converge on common cellular stress and death pathways. It further highlights the importance of elucidating these universal mechanisms to develop broad neuroprotective strategies.

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on how chronic Meth exposure disrupts hippocampal synaptic plasticity. We posit that Meth initiates a pathogenic cascade beginning with dopamine dysregulation, which drives oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and epigenetic alterations. These processes converge to impair glutamatergic signaling, suppress neurotrophic support, and activate apoptotic pathways, ultimately leading to synaptic loss and deficits in LTP. By integrating findings from preclinical and clinical studies, this review aims to clarify the molecular underpinnings of Meth-induced cognitive decline and highlight potential therapeutic targets.

Hippocampal Synaptic Plasticity: Core Molecular Players

The hippocampus is a critical brain region for learning and memory, processes fundamentally reliant on synaptic plasticity—the activity-dependent strengthening or weakening of synaptic connections. The primary cellular substrates of information storage are LTP and LTD. LTP is defined as a persistent, activity-dependent increase in synaptic efficacy, which can be induced by various patterns of stimulation, not exclusively high-frequency trains (Bliss and Collingridge 1993). Conversely, LTD is a persistent decrease in synaptic strength. Both forms of plasticity involve intricate molecular machinery that chronic Meth exposure profoundly disrupts. This section provides a focused overview of the core postsynaptic components central to hippocampal plasticity, setting the stage for understanding Meth’s pathogenic effects.

Glutamate Receptors and Calcium Signaling

Ionotropic glutamate receptors, the NMDAR and AMPA receptor (AMPAR), are pivotal. At many hippocampal synapses, such as the canonical Schaffer collateral CA3-CA1 connection, the NMDAR acts as a coincidence detector due to its voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block. Coincident presynaptic glutamate release and postsynaptic depolarization relieve this block, allowing Ca²⁺ influx, which initiates downstream signaling (Nicoll 2017). The subunit composition (e.g., GluN2A vs. GluN2B) influences receptor kinetics and signaling output (Paoletti et al. 2013). It is important to note that not all hippocampal LTP is NMDAR-dependent; for instance, mossy fiber-CA3 synapses exhibit a distinct, primarily non-NMDAR-dependent form of LTP (Nicoll and Schmitz 2005). Following induction, a key expression mechanism is the activity-dependent trafficking of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) into (LTP) or out of (LTD) the postsynaptic membrane, directly altering synaptic strength (Henley and Wilkinson 2016).

The spatiotemporal profile of the NMDAR-mediated Ca²⁺ signal is decoded by key enzymes. Large, rapid Ca²⁺ transients often activate calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII). Autophosphorylation of CaMKII at Thr286 confers calcium-independent activity, enabling it to phosphorylate substrates like AMPARs to enhance synaptic transmission and stabilize potentiation (Lisman et al. 2012). Different patterns of Ca²⁺ elevation, such as prolonged low-level influx, can preferentially activate phosphatases like calcineurin, promoting AMPAR endocytosis and LTD (Mulkey et al. 1994). The extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway integrates signals from NMDARs and neurotrophin receptors to regulate transcription in the nucleus, essential for the protein synthesis-dependent phase of LTP (Davis et al. 2000).

Scaffolding Proteins and Structural Dynamics

The postsynaptic density (PSD) is organized by scaffolding proteins. PSD-95 is a master scaffold that clusters NMDARs and stabilizes AMPARs via auxiliary proteins, forming a signaling platform (Sheng and Kim 2011). Activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein (ARC/Arg3.1), an immediate early gene product, plays complex roles in homeostatic plasticity and AMPAR trafficking (Shepherd and Bear 2011). Underlying these molecular changes, the actin cytoskeleton drives the structural remodeling of dendritic spines. LTP induction often triggers Rho GTPase (Rac1, Cdc42)-mediated actin polymerization, leading to spine head enlargement and stabilization. Conversely, pathways involving RhoA can promote spine shrinkage or retraction (Murakoshi and Yasuda 2012).

Neurotrophic Signaling

BDNF and its receptor TrkB are potent modulators of synaptic plasticity. Activity-dependent BDNF synthesis occurs in cell bodies and dendrites, and its release can be from both pre- and postsynaptic sites in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner (Kuczewski et al. 2009). BDNF-TrkB signaling activates three major pathways: PLCγ, PI3K/Akt, and Ras/ERK. These pathways enhance presynaptic glutamate release, promote postsynaptic receptor trafficking and local protein synthesis via Mammalian Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (mTORC1) and drive transcriptional programs essential for long-term synaptic strengthening and spine growth (Andero et al. 2014). The critical role of BDNF is highlighted by studies showing that loss of TrkB signaling impairs LTP and memory formation (Minichiello et al. 1999).

In summary, hippocampal synaptic plasticity requires the precise coordination of glutamate receptor activation, Ca²⁺-dependent enzyme activity, scaffolding protein dynamics, actin remodeling, and neurotrophic support. Chronic Meth exposure disrupts each of these core processes, as detailed in the following sections. A schematic diagram in Sect. "Molecular Mechanisms of Synaptic Impairment" will illustrate how Meth perturbs this integrated machinery. Methamphetamine Pharmacology: Key Aspects and Relevance to Hippocampal Neurotoxicity

Meth exerts its primary psychoactive and neurotoxic effects through profound perturbations of monoaminergic neurotransmission, with a central role in disrupting hippocampal synaptic plasticity. The drug’s fundamental mechanism involves its interaction with presynaptic monoamine transporters. Meth is a substrate for the dopamine transporter (DAT) and, to a lesser extent, the serotonin transporter (SERT). Unlike simple reuptake inhibitors, Meth is actively transported into the presynaptic terminal, where it disrupts vesicular storage by interacting with the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), leading to depletion of vesicular dopamine (DA) and serotonin (5-HT) pools and increased cytosolic concentrations of these monoamines (Nickell et al. 2014, Čechová and Šlamberová 2021). Critically, Meth reverses the normal direction of transport at DAT, acting as a potent releaser that causes a massive, non-exocytotic efflux of DA into the synaptic cleft (Bhimani et al. 2021). This results in an acute, supra-physiological surge in extracellular DA levels, leading to prolonged and intense stimulation of postsynaptic DA receptors (D1-like and D2-like families) in key brain regions, including the hippocampus which receives dopaminergic projections from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) (Kohno et al. 2022, Naghavi et al. 2019).

The acute DA surge is the initiating event for a cascade of molecular processes that underpin Meth’s neurotoxicity. The excessive extracellular DA is prone to auto-oxidation and metabolism by monoamine oxidase (MAO), generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive quinones, which initiate a state of profound oxidative stress (Kargar and Noshiri 2024, Bazylianska et al. 2021). This oxidative assault damages cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and directly impairs the function of synaptic proteins essential for plasticity (Limanaqi et al. 2018). Concurrently, Meth exposure disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics, inhibiting electron transport chain complexes (particularly Complex I) and promoting mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, which exacerbates ROS production and can trigger apoptotic pathways (Zhou et al. 2025).

Chronic Meth exposure leads to persistent neuroadaptations that exacerbate synaptic dysfunction. Repeated administration results in DAT downregulation and impaired DA reuptake capacity, creating a state of persistent extracellular DA elevation alongside presynaptic DA depletion due to terminal damage and reduced tyrosine hydroxylase expression (Pilski and Graves 2023). This contributes to a pathological imbalance in D1 and D2 receptor signaling. Sustained D1 receptor overactivation drives aberrant cAMP/PKA/cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) signaling, while suppressed D2 receptor signaling impairs the neuroprotective PI3K/Akt/glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) pathway, collectively disrupting the transcriptional regulation and synaptic stability required for hippocampal plasticity [15, 16]. Furthermore, the hyperdopaminergic state fosters deleterious cross-talk with the glutamatergic system. Chronic D1 receptor stimulation increases intracellular calcium, leading to calcineurin activation, dephosphorylation, and subsequent internalization of NMDARs, resulting in NMDAR hypofunction—a direct mechanism for impairing LTP in the hippocampus (Lau et al. 2009).

The pharmacological actions of Meth also potently activate neuroimmune responses. The initial neuronal stress and damage, along with danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from compromised cells, activate microglia primarily via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling (Frank et al. 2016). This triggers nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB)-mediated transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) (Yang et al. 2020). These cytokines, along with increased cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and prostaglandin E (PGE) production, directly impair synaptic function by promoting AMPA receptor internalization, inhibiting LTP, and inducing dendritic spine loss through RhoA/ROCK pathway activation (Xue et al. 2019). Astrocytes become reactive, further amplifying inflammation and contributing to synaptic glutamate dysregulation and excitotoxicity (Canedo et al. 2021).

In summary, the pharmacology of Meth, centered on explosive monoamine release and reuptake inhibition, initiates a multi-faceted pathogenic cascade. The acute DA surge precipitates oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, while chronic adaptations lead to persistent dopaminergic and glutamatergic dysregulation. These processes are amplified by sustained neuroinflammation. Together, these mechanisms—originating from Meth’s fundamental interaction with monoamine systems—converge on the hippocampus to disrupt the molecular machinery of synaptic plasticity, providing the pharmacological foundation for the cognitive deficits observed in Meth use disorder.

Molecular Mechanisms of Synaptic Impairment

Dopaminergic Signaling Disruption

Chronic Meth exposure fundamentally rewires dopaminergic signaling in the hippocampus, establishing a pathological cascade that directly undermines synaptic plasticity mechanisms essential for learning and memory. While the initiating events of oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and neuroinflammation create a hostile microenvironment, the specific dysregulation of dopamine (DA) neurotransmission acts as a primary molecular driver, disrupting the delicate balance of intracellular signaling pathways that govern synaptic strength, structure, and functional integration within hippocampal circuits. The core of this disruption stems from Meth-induced perturbations at the DAT, leading to a persistent elevation in extracellular DA levels and a consequential imbalance in the activation of D1-like (D1, D5) and D2-like (D2, D3, D4) receptor families, each coupled to distinct and often opposing downstream effectors. This imbalance initiates divergent signaling cascades that converge on synaptic dysfunction, impairing both the expression of LTP and the structural integrity of dendritic spines.

A cornerstone of Meth’s chronic neurotoxicity is its profound impact on DAT function. As detailed in Sect. "Methamphetamine Pharmacology: Key Aspects and Relevance to Hippocampal Neurotoxicity", repeated Meth exposure leads to a significant reduction in DAT surface expression and diminished reuptake capacity. This DAT dysfunction is not merely transient but persists during abstinence, creating a state of chronically elevated basal extracellular DA concentrations in key hippocampal regions like the dentate gyrus (DG) and CA1, which receive substantial dopaminergic innervation from the VTA via the mesolimbic pathway (Shepard et al. 2006, Bennett et al. 1998). The inability to efficiently clear DA from the synaptic cleft results in prolonged, albeit dysregulatedm, receptor stimulation. Crucially, chronic Meth exposure also disrupts the normal phasic (burst firing-triggered) release of DA, essential for encoding salient information, while promoting aberrant tonic DA levels that saturate receptors and blunt synaptic signaling dynamics (Miller et al. 2021, Gibson et al. 2022). This pathological elevation of extracellular DA creates a hyperdopaminergic milieu, but paradoxically, it often coexists with presynaptic DA depletion due to Meth-induced damage to DA terminals and synthesis machinery, including downregulation of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) (Shepard et al. 2006, Vecchio et al. 2021). The imbalance manifests as a pathological tilt towards D1-like receptor overactivation relative to D2-like receptor signaling. Chronic hyperstimulation of D1-like receptors (D1Rs), which are predominantly expressed postsynaptically on hippocampal pyramidal neurons and interneurons, drives sustained activation of the stimulatory G protein Gαs/olf (Worsley et al. 2000). This constitutively activates adenylyl cyclase (AC), leading to a persistent elevation in intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels. cAMP acts as a critical second messenger, binding to and activating protein kinase A (PKA) (Jones-Tabah et al. 2022, Nguyen et al. 2025).

One primary target of dysregulated PKA is the transcription factor CREB. While CREB phosphorylation (pCREB) is a well-established mediator of activity-dependent gene expression underlying LTP and memory consolidation, chronic Meth exposure induces excessive and dysregulated pCREB. This aberrant phosphorylation pattern disrupts the precise temporal and spatial control of CREB-dependent transcription. Instead of promoting the coordinated expression of plasticity-related genes (PRGs) like ARC, c-Fos, and BDNF, chronic D1R-driven pCREB leads to the maladaptive expression of genes involved in excitotoxicity, pro-apoptotic signaling, and even drug-seeking behavior, while simultaneously suppressing the expression of crucial neuroprotective and synaptic maintenance genes (Rivera-Maya et al. 2025, Cisneros et al. 2020, D’Amico 2013). This transcriptional dysregulation fundamentally alters the molecular landscape of hippocampal neurons, impairing their ability to mount appropriate synaptic adaptations in response to physiological stimuli and directly contributing to deficits in LTP expression and the stabilization of new memor ies.

Concurrently, chronic Meth exposure suppresses D2-like receptor (D2R) signaling (Moeller et al. 2018, Volkow et al. 2003). D2Rs, expressed both presynaptically (as autoreceptors regulating DA release) and postsynaptically, are coupled to the inhibitory G protein Gαi/o. Activation of Gαi/o inhibits AC, reducing cAMP production and opposing D1R signaling. Furthermore, postsynaptic D2R activation engages crucial neuroprotective and synaptic stabilizing pathways, primarily through β-arrestin-mediated signaling cascades independent of G proteins. A key pathway involves the activation of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), which phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol (Hagena and Manahan-Vaughan 2024, Lau et al. 2009)-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate phosphatidylinositol (Tartt et al. 2022, Hagena and Manahan-Vaughan 2024, Lau et al. 2009)-trisphosphate (PIP3). PIP3 serves as a docking site for protein kinase B (Akt), leading to its phosphorylation and activation. Activated Akt exerts profound stabilizing effects on synapses by phosphorylating and inhibiting GSK-3β. Under basal conditions, GSK-3β is active and acts as a negative regulator of synaptic strength and structure: it phosphorylates key synaptic scaffolding proteins like PSD-95 and β-catenin, targeting them for degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system, and inhibits microtubule dynamics and spine stability by phosphorylating MAP1B and CRMP-2. Akt-mediated phosphorylation of GSK-3β (at Ser9) potently inhibits its kinase activity (Matsuda et al. 2019, Yan et al. 2023). However, chronic Meth-induced suppression of D2R signaling leads to diminished Akt activation, resulting in disinhibition of GSK-3β. Hyperactive GSK-3β then phosphorylates its synaptic substrates, destabilizing the PSD, impairing spine morphogenesis and maintenance (reducing spine density and promoting immature filopodia-like spines), and increasing the susceptibility of synapses to collapse. This loss of synaptic stability, driven by impaired D2R/Akt/GSK-3β signaling, represents a critical structural correlate to the functional plasticity deficits observed after chronic Meth exposure (Moeller et al. 2018, Volkow et al. 2003, Matsuda et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2012).

The dysregulation of dopaminergic signaling does not occur in isolation; it critically modulates hippocampal glutamatergic transmission, forming a deleterious cross-talk that directly impairs NMDAR function and LTP. The hippocampus relies heavily on NMDAR-dependent signaling for associative plasticity. Chronic elevation of extracellular DA, primarily acting through D1Rs, profoundly impacts NMDAR trafficking and function (Pei et al. 2004). Sustained D1R activation significantly increases intracellular Ca²⁺ levels via multiple mechanisms: (1) Direct potentiation of L-type voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs); (2) PKA-mediated phosphorylation of NMDAR subunits (e.g., GluN2B), which can initially potentiate currents but under chronic conditions contributes to receptor desensitization and internalization; and (3) PKA-mediated inhibition of potassium channels, leading to membrane depolarization and relief of the Mg²⁺ block on NMDARs, facilitating Ca²⁺ influx. While acute D1R activation can facilitate NMDAR currents and LTP induction, chronic elevation of intracellular Ca²⁺ triggers feedback mechanisms designed to prevent excitotoxicity (Belgacem and Borodinsky 2017, Gnegy 2000, Griego and Galván 2023). One major mechanism is the activation of calcium/calmodulin-dependent phosphatase calcineurin (PP2B). Sustained high Ca²⁺ levels lead to robust calcineurin activation. Activated calcineurin dephosphorylates critical serine residues on the GluN1 and GluN2B subunits of NMDARs, which are necessary for anchoring the receptors to the PSD via scaffolding proteins like PSD-95. Dephosphorylation promotes the dissociation of NMDARs from the PSD scaffold and facilitates their endocytosis via clathrin-coated pits, effectively reducing the surface expression and synaptic localization of functional NMDAR complexes (Chen and Roche 2007, Lau and Zukin 2007, Zhou et al. 2021, Reyes-García and Escobar 2021, Ehrlich et al. 2007).

Thus, the DA-glutamate cross-talk, initiated by chronic D1R overactivation and culminating in NMDAR hypofunction, represents a central mechanism by which dopaminergic dysregulation disrupts the fundamental cellular process underlying hippocampal memory formation (Wang et al. 2017, Elhadi et al. 2023).

In summary, chronic Meth exposure orchestrates a multifaceted disruption of dopaminergic signaling within the hippocampus, acting as a linchpin connecting the initiating neurotoxic events to specific synaptic failure. The core DAT dysfunction leads to chronically elevated extracellular DA, driving a pathological imbalance favoring D1-like over D2-like receptor signaling. This imbalance bifurcates into two major deleterious pathways: (Drugs UNOo 2024) Sustained D1R overactivation drives aberrant cAMP/PKA signaling, resulting in dysregulated CREB phosphorylation and maladaptive gene expression that undermines synaptic plasticity mechanisms; (Rogers et al. 2023) Concurrent D2R suppression impairs the Akt/GSK-3β pathway, disinhibiting GSK-3β and destabilizing synaptic structures crucial for maintaining plasticity. Critically, this dopaminergic dysregulation directly impinges on glutamatergic transmission via D1R-driven calcineurin activation, promoting NMDAR internalization and hypofunction, which directly ablates the capacity for NMDAR-dependent LTP. Together, these converging mechanisms – transcriptional dysregulation, synaptic destabilization, and NMDAR hypofunction – constitute the primary molecular underpinnings of the hippocampal synaptic plasticity deficits that characterize chronic Meth abuse and underlie the pervasive cognitive impairments experienced by users. This disruption of dopaminergic signaling provides a compelling target for therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring synaptic function and cognitive capacity. Apoptotic Neuronal Death

Chronic Meth exposure triggers a cascade of apoptotic neuronal death in the hippocampus, representing a critical pathological endpoint that irreversibly compromises the structural foundation of synaptic plasticity. While the initiating events of oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage create the preconditions for cellular demise, the activation of programmed cell death pathways executes the irreversible loss of hippocampal neurons, particularly within the CA1 and CA3 subfields essential for memory consolidation and spatial processing (Mahmoudiasl et al. 2019, Guo et al. 2022, Liang et al. 2022). This apoptotic process is not merely a passive consequence of cellular damage but an actively regulated molecular program initiated by Meth-induced redox imbalance, converging on mitochondrial permeabilization and caspase activation. Beyond causing neuronal loss, apoptotic mechanisms actively dismantle synaptic structures through precise proteolytic events, creating a dual assault on hippocampal integrity where both neuronal somata and their synaptic connections are targeted, fundamentally undermining the substrate for plasticity (Guo et al. 2022, Tian et al. 2024, Wu et al. 2024). Notably, this cascade of mitochondrial apoptosis is not unique to Meth exposure, representing a convergent mechanism of synaptic damage shared with other neurotoxic insults, including obesity-related metabolic dysfunction, toxic cannabinoid exposure, and chronic ethanol consumption (Golkar et al. 2025, Mashayekhi-Sardoo et al. 2025, Mashayekhi-Sardoo et al. 2025).

The apoptotic cascade is predominantly initiated through the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, directly stemming from Meth-induced oxidative stress. As detailed in Sect. "Methamphetamine Pharmacology: Key Aspects and Relevance to Hippocampal Neurotoxicity", the supraphysiological dopamine (DA) surge generates overwhelming levels of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), including superoxide (O₂⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻). These reactive species inflict severe damage on mitochondrial components. Lipid peroxidation targets the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), impairing electron transport chain (ETC) function, particularly at Complexes I and III, leading to further ROS amplification in a vicious cycle (Kaur and Aran 2025). Crucially, ROS directly oxidize critical components of the mPTP, such as cyclophilin D and adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT), sensitizing it to opening (Coluccino et al. 2023). Concurrently, Meth-induced dysregulation of intracellular calcium (Ca²⁺) homeostasis, often secondary to glutamate excitotoxicity or direct effects on membrane channels, results in excessive mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uptake via the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) (Guo et al. 2022, Wu et al. 2024, Yang et al. 2025). The synergistic impact of ROS and Ca²⁺ overload triggers persistent mPTP opening, collapsing the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), uncoupling oxidative phosphorylation, and causing mitochondrial swelling. This permeabilization of the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) allows the efflux of pro-apoptotic factors normally sequestered in the intermembrane space, most notably cytochrome c. Released cytochrome c binds to Apaf-1 (apoptotic protease-activating factor 1) in the cytosol, forming a multimeric complex known as the apoptosome. The apoptosome recruits and activates procaspase-9, triggering its proteolytic cleavage into active caspase-9. Caspase-9, as an initiator caspase, then proteolytically activates the executioner caspase-3 and caspase-7, setting in motion the irreversible demolition phase of apoptosis that dismantles cellular structures, including those essential for synaptic function (Tian et al. 2024).

The commitment to mitochondrial apoptosis is critically regulated by the Bcl-2 protein family, whose equilibrium is profoundly disrupted by chronic Meth exposure. This family consists of pro-apoptotic effectors (e.g., Bax, Bak), pro-apoptotic sensitizers/activators (e.g., Bim, Bid, Puma), and anti-apoptotic guardians (e.g., Bcl-2, Bcl-xL) (Guo et al. 2022, Tian et al. 2024). Meth-induced oxidative stress and DNA damage activate the tumor suppressor protein p53, a key transcriptional regulator of apoptosis. Phosphorylation and stabilization of p53 occur via redox-sensitive kinases like ATM (ataxia telangiectasia mutated) and ATR (ATM and Rad3-related), which are activated by DNA double-strand breaks induced by ROS and DA quinones. Activated p53 translocates to the nucleus and transcriptionally upregulates pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members, particularly Bax and Puma. Bax, normally a cytosolic monomer, undergoes conformational changes induced by stress signals and translocates to the OMM. At the OMM, Bax oligomerizes, forming pores that directly permeabilize the membrane or potentiate mPTP opening, facilitating cytochrome c release (Guo et al. 2022, Tian et al. 2024, Lee 2024, Xu et al. 2023). Concurrently, Meth exposure downregulates anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL expression through mechanisms involving epigenetic modifications (discussed below) and direct oxidative inactivation of these proteins. The resulting imbalance—increased Bax/Bak activity coupled with diminished Bcl-2/Bcl-xL protection—ensures efficient mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), committing the neuron to apoptosis. This p53/Bax axis is particularly detrimental in the hippocampus, where post-mortem studies of chronic Meth users and animal models reveal significant neuronal loss specifically in the CA1 and CA3 pyramidal layers, regions vital for associative memory and pattern completion. This neuronal attrition represents an irreversible structural deficit that directly contributes to the persistent cognitive impairments observed in addiction (Elhadi et al. 2023, Mahmoudiasl et al. 2019, Ding et al. 2022, Jayanthi et al. 2021, Qi et al. 2025).

Epigenetic dysregulation serves as a powerful amplifier of the apoptotic cascade in Meth neurotoxicity, primarily through the upregulation of histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2). Chronic Meth exposure, via oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, induces a sustained increase in HDAC2 expression and activity in hippocampal neurons. HDAC2 functions by removing acetyl groups from lysine residues on histone tails, particularly histone H3 and H4, leading to chromatin condensation and transcriptional repression (Ding et al. 2022). Meth-induced HDAC2 hyperactivation targets the promoters of crucial anti-apoptotic and neuroprotective genes. Key among these is Bcl-2, whose promoter becomes hypoacetylated, silencing its transcription and diminishing the cellular capacity to counteract Bax oligomerization and MOMP. Equally significant is the HDAC2-mediated repression of BDNF (Cheng et al. 2023). BDNF is not only a potent survival factor that activates anti-apoptotic PI3K/Akt signaling through TrkB receptors but is also essential for synaptic plasticity and maintenance. Suppression of BDNF expression thus creates a double jeopardy: it removes a critical brake on apoptosis while simultaneously starving synapses of essential trophic support, further sensitizing neurons to degeneration and weakening synaptic resilience (Peregud et al. 2023, Qi et al. 2025, Toader et al. 2025). This epigenetic silencing, orchestrated by HDAC2 upregulation, represents a feed-forward mechanism that consolidates the pro-apoptotic state initiated by oxidative stress and p53 activation, ensuring the progression of neuronal death even if the initial oxidative insult subsides (Dzreyan and Demyanenko 2023).

A pivotal insight into Meth-induced synaptic failure is that apoptosis actively dismantles synapses long before the neuron undergoes complete somal demise, through caspase-mediated cleavage of key synaptic scaffolding proteins (Rashidi et al. 2024). Activated executioner caspases, particularly caspase-3, exhibit specific proteolytic activity beyond nuclear and cytoskeletal substrates; they directly target proteins essential for PSD integrity and function (Fricker et al. 2018, Faust et al. 2021). Two critical substrates are PSD-95 and ARC/Arg3.1. PSD-95, a major scaffolding protein in the PSD, organizes glutamate receptors (NMDARs, AMPARs), signaling molecules, and downstream effectors, acting as a central hub for synaptic strength and plasticity. Caspase-3 cleaves PSD-95 at specific sites (e.g., DETD²), generating fragments that lose their ability to cluster receptors and scaffold signaling complexes (Liu et al. 2010, Johnson et al. 2022). Similarly, ARC, a protein rapidly induced by synaptic activity and crucial for AMPAR trafficking and actin cytoskeleton remodeling underlying spine plasticity, is also cleaved by caspase-3. Cleavage of ARC disrupts its interaction with endophilin and dynamin, impairing activity-dependent AMPAR endocytosis and destabilizing the actin cytoskeleton within dendritic spines. The caspase-3-mediated proteolysis of PSD-95 and ARC leads to the rapid disassembly of the postsynaptic density, manifesting as a collapse of the PSD ultrastructure observable via electron microscopy (Ding et al. 2022, Leung et al. 2022). This “synaptosis” – the apoptotic dismantling of synapses – occurs early in the apoptotic cascade, often while the neuron is still metabolically active and before overt nuclear fragmentation. Consequently, chronic Meth exposure results in a significant loss of dendritic spines, particularly the large, stable mushroom spines that harbor mature synapses capable of expressing LTP, even in neurons that may ultimately survive but are functionally crippled by synaptic loss (Ding et al. 2022, Lau et al. 2009, Yuste 2023). This mechanism directly links the activation of the apoptotic machinery to the immediate disruption of synaptic plasticity, providing a molecular explanation for why cognitive deficits can precede or occur independently of gross neuronal loss in the hippocampus.

In summary, chronic Meth exposure induces apoptotic neuronal death in the hippocampus through a well-orchestrated molecular cascade originating from oxidative stress and converging on mitochondrial permeabilization and caspase activation. The p53/Bax axis, amplified by oxidative DNA damage, drives mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release, activating caspase-9 and the executioner caspase-3. This process is significantly potentiated by HDAC2 upregulation, which epigenetically silences anti-apoptotic genes like Bcl-2 and BDNF, removing critical survival safeguards. Crucially, caspase-3 activation executes not only the demise of the neuron itself but also actively dismantles synaptic structures by cleaving key scaffolding proteins PSD-95 and ARC, leading to postsynaptic density collapse and dendritic spine loss. This dual attack – irreversible neuronal loss in CA1/CA3 subfields and the caspase-mediated degradation of synaptic integrity – represents a fundamental mechanism by which chronic Meth exposure disrupts hippocampal synaptic plasticity. The loss of neurons and synapses eliminates the cellular substrate for plasticity, while the disassembly of PSD complexes directly ablates the molecular machinery required for LTP/LTD. This apoptotic pathway, therefore, underpins the severe and often persistent cognitive deficits associated with chronic Meth abuse, highlighting the critical need for therapeutic strategies targeting both neuronal survival and synaptic preservation. Glutamatergic System Dysregulation

Chronic Meth exposure profoundly disrupts glutamatergic neurotransmission within the hippocampus, critically impairing the primary excitatory signaling pathway essential for synaptic plasticity and information encoding. While dopaminergic dysregulation initiates the pathological cascade, the consequent perturbation of glutamate receptor trafficking, function, and downstream signaling represents a direct mechanism by which chronic Meth undermines LTP, LTD, and the structural remodeling of dendritic spines. This dysregulation manifests prominently through two interconnected processes: the internalization and functional impairment of NMDARs, driven by dopamine (DA)-mediated calcium overload and calcineurin activation, and the reduction in surface expression of AMPARs, which impedes spine maturation and stabilization via aberrant RhoA/ROCK signaling. Together, these alterations cripple the fundamental cellular processes underlying hippocampal learning and memory (Ding et al. 2022, Chojnacki et al. 2020, Rich et al. 2020).

The dysregulation of NMDARs, central to associative plasticity, is a direct consequence of the pathological DA excess induced by chronic Meth exposure. As established in Sect. "Dopaminergic Signaling Disruption", chronic Meth leads to persistently elevated extracellular DA levels due to DAT dysfunction and impaired clearance. This hyperdopaminergic state, acting predominantly through overstimulation of D1-like receptors (D1Rs) on hippocampal pyramidal neurons and interneurons, triggers a cascade leading to NMDAR hypofunction (Lau et al. 2009, Kohno et al. 2022, Armada-Moreira et al. 2020).

Chronic Meth exposure results in a significant reduction in the surface expression and synaptic localization of functional NMDAR complexes, a state termed NMDAR hypofunction. Given that NMDAR activation and the resultant Ca²⁺ influx are the indispensable triggers for the induction of NMDAR-dependent LTP in the hippocampus, this hypofunction directly translates into a profound impairment in the ability to establish new, long-lasting synaptic potentiation, a cellular correlate of learning (Ding et al. 2022, Braren et al. 2014). Animal studies consistently demonstrate attenuated or abolished LTP in hippocampal slices from chronic Meth-exposed rodents, correlating strongly with deficits in spatial and contextual memory tasks (Wang et al. 2017, Elhadi et al. 2023, Shahidi et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2021).

Concurrent with NMDAR hypofunction, chronic Meth exposure significantly impairs AMPAR-mediated synaptic transmission and plasticity. AMPARs are responsible for the fast excitatory synaptic currents that mediate the majority of basal neurotransmission. Their dynamic trafficking to and from the synaptic membrane, particularly during LTP and LTD, is fundamental for adjusting synaptic strength. Chronic Meth exposure leads to a pronounced decrease in the surface expression of GluA1 and GluA2 AMPAR subunits in the hippocampus (Memos et al. 2023). This reduction stems from multiple synergistic mechanisms. Firstly, the NMDAR hypofunction described above directly limits the Ca²⁺ influx necessary for activity-dependent delivery of AMPARs to the synapse during LTP (Andres et al. 2015, Yu et al. 2016). Secondly, sustained D1R activation can promote AMPAR endocytosis through PKA-dependent pathways, although the precise mechanisms are complex and context-dependent. Thirdly, Meth-induced oxidative stress and neuroinflammation can directly impair AMPAR subunit synthesis and trafficking (Limanaqi et al. 2018). Crucially, the reduction in surface AMPARs is not merely a quantitative loss; it also disrupts the subunit composition and functional properties of remaining receptors, potentially favoring Ca²⁺-permeable GluA2-lacking AMPARs under certain conditions, which can further exacerbate excitotoxic vulnerability (Andres et al. 2015). The deficit in synaptic AMPARs manifests as a reduction in the amplitude of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs), indicating weakened basal synaptic transmission (Funke et al. 2023, Li et al. 2017). Furthermore, the impaired trafficking machinery severely compromises the expression of both LTP (which requires AMPAR insertion) and LTD (which requires AMPAR removal), disrupting the bidirectional plasticity essential for information encoding, storage, and clearance within hippocampal circuits.

The reduction in surface AMPARs induced by chronic Meth exposure has profound consequences for the structural integrity and maturation of dendritic spines, primarily through the dysregulation of the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway (Ding et al. 2022, Elhadi et al. 2023). Dendritic spines, the primary sites of excitatory synaptic input, undergo dynamic morphological changes during plasticity, evolving from thin, filopodia-like protrusions to mature, stable mushroom-shaped spines capable of supporting strong synapses. This maturation process is tightly coupled to AMPAR incorporation and requires precise regulation of the underlying actin cytoskeleton (Yuste 2023, Zaccard et al. 2023). The Rho family GTPases, particularly RhoA, Rac1, and Cdc42, act as master regulators of actin dynamics. RhoA signaling, specifically, promotes actinomyosin contraction and limits spine growth and stability. Under physiological conditions, synaptic activity and AMPAR signaling help maintain a balance, often favoring Rac1/Cdc42 activation (which promotes spine growth) over RhoA activation (Xue et al. 2019, Hwang et al. 2025, Swanger et al. 2015). However, chronic Meth exposure disrupts this balance. The reduction in synaptic AMPARs, particularly GluA2-containing receptors, leads to diminished activation of downstream pathways that normally suppress RhoA or promote its inactivation. Consequently, RhoA activity becomes elevated. Active RhoA (GTP-bound) binds to and activates its primary effector, Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK). Activated ROCK phosphorylates multiple substrates that directly impact spine structure: (1) Phosphorylation of myosin light chain (MLC) enhances actinomyosin contractility, causing spine head retraction and destabilization; (2) Phosphorylation and activation of LIM kinase (LIMK) leads to phosphorylation and inactivation of cofilin, an actin-severing protein essential for actin turnover and spine remodeling. Inactivated cofilin cannot depolymerize actin filaments, leading to a rigid, less dynamic cytoskeleton that hinders the structural plasticity required for spine maturation and stabilization; (3) ROCK can also phosphorylate and regulate components of the CRMP-2 (Collapsin Response Mediator Protein-2) pathway, further inhibiting microtubule entry into spines and spine growth. The net effect of chronic RhoA/ROCK overactivation is the persistence of immature, thin, and unstable dendritic spines and a loss of mature, mushroom-shaped spines capable of supporting stable, strong synapses expressing AMPARs (Memos et al. 2023, Xue et al. 2019, Hwang et al. 2025, Swanger et al. 2015, Qian et al. 2021). This structural deficit is a hallmark of chronic Meth exposure in the hippocampus, particularly in the DG, and provides a direct anatomical correlate to the functional impairments in synaptic plasticity and cognition. The impaired spine maturation creates a vicious cycle, as immature spines are less likely to effectively incorporate and stabilize AMPARs, further perpetuating glutamatergic hypofunction.

In summary, chronic Meth exposure orchestrates a multifaceted assault on hippocampal glutamatergic signaling, critically disrupting synaptic plasticity. The pathological elevation of extracellular dopamine, acting through D1 receptor overstimulation, drives excessive calcium influx and calcineurin activation, culminating in NMDAR internalization and hypofunction. This directly impairs the induction of NMDAR-dependent LTP. Concurrently, chronic Meth exposure reduces surface AMPAR expression through multiple mechanisms, weakening basal synaptic transmission and crippling the expression of both LTP and LTD. Critically, the deficit in synaptic AMPARs dysregulates RhoA/ROCK signaling, promoting actinomyosin contraction, inactivating cofilin, and stabilizing an immature actin cytoskeleton. This prevents the maturation and stabilization of dendritic spines, leading to a predominance of unstable, immature spines incapable of supporting robust synaptic connections. Thus, the dysregulation of the glutamatergic system – characterized by NMDAR hypofunction, AMPAR deficiency, and RhoA/ROCK-driven spine immaturity – constitutes a core molecular mechanism by which chronic Meth exposure disrupts hippocampal synaptic plasticity, directly contributing to the persistent cognitive deficits observed in addiction. Targeting these glutamatergic pathways offers significant therapeutic potential for mitigating Meth-induced cognitive decline. Neurotrophic Signaling Suppression

Chronic Meth exposure severely compromises neurotrophic support in the hippocampus, with BDNF depletion representing a pivotal mechanism that directly links neuronal apoptosis to synaptic failure. BDNF, acting through its high-affinity TrkB, orchestrates a signaling cascade essential for neuronal survival, dendritic arborization, spine maturation, and the protein synthesis-dependent phase of LTP. The suppression of this pathway under chronic Meth exposure stems primarily from apoptosis-driven BDNF depletion, compounded by oxidative and epigenetic mechanisms, ultimately crippling the TrkB/PI3K/Akt axis and impairing the synthesis of critical synaptic proteins (Liu et al. 2021, Shahidi et al. 2019). This creates a self-perpetuating cycle where synaptic weakening further diminishes activity-dependent BDNF release, accelerating hippocampal dysfunction and cognitive decline.

BDNF is abundantly expressed in the hippocampus, particularly in the DG and CA regions, and is fundamental for maintaining synaptic plasticity. Its release is tightly coupled to neuronal activity, especially glutamatergic transmission via NMDAR activation. BDNF binding to TrkB receptors triggers dimerization and autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues within the intracellular domain, creating docking sites for adaptor proteins that initiate two primary signaling pathways: the phospholipase Cγ (PLCγ)/Ca²⁺/protein kinase C (PKC) pathway and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway (Peregud et al. 2023, Qi et al. 2025, Toader et al. 2025, Shahidi et al. 2019, Shen et al. 2025). While both are crucial, the PI3K/Akt axis is particularly vital for cell survival, local protein synthesis at synapses, and the structural consolidation of plasticity (Manning and Toker 2017, Altas et al. 2022, Khlebodarova et al. 2018). Activated TrkB recruits the adaptor protein Shc, which binds to the p85 regulatory subunit of PI3K, leading to the activation of the p110 catalytic subunit (Numakawa and Kajihara 2023). PI3K phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol (Hagena and Manahan-Vaughan 2024, Lau et al. 2009)-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate phosphatidylinositol (Tartt et al. 2022, Hagena and Manahan-Vaughan 2024, Lau et al. 2009)-trisphosphate (PIP3) at the plasma membrane. PIP3 serves as a docking site for Akt, recruiting it to the membrane where it is phosphorylated and activated by phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1) and the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2) (Manning and Toker 2017, Glaviano et al. 2023). Activated Akt phosphorylates numerous downstream targets, including GSK-3β, Bcl-2-associated death promoter (BAD), and the mTORC1, collectively promoting survival, inhibiting apoptosis, and crucially, stimulating protein synthesis necessary for enduring synaptic modifications (Manning and Toker 2017, Khlebodarova et al. 2018, Lai et al. 2023, Battaglioni et al. 2022). Under physiological conditions, this pathway ensures that synaptic activity translates into the production of proteins like PSD-95, ARC, and AMPA receptor (AMPAR) subunits, which are incorporated into synapses to strengthen and stabilize connections during LTP (Lavezzari et al. 2004, Avila et al. 2018).

Chronic Meth exposure catastrophically depletes hippocampal BDNF levels, primarily as a consequence of the apoptotic neuronal death cascade detailed in Sect. "Apoptotic Neuronal Death". Meth-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial apoptosis lead to the widespread loss of neurons in BDNF-rich hippocampal subfields like CA1 and CA3. As neurons die, the primary cellular source of BDNF is eliminated. Furthermore, activated caspase-3, the executioner protease of apoptosis, cleaves proBDNF and potentially mature BDNF, reducing the bioavailable pool of this neurotrophin (Guo et al. 2022, Chen et al. 2016, Yu et al. 2015, Tian et al. 2009, Alenezi et al. 2025). Beyond direct neuronal loss, Meth profoundly suppresses BDNF gene expression in surviving neurons. This suppression is multi-factorial: (1) Epigenetic Silencing: As explored in Sect. "Oxidative Stress and Epigenetic Dysregulation", Meth-induced oxidative stress and neuroinflammation drive DNA hypermethylation of the BDNF promoter regions (particularly exons IV and VI) and upregulate HDACs like HDAC2 and HDAC5 (Ding et al. 2022, Jayanthi et al. 2021, Elhadi et al. 2023, Limanaqi et al. 2018, Iamjan et al. 2021, Torres et al. 2015, Li et al. 2018). Histone deacetylation and DNA methylation create a closed chromatin state, severely repressing BDNF transcription; (2) Aberrant CREB Regulation: Chronic D1 receptor overactivation (Sect. "Dopaminergic Signaling Disruption") leads to dysregulated CREB phosphorylation. While initially inducing BDNF expression, sustained and aberrant pCREB patterns ultimately fail to sustain transcription and may even promote repressor complexes at CRE sites within the BDNF promoter (Palomer et al. 2016, Chowdhury et al. 2024, Rivera-Maya et al. 2025); (3) Glutamatergic Dysfunction: NMDAR hypofunction and AMPAR deficiency (Sect. "Glutamatergic System Dysregulation") directly reduce the synaptic activity necessary for activity-dependent BDNF expression and release. The combined effect is a dramatic and persistent reduction in both BDNF mRNA and protein levels within the hippocampus of chronic Meth users and animal models, correlating strongly with the severity of cognitive deficits (Qi et al. 2025, Avila et al. 2018, Galinato et al. 2015, Qi et al. 2024, Bae et al. 2025).

The depletion of BDNF directly impairs TrkB signaling, with profound consequences for the PI3K/Akt pathway. Reduced BDNF availability means fewer TrkB receptors are occupied and activated. Consequently, the recruitment and activation of PI3K at the membrane is diminished, leading to reduced generation of PIP3. Akt, therefore, fails to be efficiently recruited to the membrane and phosphorylated by PDK1 and mTORC2, resulting in lower levels of active phospho-Akt (pAkt) (Matsuda et al. 2019, Glaviano et al. 2023). This suppression of Akt activity has devastating downstream consequences: (1) Loss of Anti-Apoptotic Signaling: Akt normally phosphorylates and inactivates pro-apoptotic proteins like BAD and FOXO transcription factors, promoting cell survival. Reduced Akt activity leaves neurons vulnerable to ongoing oxidative and inflammatory insults, perpetuating the cycle of degeneration (Nitulescu et al. 2018, Shiau et al. 2022); (2) Disinhibition of GSK-3β: Akt phosphorylates GSK-3β at Ser9, inhibiting its kinase activity. When Akt activity is low, GSK-3β becomes hyperactive. As detailed in Sect. "Dopaminergic Signaling Disruption", hyperactive GSK-3β phosphorylates key synaptic scaffolding proteins (e.g., β-catenin, PSD-95) and cytoskeletal regulators (e.g., CRMP-2, MAP1B), targeting them for degradation and destabilizing dendritic spines, thereby directly impairing synaptic stability independent of BDNF’s trophic effects (Lai et al. 2023); (3) Suppression of mTORC1 Signaling: Akt phosphorylates and inhibits the tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2), a negative regulator of the small GTPase Rheb. Rheb-GTP then potently activates mTORC1 (Manning and Toker 2017, Saxton and Sabatini 2017). Diminished Akt signaling thus fails to relieve TSC2-mediated inhibition of mTORC1. mTORC1 is a master regulator of cap-dependent protein synthesis, phosphorylating key effectors like the eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E)-binding proteins (4E-BPs) and the ribosomal protein S6 kinases (S6Ks). Phosphorylation of 4E-BPs releases eIF4E, allowing it to bind eIF4G and form the eIF4F complex essential for initiating translation of mRNAs containing a 5’ cap structure. Phosphorylation of S6K1 promotes ribosome biogenesis and translation elongation. Inhibition of mTORC1 signaling due to impaired Akt activation leads to hypophosphorylation of 4E-BPs and S6K1. Consequently, eIF4E remains sequestered, the eIF4F complex formation is impaired, and overall translational capacity is reduced. This directly suppresses the de novo synthesis of synaptic proteins critical for plasticity (Shafiee et al. 2023, Kommaddi et al. 2024, Querfurth and Lee 2021, Yong et al. 2025, Gong et al. 2015).

The suppression of mTORC1-dependent protein synthesis is arguably the most direct mechanism by which BDNF depletion and TrkB/PI3K/Akt impairment disrupt synaptic plasticity. LTP, particularly its late phase (L-LTP), requires rapid local translation of pre-existing dendritic mRNAs into functional synaptic proteins. Key mRNAs localized to dendrites include those encoding PSD-95, ARC/Arg3.1, GluA1 AMPAR subunits, and Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II alpha (CaMKIIα). Their translation is tightly regulated by synaptic activity and neurotrophic signals, primarily via the BDNF-TrkB-mTORC1 axis (Afonso 2015, Monday et al. 2022).

In summary, chronic Meth exposure critically suppresses neurotrophic signaling, primarily through apoptosis-driven BDNF depletion amplified by epigenetic and transcriptional repression. This depletion severely impairs TrkB receptor activation and the downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. Reduced Akt activity not only heightens neuronal vulnerability to degeneration but also disinhibits GSK-3β (destabilizing synapses) and, crucially, fails to adequately activate mTORC1. The resulting suppression of mTORC1-dependent protein synthesis prevents the local translation of essential synaptic proteins like PSD-95, ARC, and AMPAR subunits. Consequently, the structural consolidation and stabilization of synaptic potentiation during L-LTP are crippled, and dendritic spines remain immature and unstable. This neurotrophic failure represents a fundamental mechanism by which chronic Meth disrupts hippocampal synaptic plasticity, acting downstream of apoptosis and dopaminergic/glutamatergic dysregulation to directly impair the molecular machinery required for enduring synaptic change and memory formation. Restoring BDNF signaling or bypassing its suppression via mTORC1 activation thus emerges as a key therapeutic strategy for mitigating Meth-induced cognitive deficits. Oxidative Stress and Epigenetic Dysregulation

Chronic Meth exposure establishes a self-amplifying cycle of oxidative stress that directly damages neuronal macromolecules and induces persistent epigenetic modifications, silencing critical genes required for synaptic plasticity (Fumagalli et al. 2015). The pathological elevation of extracellular dopamine (DA) serves as the primary instigator, as excess DA undergoes autooxidation, generating semiquinone radicals and ROS that attack DNA, proteins, and lipids (Kita et al. 2009). This oxidative assault triggers two interconnected pathological pathways: direct structural damage to synaptic components and epigenetic reprogramming via DNA hypermethylation of neuroplasticity gene promoters (notably BDNF) and HDAC-mediated chromatin compaction (Kandlur et al. 2020). These mechanisms converge to suppress the transcription of synaptic resilience genes, creating a permissive environment for hippocampal dysfunction even before overt neuronal loss occurs.

The oxidative cascade originates from Meth-induced hyperdopaminergia. Chronically elevated DA escapes synaptic clearance due to DAT dysfunction and diffuses into the extracellular space, where it undergoes non-enzymatic autooxidation (Kita et al. 2009, Almashhedy 2019). This process involves the donation of an electron from DA to molecular oxygen, generating the DA semiquinone radical and superoxide anion (O₂•⁻) (Cohen 1999). Semiquinone radicals are highly unstable and react rapidly with cellular nucleophiles, forming covalent adducts with proteins (e.g., impairing mitochondrial complex I function) and undergoing further oxidation to quinones that deplete glutathione (GSH) reserves. Meanwhile, O₂•⁻ is dismutated by superoxide dismutase (SOD) to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which, in the presence of transition metals like iron via the Fenton reaction, yields hydroxyl radicals (•OH) – the most reactive oxidant known. These species initiate lipid peroxidation chain reactions, generating cytotoxic aldehydes like 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA), which form adducts with ion channels, receptors, and cytoskeletal proteins critical for synaptic function (Milton and Sweeney 2012). For instance, 4-HNE modification of NMDAR subunits alters calcium permeability, while lipid peroxidation disrupts membrane fluidity essential for AMPA receptor (AMPAR) trafficking. The cumulative effect is mitochondrial electron transport chain impairment, creating a feedforward loop of ROS overproduction that overwhelms endogenous antioxidants like SOD, catalase, and GSH peroxidase (Angelova et al. 2025, Anderzhanova 2003).

Oxidative DNA damage represents a critical nexus linking free radicals to epigenetic dysregulation. •OH radicals attack deoxyribose sugars and nitrogenous bases, generating strand breaks and oxidized bases such as 8-oxo-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG). This damage activates the DNA base excision repair (BER) pathway, where 8-oxodG is excised by 8-oxoguanine glycosylase 1 (OGG1), creating an apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) site. During high oxidative stress, BER intermediates accumulate, recruiting DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) to damage sites. DNMT1, typically responsible for maintaining methylation patterns after replication, is aberrantly stabilized at these sites, leading to hypermethylation of CpG islands in promoter regions of genes adjacent to the damage (Halliwell et al. 2021). Simultaneously, ROS activate stress kinases like p38 MAPK and JNK, which phosphorylate DNMT1, enhancing its catalytic activity and promoting its binding to methyl-CpG-binding domain proteins (MBDs) like MeCP2. This establishes repressive chromatin complexes at loci governing synaptic plasticity (Fasolino and Zhou 2017). Crucially, the BDNF promoter contains multiple CpG islands, particularly within exons IV and VI, which regulate activity-dependent transcription. DNMT1-mediated hypermethylation of these regions recruits MeCP2, which then binds HDACs and other co-repressors, preventing transcription factor access to CREB binding sites (Bagheri et al. 2019). Consequently, chronic Meth exposure reduces hippocampal BDNF mRNA and protein in preclinical models, correlating with spatial memory deficits (Iamjan et al. 2021).

Complementing DNA hypermethylation, oxidative stress potently activates HDACs, further compacting chromatin structure. ROS inactivate class IIa HDACs (e.g., HDAC4, HDAC5) by promoting their nuclear export via calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII) phosphorylation. Paradoxically, this is overshadowed by the robust activation and nuclear accumulation of class I HDACs (HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3) (Liu et al. 2012, Helmstadter et al. 2021, Yang et al. 2025, Zhang et al. 2020). Meth-induced ROS inactivate GSK-3β fails to phosphorylate HDAC2, allowing its stabilization and nuclear retention. Concurrently, ROS activate the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) pathway at DNA break sites. PARP1 consumes NAD⁺ to synthesize poly (ADP-ribose) (PAR) chains on histones, creating docking sites for the recruitment of HDAC1 through PAR-binding motifs. Once enriched at target genes, HDAC1/2 complexes remove acetyl groups from histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9ac) and H4 lysine 12 (H4K12ac), marks associated with transcriptional activation. Deacetylation enhances chromatin condensation, limiting RNA polymerase II accessibility (Zong et al. 2022, Bartlett et al. 2018, Valian et al. 2018). Beyond BDNF, HDAC2 specifically targets promoters of synaptic genes including fibroblast growth factor 1 (FGF1), Homer1, Synapsin1, and GluA1 (AMPAR subunit) (Han et al. 2022, Hogan et al. 2020).

Complementing the focus on specific histone deacetylases, studies using the broad HDAC inhibitor sodium butyrate have provided functional evidence for the role of epigenetic mechanisms in Meth action. In behavioral paradigms, inhibitor sodium butyrate treatment during conditioning phases was found to facilitate the acquisition of a Meth-induced conditioned place preference, yet its administration during extinction training not only promoted the extinction of an established conditioned place preference but also completely blocked its subsequent reinstatement. Similarly, inhibitor sodium butyrate potentiated the locomotor response to a Meth challenge in sensitized mice when given during the acquisition period, although its effects on global striatal histone acetylation did not always directly correlate with these behavioral changes (Harkness et al. 2013, Zhu et al. 2017). The synergy between DNA methylation and HDAC activity creates an epigenetic blockade on synaptic gene networks. MeCP2 bound to methylated CpG islands recruits mSin3A co-repressor complexes containing HDAC1/2, while oxidized DNA sites attract PARP1-HDAC1 complexes. These assemblies further recruit DNA methyltransferases (DNMT3a/b), establishing a self-reinforcing cycle where histone deacetylation promotes additional DNA methylation and vice versa. This epigenetic “lockdown” extends beyond promoters to enhancer elements (Feltes 2024, Chaudhuri and Nussenzweig 2017, Chappidi et al. 2024). For example, the BDNF exon IV enhancer contains binding sites for CREB, which is paradoxically hyperphosphorylated but dysfunctional under chronic Meth exposure due to DA-driven cyclic AMP (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA) overactivation. Phosphorylated CREB recruits CREB-binding protein (CBP), a histone acetyltransferase (HAT), but HDAC2 directly deacetylates CBP, crippling its HAT activity. Consequently, CREB-CBP complexes fail to acetylate histones at plasticity genes despite CREB phosphorylation, decoupling signaling from transcription. This explains why Meth-exposed hippocampi exhibit high pCREB but low CRE-driven transcription, a hallmark of epigenetic resistance (Iamjan et al. 2021, Valdez et al. 2022, Krasnova et al. 2016).

Oxidative stress also disrupts ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes, which catalyze DNA demethylation. TET enzymes oxidize 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), an intermediate in active DNA demethylation. TET activity requires α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), iron (Fe²⁺), and oxygen as cofactors, making it vulnerable to redox imbalance (Joshi et al. 2022, Matuleviciute et al. 2021).

The functional consequences of oxidative-epigenetic dysregulation are profound. Reduced BDNF impairs TrkB signaling, suppressing mTOR-dependent synaptic protein synthesis (Sect. "Neurotrophic Signaling Suppression"). HDAC2-mediated GluA1 silencing exacerbates AMPAR deficiency (Sect. "Glutamatergic System Dysregulation"), while Homer1 downregulation destabilizes postsynaptic density scaffolds. Oxidatively damaged proteins like PSD-95 exhibit misfolding and aggregation, impairing NMDAR anchoring (Nayak et al. 2022).

In summary, chronic Meth exposure initiates a self-perpetuating cycle of oxidative stress and epigenetic repression that critically undermines synaptic plasticity. DA autooxidation generates semiquinone radicals and ROS, causing DNA damage that recruits DNMTs and establishes BDNF promoter hypermethylation. Concurrently, ROS activate and stabilize HDACs, which deacetylate histones and inactivate HATs like CBP, silencing CREB-dependent synaptic genes. The inhibition of TET and JMJD enzymes further stabilizes repressive chromatin states. This oxidative-epigenetic axis suppresses the transcription of neurotrophic factors, receptors, and scaffolding proteins essential for LTP, spine stability, and memory consolidation. As these modifications can outlast the initial oxidative insult, they represent a key mechanism for the persistence of Meth-induced cognitive impairment, offering prime targets for interventions combining antioxidants and epigenetic modulators. Neuroinflammation and Synaptic Injury

Chronic Meth exposure establishes a self-perpetuating cycle of neuroinflammation within the hippocampus, where apoptotic neuronal death and synaptic damage activate glial cells, triggering inflammatory cascades that further exacerbate synaptic dysfunction. This neuroinflammatory response, mediated primarily by activated microglia and astrocytes, releases a barrage of cytotoxic molecules that directly target synaptic structures and plasticity mechanisms. The core pathway involves apoptotic debris activating microglial TLR4, leading to NF-κB activation, subsequent overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β, and induction of COX-2/PGE signaling, culminating in dendritic spine elimination and synaptic instability. This process represents a critical amplification loop, transforming initial neuronal damage into widespread synaptic failure (Du et al. 2017, Park et al. 2017, Vilca et al. 2024, Shi et al. 2025, Silva et al. 2023).

The neuroinflammatory cascade is initiated by the products of Meth-induced apoptosis detailed in Sect. "Apoptotic Neuronal Death". As neurons undergo programmed cell death in hippocampal subfields like CA1 and CA3, they release apoptotic debris containing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including mtDNA, high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), heat shock proteins (HSPs), and phosphatidylserine externalized on membrane fragments (Tian et al. 2009, Frank et al. 2016). These DAMPs function as potent endogenous danger signals that activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on resident microglia, the brain’s primary immune sentinels. TLR4 is particularly crucial in this context, as it exhibits high affinity for HMGB1 and mtDNA fragments. Upon ligand binding, TLR4 recruits the adaptor proteins myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) and TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF), initiating downstream signaling cascades (Shi et al. 2018, Yang et al. 2020, Yang et al. 2010, Zhang et al. 2023, Azam et al. 2019). The MyD88-dependent pathway activates interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4), which phosphorylates IRAK1, leading to the recruitment of TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6). TRAF6 then activates transforming growth factor beta-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), which phosphorylates the inhibitor of kappa B kinase (IKK) complex. The activated IKK complex phosphorylates the inhibitory protein IκBα, targeting it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This liberates the transcription factor NF-κB (typically a p50/p65 heterodimer), allowing its translocation to the nucleus. Within the nucleus, NF-κB binds to specific κB motifs in the promoter regions of numerous pro-inflammatory genes, triggering their transcription. This molecular machinery drives the robust upregulation of TNF-α and IL-1β in microglia, establishing a state of chronic neuroinflammation within the Meth-exposed hippocampus (Basova et al. 2022, Silva et al. 2023, Vargas et al. 2020, Jeffery et al. 2025).

The cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β act as direct mediators of synaptic injury. TNF-α, primarily released by activated microglia but also by astrocytes, signals through two distinct receptors on neurons: TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1), which typically promotes pro-inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, and TNF receptor 2 (TNFR2), which can have neuroprotective effects but is often overwhelmed under chronic inflammatory conditions (Heir and Stellwagen 2020, Fischer and Maier 2020, Orti-Casañ et al. 2023, Pinto et al. 2024, Buffolo et al. 2021, Pozzi et al. 2018, Gao et al. 2023). In the context of chronic Meth exposure, sustained high levels of TNF-α favor TNFR1 signaling. TNFR1 activation recruits the adaptor protein TRADD (TNF receptor-associated death domain), leading to the activation of caspase-8 and downstream effector caspases, which can directly cleave synaptic proteins like PSD-95 and AMPA receptor (AMPAR) subunits, mirroring the actions of caspase-3 in apoptosis (Cadet and Krasnova 2022, Zhu et al. 2016, Micheau and Tschopp 2015). Furthermore, TNF-α potently stimulates the internalization of GluA1-containing AMPARs from the synaptic membrane through a mechanism involving increased surface expression of the GluA2-lacking calcium-permeable AMPARs and activation of the phosphatase STEP (striatal-enriched tyrosine phosphatase), which dephosphorylates GluA2 subunits, promoting endocytosis. This TNF-α-driven AMPAR internalization significantly weakens excitatory synaptic transmission and impairs LTP (Wigerblad et al. 2017, Rennich et al. 2023). IL-1β, signaling predominantly through the interleukin-1 receptor type 1 (IL-1R1) on neurons, exerts complementary disruptive effects. IL-1R1 activation recruits the adaptor protein MyD88, leading to the activation of interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK) and TRAF6, ultimately activating mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) such as p38 MAPK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). Activated p38 MAPK phosphorylates and modulates NMDAR subunits, altering channel kinetics and calcium permeability, while also inhibiting the phosphorylation of CREB, a transcription factor essential for synaptic plasticity-related gene expression. JNK activation contributes to cytoskeletal disruption by phosphorylating microtubule-associated proteins like MAP2 and tau, impairing dendritic stability and transport. Collectively, elevated TNF-α and IL-1β levels create a hostile synaptic environment, directly dismantling receptor complexes and inhibiting the molecular pathways required for plasticity (Li et al. 2023, Viviani et al. 2003, Prieto et al. 2019, Song et al. 2024).

Concurrent with cytokine release, NF-κB activation in microglia and astrocytes robustly induces the expression of COX-2, the inducible isoform responsible for prostaglandin synthesis during inflammation. COX-2 catalyzes the conversion of arachidonic acid (released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A (PLA) activated under inflammatory conditions) into prostaglandin H (PGH), which is then isomerized to PGE by specific PGE synthases (PGES) (Zhang 2025, Dócs 2024, Wang 2022, Singh 2022, Moussa 2023). PGE is a potent lipid mediator that diffuses from glial cells to act on neuronal E-prostanoid (EP) receptors, particularly EP1, EP2, and EP4, which are abundantly expressed on hippocampal neurons and modulate synaptic function. PGE signaling through EP1 receptors, coupled to Gq proteins, activates PLC, generating inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG), leading to intracellular calcium release and protein kinase C (PKC) activation. This calcium dysregulation can impair mitochondrial function and activate calcium-dependent proteases like calpain, which cleaves structural proteins such as spectrin and PSD-95 (Wang 2022, Moussa 2023). Signaling through EP2 and EP4 receptors, coupled to Gs proteins, increases intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels, activating protein kinase A (PKA). While acute PKA activation is crucial for plasticity, chronic elevation disrupts normal signaling, leading to aberrant phosphorylation of synaptic proteins and dysregulated CREB activity. Crucially, PGE signaling profoundly impacts the actin cytoskeleton within dendritic spines. PGE-EP2/EP4 signaling activates the small GTPase RhoA via PKA-mediated inhibition of RhoGAPs (Rho GTPase-activating proteins) or direct activation of RhoGEFs (Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factors). Activated RhoA stimulates Rho-associated kinase (ROCK), which phosphorylates and inactivates the actin-severing protein cofilin, stabilizing F-actin in a rigid conformation that prevents spine remodeling and maturation. Additionally, ROCK phosphorylates the myosin light chain (MLC), enhancing actinomyosin contractility, leading to spine head shrinkage and retraction (Xue et al. 2019, Hwang et al. 2025, Swanger et al. 2015). The combined effect is a loss of mature, mushroom-shaped dendritic spines and a predominance of immature, unstable filopodia-like spines incapable of supporting stable synapses. This PGE-driven spine loss is particularly evident in the DG and CA1 regions, directly contributing to the structural deficits underlying hippocampal cognitive impairment in chronic Meth users (Ding 2022, Kays 2019, Yamada 2014).

Astrocytes amplify the neuroinflammatory cascade initiated by microglia. Activated microglia release TNF-α, IL-1β, and other factors that trigger astrocyte reactivity, characterized by morphological changes (hypertrophy) and upregulation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Reactive astrocytes contribute to synaptic dysfunction through multiple mechanisms: (1) Excitotoxicity: Downregulation of glutamate transporters (GLT-1 and GLAST) on astrocytic processes, induced by TNF-α and oxidative stress, impairs glutamate uptake, leading to elevated extracellular glutamate levels and potential excitotoxicity (Mahmoud 2019); (2) Inflammatory Amplification: Reactive astrocytes produce additional cytokines (e.g., IL-6), chemokines (e.g., CCL2), and nitric oxide (NO), further fueling neuroinflammation and oxidative stress; (3) Synaptic Pruning: Astrocytes express phagocytic receptors like MERTK (MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase) and complement receptors (e.g., CR3), enabling them to engulf and eliminate synapses (synaptic pruning) in response to complement proteins like C1q and C3, which are upregulated by NF-κB and deposited on synapses tagged by chronic inflammation or stress (Verkhratsky 2023, Zhu 2022, Pekna 2021, Dejanovic 2022). This aberrant phagocytic activity contributes to excessive spine loss beyond physiological remodeling. Furthermore, the inflammatory milieu disrupts the tripartite synapse. Astrocytic processes normally envelop synapses, regulating ion concentrations and releasing gliotransmitters like D-serine, a co-agonist required for NMDAR activation. Meth-induced neuroinflammation disrupts this coverage and reduces D-serine release, contributing to NMDAR hypofunction and impaired LTP induction. Thus, the transition from acute microglial activation to chronic astrogliosis creates a self-sustaining inflammatory environment that persistently assaults synaptic integrity long after the initial Meth insult has subsided (Liang 2022, Shi et al. 2021, Abreu 2023).

In summary, chronic Meth exposure establishes a vicious cycle of neuroinflammation that critically amplifies synaptic injury in the hippocampus. Apoptotic debris generated by Meth neurotoxicity activates microglial TLR4 signaling, driving NF-κB-mediated transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β. These cytokines directly impair synaptic function by promoting AMPAR internalization, inhibiting NMDAR signaling, and activating caspases that cleave synaptic scaffolding proteins. Simultaneously, NF-κB induces COX-2 expression, leading to increased PGE production. PGE signaling through neuronal EP receptors dysregulates calcium homeostasis and potently activates the RhoA/ROCK pathway, resulting in cofilin inactivation and actinomyosin hypercontractility, which collapse dendritic spines and prevent their maturation. Reactive astrocytes amplify this damage through impaired glutamate uptake, further inflammatory mediator release, and aberrant synaptic phagocytosis. This neuroinflammatory assault directly dismantles the structural and functional basis of hippocampal synaptic plasticity, representing a crucial mechanism by which chronic Meth exposure translates neuronal damage into persistent cognitive deficits. Targeting this neuroinflammatory axis offers significant potential for therapeutic intervention in Meth-induced cognitive decline. Structural and Functional Consequences

The cumulative molecular pathologies induced by chronic Meth exposure converge to produce profound structural reorganization and functional incapacitation within the hippocampal circuitry. These manifests through three interconnected tiers of neurobiological disruption: synaptic degeneration evident in dendritic spine collapse and presynaptic malfunctions; neuronal network decoupling that severs critical communication pathways; and meta-circuitry failure that disables integrative cognitive operations. These alterations represent the physical and physiological embodiment of the molecular mechanisms detailed in Sect. "Molecular Mechanisms of Synaptic Impairment", transforming biochemical aberrations into tangible neurological deficits that crystallize as hippocampal-dependent cognitive impairment.

Synaptic Pathology

The most immediate consequence of chronic Meth neurotoxicity emerges at the synaptic level, where dendritic spine pathology fundamentally rewires hippocampal connectivity (Ding et al. 2022, Liang et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2025). Chronic Meth exposure leads to significant structural alterations in frontal cortex neurons, including a notable reduction in dendritic complexity and spine density, which is accompanied by the upregulation of type I interferons, indicating a neuroinflammatory response that may contribute to the observed synaptic pathology (Coutinho et al. 2008).

This spine loss originates from the synergistic assault detailed in Sect. "Glutamatergic System Dysregulation"–"Neuroinflammation and Synaptic Injury": glutamatergic dysregulation reduces surface AMPARs, destabilizing spine heads; neurotrophic suppression (BDNF/TrkB/PI3K/Akt/mTOR) impairs activity-dependent spine maturation; oxidative stress damages spine cytoskeletal proteins; and neuroinflammation (TNF-α/PGE) activates RhoA/ROCK signaling, inducing spine retraction (He 2022, Moya–Alvarado 2023, Choquet 2025, Tsai 2022). Mushroom spines, characterized by their large heads and narrow necks, are particularly dependent on actin stabilization via cofilin phosphorylation and AMPAR anchoring via PSD-95 (Lewitus et al. 2014, Purcell and Carew 2003, Hotulainen and Hoogenraad 2010).

Meth disrupts synaptic integrity and structural plasticity through convergent mechanisms involving neuroinflammation, cytoskeletal reorganization, and altered synaptogenesis. Acute Meth exposure triggers astrocytic and microglial activation in the hippocampus, accompanied by upregulation of TNF-α and its receptor TNFR1, which promotes AMPAR internalization and contributes to synaptic dysfunction without immediate cell death (Goncalves et al.). Concurrently, Meth activates the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway, leading to F-actin cytoskeleton rearrangement, downregulation of tight junction proteins, and increased blood–brain barrier permeability—factors that destabilize the dendritic spine architecture (Xue et al.). Prenatal Meth exposure further impairs axon growth and synaptogenesis, reducing presynaptic protein levels in early development while inducing a compensatory upregulation of PSD-95 in adolescence and adulthood, ultimately resulting in long-lasting memory deficits (Benya-aphikul et al.). Together, these findings indicate that Meth-induced inflammation, cytoskeletal disruption, and aberrant synaptic adaptation converge to destabilize spine morphology—favoring the proliferation of immature thin spines and dominance of stubby spines in CA1—which compromises spine head volume and neck resistance, thereby reducing postsynaptic current amplitude and synaptic signal propagation, as reflected in the structural and electrophysiological deficits described (Xue et al. 2019, Goncalves 2009, Benya-Aphikul et al. 2021). Complementing postsynaptic deterioration, presynaptic terminals undergo parallel disintegration. Immunohistochemical analyses reveal reductions in synaptophysin – a calcium-binding glycoprotein essential for synaptic vesicle docking and fusion – across hippocampal mossy fiber terminals and Schaffer collaterals. This synaptophysin depletion stems from multiple mechanisms: (Drugs UNOo 2024) Oxidative modification of cysteine residues in synaptophysin’s transmembrane domains, causing protein misfolding and proteasomal degradation; (Rogers et al. 2023) Caspase-3 cleavage of synaptobrevin (VAMP2), disrupting the SNARE complex that anchors synaptophysin to vesicles; and (Tartt et al. 2022) Epigenetic silencing of the Syp gene via HDAC2-mediated histone deacetylation (Limanaqi et al. 2018, Yang et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2017). While the nanoscale decoupling of adhesion molecules is a critical mechanism, the picture is further complicated by the compensatory upregulation of these very proteins following Meth exposure. Contrary to the fragmentation observed in chronic paradigms, acute and subacute Meth exposure has been shown to increase the expression of the postsynaptic adhesion molecule Neuroligin-1 in the hippocampus. This upregulation is linked to a concomitant increase in Synapsin-1 and PSD-95, suggesting an initial, perhaps maladaptive, attempt at synaptic strengthening. However, this molecular change is functionally detrimental, as it is associated with the inhibition of LTP and subsequent spatial memory deficits. Critically, the knockdown of Neuroligin-1 using siRNA was found to rescue these impairments, positioning this adhesion molecule not just as a passive marker of damage, but as an active and necessary mediator of Meth-induced synaptic and cognitive dysfunction (Cao et al. 2022).

Circuit Decoupling

Synaptic pathology escalates into macroscopic circuit dysfunction through hippocampal-subcortical decoupling, most critically in the hippocampal-VTA loop essential for novelty detection and memory salience. The hippocampus encodes contextual novelty via sharp-wave ripple events in CA1 that project via the subiculum to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) shell and directly to VTA dopaminergic neurons (Duszkiewicz 2019, Glangetas et al. 2015, Bossert 2015). The memory impairment in Meth-treated rats was prevented by intra-NAc infusion of an otherwise ineffective dose of the GABAA agonist muscimol, whereas it was exacerbated by the GABAA antagonist bicuculline. This bidirectional modulation was mirrored in the glutamatergic system: blocking NMDA receptors with an ineffective dose of D-AP5 rescued Meth-induced deficits, while NMDA itself worsened them. These findings suggest that Meth disrupts hippocampal-dependent learning by altering the balance of GABAA and NMDA receptor-mediated transmission in the NAc, positioning the modulation of this receptor system within the mesolimbic pathway as a potential therapeutic strategy (Heysieattalab et al. 2016).

Meta-Circuit Failure

The culmination of synaptic and circuit pathology manifests as large-scale network decoupling, detectable through functional MRI (fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) in humans. Resting-state fMRI reveals reduced functional connectivity (FC) between the hippocampus and default mode network (DMN) nodes – particularly the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) – that support autobiographical memory and future planning. This FC reduction correlates with synaptic loss markers (serum synaptophysin autoantibodies) and reflects impaired hippocampo-cortical dialogue: hippocampal theta activity normally modulates cortical gamma oscillations via direct projections and thalamic relays, but Meth-induced hippocampal damage disrupts this top-down control (Zhou et al. 2022, Gong et al. 2022, Hwang et al. 2024). Simultaneously, structural connectivity deteriorates: DTI shows reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in the fornix and cingulum bundle – white matter tracts carrying hippocampal efferents to subcortical and cortical targets. This white matter damage originates not from demyelination, but from axon terminal degeneration due to presynaptic failure and microtubule destabilization via oxidative GSK-3β activation, which hyperphosphorylates tau and disrupts kinesin-mediated transport (Weng et al. 2017, Li et al. 2019, Hooper et al. 2018).

The most behaviorally consequential disruption occurs in the hippocampal-prefrontal-thalamic tripartite circuit governing contextual memory updating. Novelty signals from the hippocampus inhibit thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) GABAergic neurons via ventral CA1 projections, disinhibiting mediodorsal thalamus (MDT) inputs to prefrontal cortex (PFC) that facilitate working memory updating. Chronic Meth exposure induces a pathological reversal: hippocampal novelty responses are blunted, failing to inhibit TRN, leading to sustained MDT suppression and PFC hypoactivation during novelty exposure (Thanos et al. 2016, Kim 2009).

Temporal Dynamics of Pathology

These structural and functional consequences evolve through distinct phases. Acute Meth exposure (days-weeks) primarily induces synaptic dysregulation: AMPAR internalization, spine shrinkage, and presynaptic vesicle depletion that reversibly impairs plasticity (Gibson et al. 2022, Liang et al. 2022, Ding et al. 2022, Keifer 2022, Rezayof et al. 2023). With chronic exposure (months), oxidative-epigenetic mechanisms dominate: DNA damage silences plasticity genes, HDAC2 compaction stabilizes maladaptive synaptic configurations, and neuroinflammation triggers microglial phagocytosis of synapses (Limanaqi et al. 2018, Fumagalli et al. 2015). In the terminal phase (years), cumulative apoptosis eliminates critical neuronal populations (CA1/CA3 pyramidal neurons, PV+ interneurons), while Wallerian degeneration severs long-range connections, producing irreversible circuit damage (Xu et al. 2021, Wu et al. 2016, Pilski and Graves 2023, Cadet et al. 2020, Jafari et al. 2023). This temporal progression explains clinical observations: cognitive deficits may partially reverse with early abstinence through synaptic recovery, but become fixed with prolonged addiction due to structural neuronal loss.

In summary, chronic Meth exposure orchestrates a systematic deconstruction of hippocampal architecture and function. At the microscale, dendritic spine loss and presynaptic failure dismantle individual synapses, eroding the basic units of information transfer. At the mesoscale, hippocampal-VTA decoupling and gamma oscillation collapse disrupt temporal coding and novelty processing. At the macroscale, white matter degeneration and hippocampo-cortical disconnection isolate the hippocampus from cognitive networks. This tripartite pathology – spanning synaptic, circuit, and network levels – provides the neurobiological substrate for the debilitating cognitive impairment that defines chronic Meth addiction, transforming a structure essential for memory and navigation into a fragmented landscape of disconnected neurons and silent synapses. Bridging Mechanisms to Cognitive Deficits

Results from this study indicate that methamphetamine exposure leads to significant impairments in cognitive functions, as evidenced by altered performance in the Morris water maze. These deficits are associated with reduced levels of BDNF and CREB, alongside elevated GSK3 activity, suggesting that disruption of neurotrophic signaling pathways and activation of neurotoxic processes contribute to the observed cognitive impairments in methamphetamine-treated rats. Selegiline treatment mitigates these effects, potentially through the involvement of CREB/BDNF and Akt/GSK3 signaling pathways (Feizipour et al. 2020). An animal study indicate that pretreatment with minocycline significantly attenuates methamphetamine-induced dopaminergic neurotoxicity and behavioral sensitization, suggesting that anti-inflammatory or anti-apoptotic mechanisms may confer protection against METH-related neuronal damage. However, quantitative evaluations linking these neuroprotective effects to specific molecular markers such as caspase-3 activation or BDNF depletion remain sparse (Zhang et al. 2006). Human MRI and DTI investigations demonstrate that chronic methamphetamine exposure is associated with significant hippocampal volume reduction, cingulate–limbic cortical gray matter deficits, and frontal white-matter microstructural abnormalities (including reduced fractional anisotropy), with diffusion changes in striatal regions correlating with earlier initiation, greater cumulative dose, and poorer memory performance—supporting a dose-dependent pattern of medial temporal and frontostriatal neurostructural injury underlying cognitive impairmen (Thompson et al. 2004, Alicata et al. 2009) In rats models of depression, administration of N-acetylcysteine (NAC) attenuates hippocampal oxidative stress, restores antioxidant enzyme activity, and mitigates neuronal injury and dendritic spine loss through suppression of p38/JNK signaling, thereby improving depressive-like behaviors—supporting an oxidative stress–related mechanism of neuroprotection and recovery (Fan et al. 2020).

Emerging evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of NAC in methamphetamine dependence through restoration of glutamatergic homeostasis and antioxidant protection; however, while preliminary clinical studies and small-scale trials suggest possible benefits in reducing craving and neurotoxicity, definitive randomized controlled trials confirming improvements in cognitive outcomes and oxidative stress markers are still lacking (McKetin et al. 2017, Grant et al. 2010).

Similarly, Rats’ research indicates that modulation of epigenetic regulation and oxidative defense systems can reverse methamphetamine-induced neuroadaptations. Inhibition of histone deacetylases with sodium butyrate alters distinct phases of methamphetamine-conditioned place preference, accelerating extinction and completely blocking reinstatement, consistent with restoration of transcriptional plasticity within reward-related circuits. Complementarily, treatment with the antioxidant N-acetyl-L-cysteine mitigates acute hyperlocomotion, prevents the development of behavioral sensitization, and preserves striatal dopamine levels in methamphetamine-exposed rats, suggesting a dopaminergic protection against oxidative stress-induced neurotoxicity. Together, these findings support converging molecular pathways—epigenetic remodeling einforcement—as key mechanisms underlying potential cognitive and motivational recovery from methamphetamine dependence (Zhu et al. 2017, Fukami et al. 2004). Experimental evidence demonstrates that minocycline exerts robust neuroprotective effects against methamphetamine-induced dopaminergic neurotoxicity and behavioral abnormalities. In mice models, pretreatment with minocycline significantly blunts methamphetamine-evoked hyperlocomotion and prevents the development of behavioral sensitization, while preserving striatal dopamine content, its metabolites, and dopamine transporter immunoreactivity. Microdialysis findings further indicate that minocycline normalizes the excessive extracellular dopamine release triggered by methamphetamine without altering its pharmacokinetics, underscoring its direct neuroprotective action. Together, these results suggest that minocycline mitigates the functional and molecular consequences of methamphetamine exposure through suppression of neuroinflammation and preservation of dopaminergic signaling, providing mechanistic insight into region-specific vulnerability and potential therapeutic reversal of addiction-related neural injury (Zhang et al. 2006).

Neuroimaging evidence in human methamphetamine abusers demonstrates pronounced neuroinflammatory activation that dynamically evolves with abstinence duration. Using ¹¹C-PK11195 Positron Emission Tomography (PET), Sekine et al. identified widespread microglial activation—exceeding 250% of control levels—across dopaminergic and serotonergic regions including the midbrain, striatum, thalamus, and orbitofrontal cortex. Notably, the magnitude of [¹¹C]-PK11195 binding inversely correlated with the length of abstinence, indicating partial normalization of glial activation over time. These findings provide direct in vivo evidence that methamphetamine-related neurotoxicity involves a protracted, yet reversible, inflammatory process that parallels the temporal trajectory of cognitive recovery described in longitudinal studies (Sekine et al. 2008). Neuroimaging across longitudinal studies reveals complex structural adaptations in the brains of methamphetamine users that evolve during abstinence. Specifically, voxel-based morphometry analyses indicate that gray-matter deficits in regions such as the orbitofrontal cortex and caudate nucleus—which may reflect pre-morbid conditions or smoking effects—show subsequent, partial recovery characterized by volume increases in several cortical areas following methamphetamine cessation. While these findings highlight the plasticity of certain gray-matter regions during early abstinence, the long-term trajectory, persistent hippocampal atrophy, and underlying molecular influences such as genetic polymorphisms or sex-specific neuroimmune signaling remain areas requiring further conclusive investigation (Morales et al. 2012). Different molecular mechanisms preferentially impact distinct cognitive domains through regional hippocampal vulnerability patterns. Findings from rodent models demonstrate that methamphetamine-induced neurotoxicity, characterized by significant dopamine depletion in the striatum, is closely linked to oxidative stress. The antioxidant N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) has been shown to mitigate these effects, dose-dependently attenuating both acute hyperlocomotion and the development of behavioral sensitization following repeated methamphetamine exposure. Furthermore, NAC treatment provides protection against striatal dopamine reduction, suggesting its potential to counteract the neurochemical deficits associated with methamphetamine abuse, though the precise relationship between the prevention of striatal dopamine loss and the recovery of specific cognitive functions remains to be fully determined (Fukami et al. 2004).

Aerobic exercise has emerged as a potential therapeutic intervention for methamphetamine dependence, with a 12-week program demonstrating beneficial effects on both cognitive function and oxidative stress markers. Specifically, such exercise improved processing speed in methamphetamine-dependent patients and significantly attenuated the spontaneous increase in serum MDA, a key marker of lipid peroxidation, during 12 weeks of abstinence. This finding is particularly relevant given that patients with verbal learning and memory deficits exhibited higher baseline MDA levels, suggesting a direct link between oxidative stress and domain-specific cognitive impairment. While the study did not find significant interactions across all cognitive domains, the selective improvement in processing speed, coupled with the mitigation of oxidative damage, supports the notion that different cognitive and biological markers may be differentially responsive to intervention and could serve as distinct targets for monitoring therapeutic efficacy in methamphetamine-related neurotoxicity (Zhang et al. 2018).

Collectively, chronic Meth exposure creates an unambiguous mechanistic bridge from molecular pathology to cognitive impairment through convergent evidence across experimental approaches. Rodent behavioral paradigms quantitatively link caspase activation and BDNF depletion to specific memory deficits, with pharmacological interventions confirming causality. Human neuroimaging demonstrates hippocampal structural and functional decline correlates with peripheral markers of oxidative stress, inflammation, and epigenetic silencing. Longitudinal progression studies and vulnerability analyses definitively establish cognitive decline as a direct consequence of evolving molecular pathology rather than an epiphenomenon, with domain-specific deficits mapping onto distinct regional mechanisms. This integrative perspective transforms cognitive symptoms from behavioral descriptors into measurable outcomes of hippocampal synaptic disruption, providing diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapeutic opportunities grounded in molecular pathogenesis. Therapeutic Targeting of Key Mechanisms

The elucidation of methamphetamine’s molecular pathogenesis provides a rational foundation for developing targeted interventions to prevent or reverse hippocampal synaptic disruption. Therapeutic strategies must address the core mechanisms driving synaptic toxicity—dopaminergic dysregulation, apoptotic cascades, neurotrophic collapse, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and epigenetic dysregulation—while navigating the complex temporal dynamics of neuroadaptation and neurodegeneration. Pharmacological agents that precisely modulate these pathways demonstrate significant potential to restore synaptic integrity and cognitive function when administered during critical intervention windows. Dopamine stabilizers represent a first-line approach targeting the initiating event in Meth neurotoxicity, where agents like aripiprazole—a D2 receptor partial agonist—normalize the pathological D1/D2 imbalance induced by chronic Meth exposure. By providing sufficient D2 receptor stimulation without causing full suppression, aripiprazole counteracts methamphetamine-induced D1 receptor overactivation while restoring physiological D2-mediated signaling cascades (Cadet and Krasnova 2022, Sakurai et al. 2022).

Preclinical work indicates that Meth impairs hippocampal LTP and disrupts intracellular signaling cascades involving CREB phosphorylation and Akt/GSK-3β, leading to synaptic protein loss. Aripiprazole, a dopamine D2 partial agonist, has been shown to stabilize dopaminergic and cAMP/PKA signaling and may mitigate stimulant-induced synaptic disruptions, though direct evidence of its efficacy in preventing methamphetamine-induced hippocampal LTP deficits with the specific quantitative effects described has not yet been published. While dopamine D₂ partial agonists such as aripiprazole have been explored for Meth use disorder primarily to reduce craving and relapse risk, direct evidence that adjunctive aripiprazole improves spatial working memory or hippocampal–prefrontal connectivity in methamphetamine-dependent individuals is not yet available. Preclinical and clinical observations in other contexts suggest that timely dopamine modulation during early abstinence may be critical, as delayed intervention after synaptic pruning may limit therapeutic benefit (Cadet and Krasnova 2022, Sakurai et al. 2022, Elkashef et al. 2008, Ishibashi et al. 2010, Jayanthi et al. 2014). In methamphetamine-exposed rodents, minocycline markedly attenuates hippocampal apoptotic signaling and preserves synaptic structure and function. Multiple recent preclinical reports and reviews show that minocycline reduces markers of apoptosis (including caspase activation, cytochrome-c release and shifts in Bax/Bcl-2), protects dendritic spine morphology in hippocampal CA1, and ameliorates METH-induced impairments in hippocampal LTP and spatial memory paradigms. Clinical pilot work in chronic METH users provides preliminary translational support for cognitive benefits (Sepehr et al. 2020, Alavi et al. 2021, Motaghinejad et al. 2023, Romero-Miguel et al. 2021, Kusui et al. 2024). The natural polyphenol resveratrol (trans-3,5,4’-trihydroxystilbene) complements this approach by targeting the p53-Bax axis upstream of caspase activation, while simultaneously activating neuroprotective sirtuin pathways. Preclinical evidence suggests that resveratrol can modulate SIRT1-dependent pathways affecting p53 and FOXO3a, reduce neuronal apoptosis, and suppress HDAC2 expression—mechanisms that plausibly counteract methamphetamine-related epigenetic and apoptotic toxicity, although precise quantitative effects in METH models remain unreported. Similarly, early-phase clinical work indicates minocycline can improve cognitive performance and reduce peripheral apoptotic or inflammatory markers in abstinent Meth users (Alavi et al. 2021, Cadet and Krasnova 2021, Li et al. 2021, Guo et al. 2019, Jiang et al. 2020, Chen et al. 2018). Neurotrophic restoration strategies address the critical BDNF-TrkB signaling collapse that underlies synaptic protein synthesis failure during Meth exposure. Direct BDNF supplementation faces bioavailability challenges, leading to the development of small-molecule TrkB agonists like 7,8-dihydroxyflavone (7,8-DHF) that cross the blood-brain barrier and mimic BDNF’s synaptotrophic effects (Limanaqi et al. 2018, Liu et al. 2016, Jhan et al. 2025, Swain and Dagda 2025).

In a mouse model of methamphetamine (METH) abuse, pretreatment with the TrkB agonist 7,8-DHF (10 mg/kg) significantly attenuated METH-induced hyperlocomotion and prevented the development of behavioral sensitization following repeated METH exposure. The neuroprotective effects of 7,8-DHF were evidenced by its ability to prevent the reduction of dopamine transporter (DAT) levels in the striatum, a key marker of dopaminergic neurotoxicity, an effect that was completely blocked by the TrkB antagonist ANA-12. Furthermore, 7,8-DHF treatment reduced METH-induced microglial activation in the striatum, suggesting an additional anti-neuroinflammatory mechanism. These findings demonstrate that 7,8-DHF, through TrkB receptor activation, can mitigate both the behavioral abnormalities and neurotoxic damage associated with METH, highlighting the therapeutic potential of TrkB agonists for psychostimulant abuse (Ren et al. 2014). Acute intranasal treatment with the TrkB agonist LM22A-4 following pediatric traumatic brain injury preserved myelin integrity in the external capsule and reduced tissue volume loss, which was associated with an amelioration of injury-induced anxiolytic behavior (Fletcher et al. 2021). Evidence from a recent comprehensive review indicates that transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) protocols yield multifaceted therapeutic benefits for individuals with methamphetamine use disorder. The findings demonstrate that TMS, through the modulation of specific brain activities, effectively reduces drug craving, alleviates affective symptoms, improves sleep quality, and enhances cognitive functions in this population, highlighting its potential as a non-invasive treatment modality (Zhang et al. 2024).

In rats models of Meth neurotoxicity, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and its derivative N-acetylcysteine-amide (NACA) provide significant multi-organ protection, with NACA (250 mg/kg) proving more potent than NAC (500 mg/kg) in preventing oxidative stress, DNA fragmentation, and protein depletion across brain, liver, and kidney tissues. Complementary metabolomic analyses reveal that repeated methamphetamine exposure broadly disrupts cerebral homeostasis, evidenced by significant reductions in glutathione, acetylcysteine, and neuronal integrity marker N-acetyl-aspartate across the hippocampus, nucleus accumbens, and prefrontal cortex, alongside neurotransmitter disturbances including decreased glutamate and GABA and region-specific dopamine and serotonin depletion. These metabolic perturbations—elevated succinic acid semialdehyde, disrupted tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, and increased glial marker myo-inositol—collectively indicate that methamphetamine induces widespread oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and gliosis, providing mechanistic support for antioxidant interventions like NAC and NACA to preserve neuronal integrity and metabolic function (Koriem et al. 2021, Bu et al. 2013).

Sulforaphane, a potent Nrf2 activator, mitigates methamphetamine-induced neurotoxicity by upregulating the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathway; this intervention restores the expression of downstream enzymes including HO-1 and GCS in both PC12 cells and the rat prefrontal cortex, leading to a marked reduction in oxidative damage and apoptosis, effects that are partially reversed upon Nrf2 knockdown (Xiong et al. 2020). Complementing these preclinical findings, open-label clinical investigations have begun to translate the therapeutic potential of minocycline to human methamphetamine users. In an eight-week open-label case series, adjunctive treatment with 200 mg daily minocycline resulted in clinically significant improvements, attenuating both positive (Cohen *d* = 0.63) and negative (Cohen *d* = 0.53) psychotic symptoms while concurrently enhancing neuropsychological function, particularly auditory working memory (Cohen *d* = 0.45). Although preliminary, these findings provide promising evidence for minocycline’s efficacy in addressing the refractory psychosis and cognitive deficits associated with chronic methamphetamine use disorder, supporting its role as a candidate adjunctive pharmacotherapy (Alavi et al. 2021).

The flavonoid luteolin exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects in microglia: it inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation upon LPS activation, leading to substantial reductions in COX-2 expression and PGE₂ production, while also promoting neuroprotection (e.g. preserving neuronal viability) in hippocampal/microglia-neuron co-culture systems (Zhu et al. 2011, Zhou et al. 2019, Tassinari et al. 2022) In human studies, combined minocycline (200 mg/day) and NAC (2400 mg/day) therapy reduces serum TNF-α by 52% and IL-6 by 47% while improving hippocampal-dependent memory more effectively than either agent alone (composite memory score increase: 38% vs. 22% for monotherapies) (Haber et al. 2013).

Epigenetic therapies using HDAC inhibitors are a promising direction. For example, sodium butyrate has been shown to increase BDNF gene transcription (via H3 acetylation) in astrocytes (Wu et al. 2008). In models of brain injury or hypoxic‑ischemia, sodium butyrate improves spatial learning and memory in the Morris water maze and increases hippocampal BDNF protein levels (Dash et al. 2009). In Meth models, sodium butyrate enhances MA‑sensitized locomotor responses, though effects on BDNF promoters IV & IX and rescue of MWM performance have not yet been fully established (Harkness et al. 2013). Selective HDAC2 inhibitors like BRD6688 show enhanced specificity, reversing aberrant histone methylation at synaptic plasticity genes without global epigenetic disruption (Wagner et al. 2015). Integrating these therapeutic approaches necessitates careful consideration of the timing relative to Meth use. During active Meth use, dopamine stabilizers and antioxidants may offer maximal protection against acute toxicity. For instance, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a potent antioxidant, has been shown to mitigate Meth -induced oxidative stress and preserve dopaminergic function in the striatum of mice (Pan et al. 2020).

Conclusion and Future Directions

The molecular pathogenesis of hippocampal synaptic plasticity disruption induced by chronic Meth exposure converges on a unified cascade initiated by dopamine dysregulation. Sustained extracellular dopamine elevation triggers ROS generation via auto-oxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, establishing profound oxidative stress. This oxidative milieu activates intrinsic mitochondrial apoptosis through p53-mediated Bax translocation, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3 activation, dismantling synaptic structures via cleavage of PSD-95 and ARC. Concurrently, glutamatergic signaling is disrupted through NMDAR hypofunction via calcineurin-mediated internalization and impaired AMPAR trafficking, crippling spine maturation. Neurotrophic support suppression, particularly BDNF-TrkB-PI3K/Akt pathway impairment, fails to counteract excitotoxicity and disrupts mTORC1-dependent protein synthesis essential for LTP consolidation. These processes are amplified by neuroinflammation (microglial TNF-α/IL-1β release, COX-2/PGE₂ signaling) and epigenetic dysregulation (HDAC2 upregulation, BDNF promoter hypermethylation), creating self-sustaining synaptic injury. The convergence of these insults manifests as dendritic spine loss (notably mature mushroom spines in DG and CA1), presynaptic vesicle depletion, impaired LTP, and enhanced LTD, translating directly to declarative memory deficits, impaired spatial learning, and defective pattern separation.

Critical research gaps warrant focused investigation. First, sex differences in Meth neurotoxicity remain underexplored; females exhibit greater hippocampal dopamine release, enhanced oxidative stress responses, and more extensive spine loss, yet the contributions of hormonal effects, sex chromosome complement, and sex-specific neuroimmune signaling are poorly defined. Second, circuit-specific vulnerability within hippocampal subfields (disproportionate DG and CA3 damage) and extended networks (hippocampal-prefrontal-thalamic loops, hippocampal-VTA circuits) demands higher resolution using region-specific ribosome profiling, chemogenetics, and high-field fMRI connectivity mapping. Third, the persistence of cognitive deficits after Meth cessation suggests stable epigenetic modifications or “epigenetic memory” involving lasting histone methylation/acetylation patterns and DNA hypermethylation at BDNF exons. Whether targeted epigenetic therapies (selective HDAC inhibitors, DNMT inhibitors) can reverse these marks and restore cognitive function constitutes a vital translational frontier.

Future therapeutic innovation should focus on multi-target ligands simultaneously modulating dopamine stabilization (e.g., D2 partial agonism), enhancing antioxidant defenses (Nrf2 activation), and providing TrkB neurotrophic support (7,8-DHF, LM22A-4). Advanced delivery systems, including hippocampal-targeted nanoparticles and BBB-penetrant viral vectors for BDNF overexpression, could improve precision. Integrating neuromodulation techniques such as closed-loop DBS responsive to hippocampal theta oscillations or transcranial focused ultrasound offers novel approaches for restoring circuit dynamics. Validating these interventions requires standardized cross-species cognitive batteries (virtual reality-based spatial navigation for humans, touchscreen-based pattern separation for rodents) and longitudinal biomarker panels tracking serum 8-oxodG, exosomal synaptic proteins (GAP-43), microglial-derived cytokines, and PBMC epigenetic marks correlated with in vivo synaptic density measurements using [¹¹C]UCB-J PET imaging. Addressing these mechanistic gaps and therapeutic opportunities can advance strategies to halt and reverse the synaptic devastation wrought by methamphetamine, preserving cognitive integrity in individuals struggling with addiction.
