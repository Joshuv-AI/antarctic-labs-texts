Human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes improve recovery from myocardial infarction in non-human primates
Authors: Xumin Guan, Pengfei Zhang, Qian Wang, Yuehui Zhang, Yunfan Zhang, He Zhang, Hang Zhang, Wanxiang Jiang, Yanxia Wu, Xijie Wang, Dongjin Wang, Jiaxian Wang, Ling Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transplantation of human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) represents a promising next-generation cell therapy for repairing injured myocardium. However, the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetics of these cells in non-human primates (NHPs) with myocardial infarction (MI) have not been systematically investigated.MethodsSixteen rhesus monkeys underwent MI surgery, with 10 monkeys receiving 1 × 108 hiPSC-CMs through intramyocardial injection and six monkeys receiving vehicle alone. Echocardiography and cardiac MRI were performed to evaluate the cardiac function and infarct size. A wearable electrocardiogram monitoring device was used to detect postoperative arrhythmias. The bio-distribution of grafted cells in a separate cohort of four monkeys was explored via positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) tracking using zirconium 89 (89Zr)-prelabeled hiPSC-CMs. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and immunostaining were used to detect the grafted cells in the host.ResultshiPSC-CM transplantation significantly improved cardiac performance at 4 and 12 weeks after MI, including left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction, fractional shortening, end-systolic volume, and internal dimension at end-systole. Furthermore, cell transplantation reduced myocardial infarct size, reversed cardiac hypertrophy, and increased angiogenesis 12 weeks after MI. Although there was a higher incidence of arrhythmias in the cell therapy group compared to the control group, it was resolved 2 weeks after cell transplantation. The survival of multiple islands of human myocardial cells in the host hearts was identified 12 weeks after cell implantation, indicating the remuscularization potential of hiPSC-CMs. Consistent with this, PET/CT tracking of 89Zr-prelabeled hiPSC-CMs showed a high retention of the radioactivity in the heart 2 weeks after transplantation. Additionally, the human mitochondrial DNA assay revealed that, except for the heart, no evidence of human cells was observed in the other organs after injection.ConclusionsThe transplantation of hiPSC-CMs can effectively repair injured cardiac tissue in an NHP MI model without any adverse off-target effects. Therefore, the direct intramyocardial injection of hiPSC-CMs is a promising, effective, and safe strategy for treating MI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04664-0.Keywords: hiPSC-CMs, Monkey, Myocardial infarction, Electrocardiogram

Introduction

Heart disease continues to be a major cause of morbidity and mortality around the world [1, 2]. The limited regenerative capacity of the mammalian adult heart makes it difficult to replace the loss of cardiomyocytes caused by coronary artery disease [3, 4]. This unmet clinical need has driven the development of stem cell-based therapies, which aim to restore cardiac function by replenishing the damaged myocardium [5–7]. Among these, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) represent a breakthrough advance. They can be derived from somatic cells through reprogramming [8] and differentiation techniques, generating unlimited numbers of transplanted cells that closely mimic native cardiomyocytes [9, 10].

The transplantation of hiPSC-CMs offers a promising strategy for myocardial repair, and their efficacy has been widely confirmed in various animal MI models [11–13]. Although autologous iPSC-CMs have been shown to survive, integrate into the host myocardium, and remain stable for up to 12 months without the need for immunosuppression [5], given their long preparation times and high costs, allogeneic hiPSC-CMs provide a more feasible and cost-effective solution and are more suitable for clinical study at present. While a few case reports have explored the clinical use of allogeneic hiPSC-CMs [14–16], comprehensive and large-scale clinical trial reports systematically evaluating these therapies remain scarce [17]. This gap underscores the challenges in translating hiPSC-CM therapies from basic research to routine clinical practice, including immune rejection, cell survival, and scalability, and highlights the necessity for more standardized preclinical studies. In light of this, further animal studies are still required to assess their safety, efficacy, long-term outcomes, and distribution after transplantation before their actual application in the clinic as a drug.

Non-human primates (NHPs) have been shown to exhibit a high degree of anatomical and physiological similarity to humans, and thus represent a valuable model for the estimation of hiPSC-CM therapy in the context of ischemic cardiac disease [18]. Here, to bridge the translational gap and advance the field toward clinical application, we generated hiPSC-CMs under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions, aiming to replicate clinical-grade cell production, and developed a therapeutic protocol in alignment with clinical standards, including the use of immunosuppressants to minimize graft rejection. In this study, we provide a systematic assessment of the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetics of hiPSC-CMs administered via intramyocardial injection in a preclinical NHP model. Innovative approaches to tracking the fate of cells and evaluating safety were incorporated, including zirconium 89 (89Zr)-pre-labeling of hiPSC-CMs with positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) imaging to dynamically monitor cell distributions and an invasive and wearable electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring device to non-invasively detect arrhythmias post-transplantation.

Methods

The work has been reported in line with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0.

Generation and characterization of hiPSC-CMs

hiPSCs were generated from the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of a healthy donor using the CTS™ CytoTune™-iPS 2.1 Sendai Reprogramming Kit with the reprogramming factors OCT4, KLF4, SOX2, and L-MYC (ThermoFisher, USA). The hiPSCs were seeded on Matrigel-coated plates and maintained in StemFlex™ Basal Medium (ThermoFisher, USA), which was supplemented with StemFlex™ Supplement. The method of cardiomyocyte differentiation from hiPSCs was similar to that previously described [9, 10], with the exception of a modification whereby 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid trisodium salt was added to enhance the differentiation efficiency. Prior to cryopreservation, the hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) were dissociated and filtered through a 100-μm nylon cell strainer (BD, 352,360, USA). Approximately 50 million cells were suspended in Cryostor® CS10 (Stemcell, 07930, USA) and cryopreserved using Cell Home-30 (KEMOSI, China).

hiPSC-CMs were characterized via immunostaining of cardiac troponin T (cTnT) and myosin light chain 2 (MLC2v), and their purity (cTnT+) was analyzed using flow cytometry. The calcium transients of hiPSC-CMs were measured using a Ca2+ recording system (IonOptix, USA) with a Ca2+ indicator (Fura-2 AM, Molecular Probes, USA) under 1-Hz electrically stimulated conditions. The field potential duration (FPD) of the hiPSC-CMs was recorded and analyzed using a CardioExcyte 96 system (Nanion, Germany). The action potentials (APs) of the hiPSC-CMs were recorded using an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices, USA) and analyzed using Clampfit 10.0 software.

Monkey MI model

All animal procedures and protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Management Committee of Jiangsu Province (approval number: IAC-A2019021-P-01). A total of 16 male rhesus monkeys, aged 3–5 years and weighing 4–6 kg, were housed in individual cages and acclimatized to the laboratory conditions for a minimum of 2 weeks prior to the commencement of the study. The animals were provided with a conventional laboratory diet and had free access to drinking water.

For major surgeries and procedures, animals were anesthetized via the intramuscular injection of Zoletil® 50 (tiletamine and zolazepam, 0.1–0.15 mL/kg), intubated with a tracheal tube, and ventilated with 2%–3% sevoflurane to maintain anesthesia. After a left thoracotomy, rhesus monkeys underwent ischemia by ligation of the middle left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery (between the first and the second diagonal artery) using a 4–0 silk suture for 3 h, followed by reperfusion. The establishment of myocardial ischemia was confirmed by signs of ST-segment elevation on ECG. During the period of ischemia, heparin was used to maintain the activated clotting time (ACT) at 250–300 s to prevent thrombogenesis.

hiPSC-CM transplantation and animal care

If there was no ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF) within 5 min of reperfusion, either hiPSC-CMs (1 × 108) suspended in 1.25 mL of 5% human serum albumin (HSA) solution (ten monkeys) or only 5% HSA vehicle (six monkeys) were intramyocardially delivered into the peri-infarct region via approximately 10 injections using a 30-gauge injection needle under surgical visualization. Two injections were administered via the same epicardial puncture, changing the trajectory of the needle for each. Subsequently, the puncture sites were compressed for several minutes to prevent leakage of the cell suspension.

The animals in both the hiPSC-CM and vehicle groups were administered immunosuppressive drugs, including intravenous methylprednisolone sodium succinate at a dose of 50 mg/kg on the day preceding surgery, followed by a maintenance daily dose of methylprednisolone tablets at a dosage of 3 mg/kg. Tacrolimus was administered at a dose of 0.2 mg/kg, commencing 5 days before cell delivery, and mycophenolate mofetil was administered at a dose of 0.1 g/kg from the day of implantation. Basiliximab (2 mg/kg i.v.) was administered before cell implantation and on the fourth day following transplantation. Cefoxitin sodium (75 mg/kg) and penicillin sodium (25,000–75,000 IU/kg) were administered within 3 days after surgery. Subsequently, cefuroxime axetil (0.25 g) and fluconazole (50 mg) were administered for a further 3–7 days, depending on the wound, to prevent bacterial and fungal infections. Morphine (0.01–0.015 mg/kg) was administered to relieve perioperative pain for no more than 3 days after surgery. Following the surgical procedure, the animals were monitored by professional veterinarians for signs of pain, infection, and symptoms of heart failure. At the end of the experiments, all the animals were euthanized by intramuscular injection of Zoletil® 50 to induce anesthesia, followed by bloodletting of the femoral artery until the animals died.

To minimize investigator bias, all surgical procedures and outcome assessments, including echocardiography, PET-CT, ECG, MRI, histological analyses, and western blot, were conducted by investigators who were blinded to group allocation until data lock.

Echocardiographic measurement

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at baseline (before infarction) and then on the 1st, 4th, and 12th weeks after MI and treatment to evaluate cardiac function using a Vivid I Portable Ultrasound Machine (GE, USA). Rhesus monkeys were sedated with Zoletil® 50 and examined in the left lateral position. The parasternal long-axis views were used to obtain 2D images. Systolic and diastolic anatomic parameters were obtained from 2D-directed M-mode tracings. All measurements were averaged over three cardiac cycles and assessed by an experienced operator who was blinded to the treatment group.

Cardiac MRI

Cardiac MRI was performed at baseline (before surgery) and then at 4 and 12 weeks after cell transplantation to evaluate cardiac function and infarct size. All MRI examinations were conducted using a 3.0 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens, Germany). Rhesus monkeys were anesthetized with Zoletil® 50, intubated with a tracheal tube, and monitored for heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiration, and temperature. The MR protocol comprised a balanced steady free-precession sequence, T2 mapping, and a phase-sensitive inversion recovery gradient-echo sequence for late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). For T2 mapping, data were obtained using the steady-state free precession technique, and three single-shot images were acquired at different T2 preparation times (0, 30, and 55 ms). The other detailed imaging parameters of the MR sequences are shown in Additional file 1: Table. S1. T2 mapping was performed with full LV coverage in the animals using a continuous stack of short-axis planes. Additionally, full LV coverage LGE and cine images were obtained in the animals. Delayed gadolinium-enhanced MRI was used to demonstrate changes in infarct size. All scans were analyzed by experienced independent reviewers who were blinded to the treatment group.

Electrocardiography

A wearable ECG monitoring device (SmartPatch, Ensense Biomedical Technologies, China) was utilized on the 1st, 3rd, 7th, 10th, 14th, 17th, 21st, 24th and 28th days after surgery to detect post-operative arrhythmic events. All ECG recordings were obtained from conscious monkeys and subsequently analyzed by trained electrophysiology cardiologists in a blinded manner. The duration of ventricular arrhythmias was recorded. The duration of arrhythmia per day (%) was calculated by dividing the time of ventricular arrhythmia occurrence per day by the total time observed. A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) was defined as a significant widening of the QRS complexes, exceeding the normal range. Ventricular tachycardia was defined as ≥ 3 consecutive PVCs originating from the ventricles, independent of atrial and atrioventricular nodal conduction. Sustained VT (SVT) was defined as a duration of > 30 s, while non-sustained VT (NSVT) was characterized by a duration of < 30 s.

89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CM tracking

Due to an insufficient supply of the target species (Macaca mulatta), four male crab-eating (Macaca fascicularis) monkeys without MI were used to investigate the bio-distribution of grafted cells by labeling them with 89Zr, a radioactive element, with PET/CT imaging. The animals were randomly assigned to two groups: Three monkeys were administered 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CMs, and another one monkey was injected with 89Zr-oxine and served as a control. The 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CMs or 89Zr-oxine alone were resuspended in 5% HSA solution and intramyocardially injected into the left ventricle via open chest surgery.

For PET/CT imaging, anesthesia was induced by intramuscular injection with Zoletil 50® and maintained by sevoflurane inhalation (2%–3%) for the duration of the PET study. The body temperature was maintained using circulating water heating pads, and the temperature, SpO2, and end-tidal CO2 were monitored throughout the procedure. The animal was positioned on the bed of a biograph PET/CT scanner (Siemens, Germany). Whole-body CT scans were acquired at each time point for PET attenuation and scatter corrections, as well as to facilitate anatomical delineation of the regions of interest (ROIs). Iterative reconstructions were performed using the same manufacturer-recommended reconstruction parameters for all PET data sets. Quantitative analysis of the ROIs was conducted using the image analysis software package PMOD (version 3.8, PMOD Technologies, Switzerland). The ROIs were delineated manually, with reference to PET and CT data of the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, bones, and brain at each time point. Subsequently, the image-derived biodistributions throughout the imaging study (2 h to 14 days post-injection) were calculated as standardized uptake values (SUV mean) in the above ROIs for all imaging data sets. The four monkeys were sacrificed 3 weeks after cell transplantation.Note: MAC () is the mean activity concentration in the ROI; ID is the total injected dose on day 0; and M is the mass of the monkey.

Histological examination

The monkey heart tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and cut into 8-μm sections for Masson’s trichrome staining, wheat germ agglutinin (WGA, Sigma-Aldrich, L4895, USA) staining, and immunofluorescent staining. Heart tissue sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight with anti-human-specific cardiac troponin I (hcTnI, Abcam, ab52862, USA), anti-cTnT (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA5-12,960, USA), anti-myosin light chain 2a (MLC2a, BD Pharmingen™, 565,496, USA), anti-MLC2v (Proteintech, 10,906–1-AP, USA), anti-connexin 43 (Sigma-Aldrich, C6219, USA), anti-CD31 (R&D Systems, AF3628, USA), anti-CD3 (Abcam, ab135372, USA), and anti-CD20 (Abcam, ab78237, USA) antibodies. The next day, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated with fluorescence-marked secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher Scientific) at room temperature for 2 h, mounted with antifade mountant containing 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and examined under a confocal microscope (SP8, Leica, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described previously [19]. Briefly, proteins extracted from heart tissues were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis in a protein electrophoresis system (BioRad, USA) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-thrombospondin-1 (TSP1, Proteintech, 18,304–1-AP, USA) and anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, Proteintech, 10,491–1-AP, USA), and subsequently with secondary antibodies. The protein bands were visualized using an Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (Li-Cor Biosciences, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

The copy number of human mitochondrial DNA in monkey tissues, including the heart and other organs such as the liver, lung, spleen, kidney, brain, and whole blood, was determined using qRT-PCR. All assay results were collated using QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR Software. The forward primer sequence was CACCGGCGCAGTCATTCTCATA, and the reverse primer sequence was GAGTCCTGTAAGTAGGAGA.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and analyzed using SPSS software (version 23.0). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. For normally distributed data, two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups, whereas for non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two groups. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of hiPSC-CMs for transplantation

The phenotype of the generated hiPSC-CMs was validated through immunofluorescence staining for cTnT and MLC2v expression (Fig. 1A, B). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that the purity of cTnT-positive cells was 97.67% ± 0.95% across 10 batches of differentiated hiPSC-CMs (Fig. 1C, D). A multi-electrode array study revealed that the hiPSC-CMs had an FPD of 300–450 ms (Fig. 1E), consistent with the findings of a previous report [20]. Live-cell imaging of the calcium handling showed that the hiPSC-CMs exhibited regular calcium transients (Fig. 1F). Moreover, the patch-clamp technique provided corroboration of the presence of typical AP features in hiPSC-CMs, including ventricular, atrial, and nodal patterns (Fig. 1G), with a ventricular cell percentage of 65.5% (Fig. 1H).

hiPSC-CMs enhanced cardiac function and reversed cardiac remodeling in ischemic NHP hearts

The cardioprotective efficiency of hiPSC-CM transplantation was evaluated in an NHP model of MI (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1: Fig. S1). There was no noticeable difference in body weight between the vehicle group and the hiPSC-CM group at any time after MI and treatment (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Echocardiography measurements (Fig. 2B) revealed that intramyocardial injection of hiPSC-CMs (1 × 108) significantly enhanced cardiac function in monkeys, including left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF), fractional shortening (LVFS), end-systolic volume (LVESV), and internal dimension at end-systole (LVIDs) at weeks 4 and 12 after MI and cell transplantation (Fig. 2C–F). At 12 weeks post-therapy, the mean heart rate was 158 beats per minute (bpm) in the vehicle group and 163 bpm in the hiPSC-CM group during functional echocardiography. Cardiac function was further evaluated via MRI. Compared to the vehicle treatment, transplantation of hiPSC-CMs significantly enhanced the LVEF at week 12 after treatment (Fig. 2G, H). Delayed gadolinium-enhanced MRI also showed a reduced cardiac infarct size in the hiPSC-CM group relative to the vehicle group at week 12 after treatment (Fig. 2I, J). These observations are supported by the results of Masson's trichrome staining, which showed that hiPSC-CM transplantation reduced the extent of cardiac fibrosis 12 weeks after MI compared to the vehicle treatment (Fig. 2K, L). Additionally, the apparent cardiac hypertrophy was progressively reversed after hiPSC-CM treatment, as evidenced by a reduction in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area detected by wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining 12 weeks after MI compared to the vehicle treatment (Fig. 3A, B). Similarly, immunofluorescence staining showed that hiPSC-CM transplantation significantly promoted angiogenesis in the infarcted hearts, as evidenced by higher CD31 expression levels (Fig. 3C, D). Through western blot and qRT-PCR, we measured the protein and mRNA expression of TSP1, a potent anti-angiogenic factor [21, 22], in the heart tissues. The results showed that the expression levels of TSP1 protein and mRNA were lower in the hiPSC-CM group than in the vehicle group (Fig. 3E-G, Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Collectively, the findings demonstrated that the intramyocardial injection of hiPSC-CMs significantly improved contractile function, decreased infarct size, reversed hypertrophy, and encouraged angiogenesis in MI monkey hearts.

Transplantation of hiPSC-CMs did not lead to severe arrhythmogenesis

To detect posttreatment arrhythmic events, three vehicle-treated and three hiPSC-CM-treated monkeys were continuously monitored using wearable electrocardiographic devices (Fig. 4A, B). The basic heart rates of the monkeys were 132 bpm in the vehicle group and 126 bpm in the cell therapy group (Additional file 1: Table S2). In the hiPSC-CM group, two monkeys showed SVT (average heart rate: 160 bpm) on day 3 after treatment, while one monkey showed NSVT (average heart rate: 150 bpm) on day 7 (Fig. 4A, C). To avoid any confounders in the incidence of cell transplantation-induced arrhythmias, no anti-arrhythmic medication was provided. These instances of cell-induced VT did not progress to fatal ventricular fibrillation and were self-restrained within the first 2 weeks after cell transplantation. Conversely, one animal in the vehicle group developed SVT on day 14 after treatment (Fig. 4A, D). Collectively, the above results demonstrate that hiPSC-CM transplantation did not lead to severe arrhythmogenesis.

hiPSC-CMs were successfully engrafted and survived after transplantation in the monkey MI hearts

Subsequently, at week 12 after cell transplantation, immunofluorescence staining of hcTnI identified the viable hiPSC-CM grafts in the monkey myocardium. Several human myocardium islands (cTnT+/hcTnI+) were found within the infarct and border region of the host myocardium (Fig. 5A). Most transplanted cells were identified as ventricular cardiomyocytes, whereas some atrial cardiomyocytes were detected in the graft mass (Fig. 5B). The gap-junction protein cardiac connexin 43 was identified to express in the transplanted cells (Fig. 5C), indicating a potential electrical integration between the transplanted cells and the host myocardium. Consistent with this, CD31 was clearly expressed in the grafted areas (Fig. 5D), indicating that revascularization had occurred within the transplanted cells. The infiltration of immune cells, including B cells (CD20+) and T cells (CD3+), was almost undetectable in the grafted areas (Fig. 5E). This suggests that, following 12 weeks of long-term transplantation, the grafted cells did not experience excessive immune rejection. Collectively, the above results demonstrate the survival and re-muscularized potential of hiPSC-CMs in monkey MI hearts.

Bio-distribution of hiPSC-CMs following transplantation

The radioactive element 89Zr, which was used to label hiPSC-CMs [23], was combined with PET imaging to examine the tissue biodistribution of the transplanted cells in a separate cohort of four monkeys (Fig. 6A). Compared to the control animal (89Zr-oxine), in which the radioactivity intensity of the heart rapidly decreased during the first 2 days, the animals intramyocardially injected with 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CMs retained a high radioactivity intensity in the hearts 2 weeks after cell transplantation (Fig. 6B, C). The real-time radioactivity in important organs, such as the lung, liver, spleen, bone, and brain, was also analyzed. In the control animal, bone was found to have absorbed free 89Zr (Fig. 6C). In contrast, the radioactivity of bone intake in the hiPSC-CM group was not as high as in the control group. It is possible that a fraction of 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CMs was stored in the heart, resulting in a slow release of 89Zr. Moreover, radioactivity in the 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CM group accumulated in the liver and spleen for up to 14 days (Fig. 6B). No radioactivity was detected in the brains of animals in either group.

The substantial quantity of human mitochondrial DNA detected in the animals’ blood after hiPSC-CM transplantation declined significantly within the first 6 h (Fig. 6D). The presence of human cells in the blood was hardly detected 24 h after cell transplantation (Fig. 6D). Following sacrifice at weeks 3, 4, or 12 after treatment, all monkeys, including the four monkeys assessed for hiPSC-CM bio-distribution and the main cohort of 16 monkeys assessed for safety and efficacy, underwent qRT-PCR analysis to determine the persistence of human mitochondrial DNA in their main organs. With the exception of the heart, no evidence of human cells was observed in any of the other organs, including the liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics of hiPSC-CM transplantation by intramyocardial injection in an NHP MI model. In the event of hiPSC-CMs being utilized in a clinical setting for the treatment of MI in the future, the number of cells produced in the laboratory will no longer be sufficient to meet patient needs. Consequently, it is imperative that these cells can be reliably produced on a large scale in a factory under GMP conditions. We generated hiPSC-CMs at HELP Therapeutics Ltd and demonstrated their high purity and good function, as evidenced by the typical calcium transients, FPD, and AP characteristics. Although proof-of-concept data can be obtained in small animals/rodents, the NHP model is still superior to other species for studying cell therapy, especially for heart disease, due to their similarity to humans in terms of behavior, physiology, biochemistry, and complexity of organ function. In line with previous reports [6, 13, 24], we confirmed that these generated hiPSC-CMs can re-muscularize monkey hearts, increase cardiac contractile function, and reverse ventricular remodeling after MI, indicating that this cell type has notable reparative potential [6, 12, 13, 24].

Ventricular arrhythmia is one of the major challenges raised by the transplantation of electroactive exogenous cardiomyocytes. In the present study, two of the three animals that received 1 × 108 hiPSC-CMs exhibited SVT on day 3 post-implantation, while one exhibited non-sustained NSVT on day 7 post-implantation. No VT or ventricular fibrillation was observed after the second week post-transplantation, and all monkeys treated with hiPSC-CMs returned to sinus rhythm. Although hiPSC-CMs have a relatively constant spontaneous beating frequency (40–60 bpm) in vitro, the VT detected in NHPs ranged from 120 to 190 bpm. It is hypothesized that the self-restrained VTs were triggered by ectopic beats at multiple sites at the graft/host interface, possibly facilitated by the presence of nodal-like cells in the graft [25]. These findings were corroborated by other research groups. Shiba et al. [13] reported that all five NHPs that received 4 × 108 iPSC-CMs developed accelerated idiopathic ventricular rhythm during the first week and shifted to SVT around day 14 after transplantation, while no further instances were observed after day 56. Liu et al. [26] reported that three out of five NHPs developed NSVT after receiving 7.5 × 108 human embryonic pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hESC-CMs), which persisted until day 25 post-transplantation. It is noteworthy that, regardless of the number of injected cells, ventricular arrhythmias reach their peak during the early transplantation period, subsequently reverting to sinus rhythm after 1 to 2 months. This observation may be consistent with the progressive maturation of the initially present nodal-like cells over time [25]. Moreover, the risk may be mitigated by optimizing the degree of cell maturation before transplantation [27] and by gene editing to knock down genes involved in depolarization while overexpressing those associated with hyperpolarization [28].

Intramyocardial injection is one of the primary choices to implant cells into the failing heart [29]. Although radio-labeling of transplanted cells to track their fate has been successfully reported in a rodent model [30], the dynamic distribution of hiPSC-CMs in animals after intramyocardial injection remains unclear. Our study is the first to utilize 89Zr for labeling hiPSC-CMs and subsequently tracking their fate in NHPs via PET/CT scanning. Although 89Zr is commonly used in oncology, its 78-h half-life, high intracellular stability, and low cytotoxicity make it particularly well-suited for the longitudinal tracking of cardiac cell engraftment in large animals. The 14-day continuous tracking demonstrated that the intramyocardially-injected 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CMs were predominantly retained within the cardiac tissue. In the remainder of the organ, high levels of radioactivity were observed in the liver and spleen. Nevertheless, subsequent qRT-PCR-based detection of human mitochondrial DNA [12] in the sacrificed monkeys revealed the absence of human cells in all organs except the heart. This finding suggests that the grafted cells did not disseminate beyond the cardiac region, ruling out potential adverse systemic effects of this therapy. It is postulated that the retention of 89Zr radioactivity in the liver and spleen may be associated with the phagocytosis of graft cell fragments by host immune cells rather than the actual presence of transplanted cells. Notably, 89Zr-labeled hiPSC-CMs were not detected in the testis of NHP, suggesting that these cardiac regenerative therapeutics have no reproductive toxicity. Furthermore, our findings indicated that free 89Zr could be taken up by bone tissue, which corroborates the previous report [31]. Therefore, the current study offers a reliable and straightforward method for investigating the early distribution of graft cells, particularly in large animals.

One of the key considerations in the use of hiPSC-CMs is the potential for immune rejection, especially given the allogeneic nature of these cells. Zhu et al. reported that 140 days after transplantation, hESC-derived cardiovascular progenitor cells were largely rejected despite the fact that they were able to restore the contractile function of NHP MI hearts [32]. To improve this, we employed a prolonged immunosuppression regimen, comprising methylprednisolone, tacrolimus, and mycophenolate mofetil, which were administered for a period of 12 weeks following cell transplantation. hiPSC-CM grafts were observed in NHP hearts 12 weeks after transplantation, although not as many as expected. One potential explanation for this is that the immunosuppression regimen was adapted from allogenic human solid organ transplant immunosuppressants rather than xenograft transplantation. A key question that remains to be answered is how to select the optimal immunosuppression regimen that balances graft survival with the minimization of drug-induced adverse events. Notably, alternative options can now be considered as a result of development in gene editing. These include the generation of hypoimmunogenic iPSC lines [33], although it should be noted that knock-down of HLA-I exposes the graft to NK-mediated cell killing [34]. Nevertheless, from a regulatory standpoint, it is notable that no tumors were observed in these cell-treated animals with severe immunosuppression.

Despite consistent reports of the efficacy of exogenous cardiomyocytes derived from pluripotent stem cells in large animal models, the underlying mechanism has yet to be elucidated. One hypothesis is the direct replacement of the irreversible myocardial loss by transplantation of exogenous force-generating cardiomyocytes [35]. However, other studies have reported minimal engraftment while concomitantly observing improved cardiac function [36]. This observation further supports the hypothesis that paracrine activation of signaling pathways may trigger angiogenesis and/or mitigate inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis [37]. In this study, the transplantation of 1 × 108 hiPSC-CMs significantly restored the cardiac performance at 4- and 12-weeks post-transplantation with the observed grafts, suggesting that both direct cell replacement and sustained paracrine effects may synergistically promote cardiac regeneration. Notably, we confirmed that hiPSC-CM transplantation effectively reduces TSP1 expression in the infarcted myocardium. As a matrix cell protein, TSP1 has been reported to be upregulated in ischemic cardiomyopathy [22, 38]. By binding to CD47, it can suppress endothelial function and angiogenesis, disrupting both the nitric oxide and VEGF signaling pathways [21, 39]. The downregulation of TSP1 levels after hiPSC-CM treatment may restore these pathways, thus enhancing cardiac neovascularization and blood flow. Additionally, TSP1-CD47 interaction can inhibit the CD47–SIRPα “don’t eat me” signal pathway, encouraging immune cell activation and phagocytosis [21, 40]. Moreover, reduced TSP1 levels may lessen interference with the CD47–SIRPα axis [41], thereby weakening immune responses and supporting the long-term retention of grafts. We have previously reported that the paracrine effect of exosomes produced by hiPSC-derived cardiac cells (comprising cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells) can reproduce much of the benefit associated with injected cardiac cells in a porcine model of MI [42]. It is conjectured that these nanoparticles (exosomes) may play a key role in the paracrine effect of hiPSC-CMs in enhancing myocardial recovery in monkeys in the present study.

Despite its strengths, our study also has several limitations. Following the ethical committee’s decision to decline repetitive open-chest surgeries for NHPs due to the high mortality rate, the hiPSC-CMs were immediately transplanted following the 3-h coronary artery ligation. Consequently, the beneficial effects observed primarily represent the effects of hiPSC-CMs in the context of acute myocardial injury with moderate infarction and cannot be directly extrapolated to a more chronic heart failure scenario. Therefore, it is essential to explore alternative, less invasive, catheter-based approaches that would be better suited to repeated cell deliveries. Additionally, our healthy ischemia–reperfusion injury model might overestimate effectiveness. Future research will evaluate hiPSC-CMs in macaques with diet-induced diabetes, obesity, and hypertension, which better reflect the severe coronary disease seen in clinical patients. Furthermore, the dose–effect relationships require further refinement.

In conclusion, we reveal that intramyocardial injection of hiPSC-CMs re-muscularized the ischemic myocardium, meaningfully restored cardiac function, and reversed structural remodeling in an NHP model. Furthermore, our study revealed for the first time that a reproducible and robust radiolabeling (89Zr) protocol enables a real-time pharmacokinetics study in an NHP model. A safety assessment of hiPSC-CMs showed no mortality, tumor formation, or severe SVT over a 3-month follow-up period. Hence, direct intramyocardial injection of hiPSC-CMs may represent a promising modality of cell transplantation therapy, paving the way for early clinical trials.
