Diagnostic accuracy of the Fonseca anamnestic index for temporomandibular disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Feihong Ma, Yayuan Wang, Dan Han, Binkui Jia, Xuehai Liu, Hongfan Li, Zhijing Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and performance of the Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI) in screening for temporomandibular disorders (TMD) through a systematic review and meta-analysis approach.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase from database inception to September 2025, using the keywords “Fonseca Anamnestic Index” and “temporomandibular disorders.” The included studies were assessed for risk of bias using QUADAS-2 tool. Threshold effects were examined with Meta-DiSc 1.4 software. Pooled sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR)and area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated via Stata 15.0. Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessments were also performed.ResultsOut of 571 records identified, nine studies involving 2,381 patients met the inclusion criteria. Spearman correlation coefficient for threshold effect was 0.376 (P = 0.332 > 0.05), indicating no significant threshold effect. Pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.89 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94) and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.57–0.88), respectively. Positive and negative likelihood ratios were 3.28 (95% CI: 2.46–4.10) and 0.14(95% CI:0.07–0.27), respectively. The pooled DOR was 26.51 (95%CI: 11.66–60.23), and the summary AUC was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.93). Subgroup analyses indicated no notable fluctuation in diagnostic performance across populations, regions, or sample sizes, while sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the results. No significant publication bias was detected by Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test (P = 0.41 > 0.05).ConclusionThe FAI exhibits high diagnostic accuracy in TMD screening and may serve as a reliable clinical adjunct tool, facilitating early detection of TMD in evidence-based practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13005-026-00601-5.Keywords: Diagnostic Accuracy, Fonseca Anamnestic Index, Temporomandibular Disorders, Systematic Review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) represent common conditions in oral and maxillofacial practice, characterized by symptoms such as joint pain, clicking sounds, and restricted mouth opening, which can markedly compromise masticatory function and quality of life. Globally, TMD prevalence is high, with regional variations reported. Epidemiological evidence has demonstrated associations between TMD and factors including masticatory habits and psychological stress [1]. The epidemiology of TMD requires a biopsychosocial perspective for comprehensive understanding. Evidence indicates that TMD-related symptoms predominantly affect young women aged 20–40 years [2], with prevalence in the general population reaching up to 50% [3]. As a multifactorial functional disorder, TMD is diagnostically challenging and controversial, warranting evaluation by multidisciplinary teams comprising multiple healthcare professionals [4]. Accurate diagnosis is critical, as misdiagnosis can adversely affect patient quality of life [5].

The Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI) is a convenient screening tool that evaluates temporomandibular joint function through a 10-item questionnaire with specific scoring criteria, facilitating preliminary assessment of TMD risk [6]. Its operational simplicity and minimal equipment requirements make it particularly suitable for primary care settings and large-scale population screening. However, reported diagnostic performance of the FAI varies across studies. Some studies report sensitivities up to 97% and specificities of 92%, whereas others document sensitivities of approximately 53% and specificities of 40%. Likewise, area under the curve (AUC) values range from 0.7 to 0.9, indicating variability from moderate to excellent diagnostic value [7]. These discrepancies may result from differences in study populations, sample sizes, and diagnostic reference standards.

Given these inconsistencies, a systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to integrate current evidence and clarify the true diagnostic value of FAI. This study comprehensively assesses the accuracy and diagnostic performance of the FAI in screening for TMD, providing evidence-based guidance for its clinical application and supporting early intervention and optimized management strategies for patients with TMD.

Materials and methods

Study compliance

In strict compliance with the PRISMA-DTA Guidelines [8], this study was carried out, and its protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD number: 420251165359).

Literature search

Comprehensive literature retrieval was performed across PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase, with coverage from the establishment of each database through September 2025. The search strategy combined the keywords “Fonseca Anamnestic Index,” “FAI,” “temporomandibular disorders,” “craniomandibular disorders”“jaw pain”“temporomandibular joint dysfunction” and “TMD” using Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” to integrate both MeSH terms and free-text words. Manual screening of reference lists from included articles was additionally conducted to avoid omission of relevant studies. A detailed search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnostic studies, including prospective and retrospective designs; (2) Study populations comprising patients suspected or confirmed with TMD, with explicit diagnostic criteria (e.g., clinical symptoms combined with imaging-based reference standards); (3) Screening or diagnostic assessment using FAI; (4) Availability of extractable diagnostic data, including sensitivity, specificity, true positive, false positive, false negative, and true negative values; (5) Studies published in English.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, guidelines, narrative reviews, letters, conference abstracts, and animal studies; (2) Studies with irrelevant disease types, objectives, or interventions; (3) Incomplete data or inability to extract valid diagnostic indicators; (4) Duplicate publications or studies with inaccessible full texts; (5) Unclear diagnostic reference standards or FAI application methods; (6) Studies with a high risk of bias.

Study selection and data extraction

Literature screening was independently performed by two investigators (Wang, Y.Y. and Ma, F.H.) using EndNote software according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Titles and abstracts were used for initial screening to exclude clearly ineligible studies. The full texts of potentially qualifying studies were then assessed to finalize inclusion. Any discrepancies were resolved by consulting a third investigator.

The same two investigators independently extracted the data, which included author(s), year of publication, country/region, sample size, gender distribution, patient age range and mean age, diagnostic reference standards, FAI scoring criteria, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, among others. If the literature did not directly report relevant metrics, calculations were derived from 2 × 2 contingency tables or using chart data extraction tools. Disagreements during extraction were resolved through discussion with a third investigator. Details of the FAI questionnaire are provided in the Appendix.

Quality assessment

The QUADAS-2 tool was employed to assess the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies around the four domains of patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Both risk of bias and applicability were evaluated for each domain, with ratings of “high,” “low,” or “unclear.” Assessments were independently performed by two investigators, and disagreements were settled via consultation with a third investigator. The results were presented in tabular and graphical formats.

Statistical analysis

Threshold effects were assessed using Meta-DiSc 1.4 software by calculating the Spearman correlation coefficient and corresponding P-value between the logit of sensitivity and the logit of (1-specificity). A P-value > 0.05 was considered indicative of no threshold effect. In the absence of threshold effects, meta-analyses were subsequently executed via Stata 15.0 software to derive the pooled sensitivity, specificity, DOR (95% CI), along with constructing the SROC curve and estimating the AUC. Subgroup analyses were designed to identify the possible sources of heterogeneity, including population subgroups (e.g., Chinese population), regional subgroups, and sample size subgroups (> 200 patients). Sensitivity analyses were carried out by excluding individual studies in sequence and re-deriving pooled estimates to evaluate whether the results were robust. Publication bias was examined using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test, with P > 0.05 indicating no significant bias. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Literature screening process and study selection

A total of 571 records were identified. Following duplicate removal using EndNote software, 318 records remained. Initial screening based on titles and abstracts excluded 302 records as ineligible. Full texts of the remaining 16 studies were retrieved for further review, and seven studies were excluded due to incomplete data or inconsistent study objectives. Ultimately, nine studies were included in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

The nine included studies were published between 2014 and 2025. One study was conducted in China, and the remaining eight were from the United States, Europe, and other countries/regions. The study designs included one prospective study and seven retrospective studies, encompassing a total of 2,381 patients. All studies utilized clinical symptoms combined with imaging modalities (e.g., CT, MRI) or arthroscopic examination as the reference standard. Quality assessment results

Quality assessment based on the QUADAS-2 tool indicated that eight studies were at low overall risk of bias, with low-risk ratings in patient selection, reference standard, and other domains. One study [14] was rated as high risk due to inappropriate application of the reference standard or incomplete follow-up. Meta-analysis results

Threshold effect

Analysis with Meta-DiSc 1.4 revealed a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.376 (P = 0.332 > 0.05), indicating no significant threshold effect and supporting the use of pooled effect estimates. The SROC curve did not display a “shoulder-arm” shape, further confirming the absence of threshold effects (Fig. 3).

SROC curve

Pooled diagnostic indicators

Meta-analysis demonstrated that the pooled sensitivity and specificity of FAI in TMD screening were 0.89 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94) and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.57–0.88), respectively. The pooled DOR was 26.51 (95%CI: 11.66–60.23), positive likelihood ratio 3.28 (95% CI: 2.46–4.10), and negative likelihood ratio 0.14(95%CI:0.07–0.27).

The SROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.93), meaning satisfactory diagnostic accuracy of the FAI (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

Subgroup analysis

Population subgroup analysis showed that in the Chinese population, pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.95 (95% CI: 0.86–1.00) and 0.88 (95% CI: 0.63–1.00), respectively. In non-Chinese populations, pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82–0.95) and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.57–0.90), with no statistically significant difference between groups. Sample size subgroup analysis revealed that studies with > 200 patients had a pooled sensitivity of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.97) and specificity of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.49–0.94),showing no significant difference compared with smaller studies. Studies with a male proportion of 50% indicated a pooled sensitivity of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.55–0.96) and specificity of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.27–1.00), suggesting that sex distribution may contribute to heterogeneity. Other subgroup analyses are presented in Fig. 7.

Subgroup analysis

Clinical applicability (Fagan nomogram)

Fagan nomogram analysis demonstrated that when the pre-test probability was set at 20%, the post-test probability increased to 48% following FAI screening. These findings indicate that the FAI provides substantial clinical utility in practice, effectively enhancing the diagnostic efficiency for TMD (Fig. 8).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses revealed that both the goodness-of-fit assessment and bivariate normality evaluation (Fig. 9a and b) supported the robustness of the bivariate diagnostic model. Influence analysis identified one outlying study, which was further confirmed through outlier detection (Fig. 9c and d). The study by de Santis et al. [14] was classified as a discrepant value. After exclusion of the study, the pooled sensitivity increased from 0.89 to 0.92, while pooled specificity slightly decreased from 0.76 to 0.75. These marginal changes indicate that the overall results remained stable, further confirming the reliability of the meta-analysis findings.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provided a comprehensive evaluation of the FAI’s diagnostic performance in TMD screening. The pooled results indicate that FAI demonstrates favorable diagnostic accuracy and is capable of effectively distinguishing patients with TMD from healthy individuals. As a convenient and non-invasive screening tool, FAI may serve as a practical option for initial clinical assessment, facilitating rapid identification of individuals at high risk for TMD and supporting early intervention strategies.

The pooled findings are largely consistent with those reported in most original clinical studies. For example, Yap et al. [11] reported a sensitivity of 94.6% and specificity of 87.7% in the Chinese population, while Kaynak et al. [13] documented the sensitivity and specificity values of 93.6% and 83.1%, respectively. These results align with the high pooled sensitivity observed in this analysis, collectively confirming the excellent discriminative ability of FAI for TMD screening. High sensitivity is particularly advantageous in clinical scenarios where early TMD manifestations are subtle, such as intermittent joint clicking or mild masticatory discomfort, as it substantially reduces the risk of missed diagnoses and prevents progression to chronic pain syndromes or persistent functional limitations. In contrast, studies by de Santis et al. [14], reported considerably lower sensitivity (53.4%), which differs markedly from the pooled estimates. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in study population characteristics. In that study, the mean participant age was only 8.18 years, and the anatomical and physiological features of the temporomandibular joint in children differ substantially from those in adults [17]. Moreover, TMD in pediatric populations predominantly manifests as functional disturbances, such as transient limitations in mouth opening, whereas structural abnormalities, including disc displacement, occur less frequently. Several FAI questionnaire items (e.g., “presence of joint clicking for more than six months”) emphasize symptoms associated with structural pathology, potentially reducing its sensitivity for detecting functional disorders in children. Additionally, children may exhibit limited comprehension and response accuracy when completing self-reported questionnaires, introducing response bias and affecting score validity. Furthermore, the use of RDC/TMD as the diagnostic reference standard in that study may have contributed to outcome variability. Compared with DC/TMD, RDC/TMD applies broader definitions for symptoms such as joint sounds, whereas DC/TMD employs more refined diagnostic thresholds for muscle-related pain and disc displacement, which more closely align with the symptom dimensions assessed by FAI. These methodological differences may collectively account for the observed heterogeneity in diagnostic performance. Moreover, a study by Stasiak et al. [7] conducted in a Brazilian population reported a markedly low specificity of only 26.0% for the FAI, which differs substantially from the pooled specificity of 0.76 observed in this analysis. Further examination suggests that this discrepancy may be attributable to the heterogeneous composition of the study population, which included mixed cases of muscular disorders, disc displacement, and other temporomandibular joint pathologies. Notably, patients with comorbid chronic headache and cervical spine disorders were not excluded. Symptoms associated with these comorbidities such as cervicogenic referred pain and headache exacerbation during mastication may overlap with typical TMD manifestations, thereby increasing the likelihood of false-positive responses to FAI items such as “pain during chewing” and “presence of facial tenderness.” This overlap may ultimately result in an underestimation of specificity. These findings underscore the importance of integrating detailed medical history assessment when applying FAI in clinical practice to minimize confounding from comorbid conditions. Where necessary, adjunctive specialist evaluations, including cervical spine radiography or standardized headache assessment tools, should be considered to improve diagnostic discrimination.

The FAI offers superior practicality and user-friendliness compared to the RDC/TMD, making it the more efficient tool for screenings in primary care and epidemiology. First, RDC/TMD, as a diagnostic reference standard, requires comprehensive assessment by trained clinicians integrating clinical examination findings with imaging results, a process that is inherently complex, time-intensive, and demands a high level of professional expertise (PMID: 1298767). In contrast, the FAI is administered as a concise 10-item questionnaire. This tool requires no specialized equipment or clinical staff, as it can be either self-reported by patients or quickly delivered by primary care providers, leading to substantial reductions in both time and financial resources. Second, the FAI is more consistent with the biopsychosocial model. It captures not only physical symptoms like joint pain and clicking but also includes dimensions of emotional tension and stress, thereby providing a more holistic understanding of the multifactorial etiology of TMD (PMID: 26118509). The RDC/TMD prioritizes somatic manifestations and structural pathology, with relatively limited consideration of psychological factors. Finally, FAI scores are numerical, allowing for quick risk stratification (high/low risk) in large-scale studies, whereas RDC/TMD’s qualitative diagnoses are not well-suited for this purpose. Importantly, the FAI is a screening tool, not a replacement for formal diagnosis, e.g., RDC/TMD or DC/TMD. A positive FAI result must be followed by a professional clinical and imaging assessment to avoid misdiagnosis.

The diagnostic performance of the FAI is fundamentally rooted in the close alignment between its questionnaire items and the core pathophysiological and psychological dimensions of TMD. Through ten targeted questions, the FAI systematically scores temporomandibular joint functional status, encompassing hallmark symptoms such as joint pain, restricted mouth opening, and impaired masticatory function [18], all of which are closely linked to the underlying pathological changes of TMD. The principal pathological mechanisms of TMD include masticatory muscle dysfunction, articular disc displacement, and osseous alterations [19]. Correspondingly, specific FAI items directly reflect these pathological processes. For instance, the item assessing “joint clicking” serves as an indirect indicator of abnormal positional relationships between the articular disc and the mandibular condyle. In cases of anterior disc displacement, abnormal disc–condyle interactions during mouth opening frequently produce clicking or catching sensations, a clinical sign with a reported sensitivity exceeding 85% in imaging-based studies [20]. Similarly, the item addressing “pain during chewing” is closely associated with masticatory muscle spasm or inflammatory changes. During muscle spasm, excessive contractile force amplifies mechanical strain on muscle fibers, particularly when chewing hard food, thereby eliciting pain. In patients with synovitis, elevated intra-articular pressure and increased joint loading during mastication further exacerbate pain symptoms [21]. Clinical evidence has further demonstrated significant correlations between FAI scores and imaging findings of the temporomandibular joint, such as disc displacement identified on magnetic resonance imaging [22, 23]. Higher FAI scores have been shown to indirectly reflect greater severity of joint pathology. Importantly, the FAI also incorporates items related to emotional tension and perceived difficulty in mastication, thereby capturing a defining feature of TMD as a psychosomatic disorder. Psychological stress functions not only as a risk factor but also as a frequent comorbidity in TMD. Stress-related activation of the hypothalamic - pituitary- adrenal axis can induce sympathetic overactivity in the masticatory muscles, leading to muscle spasm, while simultaneously amplifying pain perception, thus influencing both the onset and progression of TMD [19, 24]. Collectively, by integrating biological, psychological, and social dimensions within a concise screening instrument, the FAI effectively reflects the multifactorial etiological framework of TMD. This comprehensive coverage of TMD-related mechanisms likely underpins the high diagnostic accuracy observed under diverse clinical settings.

This study provides robust evidence-based support for the clinical application of the FAI, which is reflected in three main aspects. First, the FAI is simple to administer and does not require specialized equipment; assessment can be completed solely through a questionnaire. This feature makes it particularly suitable for large-scale population screening in primary healthcare settings and community health service centers, enabling efficient identification of individuals at high risk for TMD and facilitating early intervention. Second, within specialized dental and oral medicine hospitals, the FAI may function as an efficient triage instrument, allowing patients with higher scores to be prioritized for referral to specialists for further evaluation, thereby optimizing diagnostic workflows and improving overall healthcare efficiency. Third, among asymptomatic individuals with latent disease, the FAI may serve as an effective screening and triage tool to support rational allocation of medical resources and ensure timely and accurate diagnosis of suspected cases.

Several limitations should be acknowledged and addressed in future investigations. First, nine studies were included; although this number meets the basic requirements for meta-analysis, the overall sample size remains relatively limited, potentially constraining statistical power and the robustness of the pooled conclusions. Second, despite subgroup analyses, the sources of heterogeneity were not fully elucidated. Potential contributors include differences in FAI versions (e.g., SFAI vs. standard FAI), regional variations in cultural background and clinical practice, and discrepancies in diagnostic reference standards (RDC/TMD vs. DC/TMD). However, further subgroup analyses could not be conducted due to limitations in the available primary data. Third, one included study was assessed as having a high risk of bias, which may have led to overestimation of the diagnostic accuracy of the FAI and affected the reliability of the pooled estimates. Fourth, Chinese-language publications were not included, and only English-language studies were analyzed, introducing the possibility of selection bias.

In light of these limitations, several directions merit further exploration. First, large-scale, multicenter prospective studies employing unified and internationally recognized diagnostic criteria for TMD (such as DC/TMD) are warranted to further validate the diagnostic performance of the FAI and to establish more generalizable cutoff values. Second, investigation of combined diagnostic strategies integrating the FAI with other assessment modalities, including imaging techniques or portable electromyography, may enhance diagnostic accuracy and efficiency while maintaining practicality. Third, stratified analyses across different age groups and ethnic populations are needed to clarify the diagnostic performance of the FAI in specific subpopulations. Fourth, further refinement of the FAI scoring system is encouraged, with optimization of item selection based on clinical applicability and development of more efficient short-form versions (e.g., SFAI), followed by rigorous validation of their diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusion

In summary, the FAI demonstrates notable advantages for screening TMD, characterized by high sensitivity, strong practicality, and low cost. As such, it represents a valuable adjunctive screening tool for use in primary care settings and large-scale population-based assessments. Nevertheless, considering the scarcity of available studies and the presence of heterogeneity, its diagnostic performance requires further confirmation through high-quality research. Future efforts incorporating standardized study designs and combined diagnostic approaches are expected to further enhance the clinical utility of the FAI in the diagnosis and management of TMD, thereby providing stronger support for clinical practice.
