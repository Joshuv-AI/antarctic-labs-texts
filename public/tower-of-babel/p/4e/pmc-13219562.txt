From bench to bedside: a novel suture-augmented prosthesis for greater trochanteric fixation in osteoporotic hip arthroplasty
Authors: Maolin Yang, Junjun Yang, Xu Wang, Zhangwei Wu, Sijie Bian, Rui Sheng, Faxue Liao, Kunpeng Qin, Chi Zhang, Huaiping Yuan, Xiaoqin Ding, Congying Xie, Guangwen Ma, Jun Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStable fixation of the greater trochanter during hip arthroplasty for unstable osteoporotic intertrochanteric fractures remains a significant challenge. Conventional techniques depend on securing the bone-implant interface, which is particularly compromised in osteoporotic bone. Here, we propose a novel conceptual approach that establishes a direct mechanical bridge from the abductor tendon to the prosthesis, thereby reducing reliance on the fragile bone-implant interface.MethodsIn this two-stage translational study, we progressed from biomechanical validation to clinical application. First, using a decalcified caprine femoral model simulating osteoporosis, three fixation constructs were compared: locking plate (LP), suture-augmented locking plate (LPSA), and Kirschner-wire tension band (KWTB). Ultimate load and construct stability were evaluated. Biomechanical testing confirmed the principle of suture-mediated load sharing and highlighted the intrinsic weakness of screw fixation in osteoporotic bone. Guided by these results, we designed a novel femoral prosthesis that eliminates screw fixation, employs sutures as the primary load-bearing element, and incorporates integrated suture anchor tunnels. This prosthesis was then assessed in a retrospective series of 15 consecutive elderly patients with osteoporotic intertrochanteric fractures. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Harris Hip Score (HHS).ResultsBiomechanically, the LPSA construct sustained a significantly higher ultimate load than the LP construct in decalcified bone (P < 0.001) and exhibited a greater load at 2-mm displacement than the KWTB construct (P < 0.001). Clinically, all patients achieved fracture union without prosthesis-related complications. The mean HHS at final follow-up was 88.27 ± 6.60, and 80% of patients regained independent ambulation.ConclusionsA prosthesis-anchored, suture-mediated “tendon-to-prosthesis” load pathway provides reliable stabilization of the greater trochanter in osteoporotic bone. Biomechanical validation and favorable early clinical outcomes support the potential of this novel fixation strategy.

Introduction

Hip arthroplasty is an established treatment for unstable intertrochanteric fractures, particularly in elderly patients with osteoporosis [1, 2]. This approach enables early weight-bearing and reduces complications associated with prolonged immobility [3]. In this population, selecting an appropriate femoral stem—cemented or cementless—is critical, as stem design can influence prosthesis stability and clinical outcomes [4]. However, achieving secure fixation of the greater trochanter remains challenging. Inadequate fixation may result in complications such as prosthetic dislocation, abductor dysfunction, persistent pain, and gait abnormalities, all of which can substantially compromise patient prognosis and quality of life [5–10].

Traditional fixation methods, including wire tension bands and cable systems, have been widely used [11–15]. However, these approaches are associated with relatively high failure rates in osteoporotic bone, primarily due to wire fatigue, bone lysis, prosthesis wear, suboptimal load distribution, and limited resistance to displacement [12–17]. Although plates theoretically provide superior biomechanical stability, their use in the greater trochanter fixation is limited by the lack of dedicated designs and the frequent necessity for unicortical screw placement due to prosthetic interference [18, 19]. Additionally, clinical studies have reported suboptimal outcomes, including persistent trochanteric pain, nonunion, mechanical failure, and overall compromised functional results [20, 21]. A fundamental limitation of these techniques is their reliance on securing the bone fragment itself, which may be insufficient to counteract the substantial tensile forces exerted by the abductor tendons.

On the basis of these considerations, we hypothesized that stable fixation of the greater trochanter in osteoporotic bone could be enhanced by redirecting tensile forces from the abductor tendon directly to the prosthesis, thereby reducing reliance on fragile bone. The aim of this study was to validate this principle biomechanically and to investigate its preliminary clinical translation. Therefore, we conducted a two-stage translational study: the first stage focused on biomechanical validation of suture-mediated load redistribution in a simulated osteoporotic bone model, while the second stage assessed early clinical outcomes using a novel femoral prosthesis informed by these biomechanical findings.

Materials and methods

Biomechanical study

Specimen preparation

A total of 36 fresh adult caprine femora (age, 1–2 years; weight, 40–45 kg) were obtained from a licensed abattoir. Specimens were carefully dissected to preserve abductor tendon attachments. Pre-screening radiographic evaluation, including anteroposterior and lateral radiographs, as well as thin-slice (0.625 mm) high-resolution computed tomography (CT), was performed to exclude occult fractures, pathological abnormalities, or congenital defects. All imaging assessments were conducted in a blinded manner by two independent, experienced musculoskeletal radiologists, who were unaware of subsequent group allocation.

Morphometric and bone density analysis

Prior to biomechanical testing, morphometric measurements were performed using digital vernier calipers (precision, 0.02 mm; Fig. 1A), with each parameter recorded in triplicate to ensure reliability. Measured dimensions included the greater trochanter and femoral shaft. Decalcified bone (DB) model establishment

The 36 caprine femora were assigned to a raw bone (RB) control group or a DB group, with the latter used to simulate osteoporotic conditions. Decalcified specimens were initially fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin at 25 °C for 24 h, followed by rinsing in deionized water. A standardized 3 mm cortical defect was drilled in the piriform fossa to facilitate decalcification (Fig. 2A). Specimens were then continuously immersed in 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, pH 7.2) for 8 days, with daily solution replacement. Subsequent CT imaging confirmed a substantial reduction in BMD, as indicated by decreased HU values within predefined regions of interest.Fig. 2Experimental workflow for evaluating femoral fixation constructs. The model of greater trochanteric fractures

A standardized osteotomy was created at the base of the greater trochanter in two stages (Fig. 2B). First, a 4.0-mm transverse tunnel was drilled through the trochanter to replicate the tendon insertion site for mechanical loading. Subsequently, an osteotomy was performed along the femoral neck-greater trochanter junction at a 45° angle relative to the femoral shaft axis, generating a fracture pattern representative of clinically observed greater trochanteric fractures.

Fixation techniques

All specimen preparation and fixation procedures were performed by a single experienced orthopedic surgeon following standardized protocols. Following group allocation, specimens were randomized into three subgroups corresponding to the fixation techniques (Fig. 2C–E). For the suture-augmented locking plate (LPSA) construct, an anatomically contoured lateral locking plate (LP) was positioned along the femur and initially secured with screws. The proximal fragment was fixed with two locking screws, and the distal shaft was stabilized using one cortical and two locking screws. Suture augmentation was achieved by passing three #5 ETHIBOND Excel™ sutures (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) through predrilled tunnels in the proximal plate segment and then through parallel bone tunnels in the greater trochanteric fragment in a figure-of-eight configuration. The LP group used the identical plate and screw configuration without suture augmentation. In the Kirschner wire tension band (KWTB) group, two parallel Kirschner wires were inserted across the fracture, followed by a figure-of-eight cerclage wire applied around the K-wire ends and the trochanter.

Biomechanical testing

Each construct was subjected to uniaxial tensile loading using a materials testing system. The distal femur was potted and rigidly fixed to the machine base. A custom rod, inserted into the transverse trochanteric tunnel, was connected to the actuator via a universal joint to ensure pure tensile loading along the femoral axis (Fig. 2E). A displacement-controlled protocol was applied at 20 mm per minute until construct failure. Load and displacement were continuously recorded, and testing was terminated upon either a sustained load drop exceeding 30% of the peak load or a displacement of 10 mm. Primary outcome measures were the ultimate load to failure and the load sustained at 2 mm of displacement.

Clinical case series

Prosthesis design rationale and description

Guided by the biomechanical results—which demonstrated the limited reliability of screw fixation in osteoporotic bone and the load-sharing advantage of sutures—we developed a novel femoral prosthesis. The design was based on three key principles: (1) elimination of screw fixation into the osteoporotic bone fragment; (2) use of high-strength sutures as the primary load-bearing element; (3) integration of suture anchor points directly into the prosthesis. Guided by this design philosophy, our team collaborated with Kuanyue Xinshengshi Medical Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. to develop a custom cemented femoral prosthesis featuring a cylindrical projection with six integrated suture tunnels on its collar (Fig. 3G, H), creating a direct tendon–suture–prosthesis load pathway. Patient selection

We conducted a retrospective review of medical records for all patients with intertrochanteric fractures treated at our institution between July 2023 and September 2024 using the novel prosthesis. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 70 years; (2) primary, unilateral, closed intertrochanteric fracture; and (3) diagnosed osteoporosis, defined as BMD T-score ≤ −2.5 at the lumbar spine or proximal femur. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) pathological or open fractures; (2) concomitant polytrauma; (3) severe systemic comorbidities; and (4) incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up. Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, medications, injury mechanism, fracture laterality, and Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) fracture classification, were extracted from electronic medical records. Preoperative evaluation included anteroposterior hip radiographs and CT scans (Fig. 3A–F, L–N).

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by a single senior orthopedic surgeon following a standardized protocol to ensure consistency. Prior to prosthesis implantation, four #5 ETHIBOND Excel™ sutures (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) were passed through the integrated suture tunnels in the prosthesis collar. The cemented femoral prosthesis was implanted using conventional techniques, including appropriate canal preparation, cementation, and positioning. Once the prosthesis was in place, each suture limb was passed through the abductor tendon insertion in an anteroposterior direction. A Kessler suture pattern was employed to maximize tissue purchase and distribute tensile load within the tendon. After achieving anatomical reduction of the greater trochanteric fragment, the sutures were tensioned under direct visualization to ensure secure fragment apposition to the prosthesis collar and were then sequentially tied from anterior to posterior (Fig. 3G–K).

Postoperative assessment

Early postoperative mobilization involved protected partial weight-bearing using a front-wheeled walker under the supervision of a physical therapist. Patients were closely monitored for postoperative complications, which were systematically recorded. Functional outcomes were evaluated quantitatively using the Harris Hip Score (HHS).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. For the biomechanical study, differences among the three fixation groups within each bone quality cohort were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. BMD, expressed in CT-derived HU values, was compared between RBs and DBs using an independent samples t-test. For the clinical case series, descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and outcomes. Due to the exploratory nature and small sample size, no comparative statistical testing was performed against a control group. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Biomechanical evaluation

Biomechanical assessment commenced with validation of the experimental model. Morphometric analysis confirmed baseline homogeneity across all six experimental groups (RB: LPSA, LP, KWTB; DB: LPSA, LP, KWTB), with no significant intergroup differences in femoral parameters prior to intervention (P > 0.05 for all comparisons; Fig. 1B–F, Table 1). Although morphological parameters were comparable, baseline BMD, as measured by CT-derived HU values, was significantly reduced following decalcification. The decalcification protocol resulted in a 26.46% reduction in mean radiodensity, with the DB cohort (591.01 ± 58.27 HU) demonstrating significantly lower HU values than the RB cohort (803.66 ± 50.51 HU; P < 0.001; Fig. 1G, H). Within the DB cohort, baseline BMD was unevenly distributed among the fixation subgroups: the KWTB group exhibited significantly higher HU values (651.69 ± 46.42 HU) than both the LPSA (570.71 ± 47.60 HU; P = 0.041) and LP (550.61 ± 15.85 HU; P = 0.007) groups (Fig. 1I). In contrast, no significant differences in baseline BMD were observed among the three fixation groups within the RB cohort (P > 0.05; Fig. 1J). Early stability assessed at 2 mm displacement revealed clinically relevant differences within the DB cohort (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B). Both LPSA (436.72 ± 137.45 N) and LP constructs (354.20 ± 60.11 N) sustained significantly higher loads than the KWTB construct (203.52 ± 55.15 N; P < 0.001 and P = 0.013, respectively), whereas no significant difference was observed between LPSA and LP (P > 0.05). Consistent with the ultimate load data, no significant differences were detected among the three fixation groups in the RB cohort (P > 0.05; Fig. 4C).

Failure mode analysis further elucidated construct behavior. In the DB group, LPSA constructs failed via either traction point avulsion at the suture–bone interface (n = 3) or screw cutout from osteoporotic bone (n = 3), mirroring patterns observed in the RB group. Within the RB cohort, LP constructs predominantly failed through screw cutout (n = 4), with the remaining two specimens exhibiting traction point avulsion. In contrast, screw cutout was the exclusive failure mode for all LP constructs in the DB cohort (n = 6). KWTB constructs demonstrated a uniform failure mechanism and migration and extraction of the Kirschner wires, across both bone quality cohorts (n = 6 in RB, n = 6 in DB; Fig. 5). Patient demographics and perioperative characteristics

The mean follow-up duration was 12.67 ± 1.88 months (range 9–16 months). At final assessment, the mean HHS was 88.27 ± 6.60, indicating good-to-excellent functional outcomes. Five patients (33.33%) reported occasional mild pain, which did not limit daily activities. In terms of ambulation, 3 patients (20%) required a cane, while the remaining 12 patients (80%) were able to ambulate independently.

Postoperative complications were rare. One patient (6.67%) experienced cerebral infarction on postoperative day 5, which was promptly diagnosed and managed, resulting in good functional recovery without major residual deficits. Another patient (6.67%) sustained a new osteoporotic fracture (L1 vertebral compression fracture) from a ground-level fall 5 months postoperatively. This event was unrelated to the index hip surgery or implant fixation and was successfully managed with percutaneous vertebroplasty. Importantly, no cases of deep surgical site infection, nonunion, prosthesis failure, or revision surgery related to greater trochanteric fixation were observed.

Discussion

This two-stage translational study introduces and preliminarily validates a novel strategy for greater trochanter fixation in osteoporotic bone. The approach bypasses the fragile bone-implant interface by establishing a direct tendon–suture–prosthesis load pathway. Unlike conventional methods (e.g., wires, cables, or plates) that depend on osteoporotic bone for secure fixation, this technique transfers the primary tensile load from the abductor tendon directly to the prosthesis via high-strength sutures. By redistributing these forces, the osteoporotic bone fragment is mechanically shielded, potentially fostering a more favorable environment for healing.

The use of traditional cable cerclage and tension band constructs has declined in use for unstable intertrochanteric fractures due to their relatively high mechanical failure rates [17]. Although LP can maintain mechanical integrity under unicortical fixation owing to their inherent angular stability [20, 21], their efficacy for greater trochanteric fractures remains controversial. Previous studies indicate that the risk of internal fixation failure rises with increasing osteoporosis severity [24]. In patients with femoral prostheses or severe osteoporosis, cable cerclage has been suggested as an adjunct or alternative to conventional screw fixation when using LPs [25]. Consistent with these concerns, the mechanical performance of the LP group in the present study highlights the limitations of screw-based fixation in osteoporotic bone. Furthermore, the superficial position of the greater trochanter and its extensive tendinous attachments may complicate plate application and elevate the risk of soft tissue irritation [26, 27], potentially impeding union, particularly in cases with small fragments or limited screw purchase [21].

Reported pullout strengths for suture anchors in cadaveric hip studies range from 200 to 400 N [28, 29]. Putnam et al. [29] demonstrated that suture anchor repair of the hip abductors yielded significantly lower failure loads in decorticated greater trochanter specimens (152.3 ± 60.2 N) compared with non-decorticated specimens (206.7 ± 75.0 N), with all failures occurring at the bone–anchor interface, consistent with our experimental findings. In the LPSA group, suture rupture was absent, and failures occurred primarily through traction point avulsion at the suture–bone interface or screw cutout, supporting the load-redistributing role of the sutures. In contrast, exclusive screw cutout failures in the LP group suggested that mechanical stability alone may be insufficient in osteoporotic bone. These results align with the “stress-shielding reduction” hypothesis of Stone and Namdari [30], in which suture augmentation mitigated stress concentration at the screw–bone interface. The KWTB group exhibited high ultimate load but relatively poor resistance to early displacement, indicating limited capacity to prevent micromotion, a key factor in clinical healing. Notably, the higher ultimate load in the KWTB group may partly reflect its higher baseline BMD relative to other DB subgroups, as noted in the study limitations. In the RB group, no significant differences were observed among the three fixation techniques (P > 0.05), suggesting that suture augmentation provides limited benefit in bone of normal density, consistent with Borgiani et al. [31], who proposed that healthy bone’s intrinsic healing capacity may reduce the need for additional mechanical reinforcement.

Guided by biomechanical findings, the novel prosthesis was engineered to overcome the primary limitations of conventional fixation. In osteoporotic bone, standard plate constructs consistently failed via screw cutout, highlighting their inherent instability. Augmenting the plate with sutures created a hybrid load-sharing construct, evidenced by mixed failures at the suture–bone interface and screw cutout, yet the fundamental weakness of bone–screw fixation persisted. In response, the prosthesis was redesigned to eliminate screw fixation into the bone fragment, elevate sutures as the primary load-bearing element, and integrate suture anchors directly into the implant. This configuration establishes a tendon–suture–prosthesis load pathway, effectively suspending the bone fragment within a protected mechanical environment, thereby mitigating destructive tensile forces and promoting conditions favorable for fracture healing.

Building on the principle that multiple sutures enhance mechanical stability and clinical outcomes [32], an optimized four-suture configuration was applied in the clinical series. The mean final HHS of 88.27 indicates good-to-excellent functional recovery, particularly notable given the advanced age and frailty of the cohort. These results suggest that the fixation construct provided sufficient stability to support effective rehabilitation and functional recovery. The observation that 20% of patients required a cane at follow-up likely reflects preexisting functional limitations and common geriatric comorbidities, such as reduced pre-fracture mobility, sarcopenia, and fear of falling [33–36], rather than a deficiency of the fixation technique. Importantly, the primary surgical goal of establishing a stable foundation for safe mobilization was achieved, as evidenced by the absence of implant-related failures or reoperations throughout follow-up.

Although the prosthesis design was informed by biomechanical findings, it is important to note that validation experiments were conducted using a plate-based construct rather than the final prosthesis. Consequently, translation from bench to clinic represents a conceptual step guided by, but not directly validated on, the novel prosthesis. The most compelling clinical evidence supporting this proof-of-concept is the absence of implant-related failures or reoperations in this high-risk cohort. This outcome indicates that the tendon–suture–prosthesis load pathway can effectively neutralize abductor forces, allowing the osteoporotic bone fragment to heal within a mechanically protected environment. By directly addressing the core challenge of mechanical failure in osteoporotic bone, these results suggest that establishing a robust tendon-to-prosthesis load path can relieve the fragment from destructive tensile forces and promote fracture healing [37]. Favorable functional recovery, with a mean HHS of 88.27, further demonstrated that prosthesis-anchored fixation can restore abductor mechanics while avoiding the pain and instability commonly associated with conventional fixation failure. These findings align with prior reports that optimized suture techniques reduce revision rates [38] and that robust initial fixation is a key predictor of avoiding reoperation [39]. Overall, the absence of implant-related failures in this preliminary series provides supportive evidence for the biomechanical hypothesis: a reinforced tendon–suture–prosthesis construct can withstand physiological loads, create favorable conditions for healing, and prevent catastrophic clinical failure [40].

A new vertebral fracture in one patient (6.67%) underscored the systemic nature of osteoporosis and highlighted the need for comprehensive medical management, rather than reflecting a limitation of the surgical technique [41]. A postoperative cerebral infarction was promptly recognized and managed, and was unrelated to the prosthesis. Importantly, no fixation-related complications were observed, supporting the conclusion that suture augmentation improved mechanical stability without introducing additional device-related risks.

This study has several limitations. First, the clinical component represents an initial proof-of-concept series involving a relatively small cohort of 15 patients from a single institution, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, although the decalcified caprine model effectively simulated osteoporotic bone, it cannot fully replicate the complex biological environment of in vivo fracture healing. Moreover, baseline BMD was not evenly distributed among the fixation subgroups within the decalcified cohort: the KWTB group exhibited significantly higher CT values than the LPSA and LP groups, potentially leading to an overestimation of its ultimate load capacity. This imbalance reflects that randomization without BMD stratification resulted in unequal bone quality across subgroups within the osteoporotic model. Importantly, comparisons between the LPSA and LP groups, which had comparable BMD, remain valid. Third, regarding translational relevance, biomechanical validation was performed using a plate-based construct, whereas the clinical application employed a modified prosthesis with integrated suture tunnels. Although the load-redistribution principle informed the prosthesis design, the final prosthesis was not independently tested biomechanically. Therefore, the translation from bench to clinic represents a conceptual step guided by, rather than directly validated on, the novel prosthesis. Finally, the follow-up period, while sufficient to evaluate fracture healing and early prosthesis performance, is not long enough to assess long-term survivorship or potential late complications. These limitations underscore directions for future research. The promising early results reported here warrant, and should be followed by, a larger-scale, prospective comparative study with extended follow-up to establish the long-term efficacy and potential advantages of this technique relative to current standards of care.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the challenge of greater trochanteric fixation in osteoporotic bone could be addressed by shifting the fixation strategy from reliance on fragile bone to direct prosthesis-based support. The biomechanically informed principle of suture-mediated load redistribution was successfully incorporated into a novel prosthesis design, establishing a reliable tendon–suture–prosthesis load-transfer pathway. This approach provides a promising solution for achieving stable fixation and functional recovery in elderly patients with osteoporosis and lays the groundwork for further evaluation in larger, comparative studies.
