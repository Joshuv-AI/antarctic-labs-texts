Medication-Related Impacts on Pediatric Bone Health
Authors: Charu Jain, Luca Valdivia, Laurel Wong, Sheena C Ranade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone health during childhood and adolescence is crucial because this is when individuals develop most of their lifetime bone mass. Disruptions in bone metabolism during these years, especially those caused by medications, can have long-lasting effects. Many drugs routinely prescribed to children, including glucocorticoids, antiepileptics, chemotherapeutic agents, and psychotropic medications, have been shown to harm bone development. These effects include lower bone mineral density (BMD), impaired bone growth, and a higher risk of fractures. However, specific medications can help strengthen bones in high-risk children. Bisphosphonates, commonly used in treating osteogenesis imperfecta and glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, have proven effective in increasing BMD and lowering fracture rates. These drugs slow bone resorption and are generally well tolerated in children, with few serious long-term side effects. Pediatricians and other child health providers play a key role in identifying children at risk for poor bone health and in implementing preventive strategies. Given the lifelong implications of suboptimal bone health, the purpose of the review is to summarize the skeletal effects of medications, which should be an integral part of pediatric care.Key Concepts(1) Many commonly prescribed pediatric medications, such as glucocorticoids, antiepileptics, and psychotropics, can significantly impair bone development and increase fracture risk.(2) Medication-induced bone loss in children can occur through multiple mechanisms, including impaired osteoblast activity, altered calcium or vitamin D metabolism, and hormonal disruption.(3) Preventive strategies such as optimizing nutrition, promoting weight-bearing activity, and screening high-risk patients can mitigate bone loss during long-term therapy.(4) Bisphosphonates can be effective in treating pediatric osteoporosis but should be reserved for select cases and managed under the supervision of healthcare providers.(5) Coordination between pediatricians and other healthcare specialists is essential to ensure bone health is considered in the context of chronic medication use.Keywords: Pediatric bone health, Medication-induced bone loss, Pediatric osteoporosis

Introduction

Bone health during childhood and adolescence is essential for achieving peak bone mass, which greatly affects lifelong skeletal strength and fracture risk [1]. Although many factors influence bone development, medication exposure is an important and sometimes overlooked factor in pediatric bone health. A growing body of evidence has identified numerous commonly prescribed pediatric medications that can negatively impact bone metabolism, reduce mineral density, and increase fracture risk during critical periods of skeletal growth [2,3]. These include drugs such as glucocorticoids, anticonvulsants, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), psychotropics, and antineoplastic agents [4]. Additionally, medications used in specialized pediatric populations, such as tacrolimus after transplant or hormonal therapies like depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (Depo-Provera) and gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues (e.g., Lupron), present unique challenges to maintaining bone health [5,6].

Understanding how medications influence bone development is crucial for balancing therapeutic benefits with skeletal risks in pediatric care. This review summarizes current evidence on drugs with known or suspected effects on pediatric bone health, highlighting mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and strategies to mitigate risks (Table 1). We emphasize common agents such as PPIs, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), stimulants, and antiepileptics, along with less frequently discussed drugs like tacrolimus, methotrexate, and anticoagulants. Our goal is to assist clinicians, families, and healthcare teams in supporting bone health among children on long-term medication therapy.

Acid-reducing agents

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), including omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, and rabeprazole, have become some of the most commonly prescribed medications in pediatric populations [7]. Proton pump inhibitors greatly decrease gastric acid production by irreversibly blocking the H+/K+-adenosine triphosphatase (the proton pump) in the stomach's parietal cells [8]. This renders them highly effective pharmacological treatments for conditions such as symptomatic gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, eosinophilic esophagitis, healing of erosive esophagitis, and Helicobacter pylori eradication.

While PPIs are generally well tolerated, concerns have arisen about their link to increased fracture risk and osteoporosis [9]. A meta-analysis by Liu et al. found that PPI use was significantly associated with higher risk of fractures at any site, including hip and spine fractures, as well as osteoporosis [10]. Similarly, a large Swedish nationwide cohort study of 115,933 children reported that PPI use was linked to an 11% increased risk of any fracture, with the highest risk seen in lower-limb fractures and a dose-dependent relationship based on cumulative exposure [9]. Possible mechanisms for these effects include impaired calcium absorption due to acid suppression and gastrin-induced parathyroid hyperplasia [11]. Despite these findings, the American Gastroenterological Association presently does not recommend routine BMD monitoring in pediatric PPI users, as a causal link has not been definitively proven [12]. Given these results, PPI use in children should be limited to evidence-based indications, and pediatricians should be aware of the increased fracture risk associated with prolonged or high-dose therapy.

Histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) are medications that decrease gastric acid secretion and pepsin production by blocking histamine-2 receptors on gastric parietal cells [13]. Drugs such as famotidine, cimetidine, nizatidine, and ranitidine are prescribed for conditions like GERD, including mild-to-moderate erosive esophagitis, peptic ulcer disease, and preventing upper gastrointestinal bleeding in at-risk patients [14]. While fracture risk has been linked to PPIs, multiple studies report no statistically significant connection between H2RA use and increased fracture risk in children. For instance, a meta-analysis by Li et al. assessing H2RAs in children and young adults found no significant rise in fracture risk with H2RAs alone or combined with PPIs [15]. In a multicenter cohort study of 3,526 hospitalized children, H2RA use was not associated with increased fracture risk over two years, with fractures happening in about 1% of both the treated and control groups [16]. Another retrospective cohort study involving 851,631 children similarly found no link between H2RA use alone and fracture risk after adjusting for confounders [17]. Although some animal studies indicate that long-term, high-dose H2RA exposure might adversely affect bone metabolism in young rats [18,19], further research has not consistently shown fracture risk at doses relevant to clinical use. Overall, current evidence suggests H2RAs are not associated with increased fracture risk in pediatric populations.

Glucocorticoids

Glucocorticoids are the primary treatment for many common inflammatory and immune-mediated conditions in children [20]. They work by binding to intracellular receptors, suppressing the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and increasing anti-inflammatory genes [21,22]. Synthetic options like prednisone, dexamethasone, and budesonide are commonly used to treat acute respiratory issues such as asthma exacerbations, croup, viral-induced wheezing, and cystic fibrosis [23]. In pediatric rheumatology, glucocorticoids help manage conditions like juvenile idiopathic arthritis, lupus, vasculitis, and rheumatic fever, often serving as interim therapy until disease-modifying drugs become effective. They are also frequently used in hematologic and oncologic diseases, inflammatory bowel disease, and adrenal insufficiency [24,25].

Mechanistically, glucocorticoids impair bone health by inducing apoptosis in osteoblasts and osteocytes, extending osteoclast survival, and disrupting the formation of growth plate chondrocytes [26,27]. These effects lead to decreased bone formation, increased resorption, impaired linear growth, and growth retardation. A six-year multicenter study of over 400 children on glucocorticoids found that asymptomatic vertebral fractures often developed within the first year, increasing the risk of future fractures and progressive vertebral collapse [2]. A prospective cohort study reported a rise in vertebral fractures among pediatric leukemia patients from 16% at diagnosis to 33% after six years of glucocorticoid therapy [28]. Another cohort study observed vertebral compression fractures in 32% of Duchenne muscular dystrophy patients on long-term corticosteroids, compared to none in untreated peers [29]. Moreover, children on glucocorticoids consistently show lower bone mineral density on DEXA scans compared to healthy peers, often resulting in osteopenia or osteoporosis [30]. A systematic review by Hansen et al. found significantly lower spine BMD in children on glucocorticoids compared to age- and sex-matched healthy controls, a trend that persisted even when compared to peers with the same underlying disease not receiving glucocorticoids [30].

Glucocorticoids significantly harm bone health, especially in the spine, by reducing bone formation and increasing resorption. Therefore, the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition recommends BMD screening and monitoring for children on systemic glucocorticoids for six months or more [31]. While stopping glucocorticoids early can allow some patients to recover vertebral body health, ongoing exposure may lead to long-lasting skeletal damage.

Psychotropic medications

Psychotropic medications have been increasingly prescribed to children and adolescents worldwide over the past two decades, with the most significant rises seen in antidepressants, sedatives, antipsychotics, and stimulants [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. These increases were particularly notable among adolescents and prescriptions written by nonpsychiatrist physicians [32,34]. The skeletal effects of these medications differ by class and mechanism, but overall evidence indicates measurable impacts on bone turnover, BMD, fracture risk, and other aspects of pediatric bone health.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most commonly prescribed antidepressants for children and adolescents, with their use and prevalence increasing significantly in these groups [[37], [38], [39]]. Fluoxetine and escitalopram are the only two SSRIs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating depression in adolescents [37,40]. Other frequently prescribed SSRIs for pediatric patients include sertraline, fluvoxamine, and paroxetine, with the first two being specifically approved by the FDA to treat pediatric obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) [41] SSRIs work by blocking the presynaptic serotonin transporter, which increases levels of extracellular serotonin and boosts serotonergic signaling, thereby regulating mood and behavior. However, serotonin receptors and transporters are also found on osteoblasts and osteoclasts, enabling serotonin to influence bone health and growth [[42], [43], [44]]. Serotonin can lead to increased bone resorption by promoting osteoclast differentiation and proliferation, while simultaneously inhibiting bone formation through serotonergic activity on osteoblasts [43,45]. Consequently, elevated extracellular serotonin in bones from chronic SSRI use can impair bone development and mineralization. Although direct pediatric human data on this relationship are limited, animal and preclinical studies during skeletal development have shown reduced bone mass and altered bone structure while being treated with SSRIs [46,47]. The presence of serotonin receptors on bone cells is well established, supporting preclinical evidence that SSRIs may negatively affect bone development during growth. One study observed that antidepressant use, especially SSRIs, in children was associated with a significantly higher risk of fractures, particularly early in treatment [48]. Conversely, the link between fracture risk and SSRIs is more extensively documented and stronger in adult populations [[49], [50], [51]].

Antipsychotic medications, such as risperidone, aripiprazole, olanzapine, and paliperidone, are antagonists or partial agonists of dopamine D2 receptors and various serotonin receptors. In pediatric populations, these drugs are used and FDA-approved to treat a variety of psychiatric conditions including adolescent schizophrenia, bipolar I disorder, Tourette's syndrome, and irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder, but they pose potential risks to bone health in pediatric populations [[52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]. The dosages and agents used vary primarily based on clinical indication and response to treatment. However, unlike SSRIs, the rates of antipsychotic use in pediatric patients have declined since the 2000s across all demographic groups [[57], [58], [59]]. The impact of bone health on children taking antipsychotics is a significant concern. Antipsychotics that mainly act as D2 antagonists, especially risperidone, can cause hyperprolactinemia in patients [60]. The increased prolactin levels, in turn, interfere with the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis by inhibiting gonadotropin-releasing hormone secretion [61]. Hyperprolactinemia from prolactinomas can lead to growth failure due to delayed maturation. Likewise, antipsychotic-induced hypogonadotropic hypogonadism raises concerns about impaired bone formation and development during critical growth periods [62]. This can lead to osteoporosis and a higher risk of fractures later in life. Additionally, antipsychotics with strong serotonin receptor activity may further affect bone density, volume, and fracture risk by modifying serotonin signaling.

Benzodiazepines (such as diazepam, midazolam, and lorazepam) are sedatives used in pediatric patients mainly to treat and manage acute convulsive status epilepticus, seizures, anxiety disorders, and sleep disorders, even though there are no well-defined indications for their use in pediatric mental health conditions [[63], [64], [65], [66], [67], [68]]. The link between benzodiazepines and bone fractures in adults has been extensively studied and well-documented compared to pediatric populations. [[69], [70], [71]]. However, a large cohort study by Bushnell et al. found that children with anxiety disorders had higher fracture rates when treated with benzodiazepines compared to those receiving SSRIs [72]. While pediatric data are still accumulating, early research suggests that the fracture risk with benzodiazepines is similar to that seen in adults. Their sedative effects also raise fall risk, particularly during the initial phase of treatment or dose increases [[73], [74], [75]]. Therefore, increased fall risk can lead to more orthopaedic injuries in children.

Stimulants are the most commonly prescribed psychotropic drugs among pediatric patients [34,76]. This class of drugs mainly treats children with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), with the first-line treatment being methylphenidate-based or amphetamine-based stimulants [77]. Studies have shown that ADHD medications are often associated with lower bone mineral density (BMD) and bone mineral content (BMC) compared to nonusers in children and adolescents [[78], [79], [80]]. Studies using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry observed significantly lower BMD and BMC levels at the lumbar spine and femoral neck [78]. Interestingly, there are conflicting reports about the effects of stimulant treatment. Cohort studies and reviews have found paradoxically lower rates of traumatic fractures in stimulant-treated versus untreated ADHD groups [81,82]. This may be partly due to improved behavior and reduced impulsivity with stimulant use. However, stimulants can indirectly affect bone health by suppressing appetite, which may lead to inadequate nutrition during critical growth periods. The American Academy of Pediatrics acknowledges growth suppression as a known adverse effect of stimulant therapy [77]. Given this, clinicians should be especially cautious with pediatric patients who are already at risk for poor musculoskeletal health.

Antiepileptics

Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), including phenytoin, phenobarbital, valproate, and carbamazepine, are essential in treating pediatric epilepsy but are consistently linked to negative effects on bone health, especially reduced BMD with long-term use. Among these, the enzyme-inducing AEDs—such as phenytoin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, and primidone—are most often implicated [83]. These medications increase the activity of the cytochrome P450 enzyme system, which speeds up the breakdown of vitamin D [84]. Consequently, lower vitamin D levels impair calcium absorption, leading to hyperparathyroidism and increased bone turnover to maintain calcium levels within a normal range [69,85,86]. This chain of effects ultimately hampers bone growth and can cause osteomalacia [86,87]. Valproic acid, a common AED that does not induce enzymes, has also been linked to compromised bone health, lower BMD, and disrupted bone metabolism [86]. Research indicates that valproic acid may impair bone by directly reducing osteoblast proliferation, promoting osteoclast formation, interfering with collagen synthesis, and increasing vitamin D metabolism [88,89]. The combined impact of these AEDs may cause bone weakening and increased fragility, raising the risk of orthopaedic injuries across a broad pediatric population.

Bisphosphonates and bone-protective agents

For pediatric patients with severe skeletal fragility, bisphosphonates are frequently used as a pharmacological treatment. Short-term use of bisphosphonates is considered safe and well-tolerated in children. 90 These medications are typically prescribed for children with multiple low-trauma fractures, painful vertebral compression fractures, or confirmed osteoporosis caused by primary conditions like osteogenesis imperfecta or secondary factors such as long-term corticosteroid use, chronic immobility, or systemic inflammatory diseases [91]. According to the International Society for Clinical Densitometry, pediatric osteoporosis is diagnosed either by the presence of one or more vertebral compression fractures unrelated to trauma or localized disease, or by a combination of a significant fracture history and a bone mineral density (BMD) Z-score of ≤ −2.0 [[91], [92], [93]].

Although PPIs are associated with a slightly increased risk of fractures in children, this does not justify routine bisphosphonate use for prevention. Instead, management should focus on reducing unnecessary PPI use and encouraging bone health through adequate calcium and vitamin D intake, along with regular weight-bearing activity. Pharmacologic treatment with bisphosphonates is reserved for patients who show clear signs of decreased bone strength or osteoporotic changes, not solely due to PPI exposure [15,31,92,94].

When treatment is necessary, intravenous bisphosphonates, such as pamidronate and zoledronic acid, are usually preferred in pediatric care because of their superior effectiveness and tolerability [95,96]. Oral forms are less common, especially in children taking acid-suppressive therapy, due to gastrointestinal intolerance. Expert guidelines, including those from the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition and the American Academy of Pediatrics, recommend that bisphosphonates be administered only under the supervision of specialists experienced in pediatric bone health [31,96]. These guidelines specifically address high-risk groups, such as nonambulatory patients or those with chronic inflammatory conditions who experience debilitating fractures and a reduced quality of life.

Bisphosphonates work by inhibiting osteoclast-mediated bone breakdown, which reduces bone turnover and increases bone density [97]. This helps decrease fragility in affected children [92]. Short-to mid-term safety data in pediatric populations are generally reassuring. The most common side effect is a flu-like acute phase reaction, usually happening after the first infusion and resolving on its own [90]. Biochemical changes such as transient hypocalcemia and hypophosphatemia can occur, especially with zoledronic acid, requiring monitoring and supplementation. Serious long-term issues seen in adults, like osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures, are rare in children [98], and have not been documented in pediatric groups [99]. Although there are theoretical concerns about impaired bone remodeling or long-term skeletal toxicity, current evidence does not support these risks in children receiving properly supervised treatment [31]. Alternatives like denosumab are still being studied in pediatric populations. Their safety, effectiveness, and long-term outcomes are not yet clear and are not considered standard treatment [100]. Bisphosphonates are important, but their use should be careful and limited. Treatment must be personalized, closely monitored, and only used in children who meet strict clinical criteria under expert supervision.

Other relevant medications

Tacrolimus and cyclosporine, both calcineurin inhibitors, are commonly used for immunosuppression in pediatric transplant patients. Although glucocorticoids are the primary contributors to post-transplant bone loss, calcineurin inhibitors also impact bone health. Tacrolimus is associated with increased bone resorption, especially at higher blood levels. In pediatric kidney transplant recipients, elevated tacrolimus concentrations correlate with increased bone turnover and decreased bone mineral density, indicating a dose-dependent risk [101]. In contrast, cyclosporine may have a neutral or protective effect on bone, particularly when used with low-dose steroids. Studies suggest it may help preserve or improve BMD, possibly by promoting bone remodeling, independent of renal function, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D status [101,102]. Although direct comparisons among pediatric populations are limited, evidence indicates that tacrolimus presents a higher risk of bone loss than cyclosporine, especially when combined with other bone-toxic agents like glucocorticoids [6]. Careful drug monitoring, minimized steroid use, and early interventions are crucial to protect bone health in pediatric transplant recipients.

Methotrexate and other chemotherapeutic agents can significantly damage the developing skeleton by suppressing bone turnover, impairing growth plate activity, and disrupting normal bone architecture, leading to poor skeletal outcomes in pediatric patients [103]. Methotrexate decreases chondrocyte proliferation and induces apoptosis at the growth plate, resulting in thinner growth plates, impaired endochondral ossification, and ultimately reduced bone length [104]. It also inhibits osteoblast activity and promotes osteoclastogenesis by increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6), which enhances bone resorption and decreases trabecular bone volume [105]. During intensive chemotherapy, especially with high-dose methotrexate and glucocorticoids, bone turnover is greatly suppressed [106]. Even after stopping steroids, osteoblast recovery remains limited while osteoclast activity stays high, indicating a prolonged imbalance in bone remodeling. These effects happen independently of the growth hormone axis and reflect direct toxicity to bone cells [[107], [108], [109]]. Long-term effects include low bone mineral density, increased fracture risk, and ongoing growth problems [107]. While folinic acid might provide some protection, evidence is mostly limited to preclinical studies [110]. Overall, methotrexate's skeletal toxicity is caused by multiple factors, involving direct cellular damage and disrupted bone remodeling.

Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) can influence bone remodeling in children, mainly by decreasing bone formation. While unfractionated heparin (UFH) is clearly associated with bone loss and osteoporosis, LMWH carries a much lower risk to the skeleton [111,112]. Experimental studies indicate that both types of heparin inhibit osteoblast activity, but LMWH has little effect on osteoclasts and causes less cancellous bone loss compared to UFH. Limited clinical data in pediatric populations suggest that chronic anticoagulation may be linked to reduced bone mineral density (BMD). However, this seems to be more affected by factors such as low BMI and pubertal delay than by LMWH exposure alone [112,113]. No strong dose-response relationship between LMWH and BMD loss has been confirmed in children. For long-term anticoagulation, LMWH is considered safer than UFH due to its lower affinity for bone cells and milder effects on remodeling. Still, monitoring bone health is recommended in children on extended therapy, especially if additional risk factors for low BMD are present [114].

Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) and leuprolide acetate are hormonal agents used in pediatric populations for contraception and central precocious puberty (CPP), respectively [115]. Both affect bone health by suppressing sex hormones during critical periods of skeletal development. The use of DMPA in adolescents has consistently been linked to significant reductions in BMD. While some studies suggest that combined oral contraceptives (COCs) may also impair bone accrual, the skeletal effects of other contraceptive methods, particularly long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs), remain poorly understood. Studies in females aged 12-18 have shown BMD losses of up to 4.3% at the hip and 4.2% at the femoral neck after two years of use [116]. Notably, extended DMPA use beyond two years has been associated with incomplete BMD recovery, with deficits persisting for years after stopping [116,117]. Earlier guidelines recommended limiting DMPA use to no more than two years. However, current guidance from professional organizations has changed, acknowledging that strict time restrictions and routine BMD monitoring can unnecessarily restrict access to a contraceptive method many adolescents prefer for its privacy, convenience, and effectiveness [118].

Leuprolide acetate, a GnRH agonist used for CPP, also causes temporary decreases in BMD during active treatment, attributed to suppression of estrogen and testosterone [119]. However, studies suggest that bone mass accrual resumes after therapy ends, and final peak bone mass is generally preserved [120]. Transient changes in growth and bone maturation may occur but typically resolve after treatment. With proper monitoring, bone effects are largely reversible.

Vitamin K is essential for pediatric bone growth through its role in the γ-carboxylation of osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein, which are vital for binding calcium within the bone matrix [121]. Adequate levels of vitamin K are linked to increased bone mineral content and stronger bones in children [122]. Deficiency has been associated with decreased bone accumulation and a higher risk of fractures. These benefits are most significant when combined with sufficient vitamin D and calcium intake, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive nutritional support [123,124].

Antacids and acid-suppressive therapies (AST) can also impact bone health. Aluminum-containing antacids bind dietary phosphate, which may lead to hypophosphatemia, rickets, and osteomalacia, especially in young children [125,126]. Furthermore, large cohort studies have indicated that early exposure to proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and H2-receptor antagonists is associated with a small but statistically significant rise in fracture risk during childhood [127]. These findings advise caution in the long-term use of AST, particularly when there are no clear medical reasons.

Vitamin A, although essential in appropriate amounts, can harm bone health when consumed excessively [128,129]. Hypervitaminosis A boosts bone resorption, decreases cortical bone mass, and may hinder linear growth [130]. Both clinical and epidemiological studies show a U-shaped relationship between vitamin A levels and BMD, indicating that both deficiency and excess increase fracture risk [131]. Long-term overconsumption, often through supplements or fortified foods, can also adversely affect body measurements and bone turnover in children.

Clinical strategies for optimizing pediatric bone health during pharmacotherapy

A unified approach to preventing and reducing medication-related bone disease in children involves risk stratification based on several factors, including medication type, treatment length, and patient-specific details such as pubertal status and nutrition. For all high-risk medications (including glucocorticoids, SSRIs, AEDs), clinicians should consider applying screening protocols if therapy is expected to last for a long time. These screenings should include serum measurements of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D), the standard marker for assessing vitamin D levels, along with calcium, phosphate, and alkaline phosphatase, with frequent re-evaluations, especially if symptoms like repeated orthopaedic injuries appear (Table 2). Although 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D is the biologically active form, it is not used routinely for screening because its levels can stay normal even in deficiency cases and are affected by other factors such as kidney function [134].

Encouraging daily vitamin D and calcium supplementation appropriate for the patients' age should be universally implemented in the clinical setting. At least 600–1000 IU of vitamin D and 1,000–1,300 mg of calcium daily should be administered depending on the patient's age and risk level [132,133]. These targets may need to be exceeded in patients taking drugs that lower vitamin D levels, including enzyme-inducing AEDs or valproic acid. When appropriate, providers should consider switching to alternative medications with more favorable skeletal effects. For example, instead of using an enzyme-inducing AED like phenytoin, prescribing a non-enzyme-inducing AED such as levetiracetam would be much more beneficial for a pediatric developing patient. Another example is tapering glucocorticoid prescriptions to the lowest effective dose. For patients who experience multiple low-impact fractures or consistently demonstrate lower BMD levels despite several adjustments to their medical, nutritional, and lifestyle interventions, referral to a pediatric endocrinologist is recommended. Continuous coordination between pediatricians and subspecialists is crucial to incorporate bone health into long-term treatment plans and to reduce preventable morbidity in children with chronic conditions.

Summary

A wide range of medications, including glucocorticoids, antiepileptics, psychotropics, acid-suppressive agents, immunosuppressants, chemotherapeutics, and hormonal therapies, can negatively impact bone metabolism in pediatric patients. These drugs may impair osteoblast function, increase bone resorption, decrease mineral absorption, and disrupt growth plate activity, leading to lower bone density, impaired growth, and a higher risk of fractures.

Glucocorticoids and enzyme-inducing AEDs are among the most well-documented causes, while SSRIs, PPIs, and antipsychotics have complex effects through endocrine and neurotransmitter pathways. Methotrexate, tacrolimus, and long-term hormonal suppression also contribute to skeletal fragility through both direct and indirect mechanisms.

Although bisphosphonates provide effective treatment for high-risk patients, prevention remains essential. Nutritional optimization, weight-bearing exercise, and careful prescribing are vital strategies. Parents play a crucial role in ensuring adherence to bone health recommendations, monitoring symptoms, and supporting healthy lifestyle choices. Ongoing long-term studies and pediatric-specific guidelines will further assist clinicians and families in managing medication-related bone risks through personalized care.
