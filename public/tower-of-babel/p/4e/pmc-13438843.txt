Reliability and readability of AI platforms for pediatric health advice: a comparative analysis
Authors: Dilara Lahut, Demet Deniz Bilgin, Mine Başıbüyük, Övgü Büke, Nalan Karabayır
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as a first-line source of information for everyday questions, including pediatric health guidance, yet the quality and readability of their outputs remain uncertain. This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the clinical reliability, quality, and readability of responses generated by four widely used LLMs to real-world, caregiver-style pediatric prompts. A curated set of 28 caregiver-style pediatric questions spanning six common themes was posed on August 5, 2025, to four widely used LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok-4, DeepSeek-V2), with one fresh session per question–model pair; the first, unedited outputs were retained. Under blinded conditions, responses were scored by four pediatricians using a modified DISCERN instrument (reliability/structure) and a global quality score (perceived usefulness). Readability was assessed with standard indices and length measures. Quality varied across platforms. Grok achieved the highest DISCERN scores, indicating stronger reliability and structural rigor, whereas Gemini received the highest global quality ratings. DeepSeek was consistently rated lower by experts but yielded the most readable outputs; ChatGPT showed intermediate performance. None of the models consistently met health-literacy targets for patient materials (approximately 6th–8th grade reading level). Grok generated the longest and most complex responses (often college level), while DeepSeek and Gemini produced comparatively simpler, more concise text. Across platforms, most responses were classified as moderate in reliability and usefulness; overtly unsafe advice was not identified. These findings suggest that current LLMs provide moderately useful pediatric health information but require improvements in readability, sourcing, and consistency before routine patient-facing use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54812-6.Keywords: Pediatrics, Parenting, Large language models (LLMs), Artificial intelligence (AI), Readability, Patient educationSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Parents are often the primary decision-makers regarding their children’s health, particularly during early childhood. With the increasing accessibility of digital tools, many caregivers now turn to the internet—especially online forums and large language models (LLMs)—for preliminary guidance before consulting healthcare professionals. Recent surveys indicate that over 80% of parents search online for health-related information prior to clinical visits, with platforms like Reddit frequently used for experiential advice and emotional support1–3. While this shift reflects a broader trend toward participatory health information-seeking, it also raises concerns about the quality and reliability of content accessed through non-expert channels4.

Caregivers’ information seeking for child health is typically multi-channel and often begins outside the clinical encounter. Prior work shows that parents commonly consult personal contacts (family/friends) alongside professional sources and digital media, particularly when facing uncertainty during acute or everyday concerns5,6. Online seeking is shaped not only by access and convenience but also by affective needs (reassurance, normalization) and peer support, which are salient in parenting communities and social platforms7,8. Consistent with this, the content of caregiver queries tends to cluster around recurrent, developmentally anchored domains (e.g., sleep, feeding, behavior/discipline, screen time, common illnesses, developmental concerns), which overlap with topics explicitly addressed in pediatric preventive-care anticipatory guidance (e.g., feeding guidance, sleeping/safe sleep, media use limits, and discipline/behavior management)3,9.

Several conceptual frameworks help explain why caregivers choose particular health-information channels and how they evaluate what they find. The Comprehensive Model of Information Seeking (CMIS) posits that caregivers’ seeking behavior is shaped by personal antecedents (e.g., prior experience and beliefs) and by channel characteristics such as perceived credibility, accessibility, and tailorability10,11. In pediatrics, “proxy” information seeking further emphasizes that adults search on behalf of a child, where perceived risk, urgency, and the need for actionable guidance interact with trust in the source and the caregiver’s perceived ability to appraise information quality5,6. Together, these perspectives suggest a recurring mismatch: caregivers may favor fast, convenient, and experience-based guidance, whereas pediatric safety depends on accuracy, appropriate risk stratification, and clear thresholds for seeking professional care.

In parallel, the use of generative artificial intelligence (AI), especially LLMs, has surged in both public and professional domains. Applications such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and DeepSeek are now widely accessible and capable of producing sophisticated responses to complex medical queries12–14. These tools have been investigated across clinical education and practice—from surgical decision support 15,16, to anatomical training17,18, and patient education across specialties such as plastic surgery19, and orthodontics20. Despite growing evidence for their clinical and educational utility, little is known about how effectively these systems address real-world questions from pediatric caregivers.

Children’s health concerns are often emotionally charged, and caregivers’ questions—ranging from sleep and feeding issues to developmental worries—require not only factual correctness but also clear, practical, and appropriately risk-stratified guidance. Given the unique ethical and clinical sensitivities of pediatric care, there is a growing need to critically evaluate how current LLMs perform when presented with real-world caregiver questions.

This study aims to systematically assess the quality of responses generated by four widely used LLMs to a curated set of pediatric caregiver questions. Using expert-based scoring systems alongside objective readability and lexical metrics, we evaluate the models’ capacity to produce clear, consistent, and clinically trustworthy content. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to comprehensively examine LLM performance in the context of everyday pediatric health concerns—an area where accessible yet evidence-based information is critically needed.

Materials and methods

Study aim and design

This observational and analytical study aimed to evaluate the accuracy, readability, and clinical reliability of AI-generated responses to pediatric health concerns commonly raised by caregivers. By integrating real-world parent-style queries with expert assessment across multiple pediatric themes, the study sought to bridge the gap between layperson digital behavior and professional standards in child health guidance. The complete study design is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Visual overview of the study design and evaluation process. Experts provided 28 clinical questions, which were answered by four large-language models (LLMs): ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok-4 and DeepSeek-V2. The generated responses were evaluated for quality by pediatricians using the modified DISCERN instrument (mDISCERN) and the global quality score (GQS) and for readability using standard metrics (flesch reading-ease score [FRES], Flesch–Kincaid grade level [FKGL], simple measure of gobbledygook [SMOG], gunning fog index [GFI], Coleman–Liau index [CLI]). The study concluded with a comparative analysis of all results across the four LLMs.

Reddit data extraction and scenario-based question pool construction

To ensure ecological validity, sampling was guided by the thematic classification proposed by Thornton et al.3, in which seven core parental-concern areas were identified: Sleep; Self-care and Parental Burnout; Eating and Feeding; Behavior and Discipline; Screen Time and Media Use; Illness and Health; Developmental Concerns. Following an initial manual content review, the theme Self-care and Parental Burnout was excluded because posts primarily focused on caregiver wellbeing rather than child health; accordingly, six domains were retained for sampling and prompt construction.

Reddit is a globally accessible platform and has been widely used in prior peer-reviewed health research to elicit real-world questions and perform content analyses. In this study, it was selected for its large user base, naturalistic caregiver discourse, and broad topical coverage relevant to everyday pediatric concerns, as well as transparent engagement signals (e.g., upvotes) and a reproducible retrieval pipeline (PRAW). Because r/Parenting is an English-language subreddit, our sampling frame was restricted to English posts and likely overrepresents concerns and information-seeking norms common in English-speaking contexts (including the United States); therefore, we used Reddit as a pragmatic sampling frame while recognizing that culturally specific or regionally prevalent concerns in non-English settings may be underrepresented.

Using the Python Reddit API Wrapper (PRAW), we queried r/Parenting with sort = ’top’ and time_filter = ’year’ on 9 July 2025 (capturing approximately the preceding 12 months of activity, i.e., about July 2024–June 2025). For each of the six retained domains, we retrieved the top 250 posts ranked by upvotes (initial retrieval n = 1500 posts before screening; duplicates and cross-posts were removed). Across the retained domains, the retrieved ‘top’ posts had a median upvote score of 386 (IQR 98–972; range 0–11,191); domain-specific upvote distributions are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Two investigators (D.L. and D.D.B.) independently conducted the manual content review (title and full text) and extracted candidate scenarios; disagreements were resolved by consensus, with adjudication by N.K. when needed. Manual content review consisted of screening each post’s title and full text against predefined eligibility criteria (domain mapping, presence of a concrete pediatric scenario, and first-person caregiver question), followed by extraction of the minimal scenario elements (age/developmental stage, key context, and caregiver question) used to draft de-identified composite prompts. Posts were eligible if they (1) described a concrete pediatric scenario (age or developmental stage, key context, and a specific caregiver question), (2) were written in a first-person caregiver voice, and (3) mapped clearly to one of the retained domains. Posts were excluded if they were non-English; primarily about caregiver self-care/burnout, legal/relationship issues, or adult health; lacked sufficient contextual detail; were memes/polls or duplicate content; or contained potentially identifying information that could not be adequately de-identified without altering meaning.

From the eligible set, scenarios were rewritten as de-identified, fictionalized composite prompts (no verbatim text), preserving the topic, tone, and pragmatic caregiver needs while removing usernames, links, timestamps, and any potentially identifiable fragments. Through this process, an initial pool of 30 candidate items was assembled and prepared as AI-ready questions, with a target of five prompts per retained domain.

Content validity evaluation (CVI)

Each candidate item underwent content validity assessment using the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) based on Polit and Beck’s guidelines21. Five independent pediatricians rated relevance using a 4-point Likert scale. They were recruited by purposive sampling from our professional network (outside the author team) based on active clinical practice in pediatrics and availability, and they did not participate in response scoring. Items with I-CVI ≥ 0.79 were retained; items below 0.70 were excluded. No items required revision (0.70 ≤ I-CVI < 0.79), and two items with I-CVI < 0.70 were excluded. The final question pool comprised 28 items. All questions included in the study are presented in Supplementary File 1. A summary of the question distribution across themes, along with representative examples, is provided in Table 1.

Response generation, blinding, and unit of analysis

We evaluated four LLMs—ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024), Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google, 2025), Grok-4 (xAI, 2025), and DeepSeek-V2 (DeepSeek AI, 2024). On August 5, 2025, each of the 28 finalized caregiver-style prompts was submitted once to each model in a new, fresh chat using a standardized preamble (“Answer as if you are a pediatrician…”); the first, unedited output was recorded. Prompts were asked in a fixed order identical across platforms. To minimize account-level personalization and carry-over effects, all interactions were conducted in private/incognito browser sessions, and each prompt–model pair was run in a separate session; platforms were accessed without personalized profiles, and when authentication was required (e.g., DeepSeek), a newly created account with no prior activity and with memory/personalization features disabled was used.

The content validity panel (CVI; n = 5) was external to the author team and did not score any model outputs. All modified DISCERN (mDISCERN; 16 items scored 1–5) and the Global Quality Score (GQS; 1–5) ratings were completed by four pediatricians (the study authors), each with more than 10 years of clinical practice and active roles across a university hospital, a tertiary research/training hospital, and private practice; the author team also included advanced academic training (e.g., a PhD in Social Pediatrics). Before scoring, raters completed a brief calibration to standardize interpretation of item anchors (Table 2) and practiced scoring two example responses not included in the analytic dataset (Supplementary Methods). Subsequently, all study responses were rated independently (no consensus scoring) under blinding to model identity (Models A–D).

The unit of analysis was the rater-averaged model–question observation (mean of four raters), yielding n = 28 per platform and N = 112 overall for inferential analyses. Inter-rater reliability was computed on rater-level scores (two-way, average-measures ICC for mDISCERN; quadratic-weighted κ for GQS). No clinicians were compensated for participation. All sessions were conducted on the same device and network; no missing ratings occurred.

Readability and linguistic complexity analysis

Readability was assessed using Readable.com (accessed August 2025), a validated web-based tool for evaluating text complexity22. The following five metrics were extracted: Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRES), Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Gunning Fog Index (GFI), Coleman-Liau Index (CLI), and SMOG Index15,23,24. FRES rates text on a 0–100 scale; higher scores reflect easier readability. A score ≥ 60 is considered optimal for health communication. FKGL, GFI, CLI, and SMOG Index estimate the years of education required for comprehension, with optimal readability defined as < 7th grade level25. In addition, total word count and sentence count per AI response were calculated as measures of linguistic load.

Clinical quality evaluation (mDISCERN and GQS)

Clinical reliability and practical usefulness of AI responses were evaluated using a modified 16-item DISCERN instrument (mDISCERN) and the Global Quality Score (GQS). All clinical ratings were completed by the same four pediatrician raters described in the previous section under blinded conditions (Models A–D).

mDISCERN (16 items, 1–5 per item; total 16–80). Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor/absent; 5 = fully adequate). A total mDISCERN score per response was computed by summing the 16 items for each rater; for primary analyses, rater means were used (average of four raters). For categorical reporting, cut-points were predefined by the author team based on the theoretical total score range (16–80) to aid interpretability in descriptive summaries (not derived post hoc): Poor = 16–31; Moderate = 32–63; Good = 64–80 (approximately < 40%, 40–79%, and ≥ 80% of the maximum possible score). Item content and operational anchors are provided in the scoring table (Table 2). Although DISCERN is frequently summarized using ‘No/Partially/Yes’ labels, the original handbook operationalizes each item on a 1–5 rating continuum with interpretive anchors rather than restricting scoring to only 1/3/526. In this study, we retained the 1–5 structure and used explicit wording anchors (1 = very poor/absent; 5 = fully adequate) to standardize pediatric rater scoring of AI-generated text. We therefore treat mDISCERN as an adapted, structured expert-rating rubric for comparative analyses across models, not as a newly validated psychometric instrument for LLM outputs.

GQS (1 item, 1–5). Overall clarity and usefulness for caregivers were rated on a single 5-point scale (1 = very poor; 5 = excellent)27. The mean GQS across raters was used for continuous analyses. For categorical reporting, groupings were predefined by the author team to summarize the 5-point scale as Low = 1–2; Medium = 3; High = 4–5 and were applied to the mean GQS per response (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Distributions for all outcomes (mDISCERN, GQS, FRES, FKGL, GFI, CLI, SMOG, word/sentence counts) were inspected visually and with Shapiro–Wilk; model-wise distributions were non-normal. Accordingly, we report both mean ± SD (comparability) and median [IQR] (robustness). All between-model tests used the rater-averaged unit (28 questions per platform; n = 28 per platform, N = 112 overall).

The primary comparison was the between-model difference in mDISCERN and GQS; secondary comparisons covered each readability/structural metric and theme-level summaries. Global tests used Kruskal–Wallis; when significant (two-sided p < 0.05), Dunn post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction were applied. With four models there are six pairwise contrasts per outcome, yielding a per-comparison α = 0.05/6 = 0.0083; adjustments were applied within metric (separately for mDISCERN, GQS, and each readability index).

Rater agreement was summarized by ICC (two-way, average-measures) for mDISCERN and quadratic-weighted κ for GQS. Associations between quality and readability/structure used Spearman’s ρ (two-sided). Primary nonparametric tests and categorical agreement were performed in SPSS v21; R was used for Dunn post-hoc (FSA), ICC (psych), weighted κ (irr/irrCAC), and correlations (Hmisc). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless Bonferroni-adjusted as specified above. All prompts and full model outputs are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Ethical considerations

All clinical scenarios used in this study were created as simulated examples based on publicly accessible Reddit posts. To preserve anonymity and avoid ethical concerns, no original Reddit usernames, direct quotations, or identifiable content were used. Instead, scenarios were transformed into fictionalized, de-identified prompts that reflected common pediatric concerns in realistic caregiver language. Since no personal health data or direct human participation was involved, and all data were publicly available and synthetically generated, formal ethics committee approval was not required for this study.

DISCERN scores

Median mDISCERN scores differed significantly across platforms (Kruskal–Wallis χ2 = 29.0, df = 3, p < 0.001). Grok had the highest median (60.5, IQR 10), followed by Gemini (58, IQR 6) and ChatGPT (57, IQR 9), with DeepSeek lowest (55, IQR 7). In pairwise Dunn tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0083), only the difference between Grok and DeepSeek remained significant (Grok higher than DeepSeek, p < 0.001). The differences between Grok and ChatGPT (p = 0.034) and between Grok and Gemini (p = 0.026) were significant at the nominal α = 0.05 level but did not remain significant after multiplicity adjustment; no other pairwise differences were significant after correction.

Theme-based mDISCERN patterns

Descriptive theme-stratified summaries of mDISCERN scores are shown in Fig. 3. The 28 prompts were distributed across six domains (Sleep, n = 4; Eating and Feeding, n = 5; Behaviour and Discipline, n = 5; Screen Time and Media Use, n = 4; Illness and Health, n = 5; Developmental Concerns, n = 5). Across all theme–platform cells, mean mDISCERN scores ranged from 51.6 to 62.6, indicating modest variation by domain. GQS scores

GQS also varied by platform (Kruskal–Wallis χ2 = 38.1, df = 3, p < 0.001). Gemini had the highest median (4, IQR 1); ChatGPT and Grok were moderate (both 3, IQR 1); DeepSeek was lowest (3, IQR 0). After Bonferroni adjustment (α = 0.0083), only DeepSeek was significantly lower than Gemini (p < 0.001). The differences indicating Gemini higher than ChatGPT (p = 0.009) and higher than Grok (p = 0.016), and DeepSeek lower than Grok (p = 0.010), reached nominal significance at α = 0.05 but did not remain significant after correction. Consistent with this pattern, categorical GQS classification showed Gemini with the largest proportion of “High” responses and DeepSeek with the smallest (Table 3, Fig. 2).

Inter-rater consistency and DISCERN–GQS relationship

Inter-rater reliability for mDISCERN scores was limited at the single-rater level (ICC range: 0.12–0.36). However, reliability improved to a good level when aggregating across four raters (average ICC(3,k) = 0.70, 95% CI 0.59–0.78, p < 0.001). For GQS, quadratic weighted Fleiss–Cohen Kappa indicated fair-to-moderate agreement (κ = 0.35, 95% CI 0.23–0.46, p < 0.001). ICC analyses supported this finding: single-rater reliability was fair (ICC ≈ 0.36–0.37), whereas average measures reached good agreement (ICC ≈ 0.70, 95% CI 0.60–0.79).

Safety-netting and clinician referral statements

Clinician-referral (e.g., “consult/see/contact a pediatrician/doctor,” “seek medical advice,” and, where applicable, “consult a therapist”) language was common but varied across platforms. Such statements appeared in 19/28 (67.9%) ChatGPT responses, 17/28 (60.7%) Gemini responses, 26/28 (92.9%) Grok responses, and 21/28 (75.0%) DeepSeek responses. Additionally, a platform-specific disclaimer (“not a pediatrician/doctor”) was observed only in Grok outputs and occurred in 22/28 (78.6%) responses. A prompt-level summary is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Readability characteristics and text complexity

The readability and structural features of AI-generated pediatric responses varied significantly across models. Using seven key metrics—Flesch reading ease score (FRES), Flesch-kincaid grade level (FKGL), gunning fog index (GFI), Coleman-Liau index (CLI), SMOG index, word count, and sentence count—Kruskal–Wallis tests revealed statistically significant differences among the platforms (p < 0.001 for all variables). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons confirmed that Grok content was significantly more complex than that of ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Gemini across nearly all readability dimensions (p < 0.0083).

DeepSeek produced the most accessible and concise content overall. Although Gemini yielded the highest FRES (63.90 ± 9.61), DeepSeek exhibited the lowest FKGL (7.52 ± 1.43), GFI (9.27 ± 1.51), CLI (10.80 ± 1.62), and SMOG (10.41 ± 1.05)—suggesting content appropriate for readers at a 10th to 11th grade level (approximately 15–17 years old). DeepSeek also had the fewest words per response (315.5 ± 53.3), with a moderate sentence count (29.4 ± 7.2). Compared to Grok, DeepSeek was significantly easier to read across all indices (p < 0.001). However, its comparisons with Gemini yielded mixed results: while DeepSeek outperformed Gemini in structural simplicity (GFI p = 0.003), no significant differences were observed in FKGL (p = 0.241) or FRES (p = 0.120) after Bonferroni correction.

Gemini responses were the longest overall, with the highest word count (569.8 ± 119.9) and sentence count (39.0 ± 10.6). Despite this, Gemini maintained moderate complexity, with FKGL (8.06 ± 1.66) and SMOG (11.14 ± 1.40) scores suggesting a 12th grade to college entry–level readability. Gemini’s CLI (9.37 ± 1.45) was the lowest among all models, indicating shorter average word and sentence lengths. Gemini was significantly more readable than Grok in several metrics (FRES, FKGL, SMOG; all p < 0.001), yet not statistically superior to DeepSeek in key readability indices after correction.

ChatGPT showed a balanced performance across both readability and structural metrics. It produced moderately readable responses (FRES: 58.37 ± 10.76; FKGL: 8.32 ± 1.87), with a word count (315.1 ± 73.9) and sentence count (25.7 ± 9.3) similar to DeepSeek. These values indicate a reading level suitable for late high school students (approximately 11th–12th grade). Compared to Grok, ChatGPT responses were significantly easier to understand for FKGL (p = 0.002), CLI (p = 0.007), and SMOG (p = 0.005); however, these differences did not reach Bonferroni-adjusted significance in all pairwise comparisons.

Overall, Grok generated the most complex and verbose responses, whereas DeepSeek and Gemini produced texts with greater linguistic simplicity and structural conciseness. These variations in readability and text structure were consistently reflected across multiple indices.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analyses revealed a weak-to-moderate positive association between mDISCERN and GQS scores (ρ = 0.355, p < 0.001), indicating that both scales partly capture overlapping quality dimensions. Beyond this relationship, mDISCERN scores demonstrated significant positive correlations with text length (word count: ρ = 0.190, p < 0.001; sentence count: ρ = 0.111, p = 0.019) and with all linguistic complexity indices, including FKGL (ρ = 0.222, p < 0.001), SMOG (ρ = 0.223, p < 0.001), GFI (ρ = 0.213, p < 0.001), and CLI (ρ = 0.189, p < 0.001). Conversely, mDISCERN scores correlated negatively with FRES readability (ρ = –0.223, p < 0.001), suggesting that responses rated as higher quality were generally longer and more complex.

In contrast, GQS scores showed weaker associations. Significant positive correlations were observed with structural features such as word count (ρ = 0.273, p < 0.001) and sentence count (ρ = 0.222, p < 0.001), but GQS was not significantly associated with most readability metrics, including FRES, FKGL, SMOG, or CLI (all p > 0.05). These findings suggest that while both scales relate to response length, only mDISCERN consistently reflects linguistic and structural complexity. Discussion

Parents are typically the primary caregivers responsible for monitoring and responding to their children’s health and developmental needs. Their perspectives often reflect urgent concerns that lead them to seek accessible and trustworthy information, particularly in digital environments28,29. In recent years, online health-seeking behavior has evolved from anonymous forum participation and “mommy blogs” to real-time use of advanced digital tools such as large language models (LLMs) 30–32. The increasing integration of AI tools such as ChatGPT into daily life has been accompanied by a growing public tendency to consult these systems for health-related questions, including self-diagnosis and medical information seeking33,34. Given this broader trend, it is highly plausible that parents also use AI platforms to ask pediatric health questions concerning their children35.

The appeal of LLMs stems from their 24/7 availability, human-like responses, and capacity to summarize complex medical topics. However, this shift toward AI-mediated information access raises critical questions about the readability, accuracy, and overall quality of the content delivered. Previous studies evaluating the readability and accuracy of medical educational materials—including patient brochures, anatomy content, and caregiver narratives—have demonstrated that such information often exceeds recommended reading levels and may include inconsistencies or overly technical language17,36–39.

Within this framework, our study provides a comparative evaluation of responses generated by four widely used LLMs (ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and DeepSeek) in reply to commonly asked pediatric health questions by caregivers. By combining expert content ratings (mDISCERN and GQS) with objective readability and structural metrics (FRES, FKGL, GFI, SMOG, CLI), we aimed to systematically assess the quality and accessibility of AI-generated health information across multiple dimensions.

Our readability analyses showed that none of the evaluated models consistently achieved the recommended 6th to 8th grade readability level for patient-facing health information25. Grok produced responses at a college reading level, with the lowest FRES and highest complexity indices (FKGL, GFI, CLI, SMOG). In contrast, Gemini and DeepSeek generated content closer to a 10th to 12th grade level. Notably, DeepSeek’s output was the most accessible, achieving the lowest FKGL and SMOG scores. ChatGPT’s responses fell between these extremes, offering moderate complexity and a balanced structure.

Our standardized clinician-role preamble may have shaped style and readability. While the intent was to emulate patient-centered pediatric counseling, some models may treat ‘answer as a pediatrician’ as a cue for professional/technical register. Comparative experiments with explicit plain-language constraints and iterative clarification are needed to separate role-framing effects from baseline model tendencies. While overtly unsafe advice was not identified, safety-netting statements (e.g., contact/consult your pediatrician/doctor) were inconsistently included and differed across models (Supplementary Table 3), underscoring the need for standardized risk-triage language in patient-facing deployments.

Recent findings in other medical fields highlight the variable performance of LLMs across different domains. Our results align closely with those of a study in anatomical education, which found that Grok again ranked highest for mDISCERN and GQS, while DeepSeek scored the lowest, mirroring our reliability pattern. Moreover, their readability analysis also found Grok to be the most complex and DeepSeek the most accessible, consistent with our findings17. A subsequent publication by the same research group on ethical communication regarding body donation further supported the superior reliability of Grok and GPT38.

In contrast, other studies have reported opposing trends. For instance, a study on breast reconstruction guidelines found that ChatGPT outperformed Grok in both accuracy and readability40. Similarly, in a urology-focused study, Grok was rated as the most readable and GPT as the most complex, contrary to our pediatric-specific results41. These discrepancies suggest that model performance may vary significantly by clinical domain and prompt context, reinforcing the need for domain-specific evaluations.

Findings from other medical fields further support this conclusion. A cardiology study compared GPT, Gemini, and Grok’s performance in clinical reasoning, concluding that GPT-4 had the best overall performance14. Similarly, in a study on patient information for thyroid eye disease, another group found that Gemini trailed GPT-4o in reliability and detail, highlighting the model-specific inconsistencies observed in our study13.

These findings are particularly relevant to our pediatric study, as they collectively indicate that no single LLM consistently excels across all medical specialties or task types. A model may demonstrate stronger clinical reasoning or higher informational completeness in one domain yet perform less well in another, depending on the topic, the prompt framing, and the evaluation lens. Overall, prior studies support the view that LLM performance can vary across clinical contexts, reinforcing the need for domain-specific evaluations rather than assuming uniform performance across topics.

As shown in the results (Fig. 3), model performance was domain-sensitive, with higher mean mDISCERN scores in Developmental Concerns and Illness and Health and comparatively lower scores in Behaviour and Discipline and Screen Time and Media Use. This pattern may relate to differences in the availability of standardized, guideline-concordant counseling across topics. Developmental surveillance and common childhood illnesses are often anchored to structured frameworks (e.g., developmental milestone checklists and widely used pediatric illness management guidance), which could facilitate more coherent, medically framed outputs. In contrast, sleep, feeding, discipline, and media use questions—although frequent in routine pediatric practice—are typically more heterogeneous, preference-sensitive, and shaped by family context and cultural norms. As a result, LLM responses in these domains may rely more on generalized parenting advice than on explicitly structured clinical reasoning, which can reduce perceived trustworthiness and actionability. Given that these theme-level summaries are descriptive and not supported by formal subtheme hypothesis testing in this study, they should be interpreted as exploratory and warrant confirmation in larger, prospectively stratified prompt sets.

Our study contributes to the growing body of literature calling for more rigorous evaluation of Al-based health information tools. By using both expert-based and algorithmic scoring systems, we highlight potential inconsistencies in quality and accessibility among LLMs that are already widely used by caregivers for pediatric health guidance.

Limitations

Although large language models (LLMs) offer promising support in caregiver education and pediatric health communication, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings.

First, our question pool, while reflecting common pediatric concerns, did not include urgent or critical scenarios, limiting our ability to assess how LLMs perform in high-stakes situations. Additionally, the fixed 28-item question set, while ensuring standardization, did not allow for dynamic follow-up or rephrasing, which are common in real-world AI interactions and can significantly influence model performance. Future studies should adopt more dynamic, context-sensitive prompt designs to better simulate user interactions.

Second, our prompts were derived from English-language Reddit content, a platform-defined ecosystem whose user base is not globally representative and is disproportionately concentrated in the United States and other high-income, English-speaking contexts. Accordingly, the distribution of pediatric concerns may not reflect priorities in other cultures, regions, or health systems; generalizability to non-English queries and region-specific concerns should be interpreted with caution. Relatedly, pediatric counseling is age- and development-dependent; although prompts retained age/developmental context when present, we did not conduct age-stratified comparisons, and further stratification would yield small cell sizes. Future studies should prospectively sample and code prompts across predefined pediatric age groups.

Although widely used structured rating tools (mDISCERN and GQS) were applied to evaluate reliability and usefulness, mDISCERN represents an adaptation of DISCERN for the present context and has not been formally validated for AI-generated pediatric counseling outputs. Therefore, our mDISCERN findings should be interpreted primarily as comparative (between-model) indicators rather than absolute measures of quality. While we monitored inter-rater consistency, future research should consider larger reviewer panels or consensus-based methods to enhance robustness. Rater demographic characteristics and parental status were not captured; therefore, we could not explore whether such rater-level factors influenced scoring patterns.

Finally, the four models evaluated here—ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and DeepSeek—represent only a subset of commercially available LLMs. Their proprietary architectures and opaque training data prevent clear attribution of performance differences. Importantly, while LLMs can expand access to pediatric health advice, they should not replace individualized clinical judgment. In emotionally sensitive or diagnostically uncertain cases, human expertise remains essential.

Conclusion

This study provides one of the first comparative evaluations of four widely used large language models—ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and DeepSeek—in generating responses to frequently asked pediatric health questions by caregivers. Using both expert-based (mDISCERN, GQS) and objective readability metrics, we identified notable differences in content quality and thematic performance across platforms.

Across the six common pediatric themes included in our prompt set, overall platform differences were consistent, while theme-level patterns were explored descriptively. Grok and Gemini yielded higher overall clinical-quality ratings, whereas DeepSeek tended to be rated lower for clinical quality but produced more readable outputs.Importantly, although all platforms produced readable and broadly informative content, the absence of triage-sensitive scenarios in our prompt set limits conclusions about their safety in urgent care settings. LLMs may offer accessible, scalable support to caregivers seeking pediatric information, especially in resource-limited contexts or outside clinical hours. However, they should be used as complementary tools—not as replacements for healthcare professionals—particularly when dealing with diagnostic uncertainty, critical conditions, or individualized care needs.

Future research should explore the behavior of LLMs in emergency-related pediatric scenarios, evaluate outputs across different languages and cultural settings, and assess their integration into clinical decision-support workflows. Building AI literacy among both caregivers and clinicians will be essential to ensure safe, critical, and equitable use of these technologies in pediatric health communication.
