Effect of noninvasive brain stimulation for unilateral spatial neglect after stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ruoxuan Zhao, Tong Hao, Chengshuo Wang, Liang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnilateral spatial neglect (USN) is one of the most common behavioral cognitive impairments after stroke, limiting functional activities and impairing daily life. Increasing evidence suggests that noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) holds a promising therapeutic potential in the field of post-stroke neglect rehabilitation, but its effect still needs to be systematically evaluated.ObjectiveThe aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to assess the effect of NIBS for the treatment of USN after stroke and to examine the effect of moderating variables (e.g., stroke phase, protocol type) on the effect sizes of NIBS treatment.MethodsSystematically searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or crossover RCTs on NIBS for the treatment of USN from four databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library. The search period spanned from inception to May 1, 2025. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on the NIBS interventions (rTMS, TBS, tDCS and tACS). The primary outcomes included the line bisection test (LBT), star cancellation test (SCT), and catherine bergego scale (CBS).ResultsThe results of 17 studies (425 patients) showed that different effects of various NIBS interventions (rTMS, TBS, tDCS and tACS) on USN outcomes: rTMS significantly reduced LBT (SMD = −1.82, P < 0.00001), SCT (SMD = −1.59, P < 0.00001), and CBS (MD = −2.76, P = 0.002); TBS significantly reduced SCT (SMD = −2.59, P = 0.03) and CBS (MD = −0.93, P < 0.0001); tDCS significantly reduced LBT (SMD = −0.60, P = 0.0009) and SCT (SMD = −0.60, P = 0.001); while tACS showed no significant difference compared to the control group in improving LBT (SMD = 0.04, P = 0.93) and SCT (SMD = −0.32, P = 0.49). Additionally, subgroup analyses showed that intervention timing and NIBS protocol type exerted moderating effects on treatment effect in USN patients. Intervention during the acute stroke phase may yield greater effect compared to those in the chronic phase (P < 0.00001). Excitatory NIBS protocols targeting the ipsilesional posterior parietal cortex (PPC) may yield greater therapeutic potential than inhibitory protocols applied to the contralesional PPC (P < 0.05).ConclusionrTMS and TBS showed greater therapeutic potential than tDCS and tACS in reducing neglect severity and improving activities of daily living. In addition, modulating variables may potentially influence the therapeutic effect of NIBS intervention. Specifically, early intervention and excitatory NIBS protocols applied to the ipsilesional PPC for post-stroke USN patients may have greater therapeutic potential.Keywords: Stroke, Unilateral spatial neglect, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Transcranial electrical stimulation, Meta-analysis

Introduction

According to the World Stroke Organization (WSO), the incidence of stroke has continued to rise in recent years, and deaths from stroke are expected to increase by 50% globally between 2020 and 2050 [1]. Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability among adults worldwide, and poses a serious threat to people’s lives, health, and quality of life [2]. Unilateral spatial neglect (USN) is one of the most common post-stroke behavioral and cognitive disorders, characterized by the inability to respond correctly to spatial stimuli contralateral to the lesion, including failure to perceive visual, auditory, or tactile stimuli, accompanied by spatial orientation deficits and other behavioral abnormalities [3]. Depending on the location of the lesion, the prevalence rates for the left and right hemispheres are 22% and 61%, respectively [4]. Clinically, left-side USN is more common. This is because the right hemisphere dominates the allocation of visual spatial attention in humans, making left-side USN caused by right-brain damage more prevalent [5]. Although many patients with USN experience spontaneous neuronal recovery, up to 40% continue to exhibit neglect symptoms 1 year after stroke onset [6]. USN can be considered a strong predictor of poor functional recovery after stroke, which not only affects the patient’s activities of daily living (ADL) [7] and impairs postural balance [8], but also leads to accidents such as falls, stumbles, and collisions [9], which increases the likelihood of injury. Therefore, it is extremely important to choose the best treatment to protect the health and life safety of USN patients. Although conventional neurorehabilitation techniques for post-stroke USN such as smooth tracking eye movement training, visual scanning training, cross facilitation training, and prism adaptation can improve the severity of neglect in patients [10, 11]. Bowen et al. [12] concluded that there is insufficient evidence to demonstrate that these techniques have a positive impact on the recovery of daily behaviors in patients with USN, and that the effect persists after the intervention. Therefore, more effective treatments are needed.

The latest stroke rehabilitation guideline suggested that noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) can improve complex attention in stroke patients [13]. NIBS such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), theta burst stimulation (TBS), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) have a promising application in the rehabilitation of post-stroke neglect disorders. The theoretical basis of NIBS for the treatment of USN is mainly derived from the interhemispheric competition model [14]. According to this model, the pathological nature of USN lies in the bilateral imbalance of cortical excitability, and in healthy populations, the left and right hemispheres of the brain maintain a dynamic balance through reciprocal inhibition of the corpus callosum. However, this equilibrium is disrupted following brain injury. Damage to the unilateral hemisphere’s attentional network results in insufficient inhibition of the contralateral hemisphere via the corpus callosum, thereby triggering pathological overactivity in the contralateral hemisphere. This causes visual-spatial attention to shift toward the ipsilateral spatial region [15]. Unlike traditional neurorehabilitation techniques (e.g., visual scanning training and prism adaptation) that use a bottom-up strategy of compensating for sensory input, NIBS intervenes through a top-down bi-directional modulation mechanism to regulate interhemispheric balance.

TMS is a safe and effective NIBS technique that utilizes electromagnetic induction to generate painless electrical currents. These currents stimulate brain neurons, thereby improving cognitive function through sustained neuro-reparative effects [16]. Depending on the form of the pulses, TMS can be categorized into various modes, and the commonly used modes in the clinic are repetitive TMS (rTMS) and TBS. TBS is a special rTMS mode capable of delivering the pulses at a much faster rate of applied intermittently or continuously, and is thought to induce long-term changes in cortical excitability [17]. In general, low-frequency rTMS (LF-rTMS) (≤1 Hz) and continuous TBS (cTBS) are inhibitory to the cerebral cortex, and high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS) (>5 Hz) and intermittent TBS (iTBS) are excitatory to the cerebral cortex [18]. The mechanism of action of TMS to improve spatial attention function involves multilevel neuromodulatory effects. From the perspective of synaptic plasticity, TMS directly modulates the efficiency of synaptic transmission in cortical neurons by inducing either long time-range potentiation (LTP) or long time-range depression (LTD) effects [19]. At the neural network level, TMS modulation of specific cortical regions can produce a cross-synaptic distal effect, facilitating the reconstruction of the damaged attentional network through modulation of functional connectivity [20]. In addition, TMS upregulates the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which ameliorates spatial attention deficits [21].

Transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) is another noninvasive brain stimulation technique that modulates cortical excitability by applying weak electrical currents through scalp electrodes [22]. Some of the more widely studied tES techniques include tDCS and tACS. tDCS can prolong the duration of cortical excitability changes by applying weak direct current to polarize neural tissues for a longer period of time [23]. It has been shown that anodic transcranial direct current stimulation (a-tDCS) increases cortical excitability on the lateral side of the lesion, and cathodic transcranial direct current stimulation (c-tDCS) inhibits cortical excitability on the contralateral side [24]. The mechanism of action relies mainly on the polarizing effect of the current on the neuronal membrane potential, which affects the neuronal firing threshold and synaptic plasticity [25]. tACS modulates the synchrony and phase coupling of neural oscillations in the brain by delivering alternating currents at specific frequencies (e.g., theta, alpha, and beta bands) through scalp electrodes. Unlike the direct current polarization effects of tDCS, tACS influences the rhythmic activity of neuronal clusters through frequency-dependent neural synchronization, thereby modulating the dynamic functional connectivity of brain networks [26, 27].

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported the therapeutic potential of NIBS for USN [28–30]. However, prior studies were predominantly published in earlier periods and have yet to systematically explore the potential moderating effects of variables (e.g., stroke phase, stimulation site) on the magnitude of NIBS effects. Therefore, this study focuses on two core questions: First, to systematically synthesize the latest evidence evaluating NIBS for treating post-stroke USN; second, to analyze the potential influence of moderating variables (such as excitatory or inhibitory protocols, stroke phase, etc.) on the effect sizes of NIBS treatment.

Methods

Our systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Evaluation and Meta-Analysis [31]. The protocol for this study is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under the registration number: CRD420251090962.

Search strategy

Two authors (RXZ and TH) separately searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and crossover RCTs from Pubmed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases. The search time limit was from the establishment of the database to 1 May 2025. By combining medical subject headings (MeSH) with free words using Boolean logic operators, we synthesized the following search terms for a comprehensive search: “Perceptual Disorders”, “Unilateral Spatial Neglect”, “Hemisensory Neglect”, “Hemispatial Neglect”, “Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation”, “Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation, Repetitive”, “rTMS”, “Theta Burst Stimulation”, “TBS”, “Transcranial Electrical Stimulation”, “Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation”, “tDCS”, “Anodal Stimulation Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation”, “Anodal Stimulation tDCS”, “Cathodal Stimulation Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation”, “Cathodal Stimulation tDCS”, “Transcranial Alternating Current Stimulation”, “tACS”. In addition, we manually searched the reference lists from the retrieved articles to identify other potentially relevant studies.

Data extraction

The Endnote X9 was used to manage the retrieved records. Two authors (CSW and TH) independently reviewed all articles from which data were extracted including: first author’s name, year of publication, country, study design, participant characteristics (sample size, age, stroke phase), NIBS protocol (frequency, magnitude of current, intensity and number of pulses), duration of treatment, stimulation target, intervention and outcome measures. The extracted data were entered into an excel spreadsheet and cross-checked by two authors (CSW and TH), and the senior author (LW) was involved in the discussion and decision-making in case of disagreement during data extraction.

Quality assessment

Two authors (RXZ and CSW) independently assessed the quality and risk of bias of all included studies using the PEDro scale [33]. A total of 11 indicators were included: (1) Eligibility criteria; (2) Random allocation; (3) Concealed allocation; (4) Baseline comparability; (5) Blind subjects; (6) Blind therapists; (7) Blind assessors; (8) primary outcome measures (dropout rate <15%); (9) Intention-to-treat analysis; (10) Between-group comparisons; (11) Point estimates variability. Because the first eligibility criteria is external validity and is not included in the total score, the PEDro scale is scored out of a total of 10, with higher scores indicating higher quality. Studies with a PEDro score of 0–3 are considered to be of low quality, 4–5 are considered to be of moderate quality, 6–8 are considered to be of good quality, and 9–10 are considered to be of excellent quality. In case of disagreement during the assessment process, the decision was discussed by the authors (LW).

Statistical analysis

We used Review Manager software (version 5.4) and State software (version 18.0) to analyze the data and generate graphs. The outcomes in this study consisted of continuous variables. When the outcomes of the included studies were inconsistent, SMD was adopted to express the effect size of NIBS on the effect of neglect recovery, and when they were consistent MD was used. Statistics (sample size, mean, and standard deviation) related to neglect performance before and after NIBS treatment were extracted to calculate the SMD or MD, with a 95%CI provided for each effect size. The p-value of the chi-square test and the I2 index of the heterogeneity test were used to indicate the level of statistical heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was regarded as low, medium, and high when I2 was less than 30%, 30–75%, and more than 75%, respectively. When P ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%, a fixed-effects model was selected for statistical analysis; when P < 0.1 and I2 > 50%, a random-effects model was used to calculate the combined effect sizes, and the NIBS protocol was divided into four categories (rTMS, TBS, tDCS, and tACS) through subgroup analysis to identify the sources of heterogeneity [34, 35]. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out approach to test the stability and reliability of the findings. When different scales had opposite changes to reflect neglect severity, the corresponding values were multiplied by −1 to ensure the same directional value. The subgroup analysis was used to compare the differences in the effect sizes of the intervention measures under different moderating variables (e.g., stroke phase, stimulation site), and the statistical significance of the moderating effects was evaluated through test of over effect. Finally, the publication bias of the included literature was evaluated using the funnel plot, and Egger’ test [36, 37].

Results

Search results

A total of 1920 records were identified from the four databases. After removing duplicated records, we got 1235 records. By reading the titles and abstracts of the studies and excluding 1172 irrelevant records, we evaluated the remaining 63 studies in full text, and 22 studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in the systematic review [38–59]. Five studies were excluded from the meta-analysis because the data were not available [45, 49, 53, 56, 58]. Thus, 17 studies were included in the meta-analysis [38–44, 46–48, 50–52, 54, 55, 57, 59]. Characterization of studies

The characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1, published between 2008 and 2024. A total of 22 studies with a total of 515 patients were included, and all were middle-aged and elderly. Four studies were crossover RCTs [47, 53, 58, 59], and 18 studies were Parallel RCTs [38–46, 48–52, 54–57]. All stroke patients included in the analysis experienced right hemisphere lesions. Regarding NIBS protocols, four studies applied ipsilesional stimulation [38, 40, 54, 58], 13 studies applied contralesional stimulation [39, 41, 42, 44–53], and 5 studies applied bilateral stimulation [43, 55–57, 59]. All included studies conducted outcome assessments at the conclusion of the intervention. Additionally, several studies performed supplementary assessments at various follow-up time points: 1 study conducted an additional post-intervention assessment at 2 weeks [42], 1 study at 3 weeks [47], 4 studies at 1 month [41, 46, 50, 51], and 1 study at 3 months [52].

Meta-analysis of line bisection test

The results of meta-analysis of LBT are shown in Fig. 2.

Forest plot of LBT for subgroup analysis according to NIBS protocol. LBT, line bisection test

Fifteen studies [38–40, 42–44, 46, 48, 50–52, 54, 55, 57, 59] included 22 sets of data comparing LBT in the NIBS group and the control group. There was moderate heterogeneity among the 22 sets of data (P < 0.00001, I² = 68%). Therefore, we performed a subgroup analysis according to the NIBS protocol (rTMS, TBS, tDCS and tACS). After subgroup analysis, six studies [38–40, 42, 43, 51] included nine sets of data comparing LBT in the rTMS group and the control group. LBT of the rTMS group was lower than the control group [SMD = −1.82 (−2.51, −1.13), P < 0.00001], with a moderate heterogeneity among the nine sets of data (P = 0.0007, I² = 71%). Further sensitivity analysis excluding one study of Kim et al. [39] significantly reduced the heterogeneity of the rTMS subgroup (P = 0.41, I² = 3%). Five studies [44, 46, 48, 50, 51] included five sets of data comparing LBT in the TBS group and the control group. There was no significant difference between TBS group and control group [SMD = −0.69 (−1.45, 0.07), P = 0.08], with a moderate heterogeneity among the five sets of data (P = 0.03, I² = 63%). Further sensitivity analysis excluding one study of Yang et al. [51], the moderate heterogeneity of the TBS subgroup was eliminated (P = 0.98, I² = 0%). Four studies [54, 55, 57, 59] included seven sets of data comparing LBT in the tDCS group and the control group. LBT of the tDCS group was lower than the control group [SMD = −0.60 (−0.96, −0.25), P = 0.0009], with no heterogeneity among the seven sets of data (P = 0.74, I² = 0%). One study [52] comparing LBT in the tACS group and the control group. There was no significant difference between tACS group and control group [SMD = 0.04 (−0.86, 0.94), P = 0.93].

The results of analysis showed that the timing of intervention, stimulation protocol, and stimulation site all have significant regulatory effects on the efficacy of NIBS in improving neglect symptoms. The acute phase showed the most significant therapeutic effect, suggesting the crucial role of early intervention in functional reorganization. Excitatory protocols applied to the PPC of the lesioned hemisphere showed a greater therapeutic effect. This finding supports the neural mechanism of enhancing excitability in the damaged hemisphere to alleviate contralateral inhibition and restore interhemispheric balance, while also confirming the key role of this brain region in the spatial attention network.

Publication bias

In our study, LBT, SCT, and CBS were used as primary outcomes. Funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to assess the publication bias of the studies included in this meta-analysis. The asymmetric funnel plots indicated the presence of publication bias. In addition, according to the Egger’s test, LBT (Fig. 5A, Egger’s test p = 0.000) and SCT (Fig. 5B, Egger’s test p = 0.000) showed significant publication bias. Studies reporting CBS were not adequate, so we were unable to perform the test.

Discussion

Our study found that (1) rTMS had a significantly greater reduction in LBT, SCT and CBS compared to control group. (2) TBS had a significantly greater reduction in SCT and CBS compared to control group, while there was no significant difference in LBT. (3) tDCS had a significantly greater reduction in LBT and SCT compared to control group, while there was no significant difference in CBS. (4) tACS had no significant difference in LBT and SCT compared to control group. In terms of the moderating variables, we found that (1) The NIBS treatment in the early stroke phase seems to be more effective than that in the chronic phase. (2) The excitatory protocols applied to the lesioned hemisphere seems to be more effective than the inhibitory protocols applied to the contralesional hemisphere. (3) The stimulation site in the right PPC (Ipsilesional) seems to be more effective than the left PPC (Contralesional).

Based on our analysis, rTMS showed greater therapeutic potential compared to tDCS in reducing neglect severity and improving activities of daily living in stroke patients, consistent with previous studies [29, 30]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the mechanisms of action and stimulation characteristics between the two techniques. rTMS directly modulates cortical neuronal discharge activity through induced currents [60]. Its magnetic field penetration and spatial focus enable strong, concentrated modulation of target brain regions. In contrast, tDCS relies on weak electric fields to gradually adjust neuronal resting membrane potential, resulting in relatively diffuse effects and a more indirect mechanism of action [61]. While cTBS has a more limited scope of action, it also shows positive therapeutic effects, potentially with longer-lasting outcomes [51, 62]. However, present studies vary in the settings of stimulation parameters (intensity, frequency, coil orientation, etc.), and rTMS-related studies often hold advantages in terms of sample size and protocol standardization [63]. Consequently, these methodological differences may to some extent influence the comparative efficacy outcomes across different techniques. Future research should conduct direct comparative research by controlling stimulation parameters and balancing trial design quality to further clarify the efficacy characteristics and applicable scope of different NIBS techniques for treating post-stroke spatial neglect.

Our analysis showed significant difference between TBS and control group in both the SCT and CBS. The advantage of TBS over rTMS is its longer-lasting effects with shorter stimulation periods [19]. Yang et al. [51] found that cTBS showed more pronounced and stable effect than rTMS in improving neglect disorders. However, this significant difference was not observed in the meta-analysis of LBT, potentially due to variations in neglect measurement methods [64, 65]. Furthermore, patient heterogeneity in other sensory and cognitive functions may also lead to inconsistent responses to intervention effect across different measurement tools [66].

Our analysis showed that there is no significant difference between the tACS group and the control group in the LBT and SCT scores. Unlike rTMS and tDCS, which mainly improve neglect symptoms by regulating cortical excitability, tACS is believed to regulate attention orientation by inducing alpha oscillations in the parietal lobe. Relevant studies found that alpha-tACS on the contralateral side of the lesion can promote the transfer of attention to the neglected side [52, 53]. However, our meta-analysis only includes one tACS study that reported the main outcome indicators. Therefore, the current evidence regarding the efficacy of tACS is very limited, and the conclusion is only preliminary. The therapeutic efficacy of tACS for neglect requires further validation through more high-quality randomized controlled trials.

The timing of NIBS intervention may be a potential factor influencing post-stroke neglect recovery. According to our meta-analysis result, intervention during the acute phase (within 2 weeks post-stroke) showed greater therapeutic effect in neglect symptoms and activities of daily living compared to subacute and chronic phases. This may be due to the brain’s heightened neuroplasticity during the early post-stroke period, when abnormal interhemispheric inhibition and spatial attention network imbalances have not yet fully consolidated [67]. Conducting NIBS intervention at this time may more effectively promote functional reorganization within the brain, thereby facilitating more efficient rebalancing of the spatial attention network. Therefore, initiating NIBS interventions during the early phase following a stroke may be more conducive to functional recovery in patients with USN.

Our analysis showed that the excitatory NIBS protocols applied to the ipsilesional hemisphere may show certain advantages in improving the symptoms of neglect. Based on the hemispheric competition model [14], the inhibitory NIBS protocols applied to the contralesional hemisphere achieves functional balance by regulating interhemispheric inhibition. Its effect is constrained by the integrity of the corpus callosum structure and the efficiency of signal transmission, and its action may be limited. The excitatory protocols directly targets the ipsilesional hemisphere, especially the functionally critical and often affected PPC. It enhances the neural activity in this area to directly improve the functional state of the damaged network, and may also promote the functional reorganization of the ipsilateral frontoparietal network [68].

Limitations

This study also has several limitations. First, sample size imbalances exist for certain moderating variables (stroke phase, protocol type, etc.), potentially reducing the reliability of study findings. Second, although sensitivity analysis eliminated moderate heterogeneity among studies reporting LBT, the uneven distribution of studies across subgroups following subgroup analyses may still compromise result robustness. Additionally, inconsistent measurement methods were used for primary outcomes (LBT and SCT) within this meta-analysis. For example, some studies used raw offset distance as an indicator, while others employed percentage, potentially contributing to the high heterogeneity observed in this analysis. Third, publication bias exists in this study, necessitating caution when interpreting the strength of existing conclusions. Finally, some studies failed to report the specific location of lesions and USN subtypes, preventing further analysis.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that rTMS and TBS showed greater therapeutic potential than tDCS and tACS in reducing neglect severity and improving activities of daily living. Additionally, modulating variables may potentially influence the therapeutic effect of NIBS intervention. Specifically, Initiating NIBS intervention during the early post-stroke phase may achieve greater therapeutic outcome. Excitatory NIBS protocols targeting the ipsilesional PPC may yield greater therapeutic potential compared to inhibitory protocols applied to the contralesional PPC.
