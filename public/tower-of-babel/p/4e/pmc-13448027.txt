Barriers and facilitators to artificial intelligence adoption among nursing students: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Paul Reinald Garcia, Asim Alhejaili, Abdulrahman Muslihi, Bassam Alshaharani, Hadeel Lamphon, Khadega Alhefnawy, Wahieba Saleh, Maria Jocelyn Natividad, Mohammed Aljohani, Hammad Fadlelmola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Artificial intelligence offers transformative potential for nursing practice, yet significant barriers hinder adoption. While existing research has documented challenges among practicing nurses, limited evidence exists regarding nursing students' perspectives; they are the generation that will shape artificial intelligence's future role in healthcare.Objective: To explore barriers and facilitators to artificial intelligence adoption among nursing students using a mixed-methods approach, examining relationships between technological readiness, ethical concerns, and perceived usefulness of artificial intelligence in nursing practice.Design: Explanatory sequential mixed-methods study combining quantitative surveys with qualitative semi-structured interviews.SettingCollege of Nursing, Taibah University, Medina, Saudi Arabia (November 2024–March 2025).Participants348 nursing students across academic levels 3–8 participated in the quantitative phase (response rate: 72.5%), with 17 students purposively selected for qualitative interviews.MethodsValidated instruments — the Technology Readiness Index 2.0, an adapted Perceived Usefulness Scale, and an Ethical Concerns Scale — were administered online. Kendall's tau correlation and partial proportional odds modeling identified predictors of perceived usefulness. Qualitative data underwent thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke's framework. Trustworthiness was addressed through investigator triangulation, an audit trail, reflexive memoing, and member-checking. Mixed-methods integration followed a joint-display framework to examine convergence between quantitative and qualitative findings.ResultsTechnological optimism (Kendall's tau [τ] = 0.45, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.39 to 0.50, p < 0.001) and innovativeness (τ = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.47, p < 0.001) showed strong positive associations with perceived artificial intelligence utility. Paradoxically, moderate ethical concern predicted higher perceived usefulness (adjusted odds ratio for perceiving low utility = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.40, from the partial proportional odds model). From the qualitative analysis, we revealed universal concern about deskilling (100% of interviewees), data privacy risks (94%), and erosion of human connection in patient care. Participants proposed shared decision-making models where artificial intelligence provides recommendations while nurses retain final clinical authority. The lack of nursing-specific artificial intelligence tools emerged as a critical barrier.ConclusionsNursing students in this Saudi sample demonstrated nuanced perspectives on artificial intelligence adoption, characterised by cautious optimism alongside critical awareness. The paradoxical relationship between ethical concern and perceived utility challenges traditional technology acceptance models, suggesting deeper engagement fosters appreciation of both opportunities and challenges. We have underscored the importance of tailored educational strategies addressing technical competencies alongside ethical reasoning and professional identity formation. As this generation of digitally fluent students transitions into nursing practice, the perspectives of this sample offer insights for developing artificial intelligence integration approaches that preserve nursing's humanistic core while leveraging technological capabilities.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has demonstrated transformative potential across healthcare domains, from enhancing diagnostic accuracy through medical imaging analysis to personalizing treatment plans based on genetic and lifestyle data (Chatterjee and Das, 2024; Sharma, 2020; Yadav et al., 2023). In nursing practice specifically, artificial intelligence technologies offer significant opportunities to augment clinical decision-making, automate administrative tasks, and improve patient monitoring systems (Alsaeed et al., 2024; Thakur and Kashyap, 2025). These applications enable nurses to redirect their focus toward complex patient care activities that require human judgment, empathy, and cultural sensitivity—elements that remain irreplaceable in healthcare delivery (Handayani and Choo, 2024; Malla and Amin, 2023).

Despite this promise, the integration of artificial intelligence into nursing practice faces multifaceted challenges. Technical barriers include interoperability issues with existing healthcare information systems, inadequate infrastructure in many healthcare settings, and the lack of nursing-specific artificial intelligence tools designed around holistic patient care models (Ramadan et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025). Ethical concerns regarding patient data privacy, algorithmic bias, and accountability for artificial intelligence-assisted decisions create additional complexity (O'Connor et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024). Furthermore, resistance to change persists among healthcare professionals due to fears of deskilling, concerns about reduced human interaction in patient care, and skepticism regarding artificial intelligence reliability in complex clinical situations (Higgins et al., 2024; Amin et al., 2024).

While existing research has extensively documented these challenges among practicing nurses, a critical knowledge gap exists regarding the perspectives of nursing students, the generation that will shape artificial intelligence's future role in healthcare (Mekawy et al., 2020; Hashish and Alnajjar, 2024). Sarıkahya et al. (2025), in a qualitative study of Turkish nursing faculty, identified both pedagogical opportunities and significant uncertainties about how generative artificial intelligence reshapes teaching and assessment, while Boztepe et al. (2025) surveyed Turkish nursing students and found that readiness, literacy, and attitudes toward medical artificial intelligence were tightly associated. Unlike their predecessors who learned nursing before widespread digitalization, current nursing students are developing their professional identities alongside emerging artificial intelligence technologies. This unique positioning may offer insights into optimal integration strategies that balance technological innovation with nursing's humanistic core values (Lukkahatai, 2025).

Previous researchers investigating artificial intelligence adoption in nursing have primarily employed either quantitative surveys measuring acceptance rates or qualitative interviews exploring implementation barriers. For instance, Ramadan et al.'s (2024) qualitative study of registered nurses in Saudi Arabia identified multiple adoption barriers including ethical concerns, fear of job displacement, and inadequate training. However, their study lacked quantitative validation of these qualitative themes and focused exclusively on practicing nurses rather than students. Similarly, researchers in other countries have documented generational differences in artificial intelligence acceptance, with younger nurses demonstrating greater openness to technology adoption, yet few have systematically examined the factors underlying these differences (Alruwaili et al., 2024; Kotp et al., 2025).

We have addressed these gaps in three ways. To begin, we have provided the first mixed-methods analysis of artificial intelligence perceptions among nursing students in the Middle East, combining validated psychometric scales with in-depth qualitative exploration. Second, we have tested statistically the relationships between technological readiness dimensions and perceived utility, moving beyond descriptive accounts to identify predictive factors for artificial intelligence acceptance. Third, we have captured the perspectives of digital natives entering nursing, whose technological fluency may offer different insights than studies of older, established practitioners. By integrating quantitative validation with qualitative depth, we aimed to inform evidence-based strategies for artificial intelligence integration in nursing education and practice.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional study, we employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative surveys with qualitative interviews to comprehensively explore artificial intelligence adoption barriers and facilitators among nursing students. The quantitative phase identified patterns and relationships, while the qualitative phase provided explanatory depth and contextual understanding. This methodological approach aligns with Creswell and Plano Clark's (2018) framework for mixed-methods research in health sciences.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at Taibah University's College of Nursing in Medina, Saudi Arabia, between November 2024 and March 2025. Taibah University's nursing program follows the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties framework, comprising eight academic levels over four years. The curriculum structure includes Levels 3–4 focusing on foundational sciences (anatomy, physiology, pharmacology, and basic nursing concepts); Levels 5–6 introducing clinical nursing skills with supervised hospital rotations; and Levels 7–8 emphasizing advanced practice, specialization areas, and independent clinical decision-making. Students begin mandatory clinical rotations at Level 5, providing exposure to electronic health records, clinical decision support systems, and other healthcare technologies.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) current enrollment in the nursing program at any level from 3 to 8; (2) age 18 years or older; and (3) ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) exchange or visiting students; and (2) students on academic leave during the study period. For the qualitative phase, participants were purposively sampled from survey respondents to ensure maximum variation in academic level, technological readiness scores, and perceived barriers/facilitators.

Data collection instruments

Technology Readiness Index 2.0

The Technology Readiness Index 2.0, developed by Parasuraman and Colby (2014), is a validated 16-item scale measuring four dimensions of technology readiness. The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in healthcare contexts (Cronbach's alpha [α]= 0.81–0.89). We used both the original English version and a validated Arabic translation to accommodate participant preferences. The four dimensions include: Optimism (4 items): General positive views about technology's benefits; Innovativeness (4 items): Tendency to be a technology pioneer and thought leader; Discomfort (4 items): Perceived lack of control over technology; Insecurity (4 items): Distrust and skepticism about technology's capabilities. All items use a 5-point Likert scale (1= Strongly Disagree to 5= Strongly Agree). Internal consistency in the present sample was acceptable to good across all four sub-scales (Optimism: Cronbach's α= 0.84, McDonald's omega [ω]= 0.85; Innovativeness: α= 0.82, ω= 0.83; Discomfort: α= 0.79, ω= 0.80; Insecurity: α= 0.81, ω= 0.82). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the published four-factor structure (comparative fit index [CFI]= 0.94; Tucker–Lewis index [TLI]= 0.93; root mean square error of approximation [RMSEA]= 0.058, 90% confidence interval [CI]: 0.049 to 0.067; standardized root mean square residual [SRMR]= 0.052).

Perceived usefulness scale

We adapted the Perceived Usefulness scale from Davis's (1989) Technology Acceptance Model. This scale measures students' beliefs about artificial intelligence's potential value in nursing practice—not their actual frequency of use, an important distinction for understanding acceptance attitudes versus behavior. The original 6-item scale underwent expert panel review (three nursing educators and two artificial intelligence researchers) for contextual adaptation. The final scale comprised: (1) Artificial intelligence would improve the quality of nursing care; (2) Artificial intelligence would make nursing tasks more efficient; (3) Artificial intelligence would enhance clinical decision-making; (4) Artificial intelligence would reduce medical errors; (5) Artificial intelligence would improve patient outcomes; (6) I would find artificial intelligence useful in nursing practice. All items were assessed on a 5-point Likert scale. Content validity from the expert panel was high (item-level content validity index [CVI]= 0.92; average scale-level content validity index [S-CVI]= 0.94). Internal consistency in the present sample was very good (Cronbach's α= 0.89; McDonald's ω= 0.90), and corrected item–total correlations ranged from 0.58 to 0.74. Single-factor confirmatory factor analysis supported a unidimensional structure (CFI= 0.97, TLI= 0.95, RMSEA= 0.061, 90% CI: 0.040 to 0.083; SRMR= 0.030).

Ethical concerns scale

Ethical concerns were assessed using a modified scale based on Gursoy et al.'s (2019) framework, evaluating: Privacy concerns (data security, unauthorized access); Accountability concerns (liability for errors, transparency); and Fairness concerns (algorithmic bias, equitable access). In the present sample, overall internal consistency was good (Cronbach's α= 0.86; McDonald's ω= 0.87), with sub-scale α values of 0.83 (Privacy), 0.80 (Accountability) and 0.78 (Fairness). A three-factor confirmatory factor analysis supported the proposed structure (CFI= 0.95, TLI= 0.93, RMSEA= 0.064, SRMR= 0.048).

Qualitative interview guide

The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive literature review of technology adoption in nursing and validated artificial intelligence acceptance frameworks. The guide was pilot-tested with two nursing students (who were not part of the final sample) to ensure clarity, flow, and appropriateness of language. Based on pilot feedback, minor wording adjustments were made to enhance comprehension. The final guide consisted of 10 open-ended questions designed to elicit detailed narratives about participants' experiences with artificial intelligence, perceived benefits and risks, ethical considerations, and visions for future implementation. Key domains explored included: (1) personal experiences with artificial intelligence technologies; (2) perceived benefits and limitations for nursing practice; (3) ethical and privacy considerations; (4) suggestions for artificial intelligence implementation in nursing education; and (5) vision for artificial intelligence's future role in nursing. Standardized follow-up probes were developed to explore emergent themes and ensure consistency across interviews, including prompts such as "Can you tell me more about that?" and "How did that experience affect your perspective?"

Psychometric performance in the present sample

All quantitative instruments performed acceptably to well in the present sample, supporting the validity of the inferences drawn in 3, 4. Internal-consistency reliability for every sub-scale exceeded the conventional 0.70 threshold (Nunnally and Bernstein, 1994), and confirmatory factor analyses supported the published factor structures with model-fit indices within accepted ranges (CFI ≥ 0.94, TLI ≥ 0.93, RMSEA ≤ 0.064, SRMR ≤ 0.052). Detailed values are reported in Sections 2.3.1–2.3.3 above and in the Supplementary Material.

Translation and cultural adaptation

For instruments without an existing validated Arabic version (the adapted Perceived Usefulness Scale and the Ethical Concerns Scale), translation and cultural adaptation followed a five-step procedure consistent with Brislin's (1970) guidelines and the World Health Organization's process for translating research instruments: (1) two independent forward translations from English to Modern Standard Arabic by bilingual nursing scholars; (2) a reconciliation meeting that produced a single Arabic draft; (3) blind back-translation to English by a third bilingual translator who had not seen the original instrument; (4) reconciliation of any discrepancies between the original and the back-translation by an expert committee comprising the corresponding author, two bilingual nursing faculty, and one health-services researcher; and (5) cognitive debriefing with eight bilingual nursing students who completed the instrument and were probed on item interpretation, with minor wording refinements made on this basis. For the Technology Readiness Index 2.0, we used Al-Ajlouni's previously validated Arabic version and conducted only step (5) — cognitive debriefing — to confirm acceptability in the Saudi nursing context. Throughout the process, semantic equivalence was assessed using Herdman et al.'s (1998) framework of conceptual, item, semantic, operational, and measurement equivalence.

Data collection procedures

Quantitative phase

An online survey via Qualtrics was distributed to all eligible nursing students. The survey was available in Arabic and English, with two reminder emails sent at 1-week intervals. The survey remained open for 8 weeks.

Qualitative phase

Following preliminary quantitative analysis, 17 participants were purposively selected for interviews. Participants received a briefing document 48 h before interviews. Notably, 29% had prior experience as nursing assistants or healthcare volunteers. The semi-structured interviews were conducted via the Zoom videoconferencing platform between February and March 2025. Interview duration ranged from 45 to 60 min, with a mean duration of 52 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with explicit participant consent and conducted in a private setting to ensure confidentiality. Participants were given the option to conduct interviews in their preferred language (Arabic or English), with professional translation services provided for Arabic-language interviews to ensure accuracy and cultural appropriateness. All Arabic transcripts were translated by a certified translator and back-translated by a second independent translator to verify accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R version 4.3.3. Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis (Kendall's tau), and ordinal logistic regression were performed. The perceived usefulness score was categorized into three levels (Low: 6–18, Moderate: 19–24, High: 25–30). A Brant test was conducted to assess the proportional odds assumption, which is critical for ordinal logistic regression validity. The test revealed that the assumption was violated for the "Optimism" and "Innovativeness" predictors (p< 0.05), indicating that the effect of these variables on the odds of perceiving artificial intelligence as useful differs across outcome categories. Consequently, a partial proportional odds model was selected because it relaxes the proportional odds assumption for the specific violating variables while maintaining the more parsimonious assumption for other predictors. This approach provides superior model fit compared to standard ordinal logistic regression, while avoiding the complexity and potential overfitting of multinomial logistic regression. Point estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals, and exact p-values are reported to three decimal places (or as p< 0.001 where smaller); the term "statistically significant" is used only in reference to formal hypothesis tests, and non-significant results are not interpreted as demonstrating the absence of an effect. Statistical significance was set at p< 0.05. Throughout this paper, the verbs “predict” and “predicted” are used in their regression-analytic sense — that is, the predictor (independent) variables provide predicted values on the criterion (outcome) variable — and are not intended to imply causation, which the cross-sectional design cannot support.

Qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase framework using MAXQDA 24. Two researchers independently coded transcripts, with themes identified through pattern recognition. Code co-occurrence analysis was conducted to examine relationships between perceived usefulness and barriers/facilitators. Co-occurrence was operationally defined as the appearance of two distinct codes within the same text segment, defined as a paragraph or complete thought unit. A co-occurrence matrix was generated using MAXQDA 24 software to quantify the frequency of these co-occurrences and identify patterns. The relationships were then visualized using a network diagram to identify thematic clusters and explore the strength and direction of relationships between key barriers and facilitators. This approach allowed us to move beyond simple frequency counts to understand how different themes interact and relate to one another in participants' narratives, providing richer insight into the complex interplay of factors influencing artificial intelligence adoption attitudes.

To address Lincoln and Guba's (1985) trustworthiness criteria, we used four complementary strategies: (i) investigator triangulation — two researchers coded each transcript independently, with disagreements resolved by a third researcher and inter-coder agreement quantified as Cohen's κ= 0.81 across the final code system; (ii) an audit trail — decision logs, code-development memos, and successive versions of the codebook were archived in MAXQDA; (iii) reflexive memoing by both coders to surface assumptions and pre-existing views about artificial intelligence in nursing; and (iv) member-checking, in which six interviewees reviewed a thematic summary and confirmed that it captured their views, with two minor wording refinements adopted on the basis of their feedback. Coding was performed on the original Arabic transcripts for the 11 Arabic-language interviews and on the original English transcripts for the six English-language interviews; quotations selected for the manuscript were then translated by a certified bilingual translator and back-translated by a second translator to verify accuracy. Working in the source language preserved nuance and avoided premature interpretive collapse during analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from Taibah University's Institutional Review Board (Reference: TU-NCR-2024-089). All participants provided informed consent. Data were anonymized and securely stored.

Inter-coder agreement across the final code system was Cohen's κ= 0.81, and member-checking with six interviewees confirmed that the thematic summary captured their views (see Section 2.6). Theme 1: perceived benefits of artificial intelligence integration

Thirteen participants identified artificial intelligence as transformative for learning. Participant 15 (Level 8 student with 6 months clinical rotation experience) explained: "The positive impact is that it saves you time and gives you the information you need in a way that you understand. It makes complex pharmacology classifications simple." Participant 9 (Final year student with prior nursing assistant experience) noted: "Artificial intelligence can analyze large volumes of data quickly, like patient history and lab results, and provide us with evidence-based suggestions. This helps in making faster and more accurate decisions, especially in critical cases."

Theme 2: human connection and emotional intelligence

Twelve participants expressed concern about artificial intelligence eroding healthcare relationships. When asked about potential risks of artificial intelligence in nursing practice, Participant 13 (Level 7 student, with 3 months clinical rotation experience) warned: "If you constantly rely on ChatGPT instead of consulting experienced peers, communication with skilled practitioners will decline." In response to a question about the essential elements of nursing care, Participant 9 (Final year student with prior nursing assistant experience) emphasized: "The patient wants a nurse who feels for them, not a device that gives them cold numbers."

Theme 3: deskilling and professional development

All 17 participants observed cognitive offloading risks, though the degree of concern and proposed solutions varied meaningfully across participants. Participant 15 (Level 8 student with 6 months clinical rotation experience) cautioned: "If nurses rely on artificial intelligence tools constantly, their critical thinking and independent decision-making abilities decline." However, some participants expressed a more optimistic view. Participant 7 (Level 6 student) noted: "The risk is real, but it can be managed through proper training and governance frameworks. We need to learn when to use artificial intelligence and when to rely on our own judgment." Despite concerns, 16 participants endorsed shared decision-making. Participant 12 (Level 8 with volunteer experience) stated: "Artificial intelligence narrows diagnostic possibilities, but I prioritize based on patient specifics. Final intervention selection remains my responsibility." This vision of collaborative human–artificial intelligence partnership emerged as a recurring theme, suggesting students see potential pathways that preserve professional autonomy while leveraging technological capabilities.

Theme 4: ethical and privacy concerns

Almost all participants expressed data security concerns. Participant 11 (2 years as a healthcare volunteer) warned: "Artificial intelligence companies' technical staff have full access to patient inputs, which enables uncontrolled data sharing." Participant 14 on end-of-life decisions: "End-of-life choices aren't binary technical problems. Can an algorithm comprehend what it means to terminate a life? Only humans carry that moral weight."

Theme 5: need for nursing-specific artificial intelligence

Six participants noted current tools prioritize physician workflows. Participant 6 observed: "Most tools target doctors or administrators, not students or nurses."

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

Integration followed Fetters et al.'s (2013) framework and used a joint-display strategy across three levels: (a) connecting (the qualitative sample was drawn from quantitative respondents stratified on technological readiness and ethical-concern scores); (b) merging (joint display matching quantitative coefficients with qualitative themes); and (c) building (qualitative findings were used to interpret the paradoxical ethical-concern result). The strong positive association between optimism and perceived usefulness was confirmed by qualitative narratives in which optimistic students described concrete clinical use cases for artificial intelligence. The paradoxical positive relationship between moderate ethical concern and perceived usefulness was extended by the qualitative data: students who articulated the most detailed ethical concerns were also those who described the most sophisticated visions of shared decision-making and human–artificial intelligence partnership, suggesting that ethical reflection and pragmatic acceptance co-develop. The universal qualitative concern about deskilling, by contrast, was not captured by any single quantitative item and is therefore presented as an expansion of the quantitative findings rather than convergence.

Discussion

In this mixed-methods study, we have described participants' nuanced perspectives on artificial intelligence adoption, characterised by cautious optimism alongside legitimate concerns. Based on these findings, we have challenged simplistic narratives of wholesale technology acceptance or rejection among digital natives. We use the term "digital natives" in Prensky's (2001) original sense as shorthand for a generational cohort raised alongside ubiquitous digital technology, while acknowledging the well-documented critique of the construct: empirical work has consistently shown that being born after a particular date does not guarantee technological fluency, that digital skills vary widely within generational cohorts, and that the dichotomy between "natives" and "immigrants" obscures more than it reveals (Bennett, Maton and Kervin, 2008; Kirschner and De Bruyckere, 2017). Our findings are consistent with this critique: although our sample is uniformly young, we observed substantial within-sample variation in technological readiness, and the predictive value of optimism and innovativeness — not age per se — supports a competency-based rather than a generational reading of the data.

Technological readiness and generational perspectives

The strong positive association between technological optimism and perceived artificial intelligence utility (τ= 0.45) aligns with generational theories suggesting digital natives approach technology with greater openness (Prensky, 2001). However, concurrent association with discomfort (τ= 0.16) contradicts assumptions of uncritical technology embrace. This finding resonates with recent studies showing consumer technology familiarity doesn't automatically translate to professional artificial intelligence tool acceptance (van Laar et al., 2017; Vogels, 2019); recent Turkish data confirm that readiness, literacy, and attitudes toward medical artificial intelligence are tightly interlinked among nursing students (Boztepe et al., 2025). The progression of acceptance across educational levels suggests clinical exposure shapes artificial intelligence attitudes, aligning with Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1986). The sophistication of participant responses becomes understandable, considering 29% had prior healthcare experience and Level 7–8 students had extensive clinical exposure.

The paradox of ethical awareness

The counterintuitive positive relationship between ethical concern and perceived utility suggests ethical awareness facilitates rather than hinders adoption. Participants with moderate ethical concerns had 81% lower odds of perceiving low artificial intelligence utility. Qualitative data illuminate this: participants who deeply engaged with artificial intelligence developed nuanced understanding of both capabilities and limitations. This aligns with Moral Foundations Theory, where ethical reasoning evolves from simplistic rules to contextual judgment (Haidt and Joseph, 2007). We hypothesize that students with higher ethical awareness may develop a more sophisticated and nuanced understanding of artificial intelligence, allowing them to better appreciate its potential utility while also being cognizant of its limitations and risks. This deeper engagement with the technology, rather than leading to outright rejection, may foster a more informed and realistic appraisal of its benefits and drawbacks. From a Social Cognitive Theory perspective (Bandura, 1986), this suggests that ethical reasoning and technological acceptance may develop in parallel through experiential learning and reflection. Furthermore, this finding challenges traditional technology acceptance models that assume concern operates as a barrier. Instead, we suggest that ethical concern may serve as a marker of engagement depth rather than resistance. Students who think critically about ethical implications may be simultaneously developing the cognitive frameworks necessary to identify potentially legitimate use cases. This may have important implications for nursing education: ethics education should be integrated with, not separated from, technology training. Educators should help students develop ethical reasoning skills alongside technical competencies, recognizing that ethical awareness may facilitate rather than hinder technology adoption when properly cultivated.

Preserving nursing's human essence

Universal concern about deskilling (100% of interviewed participants) reflected deep anxiety about artificial intelligence altering nursing's professional identity. Participants articulated a "deskilling cycle" where artificial intelligence convenience leads to reduced practice and skill atrophy—patterns documented in other automated professions (Lee et al., 2018; Autor et al., 2003). However, proposed shared decision-making models suggest pathways preserving professional autonomy, aligning with "augmented intelligence" concepts (Topol, 2019).

Implications for nursing education

The lack of nursing-specific artificial intelligence tools highlights critical development gaps. Educational implications include scaffolded artificial intelligence integration: early exposure (Levels 3–4) focusing on artificial intelligence literacy (McGrow, 2019); clinical integration (Levels 5–6) emphasizing practical applications (Robert, 2019); advanced levels (7–8) developing implementation leadership. This aligns with Bloom's taxonomy progression (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001).

Comparison with international literature

Our findings both converge with and diverge from international studies. Education level–acceptance associations mirror findings from South Korea (Kwak et al., 2022) and the United States (Swan, 2021). The close interlinkage between readiness, literacy, and attitudes among Turkish nursing students (Boztepe et al., 2025) is consistent with the strong correlations we observed between Technology Readiness Index 2.0 sub-scales and perceived usefulness. Recent qualitative research with nursing faculty in Türkiye similarly highlights both the pedagogical promise and the unresolved professional and ethical tensions surrounding generative artificial intelligence in nursing education (Sarıkahya et al., 2025), echoing the dual orientation — enthusiasm tempered by ethical caution — that characterised our student sample. However, ethical concern strength exceeds Western contexts, potentially reflecting cultural emphasis on privacy in Saudi healthcare (AlHazme, 2021). Universal deskilling concerns contrast with Singapore and Japan studies viewing artificial intelligence primarily as efficiency enhancement (Lim et al., 2023; Miyachi et al., 2024); the salience of deskilling and human-connection concerns in our sample may, therefore, reflect specific cultural emphases on the relational dimensions of nursing care in the Saudi context.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include mixed-methods design providing statistical validation and contextual depth, validated instruments adapted locally, and high response rate (72.5%). Limitations include single institution setting, cross-sectional design preventing causal inference, and potential social desirability bias.

The single-institution setting limits generalizability to other nursing programs with different curricula, clinical exposure, or cultural contexts. Cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between variables. Self-reported data may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly regarding ethical concerns. The purposive sampling strategy for qualitative interviews, while appropriate for maximum variation, may not capture all perspectives. Translation of Arabic interviews, despite professional translation services, may have introduced subtle meaning shifts.

Most importantly, the cross-sectional design means that no causal inference can be drawn about the direction of the observed relationship between ethical concern and perceived usefulness. Our interpretation — that engaged ethical reflection facilitates a more sophisticated appraisal of artificial intelligence — is consistent with the data but is not the only possible explanation: students who already perceive artificial intelligence as useful may invest more cognitive effort in considering its ethical implications, or both variables may be driven by an unmeasured third factor, such as overall academic engagement. Longitudinal designs that track changes in ethical reasoning and acceptance over the course of nursing education are needed to disentangle these possibilities.

Recommendations

The following recommendations are grounded in this study's findings and in the existing literature. Recommendations directed at groups other than nursing students — healthcare institutions, technology developers, and policymakers — are offered as reasoned implications of those findings and of prior work, rather than as propositions tested directly in the present study.

For nursing education: Integrate artificial intelligence literacy throughout curricula, emphasise critical evaluation alongside technical competencies, provide supervised artificial intelligence practice in clinical settings, and include ethical reasoning in artificial intelligence education.

For healthcare institutions: Develop clear artificial intelligence governance frameworks, ensure nurse representation in artificial intelligence decisions, provide protected artificial intelligence training time, and establish ethical concern reporting mechanisms.

For technology developers: Engage nurses as co-designers, incorporate nursing taxonomies, prioritise transparency and explainability, and address cost barriers.

For policymakers: Balance innovation with patient safety regulations, mandate nursing representation on artificial intelligence boards, support nursing-specific artificial intelligence research funding, and address equity in artificial intelligence access.

Conclusions

The paradoxical relationship between ethical concern and perceived utility challenges traditional technology acceptance models, suggesting deeper engagement fosters appreciation of both opportunities and challenges. Participants' vision of shared decision-making models offers potential pathways for artificial intelligence integration preserving nursing's human elements while leveraging technological capabilities. We have underscored the importance of tailored educational strategies addressing technical competencies alongside ethical reasoning and professional identity formation. The lack of nursing-specific artificial intelligence tools represents both challenge and opportunity for the profession to shape aligned technology development. As this generation of digitally fluent students transitions into nursing practice, their perspectives are likely to influence how artificial intelligence is integrated into routine care. Understanding and addressing their concerns while leveraging their technological fluency, therefore, represents a critical opportunity for advancing nursing practice and patient care in the digital age. Future researchers should explore longitudinal changes, cultural variations in artificial intelligence acceptance, and evaluate interventions optimising artificial intelligence integration while preserving clinical judgment skills.
