Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound combined with microbubbles enhances amphotericin B delivery across the blood–brain barrier for improved therapy of cryptococcal meningitis
Authors: Junyu Liu, Zhengtu Li, Dongcheng Li, Jia Liu, Yutian Su, Yifei Leng, Yongming Li, Li Xu, Lan Sun, Xiaohong Su, Xiaotong Li, Changyong Tang, Ying Jiang, Xintao Shuai, Fuhua Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryptococcal meningitis (CM), a life-threatening fungal infection of the central nervous system, faces therapeutic challenges due to poor blood‒brain barrier (BBB) penetration of amphotericin B. This study investigates the efficacy of low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) combined with microbubbles in enhancing BBB permeability and improving prognosis in a murine CM model. Our findings suggest that LIPUS combined with microbubbles treatment significantly increased BBB permeability, leading to elevated cerebral concentrations of amphotericin B and improved fungicidal activity against Cryptococcus neoformans in the brain. Mechanistic investigations revealed that LIPUS-microbubble treatment transiently upregulated vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression levels in cerebral tissues. Notably, these protein expression alterations and the associated BBB permeability enhancement completely normalized within 6 hours post-treatment. Pharmacological inhibition of VEGF abolished the ultrasound-induced BBB permeability increase, indicating that VEGF may serve as a pivotal upstream regulator mediating this transient barrier modulation. Compared with amphotericin B monotherapy, the combined regimen of LIPUS plus microbubbles and amphotericin B achieved a greater reduction in cerebral cryptococcal burden. The India ink staining revealed reduced cryptococcal capsule thickness and smaller yeast cell diameters in treated mice. Furthermore, the intervention ameliorated CM-associated clinical manifestations, including weight loss and neurological deficits, while prolonging survival time. These results suggest the potential of LIPUS-microbubble-mediated BBB modulation as a promising adjunctive strategy to optimize amphotericin B delivery and improve therapeutic efficacy in CM, offering new insights into overcoming the limitations of conventional treatments for neurotropic fungal infections.

Introduction

Cryptococcal meningitis (CM), caused primarily by Cryptococcus neoformans infection, remains a life-threatening fungal infection of the central nervous system (CNS) with mortality rates of approximately 20–60%, even with optimal antifungal therapy (Jarvis et al. 2022; Longley et al. 2008; Molloy et al. 2018; Mwaba et al. 2001). A formidable barrier to effective CM treatment is the blood‒brain barrier (BBB). This highly selective interface, designed to protect the CNS, becomes a significant therapeutic obstacle by severely restricting the penetration of systemically administered antifungals into the infected brain parenchyma. Amphotericin B (AmB), the cornerstone of induction therapy for CM, exhibits notoriously poor BBB penetration. While lipid formulations offer some improvement in toxicity profiles, their CNS bioavailability remains suboptimal, often failing to achieve therapeutic concentrations at the site of infection (Hamill et al. 2010; Strenger et al. 2014). Direct intrathecal administration of antifungals has been explored as a means to bypass the BBB. However, this invasive approach is fraught with substantial drawbacks, including severe neurotoxicity, procedural risks, significant patient discomfort, and the requirement for specialized expertise and repeated interventions (Einstein et al. 1961; Winn 1964). These limitations highlight an urgent and unmet clinical need: the development of safe, effective, and noninvasive strategies capable of transiently enhancing BBB permeability to facilitate adequate antifungal drug delivery to the CNS while minimizing systemic and neurological toxicity.

Recent breakthroughs in biomedical engineering have identified low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) combined with intravascular microbubbles (MBs) as a highly promising physical modality for achieving localized and reversible BBB disruption (Drean et al. 2019; He et al. 2024). The underlying mechanism hinges on the phenomenon of acoustic cavitation. When circulating microbubbles are exposed to pulsed ultrasound waves within the targeted brain vasculature, they undergo volumetric oscillations (stable cavitation) and, under specific conditions, collapse (inertial cavitation). This mechanical activity generates localized shear stresses and microstreaming forces that transiently loosen the tight junctions between cerebrovascular endothelial cells, primarily by modulating key junctional proteins (Kodama et al. 2006; Watanabe et al. 2008). Crucially, this disruption is transient and reversible, creating defined temporal ‘windows’ (typically hours) during which therapeutic agents, including large molecules and nanoparticles, can extravasate into the brain parenchyma without causing permanent structural damage (Kodama et al. 2006; Watanabe et al. 2008). The safety and efficacy of this LIPUS-MB technique have been robustly demonstrated in preclinical models and are rapidly advancing in early clinical trials, primarily focused on enhancing chemotherapy delivery for malignant brain tumors (Arrieta et al. 2024; Habashy et al. 2024; Sonabend et al. 2023) and facilitating the transport of biologics in neurodegenerative disorders (Geraudie et al. 2023; He et al. 2024).

Despite this significant progress in other neurological diseases, the application of LIPUS-MB for enhancing antifungal therapy in CNS infections, particularly for fungal meningitis like CM, remains virtually unexplored. This represents a critical research gap. CM poses unique challenges, including the need for AmB to penetrate the BBB and reach encapsulated yeasts residing within the brain and meninges. The potential of LIPUS-MB to overcome the BBB bottleneck specifically for antifungal agents in the context of an active, life-threatening fungal infection warrants thorough investigation. Understanding whether enhanced drug delivery translates into tangible improvements in microbiological clearance, reduction of key virulence factors (like the polysaccharide capsule), preservation of neurological function, and ultimately, survival, is paramount.

The present study investigates the therapeutic potential of LIPUS-MB-mediated BBB modulation in a murine CM model, with dual focus on pharmacokinetic enhancement of AmB and therapeutic outcomes (Figure 1). Through a multimodal approach combining drug concentration assays, microscopic analysis, fungal burden quantification, and longitudinal behavioral assessments, we systematically evaluate: 1) The profile of AmB accumulation in brain parenchyma post-LIPUS-MB treatment; 2) The correlation between enhanced drug delivery and fungal burden; 3) morphological changes in cryptococcal capsules—a critical virulence determinant; 4) The correlation between enhanced drug delivery and neurofunctional outcomes; 5) survival benefits in the context of optimized antifungal therapy.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles (SonoVue®, Bracco Imaging, Italy; 59 mg/vial) were purchased from Yangxinhe Pharmacy in Dongguan. Amphotericin B deoxycholate (AmB-d) (North China Pharmaceutical Group Corp., China; 5 mg/vial) and liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) (Gilead Sciences, USA; 50 mg/vial) were provided by The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Primers related to the blood‒brain barrier were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) antibody (anti-mouse) (GB111971) was purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Bevacizumab was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; Cat. No. HY-P9906). IBA1 antibody (anti-mouse, CY7217) was purchased from ABWAYS, and GFAP antibody (anti-mouse, 16825-1-AP) was purchased from Proteintech.

Brain-derived Endothelial cells.3 (BEnd.3)

BEnd.3 cells (ATCC® CRL-2299) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). The culture medium was replaced every 2‒3 days.

Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice (specific pathogen-free, SPF grade), aged 6‒8 weeks with body weights of 18‒20 g, were purchased from Guangzhou CarrotBio Co., Ltd. (License No.: SCXK (Yue) 2021-0059, China). Following a 4-day acclimatization period, during which all mice were monitored daily for body weight, coat condition, activity levels, and signs of distress, only those maintaining stable health and a body weight of approximately 20 g were randomly assigned to experimental groups.

In vitro BBB model

BBB model construction

For the BBB model, BEnd.3 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/cm² on Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, Corning) and allowed to differentiate for 7 days. Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured daily using an EVOM2 voltmeter (World Precision Instruments); experiments proceeded when TEER exceeded 30 Ω·cm² (Fan et al. 2023).

LIPUS-MB treatment and TEER measurement

Sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles were reconstituted as per the manufacturer's instructions by diluting the entire vial (59 mg) in 5 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution to obtain a uniform microbubble suspension. For LIPUS exposure, 100 µL of this suspension was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell system. The ultrasonic coupling gel was uniformly applied to the lower chamber of the Transwell system. The lower chamber of the Transwell system was coated with ultrasound coupling gel, and the ultrasound probe was thoroughly coupled to the gel. Ultrasound stimulation was then applied using the following parameters based on a previous study (Nie et al. 2022): frequency 1.0 MHz, intensity 1 W cm−2, duty cycle 50%, for a duration of 1 minute. TEER measurements were subsequently performed at 0, 2, 6, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours post-sonication.

AmB permeability assay

After LIPUS-MB treatment, 5 μg/mL AmB (National Institutes for Food and Drug Control) was added to the upper chamber. Samples from the lower chamber were collected at 2, 4, 8, and 24 hours. AmB concentration was quantified via ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) (Agilent 1290 Infinity II) with a C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phase: methanol: 0.005 mol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (70:30, v/v); flow rate: 1 mL/min; detection wavelength: 383 nm. Column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL. A calibration curve was constructed using AmB standards (0.02–10 μg/mL) prepared in acetonitrile, with peak areas plotted against concentrations via linear regression (GraphPad Prism 8.0.2). The coefficient of determination (R²) was >0.990 for all runs (Figure S1).

Cytotoxicity assessment

BEND3 viability post-LIPUS-MB was evaluated using calcein-AM/PI staining (Guangzhou Huayun Biotech Co., LTD.). Cells were incubated with 4 μM calcein-AM (green, live cells) and 6 μM PI (red, dead cells) for 20 min. Fluorescence was imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8).

CM mouse model

Intracerebroventricular (ICV) inoculation model

Mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally by the injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (2.5 mL/kg) and positioned in a stereotaxic frame. A burr hole was drilled at coordinates (AP: −0.5 mm, ML: −1.0 mm, DV: −2.0 mm). Cryptococcus neoformans strain H99 (5 × 104 CFU in 5 μL PBS) was injected into the right lateral ventricle at 0.5 μL/min using a Hamilton syringe. Successful establishment of CM was confirmed 24 hours postinoculation in randomly selected sentinel mice (n = 3) by microscopic visualization of encapsulated yeasts in India ink-stained samples of cerebrospinal fluid or brain homogenate.

Intranasal inoculation model

To validate key findings in a more translational model that recapitulates natural CNS entry, an intranasal infection model was additionally established. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 3–4%, maintenance: 1.5–2% in 100% O₂) and placed in a supine position. A total of 50 μL of Cryptococcus neoformans H99 suspension (2 × 10^8 CFU/mL in sterile PBS) was instilled dropwise into the nostrils using a micropipette. Infection was confirmed 7 days post‑inoculation by India ink staining of brain homogenates from sentinel mice (n = 3).

LIPUS-MB treatment and AmB administration

At 24 hours post-infection, mice were randomly allocated into six experimental groups: Control: Intravenous (IV) administration of 200 μL 5% glucose daily for 14 days; AmB-d: Daily IV injection of AmB-d (1 mg/kg) dissolved in 5% glucose (Ke et al. 2024);L-AmB: Daily IV treatment with L- AmB (5 mg/kg) in 5% glucose (Ullmann et al. 2022); LIPUS + MB: Sequential IV delivery of 50 μL Sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles microbubbles followed by 200 μL 5% glucose, combined with LIPUS (frequency 1.0 MHz, intensity 1 W cm−2, duty cycle 50%, for a duration of 1 minute) applied transcranially (The in vivo LIPUS-MB protocol was adapted from established methods for non-invasive BBB modulation (He et al. 2024), with specific parameters optimized based on pilot studies in our CM model); LIPUS + MB + AmB-d: Co-administration of 50 μL Sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles with AmB-d (1 mg/kg) IV, paired with LIPUS exposure;.LIPUS + MB + L-AmB: Concurrent IV injection of 50 μL Sulfur hexafluoride microbubbles and L-AmB (5 mg/kg), followed by LIPUS treatment. All IV were maintained for 14 consecutive days, with LIPUS applied immediately after microbubble injection to BBB modulation with drug delivery. For each daily LIPUS session, the following standardized procedure was followed immediately after microbubble injection to achieve BBB modulation: Mice were under sustained anesthesia and positioned prone on a heated pad. The hair over the skull was carefully removed using electric clippers. A layer of standard ultrasound transmission gel was generously applied to the intact scalp to ensure efficient acoustic coupling. A planar ultrasound transducer was fixed in a stereotaxic holder, positioned perpendicular to the skull surface, and lowered until it made gentle contact with the coupling gel layer over the bregma, maintaining a transducer-skull distance of 0 mm (direct contact via gel).

Pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution analysis

At 7 days and 14 days post-treatment, mice were euthanized. Blood, brain, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested. Brain, liver, kidney, spleen, and lung tissues were weighed and homogenized in 1 ml PBS using a tissue homogenizer (60 Hz, 60 seconds). Homogenates were centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 minutes), and 200 μL supernatant was mixed with 200 μL acetonitrile, vortexed (30 seconds), incubated (5 minutes), and centrifuged (10,800 rpm, 5 minutes, 4 °C). The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane into an Agilent-certified vial. Blood was centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 minutes). Plasma (200 μL) was deproteinized with 200 μL acetonitrile using the same protocol as tissue samples. AmB concentrations in plasma and tissue homogenates were analyzed as described in Section 2.4.3.

BBB integrity and pathophysiological assessments

Evans Blue (EB) assay

Mice received 2% EB (4 mL/kg, i.v.) 1 hour before euthanasia. Brains were homogenized in 1 ml 50% trichloroacetic acid solution, incubated at 60 °C for 24 hours, and centrifuged (10,000 × g, 20 minutes). EB concentration in supernatants was measured at 620 nm (UV-Vis spectrophotometer, Thermo Scientific). A standard curve was constructed using EB diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) across a concentration range of 0.8–40 μg/mL. Absorbance was measured at 620 nm, and the curve was generated via linear regression (GraphPad Prism 8.0.2), with acceptance criteria requiring a coefficient of determination (R²) > 0.990 (Figure S2).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from brain tissues using TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 μg total RNA using the SureScript™ First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (GeneCopoeia, USA), incorporating oligo(dT) primers and RNase inhibitor. qRT‒PCR assays were conducted on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using BlazeTaq™ SYBR® Green qPCR Mix 2.0 (GeneCopoeia). The primer sequences targeting tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin-5, ZO-1), endothelial adhesion markers (VE-cadherin, β-catenin, Cx43), efflux transporters (P-glycoprotein) and VEGF are listed in Supplementary Table S1. GAPDH served as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Reactions (20 μL total volume) included 10 μL SYBR Green Master Mix, 2 μL cDNA template, and 0.2 μM forward/reverse primers. Thermocycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 seconds and 60 °C for 30 seconds. Melt curve analysis (60–95 °C) confirmed primer specificity. Relative mRNA expression was calculated via the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with technical triplicates per sample.

Western blot

Brain tissue samples were washed 2–3 times with ice-cold PBS to remove blood contaminants, minced into small pieces, and placed in homogenization tubes containing two 4 mm homogenization beads. A volume of lysis buffer (supplemented with protease inhibitors immediately before use) equivalent to 10 times the tissue volume (adjustable to increase protein concentration) was added, and samples were homogenized using a programmed homogenizer. Following homogenization, samples were incubated on ice for 30 minutes with intermittent vortexing every 5 minutes to ensure complete lysis. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant (total protein extract) was collected. Protein concentration was optionally determined using a BCA assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. For denaturation, the protein solution was mixed with 5 × reducing sample loading buffer in a 4:1 ratio and boiled for 15 minutes before storage at −20 °C. SDS-PAGE was performed using cleaned glass plates assembled in a gel caster. Separation gels were prepared as per kit instructions, allowed to polymerize, and loaded with denatured samples after comb removal. Electrophoresis proceeded in running buffer until the bromophenol blue dye front approached approximately 1 cm from the gel bottom. For immunoblotting, proteins were transferred to a pre-activated (ethanol-treated) 0.45 µm PVDF membrane using a semi-dry transfer system. The transfer sandwich consisted of two sponges and six pieces of filter paper (7 × 9 cm), assembled with three filter papers on each side of the gel/membrane stack, ensuring no bubbles between the gel and membrane. Transfer was conducted at 350 mA constant current for 30 minutes. The membrane was then briefly rinsed with TBST and blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 30 minutes at room temperature on a shaker. After blocking, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking in primary antibody solution diluted appropriately in TBST (as per manufacturer's instructions) targeting VEGF. The primary antibody was recovered, and the membrane was washed three times quickly with TBST followed by three 5-minute washes with TBST on a shaker. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:5000 in TBST for 30 minutes at room temperature with shaking, followed by another series of three quick TBST rinses and three 5-minute TBST washes. For detection, ECL substrates A and B were mixed 1:1 and applied to the membrane for 1 minute. Excess liquid was blotted off, and chemiluminescent signals were captured using an imaging system, saving images in TIFF format. Protein band quantification was performed using AIWBwellTM software (Servicebio Inc.). This AI-driven software automatically corrected image orientation, allowed manual molecular weight marker assignment, detected bands within user-defined rectangles, calculated integrated density values (IDV), and generated a report. VEGF expression levels were normalized to β-actin by calculating the ratio of the VEGF band IDV to the β-actin band IDV.

Fungal burden

Brain tissues were homogenized using a cryogenic tissue grinder precooled to −20 °C. Briefly, tissues were placed in 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing 1 mL sterile PBS and 3 mm stainless steel grinding beads, then homogenized at 60 Hz for 60 seconds. The resultant homogenates were serially diluted 10-fold (10^1, 10^2, 10^3) in PBS. For colony counting, 20 μL aliquots from each dilution were evenly spread onto Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) plates in triplicate. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 hours, after which cryptococcal colonies were manually counted and expressed as log10-transformed values.

Morphological analysis

Cryptococcal diameter and capsular thickness were measured in India ink-stained smears using upright microscope (Leica DM2500LED). To prevent observer bias, all image acquisition and morphometric measurements were conducted by an experimenter who was blinded to the treatment group assignments. Field selection and cell measurement were carried out as follows: For each sample, microscopic fields were selected in a systematic, unbiased manner by moving the microscope stage in a predefined meandering pattern to cover the entire smear area. This ensured that all regions had an equal probability of being analyzed and guaranteed spatial representativeness. From these, at least 27 nonoverlapping fields of view were captured per sample. A minimum of 5 distinct Cryptococcus cells per mouse were measured. Only cells that were well dispersed (not in clumps) and exhibited a clearly defined, intact capsule halo were included in the analysis. Cellular diameter and the surrounding capsule thickness were measured using the scale-calibrated software associated with the microscope (Leica Application Suite X). The mean capsule thickness was calculated as [(total cell diameter)—(cell body diameter)].

Behavioral and survival analysis

Neurological Scoring

Neurological function was evaluated daily using a modified Loeffler 5-point scoring system, with examiners blinded to group assignments (Leib et al. 2001). The criteria were defined as follows:5 points: Normal activity when grasped by the dorsal skin, successful righting reflex within 5 seconds; 4 points: Reduced spontaneous activity but achieves righting reflex within 5 seconds; 3 points: Successful righting reflex with latency > 5 seconds; 2 points: Failure to right despite limb movement; 1 point: Absence of locomotor activity;0 points: Death. Scores were recorded at consistent circadian timepoints (9:00–11:00 AM) to minimize diurnal variability.

Weight and survival analysis

Body weight was recorded daily. Survival was monitored for 60 days post-infection.

Safety assessments

Plasma biochemical analysis

Blood was collected via cardiac puncture into heparinized tubes and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4 °C to obtain plasma. Plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine were measured using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Chemray240, Rayto, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. All assays were performed in duplicate.

Immunohistochemistry

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0, microwave), sections were incubated with 3% H₂O₂ to block endogenous peroxidase, followed by 10% goat serum for 30 minutes. Primary antibodies against IBA1 (1:500) and GFAP (1:500) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing, HRP‑conjugated secondary antibody was applied for 1 hour at 37 °C, and signal was developed with DAB. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The proportion of IBA1‑positive or GFAP‑positive cells was quantified in five randomly selected high‑power fields (400×) per brain region (cerebral hemisphere, brainstem, cerebellum) using ImageJ.

Prussian blue staining

Brain sections were stained with a Prussian blue staining kit (Servicebio, S191029) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, sections were incubated in a 1:1 mixture of potassium ferrocyanide and hydrochloric acid for 1 hour, then counterstained with nuclear fast red. The presence of blue granules indicated iron deposition.

Nissl staining

Brain sections were stained with 0.5% toluidine blue solution for 1–2 minutes, differentiated with 0.5% acetic acid, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Neuronal morphology and Nissl body integrity were examined under light microscopy.

Statistics analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. For in vivo experiments assessing fungal burden and cryptococcal morphology, the sample size was at least n = 6 per group. For all other in vivo measurements and in vitro experiments, at least n = 3 per group was used due to resource constraints. All datasets were first assessed for normality of distribution using the Shapiro‒Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Brown–Forsythe test. Comparisons used one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (GraphPad Prism 8.0). Survival curves were analyzed via the Kaplan‒Meier log-rank test. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

LIPUS-MB enhanced the permeability of the BBB in vitro

An in vitro BBB model was established using BEnd.3 cells (Figure 2A and B). Cell viability assays confirmed the safety of LIPUS-MB treatment (Figure 2C). LIPUS-MB treatment significantly (P = 0.006) reduced TEER values in vitro, decreasing from a baseline of 31.38 ± 0.47 Ω·cm² to 11.18 ± 6.12 Ω·cm² immediately (0 hour) posttreatment, indicating a substantial increase in BBB permeability. TEER values returned to baseline levels 48 hours post-treatment (Figure 2D). Consistent with this, the concentration of AmB in the lower chamber of Transwell inserts significantly increased following LIPUS-MB treatment compared to the control group at multiple time points: 0.36 ± 0.06 μg/ml vs. 0.23 ± 0.07 μg/ml at 2 hours (P = 0.047); 0.68 ± 0.06 μg/ml vs. 0.37 ± 0.07 μg/ml at 4 hours (P = 0.002); 0.81 ± 0.09 μg/ml vs. 0.43 ± 0.06 μg/ml at 8 hours (P = 0.001); and 1.56 ± 0.08 μg/ml vs. 0.56 ± 0.09 μg/ml at 24 hours (P < 0.001) (Figure 2E).

LIPUS-MB increased BBB permeability in vivo

LIPUS-MB treatment significantly enhanced BBB permeability in CM mice in vivo. This effect was transient and reversible, with permeability normalizing within 6 hours post-treatment (Figure 3A). Daily treatment with LIPUS-MB combined with either AmB-d or L-AmB resulted in significantly increased AmB concentrations in brain tissue at both time points. At 7 days, brain AmB levels reached 57.44 ± 17.28 ng/g in the LIPUS + MB + AmB-d group (vs. 37.43 ± 2.72 ng/g in AmB-d alone) (P = 0.032) and 72.87 ± 19.52 ng/g in the LIPUS + MB + L-AmB group (vs. 43.44 ± 6.88 ng/g in L-AmB alone) (P = 0.035). By 14 days, concentrations further increased to 66.94 ± 13.10 ng/g (LIPUS-MB + AmB-d vs. 39.51 ± 2.83 ng/g AmB-d alone) (P = 0.024) and 128.69 ± 43.86 ng/g (LIPUS + MB + L-AmB vs. 53.84 ± 12.56 ng/g L-AmB alone) (P = 0.002). Importantly, AmB concentrations in plasma, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney remained unchanged across all groups (Figure 3B and C and Figure S3).

The underlying mechanisms of LIPUS-MB-mediated BBB opening in CM

To investigate the mechanism of BBB modulation, qPCR analysis revealed a significant transient upregulation of VEGF expression in brain tissue of CM mice immediately (0 hour) following LIPUS-MB treatment (2.41-fold increase vs. control), which returned to control levels by 6 hours (Figure 4A). Western blot analysis confirmed this increase, showing 2.74-fold higher VEGF protein expression (Figure 4B). Crucially, administration of a VEGF inhibitor reversed the LIPUS-MB-induced increase in BBB permeability (Figure 4C).

LIPUS-MB delivery of AmB reduced fungal burden in cerebral tissues of CM

Assessment of cerebral fungal burden in the ICV infection model demonstrated that LIPUS-MB combined with L-AmB significantly reduced fungal burden in brain tissue compared to L-AmB treatment alone at both time points: at 7 days post-treatment, log₁₀(CFU/g) values were 4.72 vs. 5.86 (LIPUS + MB + L-AmB vs. L-AmB alone); by 14 days, this reduction markedly intensified to 1.85 vs. 4.67 log₁₀(CFU/g) (Figure 5A–C). To validate these findings in a more physiologically relevant model, we assessed fungal burden in the intranasal infection model after 14 days of treatment. Notably, the LIPUS + MB + AmB‑d group achieved a significantly greater reduction in fungal burden compared to AmB‑d alone (Figures S4 and S5).

LIPUS-MB delivery of AmB reduced both capsule thickness and cellular diameter of cerebral Cryptococcus neoformans

LIPUS-MB treatment alone significantly reduced both cellular diameter and capsule thickness of Cryptococcus neoformans in brain tissue compared to the untreated control at both time points. At 7 days post-treatment, the LIPUS + MB group showed reduced fungal diameter (25.71 vs. 32.33 μm in the control) and capsule thickness (17.71 vs. 20.58 μm in the control). At 14 days, these reductions were maintained: diameter decreased to 23.37 vs. 31.53 μm, and capsule thickness to 15.43 vs. -19.89 μm in control mice. LIPUS-MB delivery of AmB significantly reduced both cellular diameter and capsule thickness of Cryptococcus neoformans in brain tissue compared to AmB-d alone at both time points. At 7 days post-treatment, the LIPUS-MB + AmB-d group showed reduced fungal diameter (19.18 vs. 26.35 μm) and capsule thickness (12.51 vs. 17.93 μm). By 14 days, these reductions were more pronounced: diameter decreased to 19.66 vs. 23.92 μm, and capsule thickness to 11.90 vs. 14.98 μm (Figure 6A–C).

LIPUS-MB delivery of AmB ameliorated the prognosis of CM

Combination therapy (LIPUS-MB + AmB) significantly improved the clinical prognosis of CM mice compared to AmB monotherapy. This included attenuated weight loss (Figure 7A and B), improved neurological scores (Figure 7C), and dramatically enhanced survival outcomes: during the 60-day follow-up, median survival was 39 days (20.0% survival) for AmB-d and 51 days (40.0% survival) for L-AmB monotherapy groups, whereas the LIPUS-MB + AmB (either AmB-d or L-AmB) group exhibited 100% survival with no mortality (Figure 7D).

Long‑term safety of repeated LIPUS‑MB application

To address the potential cumulative toxicity of daily LIPUS‑MB for 14 days, we performed comprehensive safety evaluations. Plasma biochemical analysis was performed in both the ICV and intranasal infection models after 14 days of daily treatment. In both models, no significant differences in ALT, AST, blood urea nitrogen, or creatinine levels were observed among any of the treatment groups, including the control, AmB-d, L-AmB, LIPUS + MB, LIPUS + MB + AmB-d, and LIPUS + MB + L-AmB (Figures S6 and S7). Immunohistochemical analysis of brain sections showed no significant increase in Iba1‑positive microglia or GFAP‑positive astrocytes in the LIPUS + MB or LIPUS + MB + AmB groups compared to sham‑treated controls, indicating the absence of sustained neuroinflammation (Figures 8 and 9). Prussian blue staining revealed no blue iron deposits in any brain region of LIPUS‑MB‑treated mice, demonstrating no microhemorrhage (Figure S8). Nissl staining showed intact neuronal morphology with clear Nissl bodies in all groups, ruling out neuronal degeneration (Figure S9).

Discussion

This study provides preliminary evidence that LIPUS-MB may represent a safe and effective strategy for transiently and noninvasively enhancing BBB permeability, thereby improving the intracerebral delivery and therapeutic efficacy of amphotericin B in a murine model of CM.

An in vitro BBB model was established, mimicking the structural and functional characteristics of the human BBB. The model utilized Bend3 cells, which are widely recognized as a suitable cellular component for BBB studies due to their ability to form tight junctions and maintain barrier properties (Park et al. 2018; Yuan et al. 2010; Zhang et al. 2020). The live‒dead staining assay confirmed that the LIPUS-MB treatment did not compromise the viability of BEnd.3 cells, indicating that the ultrasound and microbubble parameters employed were safe for endothelial cells. This is crucial, as maintaining cell viability is essential for preserving the integrity of the BBB model during experiments. The significant decrease in TEER values following LIPUS-MB exposure provided direct evidence of enhanced BBB permeability. TEER is a well-established indicator of BBB tightness and is widely used to assess the barrier function of in vitro models (Digiovanni et al. 2024; Lizano et al. 2023; Xue et al. 2022). The transient nature of the permeability increase, with TEER values returning to normal after 48 hours, suggests that the LIPUS-MB treatment induces a reversible opening of the BBB. This transient effect is highly desirable for drug delivery applications, as it allows for controlled drug administration while minimizing the risk of prolonged disruption to the BBB. Furthermore, the detection of significantly higher AmB concentrations in the lower chamber of the transwell inserts confirmed that LIPUS-MB effectively facilitates the transport of drugs across the in vitro BBB model. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the ability of LIPUS-MB to enhance the delivery of various therapeutic agents across the BBB (Geraudie et al. 2023; Habashy et al. 2024; He et al. 2024). These results collectively establish the proof of concept for using LIPUS-MB to enhance BBB permeability in vitro and pave the way for further investigations in vivo.

The in vivo experiments corroborated the findings from the in vitro studies and extended them to a relevant animal model. The results demonstrated that LIPUS-MB treatment significantly increased BBB permeability in CM mice, with the effect being reversible and returning to normal levels after 6 hours. This transient increase in permeability is advantageous for clinical applications, as it allows for the delivery of drugs to the brain while maintaining the protective function of the BBB in the long term. The measurement of AmB concentrations in various tissues revealed a notable increase in brain tissue levels in the LIPUS-MB-treated mice compared to those receiving AmB alone. This finding highlights the specificity of the LIPUS-MB approach in enhancing drug delivery to the brain, as no significant changes were observed in the concentrations of AmB in plasma, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney. This tissue-specific delivery is particularly important for antifungal treatment in CM, where achieving effective drug concentrations in the brain is challenging due to the limited ability of AmB to cross the BBB (Hamill et al. 2010; Strenger et al. 2014). By using LIPUS-MB to facilitate AmB penetration into the brain, it is possible to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of the drug while potentially reducing systemic side effects. Previous research has also shown that ultrasound combined with microbubbles can effectively increase BBB permeability and improve drug delivery to the brain (Habashy et al. 2024; He et al. 2024; Rezai et al. 2020). For example, a study by Fan et al. (2013) showed that ultrasound and MBs could enhance the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents to brain tumors. Another study demonstrated that this approach could increase the brain uptake of superoxide dismutase 1 (Ediriweera et al. 2025), suggesting its potential for treating various neurological disorders. Our results further add to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of LIPUS-MB for enhancing BBB permeability and targeted drug delivery to the brain.

To elucidate the mechanisms by which LIPUS-MB increases BBB permeability in CM mice, we investigated the expression of various genes and proteins associated with BBB function. The results revealed a significant upregulation of VEGF expression in the brains of CM mice following LIPUS-MB treatment, with levels returning to normal after 6 hours. VEGF is a well-known angiogenic factor that enhances BBB permeability (Matsuno et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2020, 2024). Our data show a strong temporal correlation between LIPUS-MB treatment and VEGF upregulation, but this does not prove direct causation. In our study, the increase in VEGF expression was accompanied by a transient increase in BBB permeability, suggesting that VEGF may be a key mediator of the LIPUS-MB-induced BBB opening. Additionally, the use of a VEGF inhibitor was able to reverse the LIPUS-MB-mediated increase in BBB permeability, further confirming the involvement of VEGF in this process. However, these experiments do not demonstrate a direct physical interaction between LIPUS-MB and the VEGF protein; the initial events upstream of VEGF remain to be fully elucidated. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating the role of VEGF in modulating BBB permeability. For instance, a study by Zhang et al. (2000) showed that VEGF could promote blood‒brain barrier leakage in the ischemic brain. The lack of significant changes in the expression of other BBB-related genes and proteins (occludin, claudin-5, ZO-1, VE-cadherin, β-catenin, Cx43, and P-glycoprotein) suggests that the LIPUS-MB-mediated BBB opening in CM mice may primarily involve the VEGF signaling pathway. Future studies could further explore the intricate interactions between LIPUS-MB, VEGF, and other signaling pathways involved in BBB regulation to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

The therapeutic efficacy of LIPUS-MB-mediated AmB delivery was evaluated by assessing the fungal burden in the brain tissues of CM mice. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in the number of Cryptococcus neoformans in the LIPUS-MB plus AmB group compared to the AmB alone group at both 7 days and 14 days post-treatment. This benefit was also confirmed in the intranasal infection model, where LIPUS-MB combined with AmB-d significantly reduced fungal burden compared to AmB-d alone. This indicates that the enhanced delivery of AmB to the brain achieved through LIPUS-MB leads to more effective antifungal activity and a greater reduction in fungal load. This finding is of great clinical significance, as it suggests that LIPUS-MB could potentially improve the outcomes of antifungal therapy for CM by overcoming the limitations of poor drug penetration into the brain. Furthermore, the analysis of Cryptococcus neoformans capsule thickness and cellular diameter revealed that LIPUS-MB treatment alone significantly reduced both capsule thickness and cell diameter compared to untreated controls, indicating that the mechanical forces from LIPUS-MB directly attenuate this critical virulence factor. When combined with AmB, LIPUS-MB plus AmB resulted in even more pronounced reductions. The capsule of Cryptococcus neoformans is a critical virulence factor that helps the fungus evade host immune responses and survive within the host (Casadevall et al. 2019; Kozel 1995). Thus, the remarkable survival benefit likely arises from a synergistic mechanism: LIPUS-MB both enhances AmB delivery across the BBB and directly weakens the fungal capsule, rendering the pathogen more susceptible to both the drug and host immune clearance. These results highlight the multifaceted benefits of combining LIPUS-MB with AmB for the treatment of CM and suggest that this approach could potentially reduce treatment duration and improve patient outcomes.

The impact of LIPUS-MB-mediated AmB delivery on the prognosis of CM was assessed by evaluating various parameters, including body weight, neurological behavior, and survival rate of the mice. The results showed that the LIPUS-MB plus AmB group exhibited significantly less weight loss and better neurological behavior compared to the AmB alone group. Notably, the LIPUS-MB-only group, despite showing capsule thinning, did not exhibit improved survival or a significant reduction in fungal burden, indicating that direct mechanical attenuation of virulence alone is insufficient to control infection without effective antifungal drug delivery. These findings collectively indicate that the enhanced delivery of AmB to the brain via LIPUS-MB leads to improved therapeutic outcomes and a better prognosis for CM. This is likely attributed to the more effective reduction in fungal burden and the associated reduction in inflammation and tissue damage in the brain. The improvement in neurological behavior and survival rate further underscores the clinical relevance of this therapeutic approach, as these parameters directly reflect the overall health status and quality of life of the animals. Furthermore, comprehensive safety evaluations (including plasma biochemistry, IBA1/GFAP immunohistochemistry, Prussian blue staining, and Nissl staining) demonstrated that 14 consecutive days of LIPUS-MB treatment did not induce detectable hepatic, renal, or neurotoxicity, supporting the long‑term tolerability of this strategy. Previous studies have also shown that improving drug delivery to the brain can enhance treatment outcomes in various neurological disorders (Ediriweera et al. 2025; He et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). For example, a study by Ediriweera et al. (2025) demonstrated that ultrasound-mediated drug delivery improved motor function in a mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Another study showed that enhanced delivery of anti-Alzheimer's disease drugs to the brain using ultrasound and microbubbles resulted in improved cognitive function and reduced amyloid-beta plaques (He et al. 2024). Our results in the context of CM add to this body of evidence and support the potential of LIPUS-MB as a valuable tool for improving the prognosis of patients with CNS infections and other neurological conditions.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the ICV inoculation model used in most experiments bypasses the intact BBB and does not fully recapitulate natural infection, which is an inherent limitation. Second, while our VEGF inhibitor experiment confirmed that VEGF signaling is essential for LIPUS‑MB‑induced BBB opening, it does not prove a direct physical interaction between LIPUS-MB and the VEGF protein. The initial events upstream of VEGF remain to be fully elucidated. Third, several key quantitative measurements (including in vivo amphotericin B tissue concentrations, Evans Blue permeability, qPCR, and Western blot) were performed with a sample size of n = 3 per group due to funding constraints. Finally, the in vitro BBB model based on BEnd.3 cells, while widely used for preliminary screening, has TEER values substantially below those of the physiological BBB in vivo. Therefore, the absolute permeability values and transport kinetics from this model may not fully recapitulate the intact neurovascular unit.

Conclusion

LIPUS-MB may represent a promising noninvasive strategy for targeted CNS drug delivery. By transiently modulating BBB permeability, it enhances the therapeutic efficacy of amphotericin B against CM, improving survival and neurobehavioral outcomes. These preliminary findings warrant further investigation into the clinical translation of LIPUS-MB for the treatment of CNS infections and other neurological disorders requiring enhanced drug delivery.
