ACSF2 Functions as a regulatory switch for PARP inhibitor sensitivity in platinum-sensitive and homologous recombination–proficient high-grade serous ovarian cancer
Authors: Sijia Cheng, Jiayi Li, Yunzhi Ling, Jiaru Luo, Dan Zhou, Tianle Mao, Tingting Luo, Yanyang Tu, Jinlin Du, Minghui Zhou, Qingsong Qin, Yanqin Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor resistance represents a major clinical challenge in both homologous recombination deficiency-positive (HRD+) and deficiency-negative (HRD−) high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC). ACSF2 is aberrantly overexpressed in various malignancies and associated with poor patient prognosis. However, its role and underlying mechanism in PARP inhibitor resistance remain unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate whether ACSF2 modulates PARP inhibitor resistance by regulating p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1) nuclear foci formation and to determine if ACSF2 influences histone H4 Lysine 20 di-methylation (H4K20me2)-mediated 53BP1 recruitment via KMT5B in HRD− HGSOC.MethodsUsing machine learning, ACSF2 was identified as a key resistance gene to PARP inhibitor. This discovery was based on an integrated analysis of six platinum-sensitive recurrent ovarian cancer (OC) cell lines (HRD±), four PARP inhibitor-resistant patient-derived organoid (PDO) models, and 181 °C tissue samples. Isogenic resistant cell lines were established to validate the role of ACSF2. The functional impact of ACSF2 perturbation was assessed by evaluating 53BP1, breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein (BRCA1), and phosphorylated H2A histone family member x (γH2AX) foci formation, along with epigenetic markers (KMT5B and H4K20me2). The therapeutic potential of targeting ACSF2 was further examined through in vitro, in vivo, and PDO models.ResultsACSF2 was provisionally identified as key mediator of PARP inhibitor resistance, with high expression correlating with poorer median overall survival in patients (42.00 vs. 49.60 months, P < 0.05). Similarly, ACSF2 acted as a key regulator of Niraparib sensitivity. Overexpression of ACSF2 increased the IC50 (72 h) from 146.89 ± 9.08 µmol/L to 192.27 ± 4.24 µmol/L (P < 0.001), while its knockdown reduced the IC50 to 92.81 ± 2.62 µmol/L (P < 0.001). Mechanistically, ACSF2 suppression significantly enhanced 53BP1 nuclear foci formation (positive cells increased from 12.00 ± 2.90% to 76.65 ± 7.23%; foci count increased > 12-fold; P < 0.001) and suppressed Niraparib-induced BRCA1 foci (median foci reduced from 38.00 [23.24–52.76] to 3.86 [1.50–6.21]), concomitantly promoting DNA damage. This process was associated with upregulation of H4K20me2, which is regulated by KMT5B.ConclusionACSF2 promotes Niraparib resistance in HRD− HGSOC by modulating the KMT5B-H4K20me2 axis, thereby inhibiting 53BP1 nuclear aggregation. Targeting ACSF2 restores 53BP1-mediated DNA damage response and re-sensitizes tumors to PARP inhibitor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04426-9.

Introduction

High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) is the most common and aggressive subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer, with the majority of patients diagnosed at an advanced stage [1]. HGSOC patients have a poor prognosis [2], with a recurrence and metastasis rate exceeding 70% within three years [3] and a five-year survival rate below 30% [4, 5]. Approximately half of HGSOC patients exhibit homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), which renders them sensitive to Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, such as olaparib and Niraparib, based on the principle of synthetic lethality [6, 7]. PARP inhibitors significantly improve prognostic indicators like progression-free survival (PFS) in HRD-positive (HRD+) patients [8–10] and have become an important targeted therapeutic option.

Notably, clinical observations have shown that some HRD-negative (HRD−) patients also benefit from PARP inhibitor therapy, a phenomenon that extends beyond the traditional synthetic lethality paradigm. For instance, the ATHENA-MONO study [11] reported that rucaparib treatment increased PFS from 9.1 to 12.1 months in HRD− patients; the PRIMA trial [12] demonstrated that Niraparib improved PFS from 5.4 to 8.4 months. The PRIME clinical trial involving 384 Chinese patients with stage III ovarian cancer also indicated that Niraparib extended PFS in HRD− HGSOC patients [9]. Based on this, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Niraparib in 2017 for maintenance therapy in platinum-sensitive recurrent HGSOC, irrespective of breast cancer type 1 or 2 susceptibility protein (BRCA1/2) mutation (HRD+) status. However, with longer-term follow-up and in-depth analysis, recent evidence from the PRIMA trial indicates that HRD− HGSOC patients do not benefit from Niraparib and may even experience impaired overall survival (OS) [13]. Consequently, in 2025, the FDA no longer recommends Niraparib for maintenance therapy in HRD− HGSOC. This contradiction between short-term PFS benefit and lack of long-term OS gain suggests differential response patterns to PARP inhibitors in HRD− HGSOC patients, largely attributable to tumor cells eventually developing resistance through various mechanisms. Additionally, 32% of HGSOC patients exhibit weak or no response to Niraparib therapy [9], and the majority of patients treated with Niraparib (regardless of HRD status) ultimately develop acquired resistance [14]. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the common mechanisms of PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD− tumors is critically important.

The mechanisms of PARP inhibitor resistance are complex and diverse, including restoration of homologous recombination (HR) repair (HRR) function, increased drug efflux, and replication fork protection [15, 16]. Among these, the loss or functional inactivation of p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1), which suppresses the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway and partially restores the antagonized HR pathway, is a key factor in PARP inhibitor resistance [16]. During PARP inhibitor-induced DNA damage, 53BP1 is recruited to DNA damage sites to repair double-strand breaks (DSBs). However, this repair is low-fidelity, secondarily leading to replication fork collapse and competitively inhibiting HRR, thereby promoting tumor cell apoptosis [17–19]. This process is regulated by the histone methyltransferase lysine methyltransferase 5B (KMT5B), which catalyzes histone H4 Lysine 20 di-methylation (H4K20me2), a crucial modification for 53BP1 recognition and binding to DNA damage sites [20]. Whether 53BP1 is involved in Niraparib resistance in HRD− HGSOC and its specific molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Based on this, our preliminary study, through omics screening, initially identified acyl-CoA synthetase family member 2 (ACSF2) as a common key prognostic gene associated with Niraparib resistance in both HRD− and HRD+ HGSOC tumors. ACSF2 is involved in fatty acid oxidation (FAO), regulating cellular energy metabolism and ferroptosis, thereby influencing tumor progression and drug resistance [21–23]. Current research confirms that ACSF2 is significantly overexpressed in various malignancies and has been identified as a potential diagnostic or prognostic biomarker for specific cancer types [23–25]. Furthermore, ACSF2 indirectly regulates the activation and function of PARP1 and other DNA damage response proteins [26–29]. Yet, it remains unknown whether ACSF2 modulates 53BP1 and its mediated NHEJ in HRD− HGSOC, thereby affecting PARP inhibitor sensitivity, and whether KMT5B-regulated H4K20me2 plays a significant role in this process. To validate this, this study will preliminarily verify role of ACSF2 in regulating Niraparib sensitivity in HRD− HGSOC cells by constructing resistant cell lines. Concurrently, by intervening in ACSF2 expression, we aim to clarify how ACSF2, via KMT5B, regulates H4K20me2, thereby influencing 53BP1 nuclear recruitment and its involvement in Niraparib resistance. By revealing ACSF2 as a novel regulator of PARP inhibitor resistance and highlighting the role of 53BP1, this study deepens the understanding of resistance mechanisms and provides targets for developing new reversal strategies. Additionally, stratifying patients based on ACSF2 expression levels and 53BP1-positive expression could advance precision oncology and potentially extend the benefits of PARP inhibitors from the limited cohort of HRD+ patients to a broader group of HRD− HGSOC patients.

Materials and methods

Establishment of Niraparib-resistant homologous recombination-proficient high-grade serous ovarian cancer cell line

SKOV3 and CAOV3 cells (SK-OV-3 and CAOV-3; Guangzhou Xinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) were continuously cultured for 8 months in McCoy’s 5 A (biospecies-0011; Guangzhou Xinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) or DMEM (XY-007; Guangzhou Xinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) complete medium containing 21 or 8 µmol/L Niraparib (SST-C038; ACON Biotechnology (Hangzhou) Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) to induce mild Niraparib resistance (SKOV3/Niraparib; CAOV3/Niraparib), achieving a resistance index (RI) > 5. The cells were maintained at 37℃ with 5% CO2, and the medium was replaced every three days. Authentication by short tandem repeat analysis confirmed the unique identity of the OC cell lines and the absence of both cross-contamination and mycoplasma contamination (Supplementary Fig. 1).

SiRNA-mediated knockdown of ACSF2 in homologous recombination-proficient high-grade serous ovarian cancer cells

SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells were seeded in 6-well plates. At 65% confluence, the cells were transfected for 6 h with a serum-free medium mixture containing UltraFectin liposomal transfection reagent (SST-C043; ACON Biotechnology (Hangzhou) Co., Ltd.) and ACSF2 siRNAs (30 pmol, synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; sequences provided in Supplementary Table 1) at a 1:1 ratio. The medium was then replaced with complete medium with or without 21 µmol/L Niraparib for an additional 66 h. Total RNA and protein were extracted to assess ACSF2 expression, and the siRNA with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments. A nonspecific siRNA negative control (siRNA NC) was used as a control.

Construction of ACSF2 overexpression vector

The ACSF2 coding sequence (CDS; CCDS ID: 11567.1, length: 1848 nt; synthesized by Sangon Biotech) was cloned into the pLenti-CMV-GFP plasmid using BmtI (R3658; NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) and PspOMI (R0653; NEB) double digestion and T4 DNA ligase (M0202V; NEB). The constructed plasmid (pLenti-CMV-GFP-ACSF2 CDS) was transformed into DH5α competent cells (C2987H; NEB), and positive clones were selected on Amp+ LB solid medium (L2010; Solarbio, Beijing, China). After verification by colony PCR and sequencing, the plasmid was amplified and purified using a plasmid extraction kit (DP103; Tiangen Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with pLenti-CMV-GFP-ACSF2 CDS using UltraFectin liposomal transfection reagent for 6 h, followed by culture in complete medium for 66 h before ACSF2 expression analysis. The empty pLenti-CMV-GFP vector served as a control (pLenti-CMV-GFP-NC).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells treated with ACSF2 siRNA or overexpression vectors and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using StarScript III one-tube genome-free reverse transcription premix reagent (A230-02; GeneStar, Beijing, China). Quantitative PCR was performed using target-specific primers (ACSF2: forward 5’-GCCTCAGCTACGTTCAGGG-3’, reverse 5’-GCATAGGAGTTAGGTCCCCACAT-3’; GAPDH: forward 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’, reverse 5’-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’) and 2× RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix (A301-01; GeneStar). ACSF2 mRNA levels relative to GAPDH were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCq method.

Western blot analysis

SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells were treated with Niraparib (21 µmol/L) alone or in combination with ACSF2 siRNA or pLenti-CMV-GFP-ACSF2 CDS. Total protein or nuclear protein (extracted using the NuCLEAR™ kit, NXTRACT-1KT; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (KTD3001; Guangzhou LaiSai Bio., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). For each sample, 20 µg of denatured protein was separated by electrophoresis: 53BP1 and BRCA1 were resolved on 8% Tris SwePAGE precast gels (G2306-10; Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China), while other proteins (ACSF2, phosphorylated H2A histone family member x (γH2AX), KMT5B, H4K20me2) were analyzed using 10% SDS-PAGE (G2037-50T; Servicebio). Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for 20 min, followed by 120 V until optimal separation. Proteins were transferred to membranes at 200 mA for 100 min at low temperature. Primary antibodies included: ACSF2 rabbit polyclonal (1:1000, 16140-1-AP; Proteintech (Wuhan Sanying Biotech Co., Ltd.), Wuhan, Hubei, China), BRCA1 (D-9) mouse monoclonal (1:1000, sc-6954; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), 53BP1 rabbit monoclonal (1:1000, ab175933; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), phospho-Histone H2A.X (Ser139) mouse monoclonal (1:1000, sc-517348; Santa Cruz), KMT5B mouse monoclonal (1:1000, GT282; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), H4K20me2 mouse monoclonal (1:1000, GT282, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Histone H4 rabbit polyclonal (1:1000, PA5-102611; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), Lamin B1 (A-11) mouse monoclonal (1:1000, sc-377000; Santa Cruz), and GAPDH mouse monoclonal (1:25000, 60004-1-Ig; Proteintech). Secondary antibodies were goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:20000, AB205718; Abcam) and goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, A0216; Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells were seeded on coverslips in 12-well plates. At the logarithmic growth phase, cells were transfected with ACSF2 siRNA or pLenti-CMV-GFP-ACSF2 CDS and co-treated with Niraparib for 72 h. Cells were fixed with cold 10% formaldehyde for 10 min, blocked with 5% BSA (A19f33-25G; Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated with primary (ACSF2 antibody, 1:100; 53BP1 antibody, 1:500; BRCA1 antibody, 1:500; γH2AX antibody, 1:500; KMT5B antibody, 1:200) and secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with 1 × 4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution (GD3410-50ML; Genview, Beijing, China). Protein localization (e.g., 53BP1, BRCA1, γH2AX) was visualized using a confocal microscope. The secondary antibody used was Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, GB21303; Servicebio).

Detection of histone methylation levels

Histones were extracted and purified from cells using an acid extraction method [30]. Briefly, cells were incubated with a nuclear isolation buffer containing 10% NP-40 (mlsw-1931; mlbio, Shanghai, China) for 5 min to lyse and collect nuclei. The nuclei were then treated with five volumes of 0.4 N sulfuric acid at low temperature for 3–4 h to solubilize histones. Histones were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid, washed sequentially with acetone containing 0.1% HCl once and 100% acetone twice (270725; Sigma-Aldrich), and air-dried. 13% SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis for separation. A total of 100 µg of purified histones were concentrated and mixed with a 2:1 mixture of anhydrous isopropanol/propionic anhydride (278475, 240311; Sigma-Aldrich), followed by incubation at 37℃ for 15 min for propionylation. This propionylation step was repeated to fully block all unmodified and monomethylated lysine residues [31]. The histones were then digested overnight (6–8 h) at 37℃ with sequencing-grade trypsin (trypsin-to-histone ratio = 1:50) (11418475001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to generate peptide mixtures suitable for mass spectrometry. Two additional rounds of propionylation were performed using regular (hydrogen-type) and deuterated (heavy hydrogen-type) propionic anhydride, respectively. Peptides were desalted and purified using C18 STAGE-Tips (Empore, Oxford, PA, USA), separated by reverse-phase C18 chromatography with a water-acetonitrile gradient elution over 65 min, and analyzed via an LTQ-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Full-scan spectra were acquired over m/z 290–1000 with a resolution of 30,000, followed by data-dependent fragmentation. Ion chromatograms extracted from Orbitrap data were integrated to identify and quantify the relative abundance of target histone peptides and their methylation modifications [32].

Cell proliferation assay

SKOV3/Niraparib, CAOV3/Niraparib and their parent cells from each treatment group were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (61135; Sigma-Aldrich) for microscopic examination. Additionally, cells were stained with 0.4% trypan blue (T8154; Sigma-Aldrich) for cell counting, and the proliferation rate was calculated.

Organoid culture and treatment

Fresh ovarian cancer specimens were obtained from three patients under informed consent. The specimens were washed with PBS containing double antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin) (15140122; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), tetracycline (T7660; Sigma-Aldrich), and a mycoplasma removal agent (Bios-MK01-002 J; Guangzhou Xinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) to eliminate contaminants, followed by thorough removal of adipose and necrotic tissues. Under sterile conditions, the tissues were minced into fragments (~ 1–2 mm3) using a scalpel and mechanically dissociated through a 100 µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. The filtered cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin (Bios-G0056; Guangzhou Xinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) for 30 min at 37℃, treated with three volumes of red blood cell lysis buffer (4℃, 10 min) (GS3309-100ML; Genview) to remove erythrocytes, and centrifuged at 4℃ (1200 rpm) to pellet the cells. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting cancer cells were resuspended in basal medium. The cell suspension was mixed with Matrigel matrix gel (CLS354230; Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA) at a 1:1 ratio, and 50 µL of the mixture was seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2 for gel polymerization, followed by addition of organoid culture medium to initiate organoid growth. After one week, organoids were dissociated and counted, and approximately 2,000 live cells/well were reseeded. Cell suspensions were mixed with collagen P (CLS354236; Corning) at a 1:1.3 ratio, and 10 µL of the mixture was added per well and incubated for 30 min to solidify. Subsequently, 54 µL of organoid culture medium was added. After reaching an appropriate growth phase, the organoids were randomly divided into three experimental groups: Control group (Maintained under standard culture conditions without any intervention), Niraparib group (Treated with Niraparib at a concentration equivalent to half the IC50 value, diluted in the culture medium and Niraparib + ACSF2 siRNA group (Initially transfected with ACSF2 siRNA complexed with a liposomal transfection reagent in serum-free medium for 6 h. The medium was then replaced with complete culture medium containing Niraparib for continued incubation). Following 72 h of treatment, organoid viability was quantified using a commercial ATPase assay kit (HY-K0302; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [33].

Drug sensitivity testing

SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV/Niraparib cells subjected to transfection were treated with Niraparib (0, 1, 10, 100, 500, and 1000 µmol/L) for 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8; KTA1020; Abbkine, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Organoid viability after treatment with siRNA NC or ACSF2 siRNA combined with Niraparib (0, 0.1, 1, 10, 50, 100, and 200 µmol/L) was measured using an ATP assay kit.

Cell line-derived xenograft model

To establish a SKOV3/Niraparib cell line with inducible ACSF2 knockdown, a triple-vector lentiviral system was employed. The system comprised three distinct lentiviral constructs: (1) a pLenti-CMV-Luc-Puro vector (17477; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) expressing firefly luciferase (Luc) and a puromycin resistance gene; (2) a pRetroX-Tet-On Advanced vector (632104; Takara Bio Dalian Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, USA) for constitutive expression of the reverse tetracycline-controlled transactivator (rtTA) protein; and (3) a pLKO-tet-on-ACSF2 shRNA-Puro vector (constructed with the assistance of Sangon Biotech) encoding a doxycycline (Dox)-inducible short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting ACSF2 (shRNA sequence: 5’-CACCGGAAGATCTCTCACTTCAATTCTCGAGAATTGAAGTGAGAGATCTTCC-3’). Each vector was individually packaged into lentiviral particles by co-transfecting them with packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G) (12260 and 12259; Addgene) into HEK293T cells (293T; Guangzhou Xinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) using a standard protocol. The resulting viral supernatants were collected, concentrated, and titered. Subsequently, SKOV3/Niraparib cells were transduced sequentially with the three lentiviral preparations. Following transduction, cells were subjected to continuous antibiotic selection for over four weeks using puromycin (P4512; Sigma-Aldrich) (to select for pLenti-CMV-Luc-Puro and pLKO-tet-on-ACSF2 shRNA-Puro integration) and/or G418 (10131035; Gibco) (to select for pRetroX-Tet-On Advanced integration). This process yielded a stable polyclonal population, designated SKOV3/Niraparib (Luc), capable of expressing Luc and exhibiting Dox-inducible knockdown of ACSF2. These cells (5 × 105 cells) were injected into the ovaries of 6–8-week-old female nude mice (nu/nu) (purchased from Guangdong Provincial Medical Laboratory Animal Center, Foshan, Guangdong, China). After tumor formation confirmed by ultrasound, mice were divided into two groups: Niraparib group (intraperitoneal injection of 40 mg/kg Niraparib, 5 times/week) and ACSF2 shRNA + Niraparib group (fed with 1 mg/mL DOX (D9891; Sigma-Aldrich) to induce ACSF2 shRNA expression, twice/week). Treatment lasted 4 weeks. Tumor size was monitored weekly via ultrasound. For in vivo imaging, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 15 mg/mL D-luciferin sodium salt (150 mg/kg, L2912; Invitrogen) and anesthetized with 3% isoflurane 8 min prior to imaging. Mice were euthanized if body weight loss exceeded 20% or tumor volume surpassed 2 cm3.

Ultrasound imaging and tumor volume measurement

Orthotopic ovarian tumor growth was dynamically monitored via transabdominal high-frequency ultrasonography. Imaging was performed using a FUJIFILM VisualSonics Vevo 2100 system (Toronto, ON, Canada) equipped with an MS550D linear array transducer (40 MHz). Mice were maintained under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia and secured in the supine position on a thermostatic platform. Following application of acoustic coupling gel, multi-directional scanning was conducted to visualize the tumor and surrounding anatomical structures. The lesion was fully characterized, and the longest diameter (L) and its perpendicular short diameter (W, in mm) were recorded. Reflecting the irregular morphology of ovarian masses, tumor volume (V) was calculated using the modified prolate ellipsoid formula: V= (L×W2) / 2.

Screening of PARP inhibitor resistance-associated genes and prognostic analysis in high-grade serous ovarian cancer

To characterize the transcriptional landscape of PARP inhibitor resistance, we queried the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository using the keywords ‘ovarian cancer’, ‘PARP inhibitor’, and ‘resistance’. We selected datasets containing bulk RNA-sequencing data from established cell lines, patient-derived xenografts (PDX), or organoid models. After excluding studies lacking clear phenotypic contrast between sensitive and resistant groups, four datasets met our inclusion criteria: GSE153867, GSE191231, GSE229119, and GSE235980. This integrated cohort encompassed a total of 52 transcriptomic profiles, consisting of 32 resistant samples (derived from chronic Olaparib treatment or inherent resistance models) and 20 treatment-sensitive controls (Supplementary Table 2). Data were quality-controlled and batch-corrected using the “edgeR” package (v3.40.2) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Resistance-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened with the “limma” package (v3.54.2) (adjusted P < 0.05, |log2 fold change| > 1). Univariate Cox analysis was performed using the “survival” (v3.8-3) and “survminer” (v0.5.0) packages to identify prognosis-related DEGs. A support vector machine (SVM) model was constructed with the “e1071” package (v1.7-14) and validated in an independent dataset to derive a PARP resistance-associated prognostic gene signature.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R v4.1.0 (R Core Team 2023, Vienna, Vienna, Austria), RStudio Server v1.4.1717 (Posit PBC, Boston, MA, USA), and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Group comparisons for continuous variables used the Wilcoxon test; survival analysis employed Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests; correlations were assessed by Spearman’s method. Normality was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD, compared by t-test or Mann-Whitney test. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ACSF2 was identified as a important characteristic highly expressed gene in PARP inhibitor resistance

To identifysignature genes associated with PARP inhibitor resistance in HGSOC, we systematically screened transcriptomic data from six cell lines, four patient-derived organoid models, and 181 clinical HGSOC samples. We initially identified 1058 DEGs in PARP inhibitor-resistant samples. Subsequently, to refine the biomarker discovery, we constructed SVM models based on distinct prognostic gene sets: 20 DEGs significantly correlated with both OS and DFS, 52 OS-related DEGs, and 73 DFS-related DEGs. Feature selection was performed within each model. Notably, ACSF2 was selected as a core feature gene in both independent SVM models (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 3). ACSF2 served as a characteristic gene for PARP inhibitor resistance, showing significantly elevated expression in PARP inhibitor-resistant HRD− and HRD+ HGSOC cells, including SKOV3, OVCA432, and UWB1.289, as well as in organoid models (P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 3). In clinical OC samples, high ACSF2 expression was associated with poorer prognosis. ACSF2 levels were significantly lower in tissues from patients with high median OS (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B and C). These results suggest that ACSF2 is a potential marker of PARP inhibitor resistance in platinum-sensitive HGSOC. Notably, clinical trials such as PRIMA [9, 12, 13] have shown that HRD− HGSOC patients receiving Niraparib maintenance therapy had significantly shorter median progression-free survival (mPFS) compared to HRD+ patients (15.63 months vs. not reached), underscoring HRD status as a key determinant of PARP inhibitor response. However, ACSF2 may appears to promote resistance in the HRD− population.

To further validate that ACSF2 overexpression mediates PARP inhibitor resistance, we established a Niraparib-resistant SKOV3 (SKOV3/Niraparib) (resistance index, RI > 4; Fig. 1D and E) and CAOV3 (CAOV3/Niraparib) (P < 0.001; Fig. 1F and G) cell lines by chronic exposure to Niraparib for 8 or 6 months. Consistent with our omics screening, ACSF2 expression was significantly upregulated in SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells at both the mRNA (Fig. 1H and I, P < 0.01) and protein levels (Fig. 1J and K, P < 0.001), with notably enhanced nuclear accumulation of ACSF2 (Fig. 1L and M). These results validate that Niraparib-resistant OC cells exhibit ACSF2 overexpression, aligning with the initial transcriptomic signature.

ACSF2 serves as a key functional gene in PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD− high-grade serous ovarian cancer

HRD− HGSOC exhibits higher proliferative capacity under Niraparib treatment compared to sensitive counterparts. To validate the functional impact of ACSF2 on Niraparib resistance in HRD− HGSOC, we modulated ACSF2 expression in SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells via siRNA-mediated knockdown (P < 0.001; siRNA-1 achieved maximal silencing efficiency of 82.37% and was used for subsequent experiments) (Fig. 2A and G) or overexpression (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2H and M). Proliferation assays showed that, compared to untreated SKOV3/Niraparib controls (CON, 100.00 ± 1.98%), ACSF2 knockdown alone reduced cell proliferation by 53.19% (46.81 ± 6.20%, P < 0.001), while Niraparib monotherapy decreased proliferation by 17.42% (82.58 ± 3.35%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2N and O). In contrast, ACSF2 overexpression increased proliferation by approximately 68% (168.24 ± 14.04%, P < 0.001). The combination of ACSF2 siRNA and Niraparib reduced proliferation to 36.52 ± 2.47%, which was significantly lower than either treatment alone (P < 0.001; Fig. 2N and O). Conversely, ACSF2 overexpression attenuated the anti-proliferative effect of Niraparib (129.17 ± 10.48% vs. Niraparib alone, P < 0.001; Fig. 2N and O). Similar results were observed in CAOV3/Niraparib cells. ACSF2 siRNA enhanced the inhibitory effect of Niraparib on cell proliferation, whereas ACSF2 overexpression attenuated this effect (P < 0.001; Fig. 2N and P). These results collectively indicate that ACSF2 is critical for mediating Niraparib resistance in HRD− HGSOC.

Drug sensitivity assays in SKOV3/Niraparib cells validated that ACSF2 upregulation diminished cellular responsiveness to Niraparib, elevating the IC50 from 146.89 ± 9.08 µmol/L to 192.27 ± 4.24 µmol/L (P < 0.001; Fig. 2Q). Conversely, ACSF2 silencing sensitized SKOV3/Niraparib cells to Niraparib, lowering the IC50 to 92.81 ± 2.62 µmol/L. The drug sensitivity results in the CAOV3/Niraparib cell model were consistent with this finding (P < 0.001; Fig. 2R). This suggests that targeting ACSF2 can re-sensitize HRD− HGSOC cells to Niraparib.

ACSF2 inhibition is pivotal for 53BP1 foci formation and DNA damage repair

To further elucidate whether ACSF2 regulates DNA damage repair to mediate Niraparib resistance in HRD− HGSOC, we examined changes in relevant nuclear protein expression (Fig. 3A and B) and foci formation (Fig. 3C and D). ACSF2 knockdown was associated with DNA damage, as evidenced by an 8.80-fold increase in γH2AX foci formation (5.52 [2.84, 8.19] vs. 48.53 [23.23, 73.84]; P < 0.01; Fig. 3A), a rise in the percentage of γH2AX foci-positive cells from 19.59 ± 6.77% to 70.88 ± 14.57 (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A), and elevated nuclear γH2AX protein levels (0.44 ± 0.13 vs. 0.88 ± 0.05; P < 0.01; Fig. 3B). Concurrently, 53BP1 nuclear foci formation (3.43 [2.68, 4.18] vs. 43.77 [37.87, 49.67]; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A), the percentage of 53BP1-positive cells (12.00 ± 2.90 vs. 76.65 ± 7.23; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A), and 53BP1 protein expression (0.23 ± 0.17 vs. 1.10 ± 0.16; P < 0.001; Fig. 3C) were significantly enhanced. In contrast, no significant changes in BRCA1 nuclear expression and foci formation were observed (P > 0.05; Fig. 3).

The Niraparib-alone treatment group exhibited trends similar to ACSF2 knockdown, with increased 53BP1 foci formation (foci count: 14.16 [11.98, 16.34], P < 0.001; positive cells: 36.27 ± 11.48%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3A) and nuclear protein levels (0.75 ± 0.17; P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). However, unlike ACSF2 silencing, Niraparib treatment also promoted BRCA1 recruitment, elevating BRCA1 foci formation (2.45 [1.44, 3.47] vs. 38.00 [23.24, 52.76]; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A) and nuclear expression (0.36 ± 0.17 vs. 1.63 ± 0.26; P < 0.001; Fig. 3C), while γH2AX levels showed no significant change (P > 0.05; Fig. 3C). These data indicate that both ACSF2 knockdown and Niraparib trigger a DNA damage response, but divergent repair pathways are engaged. ACSF2 silencing promotes 53BP1 foci formation without concomitant BRCA1 induction, a profile consistent with a bias toward NHEJ engagement and the accumulation of unrepaired breaks; whereas Niraparib alone elicits a coordinated response involving both 53BP1 and BRCA1, suggesting an attempted HRR-mediated repair.

Notably, ACSF2 overexpression did not elicit detectable alterations in DNA damage response markers, with no significant alterations in 53BP1, BRCA1, or γH2AX foci formation or protein levels (P > 0.05; Fig. 3A and C). In the combination group (ACSF2 siRNA + Niraparib), SKOV3/Niraparib cells showed further enhanced 53BP1 nuclear expression (1.01 ± 0.11 vs. Niraparib alone; P < 0.01; Fig. 3C) and foci formation (32.04 [26.11, 37.97]; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A), while suppressing Niraparib-induced BRCA1 upregulation (nuclear expression: 0.28 ± 0.16; foci count: 3.86 [1.50, 6.21]; P < 0.001 and P < 0.01, respectively; Fig. 3A and C). This was accompanied by a marked increase in γH2AX foci (63.94 [35.71, 92.17]; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A) and protein expression (0.98 ± 0.04; P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). Conversely, ACSF2 overexpression combined with Niraparib yielded results similar to Niraparib monotherapy (Fig. 3).

The results obtained from the CAOV3/Niraparib model (Fig. 3B and D) were consistent with those from the SKOV3/Niraparib model, supporting the robustness of these observations. The antagonism between 53BP1-mediated NHEJ and BRCA1-dependent HRR in DNA damage repair was evident. Collectively, these findings support that ACSF2 silencing enhances 53BP1 foci formation and biases repair toward NHEJ, thereby sensitizing HRD− HGSOC to Niraparib.

H4 histone dimethylation is involved in ACSF2-mediated regulation of 53BP1 nuclear foci formation

KMT5B, a methyltransferase, catalyzes dimethylation at H4K20me2, which facilitates the recruitment and proper binding of 53BP1 to DNA damage sites [34, 35]. To investigate whether KMT5B-dependent H4K20me2 generation plays a critical role in ACSF2-regulated 53BP1 nuclear aggregation, we modulated ACSF2 expression with or without Niraparib treatment and assessed changes in H4K20me2 and its upstream regulator KMT5B. Compared with controls, ACSF2 overexpression significantly suppressed both KMT5B mRNA (P < 0.01 or P < 0.05; Fig. 4A) and nuclear protein levels (P < 0.01; Fig. 4B and C). Niraparib alone had no significant effect on KMT5B mRNA (P > 0.05; Fig. 4A). However, it elevated nuclear KMT5B protein levels, and concurrently increased the levels of the methylated product H4K20me2 (P < 0.05; Fig. 4B and C). When ACSF2 was overexpressed in the presence of Niraparib (pLenti-CMV-GFP-ACSF2 CDS + Niraparib group), the Niraparib-induced upregulation of KMT5B mRNA (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A), protein (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B and C), and H4K20me2 levels (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B and D) was suppressed. Total H4 levels remained unchanged across all treatments (P > 0.05; Fig. 4B and C).

Conversely, ACSF2 knockdown significantly enhanced KMT5B mRNA (P < 0.001; Fig. 4A), protein (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B and C), and nuclear H4K20me2 levels (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B and E). The combination of ACSF2 silencing and Niraparib resulted in even higher KMT5B and H4K20me2 expression than either intervention alone (P < 0.001; Fig. 4A–E).

To further probe the KMT5B–53BP1 axis, we inhibited KMT5B pharmacologically using A-196 (S7983, Selleck) in SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 4F and L). In SKOV3/Niraparib, KMT5B inhibition significantly reduced nuclear 53BP1 foci formation (18.30 [8.43–28.17] vs. 6.13 [4.14–8.12]; P < 0.001; Fig. 4G) and the percentage of foci-positive cells (10.89 [9.35–12.43] vs. 2.739 [2.07–3.41]; P < 0.001; Fig. 4G), while H4K20me2 levels were also significantly lowered (P < 0.05; Fig. 4J K). In CAOV3/Niraparib cells, we also validated that concomitant KMT5B inhibition reduced H4K20me2 levels (P < 0.01; Fig. 4L and M) and significantly decreased both 53BP1 expression and focus formation (P < 0.01; Fig. 4I and L). These results suggest that ACSF2 silencing may upregulates KMT5B to enhance H4K20me2 levels, promoting 53BP1 recruitment in HRD− HGSOC cells.

Furthermore, compared with Niraparib alone, co-treatment with a KMT5B inhibitor significantly promoted SKOV3/Niraparib and CAOV3/Niraparib cells proliferation (P < 0.001; Fig. 4N). In SKOV3/Niraparib cells, this combination resulted in a 3.94-fold increase in the Niraparib IC50, rising from 149.60 to 739.30 µmol/L (Figs. 1E and 4O). Similarly, in CAOV3/Niraparib cells, the IC50 increased by 2.10-fold, from 115.40 to 358.00 µmol/L (Figs. 1G and 4P). This indicates that KMT5B inhibition promotes PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD− HGSOC. Furthermore, in the presence of a KMT5B inhibitor, ACSF2 knockdown via siRNA increased the IC50 of Niraparib from 79.40 to 132.00 µmol/L in SKOV3/Niraparib cell (Fig. 4O) and from 63.19 to 108.20 µmol/L in CAOV3/Niraparib cell (Fig. 4P). In summary, targeting ACSF2 to modulate specific methyltransferase activity represents a potential strategy to overcome clinical PARP inhibitor resistance.

ACSF2 silencing potentiates the in vivo efficacy of Niraparib in HRD− high-grade serous ovarian cancer

To validate whether ACSF2 inhibition enhances the antitumor activity of Niraparib in vivo, we established an orthotopic xenograft model of Niraparib-resistant HRD− HGSOC by injecting female immunodeficient nude mice with SKOV3/Niraparib (Luc) cells stably expressing DOX-inducible ACSF2 shRNA (Fig. 5A). DOX administration (2 µg/mL) significantly suppressed ACSF2 expression (P < 0.001 or 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 5A). After tumor formation, mice received Niraparib treatment for 4 weeks. Compared with the control group, Niraparib monotherapy reduced total tumor weight from 0.39 ± 0.08 g to 0.28 ± 0.06 g (Fig. 5B, P < 0.05). Additionally, the bioluminescent signal (reflecting tumor cell burden) decreased by 26.806% (95.70 ± 8.08 × 108 vs. 70.05 ± 15.65 × 108; P < 0.001; Fig. 5C), and tumor volume declined by 39.92% (1117.78 ± 234.72 mm3 vs. 671.52 ± 54.25 mm3; P < 0.01; Fig. 5D).

In the combination group (ACSF2 siRNA + Niraparib), tumor weight was further reduced to 0.13 ± 0.07 g (P < 0.05 vs. control; P < 0.01 vs. Niraparib alone; Fig. 5B). The bioluminescent signal (0.04 ± 0.02 × 108; P < 0.001 or P < 0.01) and tumor volume (53.34 ± 4.60 mm3; P < 0.001 or P < 0.01) were also significantly suppressed compared with both control and Niraparib monotherapy groups (Fig. 5C–D). Nude mice exhibited no significant changes in body weight during this period (Fig. 5E).

Parallel experiments using patient-derived organoids (culture workflow in Fig. 5F) corroborated these findings. Patient-derived organoids were established from a clinically diagnosed HRD− HGSOC case. Drug sensitivity testing revealed a IC50 of Niraparib exceeding 50 µmol/L (Supplementary Fig. 4), with both values surpassing 1 µmol/L, suggesting intrinsic resistance to Niraparib. Organoids treated with Niraparib alone or combined with ACSF2 siRNA exhibited smaller sizes, reduced counts, and irregular morphology with shrinkage, darkening, and peripheral debris—features suggestive of apoptosis—particularly in the combination group (Fig. 5G). Viability assays confirmed that Niraparib alone reduced organoid viability by 65.581% (100.00 ± 6.77% vs. 73.51 ± 9.87%; P < 0.001; Fig. 5G). ACSF2 silencing (validated in Supplementary Fig. 5B, P < 0.001) further suppressed viability to 34.42 ± 10.12% (P < 0.01; Fig. 5G). The IC50 of Niraparib in resistant organoids decreased by 82.34% (59.40 µM to 10.49 µM; Fig. 5H and I) upon ACSF2 knockdown. These results indicate that combining ACSF2 silencing with Niraparib exerts enhanced suppressive effects on organoid ex vivo, highlighting the potential of targeting ACSF2 to augment Niraparib efficacy in HRD− HGSOC.

Discussion

Our findings underscore the critical role of 53BP1 in modulating the response to PARP inhibitor therapy in HRD− HGSOC. It is well-established that loss or functional impairment of 53BP1 can partially restore HR proficiency, thereby conferring PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD+ tumor cells. In HRD+ cancer cells, underlying defects in high-fidelity HR repair (e.g., due to BRCA1/2 mutations) create a state of synthetic lethality upon PARP inhibitor exposure, which causes replication fork stalling and DNA DSBs [36, 37]. These cells become reliant on error-prone backup repair pathways like NHEJ for survival, and the accumulation of DNA errors ultimately leads to cell death [38]. Depletion of 53BP1 may suppress the error-prone NHEJ pathway, potentially allowing for the activation of alternative repair mechanisms (e.g., Polθ-mediated TMEJ) or residual HR function, enabling cell survival under PARP inhibitor pressure [17, 39–41].

In our study, we observed that functional inhibition of 53BP1 also contributes to PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD− tumors. Concurrently, we detected a corresponding increase in BRCA1 foci formation, which aligns with the established theory that 53BP1 loss can lead to partial HR restoration and subsequent PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD+ contexts. However, this regulatory paradigm is not absolute but is highly dependent on the specific cellular and genetic background. For instance, in an HRD− setting with intrinsically proficient HR, mere inactivation of 53BP1 might be insufficient to induce significant PARP inhibitor resistance, as the robust HR machinery can effectively repair DNA damage. Nevertheless, evidence also suggests that 53BP1 can influence the choice of repair pathway and modulate PARP inhibitor response, possibly by altering higher-order chromatin structure, even in HR-proficient backgrounds [42, 43].

The recruitment of 53BP1 to DSB sites is a critical step for initiating NHEJ. This process involves KMT5B, which catalyzes H4K20me2, creating a key “recruitment platform” for 53BP1 at DSB sites [44]. The molecular basis lies in the specific recognition and binding of the H4K20me2 epigenetic mark by the tandem Tudor domain of 53BP1. Following a DSB, KMT5B is recruited to the damage site, where it facilitates the conversion of H4K20 monomethylation (H4K20me1) to dimethylation (H4K20me2). This H4K20me2 mark serves as a beacon for 53BP1 accumulation [35]. Once bound, 53BP1 recruits effector complexes like RIF1 and Shieldin, which inhibit DNA end resection, thereby promoting NHEJ and competitively inhibiting HRR [35, 45]. Consequently, loss or inhibition of KMT5B reduces H4K20me2 levels, impairing proper 53BP1 recruitment and function. This shifts the balance of DNA damage repair towards HRR, a finding consistent with our observation that KMT5B inhibition reduces PARP inhibitor sensitivity in HRD− HGSOC.

Our study identified ACSF2 as a novel prognostic gene associated with PARP inhibitor resistance. We found that ACSF2 negatively regulates KMT5B expression and H4K20me2 levels, thereby modulating the chromatin environment necessary for 53BP1 recruitment. Consistent with existing research linking ACSF2 to metabolic pathways like FAO, which can indirectly influence chromatin modification and PARP1 activity. Our bioinformatic analysis revealed that high ACSF2 expression correlates with poorer OS in HRD− HGSOC patients and serves as a key factor in PARP inhibitor resistance across HRD statuses [22, 23]. Experimentally, we confirmed that ACSF2 is significantly overexpressed in PARP inhibitor-resistant HRD− HGSOC models. Upregulation of ACSF2 decreased PARP inhibitor sensitivity, whereas its knockdown enhanced PARP inhibitor sensitivity. These findings were further validated in vivo, where silencing ACSF2 potentiated the inhibitory effect of PARP inhibitor on the growth of resistant HRD− HGSOC xenografts in nude mice.Although no direct evidence previously linked ACSF2 to the DNA damage response (DDR) or PARP inhibitor sensitivity via DDR modulation, its involvement in FAO suggests a potential indirect role in influencing protein acetylation affecting chromatin state and PARP1 function [29, 46]. Based on this premise, we assessed DNA damage markers following ACSF2 intervention. Our results demonstrated that ACSF2 knockdown increased DNA damage, particularly when combined with Niraparib, as indicated by elevated γH2AX foci formation. Furthermore, we found that ACSF2 silencing promoted 53BP1 nuclear aggregation, which coincided with enhanced γH2AX foci formation. Our data suggest its function extends beyond bioenergetics to actively sculpt the epigenetic landscape of DNA repair. This aligns with the emerging concept that metabolic enzymes act as “moonlighting” regulators, where the flux through metabolic pathways directly influences histone modification states to dictate therapeutic outcomes.

A pivotal implication of our work extends the understanding of 53BP1 regulation in HRD− contexts. Prevailing models posit that 53BP1 loss confers PARP inhibitor resistance primarily in HR-deficient tumors (e.g., BRCA1/2-mutated) by restoring resection and homology-directed repair [47]. However, we demonstrate that ACSF2-mediated suppression of the KMT5B-H4K20me2 axis impairs 53BP1 recruitment even in HRD− tumors. This suggests that 53BP1’s function as a key regulator of PARP inhibitor sensitivity is not dependent solely on BRCA status, but rather exists on a continuum modulated by the cellular metabolic state [48]. By dampening KMT5B activity, ACSF2 establishes a chromatin environment less permissive for NHEJ initiation, thereby lowering the threshold for PARP inhibitor.

These findings carry significant translational weight. Currently, PARP inhibitor resistance in HRD− patients remains an enigma, as they lack the canonical BRCAness signatures that predict sensitivity [49]. Our data indicate that ACSF2 is a critical biomarker. High ACSF2 expression correlates with poor overall survival and diminished PARP inhibitor efficacy, suggesting that metabolic rewiring rather than genetic reversion may be a dominant driver of resistance in the clinic [50]. This resonates with recent observations in other malignancies, where elevated FAO rates correlate with platinum resistance and altered DNA repair fidelity [51].

Furthermore, the discovery of the ACSF2-KMT5B-H4K20me2-53BP1 axis provides a mechanistic foundation for overcoming resistance. While previous strategies have focused on directly inhibiting DNA repair kinases (including ATM, ATR and so on), our findings suggest that modulating the metabolic regulators of chromatin state may offer an alternative therapeutic avenue. Modulating ACSF2 activity could potentially restore the H4K20me2 “recruitment platform” necessary for 53BP1 aggregation, thereby re-sensitizing refractory tumors to PARP inhibition [52]. These data provide a rationale for exploring combinatorial regimens involving PARP inhibitors and FAO pathway modulators, particularly in preclinical models or patient subgroups characterized by elevated ACSF2 expression.

In summary, this study provides initial evidence that ACSF2 modulates PARP inhibitor sensitivity in HRD− HGSOC by regulating 53BP1 nuclear aggregation and DNA damage response via the KMT5B-H4K20me2 pathway. However, limitations remain. First, our interrogation of the ACSF2-KMT5B axis relied exclusively on pharmacological inhibition with A-196. While this recapitulated ACSF2 modulation phenotypes, we lack complementary genetic validation (e.g., KMT5B overexpression or CRISPR silencing) to confirm on-target specificity and exclude off-target effects. Second, the causal relationship between FAO flux and PARP inhibitor sensitivity remains correlative; we have not demonstrated that manipulating FAO alone is sufficient to alter KMT5B activity or drug response. Mechanistic studies, including stable isotope-resolved metabolomics and metabolite rescue experiments, are required. Third, the in vivo relevance of KMT5B inhibition requires substantiation. Current validations were restricted to cell lines, further validation in xenograft models is required to confirm the role of KMT5B in ACSF2-mediated regulation of PARP inhibitor resistance. Finally, we lack direct evidence of functional epistasis between ACSF2 and KMT5B. Combinatorial genetic or pharmacological experiments (e.g., co-silencing) are needed to test whether KMT5B functions downstream of ACSF2 in a linear pathway and to map their hierarchical relationship definitively. Addressing these gaps is crucial to validate the ACSF2-KMT5B axis as a therapeutic target. Further investigation is needed to identify the specific molecular bridge or signaling cascade connecting ACSF2 to KMT5B regulation within the nucleus.

Conclusion

ACSF2 contributes to PARP inhibitor resistance in OC, potentially through modulation of the KMT5B-H4K20me2 axis to suppress 53BP1 recruitment. Targeting ACSF2 re-sensitizes resistant cells, supporting its evaluation as a potential therapeutic target to mitigate PARP inhibitor resistance in OC.
