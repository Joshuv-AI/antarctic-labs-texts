Theater-Based Program for Collective Healing Among Asian American Health Care Professionals
Authors: Elizabeth Y. Li, Jessica A. Xu, Elizabeth Ma, Sarah J. Ho, Catherine T. Yu, Jennifer J. Han, Michael B. Zarra, Tala Barias, Pooja R. Amin, Jessica X. Ouyang, Steven Sust, Neha Sharma, Eunice Y. Yuen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This qualitative study assesses the acceptability, feasibility, and participant-reported outcomes of an improvisational skit and discussion program to teach health care trainees about cultural dilemmas and biases faced by Asian American health care professionals.

Introduction

Asian American individuals are diverse, with over 40 ethnic origins, and their population is growing within the health care workforce.1 Often aggregated into a monolithic racial category, Asian American individuals can feel unheard when they are valorized over historically marginalized groups, excluded from equity efforts, and aggregated into harmful stereotypes.2,3 These dynamics are rooted in a long history of structural racism against Asian individuals in the US, from the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 through Japanese American incarceration during World War II and to contemporary anti-Asian violence.4 The model-minority stereotype, perpetual-foreigner trope, and systematic exclusion of Asian American individuals from health equity data have rendered these experiences largely invisible within public health and medical education.2,4

Asian American health care professionals and trainees frequently encounter what we refer to as cultural dilemmas: situations in which individuals must navigate conflicting cultural expectations, identity-related stressors, and systemic barriers within professional settings. These dilemmas operate at multiple levels: internalized stigma and racism at the individual level; microaggressions, model-minority stereotyping, and in-group or out-group tensions at the interpersonal level; and institutional policies that exclude Asian American individuals from diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives at the structural level.2,3,5 The cumulative psychological impact constitutes a form of racial trauma that compounds the demands of clinical training, contributing to burnout, diminished well-being, professional disengagement, and an eroded sense of belonging.6,7 The COVID-19 pandemic and geopolitical tensions have intensified anti-Asian sentiments and further exacerbated these trends.5,8,9

Ko et al3 have called for a multifaceted approach that includes engaging institutional leaders, recognizing the diverse identities of Asian subgroups, examining systems of oppression to address anti-Asian racism, reflecting on Asian American roles in perpetuating stereotypes, and initiating healing from racial trauma.2,3 Vela et al10 have argued that practitioner-level bias interventions will fall short without accompanying structural changes. This insight is consistent with liberation psychology, which emphasizes that sustainable healing requires both critical consciousness at the individual level and collective action at the systemic level.11,12 A necessary first step is creating spaces for collective healing in which participants can process racial trauma across individual, interpersonal, and systemic levels, develop coping and advocacy skills, and cultivate peer-mentorship relationships.13 A webinar series called Acting Together was developed to serve this function: not only to name cultural dilemmas but to provide a structured intervention equipping participants with practical skills, community support, and psychological safety to address them.

In health care training, interactive theater and improvisational exercises have been used to teach communication,14,15,16 explore sensitive topics, and practice responses to challenging scenarios.17 Much of the literature focuses on general communication skills. Acting Together draws on Augusto Boal’s Theatre of the Oppressed.18,19 Developed in the 1970s as an extension of Paulo Freire’s critical pedagogy, Theatre of the Oppressed uses participatory theatrical techniques to foster critical consciousness and collective action against systems of oppression.18 Its central method, Forum Theatre, transforms audience members into “spect-actors” who can interrupt a scene depicting oppression and rehearse alternative responses, fostering critical consciousness and shifting participants from passive observers to agents of change.20 Unlike traditional didactic methods, interactive theater is particularly suited to Acting Together’s goals toward social transformation, because participants can embody and practice resistance to structural inequities.16,21

Acting Together was designed as a culturally tailored intervention modeled on this participatory framework, directly addressing the psychological impacts of implicit bias, racial trauma, and cultural dilemmas at individual, interpersonal, and systemic levels among Asian American professionals in health care.22,23 This article describes Acting Together’s development and evaluates the program’s impact across 3 domains: (1) acceptability and feasibility as a scalable model for integration into training frameworks addressing both intrapersonal-interpersonal and structural dimensions of bias; (2) participant-reported changes in self-perceived skills, confidence, and ability to recognize and address implicit bias and cultural dilemmas at individual, interpersonal, and systemic levels; and (3) qualitative themes related to psychological safety, collective healing, community-building, and preparedness to navigate cultural dilemmas.

Methods

Acting Together Conceptualization Model

In this qualitative study with a mixed-methods design, Acting Together was organized around 4 learning objectives: (1) recognizing implicit bias and cultural dilemmas at individual, interpersonal, and systemic levels; (2) building communication and adaptability skills through improvisation and role-play; (3) fostering psychological safety and community among interprofessional trainees; and (4) equipping participants with actionable strategies through postskit discussion and mentorship. Three webinars were held: 2 in August and November 2023 and 1 in March 2024. Each consisted of facilitator-led improv exercises (eTable 1 in Supplement 1), interactive skit viewing and role-play depicting cultural dilemmas, and panel discussion with Asian American health care professionals (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Establishing psychological safety was a central design consideration (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1), with activities intentionally sequenced from low-stakes identity-sharing exercises to higher-stakes skit viewing and role-play (“Establishing Psychological Safety” in the eMethods in Supplement 1). The study was approved by the Yale University institutional review board. Informed consent for survey participation was obtained in the first question of the survey. For the qualitative focus groups, written consent to participate and record was obtained for initial participation and verbal consent to participate was requested at the beginning of each focus group. All results were reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) reporting guideline.24

Skit Development and Implementation

Research team members (including E.Y.L.), themselves Asian American health care professionals and trainees, contributed personal narratives of cultural dilemmas encountered in clinical settings. Through structured discussions, individual experiences were distilled into composite scenarios preserving emotional and structural authenticity while protecting anonymity, using a previously reported protocol (“Skit Development: Additional Details” in the eMethods in Supplement 1).25,26 Skit scenarios represented cultural dilemmas across individual (internalized stigma), interpersonal (microaggressions, model-minority stereotyping), and systemic (gaps in institutional support for culturally concordant care) levels (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Webinar 1 depicted microaggressions in clinical settings (“Model-Minority Bias in the Medical Profession” and “The Sign-out Conundrum”). Webinar 2 addressed stereotypic judgment by a supervisor (“The Quiet Word”). Webinar 3 explored challenges of caring for patients from one’s own cultural background (“Navigating Boundaries and Empathy With Patients From a Similar Culture”) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Each skit followed the Theatre of the Oppressed’s Forum Theatre structure18,20: a problematic scenario was performed by research team members, after which participants moved into breakout rooms for facilitated discussion using semistructured prompts; they then actively role-played alternative responses, functioning as Boal’s spect-actors to rehearse strategies for navigating cultural dilemmas (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).

Data Collection

Participants were recruited nationally through institutional listservs, social media, and word of mouth (“Recruitment” in the eMethods in Supplement 1). Race and ethnicity were self-reported using US Census–based categories (“Demographic Data Collection” in the eMethods in Supplement 1); race categories were African American or Black, Asian American (included Asian, South Asian, and Pacific Islander), biracial, and White, and ethnicity categories were Hispanic or Latinx and non-Hispanic or non-Latinx. Prewebinar and postwebinar surveys on Qualtrics assessed impact using items adapted from the Implicit Bias Attitude Scale,27 Medical Improv Curriculum Survey,28 Acceptability of Intervention Measure, Intervention Appropriateness Measure, and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).29,30 Attention-check questions confirmed thoughtful responses.31,32 Survey responses were matched using participant identifiers; unpaired, incomplete, and duplicate responses and those with a failed attention check were removed. For repeat attendees, only the first set of responses was retained to avoid nonindependence (“Survey Matching and Exclusions” in the eMethods in Supplement 1). Participants received $20 for pre-post survey completion and $30 for completing a full focus group.

Statistical Analysis

Postwebinar acceptability and feasibility were assessed via 9 items on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” and 6 = “strongly agree”)29; negatively worded items were reverse-coded. The proportion of participants endorsing agreement is represented by a diverging stacked bar chart, and summary statistics, including means with SDs and medians with IQRs, are reported as the primary descriptive measure, consistent with recommended scale use.30 Internal consistency (Cronbach α),33 convergent validity (Pearson and Spearman correlations),30 and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) tests against a neutral reference distribution were computed as supplementary analyses (“Acceptability and Feasibility Analysis” in the eMethods in Supplement 1). Program impact was assessed by 7 pre-post items on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” and 5 = “strongly agree”).28 Pre-post changes were tested with paired t tests and described by percentage change, mean within-person change, and effect sizes calculated using Cohen dz34,35 (“Impact Analysis” in the eMethods in Supplement 1). Implicit bias attitudes were assessed using 23 adapted Implicit Bias Attitude Scale items27 on a 6-point scale (1 = “strongly disagree” and 6 = “strongly agree”), with pre-post changes assessed by paired t tests (“Implicit Bias Analysis” in the eMethods in Supplement 1). Analyses were conducted using R, version 2003.09.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Two-sided P < .05 was considered significant.

Ten focus groups (2 to 5 participants and 2 cofacilitators per group) were conducted via a videoconferencing platform. Cofacilitators (E.Y.L., J.A.X., J.J.H., T.B., and P.R.A.) were trainee members of the study team, self-identifying as Asian American, who received structured training in semistructured facilitation.36 Semistructured, open-ended questions focused on eliciting webinar experience, efficacy, personal takeaways, and emotional responses (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).37,38 To reach data sufficiency, participants were encouraged to answer questions until there were no additional responses before moving to the next question.39 Qualitative analysis was conducted manually using spreadsheets (Microsoft Excel, version 2024 [Microsoft Corporation]) and annotations through 3 phases: initial thematic coding by paired researchers (J.A.X., E.M., S.J.H., C.T.Y., J.J.H., M.B.Z., T.B., and P.R.A.), secondary cross-webinar synthesis by a separate pair, and narrative summary (“Qualitative Analysis: Additional Details” in the eMethods in Supplement 1).40,41,42

Results

Demographics

Of 112 national participants, 67 completed paired pre-post surveys (59.8% response rate) (“Survey Matching and Exclusions” in the eMethods in Supplement 1), with 41 (61.2%) participating in the first webinar, 13 (19.4%) in webinar 2, and 13 (19.4%) in webinar 3. A total of 38 (56.7%) participated in focus groups (17 of the participants in webinar 1 [41.5%], 11 [84.6%] of those in webinar 2, and 10 [76.9%] of those in webinar 3). Participants were predominantly aged 25 to 30 years (35 [52.2%]), followed by 18 to less than 25 years (20 [29.9%]) and older than 30 years (12 [17.9%]). The sample included 41 participants who self-identified as female (61.2%), 22 as male (32.8%), and 4 as nonbinary or gender-fluid (6.0%). The study sample had a wide geographic reach (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1) and was racially diverse: 22 participants (32.8%) were African American or Black, 34 (50.7%) were Asian American, 3 (4.5%) were biracial, and 8 (11.9%) were White; 8 (11.9%) identified as Hispanic or Latinx and 59 (88.1%) as non-Hispanic or non-Latinx (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1).43 Participants represented diverse training disciplines spanning the full professional hierarchy: medical students (34 [50.7%]), undergraduates (8 [11.9%]), nursing students (7 [10.4%]), non–health care graduate students (5 [7.5%]), social work students (5 [7.5%]), residents and fellows (4 [6.0%]), practicing clinicians (2 [3.0%]), and public health students (1 [1.5%]).

Acceptability and Feasibility

Participants reported high acceptability and feasibility across all 9 items (Figure 1). Agreement ranged from 88.1% to 94.0% for positively worded items: 63 participants (94.0%) agreed that Acting Together fit into their existing clinical work, 62 (92.5%) that it was adaptable to their training program, 62 (92.5%) that they would recommend it to colleagues, and 59 (88.1%) that they would rejoin. Disagreement with negatively worded items ranged from 68.7% to 77.6%: 52 (77.6%) disagreed that the program was inflexible, 51 (76.1%) disagreed that it had too many components, 49 (73.1%) disagreed that it caused too much extra work, and 46 (68.7%) disagreed that it was too long (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Subscales demonstrated good-to-excellent internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.87 and 0.90).30,33 The mean (SD) composite score on the 6-point Likert scale was 4.49 (0.79) points and the median score was 4.56 points (IQR, 3.89-5.00 points), with no respondent below the scale midpoint, indicating favorable ratings. K-S tests confirmed significant departure from the neutral baseline across all items (Cohen dz, 0.22-0.58). Regarding implicit bias competency, 48 participants (71.6%) endorsed at least slight agreement on the 6-point scale that the program improved their ability to recognize bias (mean [SD] score, 3.85 [0.80] points; median, 4 points [IQR, 3-4 points]; K-S test P < .001; Cohen dz, 0.49), though only 13 (19.4%) endorsed at the “agree” or “strongly agree” level, consistent with appropriately cautious self-assessment following a brief intervention (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). Per-respondent subscale mean scores for acceptability and feasibility were moderately positively correlated (Pearson r, 0.42 [95% CI, 0.20-0.60]; P < .001; Spearman ρ, 0.51; P < .001), supporting acceptability and feasibility as related but conceptually distinct dimensions of program evaluation (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Program Impact

Baseline (prewebinar) mean (SD) scores ranged from 3.46 (0.88) points (wellness) to 3.88 (0.64) points (engagement) on the 5-point Likert scale (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). The engagement item showed the highest baseline score and narrowest distribution, suggesting a ceiling effect; it had the smallest improvement (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.21 [0.75] points; Cohen dz = 0.28; percentage change, 5.4%) and the highest proportion of participants with unchanged scores (36 [53.7%]). Domains with lower baselines and wider distributions showed the largest gains. Percentage change in scores from prewebinar to postwebinar ranged from 5.4% to 13.8%. Effect sizes were in the small-to-moderate range (Cohen dz = 0.28-0.45).

Paired analysis revealed significant positive changes in all 7 measures (Figure 2). Measures aligned with learning objectives 1 and 2 (recognizing bias and building skills) showed improvements in adaptability (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.30 [0.98] points; Cohen dz = 0.30; percentage change, 8.4%; P = .02; 25 [37.3%] had improved scores), communication (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.46 [1.03] points; Cohen dz = 0.45; percentage change, 13.2%; P < .001; 31 [46.3%] had improved scores), and confidence (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.40 [1.09] points; Cohen dz = 0.37; percentage change, 11.2%; P = .004; 32 [47.8%] had improved scores). Measures aligned with learning objective 3 (psychological safety, community) showed improvements in connectedness (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.34 [0.95] points; Cohen dz = 0.36; percentage change, 9.3%; P = .004; 31 [46.3%] had improved scores) and wellness (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.48 [1.08] points; Cohen dz = 0.45; percentage change, 13.8%; P < .001; 35 [52.2%] had improved scores). Measures aligned with learning objective 4 (actionable strategies) showed improvements in engagement (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.21 [0.75] points; Cohen dz = 0.28; percentage change, 5.4%; P = .03; 21 [31.3%] had improved scores) and career proactiveness (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.39 [1.06] points; Cohen dz = 0.37; percentage change, 10.5%; P = .004; 27 [40.3%] had improved scores).

Implicit Bias

Webinar 3 focus group participants reported that skits introduced new perspectives on implicit biases, fostering self-reflection on biases about their own and other cultural groups. They valued learning techniques to address biases through discussion, role-play, and feedback (Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3). These findings align with quantitative results showing significant improvements in comfort discussing race (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.45 [1.08] points; Cohen dz = 0.42; P = .01; 27 participants [40.3%] had improved scores), desire to reduce bias in clinical decisions (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.34 [1.24] points; Cohen dz = 0.28; P = .03; 22 [32.8%] had improved scores), awareness of minority assumptions (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.30 [1.09] points; Cohen dz = 0.27; P = .03; 24 [35.8%] had improved scores), support for bias training in clinical care (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.30 [1.11] points; Cohen dz = 0.27; P = .03; 21 [31.3%] had improved scores), and recognizing that implicit bias learning supports communication skills (mean [SD] within-person change, 0.21 [0.84] points; Cohen dz = 0.25; P = .047; 18 [26.9%] had improved scores) (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). Significant positive change emerged in some but not all implicit bias measures, suggesting the intervention’s potential to raise awareness while highlighting the need for more in-depth exposure to achieve lasting changes across all domains.

Qualitative Focus Groups

Participants had positive experiences with Acting Together, finding the theater format more effective, engaging, and relatable than conventional didactic methods (Table 1 and eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Participants indicated that improv exercises fostered connection, the skits were realistic and evocative, and postskit discussion and role play exercises helped create a safe environment and community that bridged training levels and professions, along with building participant confidence and skills to better deal with similar scenarios in clinical practice in the future (Table 2 and eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Participants also provided constructive suggestions, including improved time management, clearer connections between improv and skits, more time for postskit discussion, and graded skit difficulty, which were iteratively incorporated into later webinars.

At a deeper level, participants discussed the personal growth and therapeutic benefit that transpired from the webinars (Table 3). Some participants described structural silencing around racial experiences in health care and the importance of Acting Together in introducing new perspectives on implicit biases by fostering self-reflection and consideration of others. One noted that accumulated fatigue from racism had created norms in which difficult experiences were no longer discussed (Table 3, quote 6). Participants reported that Acting Together promoted collective healing from this pattern by creating space for naming of experiences (Table 3, quote 5), recognizing cultural dilemmas as systemic rather than isolated (Table 3, quote 6), engaging with questions of power and hierarchy, and reflecting on intragroup and interpersonal dynamics. One described how developing a deeper understanding of the power of storytelling, empathy, and collective action through acting can raise awareness, challenge stereotypes, and amplify marginalized voices (Table 3, quote 7). Others noted they had previously rarely examined how biases about their own cultural group affected their clinical behavior (Table 3, quote 11) and appreciated how important the discussions were for addressing model-minority and perpetual-foreigner stereotypes (Table 3, quote 12).

Discussion

Though most existing bias interventions use didactic approaches that may not fully engage participants or foster community,2,44,45 interactive theater has been implemented in other contexts, including Asian American parent-adolescent communication,36,46 conversations with unvaccinated patients,47 and discussing racism with Black youth clients.48 All reported positive preliminary results but called for larger studies.

Acting Together is an innovative healing education program using improvisational theater to address cultural dilemmas at individual, interpersonal, and structural levels faced by Asian American health care professionals. The history of structural racism against Asian American individuals has rendered these experiences largely invisible within medical education and equity discourse.2,4 Acting Together responds by creating a psychologically safe, culturally specific space for interactively naming, processing, and collectively healing from these harms.

Participants reported that the online format was highly adaptable with minimal additional resources. Perceived feasibility did not change significantly across webinars despite different content, suggesting reliability. Participants spanned professional hierarchy and racial and ethnic backgrounds, supporting the generalizability and acceptability of the Acting Together model.

Given that participants were disproportionately from racial and ethnic minority backgrounds relative to the general US population,43 their participation may have been motivated by interest in finding a community of solidarity and allyship and creating a sense of collective healing and belonging in the health care community. The theater and improvisation model maintained a distinction from everyday clinical experiences, which allowed participants to freely explore responses, make mistakes, and improve on action-taking ability in response to conflicts, biases, and microaggressions, consistent with previous literature on improvisational theater in health care education.49,50

The quantitative instruments captured consistent positive change in individual-level skills (learning objectives 1 and 2), with the largest effects in wellness and communication (dz = 0.45). Community-level outcomes (learning objective 3) were partially reflected quantitatively but more fully captured qualitatively, including collective solidarity, shared recognition, and belonging. Learning objective 4 was most clearly reflected in qualitative themes of preparedness, new behaviors, and empowerment. The structural and systemic dimensions of cultural dilemmas emerged as prominent qualitative themes, underscoring the value of mixed-methods design.

The program operated simultaneously at intrapersonal, interpersonal, and structural levels. Participant responses indicated that the theater model improved skills and confidence while creating peer networks that reduced isolation. At the structural level, participants identified the program as a counterpoint to institutional silence around racial experiences, a silence maintained by the absence of sanctioned spaces for processing racial trauma within medical training. The skits evoked strong emotions because they named experiences participants recognized as systemic, connecting individual incidents to broader patterns of power, hierarchy, and institutional norms (Table 3). Community-building emerged as a central theme, underscoring participatory education’s value in promoting psychological safety and collective resilience.44,51 These themes are consistent with radical healing11 and liberation psychology,12 which identify collectivism, critical consciousness, and cultural authenticity as anchors for healing from racial trauma. These findings suggest community and allyship should be core outcomes in future educational interventions supporting underrepresented groups.52,53

The conceptual model evolved over the project. Survey instruments were selected at the outset for communication, implementation, and bias awareness; as the model expanded to incorporate structural dimensions, collective healing, and community-building, qualitative focus groups proved essential for capturing these dimensions. Future studies should more comprehensively assess structural awareness and community-level outcomes quantitatively. Participants described collective naming as therapeutic, and the program fostered critical consciousness alongside individual skills that align with radical healing frameworks11 and liberation psychology.12 However, as Vela et al10 have shown, intrapersonal and interpersonal interventions alone are insufficient without structural change. Acting Together operates primarily at the individual and interpersonal levels, but the qualitative data suggest it may bridge to structural work, as participants reported increased confidence to advocate, greater comfort discussing race, and stronger collective purpose. Future iterations could more explicitly incorporate structural analysis and bystander intervention training.

Limitations

This study has limitations. We acknowledge that voluntarily recruited participants may exhibit a baseline positive tendency due to selection bias or social desirability, contributing to ceiling effects. The study assessed impact after a single webinar and did not capture cumulative effects of repeated exposure. An important consideration for future implementation is how Acting Together translates to implementation in required curricular settings, which would likely necessitate additional attention to facilitator training, psychological safety protocols, and scaffolding due to less familiarity and comfort discussing topics of race, bias, and cultural identity. The current sample did not permit subgroup analyses comparing Asian American and non–Asian American participants, though participants spanned various racial backgrounds (about 51% were Asian American and about 49% were non–Asian American), training levels (from undergraduates to practicing clinicians), or multiple disciplines. Future, larger studies should examine demographic differences in outcomes, test adaptations for required curricular context, and assess beyond item-level analysis of implemented measures with subscale-level analysis.9,51

Conclusions

To our knowledge, Acting Together is the first-reported culturally responsive healing program targeting unique challenges of Asian American health care professionals and trainees. The findings of this pilot qualitative study suggest that Acting Together promotes psychological safety and equips participants with practical skills and confidence to navigate implicit bias and cultural dilemmas. Thus, interactive theater-based models may hold promise as a scalable educational approach for supporting interprofessional racial and ethnic minority trainees in collective healing from internalized structural, racial traumas.
