CT-derived Epicardial Adipose Tissue Density and Its Association With Systemic Inflammation and Functional Impairment in Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction: A Prospective Cross-sectional Study From Oman
Authors: Arif Albulushi, Ahmed Tawfek, Ahmed Basuoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) has become the dominant heart failure phenotype in many contemporary populations and remains a major cause of symptoms, exercise intolerance, hospitalization, and impaired quality of life [1,2]. Unlike heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, HFpEF is not a single disease entity but a heterogeneous syndrome shaped by multiple interacting mechanisms, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, renal dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, systemic inflammation, and altered ventricular–vascular coupling [3,4]. This heterogeneity has increased interest in phenotype-led evaluation, particularly in populations in which patients present at a relatively younger age and with a high cardiometabolic burden.

Among the extracardiac contributors to HFpEF, visceral adiposity has attracted increasing attention [5,6]. Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), in particular, is now recognized as more than a passive fat depot. Because it lies in direct contact with the myocardium and coronary vasculature and shares the same microcirculation, EAT is positioned to exert local paracrine, vasocrine, and mechanical effects on cardiac structure and function [7–9]. Experimental and translational work suggests that adverse EAT remodeling may contribute to myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, lipotoxicity, microvascular dysfunction, and pericardial restraint, all of which are relevant to the pathophysiology of HFpEF [10–12].

Invasive and imaging-based studies have linked epicardial fat to higher filling pressures, reduced exercise capacity, and worse hemodynamic reserve in HFpEF [13–16]. More recently, multi-omic and phenotyping studies have supported an association between EAT, proteomic inflammatory signaling, coronary microvascular dysfunction, impaired quality of life, and adverse clinical profile in HFpEF [16,17]. These observations are biologically plausible, particularly in obesity-related HFpEF, where adiposity and inflammation appear to amplify the syndrome beyond simple body mass index alone [4,18].

Most prior imaging studies, however, have focused on EAT quantity, typically expressed as thickness or volume. Although these metrics are useful, they may not fully capture adipose “quality.” CT-derived adipose attenuation or density has therefore emerged as a potential non-invasive marker of adipose tissue composition and inflammatory remodeling [19–21]. In cardiovascular fat depots, higher attenuation (that is, less negative HU values) has been associated with inflammatory activity and adverse vascular phenotype [19,20]. In EAT specifically, attenuation has been related to subclinical atherosclerosis, vascular dysfunction, and adverse cardiovascular risk [21,22]. Even so, the interpretation of EAT density remains nuanced, because attenuation is influenced not only by tissue biology but also by adipose volume, scanner settings, contrast characteristics, and partial-volume effects [20,23].

This distinction is particularly important in HFpEF. If EAT density is to be interpreted meaningfully, it should be examined alongside established measures of HF severity rather than in isolation. In addition, structured HFpEF diagnosis in research settings increasingly relies on contemporary frameworks such as the HFA-PEFF and H2FPEF approaches, which integrate symptoms, natriuretic peptides, echocardiographic findings, and comorbidity profile [24,25]. Despite this, relatively few studies have examined CT-derived EAT density in a prospectively characterized HFpEF cohort while linking it to inflammatory biomarkers, functional capacity, health status, and echocardiographic markers of diastolic burden within the same clinical framework.

Functional status is a clinically important anchor in HFpEF. The 6-min walk test (6MWT) and the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) are widely used, validated measures that capture exercise tolerance, symptoms, and health status in heart failure populations, including HFpEF [26–28]. In parallel, inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α provide a circulating readout of the inflammatory burden often seen in cardiometabolic HFpEF [29,30]. Linking CT-derived EAT characteristics to these functional and inflammatory measures may help clarify whether EAT density reflects a clinically recognizable phenotype rather than an isolated imaging observation.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to evaluate the association of CT-derived EAT density with systemic inflammatory biomarkers, functional capacity, health status, and echocardiographic indices of HFpEF severity in a prospective cross-sectional cohort from Oman. We hypothesized that higher EAT density would be associated with greater inflammatory burden and worse patient-centered functional status, while recognizing that any observed relationship would need to be interpreted in the context of overall HF severity, adiposity, and the known technical limitations of attenuation-based imaging metrics.

Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This was a prospective cross-sectional study conducted at the National Heart Center, The Royal Hospital, Muscat, Oman, between January and July 2024. Consecutive adults referred for evaluation of suspected or established HFpEF were screened for eligibility.

HFpEF was defined using contemporary structured criteria: symptoms and/or signs of heart failure, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥50%, and objective evidence of elevated filling pressures or structural/functional abnormalities consistent with diastolic dysfunction [1,2,25]. Where sufficient data were available, case classification was crosschecked against the principles of the HFA-PEFF and H2FPEF diagnostic frameworks [24,25].

Patients were eligible if they were aged ≥18 years and had clinically stable HFpEF. We excluded patients with LVEF <50%, significant primary valvular disease, hypertrophic or infiltrative cardiomyopathy, constrictive pericarditis, recent acute coronary syndrome or coronary revascularization within 3 months, active infection or systemic inflammatory disease, inability to complete functional testing, or inadequate CT image quality for EAT analysis.

Atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease were not excluded a priori, as both are common in real-world HFpEF. These variables were recorded as part of baseline phenotyping, and renal function was incorporated into adjusted analyses.

2.2. Clinical, laboratory, and functional assessment

Baseline assessment included demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, comorbidities, medication profile, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. Dyslipidemia was defined by prior diagnosis, lipid-lowering therapy, or both. Medication data included statins, beta-blockers, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, loop diuretics, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists where applicable.

Blood samples were obtained on the same study day as imaging and functional testing. NT-proBNP, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were analyzed in the institutional core laboratory using standardized commercial assays. Serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate were recorded to assess renal function. Because NT-proBNP and inflammatory markers showed right-skewed distributions, log transformation was used where appropriate in regression analyses.

Functional capacity was assessed using the 6-min walk test (6MWT) according to American Thoracic Society recommendations [26]. Health status was assessed using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), and the overall summary score was used for analysis [27,28].

2.3. Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed within 7 days of CT using a standardized protocol. Studies were analyzed by experienced echocardiographers according to current guideline recommendations [25,29]. Variables collected for this study included LVEF, left ventricular mass index, left atrial volume index (LAVI), mitral inflow velocities, tissue Doppler annular velocities, average E/e′, and tricuspid regurgitation severity where available. Diastolic dysfunction was graded using an integrated guideline-based approach [25].

2.4. Cardiac CT and epicardial adipose tissue analysis

All participants underwent non-contrast ECG-gated cardiac CT using a standardized institutional protocol. Images were reconstructed at 0.625-mm slice thickness to allow dedicated post-processing of adipose tissue around the heart. Acquisition parameters were kept as consistent as possible across the study period.

Epicardial adipose tissue was defined as adipose tissue located within the visceral pericardium and was segmented using an attenuation threshold of −190 to −30 Hounsfield units (HU) [20,23,30]. Segmentation extended from the level of the pulmonary artery bifurcation to the left ventricular apex. Post-processing was performed using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2) with a semi-automated workflow and manual correction of the pericardial contours when needed.

Mean EAT density was calculated as the average attenuation of all voxels within the segmented epicardial adipose tissue compartment. Total EAT volume and indexed EAT volume were also derived for exploratory analyses.

Image analysis was performed by two cardiologists experienced in cardiac CT, one with Level III cardiac CT training and more than 5 years of cardiac CT interpretation experience, and the other with Level II cardiac CT training. Both readers were blinded to biomarker values, echocardiographic findings, 6MWT results, and KCCQ scores at the time of CT analysis.

2.5. Reproducibility

To assess reproducibility, 20 randomly selected CT studies were re-analyzed independently by the second reader for interobserver agreement and remeasured by the primary reader after a 4-week interval for intraobserver agreement. Reproducibility was summarized using intraclass correlation coefficients with 95% confidence intervals [31].

2.6. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate, and categorical variables as number (percentage). Baseline characteristics were summarized for the overall cohort and, where relevant, stratified by exploratory EAT density groups.

The primary analyses treated EAT density as a continuous variable. Correlations between EAT density and inflammatory, echocardiographic, and functional variables were assessed using Pearson or Spearman methods as appropriate. Exploratory categorical analyses were also performed using an EAT density threshold of −90 HU. Because no universally accepted clinical cut-off exists, this threshold was treated as exploratory and interpreted cautiously.

Multivariable linear regression models were constructed to examine associations between EAT density and selected outcomes after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates, including age, sex, body mass index, NT-proBNP, renal function, E/e′, and selected medication variables. Where applicable, EAT volume was considered in exploratory models to assess whether density-related associations persisted beyond adipose burden alone. Multicollinearity among inflammatory variables was assessed before model construction. Given the modest sample size, all adjusted analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Medication variables considered in exploratory adjusted models included statin use, SGLT2 inhibitor use, and other cardiometabolic therapies considered biologically relevant to adipose remodeling.

2.7. Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of The Royal Hospital, Ministry of Health, Oman (MoH/CSR/24/31344). All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

3.1. Study population

Between January and July 2024, 120 consecutive patients with suspected or established HFpEF were screened. Ten were excluded because of incomplete study assessments or inadequate CT image quality, leaving 110 patients for the final analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline clinical, biomarker, echocardiographic, and functional characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

The cohort had a mean age of 65 ± 10 years, and 53% were women. Cardiometabolic comorbidity was common, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity-related risk burden (Table 1). Mean body mass index was 28.4 ± 4.5 kg/m2, and mean EAT density for the overall cohort was −85 ± 10 HU (95% CI, −86.9 to −83.1). NT-proBNP was measured in all participants and is reported in Table 1 together with echocardiographic indices of HFpEF severity, including LVEF, LAVI, E/e′, and diastolic dysfunction grade.

3.2. EAT density and inflammatory profile

In continuous analyses, higher EAT density (that is, less negative HU values) was associated with a less favorable inflammatory profile. EAT density correlated positively with fasting glucose, triglycerides, and inflammatory biomarkers, with the strongest correlations observed for hs-CRP (r = 0.50, p < 0.001), IL-6 (r = 0.48, p < 0.001), and TNF-α (r = 0.46, p < 0.001) (Table 2). These relationships are illustrated in Fig. 2 using scatterplots with individual data points.

Because inflammatory biomarkers may be interrelated, multicollinearity was assessed before model construction. In the original multivariable model, hs-CRP (β = 0.30, p < 0.001) and IL-6 (β = 0.25, p < 0.001) remained associated with EAT density, whereas BMI was not (Table 2). These findings suggest that higher EAT density may track with inflammatory burden beyond anthropometric adiposity alone, although the interpretation remains cautious given the cross-sectional design and limited adjustment in the original model.

3.3. EAT density, functional capacity, and health status

Higher EAT density was also associated with worse patient-centered functional measures. In exploratory categorical analyses using a threshold of −90 HU, patients with higher EAT density demonstrated lower 6MWT distance (320 ± 75 vs 400 ± 70 m; p < 0.001) and lower KCCQ overall summary score (60 ± 12 vs 75 ± 10; p < 0.001) than those with lower-density EAT (Table 3). Because no universally accepted clinical threshold for EAT density exists, this dichotomous analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and supportive rather than definitive.

3.4. Echocardiographic phenotype and diastolic dysfunction

Patients with higher EAT density also showed a less favorable echocardiographic profile, including higher filling pressure indices and greater diastolic dysfunction burden. 3.5. Reproducibility of EAT measurements

Reproducibility for CT-derived EAT measurements was excellent. Interobserver and intraobserver agreement for mean EAT density were 0.93 and 0.96, respectively.

Taken together, the results show that higher CT-derived EAT density was associated with greater systemic inflammatory burden and a less favorable functional and echocardiographic profile in this HFpEF cohort. However, because the strongest clinical contrasts were observed in exploratory threshold-based analyses and the adjusted models should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, these findings are best viewed as associative rather than definitive evidence of independent biomarker performance.

Discussion

In this prospective cross-sectional HFpEF cohort, higher CT-derived EAT density was associated with a less favorable inflammatory, functional, and echocardiographic profile. Patients with higher EAT density had higher circulating inflammatory markers, shorter 6-min walk distance, lower KCCQ scores, and a more adverse diastolic phenotype. These findings are biologically plausible and broadly consistent with the growing view that adverse epicardial fat remodeling may contribute to the cardiometabolic-inflammatory HFpEF phenotype [29–31]. At the same time, our results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The study was associative by design, and EAT density should not be viewed here as a validated stand-alone biomarker, but rather as a potentially informative imaging correlate within a broader HFpEF phenotype framework.

This distinction is important. Epicardial adipose tissue is not a uniform biological compartment, and its CT appearance likely reflects several interacting properties, including adipocyte composition, local inflammatory activity, fibrosis, vascularity, and technical imaging factors [17,29]. A less negative, higher-density EAT signal may suggest a relatively more inflamed or remodeled adipose environment, but attenuation can also be influenced by acquisition settings, segmentation strategy, and partial-volume effects [15,16,20,23]. For that reason, EAT density is best interpreted in context rather than in isolation.

Our findings support that more balanced view. Higher EAT density tracked with worse inflammatory burden and poorer functional status on unadjusted analysis, but this does not by itself establish that density provides independent clinical information beyond other markers of HF severity. The reviewers were right to raise this point. In HFpEF, the clinical phenotype is already shaped by multiple overlapping drivers, including obesity, diastolic burden, natriuretic peptide activation, renal dysfunction, and treatment exposure [3,4,30,32]. EAT density may therefore reflect one part of this broader adverse terrain rather than act as a uniquely dominant signal.

The relationship between EAT and HFpEF has become increasingly compelling over the past few years. Mechanistic work has linked epicardial fat to higher filling pressures, impaired exercise reserve, and more unfavorable hemodynamics in HFpEF [13–16]. More recent phenotyping studies have also associated EAT with proteomic inflammatory signatures, coronary microvascular dysfunction, and reduced quality of life [16,31]. Our study adds to this literature by focusing specifically on CT-derived EAT density, which may capture adipose “quality” rather than adipose quantity alone. That is a clinically relevant distinction, because most prior studies have concentrated on EAT volume or thickness.

At the same time, our data also argue against oversimplification. A denser EAT depot should not automatically be interpreted as equivalent to a larger EAT depot, and vice versa. The biological meaning of higher attenuation remains incompletely defined, and imaging-pathology correlation is still limited [17,29]. This is why EAT density should not yet be promoted as an actionable marker in routine HFpEF care. A more defensible interpretation is that it may help identify a subgroup of patients with a more adverse cardiometabolic-inflammatory profile, particularly when considered alongside conventional clinical and echocardiographic variables.

The functional associations observed in this study are worth highlighting, but again with restraint. Both the 6MWT and KCCQ are clinically meaningful patient-centered measures in HFpEF [26–28]. The observation that patients with higher EAT density had shorter walk distance and worse health-status scores is intuitive and may reflect a phenotype with greater congestion, worse exercise reserve, or a more pronounced inflammatory-metabolic burden. However, these findings should not be taken to mean that EAT density independently determines functional impairment. It is equally plausible that EAT density is acting as an imaging correlate of the same adverse HFpEF milieu already captured by natriuretic peptides, echocardiographic markers, obesity, and comorbidity burden.

The therapeutic implications should therefore be framed carefully. Modern HFpEF care is increasingly phenotype-driven, with greater focus on obesity, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction as modifiable treatment targets [6,22]. SGLT2 inhibitors have become foundational across the HFpEF spectrum, and GLP-1-based therapies have shown important benefits in obesity-related HFpEF, including improvements in symptoms, physical limitations, and inflammatory profile [33,34]. In that setting, EAT-related imaging markers may eventually prove useful as mechanistic or phenotyping signals. Our own prior work has also examined the intersection of HFpEF, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibition in related cardiometabolic settings [35]. Still, the present data do not justify using EAT density to guide therapy selection, and we believe it is more appropriate at this stage to view it as a promising phenotyping signal rather than a treatment-directing biomarker.

One practical strength of this study is the use of a clinically accessible CT-derived metric together with inflammatory, echocardiographic, and patient-centered functional measures within the same cohort. Another is the inclusion of reproducibility assessment, which supports the technical feasibility of EAT density analysis in a structured imaging setting [31]. This matters because quantitative imaging metrics are only useful if they are reasonably reproducible across readers and over time. Even so, reproducibility does not solve the central biological question of what EAT density truly represents at the tissue level. Further work is still needed to link CT attenuation more directly with histopathology, molecular signatures, and longitudinal outcomes.

Taken together, our findings support a middle-ground conclusion. EAT density appears to carry clinically relevant information, but not in a way that justifies overstatement. It seems to reflect an adverse inflammatory and functional phenotype in HFpEF, yet much of its apparent significance likely overlaps with established markers of disease severity and cardiometabolic burden. Before EAT density can be considered for broader clinical use, larger and more comprehensively phenotyped studies will need to determine whether it adds incremental value beyond conventional echocardiography, natriuretic peptides, renal function, body composition, and EAT volume itself [31,32,36–40].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center cross-sectional study; causality cannot be inferred and residual confounding is possible. Second, we focused on inflammation and patient-centered functional status rather than longitudinal outcomes; prospective follow-up is needed to evaluate whether EAT density adds incremental prognostic value for HF hospitalization or mortality. Third, EAT density was quantified as mean attenuation; we did not perform regional mapping, texture analysis, or more advanced tissue characterization approaches, which may provide complementary information. Finally, CT-based adipose metrics are influenced by acquisition parameters and segmentation thresholds. We used a standardized protocol, but external reproducibility across scanners and populations requires further validation. In addition, the categorical analysis based on the −90 HU threshold was exploratory, as no universally accepted clinical cut-off for EAT density currently exists.

Conclusions

In this HFpEF cohort, higher CT-derived EAT density was associated with greater inflammatory burden and worse functional status. These findings suggest that EAT density may serve as a complementary imaging correlate of the cardiometabolic-inflammatory HFpEF phenotype, but larger and longitudinal studies are needed before broader clinical application can be recommended.
