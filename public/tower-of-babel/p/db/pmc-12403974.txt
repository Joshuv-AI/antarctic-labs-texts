Carotid plaque characteristics in the CREST-2 trial
Authors: Brajesh K. Lal, Georges Jreij, Matthew Chrencik, Wayne M. Clarke, Ángel Chamorro, D. Christopher Metzger, Tara McDonald, John Yokemick, George Howard, Lloyd Edwards, Thomas G. Brott, James F. Meschia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The modest stroke prevention from surgery for asymptomatic carotid disease has prompted a search for predictors that may improve risk stratification beyond luminal stenosis. Plaque disruption and atheroembolization are associated with unique anatomical and histological changes. The Carotid Revascularization and Medical Management for Asymptomatic Carotid Stenosis Trial (CREST-2) collects information on duplex ultrasound (DUS) plaque biomarkers with the goal of evaluating their relationship to the periprocedural and long-term risks of stroke. In this study, we examine the reliability with which carotid plaque features can be measured from DUS images, and report baseline carotid DUS-derived stenosis and plaque features using semiautomated digital image analysis of patients enrolled in CREST-2.Methods:We studied the first 503 patients in CREST-2. Patients underwent standardized carotid DUS evaluation pre-enrollment. B-Mode images were scaled linearly to normalize brightness. Plaques were outlined manually. Dedicated software automatically measured the longitudinal sectional area (mm2), grayscale median (GSM), Gray-Weale classification, and tissue composition (mm2) of intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH), lipid, fibrous tissue, muscle, and calcium. We present the mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, minimum and maximum range, and proportions of carotid peak systolic velocities (PSVs) and plaque morphological features. We tested for autocorrelation among plaque features and computed the proportion of potentially unstable plaques in the cohort. Reliability of the image analysis techniques was tested in 100 patients using Bland-Altman plots and intraclass and interclass correlation coefficients.Results:Most patients were male (58.4%), older (mean age of 69.3 years), White (87.5%), and had a PSV of ≥230 cm/s (98.6%); the 1.4% with a PSV of <230 cm/s were enrolled based on catheter angiography. Plaques in this study were large; the mean longitudinal sectional area was 62 ± 37 mm2 (range, 6.2–256.5 mm2). The mean GSM was 58 ± 30 (unitless) (range, 0–168) and Gray-Weale classification was 3.5 ± 0.9 (range, 1–5). The mean areas of tissue types were IPH 5.3 ± 8.9 mm2, lipid 9.3 ± 8.6 mm2, fibrous tissue 10 ± 10 mm2, muscle 17 ± 12 mm2, and calcium 1.6 ± 4.1 mm2. The PSV of patients showed poor correlation with plaque features. The proportion of plaques with a GSM of ≤35 was 22.8%, IPH of ≥5 mm2 was 30.0%, and a lipid-rich necrotic core of ≥40% was 3.0% of the cohort. Plaque measurements could be performed with high reliability with good interobserver and intraobserver correlations.Conclusions:Site-generated, core laboratory-interpreted ultrasound examination provides a reliable way of characterizing carotid plaque morphological features across studies performed at many CREST-2 sites. The initial cohort of patients randomized in CREST-2 had heterogeneity of plaque features despite causing high-grade stenosis. Completion of the trial will provide an opportunity to assess whether plaque heterogeneity interacts with response to revascularization and medical management. (JVS-Vascular Insights 2024;2:100134.)

Previous randomized trials have reported a risk reduction in stroke and death from revascularization in patients with high-grade asymptomatic carotid stenosis.1,2 Although surgery offered a relative reduction in stroke of approximately 50% over 5 years, this translated to a modest absolute risk reduction of 0.5 to 1.0% per year when compared with best medical therapy. Subsequent advances have improved outcomes in patients treated with either medical therapy alone or in combination with revascularization.3 CREST-2 (Carotid Revascularization and Medical Management for Asymptomatic Carotid Stenosis Study) is designed to address this new state of equipoise and is nearing completion of enrollment.4

The selection of patients in CREST-2 is complicated by findings from secondary analyses of data from randomized trials that did not identify a relationship between stenosis severity and outcomes. The findings contradicted prior observational study data suggesting an increasing risk of stroke with increasing stenosis in asymptomatic carotid disease.5,6 More recently, a population-based study has reported a linear relationship between stroke risk and degree of stenosis.7 Unlike asymptomatic patients, secondary analyses of trials of patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis have confirmed a relationship between degree of stenosis and stroke risk.8 In the absence of a proven alternative, and to maintain comparability with prior published studies, CREST-2 has implemented standardized entry criteria to ensure enrollment of patients with ≥70% stenosis.

The modest stroke prevention from surgery seen in randomized trials has prompted a search for predictors that may improve risk stratification beyond measurement of luminal stenosis. Carotid plaque atheroembolization is associated with unique anatomical and histological changes.9 These plaque characteristics, when identified on noninvasive imaging, may predict future stroke better than the degree of stenosis.10,11 There is limited prospective information confirming the utility of plaque imaging biomarkers, and the Society for Vascular Surgery guideline does not recommend routinely using them for identifying high-risk patients.12 Conversely, the European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines suggest using plaque biomarkers to inform revascularization decisions, although as a Class IIb recommendation.13 Although imaging biomarkers are not incorporated into patient selection, CREST-2 is collecting information on duplex ultrasound (DUS) plaque biomarkers, with the goal of evaluating their relationship to periprocedural adverse events and long-term risk for stroke. We have developed, implemented, and histologically validated semiautomated image analysis techniques to measure plaque features from B-mode ultrasound images.14–16 This approach yields information on longitudinal-sectional plaque area, Gray-Weale classification,17 grey scale median (GSM),18 and intraplaque pixel distribution yielding tissue composition (intraplaque hemorrhage [IPH], lipid, fibrous tissue, muscle, and calcification).14–16

In this study, we hypothesized that a centralized analysis of archived digital DUS B-mode images generated across multiple clinical laboratories provides reliable estimates of carotid plaque morphological features and report these morphological features along with stenosis estimates at baseline for patients enrolled in CREST-2.

METHODS

Patients.

We studied the first 503 patients in CREST-2. The list of inclusion and exclusion criteria and definitions of cardiovascular risk factors for the trial are published elsewhere.4 Briefly, eligibility includes men and women ≥35 years old who have asymptomatic ≥70% stenosis involving the cervical carotid artery. Patients are considered asymptomatic in the absence of ipsilateral symptoms within 180 days before randomization with a modified Rankin score of 0 or 1. A stenosis of ≥70% is defined by (1) a peak systolic velocity (PSV) ≥230 cm/sec plus any one of the following: end-diastolic velocity of ≥100 cm/sec, internal carotid artery-to-common carotid artery (ICA:CCA) velocity ratio of 4, computed tomography angiography or magnetic resonance angiography showing ≥70% stenosis; or (2) by a catheter-based angiogram showing ≥70% stenosis. Patients with recurrent carotid lesions, dissection, or isolated CCA stenosis are ineligible. All patients undergo standardized carotid DUS evaluation before enrollment. Additional radiological imaging is performed in select patients to confirm the degree of stenosis. Deidentified images are collated and reviewed centrally at the VIC facility (University of Maryland). Reviewers are blinded to treatment assignment and clinical outcomes.

Protocol approvals, registrations, and participant consents.

The CREST-2 protocol is approved by a central Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Cincinnati and by each of the IRBs of participating institutions not governed by the central IRB. The trial is registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02089217). All participants provide written informed consent before randomization.

CREST-2 carotid ultrasound protocol.

Diagnostic vascular laboratories of CREST-2 centers follow a standardized imaging protocol defined by the vascular imaging core (VIC) (Fig 1, A and B).19 Vascular technologists submit sample DUS studies for review by the VIC before being certified to perform examinations for the trial. After certification, study images are submitted to the VIC, where they are reviewed for proper application of the protocol and reading of velocities.20 The imaging protocol was derived from recommendations of the Intersocietal Accreditation Commission and from experience with the previous CREST imaging protocol.21 Emphasis is placed on standardized examination angles22 with 12 velocity waveform samples taken from prespecified arterial locations (proximal, middle, and distal CCA; proximal, middle, and distal ICA). Correctly aligned Doppler angles of <60° are also acceptable, although the velocities are recalculated. The highest PSV and end-diastolic velocity in the ICA are verified and recorded. The highest PSV in the ICA and the CCA are used to compute the ICA/CCA ratio.

Carotid plaque analysis.

Integral to the CREST-2 DUS protocol is the collection of images of the carotid plaque in the longitudinal plane using a standardized approach. Digital recordings of B-mode grayscale and color flow images of the carotid artery bifurcation depicting the carotid plaque are saved in a DICOM format, deidentified, and uploaded to the VIC through a secure image transfer portal. Images are stored in a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant, firewall-protected server at the VIC.

The following image quality criteria are used before processing: the image set must include the entire plaque at the carotid bifurcation, including the proximal and distal edges, both far and near walls of the artery must be in the same frame, and a magnified grayscale and color flow image of the bifurcation must be included. We found that 96.4% of the studies fulfilled these criteria. The image processing approach for plaque analysis has been described elsewhere.14 The protocol has demonstrated correlation with histology.15 Reliability and repeatability of this analytical approach was published previously, showing high interclass and intraclass correlation, low interobserver and intraobserver variability, and high Dice similarity coefficients.14,15

Image analysis was performed in the VIC using MATLAB (MATLAB version R 2016b; MathWorks Inc, Natick, MA). The color flow image was used to guide manual outlining of the plaque in the grayscale image (Fig 2, A). Image brightness was scaled linearly to normalize data. Blood within the lumen was representative of a brightness of 0, and adventitia surrounding the artery was representative of a brightness of 190. The software then automatically computed the area and the brightness intensities of pixels within the outlined plaque. Grayscale images are stored electronically as a collection of individual pixels, each with a brightness ranging from 0 for black through 255 for white. After preprocessing, the following measurements were automatically obtained (Fig 2, B). (1) Longitudinal sectional area (mm2), defined as the area of visible plaque in the longitudinal sectional image of the carotid artery bifurcation. (2) Grayscale median (GSM, unitless), defined as the median pixel brightness of the entire plaque, providing an overall index of echogenicity of the plaque.10 (3) Gray-Weale classification,23 which was operationalized as follows: type 1, hypoechoic throughout, with an echogenic edge; type 2, predominantly hypoechoic with echogenic areas representing no more than 25% of the plaque area; type 3, predominantly hyperechoic with hypoechoic areas representing no more than 25% of the plaque area; type 4, homogenously hyperechoic throughout; and type 5, heavily calcified. (4) Tissue composition (mm2). Prior histological validation studies have confirmed that visually dark regions in a plaque B-mode image correlate with IPH and/or lipid, while visually bright regions correlate with fibromuscular tissue and/or calcification. Our histological validation studies have defined the pixel brightness ranges corresponding with individual tissue types.14,15 The area occupied by the following tissues within the outlined carotid plaque image was measured: IPH, lipid, fibrous tissue, muscle, and calcium.

Statistical analysis.

Categorical data on patient demographics, clinical features, and plaque features are summarized as frequency and percentages, and continuous data as means and standard deviations (SD). We present the PSV mean, SD, maximum, and minimum values across the cohort. We describe the mean, SD, median, interquartile range, and minimum and maximum values, of each of eight plaque features measured in the study (longitudinal sectional area [mm2], GSM, [unit-less], Gray-Weale classification [types 1–5]), and tissue composition (mm2) of IPH, lipid, fibrous tissue, muscle, and calcium. We present the relative prevalence of individual tissue types (IPH, lipid, fibrous tissue, muscle, calcium) within individual plaques as (area of tissuetotal plaque area)*100. We applied thresholds that may define a high-risk plaque (eg, GSM of ≤35, IPH of >mean of cohort, lipid >40%) on our cohort to compute the proportion of potentially unstable plaques among our patients.24 We tested for autocorrelation between different plaque features because the presence of one feature may be biologically related to another feature. To measure the relationship between degree of stenosis and plaque morphological features, we displayed scatterplots and computed Pearsons’ correlation coefficient for PSV vs each of the eight plaque features measured in the study.

We tested the reliability of our image analysis technique in 100 randomly selected patients. Two blinded readers at the VIC independently analyzed images from the same 100 patients. In addition, one reader analyzed the images from the same 100 patients twice in staggered fashion, ≥4 weeks apart to avoid recall bias. Bland-Altman plots were used to test intraobserver and interobserver repeatability of total plaque area measurements. Intraclass and interclass correlation coefficients were computed for total plaque area measurements, GSM, and each of the tissue measurements. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was conducted in SAS/STAT version 15.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics.

The 503 patients included in this analysis were enrolled from 106 CREST-2 clinical centers, all from the United States. Table I reports the demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients. Most patients were male (58.4%), older (mean age 69.3 years), and White (87.5%). Medical comorbidities were highly prevalent, particularly hypertension (86.4%), dyslipidemia (93.2%), and coronary artery disease (51.2%).

Plaque features.

Most patients had a PSV of ≥230 cm/s (98.6%); the small proportion of patients (1.4%) with a PSV of <230 cm/s were enrolled based on radiological confirmation of a high-grade stenosis (Fig 3). A broad range of PSVs was represented, with a mean of 370 ± 100 cm/s, minimum of 53 cm/s, and maximum of 761 cm/s. Results of the plaque image analysis are presented in Table II. As expected, the plaques in this study of patients with severe carotid stenosis were large, with a mean longitudinal-sectional area of 62 ± 37 mm2. The mean GSM was 58 with a large variance (SD = 30), indicating heterogeneity within the cohort. This finding was substantiated further by the variance observed in the Gray-Weale classification (mean, 3.5 ± 0.9). The least prevalent tissue type was calcification (mean, 1.6 ± 4.1 mm2). The most prevalent tissue type was muscular tissue (mean, 17 ± 12 mm2). The proportional distribution of plaque features and tissue types is presented in the Supplementary Fig (online only). The proportion of plaques with a GSM of ≤35 was 22.8%, with an IPH of ≥5 mm2 was 30.0%, and with lipid occupying ≥40% of the total plaque area was 3.0% of the cohort. Patients with at least one of the three high-risk features comprised 35.6% of the cohort. The PSV, a measure of percent stenosis, did not correlate well with any of the plaque morphological features (longitudinal sectional plaque area, GSM, Gray-Weale classification, or area occupied by individual tissue components) (Fig 4, A-F). Several plaque features were intercorrelated (Table III), particularly longitudinal sectional plaque area with muscle (r = 0.8) and fibrous tissue area (r = 0.7), GSM with fibrous tissue area (r = 0.6), fibrous tissue with calcification area (r = 0.6), or GSM with Gray-Weale classification (r = 0.6).

Reliability of plaque measurements.

The reliability of plaque measurements was tested in a randomly selected subset of 100 patients. Intraobserver variability in plaque area measurement was low; the mean difference in area measurements performed twice by the same observer was 1.6 mm2, and only 5/100 pairs of measurements had a mean difference of>2 SDs above the group mean difference. Interobserver variability was also low (mean difference, 3.5 mm2, only 6/100 pairs of measurements had a mean difference of>2 SD of the mean difference). Intraclass and interclass correlation coefficients of measurements of plaque area, GSM, IPH, lipid, fibrous tissue, and muscle were high; measurements for calcification were less reliable (Table IV). The mean time taken to perform one plaque analysis was 25 ± 12 minutes per artery.

DISCUSSION

Site-generated, core laboratory-interpreted ultrasound examination provides a reliable way of characterizing carotid plaque morphological features across studies performed at many CREST-2 sites. The initial cohort of patients randomized in CREST-2 have heterogeneity of plaque features, despite all of them causing a high degree of luminal stenosis. Completion of the trial will provide an opportunity to assess whether plaque heterogeneity interacts with response to revascularization and medical management.

Patients included in CREST-2 had stenoses across the spectrum of what would generally be considered high grade and, therefore, reasonable candidates for consideration of revascularization. Of the little over 500 patients reviewed, most had PSVs of >300 cm/s, with scores of patients with velocities of >400 cm/s (Fig 3). Healthy participant bias can affect interventional trials, leading to outcomes better than that expected in clinical practice, often creating skepticism about the generalizability of trial findings. Even noninterventional, nonrandomized studies can fall victim to healthy participant bias. For example, the men and women in the UK Biobank study involving approximately one-half of a million individuals had lower all-cause mortality and cancer incidences than nonparticipants.25 The ultrasound findings of the first 503 patients in CREST-2 demonstrate that investigators are generally not selecting patients with borderline high-grade stenosis.

The VIC has been a vital component of CREST-2. Although eligibility for participation in the trial is determined at the site level by site investigators, the VIC provides quality assurance. This is similar to what was done in the CREST trial, which had an ultrasound core laboratory (UCL), which showed a low 4.4% reclassification rate for stenosis eligibility in baseline ultrasound studies.26 The UCL had detected challenges with Doppler angle alignment used by the sites in CREST. In response to this experience, the VIC emphasized proper angle alignment with the CREST-2 sites in training before certification to participate in the trial. CREST-2 reached its enrollment target in July 2024. After completing a review of all ultrasound images, we plan to perform a comparison similar to what was done in CREST between site and core facility readings. In addition to other parameters, the VIC monitors angle alignment in all studies received by the core. The CREST-2 VIC has a larger mandate than the CREST UCL in that the VIC also serves as a central repository for other noninvasive imaging studies when performed by the sites as part of clinical care. This change reflects the evolving importance of computed tomography angiography and magnetic resonance angiography in clinical practice, where assessment of aortic arch anatomy is important when considering transfemoral stenting as an option for revascularization.

In addition to providing quality control regarding eligibility, the VIC is exploring opportunities to assess plaque morphology using study ultrasound B-mode images. The European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines emphasize the clinical importance of acknowledging the heterogeneity of plaque and its risk for embolism. Having the VIC assess plaque morphology is more feasible than attempting to do so at the site level, where standardization and quality control would be challenging. The core approach as opposed to the site approach decreases variability among carotid readouts. It also allows for adapting to evolving technologies to characterize plaque. One limitation of the core approach is that, if some plaque measures are found to interact with treatment effects in the trial, for example, identify patients who benefit most from revascularization, there will be questions about how the finding can be generalized to diverse clinical ultrasound laboratories around the country. The dedicated program developed to perform the plaque analysis in our study is based on commercially available software, is readily learned and implemented, and can be performed in a relatively short time (25 ± 2 minutes per artery). The program can be integrated easily into currently available ultrasound machines, and we anticipate that the image analysis methodology will become integral to future vascular laboratory testing and interpretation protocols.

Several approaches to measuring ultrasound plaque morphological features have been proposed over the years. In the past two decades, ultrasound imaging has improved, and subjective visual estimation of images has transitioned to quantitative methods of evaluation. The basic premise of interpretation of B-mode images, however, has remained unchanged, and in fact has been strengthened through histological validation studies. Hypoechoic (dark) areas represent hemorrhage or lipid, whereas bright areas represent fibromuscular tissue or calcium.27 Using image brightness quantification, our results provide important information on the distribution of plaque types in a cohort that is representative of patients with high-grade asymptomatic carotid stenosis seen in a typical clinical practice (Table II). Although all the plaques were classified by current risk stratification scheme as being high grade, and by implication high risk, they were quite heterogeneous based on morphological features. The GSM value, an estimate of the overall plaque tissue echodensity, ranged from 0 (more hemorrhage or lipid) through 168 (more fibromuscular tissue or calcium). This finding was mirrored by a broad range of Gray-Weale classes ranging from 1 (predominantly hypoechoic) through 5 (heavily calcified). Our analyses of tissue composition further confirmed a wide distribution of individual tissue types across the plaques. Direct histological evaluation of explanted carotid plaques, and indirect evaluation of ex vivo or in vivo magnetic resonance and computed tomography imaging suggest that increased IPH and lipid correlate with future plaque rupture and stroke events, whereas increased fibromuscular tissue and calcification correlate with a more benign course. This finding implies potential heterogeneity in the natural history of future plaque behavior and in the development of adverse neurologic events in a group of patients who, by current clinical standards, are all identified as being high risk. Thresholds for high-risk ultrasound plaque features have been proposed. Although they have limited prospective validation, applying those criteria identified approximately 35% of our cohort to potentially harbor high-risk features. The prerequisites for identifying which, if any, ultrasound plaque features best predict a true high-risk subset of asymptomatic patients are (1) a standardized and reliable estimate of the plaque features, (2) a prospective study enrolling patients free of selection bias, and (3) low neurological event rates necessitating a large sample size. Once enrollment is complete, CREST-2 is well-positioned to address this question.

The VIC assessed the degree of agreement within and between two raters for cross-sectional plaque area measurements and found minimal bias. Mean differences across the range of plaque areas were low for both the intrarater and inter-rater Bland-Altman plots (Fig 5, A and B). Further, there was no clear funneling seen on the Bland-Altman plots to suggest bias as a function of plaque area. The reliability for VIC measurements of plaque area, GSM, Gray-Weale classification, and tissue composition were high (Table IV). Whether this degree of reliability can generalize to measurements performed by diverse clinical ultrasound laboratories is not known. However, the image analysis protocol used was semiautomated, based on readily available software that can be incorporated into existing ultrasound machines, and not time or effort intensive. These technological advances may make plaque feature measurements more automated and easier to accomplish across diverse settings.

In conclusion, this analysis of baseline ultrasound studies in just over 500 cases enrolled in CREST-2 demonstrates that investigators are enrolling patients with a diverse range of stenoses by velocity criteria within the high-grade category. This work will help to generalize the eagerly anticipated findings of the trial to a broad range of patients with high-grade stenosis and not just borderline cases. There is significant heterogeneity in the plaque morphological features of this group of asymptomatic patients. This finding implies that there may be heterogeneity in the natural history of future plaque behavior and in the development of adverse neurologic events in a group of patients who, by current clinical standards, are all identified as being high risk. Finally, the study shows that the VIC can measure plaque morphological features reliably in archived ultrasound B-mode images performed across diverse trial site vascular laboratories.
