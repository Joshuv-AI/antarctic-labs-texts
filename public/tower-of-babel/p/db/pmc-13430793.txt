Non-HIV Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia–associated immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome: a case report and systematic review
Authors: Gulxan Turhon, Chang Gao, Yi Chi, Longxiang Su, Yingying Yang, Fang Wang, Qianlin Wang, Yun Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is a common opportunistic infection with high mortality among immunocompromised patients. Although adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy improves outcomes in HIV-associated PJP, its role in non-HIV patients remains controversial. Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), a potentially overlooked inflammatory response, may lead to rapid clinical deterioration.Case presentationA 60-year-old woman developed non-HIV-associated PJP following high-dose glucocorticoid therapy for anti-IgLON5 antibody-associated encephalopathy, which progressed to severe hypoxic respiratory failure. During anti-PJP treatment, glucocorticoids were rapidly tapered and discontinued because of concerns regarding immunosuppression. Subsequently, the patient developed recurrent fever, worsening oxygenation, and radiographic progression, while IL-6 levels increased from 5.3 pg/mL to 574 pg/mL. Microbiological testing revealed no evidence of new infection. Considering the recent immune reconstitution, PJP-associated IRIS was diagnosed. After glucocorticoid therapy was reinitiated while the original anti-PJP regimen was maintained, inflammatory markers decreased significantly, respiratory function improved, and mechanical ventilation was successfully discontinued. Additionally, we reviewed previously reported cases of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS. Combined with the present case, eight patients were included. Most cases had a clear immune reconstitution trigger and presented with progressive hypoxemia, radiographic deterioration, and inflammatory activation. Most patients improved after intensive glucocorticoid therapy.ConclusionIn non-HIV-associated PJP, clinical deterioration does not necessarily indicate antimicrobial treatment failure, and PJP-associated IRIS should be considered. Early recognition of inflammatory activation and timely immunomodulatory therapy may improve prognosis. Dynamic monitoring of inflammatory markers such as IL-6 may help guide glucocorticoid therapy.

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is a common opportunistic infection with a high mortality rate that occurs primarily in immunocompromised or immunodeficient patients [1]. PJP typically presents as acute interstitial pneumonia, with pathological features including diffuse alveolar damage, foamy alveolar exudates, and Pneumocystis cyst formation, and may rapidly progress to severe hypoxic respiratory failure [2]. In recent years, with the widespread use of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and anticancer chemotherapeutic agents, the incidence of non-HIV-associated PJP has gradually increased. Compared with HIV-associated PJP, non-HIV-associated PJP is characterized by more rapid disease progression and poorer prognosis.

Currently, adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy has been shown to reduce mortality in patients with severe HIV-associated PJP [3]; however, in non-HIV patients, significant controversy remains regarding the timing, dosage, and duration of treatment. On the one hand, glucocorticoids may exacerbate immunosuppression and increase the risk of secondary infections; on the other hand, some patients may experience disease exacerbation due to abnormal inflammatory activation even after a decline in pathogen burden. Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) is one of the key mechanisms underlying this process. It typically occurs following rapid reversal of immunosuppression and may manifest as worsening oxygenation, progression of pulmonary imaging findings, and marked elevation of inflammatory markers. Although IRIS is well recognized in HIV-associated infections, a comprehensive understanding of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS remains limited. Reports of this condition are scarce, and its clinical features, diagnostic criteria, and optimal immunomodulatory strategies have yet to be clearly defined.

This report describes a patient with anti-IgLON5 antibody-associated encephalopathy who developed severe non-HIV-associated PJP following high-dose glucocorticoid therapy and subsequently experienced PJP-associated IRIS during glucocorticoid tapering. The patient achieved a favorable outcome after glucocorticoid therapy was resumed under the guidance of dynamic monitoring of inflammatory markers. Furthermore, we conducted a systematic review of previously reported cases of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS to summarize their clinical characteristics and explore the potential value of dynamic monitoring of inflammatory markers in facilitating the identification of IRIS and guiding glucocorticoid therapy.

Case report

A 60-year-old woman was diagnosed with anti-IgLON5 antibody-associated encephalopathy. The patient had no major chronic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease, or malignancy. Before the diagnosis of anti-IgLON5 antibody-associated encephalopathy, she had not received immunosuppressive agents (e.g., mycophenolate mofetil or rituximab) or prophylactic antimicrobial therapy for opportunistic infections. She denied smoking and alcohol consumption. She also reported no recent travel, no significant environmental exposures (e.g., birds, soil, or mold), and no known contact with patients with respiratory tract infections. The patient received high-dose glucocorticoid pulse therapy consisting of methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day for 3 days, followed by 500 mg/day for 3 days, and was subsequently transitioned to oral prednisone. The initial prednisone dose was 1 mg/kg/day and was tapered by 5 mg weekly. During the second week of tapering, the patient developed shortness of breath, fever, and hypoxemia. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed an increased extent of diffuse ground-glass opacities in both lungs, complicated by respiratory failure, which led to transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Upon ICU admission, high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy was initiated (oxygen flow rate: 40 L/min; FiO₂: 53%), with a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio of 288 mmHg. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 9.29 × 10⁹/L (reference range: 3.5–9.5 × 10⁹/L), C-reactive protein (CRP) of 27.3 mg/L (reference range: 0–10 mg/L), procalcitonin (PCT) of 0.09 ng/mL (reference range: <0.15 ng/mL), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) of 5.7 pg/mL (reference range: 0–7 pg/mL). Physical examination revealed bilateral crackles on lung auscultation. Laboratory findings demonstrated Pneumocystis jirovecii (PJ)-DNA at 23.02 copies/mL and [4, 5]-β-D-glucan at 464.9 pg/mL. Together with the chest CT findings of diffuse ground-glass opacities, these results confirmed the diagnosis of PJP. The patient presented with tachypnea, fever, and hypoxemia. Given her immunocompromised status, an infectious etiology was initially suspected, and a systematic diagnostic workup was performed. First, bacterial and atypical pathogens were considered. The patient produced white mucoid sputum, but chest auscultation revealed no focal findings, and chest CT showed no focal or lobar consolidation. Procalcitonin and white blood cell counts were not significantly elevated. Repeated sputum and blood cultures were negative, and the clinical course was inconsistent with bacterial pneumonia. Second, viral pathogens were evaluated. Cytomegalovirus PCR was negative, as were tests for common respiratory viruses, including influenza A/B, adenovirus, and respiratory syncytial virus. Third, Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection was excluded because the patient had no prior history of tuberculosis, acid-fast staining and T-SPOT testing were negative, and there were no radiographic features suggestive of tuberculosis. Fourth, other fungal infections were considered unlikely. Invasive aspergillosis and cryptococcal pneumonia were not supported by imaging findings, and serum galactomannan testing and fungal cultures were negative. Non-infectious etiologies, including drug-induced pneumonitis, autoimmune lung disease, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and cardiogenic pulmonary edema, were also excluded. Treatment was initiated with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) at 15 mg/kg/day based on the trimethoprim component, together with intravenous methylprednisolone 40 mg/day for anti-inflammatory therapy beginning on the first day of ICU admission. On day 4, follow-up chest CT demonstrated progression of diffuse ground-glass opacities, and the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio decreased to 143 mmHg. Oxygen support was escalated to intermittent non-invasive ventilation (S/T mode; IPAP 14 cmH₂O, EPAP 5 cmH₂O, FiO₂ 100%). While the glucocorticoid dose remained unchanged, the antimicrobial regimen was intensified by increasing TMP-SMX to 20 mg/kg/day and adding caspofungin at 50 mg/day. Subsequently, the absolute lymphocyte counts gradually declined from 0.87 × 10⁹/L on day 1 (D1) to a nadir of 0.22 × 10⁹/L on day 10 (D10). To reduce the potential immunosuppressive effects of glucocorticoids, the glucocorticoid dose was gradually tapered and discontinued on day 13.

Following glucocorticoid withdrawal, the patient’s condition deteriorated rapidly. Over the subsequent 6 days (days 13–18), fever recurred and inflammatory markers increased markedly. IL-6 levels rose from 5.3 pg/mL to 574 pg/mL, accompanied by an increase in CRP levels. At the same time, respiratory failure progressed, with the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio decreasing to below 100 mmHg by day 17, necessitating endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome was considered based on the marked elevation of inflammatory markers, negative comprehensive microbiological investigations excluding new infection, and the clinical context of recent glucocorticoid withdrawal with immune recovery. Accordingly, while continuing the original anti-PJP regimen, glucocorticoid therapy was reinitiated on day 18 with intravenous methylprednisolone 60 mg/day, followed by gradual tapering to 20 mg/day by day 60, when the patient was discharged from the ICU. This therapeutic strategy proved highly effective. Inflammatory markers decreased significantly, respiratory failure gradually improved, and the patient was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation and extubated on day 53 (Fig. 1, clinical course of the patient).

Discussion

We report a case of severe non-HIV-associated PJP in a patient with anti-IgLON5 antibody-associated encephalopathy. The patient developed immunosuppression following high-dose glucocorticoid therapy, which subsequently led to PJP and severe hypoxic respiratory failure. During anti-PJP treatment, the patient developed recurrent fever, worsening oxygenation, and progressive pulmonary imaging findings following glucocorticoid withdrawal, accompanied by a marked increase in inflammatory markers. However, repeated comprehensive microbiological investigations revealed no evidence of new infection. After glucocorticoid therapy was reinitiated, inflammatory markers rapidly decreased, respiratory function gradually improved, and the patient was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation. Considering the recent immune reconstitution, clinical course, and response to glucocorticoid therapy, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with PJP-associated IRIS.

The classic definition of IRIS refers to an abnormal and excessive inflammatory response directed against pathogens or self-antigens following restoration of immune function. Although no uniform diagnostic criteria currently exist, the diagnosis in non-HIV patients generally requires the following features: (1) a clear event of immune recovery; (2) paradoxical inflammatory worsening; and (3) exclusion of progressive infection, antimicrobial treatment failure, and other alternative diagnoses [6, 7]. Based on clinical presentation, IRIS can be further classified into paradoxical IRIS and unmasking IRIS. Paradoxical IRIS refers to inflammatory worsening occurring after effective antimicrobial therapy as immune function recovers, whereas unmasking IRIS refers to a previously latent infection that becomes clinically apparent during immune recovery and manifests as a marked inflammatory response [8]. The present case demonstrated the typical features of paradoxical IRIS.

Although IRIS is widely recognized in people living with HIV (PLWH), non-HIV-associated IRIS has received relatively limited attention, and reports of PJP-associated IRIS remain particularly scarce. To further clarify the clinical characteristics of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS, we conducted a systematic search of English-language literature in the PubMed database from inception to May 2026. The search strategy was as follows: (“Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia” OR PJP OR “Pneumocystis carinii pneumonia” OR PCP) AND (“immune reconstitution”) AND (“non-HIV”). After retrieval of the initial records, titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened sequentially to exclude studies unrelated to the topic of interest. Ultimately, only case reports explicitly describing non-HIV patients with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia complicated by immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome were included for detailed analysis. Following screening, seven studies met the inclusion criteria. Combined with the present case, a total of eight patients were included. A summary of the clinical characteristics of previously reported non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS cases is presented in Table 1. Most patients were female (7/8), and the underlying conditions included autoimmune diseases, hematologic malignancies, primary immunodeficiencies, and solid organ transplantation. Previous immunosuppressive therapies mainly included glucocorticoids, methotrexate, tacrolimus, azathioprine, and chemotherapeutic agents.

It is noteworthy that all cases were associated with a clear trigger for immune recovery, including tapering or discontinuation of glucocorticoids, withdrawal of immunosuppressive agents, administration of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) to promote leukocyte recovery, vitamin B12 therapy to correct bone marrow suppression, and discontinuation of calcineurin inhibitors. These findings suggest that rapid reversal of immunosuppression may represent a common mechanism underlying the development of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS. Most cases presented as paradoxical IRIS, whereas only one case was classified as unmasking IRIS. IRIS generally occurred during the early phase of immune recovery following PJP treatment, most commonly within 3–10 days. However, some patients developed relatively delayed manifestations during glucocorticoid tapering, suggesting that immune reconstitution-associated inflammation may persist even after pathogen burden has declined.

The clinical manifestations were relatively consistent across cases. Most patients developed progressive hypoxemia or worsening respiratory failure (7/8), including three cases that progressed to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and three that required invasive mechanical ventilation. Radiologically, most patients demonstrated expansion of bilateral ground-glass opacities (GGOs) or progression of pulmonary consolidation (6/8). In addition, inflammatory activation was common and was characterized by elevated CRP levels, leukocytosis, or markedly increased cytokine levels (5/8). In cases with available microbiological data, most patients showed no evidence of new infection, suggesting that clinical deterioration more likely reflected immune-mediated inflammatory injury rather than ongoing pathogen replication.

Regarding treatment, after exclusion of new infection, high-dose glucocorticoid therapy or glucocorticoid pulse therapy was the most commonly used and effective intervention. Except for one pediatric patient with ARDS who died, all other patients improved following high-dose glucocorticoid therapy. These findings further support the concept that the core mechanism of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS is predominantly abnormal inflammatory activation rather than simple progression of infection.

Compared with previous reports, the present case represents a severe case of non-HIV-associated PJP-IRIS requiring mechanical ventilation support. Previous studies have shown that non-HIV-associated PJP patients admitted to the ICU have a high mortality rate [14]; therefore, early identification of reversible causes of clinical deterioration is particularly important. A major feature of this case is the detailed documentation of dynamic changes in oxygenation status, inflammatory markers, and imaging findings following glucocorticoid withdrawal. After glucocorticoid discontinuation, the patient’s IL-6 level rapidly increased from 5.3 pg/mL to 574 pg/mL, accompanied by recurrent fever, worsening oxygenation, and progression of pulmonary imaging findings, while repeated comprehensive microbiological investigations remained negative. Following reinitiation of glucocorticoid therapy, inflammatory markers decreased significantly, accompanied by a gradual improvement in respiratory function. This clinical course further supports the view that the deterioration was primarily driven by abnormal inflammatory activation rather than recurrence of infection.

Previous studies have suggested that inflammatory biomarkers may help identify a hyperinflammatory phenotype in non-HIV-associated PJP. One study demonstrated that bronchoalveolar lavage fluid CRP levels were an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with non-HIV-associated PJP [15]. Another study found that a CRP level > 135 mg/L was significantly associated with poor prognosis, with a markedly higher mortality rate observed in the hyperinflammatory group [16]. Furthermore, IL-6, a key pro-inflammatory mediator in IRIS, promotes downstream CRP production and contributes to tissue injury [17]. Experimental studies have shown that IL-6 blockade can attenuate inflammation and improve survival [18]. In a retrospective study of critically ill patients with non-HIV-associated PJP, adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy significantly reduced IL-6 levels and improved clinical outcomes [19]. Consistent with these previous findings, the present case suggests that dynamic monitoring of CRP and IL-6 may not only facilitate identification of IRIS-associated hyperinflammatory states but also provide potential guidance for determining the timing of glucocorticoid intervention and evaluating therapeutic response.

Although the efficacy of adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy in HIV-associated PJP has been well established, its use in non-HIV patients remains highly controversial. Previous studies have focused primarily on whether glucocorticoids were administered [20, 21], with relatively limited attention paid to the risks associated with glucocorticoid tapering. The present case suggests that overly rapid glucocorticoid withdrawal may trigger marked immune rebound and lead to uncontrolled pulmonary inflammation in some patients. Therefore, in patients who have recently undergone immune reconstitution and subsequently develop worsening respiratory failure accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers in the absence of evidence of new infection, clinicians should maintain a high level of suspicion for PJP-associated IRIS in addition to considering antimicrobial treatment failure or secondary infection. This approach may help avoid delays in initiating immunomodulatory therapy caused by simply intensifying antimicrobial treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, this case report and literature review suggest that disease progression during antimicrobial therapy in patients with non-HIV-associated PJP does not necessarily indicate treatment failure; the possibility of PJP-associated IRIS should also be considered. In patients who have recently undergone immune reconstitution and subsequently develop worsening oxygenation, radiographic progression, and markedly elevated inflammatory markers in the absence of evidence of new infection, IRIS driven by abnormal inflammatory activation should be suspected. Timely recognition and reinitiation of glucocorticoid therapy may be critical for improving prognosis. Furthermore, dynamic monitoring of inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP may provide important guidance for individualized immunomodulatory therapy in patients with non-HIV-associated PJP.
