Artificial intelligence in pediatric pain: a systematic review
Authors: Ziyang Wang, Jinjiu Hu, Jinsong Zeng, Hangyang Li, Hongyao Leng, Qiao Shen, Xianlan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPain is a challenging, multifaceted symptom reported by most pediatric patients. This systematic review aims to explore the progress and effectiveness of applying artificial intelligence (AI) technology in pediatric pain management.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov was conducted. The search combined pain-related terms ("Pain management", "Pain assessment", "Pain measurement", "Pain relief", "Pain control", "Analgesics", "Pediatric pain"), age-related terms ("Children", "pediatrics", "Neonate", "Infant"), and AI core sub-domain terms ("Artificial intelligence", "Machine learning", "Deep learning", "Convolutional neural network", "Support vector machine", "Random forest", "Long short-term memory") using strict Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT) published up to March 20, 2026. AI models, their validation, and performance were summarized.ResultsThe analysis of 71 studies revealed distinct AI application patterns in pediatric pain management. Fifty-nine studies focused on pain assessment using deep learning (post-2020: 86.7%) and classical machine learning (pre-2015: 83.3%) through facial expression analysis (40.8%), multimodal fusion (25.4%), or physiological signals (9.9%). Most employed observational designs (57.7%) with small (<50 participants, 42.2%) to medium (50-200, 33.8%) samples, reflecting clinical data challenges. Multimodal approaches significantly outperformed unimodal methods (AUC difference: +0.13, p<0.01). The remaining 12 studies (16.9%) explored pain management, primarily using robot-assisted interventions with cognitive-behavioral strategies like guided breathing and gamification during procedural pain. However, a substantial proportion of the included studies (47.5% of assessment studies and 11 of 12 intervention studies) showed high risk of bias.ConclusionThis review offers substantiation that AI technology has been employed to enhance the efficiency of pain detection and assessment, thereby assisting healthcare professionals and patients in more adeptly managing acute pain. Future progress in precise pain assessment and management will depend on integrating deep learning with multimodal data and large clinical databases, alongside efforts to establish standardized datasets and validate models in real-world settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03571-5.Keywords: Pediatrics, Pain, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Deep learning

Introduction

Pediatric pain represents a significant global public health issue that necessitates collective attention and concerted efforts from the entire society to address. Hospitalized children undergo numerous painful procedures daily, with studies reporting 7.5–17.3 procedural pain stimuli per child per day [1], increasing to 26 times for preterm infants [2]. Critically, 83% of common clinical procedures cause severe pain [3], while over 85% of children experience postoperative pain and 63% report significant pain persisting after discharge, triggering substantial adverse effects on quality of life, emotional state, and behavior [4]. Inadequate acute pain management can delay postoperative mobilization, increase opioid consumption and side effects, and is correlated with impaired wound healing and chronic pain development [5, 6]. A recent meta-analysis indicates 20.8% prevalence of chronic pain in pediatric populations, equating to one in five children and adolescents affected [7]. As both an unpleasant sensory experience and the fifth vital sign [8], pain requires precise assessment for effective management [9], yet pain remains substantially underassessed and undertreated in children. The principal barriers include limitations in existing assessment tools [10], with flaws in design, subjective scoring nature, and variable interpreter perceptions often leading to inconsistent implementation [11]. The absence of a universal assessment method necessitates consideration of each child’s age, linguistic capacity, ethnicity, and cognitive abilities.

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in medicine presents transformative opportunities [12]. AI encompasses machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and natural language processing, technologies capable of analyzing complex datasets, identifying patterns, and generating predictive insights to support clinical decision-making [13–15]. ML algorithms excel in medical image analysis, outcome prediction, and personalized treatment planning [16, 17], while neural networks and DL architectures like convolutional neural networks advance diagnostic capabilities [16]. These methodologies, categorized into supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning paradigms [18]. Such algorithms enable applications ranging from personalized treatment to robotic surgery and drug discovery [19–21]. The classification of these methods is further refined by their functional tasks, which include segmentation, regression, and classification. Natural language processing (NLP) has emerged as a particularly effective tool in the automation of clinical documentation and patient engagement through virtual assistants [17]. In sum, these technological advancements collectively underscore the transformative potential of AI in improving diagnostic precision, therapeutic decision-making, and operational efficiency across healthcare systems [13].

AI applications have begun to make inroads into pediatric pain management, offering promising improvements in pain assessment accuracy, personalized treatment recommendations, and patient outcomes [16]. However, no study has comprehensively evaluated the use of AI applications in pediatric pain management. A comprehensive synthesis of current AI-based interventions and related outcomes is imperative for both medical personnel, pursuing methods to facilitate pain assessment and intervention over extended periods, and software engineers, seeking a foundation for further research aligned with the actual needs of pediatrics. This systematic review therefore addresses two critical objectives: (1) to evaluate current applications and performance of AI-based techniques for pediatric pain assessment and management, and (2) to determine the clinical outcomes of these approaches across healthcare environments for children from birth to 18 years.

Methods

The study was conducted and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22] (see Supplementary Material A for checklist). The review protocol was registered prospectively on PROSPERO (CRD42024621290).

Goal and research questions

This systematic review aims to map the current research landscape of AI technology in pediatric pain management. The study addresses two primary questions:

Which areas of pediatric pain (e.g., pain type, age range, algorithm) do current AI studies focus on?

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across seven databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Library) and one register (ClinicalTrials.gov). The search strategy combined pain-related terms (e.g., “Pediatric pain” OR “Children’s pain” OR “Pain assessment” OR “Pain management”) AND age-related terms (e.g., “Children” OR “Pediatrics” OR “Neonate” OR “Infant”) AND AI core sub-domain terms (e.g., “Artificial intelligence” OR “Machine learning” OR “Deep learning” OR “Convolutional neural network” OR “Support vector machine” OR “Random forest” OR “Long short-term memory”) with strict Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT) for publications up to March 20, 2026. All search terms were aligned with MeSH database terms to ensure the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the literature search. The complete strategy is in Supplementary Material B.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Feasibility, pilot, evaluation, experimental, or quasi-experimental studies; (2) Focus on AI applications for pain assessment/management in pediatric populations (<18 years). Exclusion criteria were: (1) Non-English articles; (2) Studies not developing/testing AI-based techniques or focusing solely on physiological pain signals; (3) Qualitative studies, case studies, conference abstracts, editorials, and reviews; (4) Adult populations.

Study selection

Abstracts were reviewed for inclusion by two independent reviewers (ZYW and JJH). Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and a third reviewer was involved when necessary. Retrospective articles meeting inclusion criteria were reviewed fully by another author (JSZ) using an a priori data extraction form. Prospective articles meeting inclusion criteria were reviewed fully by two authors (ZYW and JJH) using a different a priori data extraction form. Disagreements were discussed and resolved with a third reviewer if required. When the eligibility of a study was ambiguous in the publication, attempts were made to contact the author(s) via email to ascertain the eligibility.

Data extraction and management/data collection

Data extraction was performed by one researcher (ZYW) and verified by a second (HYL). Extracted data included: Author, Country, Data sources, Number of patients, Gender, Age, Pain/operation types, Study design, AI Method(s), Apparatus, Scales, Modal/indicator, Comparison, Model’s performance, Intervention, Control, and Output Outcomes. The extraction sheet was piloted and refined prior to full data extraction.

Quality assessment/risk of bias

Two independent researchers conducted a comprehensive evaluation of methodological quality and risk of bias. The Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [23, 24] was used to systematically evaluate biases across four domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis. This 20-item tool classified overall risk of bias as low, high, or unclear [24, 25]. Reporting quality was assessed using TRIPOD guidelines [26], focusing on model development, validation, and clinical applicability. For intervention studies, the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (ROB 2) [27] evaluated randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Evidence certainty was graded using the GRADE approach [28], which considers study limitations, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias.

Data synthesis

Due to the heterogeneity of the study designs and outcome measures, the results were synthesised narratively.

Results

Search and screening

A comprehensive search was conducted across seven databases and one register, yielding 3,939 initial records. After removing 492 duplicate records, 3,447 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 3,234 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Full-text retrieval was attempted for 213 articles, with 67 unavailable (e.g., conference abstracts without full text). The remaining 146 full-text articles were assessed, and 75 were excluded due to non-pediatric populations (n = 25), lack of AI application (n = 5), absence of pain assessment/management (n = 20), non-human research (n = 2), retracted status (n = 2), or unavailability of full text (n = 21). Ultimately, 71 studies [29–99] were included in the review (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Most included studies were published in the last 5 years (n = 45, 63.4%). About half of the studies were conducted in the United States (n = 19, 26.8%) and China (n = 19, 26.8%). The sample size ranged from 10 to 4104, varying by the characteristics of the participants and the study aim. Nearly half of the studies (n = 30, 42.2%) had a sample size of less than 50, and only 8.5% exceeded 1000 subjects. The population focus skewed toward younger patients, with neonates (n = 36, 50.7%) and infants (n = 6, 8.5%). Pain assessment (n = 59, 83.1%) dominates in pain management, while the remaining research focuses on pain intervention (n = 12, 16.9%), with acute pain being the most studied condition (n = 38, 53.5%). Observational designs (n = 41, 57.7%) were most common, while facial expression analysis (n = 29, 40.8%) emerged as the primary assessment modality. Results of classification

AI applications for participants

Among the studies, participants range in age from neonates to adolescents. Ages 0–1 were the majority of AI in pain management studies at 59.2% [31–33, 36–40, 43, 45, 46, 49, 52–57, 59–73, 75–78, 80, 81, 83, 86, 87], followed by ages 6–12 (9.9%) [35, 41, 84, 89, 92, 97, 98], and adolescents only account for 1.4%. Eighteen studies had mixed ages, with an overall range from 18 hours to 18 years. The age of participants in 3 studies (4.2%) [47, 82, 99] was not specified. Almost half of the included studies 42.2% (n = 30) [30, 35–39, 43, 46, 49, 50, 55, 61, 62, 64, 67–71, 73, 75, 81, 83, 86, 87, 90, 91, 94, 95, 97] had sample sizes fewer than 50 participants, 33.8% (n = 24) [29, 31, 45, 48, 51–53, 57, 63, 65, 72, 74, 76, 77, 80, 82, 85, 88, 89, 92, 93, 96, 98, 99] had a sample size ranging from 50 to 200 participants, 15.5% (n = 11) [32, 33, 40–42, 44, 58, 59, 66, 78, 79] encompassed 200 – 1000 participants, and 8.5% (n = 6) [34, 47, 54, 56, 60, 84] involved more than 1000 participants. Notably, studies with larger sample sizes (>200 participants) were predominantly concentrated in the research on procedural and postoperative pain. The primary data source for the studies was real-patient clinical settings 63.4% (n = 45) [29, 31, 45, 48, 51–53, 57, 63, 65, 72, 74, 78, 81, 82, 84–86, 88, 89, 92, 93, 96, 98, 99] only 20 [31, 36–40, 43, 46, 55, 57, 59, 61, 62, 66, 73, 75, 76, 83, 87] from public datasets accounted. Custom datasets (7.0%, n = 5) [47, 56, 77, 79, 80] were typically developed for specialized pain analysis (e.g., cry acoustics or facial expression libraries), and one [49] was mixed sources. Forty-eight studies focused on acute pain, including procedural pain [32, 33, 35–39, 42, 43, 52–56, 59, 60, 64, 66, 67, 70, 71, 74, 76–78, 80, 83, 85–92, 95–97], postoperative pain [29, 45, 48, 50, 57, 72, 79, 82, 84], and acute abdominal pain [43]. Only three reports focused on chronic/multi-site pain [41, 51, 84], and 20 studies were of mixed types [30, 31, 34, 40, 44, 46, 47, 49, 58, 61–63, 65, 68, 69, 73, 75, 81, 93, 94].

AI for objectives in pediatric pain

Analysis revealed distinct AI application patterns across the 71 included studies. Most studies (n = 59) focused on pain assessment, employing deep learning and classical machine learning to quantify pain intensity through facial expression analysis (40.8%), multimodal fusion (25.4%), or physiological signals (9.9%). These primarily used observational (57.7%) and experimental (25.4%) designs with small-to-medium datasets (<50 participants: 42.2%; 50–200: 33.8%), reflecting data acquisition challenges in clinical settings (Supplementary Material C1). The remaining studies (16.9%, n = 12) investigated pain intervention, predominantly using robot-assisted interaction. These employed cognitive-behavioral strategies like guided breathing and gamification to mitigate procedural pain stress (11.3%). All intervention studies utilized randomized controlled trials and often incorporated hybrid algorithms, demonstrating practical implementation considerations (Supplementary Material C2).

AI modals used for pediatric pain assessment research

The evolution of pain assessment modalities shows a distinct shift from unimodal to multimodal approaches (Fig. 2). Facial expression analysis predominated (40.8%), primarily focusing on standardized action units (AUs) like brow lowering (AU4), eye constriction (AU6/7), and nasolabial furrow deepening (AU9/10) as key pain indicators [29, 30]. Physiological signals were used in 7 studies (9.9%), especially in preprocessed raw EEG signals [35, 51, 86, 87] and measuring skin potential signals [42], while only 5 studies explored audio assessment [40, 44, 77, 81, 83]. Multimodal systems demonstrated specific integration patterns, with common combinations including facial-audio-movement-vital signs [32, 33, 53, 61, 71], facial-movement [55, 56, 66], and facial-physiological signals [50, 52, 67]. Two studies explored the combination of facial expression and audio with body movement [46, 57], facial expression with audio and physiological indicators [62], facial expression with physiological indicators and body movement [70], and facial expression and audio [79]. There are also two studies [41, 58] that combine physiological indicators with clinical laboratory tests and imaging results to detect the incidence of multi-site pain and growth pain. More comprehensive systems integrated all primary modalities with additional behavioral measures (5 studies) [32, 33, 53, 61, 71].

Distribution of artificial intelligence modalities in pain management in pediatrics

Multimodal approaches showed superior performance: Salekin et al. [46] achieved AUC 0.90 and 78.9% accuracy; Chen et al. [32] attained 82.8% accuracy by combining facial expressions, crying sounds, and vital signs; feature-level fusion [61] reached 95.56% accuracy. Robot-assisted pain management [88–99] demonstrated high feasibility but mixed outcomes - Jibb et al. [91] reported no significant pain reduction (p = 0.68) with MEDiPORT despite improved adaptive behaviors (p < 0.05). Modality selection reflected developmental considerations: neonates were assessed more through facial-physiological combinations (68%) versus older children’s facial-behavioral approaches (56%).

AI algorithms used in pediatric pain research

Analysis of included studies identified three primary AI algorithm categories: traditional machine learning, deep learning, and hybrid approaches. Traditional machine learning was most common (45.1%, n = 32), with Support Vector Machines and Random Forest predominating. These methods achieved median accuracy of 82.4% (range: 70–98%) in binary pain classification using structured clinical data and extracted facial features. Deep learning approaches (45.1%, n = 32) demonstrated superior performance with complex multimodal data, particularly convolutional neural networks for image analysis and long short-term memory networks for temporal data processing. The highest reported accuracy reached 93.1% using VGG-Face with gradient-weighted class activation mapping [65], while transformer architectures achieved 87.8% accuracy in EEG-based detection [35]. Hybrid methods (9.9%, n = 7) combining traditional and deep learning components showed particular efficacy in small-sample settings, delivering median accuracy improvements of 7.2% over pure machine learning approaches. A clear temporal evolution emerged: studies before 2015 predominantly used traditional machine learning (83.3%), while the majority of post-2020 studies (86.7%) incorporated deep learning components. Among post-2020 studies, 13.3% continued to employ traditional machine learning approaches. Multimodal approaches significantly outperformed unimodal methods (AUC difference: +0.13, p < 0.01), though requiring substantially greater computational resources. Risk of bias and quality of evidence

Adherence to TRIPOD guidelines

The range of compliance with the TRIPOD checklist ranges from 39% to 95%. While the adherence rate for components such as study design, eligibility criteria, and statistical methods exceeded 80%, it declined to less than 50% for critical elements, including the blinding of outcomes/predictors, the management of missing data, model development, and the identification of risk groups. Specifically, non-compliance is mainly manifested in the methods and results sections, including aspects such as the rationality of the sample size, the handling method of missing data, the ethical approval status, and the verification results of model performance.

Risk-of-bias assessment and quality of studies

Among 59 analyzed studies, 28 (47.5%) demonstrated high overall risk of bias, 9 (15.3%) unclear, and 22 (37.3%) low. The analysis domain showed the highest risk (40 studies, 67.8%), primarily due to unreported missing participant data, unperformed model calibration, and unaddressed overfitting. In the participant domain, 28 studies (47.5%) were high risk, mainly from small sample sizes and homogeneous populations. For predictors, 24 studies (40.7%) showed high risk due to inadequate missing data management and unclear predictor availability. In outcomes, 26 studies (44.1%) were high risk because of vague outcome definitions, lack of blinding, and unclear time intervals between predictor and outcome assessment.

Among 12 intervention studies, 11 exhibited high risk of bias, primarily concerning blinding of participants/personnel, outcome assessment blinding, and random sequence generation. GRADE assessment revealed eight studies with moderate quality, three with low quality, and one with very low quality. Overall, the certainty of evidence for the 12 intervention studies ranged from moderate to very low. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Material D1–D3.

Discussion

Pediatric pain management remains a persistent clinical challenge. While artificial intelligence demonstrates significant potential to transform this field, successful clinical implementation requires enhanced awareness and understanding among healthcare professionals regarding AI capabilities and limitations. This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on AI applications in pediatric pain management, spanning pain diagnostics [100], objective assessment [101], resource optimization [102], and research methodologies [103]. By enabling earlier detection of pain management needs and facilitating personalized intervention strategies, AI technologies hold promise for improving patient outcomes. However, clinicians must critically consider the limitations and challenges identified in this review. The following sections analyze the reviewed evidence in relation to our research questions, examine current methodological approaches and their constraints, and propose directions for future research.

Input

A notable discrepancy exists between unimodal and multimodal approaches in pain assessment modalities. While 57.7% of studies employed unimodal methods, vision-based approaches predominated (29 studies), substantially outnumbering contact-sensor (7 studies) and audio-based approaches (5 studies). This imbalance reflects both the technical complexity of multimodal frameworks and practical limitations of contact sensors in clinical environments. Fang et al. [34] developed the Clinical Pain Expression of Children (CPEC) dataset and corresponding Children’s Postoperative Pain Assessment Neural Network (CPANN), achieving 82.1% accuracy and 73.9% F1 score in automated facial expression-based pain assessment. However, single-modality datasets may inherently limit deep learning potential. Similarly, Jimenez et al. [72] reported 92.9% accuracy using VGG-19 models, though limited dataset size compromised model generalizability and introduced sensitivity concerns. Multimodal approaches demonstrate performance advantages. Salekin et al. [46] integrated visual and acoustic signals in a spatiotemporal framework, achieving AUC 0.87 and 79% accuracy - representing 6.67% and 6.33% improvements over unimodal methods, respectively. Nevertheless, this study was constrained by small dataset scale and brief monitoring duration, highlighting the critical need for expanded data collection. Liang et al. [79] further advanced multimodal approaches by developing a multi-expert framework (MMF) that integrates facial and vocal expressions. On a postoperative pain database of 701 children, their method achieved a mean absolute error of 1.04 and a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.888, demonstrating the potential of expert-based multimodal fusion for pain intensity estimation.

Electrodermal activity (EDA) emerges as particularly valuable among physiological signals. Susam et al. [50] combined EDA monitoring with facial expression analysis, implementing weighted Bayesian fusion with linear SVM to achieve 90.91% classification accuracy for clinically significant pain. Despite such promising results, the limited number of multimodal studies (n = 18) precludes definitive conclusions regarding optimal modality combinations. Physiological signal-based assessment faces distinct challenges. While EEG, EMG, and ECG provide rich neurophysiological data [104–108] and autonomic responses (heart rate variability, cortisol levels) offer indirect pain biomarkers [109–111], these signals require correlation with behavioral indicators and remain susceptible to confounding from anxiety, environmental factors, and developmental variations. Furthermore, physiological measures cannot fully capture subjective pain experience and raise practical concerns regarding cost, ethics, and privacy. The technical complexity of biopotential acquisition, combined with individual variability and situational influences, necessitates integration with behavioral indicators and sophisticated AI analytics. Future research should prioritize multimodal fusion while accounting for the complex interplay between pain and concurrent emotional states.

Processing

Automated pain assessment methodologies comprise two main categories: traditional feature-based approaches and deep learning-based methods. Early research employed predefined algorithms for feature extraction, yet these methods demonstrated limited representational capacity. For example, Brahnam et al. [30] combined principal component analysis (PCA) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) with distance-based classifiers and support vector machines (SVMs) for pain state classification. However, such hand-engineered features often fail to capture complex patterns in pain expression. In recent years, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely adopted for automated feature extraction. Most deep learning applications have concentrated on infants under one year, with Celona et al. [73] using pretrained CNNs for binary pain classification and Zamzmi et al. [70, 71] developing specialized Neonatal CNNs for end-to-end assessment. While demonstrating feasibility and potential advantages over clinical standards, these methods primarily address binary classification and neglect pain intensity quantification. Notably, automated deep learning approaches remain limited for children over one year. Sikka et al. [48] established a computer vision model for pain intensity estimation in children aged 5–18 years, reporting moderate to strong correlations (r = 0.65–0.86; r = 0.47–0.61) that support algorithm effectiveness in this age group. Emerging transformer-based architectures present promising opportunities, particularly for temporal modality analysis, as evidenced by their state-of-the-art performance in other AI domains [112]. Recent studies have validated this potential in pediatric pain assessment. Jiang et al. [74] applied a Swin Transformer to facial expression recognition in critically ill children aged 1 to 18 years, achieving 88.3% accuracy on a dataset of 6951 images. Yan et al. [80] proposed a Cross-hierarchical Multi-head Sparse Vision Transformer Network (CMS-ViT) for neonatal pain assessment, attaining 93.68% accuracy for binary classification on the FENP dataset while reducing computational complexity through token sparsification.

The transition from traditional machine learning to deep learning reflects not only technical progress but also an increased capacity to handle complex, multimodal clinical data. However, this evolution introduces new challenges that currently hinder widespread clinical adoption. These include the need for large annotated datasets, greater computational demands, and reduced model interpretability. Future efforts must therefore balance algorithmic innovation with practical deployability in real-world pediatric settings.

Evaluation

This review identified two primary output modalities in automated pain detection: binary recognition (pain presence/absence) and multi-class intensity assessment. Among analyzed studies, 39.7% (n = 23) focused solely on binary classification, representing a significant clinical limitation since pain severity quantification is essential for treatment planning. Technically, binary classification presents substantially lower computational complexity than continuous intensity estimation.

A limited number of studies have explored alternative pain assessment perspectives. Zhao et al. [54] developed a Pose-invariant and Occlusion-robust Pain Assessment (POPA) framework for neonatal facial pain assessment under challenging conditions, demonstrating superior performance on a dataset of 1,091 neonates. Zhi et al. [55] achieved 89.33% accuracy while investigating demographic and viewing angle factors, identifying gestational age as the most influential variable. Zhu et al. [56] created a video-based method robust to four real-world disturbances, attaining 91.04% accuracy on a comparable neonatal dataset. Several studies [30, 33, 44, 49, 63, 65, 68, 81] addressed genuine pain detection versus similar behaviors, while others investigated gender differences [41] and multi-site pain incidence. Study [51] compared clinical versus laboratory pain assessment. The limited research exploring pain beyond conventional paradigms highlights current methodological constraints in real-world clinical applications. Emerging studies have begun to address these constraints through novel methodologies. Ferreira et al. [76] introduced a continuous pain sign visualization tool that tracks dynamic changes in neonatal facial expressions, offering interpretable temporal patterns (stable, irregular, unstable) for clinical use. Ni et al. [78] developed ScorER to identify annotation bias in video-based assessments, using heatmap analysis to pinpoint facial regions most prone to scoring discrepancies. Yan et al. [77] constructed the Neonatal Pain Speech (NPS) database, the first multi-category speech dataset for neonatal pain, comprising 461 samples across four pain-related states.

Intervention

The reviewed studies employed various robotic and AI-based interventions to alleviate pain and distress during pediatric medical procedures. Humanoid robots (NAO, MEDi™) delivered cognitive-behavioral strategies (deep breathing, interactive storytelling) and emotional support, demonstrating efficacy in pain reduction [88, 94, 95], distress alleviation [89, 96], and improved patient cooperation [93]. Intervention effectiveness varied with design: valence-adaptive robot behaviors showed limited efficacy for highly anxious children [88], while empathy-focused interactions surpassed distraction-based approaches in reducing pain and fear [95]. Technical implementation barriers included workflow disruptions [91] and device limitations [89].

AI techniques, including CNN algorithms [98] and machine learning systems [99] showed promising accuracy for pain management and decision support, though require further clinical validation. Overall, successful implementation of robotic and AI interventions depends on tailored designs, reliable technical performance, and consideration of patient-specific factors including age and baseline anxiety.

Current challenges and future directions

Current research faces several significant challenges. Studies predominantly focus on specific age groups (newborns, infants, or youth) [48, 69, 73], while clinical postoperative pain spans broader age ranges, limiting practical applicability of existing automated assessment tools. Furthermore, deep learning’s data-dependent nature requires substantial training datasets, yet public resources remain limited in scale and primarily focus on infant populations [30, 113] Most datasets are collected in controlled experimental environments, lacking real-world clinical samples, which significantly impedes development of robust algorithms.

Multidimensional evaluation using multimodal data fusion should be prioritized in subsequent investigations. Integrating physiological indicators, behavioral responses, and facial expressions promises to enhance assessment accuracy and reliability while reducing resource demands. Current technologies mainly address neonatal procedural pain, whereas evaluating postoperative and chronic pain in children aged 1–18 years remains challenging. Chronic pain recognition provides crucial diagnostic value by indicating disease progression and improving treatment efficacy. The high risk of bias identified in this review, especially in the analysis domain (67.8%), highlights several recurring methodological shortcomings that future studies should address. Missing data handling was frequently unreported, and model calibration was rarely performed, raising concerns regarding overfitting, particularly in studies with small sample sizes. Justification for sample size was often lacking, which is especially problematic for deep learning studies requiring large datasets. Although blinding of outcome or predictor assessment can be challenging in video-based studies, this limitation may be partially mitigated by using independent annotators or explicitly acknowledging this constraint. Future work would benefit from adopting PROBAST and TRIPOD checklists proactively during study design, rather than applying them retrospectively. These steps can improve the reliability and reproducibility of AI models for clinical use. Expanding algorithmic applications across diverse pain types, exploring universal and pain-specific response patterns, and validating early-warning signatures will be key next steps. Achieving these objectives requires interdisciplinary collaboration between clinicians and AI researchers to refine tools for real-world clinical use.

Furthermore, rigorous ethical and legal review is critical to ensure responsible deployment and protect the privacy and wellbeing of pediatric populations. Pediatric facial data used for pain assessment are considered sensitive biometric information, raising concerns about consent, data storage, and secondary use. Few included studies explicitly addressed whether parental consent covered algorithmic re-analysis or data sharing. Algorithmic fairness across diverse populations remains underexplored, with most data derived from a limited number of countries and gender differences rarely examined. Without systematic evaluation across race, gestational age, and socioeconomic status, deployed models may carry a risk of systematic under-assessment or over-assessment in underrepresented groups. Future research should report demographic composition transparently and test for performance disparities.

Limitation

While this review provides a comprehensive exploration of AI implementation in pediatric pain medicine, several methodological limitations warrant cautious interpretation of findings. The most important limitation is that substantial heterogeneity across populations, study designs, and AI methodologies prevented meta-analysis. Included studies applied a wide spectrum of algorithms from traditional machine learning to deep learning, and reported outcomes using inconsistent metrics including accuracy, AUC, and kappa. Such heterogeneity is exemplified by key differences between unimodal facial analysis and multimodal frameworks. Given these substantial variations, meta-analysis would be methodologically inappropriate and could produce misleading results. Nevertheless, findings consistent across multiple study designs and populations, such as the superiority of multimodal over unimodal approaches and the predominant use of facial expression analysis, can be considered more robust. By contrast, direct comparisons of absolute accuracy across algorithms should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in datasets and evaluation protocols. Additionally, the exclusion of non-English articles may introduce language bias, potentially omitting relevant studies published in other languages and limiting the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review provides the first comprehensive synthesis of AI applications in pediatric pain management. Key findings indicate that AI-based pain assessment predominantly relies on facial expression analysis, with multimodal approaches outperforming unimodal methods. Most studies focus on neonates and acute procedural pain, while research on older children and chronic pain remains limited. Given the high risk of bias across a substantial proportion of included studies, clinical readiness of these technologies remains limited. Future research should prioritize methodological rigor and validate integrated systems across diverse clinical settings through interdisciplinary collaboration.
