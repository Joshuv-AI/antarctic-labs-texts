Calcium phosphate polymer induced liquid precursor improves dentin bonding performance of primary teeth with dentinogenesis imperfecta type II
Authors: Aochen Wang, Xin Liu, Yan Wang, Xin Li, Jinghui Yang, Ruikang Tang, Zhuo Chen, Guobin Yang, Xu Chen, Zhi Chen, Guohua Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe current study aims to assess the dentin bonding performance of primary molars with dentinogenesis imperfecta type II (DGI-II). Moreover, we assess the efficacy of calcium phosphate polymer induced liquid precursor (Ca/P-PILP), a biomimetic remineralization material, in improving the resin bonding performance of DGI-II dentin.Materials and methodsDentin specimens from normal and DGI-II primary molars were treated with either Ca/P-PILP or double-distilled water. Morphology of these dentin specimens were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the calcium and phosphorus contents were determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Micro-tensile bond strength (µTBS) analysis was conducted to evaluate the resin bonding strength of dentin.ResultsSEM results showed that compared with normal dentin, DGI-II dentin exhibited significantly fewer dentinal tubules with nearly no intact tubular structures. Meanwhile, the dentin collagen fibers exhibited disorganized arrangement and compromised structural integrity. Compared with normal dentin, DGI-II dentin displayed inferior bonding performance with resin as shown by µTBS analysis, and EDS analyses showed that DGI-II dentin contained lower calcium and phosphorus contents. After treatment with Ca/P-PILP, the calcium and phosphorus contents of DGI-II dentin were significantly increased as shown by EDS analyses, and µTBS analysis showed that the bonding performance of DGI-II dentin was improved, while no notable effect was observed in normal dentin.ConclusionsPrimary molars with DGI-II display structural defects and reduced calcium and phosphorus contents in dentin. The reduced mineral contents are partially responsible for the compromised bonding performance of DGI-II dentin, for Ca/P-PILP which effectively enhances the mineral contents of DGI-II dentin improves the resin bonding performance of DGI-II dentin.Keywords: Dentinogenesis imperfecta type II, Dentine bonding, Dental adhesive, Micro-tensile bond strength, Calcium phosphate polymer-induced liquid precursor

Introduction

Dentinogenesis imperfecta (DGI) is a rare genetic disorder that is traditionally classified into DGI type I (DGI-I), DGI-II and DGI-III. Among them, DGI-II is the most prevalent, with a prevalence rate of 1/8000 ~ 1/6000 in the general population [1]. As an autosomal dominant hereditary dentin disease, DGI-II exhibits near complete penetrance and often occurs in successive generations within affected families [2, 3].

In patients with DGI-II, both primary and permanent teeth exhibit impaired dentin development, but the development of other organs is usually not affected [4]. Clinically, affected teeth present an abnormal coloration varying from yellow to brown or from gray to blue with a special opalescent, translucent shine [5, 6]. Hence, DGI-II is also known as hereditary opalescent dentin. Radiographic examination reveals pathological features including thin and short roots with narrowed or even completely obliterated pulp cavity [7]. Although the enamel structure of affected teeth may be normal, they are prone to peel off from the teeth. This is attributed to the abnormal dentin-enamel junction (DEJ) which loses its characteristic scalloped morphology and instead appears as a straight line [8, 9]. The fast stripping of enamel brings about the dentin exposure and subsequent worn out, making the teeth shorter and partial occlusion [5]. Primary teeth are fundamental for the proper growth and development of jaws and muscles in children, as well as for maintaining the appropriate place for the later eruption of permanent teeth [10, 11]. If these primary teeth are not treated in time, abnormal jaw development, malocclusion and disrupted eruption patterns of permanent teeth may occur, affecting the facial esthetics, systemic health and even overall growth of these children [12, 13]. Resin composite restoration is one of the most widely used treatment modality to repair missing dentin tissue. A previous study conducted in vitro experiments by bonding composite resin to extracted permanent teeth from a DGI-II patient. The results revealed abnormal adhesive interfaces between resin and dentin with failure to form a real hybrid layer, suggesting potentially compromised adhesive performance in the dentin of permanent teeth with DGI-II [14]. However, the bonding performance between composite resin and dentin of primary teeth with DGI-II remain unexplored. Moreover, how to efficiently optimize the bonding ability of DGI-II dentin is enigmatic.

Calcium phosphate polymer induced liquid precursor (Ca/P-PILP) is an effective biomaterial for dentin remineralization. Two biocompatible, biodegradable, non-toxic polymers, polyacrylic acid (PAA) and polyaspartic acid (PASP), are used in the synthesis of Ca/P-PILP, and they help keep Ca/P-PILP stabilized at a high Ca2+/PO43− concentration [15]. Based on the nonclassical crystallization pathway theory, Ca/P-PILP backfill dentin with amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) and noncollagenous protein (NCP) analogues, causing changes in the ultrastructure of dentin collagen and thereby increasing the mineralization level of dentin [15]. Our previous study has found that Ca/P-PILP can partially remineralize the artificial caries lesions in dentin, and significantly enhance dentin bonding strength [16]. Therefore, can Ca/P-PILP effectively improve the bonding ability of dentin in primary teeth with DGI-II through enhancing its mineralization extent?

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to compare the bonding performance of dentin from DGI-II primary teeth with dentin from normal primary teeth and to evaluate whether Ca/P-PILP, a biomimetic remineralization material, can promote the remineralization of DGI-II dentin and consequently enhance its bonding strength with resin composites.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of biomimetic remineralization solution

The Ca/P-PILP mineralization fluid was prepared as follows: 2.0 mL of 0.1 M Disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) solution was mixed with 0.4 mL of a solution containing 0.3 g/mL PAA (average Mw = 450 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 0.15 g/mL PASP (Mw = 6–8 kDa; Aike Agent, China). The mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature overnight. Then the mixed solution was successively dropped into another mixed solution containing 2.0 mL of 0.1 M Calcium chloride (CaCl2, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 0.2 mL of 0.3 g/mL PASP with vigorous stirring. Finally, the pH of the final mixed solution was adjusted to 7.4 using 3 M NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

Tooth collection and specimen preparation

The study was approved by Ethics Committee of the School and Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University, China (No. 2023B15). Eighteen exfoliated or extracted primary molars from five patients diagnosed with DGI-II were collected after the consents of patients’ parents or guardians. Eighteen exfoliated primary molars from six healthy individuals with no systemic diseases were collected and served as control teeth. These teeth were stored in 0.5% thymol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) solution at 4℃ until use. All collected teeth were used for in vitro experiments. This study did not involve any clinical trials in humans and no clinical trial was registered.

A schematic of the experimental procedure in this study was depicted in Fig. 1. In order to expose the midcoronal dentin surface, each tooth was individually cut twice perpendicular to its longitudinal axis using a water-cooled low-speed diamond saw (Isomet; Buehler, Evanston, IL, USA) and ground with 600-grit silicon carbide (SiC) sandpaper for 60 s under wet condition. Then the specimens were thoroughly rinsed with deionised water and dried using compressed air. Nine normal dentin specimens and 9 DGI-II dentin specimens were randomly selected, and then each specimen was stored in 5 mL of freshly prepared Ca/P-PILP mineralization fluid for 20 d at 37℃ and the solution was refreshed every 5 d. The other 9 normal and 9 DGI-II dentin specimens were stored in double-distilled water (ddH2O) for 20 d with the solution being refreshed every 5 d.

Schematic of the experimental process adopted in this study

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis

Three dentin specimens from each group were prepared for SEM analysis. The specimens were fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 12 h at 4℃. Subsequently, specimens were rinsed with deionized water and then successively dehydrated with increasing gradient of ethanol concentration (25%, 50%, 75%, 95% and 100%). After drying with Critical Point Dryer (Leica EM CPD300, Germany), the specimens were sputter-coated with gold and observed under SEM (Tescan Clara, Brno, Czech Republic). The morphology of the DEJ and the surface structure of dentin were observed. The elemental mappings of dentin specimens were characterized by EDS coupled with SEM, with subsequent quantitative analysis of relative calcium and phosphorus contents.

Microtensile bond strength (µTBS) test

Six coronal dentin specimens from each group were used for µTBS test. In this experiment, one-step self-etch adhesive (AdperTM Single Bond Universal, 3 M, USA) and nanocomposite resin (FiltekTM Z350 XT, 3 M, USA) were used following the manufacturer’s application guide and light-cured for 20 s using a light-curing unit (wave length = 430–480 nm, light intensity = 600 mW/cm 2, Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA). Four-mm composite was built up incrementally on the dentin surface of each specimen. After storage in ddH2O at 37℃ for 24 h, each specimen was sectioned perpendicular to the adhesive surface, to obtain 1 mm dental slabs. Then, these slabs were further sectioned vertically to produce 1 mm × 1 mm beams. The prepared beams were attached to the microtensile tester (Bisco Inc, Schaumburg, IL, USA) using the cyanoacrylate adhesive (Zapit; Dental Ventures of America, Corona, CA, USA). Load in tension was applied at 1 mm/min cross-head speed. A total of 18 beams in each group were used for µTBS tests. The µTBS was calculated as the maximum load at failure divided by the cross-sectional area, and was expressed in MPa.

Failure pattern analysis

The light microscope (LV100POL, Nikon, Japan) with 20 × magnification was used to observe the surface of fractured specimens after µTBS tests. The failure pattern was categorized into four types: (A) adhesive failure; (B) cohesive failure in dentin; (C) cohesive failure in composite; or (D) mixed failure. Specimens from each of the failure pattern were air-dried and examined under SEM. High-resolution images of the specimens were obtained for demonstration of the failure patterns.

Statistical Analyses

The data of µTBS, calcium content and phosphorus content were separately expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test in SPSS 22.0. The statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Clinical and radiological characteristics of the primary teeth with DGI-II

We diagnosed primary teeth with DGI-II through clinical and radiological examinations. For example, the intraoral photographs in Fig. 2A-C show normal primary teeth of a child at 4 years and 10 months old, which are intact with milky appearance. However, both anterior and posterior primary teeth with DGI-II from a patient at 4 years and 6 months old display severe attrition, and their exposed dentin shows an amber or dark yellow appearance (Fig. 2D-F). Radiologically, the pulp chambers and root canals of normal primary teeth are clearly seen in the panoramic radiographs (Fig. 2G), but the pulp chambers and root canals of DGI-II primary teeth are fully obliterated (Fig. 2H).

Additionally, SEM shows that the DEJ of normal primary molars exhibits a scalloped appearance with the convexity toward the dentin and the concavity toward the enamel (Fig. 2I), but the DEJ of primary molars diagnosed with DGI-II is absent of scalloping and in a nearly straight line (Fig. 2J).

The surface morphology of dentin from normal and DGI-II primary teeth with or without Ca/P-PILP treatment

To evaluate the effect of Ca/P-PILP on the surface morphology of normal and DGI-II dentin, SEM analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 3A1-A2, dentinal tubules in normal dentin are open and clearly seen with normal circular outlines. Collagen fibers inside and around the dentinal tubules of normal dentin are orderly arranged, intact and contiguous in morphology (Fig. 3A3). After remineralization for 20 d with Ca/P-PILP, collagen fibers and dentinal tubules of normal dentin show no obvious changes (Fig. 3B1-B3). However, dentinal tubules are hardly visible in DGI-II dentin. The affected dentin appears with a rugged surface. Only several large dentinal tubules can be seen, and the shapes of these tubules are irregular (Fig. 3C1- C2). Collagen fibers of DGI-II dentin are loose, and the arrangement of them is disorganized and fragmented (Fig. 3C3). Treatment of DGI-II dentin with Ca/P-PILP achieved collagen remineralization (Fig. 3D1-D3).

The effects of Ca/P-PILP treatment on the calcium and phosphorus contents of dentin from normal and DGI-II primary teeth

To observe the spatial distribution of calcium and phosphorus contents, elemental mappings were performed by EDS. The results showed that no matter with or without Ca/P-PILP treatment, on the cross-sections perpendicular to dentinal tubules, the fluorescent signals for calcium (purple dots) (Fig. 4A and B) and phosphorus (yellow dots) (Fig. 4E and F) were strong in and around the dentinal tubules but weak in other areas of normal dentin. However, the fluorescence intensity of calcium and phosphorus in DGI-II dentin was overtly at a low level (Fig. 4C and G). After Ca/P-PILP treatment, the fluorescent signals of calcium and phosphorus became stronger but were distributed evenly on the surface of DGI-II dentin (Fig. 4D and H). The relative contents of calcium and phosphorus in a representative sample of each group by EDS are shown in Fig. 4I and L.

In normal dentin treated with ddH2O, the atomic percentages of calcium and phosphorus were 21.45 ± 1.75% and 8.44 ± 0.70%, respectively. For those treated with Ca/P-PILP, the atomic percentages of calcium and phosphorus were 21.48 ± 1.59% and 8.79 ± 0.65%, respectively, which was not significantly changed compared with the ddH2O group. However, in the DGI-II dentin treated with ddH2O, the atomic percentages of calcium and phosphorus were only 5.82 ± 1.04% and 3.24 ± 0.66%, respectively, which were apparently lower than normal dentin. However, Ca/P-PILP treatment significantly increased the contents of calcium and phosphorus in DGI-II dentin, with the atomic percentage of calcium and phosphorus as 15.52 ± 0.98% and 5.73 ± 0.72%, respectively (Fig. 4M and N).

Bonding strength and failure mode analysis of dentin from normal and DGI-II primary teeth

µTBS tests were performed to evaluate the effect of Ca/P-PILP on the bonding strength of normal and DGI-II dentin. All the data were in accordance with normal distribution and homogeneity of the variance test, and the mean values of µTBS tests for four groups are presented in Fig. 5A. For specimens treated with ddH2O (the control groups), DGI-II dentin (20.38 ± 2.81 MPa) showed lower bonding strength than normal dentin (29.19 ± 3.18 MPa). For DGI-II dentin treated with Ca/P-PILP, a significant increase of the bonding strength (24.72 ± 3.27 MPa) was observed, although it remained lower than that of normal dentin. Moreover, no significant differences were detected between normal dentin treated with Ca/P-PILP (30.89 ± 3.02 MPa) and normal dentin treated with ddH2O.

For normal dentin with and without Ca/P-PILP treatment as well as DGI-II dentin with Ca/P-PILP treatment, mixed failure was the most prevalent failure mode (Normal + ddH2O: 44.44%, Normal + Ca/P-PILP: 55.56%, DGI-II + Ca/P-PILP: 50.00%), followed by adhesive failure (Normal + ddH2O: 33.33%, Normal + Ca/P-PILP: 33.33%, DGI-II + Ca/P-PILP: 33.33%). Discussion

DGI-II is a rare autosomal dominant hereditary disorder with abnormal dentin formation and mineralization [17]. As reported in previous studies, we also observed distinct gaps between enamel and dentin in the teeth affected by DGI-II, along with the loss of characteristic scalloped morphology at the DEJ [8, 9]. These structural defects are considered the primary cause of enamel delamination. Additionally, due to poor resistance of the exposed dentin to masticatory forces, DGI-II dentin often undergoes rapid attrition, which may affect the normal development of the jaw bones and even compromise overall health [18, 19]. Therefore, exploration and improvement of the bonding performance of DGI-II dentin are of important implications. A previous study has proposed that sandblasting to enhance dentin micro-roughness may improve bonding performance in DGI-II-affected teeth [20]. However, this has not been experimentally validated. To the best of our knowledge, strategies for improving the adhesive performance of DGI-II dentin remain unexplored.

Previous studies have revealed that changes in the microstructure affect mechanical properties and bonding performance of dentin [14, 21]. We found that the dentinal tubules of normal dentin were clearly visible, and exhibited quite regular round shape by SEM. Meanwhile, dentin collagen fibers were regularly arranged with intact morphological structures. However, the dentinal tubules of DGI-II dentin were distributed disorderly and sparsely. Mao et al.. found that the diameter of DGI-II dentinal tubules was smaller than that of normal dentinal shown by atomic force microscopy [22], but we found that the diameter of the dentinal tubules in DGI-II teeth appeared larger than that of normal teeth, with their collagen fibers disorganized and even fragmented. These structural alterations may compromise dentin bonding.

Several studies have revealed that the mineralization extent of dentin can affect its adhesive properties [23, 24]. Compared with normal dentin, the bonding strength and stability of demineralized dentin to resin-based composite are significantly decreased [25]. Dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) plays a crucial role in dentin formation and mineralization [26, 27], and its genetic mutations lead to impaired dentin mineralization in DGI-II patients [28, 29]. The dentin of normal permanent teeth consists of approximately 70 wt% mineral content (40–45 vol%), 20 wt% organic components (30 vol%), and 10 wt% water (20–25 vol%) [30]. However, in the dentin of permanent teeth with DGI-II, the mineral content is decreased while the organic and water contents are increased, causing easier dentin abrasion [31]. Additionally, Park et al.. found that DGI-II dentin had a reduced mineral density than normal dentin [32]. Both calcium and phosphorus are essential for the mineralization process of dentin, which have been used in several studies to assess the level of dentin mineralization [33–35]. In this study, EDS elemental mapping showed that the fluorescence of calcium and phosphorus were apparently concentrated in and around dentinal tubules of normal dentin, suggesting the degree of mineralization of peritubular dentin is higher than that of inter-tubular dentin, which may provide a favorable environment for resin composite bonding. However, the intensities of the fluorescence for calcium and phosphorus were very weak in DGI-II dentin, and the calcium and phosphorus contents of DGI-II dentin were also much lower than those of normal dentin in EDS spot spectrum analysis. It is well known that hypo-mineralized dentin exhibits significantly compromised bonding performance compared to normal dentin [23, 24]. Consistently, we also found that the bonding performance of DGI-II dentin was significantly worse than normal dentin in µTBS tests. Failure mode analysis indicated that DGI-II group had more cohesive failure in dentin, suggesting that the reduction of mineral substances in DGI-II dentin may affect its mechanical properties. Based on these results, we inferred that reduced number of dentinal tubules combined with structural abnormalities as well as the decrease in mineral contents may explain the deterioration of the bonding performance of DGI-II dentin.

The recovery of dentin tissue is a complex process that involves not only the remineralization of demineralized dentin but also the recovery of the collagen matrix structure [36, 37]. Biomineralization utilizes biomimetic analogs of dentin matrix proteins to induce the formation of ACP nano-precursors within the intrafibrillar compartments of collagen fibrils [15]. This strategy relies on the generation of nanocrystals small enough to penetrate the gap zones between adjacent collagen molecules. Therefore, biomimetic remineralization of dentin has been shown to effectively restore both the mechanical properties and bonding performance of demineralized dentin [23, 38, 39]. However, whether biomimetic remineralization material can also promote the remineralization of poorly mineralized DGI-II dentin and improve its bonding performance was unknown and worth exploring. Ca/P-PILP is an effective remineralization material based on collagen fiber mineralization, promoting the deposition of mineral crystals within collagen fibers when exposed to a high concentration calcium phosphate solution [40]. Ca/P-PILP has been shown to have excellent bone inductivity in osteoporotic bone, which uniquely allows the intrafibrillar mineralization of collagen fibrils [15]. Meanwhile, our previous study found Ca/P-PILP induced intrafibrillar and extrafibrillar biomimetic remineralization of demineralized dentin collagen, and partially remineralized artificial caries dentin lesion. The bonding interfacial integrity of remineralized artificial caries dentin lesion was improved and the bonding strength was significantly increased [16]. In another study, the remineralization effect of Ca/P-PILP enhances the structural stability of dentin and improves the anti-enzymatic properties, which may be of great benefit to the clinic of resin–dentin bonding and dentin hypersensitivity [41]. Therefore, the Ca/P-PILP-mediated biomimetic remineralization strategy was used to enhance resin bonding performance in hypo-mineralized DGI-II dentin.

EDS analysis confirmed the biomimetic mineralization effect of Ca/P-PILP on DGI-II dentin, as demonstrated by significantly elevated calcium and phosphorus contents compared to untreated DGI-II dentin. In addition, the collagen fibrils were refilled with minerals. Collagen fibers are an essential component of dentin and a key factor influencing bonding performance. Although Ca/P-PILP can enhance the mineralization level of DGI-II dentin, it cannot restore the structurally compromised collagen fibers of DGI-II dentin to a fully healthy state. µTBS tests showed that while the bonding strength of remineralized DGI-II dentin remained lower than that of the normal dentin, it showed a substantial improvement compared to untreated DGI-II dentin controls. However, the bonding performance of normal dentin after Ca/P-PILP treatment did not change, which may be related to the unaltered mineralization of normal dentin. Based on these findings, we demonstrate for the first time that Ca/P-PILP, a biomimetic remineralization strategy, can effectively enhance the bonding performance of dentin from DGI-II primary teeth by improving its mineralization status. However, given the limited availability of primary teeth diagnosed with DGI-II, the current investigation focused exclusively on assessing immediate dentin bonding characteristics. In the future, we will evaluate the long-term bonding durability conferred by the Ca/P-PILP strategy and to employ micro-Raman spectroscopy for a detailed analysis of the resin-dentin interfacial microstructure. Furthermore, the clinical translation of Ca/P-PILP requires optimization to enhance procedural efficiency and reduce treatment duration.

Conclusion

In this study, we identify structural defects and reduced mineral content of DGI-II dentin which may be critical factors affecting the bonding performance of DGI-II dentin. We find that a biomimetic remineralization material, Ca/P-PILP, can remineralize DGI-II dentin leading to a significant increase in its calcium and phosphorus contents and bonding strength. Although the bonding strength is still inferior to normal dentin, it is significantly improved compared with untreated DGI-II dentin. Therefore, this study sheds light on the potential application of Ca/P-PILP in improving the prognosis of resin-based restorations for DGI-II dentin, providing an experimental foundation for dental restoration in such patients.
