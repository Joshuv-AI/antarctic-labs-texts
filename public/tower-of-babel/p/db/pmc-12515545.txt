Mediastinal Cystic Teratoma in a Patient From Papua: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Andika Chandra Putra, Bernadina Chyntia Carsantiningrum, Renaningtyas Tambun, Liem Arinuryanto Lios, Wiwien Heru Wiyono, Mohamad Fahmi Alatas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMediastinal teratomas are rare benign germ cell tumours that develop between the lungs, representing approximately 1%–5% of all mediastinal tumours. This case report presents a 41‐year‐old male patient from Papua with a history of chronic cough for 3 months and fatigue for about a month. Physical examination revealed decreased breath sounds in the right upper lung field, while laboratory tests were normal. A chest CT scan identified a benign cystic mass in the right anterior inferior mediastinum. Trans‐thoracic CT‐guided fine‐needle aspiration with ROSE cytology suggested an infected cyst content that may be part of a cystic teratoma or a bronchogenic cyst. Separately, a transbronchial lung biopsy revealed histology consistent with chronic inflammation. The patient declined intervention and returned to Papua. This case highlights the challenges in managing patients who decline standard treatment, particularly those from remote areas, and emphasises the importance of patient education regarding the potential complications of untreated mediastinal teratomas.

Introduction

Mediastinal teratomas are rare benign germ cell tumours that develop in the space between the lungs. These tumours arise from pluripotent stem cells that fail to migrate from the yolk endoderm to the gonads during embryonic development [1, 2]. Teratomas contain elements from all three embryological layers: endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm [2, 3]. Mediastinal mature cystic teratomas represent approximately 1%–5% of all mediastinal tumours, making them an uncommon clinical entity [2]. They predominantly occur in the anterior mediastinum and are considered the most common extragonadal germ cell tumours.

These slow‐growing tumours are typically benign and may remain asymptomatic for extended periods. However, as they enlarge, they can affect neighbouring anatomical structures, leading to chest pain, persistent cough, dyspnea, and hemoptysis. The diagnosis of mediastinal teratomas poses challenges due to their nonspecific clinical and radiological findings. However, a definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination following surgical excision. Early recognition and appropriate surgical intervention are crucial for optimal outcomes.

This case report presents a mediastinal mature cystic teratoma, highlighting its clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, and management strategies to enhance awareness among clinicians.

Case Report

A 41‐year‐old male patient presented with a history of chronic cough for 3 months and fatigue for about a month. Dyspnea worsened with activity, with no history of fever, chest pain, or hemoptysis. He used to smoke approximately 20–30 cigarettes per day, but had stopped since 2013. He had a history of diabetes and hypertension. His medical and family histories were insignificant for cardiovascular or pulmonary diseases or previous operations.

The patient was admitted to the hospital for further diagnostic workup and observation. Upon his arrival at the hospital, the patient was in good general condition with no signs of difficulty breathing. Vital signs were stable: blood pressure of 120/90 mmHg, heart rate of 102 beats/min, and respiratory rate of 30 breaths/min. Physical examination were normal; there were no rhonchi or wheezing. There was no palpable lumps in the cervical or thoracic region. Laboratory tests showed normal levels of haemoglobin (10.20 g/dL), white blood cells (8.340/mm3), and platelets (235/mm3). The liver function test was normal. Ureum was 15, and creatinine was 0.9. LDH was 149. Tumour markers carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) 4.09, alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) 4.09, and β‐human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) were within normal ranges (< 1.2). A Genexpert sputum examination showed that Mycobacterium tuberculosis had not been detected. A chest CT scan (Figure 1) showed a benign cystic mass in the right anterior inferior mediastinum, measuring approximately 10.5 × 5.3 × 5.3 cm. CT‐guided transthoracic fine‐needle aspiration (Figure 2) with ROSE cytology suggested an infected cyst content that may be part of a cystic teratoma or a bronchogenic cyst. Separately, a transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) revealed histology consistent with chronic inflammation. (Figure 3).

Discussion

Mediastinal teratomas are rare germ cell tumours originating from pluripotent embryonic cells, differentiating into tissues representing all three germ cell layers [3]. The tumours are predominantly diagnosed during the second and third decades of life, with an equal distribution between males and females [1, 2]. In our case, the patient was 41 years old, slightly older than the typical age of presentation.

The clinical presentation of mediastinal teratomas varies widely. More than 60% of patients with mediastinal masses are asymptomatic, with the lesions discovered incidentally during imaging for other medical purposes [3]. When symptomatic, patients typically present with chest pain, cough, dyspnea, and occasionally hemoptysis due to compression of surrounding structures [3, 4]. Our patient presented with chronic cough and fatigue, which aligns with the typical symptomatology of mediastinal teratomas.

Chest radiographs are typically the initial imaging modality. Still, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the preferred methods for detailed characterisation [4]. In our case, CT imaging revealed a benign cystic mass in the right anterior inferior mediastinum measuring approximately 10.5 × 5.3 × 5.3 cm, consistent with the typical radiological presentation of a mediastinal teratoma. Laboratory evaluation, including tumour markers such as alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) and beta‐human chorionic gonadotropin (β‐HCG), is essential to diagnosing and following up mediastinal germ cell tumours. These markers help distinguish between mature teratomas and malignant germ cell tumours, as patients with benign teratomas typically do not show elevated tumour markers [1]. In our patient, all tumour markers were within normal ranges, supporting the diagnosis of a mature teratoma rather than a malignant germ cell tumour. In our case, histology revealed findings consistent with chronic inflammation. CT‐guided transthoracic fine‐needle aspiration with ROSE cytology suggested an infected cyst content (abscess) that may be part of a cystic teratoma (mature) or a bronchogenic cyst.

Complete surgical resection is the standard treatment for mediastinal mature teratomas [1, 4]. This approach provides a definitive diagnosis and prevents potential complications such as rupture, infection, or compression of vital structures. Video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) is increasingly preferred over open thoracotomy due to its minimally invasive nature and associated benefits.

The patient's choice to reject surgical care and return to Papua underscores a significant connection between clinical medicine and socio‐economic reality. Although his particular reasons were unclear, many challenges among patients from remote areas of Indonesia likely had a role. The impact of health beliefs, culture, finances, and geography cannot be ignored. Staying in Jakarta for surgery would cost a lot of money for extended stays, income loss, family logistics, and medical bills. Some people may be scared of the suggested treatment since there may be a gap between contemporary surgical practices and traditional health beliefs. Additionally, being away from their regular surroundings and family support could make them anxious, so a return home is the most reassuring choice. This case shows that to give the best care to patients, we need to understand all of the non‐clinical barriers that can impact the ability to support and counsel patients from different backgrounds.

It is essential to develop good strategies for people who don't want to get treatment right away. Patient education must be clear, kind, and delivered in a way that is appropriate for their culture. The information should clearly explain the disease's natural history, including the danger of gradual enlargement, rupture leading to potentially life‐threatening mediastinitis or pericarditis, infection of the cyst, and the rare but serious risk of malignant transformation [1, 4]. For patients returning to rural places, follow‐up cannot be done as usual. We need to put valuable and possible strategies: (1) coordinated follow‐up: setting up a formal referral and communication channel with the nearest provincial hospital or primary healthcare centre (PUSKESMAS) in the patient's home area for monitoring with chest radiographs. (2) Using technology: Utilising telemedicine consultations to maintain a direct line of communication. (3) Culturally competent communication: Providing education materials in simple Bahasa Indonesia and educational visual aids (e.g., diagrams of the mass) to overcome language and literacy barriers. (4) Safety netting: Establishing a clear emergency plan detailing which symptoms (e.g., acute chest pain, fever, severe dyspnea) should prompt immediate return to care.

In conclusion, mediastinal cystic teratomas are an uncommon but clinically significant condition. This case highlights the challenges in managing patients who decline surgical intervention, especially those from remote areas with limited healthcare access. In conclusion, this case contributes to the existing literature on mediastinal cystic teratomas by highlighting the challenges in managing patients who decline surgical intervention. Beyond the technical aspects, this report emphasises the critical need for a patient‐centred approach that acknowledges and addresses profound socio‐economic and geographic barriers. Future efforts must create structured, culturally competent education protocols and robust telemedicine‐supported follow‐up systems to improve outcomes. This case illustrates the necessity of effective medicine, which requires adapting clinical expertise to the practical realities of patients' lives.
