Epitranscriptomic reprogramming in response to low CO2 stress and m6A engineering to enhance biomass production in Nannochloropsis oceanica
Authors: Han Zhu, Yikai Wu, Meng Feng, Lingyu Ouyang, Hang Su, Li Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYN6‐adenine methylation (m6A) as an epitranscriptomic mark is the most abundant modification in eukaryotic RNA and plays a dynamically regulated role. However, m6A dynamics, deposition and engineering in microalgae remain largely unknown. Here, in Nannochloropsis oceanica, the dynamic alterations and reprogramming in m6A RNA modifications after the shift from high to low CO2 conditions were first investigated using methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing. The m6A peaks in N. oceanica were mainly enriched in 3′UTR. A positive association between m6A abundance and mRNA transcription of CO2‐responsive genes was observed; moreover, N. oceanica cells adopted versatile strategies in a dynamic reprogramming of m6A in response to low CO2 stress. Secondly, knockout of two putative m6A methylases including NoMTA (NO04G02990) and NoMTB (NO07G02450) by genome editing induced methylation reprogramming, which was associated with expression changes of low‐CO2 responsive genes such as carbon/nitrogen metabolism, and photorespiration genes that underlie reductions in growth and biomass. Lastly, m6A modification reprogramming was first engineered to increase low‐CO2 stress tolerance and biomass productivity by the CRISPR/dCas13 system combined with MTA and NoMTB under low CO2 in N. oceanica. Therefore, these strides would pave the way for microalgal epigenetics and future industrial applications.

INTRODUCTION

Nannochloropsis oceanica, a prominent microalga in marine biotechnology, has gained significant attention for its potential in carbon sequestration and industrial production (Chua & Schenk, 2017; Ferreira et al., 2013; Moody et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2022). The adaptive mechanisms of this alga to environmental stresses, such as varying CO2 concentrations, are critical for optimizing its cultivation and enhancing its industrial applications (Razzak et al., 2015). The physiological and transcriptional responses of N. oceanica to varying CO2 concentrations are pivotal in understanding its ability to adapt to changing environmental conditions. The limitation of CO2 is known to decrease the consumption of ATP and NADPH by the Calvin‐Benson cycle, resulting in an overreduced photosynthetic electron transport chain, which may subsequently induce oxidative stress (Fang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015; Zavřel et al., 2018). Therefore, the majority of microalgae species have evolved a highly efficient carbon‐concentrating mechanism (CCM) system to improve the CO2 level around the ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase enzyme (Gee & Niyogi, 2017; Wei et al., 2017). On a molecular level, varying CO2 concentrations trigger significant changes in the transcriptome of Nannochloropsis (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). Genes related to carbon‐responsive genes (bicarbonate transporters and carbonic anhydrases, etc.) are upregulated in response to low CO2 conditions, facilitating the efficient uptake and utilization of available carbon. Although modulation of nuclear gene expression is evident in response to carbon dioxide depletion (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019), information on the molecular mechanisms underlying these epitranscriptomic changes is limited.

Epitranscriptomics, particularly the study of N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modifications, plays a vital role in the regulation of transcription and abiotic stress responses (Hu et al., 2022; Moshitch‐Moshkovitz et al., 2022). The dynamic reinvention of m6A modifications allows cells to rapidly remodel their gene transcription in response to abiotic or biotic stresses (Sharma et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023). In the epitranscriptomic machinery, m6A is predominantly enriched in the regions around stop codons, within exons, and in untranslated regions (UTRs) of the mRNA. A methyltransferase complex is responsible for the installation of m6A, commonly referred to as “writers”, which includes key components such as methyltransferase‐like (METTL3 and METTL14). Conversely, the removal of m6A involves demethylases like FTO and ALKBH5, termed “erasers,” while “readers” such as YTHDF proteins recognize and bind to m6A sites to mediate their effects (Tang et al., 2023). “Writers” refer to the enzyme complexes that catalyze the addition of m6A marks on RNA molecules. These complexes are responsible for the selective methylation of adenosine residues, which can influence the fate of RNA by affecting its processing, nuclear export, translation, and decay. In plants, m6A has been extensively studied for its dynamic and reversible nature, influencing various aspects of RNA metabolism and function. However, the knowledge of m6A modification is limited to microalgae.

CRISPR/dCas13 technology, derived from the CRISPR/Cas13 system initially designed for RNA targeting, has become an increasingly powerful tool for epigenome editing (Kim et al., 2024). Unlike the traditional CRISPR/Cas9 system, which induces double‐strand breaks (DSBs) to edit genes, dCas13 (dead Cas13) lacks nuclease activity. It allows for the precise regulation of gene expression without modifying the underlying DNA/RNA sequence. By utilizing RNA‐guided specificity, dCas13 can target particular genomic regions, offering a flexible platform for investigating and modulating the epigenome (Wilson et al., 2020). The dCas13‐based epigenome editing approach hinges on its ability to modify gene expression via epigenetic alterations. In plants, dCas13‐mediated epigenome editing has similarly shown potential for regulating key genes involved in growth, stress responses, and productivity (Shi et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024). However, it has not been established in microalgae, hindering the research and engineered improvement of microalgal epigenetics.

Here, we have conducted a comprehensive analysis of the epitranscriptome of N. oceanica after the shift from high CO2 to low CO2. By employing MeRIP‐seq, we have mapped the distribution of m6A modifications across different CO2 concentrations and analyzed their potential regulatory roles in metabolic adaptation. Additionally, through targeted gene editing of the m6A methyltransferase complex, commonly known as the m6A “writer,” we have further explored the direct impacts of this modification on the physiological and metabolic traits of the algae under low CO2. Lastly, we engineered m6A modification by dCas13‐mediated epigenome editing in N. oceanica, which is the first to achieve gene editing for improving growth at the epigenetic level in microalgae. Taken together, these strides would pave the way for microalgal epigenetics and future industrial applications.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

mRNA m6A modification is present and abundance in N. oceanica

To explore the involvement of m6A methylation in the CO2 oscillation response of N. oceanica, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry was utilized to characterize the m6A levels under dynamic CO2 stress conditions. The CO2 treatments were performed as described in our previous study (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019; Wei, Shen, et al., 2019) (Figure S1a). RNA samples were isolated from N. oceanica cells at different time points after the shift from high CO2 (defined as w0) to low CO2 (at 12, 24, and 48 h; defined as w12, w24 and w48). These samples were used to conduct the experiment for physiological and omics analysis. A light increase in the distribution of m6A was revealed under varying CO2 stress conditions or at different stages of low CO2 stress with the methylation levels of N6‐methyladenine (m6A/A) showing notable changes in total RNA of N. oceanica (Figure S1b). Thus, the m6A level would be potentially affected in response to CO2 stress.

To interrogate m6A methylation abundance of N. oceanica, the m6A‐targeted antibody was used and a transcriptome‐wide m6A map was constructed to profile the m6A modifications by the MeRIP‐seq (m6A immunoprecipitation followed by RNA sequencing) with two biological replicates in N. oceanica, which showed good repeatability (Figure S2). Totally, a total of 97.56G reads were produced and there were almost 40–50 million reads per library (including input and IP libraries) obtained with Q30% above 92.55 (Table S1). After quality control of raw data, those clean reads were uniquely mapped into the N. oceanica reference genome (http://nandesyn.single‐cell.cn/) (Gong et al., 2020). Impressively, the results indicated that 92.88–95.66% of the clean reads were successfully mapped to a specific position in the N. oceanica genome, demonstrating good mapping efficiency.

The m6A peaks were identified between the IP and control samples using the MeTDiff software package (Cui et al., 2018). Totally, we detected 14 013, 15 864, 15 451 and 15 355 m6A enriched peaks for w0, w12, w24 and w48 in N. oceanica (Figure 1a), which have an average length of 321–337 bp and a median length of 250 bp. There were 8460 core peaks shared by w0, w12, w24 and w48 (Figure 1b). Based on these findings, we observed that the N. oceanica epitranscriptome contains 0.2–0.3 m6A peaks per 1000 nucleotides, a range comparable to that found in mammals and higher plants, such as rice and Arabidopsis (Wang et al., 2024), cotton (He et al., 2024), and maize (Miao et al., 2020). These results further highlight the abundant presence of m6A modification, which is associated with CO2 fluctuation in N. oceanica. The MeRIP‐seq results were further confirmed through independent m6A immunoprecipitation, followed by quantitative PCR analysis (Figure S3; Table S2). Next, the m6A peaks distribution was investigated based on gene annotations. The frequency of m6A peak reads reaches its highest level in the 3′UTRs and 5′UTRs (Figure 1c; Figure S4). In Arabidopsis (Wang et al., 2024), cotton (He et al., 2024), Malus domestica (Xu et al., 2023) and Chlamydomonas (Lv et al., 2023), the level of m6A peaks is predominantly present in the 3′UTR.

Earlier research has indicated that the distribution of m6A peaks across various mRNA regions is dynamic and can shift towards the 5′UTR to respond to stress. (Mao et al., 2019; Perlegos et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025). To compare the dynamics of m6A peaks in response to low CO2, we executed the absolute analysis of m6A peaks located in exons, UTRs, start codons, and stop codons at different timepoints in N. oceanica. Overall, the CDS region shows the highest proportion of m6A throughout all time points, accounting for approximately 50–51% of total modifications. The 3′UTR region remains the second largest proportion, contributing around 18–20%, while the 5′UTR accounts for about 16–17% (Figure 1d). Notably, m6A levels around the start and stop codons show a transient increase from w0 (6.44 and 6.16%) to w24 (6.7 and 7.33%), followed by a slight decrease at w48 (6.35 and 6.73%), indicating dynamic but moderate modulation during the time course (Figure 1d). As shown in Figure 1(e), the overall m6A levels remain largely unchanged over time. These subtle shifts suggest a fine‐tuned redistribution of m6A marks during the progression, rather than drastic regional changes in N. oceanica in response to low CO2 stress. To identify the consensus sequence containing m6A peaks in the epitranscriptome, we utilized HOMER to perform a motif search. Motifs such as RRACH and DRAC were detected in N. oceanica (Figure 1f). Notably, the DRAC motif, a conserved m6A motif, has been identified in both Arabidopsis and Chlamydomonas. These top three motifs also change at different time points. (Figure S5), which suggests that m6A might be dynamic under low CO2 stress in N. oceanica.

m6A methylation is dynamic in response to low CO2 stress in N. oceanica mRNA

To interrogate the change of m6A methylation in N. oceanica response to CO2 stress, we performed a comparative analysis of differential m6A peaks. By comparative analysis, there were 1059, 912 and 866 m6A differential methylated peaks (DMPs; Fold change >1.5, P < 0.05) identified under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0 and w24 versus w0, respectively. These DMPs included 855, 640 and 571 hypermethylated peaks and 202, 272 and 295 hypomethylated peaks under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0 and w24 versus w0, respectively (Figure 2a). However, the enrichment level of DMPs showed no changes (Figure 2b). These results demonstrated that the upregulated peaks were far more than the downregulated peaks, which revealed a significant increase under low CO2 stress in N. oceanica. Further, this might imply that the differential m6A methylome is associated with gene expression under low CO2 conditions in N. oceanica. The Venn diagram displayed that there were 82 differentially methylated m6A peaks (Dataset S1) shared by w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0 and w48 versus w0 and there were 706, 554 and 551 unique DMPs (Figure 2c). In drought stress, the dynamic profile of m6A was also observed in Populus trichocarpa (Gao et al., 2022). The large‐scale shifts in m6A modification in response to low‐temperature stress were found in Arabidopsis (Govindan et al., 2022). Here, m6A was also regulated in response to low CO2 in microalgae, which implies that m6A reprogramming would occur to cope with different stresses for metabolic acclimation.

To assess whether these genes with DMPs participated in low CO2 stress, we further performed KEGG enrichment analysis of those genes containing DMPs based on functional analysis at various time points (Figure S6). Under w12 versus w0, these transcripts with DMPs mainly enriched in pathways such as pentose and glucuronate interconversion, galactose metabolism, and ABC transporter (Figure 2d). Under w24 versus w0, one carbon metabolism related to folate, photosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, and glycerolipid metabolism pathways were enriched (Figure 2e). Under w48 versus w0, these transcripts with DMPs mainly enriched in pathways such as central carbon metabolism, N‐glycan biosynthesis, and one carbon metabolism by folate (Figure 2f). These transcripts with 82 differentially methylated m6A peaks mainly enriched in photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid metabolism (Figure S7). Notably, in our previous studies, those genes related to one carbon metabolism by folate, ABC transporter and photosynthesis showed differential regulation at the transcriptional level in N. oceanica (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). These results demonstrated that m6A methylation is dynamic and might be associated with low CO2 responsive gene expression in N. oceanica.

Specifically, in Nannochloropsis, the versatile CCMs involved in the biophysical and biochemical cycle have been proposed (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). As for biophysical CCM, among five carbonic anhydrases (CAs), there were three CAs present m6A modification (Figure S8a). Notably, the m6A peaks were present at the region of the exon of CA5 (NO20G00630; α‐type; with type II signal peptide) with 2–3 fold enrichment under w0, w12, w24 and w48 and its m6A peak also displayed a dynamic alternation (Figure S8b). The essential role of CA5 is further substantiated by the recent identification of CAH1, a homolog of CA5 from N. oceanica CCMP1779, which has been recognized as a critical component of CCM and is specifically targeted to the ER lumen. (Gee & Niyogi, 2017). This implies that m6A may play a crucial role in biophysical CCM in N. oceanica. As for biochemical CCM, there were some genes modified by m6A including phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase kinase (PEPCK: NO07G03330), pyruvate orthophosphate dikinase (PPDK: NO26G01010 and NO12G00860) and NADP‐dependent malic enzyme (NADP‐ME: NO06G00510) (Figure S8a). PEPCK catalyzes the conversion from oxaloacetate (OAA) to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) with 2.5–3.2 fold enrichment of m6A. PPDK is involved in converting pyruvate into PEP with 2.8–4.5 fold enrichment of m6A under w0, w12, w24 and w48 (Figure S8b). In addition, because photorespiration metabolism was enhanced by low CO2 in our previous study (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019), its m6A was also concerned. Indeed, some genes responsible for photorespiration showed an m6A modification including glycolate oxidase (GOX, NO02G03110), glycerate kinase (GK, NO01G03100), glycine decarboxylase complex proteins (GDC, NO08G02840 and NO04G04760), alanine aminotransferase (AT, NO29G00680), and serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT, NO20G01610) (Figure S8), which is associated with the photosynthetic C1 pathway (Jardine et al., 2024). Thus, low CO2 responsive genes were modified by m6A in N. oceanica and they also provided potential targets for artificially manipulating m6A level.

Association between m6A epitranscriptome and transcription of CO2 stress‐related genes

The m6A modification in RNA has been shown to regulate transcript levels either positively or negatively, depending on the development or environment. In Arabidopsis, the regulation of m6A levels at specific transcript sites plays a crucial role in the transcriptional activation of defense genes and the response to pathogens (Furci et al., 2024). In tomato, m6A methylation has been found to be negatively correlated with mRNA abundance during the ripening process (Zhou et al., 2019). The m6A modifications and gene expression levels exhibit an inverse correlation during Kiwifruit ripening (Su et al., 2024). In cotton, the association between m6A modification and gene transcription is intricate and multifaceted (He et al., 2024). In Chlamydomonas, m6A modification is negatively correlated with gene expression during sexual reproduction (Lv et al., 2023). To assess the impact of m6A methylation differences on CO2 responsive gene expression in N. oceanica, in parallel, we conducted RNA‐seq analysis (Table S3; Figure S9) on the same samples used for MeRIP‐seq and performed an association analysis (Figure 3a). Firstly, differentially expressed genes were analyzed under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0 and there were 2532, 2362, and 2292 upregulated and 2427, 2308 and 2385 downregulated genes, respectively (Figure 3b), this result was further demonstrated by qPCR (Figure S10). The Venn diagram showed that there were core 2520 differentially expressed genes shared by w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0 (Figure 3c; Dataset S2). By KEGG enrichment analysis, differentially expressed genes were mainly enriched in the top 20 pathways related to photosynthesis, proteasome, N‐glycan biosynthesis etc. under w12 versus w0 (Figure 3d). As regarded to w24 versus w0, the top 20 pathways including carbon fixation, amino acid metabolism involved in glycine, serine and threonine, and sucrose metabolism etc. were enriched (Figure 3e). Under w48 versus w0, the top 20 pathways including photosynthesis, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, and pentose metabolism etc. were enriched (Figure 3f). These core 2520 differentially expressed genes were mainly in photosynthesis, carbon fixation and carbohydrate metabolism pathways (Figure S11). Notably, some low‐CO2 responsive genes displayed differential regulations.

Secondly, the differentially expressed genes with transcripts exhibiting differential m6A methylation were compared, which revealed that 833, 672, and 645 transcripts with m6A hypomethylation or hypermethylation in N. oceanica at w12, w24, and w48 compared to w0 exhibited lower and higher expression, respectively. We categorized these genes into two groups: m6A methylation and non‐m6A methylation in N. oceanica. Whether it was high CO2 (w0) or low CO2 stress (w12, w24 and w48), the mRNA levels of genes containing m6A modifications were significantly higher compared to those lacking m6A modifications (Figure 4a–d), which suggests an association between m6A modification and mRNA abundance. Notably, under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0, there were 442, 329, and 270 upregulated genes with m6A hypermethylation (defined as hyper‐up) and 101, 119, and 165 downregulated genes with m6A hypomethylation (defined as hypo‐down), respectively (Figure 4e–g).

Lastly, accumulating evidence had confirmed that m6A deposition affects mRNA abundance. To further estimate the potential relationship between m6A methylation and mRNA abundance in response to CO2 stress in N. oceanica, accordingly, among these transcripts, there were 193, 250, and 276 transcripts showing hypomethylated m6A peaks in N. oceanica under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0, 733, 568, and 514 transcripts showed hypermethylated m6A peaks. For those hypermethylated m6A peaks, ~53–60% transcripts displayed the upregulation. However, for those hypomethylated m6A peaks, ~48–60% transcripts showed the downregulation. We then expanded this analysis to the entire transcriptome, focusing on all m6A‐modified genes. These genes were categorized into three groups: non‐differential, hypermethylated, and hypomethylated under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0 (Figure 4h–j). Indeed, genes bearing hypermethylated m6A peaks predominantly exhibited higher expression, while genes with hypomethylated m6A peaks predominantly exhibited lower expression in N. oceanica under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0 (Figure 4h–j). This above pattern was not observed in genes modified with non‐differential m6A. Further, taking into account the distribution patterns of hypermethylated m6A peaks potentially affected gene transcription, we analyzed gene expression level based on their m6A regions in 5′UTR, 3′UTR, start codon, exon, and stop codon. The results demonstrated that genes bearing m6A peaks located in exon and 5′UTR displayed higher expression, while genes with m6A peaks located in stop codon displayed lower expression in N. oceanica under w12 versus w0, w24 versus w0, and w48 versus w0 (Figure 4k–m). In addition, we found that hypermethylated peaks were enriched in 5′UTR. m6A deposition in the CDS region generally promotes gene transcription, whereas m6A modification near the stop codon or 3′UTR is typically negatively correlated with transcript levels (Figure 4k–m). This result is consistent with that in tomato (Zhou et al., 2019). These results indicate a general positive correlation between m6A modification and transcript levels under CO2 stress in N. oceanica, underscoring the complex and multifaceted influence of m6A modification on gene expression. However, a negative correlation between m6A modification and mRNA abundance was observed in Chlamydomonas (Lv et al., 2023). The relatively high gene density and lower levels of 5mC methylation in Nannochloropsis may contribute to the positive correlation between m6A modification and transcript abundance.

NoMTA and NoMTB are involved in m6A modification during low CO2 stress in N. oceanica

Upon observing the alterations in m6A methylation across low carbon‐responsive genes and signaling genes (Figure 3d–f; Figure S6) under low CO2 stress, moreover, some m6A‐related genes showed a differential regulation under low CO2. We hypothesize that low CO2‐specific hypermethylation is orchestrated by m6A methyltransferases. In mammals, METTL3 and METTL14 form a stable heterodimer, with METTL3 acting as the catalytic subunit responsible for m6A modification, while METTL14 is crucial for facilitating RNA binding (He & He, 2021; Murakami & Jaffrey, 2022). The MTA and MTB in Arabidopsis can serve as homologs of METTL3 and METTL14 from mammals respectively, which implies the methyltransferase complexes are conserved (Shen, 2023). The homologs (NoMTA and NoMTB: NO04G02990 and NO07G02450 respectively) of MTA and MTB from N. oceanica were identified; moreover, they exhibited high similarity among plant and algal species by phylogenetic analysis (Figure S12). They contain the highly conserved MT‐A70 domain (Figure S13), which exhibits protein sequences remarkably similar to those found in mammals and Arabidopsis (Figure S13). As above‐mentioned, transcriptome analysis showed that the gene encoding NoMTA showed a dynamic mRNA pattern and the gene expression of NoMTB decreased significantly by 2–3 fold under from high to low CO2 condition (from w0 to w48) (Figure S14). However, the mRNA expression dynamics of NoMTA and NoMTB are inconsistent with the m6A levels in the cells (Figure S1b), which may be due to several factors. For instance, the increased m6A levels at W12, W24, and W48 could result from the activity of other m6A writers, accessory subunits, or m6A erasers and readers. N. oceanica may possess homologs of m6A writer complexes such as FIP37/WTAP (NO11G02460), METTL5 (NO20G02010), METTL16 (NO17G01520), and METTL1 (NO20G02010), whose expression varies dynamically under low‐carbon conditions, potentially contributing to the fluctuations in m6A levels. Moreover, m6A modification might also be influenced by post‐translational modifications or other cellular factors, including RNA stability, splicing, and translation efficiency. The mRNA expression of NoMTA was lower than that of NoMTB in a previous study (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). The NoMTA and NoMTB also exhibited upregulated expression from nitrogen repletion to nitrogen deprivation condition (Li et al., 2014), suggesting that the two putative methyltransferases may serve an important function in response to carbon or nitrogen stress.

To investigate its potential role as the putative RNA m6A methyltransferase in vivo, NoMTA and NoMTB were knocked out by gene editing to generate corresponding mutants (w1248‐3 and w67982‐5) (Figure S15). As for these two mutants, by sequencing of targeted sites, there was a “TA” deleted and “C” inserted into the target sites of w1248‐3 and w67982‐5, respectively (Figure 5a,b). We further assessed their physiological and molecular phenotypes. Under low CO2, w1248‐3 and w67982‐5 mutants displayed slightly slower growth than wild‐type (Figure 5c–e), while the growth of w67982‐5 was slower than that of w1248‐3. We then examined m6A levels in total RNAs isolated from low‐CO2 cultivation by dot blot experiment using an anti‐m6A antibody (Figure 5f). The result showed that the levels of m6A modification exhibited a slight reduction, which confirmed the m6A methylase activity that the m6A levels were significantly reduced in the NoMTA or NoMTB knockout mutants (w1248‐3 and w67982‐5) compared with WT (Figure 5f). Further quantitative measurement of m6A levels by LC–MS/MS revealed that m6A levels of total RNA were decreased by ≈11% and ≈15% in mutants (w1248‐3 and w67982‐5) as compared with those in wild‐type, respectively, (Figure 5g). Despite the knockout of NoMTA and NoMTB, we observed only a modest reduction in the overall m6A level. This limited effect suggests that Nannochloropsis may possess additional, as yet unidentified, m6A writer enzymes that compensate for the loss of these two genes. Consistent with this hypothesis, our genome‐wide BLAST analysis revealed putative homologous candidates that may contribute to m6A methylation in this organism. In addition, we also measured the photosynthetic parameter (F v/F m) and photosynthetic oxygen evolution in mutants (w1248‐3 and w67982‐5) and WT (Figure 5h,i). In mutants, Fv/Fm and oxygen evolution displayed a significant reduction. Fv/Fm and oxygen evolution of w67982‐5 were decreased by 14% and 16% (P < 0.05) compared to WT, respectively. These results suggest that NoMTA and NoMTB are involved in m6A methylation in N. oceanica.

To identify potential m6A methylation targets of NoMTA and NoMTB, we applied the ChIPseeker algorithm to calculate the differential m6A peaks (DMPs) between the wild‐type and mutants (w1248‐3 and w6798‐5). We identified 417 and 984 confident hypomethylated m6A peaks corresponding to 321 and 768 transcripts/genes under w1248‐3 versus w and w67982‐5 versus w, respectively (Figure 6d). The number of hypomethylated m6A peaks in the mutant w67982‐5 was significantly higher than that in the mutant w1248‐3 (Figure 6e,f), suggesting that the knockout of MTB had a more substantial global impact on m6A methylation than the knockout of MTA. In w1248‐3, these transcripts with DMPs mainly enriched in those pathways such as carbohydrate, lipid metabolism, and signal transduction (Figure 6g), while these transcripts with DMPs mainly enriched in amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism pathways in w67982‐5 (Figure 6h).

We subsequently performed combining analysis with our mRNA‐seq data in wt and mutants (w1248‐3 and w67982‐5) under low CO2 condition. Although the average mRNA level showed no difference in mutants and wild‐type (Figure 7a), there were 149 and 1739 downregulated genes and 351 and 1644 upregulated genes identified in w1248‐3 and w67982‐5 compared to wt, respectively (Figure 7b,c). The result showed that there were 6 and 262 downregulated genes with hypomethylation, and 8 and 45 upregulated genes with hypermethylation under w1248‐3 versus w and w67982‐5 versus w (Figure 7d,e). This result confirmed that the knockout of NoMTA exerted a much smaller impact on algal cell methylation compared to the knockout of NoMTB. In addition, we observed inconsistencies in the positive correlation between methylation and gene expression, which may be attributed to the compensatory methylation of m6A by other homologous genes following the knockout, or to the involvement of alternative regulatory mechanisms such as RNA stability, splicing, and translation efficiency. To explore the function of NoMTA and NoMTB, we conducted GO analysis on m6A hypomethylated genes, as well as differentially expressed genes in the mutants w1248‐3 and w6798‐5, respectively. Under w67982‐5 versus w, the m6A hypomethylated genes were significantly enriched in processes related to biological regulation, cellular process, and membrane part, and regulation of response to stimulus, macromolecule complex, and transporter activity by GO analysis (Figure S18). NoMTA deficiency impacted carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, replication and repair, and signal transduction (Figure S19) and NoMTB deficiency affected carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism, and cell growth and death by KEGG analysis of the differentially expressed genes with m6A peaks (Figure S16). These gene functions showed clear correlation with the observed phenotypes in NoMTA or NoMTB mutants.

Specifically, in knockout mutant w6789‐5, some potential CO2 responsive genes such as sodium/hydrogen exchanger family protein (NO03G02820 and NO30G00340), malate dehydrogenase (NO09G03170), and oxalate:formate antiporter‐like (NO12G01190) showed downregulated m6A peaks compared to wild‐type under low CO2 conditions (Figure 7f), meanwhile, at mRNA level, they also showed a downregulated trend. For instance, the genes coding sodium/hydrogen exchanger family protein (NO03G02820 and NO30G00340) showed a 1.5‐ and 1.9‐fold downregulation at the epitranscriptomic level under w6789‐5 versus w (Figure S20), which might be related to CCM in N. oceanica. It is essential for maximal HCO3−‐dependent oxygen evolution in cyanobacterial photosynthetic CCM (Woodger et al., 2007). Additionally, we also found that m6A alteration with 1.7‐fold reduced the expression levels of nitrite transporter (NAR; NO07G02740), which suggests that the nitrogen use efficiency would be reduced in mutant w6789‐5. The triosephosphate/phosphate translocator (TPT; NO20G02530) mediates the export of photoassimilates from the chloroplasts in the form of triose phosphates in the light, which showed a downregulation. In knockout mutant w1248‐3, the gene (NO09G02140) encoding aquaporin displayed a 1.6‐fold downregulated m6A peak compared to WT (Figure S20), which might be responsible for CO2 transport in CCM of N. oceanica. In terrestrial C3 plants, it has been demonstrated that photosynthesis and growth could be increased by expressing CO2‐transporting aquaporins (Raven & Beardall, 2016). In Chlamydomonas, the CO2‐conducting role of aquaporins and analogous protein pores in CCM have been observed for the plasmalemma vesicles derived from high and low CO2‐grown (Raven & Beardall, 2016). In w6789‐5 and w1248‐3, the gene encoding 5‐formyltetrahydrofolate cyclo‐ligase (NO10G00370) displayed 1.8‐fold downregulated peaks (Figure S20), which might be involved in a branch for the photorespiratory carbon cycle coupled with the photosynthetic C1 pathway in N. oceanica (Jardine et al., 2024; Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019), in our previous study, this gene is also a low CO2‐responsive gene. These results imply that the knockout of m6A methylase (NoMTB) had an important effect on carbon and nitrogen metabolism; thus, physiological and photosynthetic phenotypes were affected.

Engineering m6A modification for increasing the growth by CRISPR/dCas13‐based editing in N. oceanica

To artificially manipulate m6A modification and improve microalgal productivity, or stress tolerance, a CRISPR/dCas13 system targeting mRNA methylation was constructed, which is firstly accomplished for engineering mRNA modification in microalgae. Firstly, appropriate catalytic complexes as m6A writers must be selected for fusion with dCas13. After the knockout of candidate methyltransferase genes (NO04G02990 and NO07G02450) containing the MTA‐70 domain, the m6A level of mutants (w67982‐5) was significantly decreased under low CO2 (Figure 5f,g), moreover, the transcript of NO07G02450 was much higher than that of NO04G02990 (FPKM <10) (Figure S14). Thus, NoMTB was selected for the construction. However, due to the MTA from NO04G02990 with lower expression (FPKM <10) in N. oceanica and no interaction between NoMTA and NoMTB detected in our yeast two‐hybrid assays, we did not select NoMTA (NO04G02990) for m6A editing. As reported in the literature, the catalytic function of MTA requires interaction with MTB. Previous studies in Arabidopsis have experimentally demonstrated that MTA (AT4G10760) serves as the core m6A methyltransferase, and its catalytic activity is achieved through association with MTB (Shi et al., 2024). Moreover, by fusing the catalytic domain of MTA (AT4G10760) and MTB (AT4G09980), the reprogramming or deposition of m6A methylation has been successfully achieved in both Nicotiana benthamiana and Arabidopsis thaliana (Shi et al., 2024). Based on this knowledge, in our study we chose to express exogenous MTA together with endogenous MTB to achieve m6A editing in N. oceanica. Consequently, MTA from Arabidopsis and NoMTB from Nannochloropsis were truncated (Dataset S3) and fused as the core domain of methyltransferase to deposit m6A. Based on this fusion with dCas13 (codon optimization) referenced the method for epigenetic m6A editing (Figure 8a).

To test the function of m6A modification in the above vector construct, carbonic anhydrase (CA5: NO20G00630) with a well‐characterized function was selected as the target because the transcription level of CA5 is markedly upregulated under low‐carbon conditions (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019), and it has been demonstrated that CA5 is a critical component of the CCM via gene knockout (Gee & Niyogi, 2017). In our previous study, the CA5 gene is also regulated by multiplex epigenetic markers, including histone modifications and the three‐dimensional organization of chromatin (Gong et al., 2024, 2025; Liu & Wei, 2023; Yan et al., 2023). Moreover, our MeIP‐Seq revealed significant m6A peak enrichment at this locus (Figure S21). Based on these considerations, we therefore chose CA5 as the target gene. According to the region of the m6A peak in CA5 from MeRIP‐seq and bioinformatic prediction using SRAMP (Fan et al., 2024), the target of PAM located in the exon was designed (Labun et al., 2016). Two independent CA5‐m6A‐activation mutants, named M1 and M3, and MTA‐NoMTB‐dead control (C0) were generated via electroporation (Figure 8b), where the CA5 transcript level was increased by about 4‐fold under air‐level cultivation conditions (0.04% CO2) for 10‐day cultivation, respectively (Figure 8c). Consistently, the m6A level increased by about 3.2‐fold in M1 and M3 compared to wild‐type and C0 (Figure 8d). However, the global m6A/A ratio was measured by LC–MS and the result showed that no significant change was observed in M1 and M3 compared to WT and C0, respectively (Figure 8d). As an independent validation, we employed a non‐antibody‐based approach to quantify site‐specific m6A modifications. The recently developed SELECT assay leverages the inhibitory effect of m6A on both single‐nucleotide extension by DNA polymerases and nick ligation by ligases (Xiao et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2024) (Figure S22a). In agreement with the antibody‐based analyses, compared to the MTA‐NoMTB‐dead mutant (C0), the SELECT assay demonstrated a marked decrease in ligation products at the targeted site of CA5, but not at the non‐targeted site (Figure S22b). These results demonstrated that the constructs of m6A editing could work in N. oceanica for enhancing m6A levels. In addition, to avoid the interruption from endogenous genes (NoMTA or NoMTB), we did not observe significant gene expression changes of two endogenous genes (NO04G02990 and NO07G02450) between wild‐type (or C0) and mutants (Figure S23), which suggests that the observed increase in both the m6A level and the transcript abundance of the CA5 gene in the mutants is likely attributable to m6A modification mediated by the fused construct. The growth in M1 and M3 was assessed by OD750 and cell count (Figure 8f,g). Under air cultivation, M1 and M3 exhibit significantly higher growth rates than the WT throughout the 10‐day culture period, as shown by the growth curve (Figure 8f). Correspondingly, the cell counts of M1 and M3 are approximately 20% higher than that of WT, indicating a substantial increase in biomass productivity. (Figure 8g). Meanwhile, the photosynthetic parameter Fv/Fm of M1 and M3 cells is higher than WT after 10‐day cultivation under low CO2 (Figure 8h), which is consistent with the results from the growth. On the other hand, oxygen evolution in M1 and M3 increased by 11% and 14% after 10‐day cultivation under low CO2 (Figure 8i). Thus, the superior productivity of biomass in N. oceanica might be achieved by artificially manipulating m6A modification.

CONCLUSION

For the model industrial oleaginous microalga Nannochloropsis oceanica, the epitranscriptomic dynamics driving cellular adaptation to the shift from high to low CO2 revealed a substantial reprogramming of cellular metabolism. By employing MeRIP‐Seq and LC–MS, we have mapped the distribution of m6A modifications across different CO2 concentrations and analyzed their potential regulatory roles in metabolic adaptation. Through a combined analysis of m6A modification and gene transcription, a general trend of the positive correlation between m6A modifications and transcript levels was observed during CO2 stress in N. oceanica, which is different from that in Chlamydomonas. Additionally, through targeted gene editing of the m6A methyltransferase complex, commonly known as the m6A “writer”, we have knocked out two candidate m6A methylases (NoMTA and NoMTB) and further explored the direct impacts of this modification on the physiological and metabolic traits of the algae under low CO2 by comparative transcriptome and epitranscriptome, combining with physiological and biochemical analysis. Lastly, we constructed a vector for artificially manipulating m6A levels and engineered m6A modification by dCas13‐mediated epigenome editing in N. oceanica, which is the first to achieve gene editing at the epigenetic level to enhance growth in microalgae. Taken together, these strides would pave the way for microalgal epigenetics and future industrial applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cultivation of N. oceanica cells

Nannochloropsis oceanica IMET1 was cultured in modified f/2 liquid medium (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). The inoculum was introduced into the medium at 25°C, with continuous illumination at 50 ± 5 μmol m−2 sec−1 using LED light in a 200 ml column bioreactor (inner diameter: 3 cm), and aerated with 5% CO2 at a flow rate of 50 ml min−1. Cells were harvested during the logarithmic phase (OD750 = 3.0) by centrifugation at 2500 g , washed three times with fresh f/2 medium, and subsequently used for further experiments. Nine identical reactors were used for culturing N. oceanica, each with an initial OD750 of 1.5. To maintain pH stability, a 20 mm Tris–HCl buffer (pH = 8.2) was added to each reactor containing 180 ml of fresh modified f/2 medium (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). The cultures were pre‐adapted for 1 h under 5% CO2 aeration and 50 ± 5 μmol m−2 sec−1 light intensity. After the pre‐adaptation period, three cultures with 5% CO2 were designated as controls (w0), while the remaining nine cultures were exposed to 0.04% CO2 for sampling (CO2 was supplied by Jiateng Gas Company, Haikou, Hainan, China) (Wei, El Hajjami, et al., 2019). Cell samples were collected at 12 h (w12), 24 h (w24), and 48 h (w48) for epitranscriptomic profiling analysis. Mutant strains were cultivated under ambient CO2 conditions for phenotypic measurement.

LC–MS/MS assay of m6A in RNA

The m6A LC–MS/MS analysis was performed by enzymatically digesting total RNA into nucleosides. This was achieved through sequential treatment with S1 nuclease, phosphodiesterase, and alkaline phosphatase in ammonium acetate buffer (pH 5.3) at 37°C for 2 h. The reaction mixture was extracted with chloroform, and the aqueous phase was collected, concentrated under vacuum, and reconstituted in ultrapure water. Separation was carried out on an ACQUITY HSS T3 column at 40°C, using mobile phases (A) 2 mm ammonium bicarbonate in water and (B) 2 mm ammonium bicarbonate in methanol. A gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.3 ml min−1: 95% A (0–1 min), decreasing to 5% A by 8 min, held for 1 min, and re‐equilibrated to 95% A by 9.1 min (total run time: 12 min). Detection was performed on an AB SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ system in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode. Nucleosides were quantified by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) using a 17‐point calibration curve (0.0005–1000 pmol) constructed with pure nucleoside standards. m6A levels were calculated based on this calibration curve.

MeRIP‐Seq, RNA‐Seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Nanodrop 2000, with a RIN value greater than 7.0. mRNA was isolated from approximately 50 μg of total RNA using poly‐T magnetic beads, then chemically fragmented to ~150 nt using divalent cations at elevated temperatures. The RNA fragments were incubated with an m6A‐specific antibody (Synaptic Systems, Canonsburg, PA, USA) in IP buffer, followed by incubation with protein‐A beads and elution using an elution buffer containing m6A. The eluted RNA was precipitated with 75% ethanol and converted into a cDNA library using a strand‐specific dUTP method, with an average insert size of ~150 bp. Paired‐end 2 × 150 bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. Raw reads were processed for quality control using fastp (Chen et al., 2018), which included trimming of adaptors, low‐quality bases, and poly‐N sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the N. oceanica genome using Hisat2 (Kim et al., 2015), retaining only uniquely mapped reads with high mapping quality. MeTDiff (Cui et al., 2018) was employed to identify m6A‐enriched peaks, with filtering criteria of P‐value ≤0.05 and fold change ≥1.5. Peak annotation was performed using ChIPseeker (Yu et al., 2015). Differentially methylated peaks were identified using the same filtering criteria and annotated with ChIPseeker. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were carried out in R.

For RNA sequencing, libraries were generated and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150‐bp paired‐end reads. Raw fastq files were processed using fastp to eliminate low‐quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the N. oceanica genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015), and FPKM values were calculated using HTSeq‐count (Anders et al., 2015). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2, applying a threshold of Q‐value <0.05 and fold change >2 or <0.5 (Love et al., 2014). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized through hierarchical clustering in R, and a radar plot displaying the top 30 DEGs was generated using the ggradar package. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed for DEGs, and significant terms were visualized using R.

In RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq), differentially expressed genes (mRNA) were categorized as upregulation and downregulation. In MeRIP‐Seq, the differential regulation of gene methylation was noted according to the changes in peak abundance. In this study, correlation analyses for the two omics contents were performed using the Python (v.2.7.12) script to simultaneously compare transcription and methylation levels.

Dot blot analysis of m6A levels

Purified mRNA was denatured by heating at 95°C for 3 min, followed by rapid cooling on ice. The denatured mRNA was then spotted onto a Hybond‐N+ membrane (Amersham) for nucleic acid transfer. UV crosslinking was performed using a Stratalinker 2400 (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA), after which the membrane was washed with 1× PBST, blocked with 5% non‐fat milk in PBST, and incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti‐m6A antibody (1:250; Synaptic Systems). Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), membrane visualization was achieved using a DAB Western Blotting Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology).

Phylogenetic analysis of m6A methyltransferases in N. oceanica

To identify potential m6A methyltransferases in Nannochloropsis oceanica, the amino acid sequences of NO04G02990 and NO07G02450 were retrieved and analyzed through the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/). Sequences harboring the MT‐A70 domain were considered as candidate m6A methyltransferases. Known m6A methyltransferases METTL3 (MTA) and METTL14 (MTB) from mammals and Arabidopsis were employed for BLASTP searches on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to validate the identified homologs. For phylogenetic analysis, the sequences of the m6A methyltransferases identified in N. oceanica were aligned with those from other microalgae using ClustalX 2.1 with default settings (Thompson et al., 1997). The alignment was subsequently imported into MEGA software (version 11), where a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor‐Joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates (Tamura et al., 2021).

CRISPR‐based knockout of NO08G00280 and NO06G02500

For CRISPR‐based targeted knockout in N. oceanica, the pNOC‐ARS‐CRISPR modular CRISPR–Cas9 toolbox system was employed to construct the CRISPR plasmids. The gRNA target sequence was designed using the CHOPCHOP platform (http://chopchop.cbu.uib.no) (Labun et al., 2016) and formed a primer dimer (Table S2) through annealing. Linearized vectors containing a bleomycin resistance gene, digested with AseI, were introduced into the microalgae via electroporation. Following electroporation, the cells were plated onto 50% fresh seawater agar plates (1% agar) supplemented with 5 mg ml−1 zeocin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After 2–3 weeks of incubation under white light (approximately 100 μmol m−2 sec−1) at 20°C, individual resistant colonies were transferred into liquid f/2 medium containing 5 mg ml−1 zeocin. To confirm mutations in the target region, transformants were screened using specific primers (Table S2).

Construction of Nannochloropsis epigenetic m6A editing lines

To construct the m6A catalytic domain complex, the truncated endogenous MTB (No07G02450; residues 71–414) in N. oceanica and exogenous MTA (AT4G10760; residues 446–667) from plant (Shi et al., 2024) were connected with the linker of (GGS)10 and were fused as MTA‐NoMTB (M3M14‐like) catalytic domain containing an SV40 nuclear localization signal (SV40NLS; bp) and SunTag sequence (705 bp) (Dataset S3) with a codon‐optimized dCas13 sequence. The M3M14 domain derived from sequences was commonly used to optimize codons responsible for catalyzing mRNA methylation, which facilitates expression in the marine microalga Nannochloropsis. The SunTag (light‐inducible nuclear export tag (LEXY)) derived from plants (Arabidopsis thaliana) was selected and codon‐optimized according to existing techniques to enable its expression in Nannochloropsis. The sequences of dCas13 (with PspXI and HpaI) and MTA‐NoMTB (M3M14‐like) containing the SunTag sequence (with HpaI and MfeI) were artificially synthesized, respectively (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and they were connected to the vector backbone of pNOC‐ARS‐CRISPR for constructing the epigenetic m6A editing vector. The Ribi promoter in pNOC‐ARS‐CRISPR was used to drive the expression of dCas13 and MTA‐NoMTB (M3M14‐like) catalytic domain (Figure 8a). As for the MTA‐NoMTB‐dead construct, the DPPW motif in the original M3M14‐like catalytic domain complex amino acid sequence was replaced with APPD, and the corresponding DNA sequence was artificially synthesized (Dataset S4). Thus, CRISPR/dCas13 with M3M14 or MTA‐NoMTB‐dead was constructed in N. oceanica.

Validation of transcript abundance or m6A peaks using real‐time qPCR

To validate gene transcription in mRNA‐Seq, m6A peak abundance in MeRIP‐seq, and targeted gene expression of mutants, RNA extracted or immunoprecipitated was subjected to cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript® RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). Additionally, qRT‐PCR was conducted following standard protocols (Roche, Switzerland). Ct values were obtained from triplicate independent technical replicates conducted on triplicate biological cultures (n = 3). The relative fold difference was calculated using the ΔCt method, with actin amplification products serving as the internal standard. Primer pairs used for qRT‐PCR analyses are listed in Table S2, with amplification product sizes ranging from 100 to 300 bp.

Validation of m6A sites in targeted mRNAs

Total RNA (5 μg) was mixed with 40 nm Up and Down primers and 5 μm dNTP in 17 μl of 1× CutSmart buffer (50 mm potassium acetate, 20 mm Tris‐acetate, 10 mm magnesium acetate, 100 μg ml−1 BSA) (Xiao et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2024). Samples were annealed sequentially at 90°C, 80°C, 70°C, 60°C, and 50°C for 1 min each, followed by 40°C for 6 min. The annealed mixture (17 μl) was supplemented with 3 μl enzyme solution containing 0.01 U Bst 2.0 DNA polymerase, 0.5 U SplintR ligase, and 10 nmol ATP, and incubated at 40°C for 20 min, then heat‐inactivated at 80°C for 20 min. Quantitative PCR was performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) on a LightCycler 480 System (Roche, Switzerland) with the following program: 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 45 s. SELECT signals were normalized to the corresponding non‐m6A sites of each transcript. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Measuring photosynthetic oxygen evolution rate and the photosynthetic activity

Chlorophyll a (Chl a) content was quantified using a modified extraction method (Ritchie, 2006). Briefly, an algal culture sample was centrifuged at 2500 g, and the filter was incubated in the dark for 24 h with 100% ethanol saturated with MgCO3. The samples were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was analyzed spectrophotometrically. Extinction values at 632, 649, 665, and 750 nm were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Mutant and wild‐type (WT) strains, grown under air conditions, were sampled during the logarithmic phase, centrifuged at 2500 g for 5 min, and transferred to fresh f/2 medium containing Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.0) for subsequent photosynthetic O2 rate measurements. The oxygen evolution rate was determined using a Clark‐type oxygen electrode (Hansatech, UK) and normalized to the Chl a content. For photosynthetic activity, N. oceanica cultures were kept in the dark for 20 min before being exposed to a saturating light pulse (1000 μmol m−2 sec−1) for 1 sec. Chlorophyll fluorescence intensities were measured using a pulse amplitude‐modulated (PAM) kinetics system with an imaging‐PAM M‐Series (Walz, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The F v/F m (variable/maximum fluorescence ratio) was then calculated.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad, USA), with P‐values determined through one‐way analysis of variance.

The sequences of the m6A methyltransferases identified in N. oceanica were aligned with those from other microalgae using ClustalX 2.1 with default settings. The alignment was subsequently imported into MEGA software (version 11), where a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor‐Joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Figure S22. Validation of targeted m6A editing in CA5. (a) Schematic illustration of the site‐specific m6A detection termed SELECT. The m6A modification (indicated as a red “A”) impedes DNA polymerase‐mediated extension by blocking thymidine incorporation opposite the modified site and also prevents DNA ligase‐catalyzed nick ligation. The resulting elongated and ligated products are subsequently quantified by qPCR. (b) Measurement of m6A levels at targeted sites of CA5 in mutant samples (M1 and M3) and control (C0; MTA‐NoMTB‐dead). Notably, reduced ligation product abundance indicates higher m6A modification levels. Values shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. P‐values determined through unpaired Student's t‐test (**P < 0.01; *P < 0.05).
