Overlooked and overexploited: Extensive conversion of grasslands and wetlands driven by global food, feed, and bioenergy demand
Authors: Siyi Kan, Samuel A. Levy, Elise Mazur, Leah Samberg, U. Martin Persson, Lindsey Sloat, Ana L. Reboredo Segovia, Leandro Parente, Thomas Kastner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceVast natural ecosystems are being cleared for agriculture to meet rising domestic and international demand. While forests have received significant policy and research attention, land-use pressure is increasingly shifting to grasslands and wetlands, which are ecologically important but often underprotected and understudied. We analyzed natural non-forest ecosystem conversion to both pasture and cropland between 2005 and 2020, the contributing commodities, their end uses (food, feed, or bioenergy), and market destinations. We found that the conversion rate of natural non-forest ecosystems was nearly four times that of lands with tree cover exceeding 5 m (a common forest height threshold), with a large share linked to exports, highlighting the need to expand conservation efforts beyond forests and engage both producers and consumers.

As global efforts to eliminate deforestation from agricultural supply chains progress, agricultural expansion is increasingly putting pressure onto natural non-forest ecosystems. These ecosystems, including grasslands and open wetlands, are vital for conservation, but are often overlooked, underprotected, and understudied. Grasslands alone hold 20-35% of global carbon stocks (1) and 33% of global biodiversity hotspots (2), in addition to supporting critical ecosystem services locally and globally (3). Despite their importance, these ecosystems continue to experience growing threats due to agricultural expansion (4, 5), which undermines progress toward achieving global targets on biodiversity (6), sustainable development (7), and climate change (8). Historically, these non-forest ecosystems have received limited attention in both research and protective policies compared to forests. While emerging policies, by both national governments [e.g., Brazil’s Decree no. 11.367/2023 (9)] and supply chain initiatives [e.g., Agriculture Sector Roadmap to 1.5C signatories (10)] are attempting to protect non-forest ecosystems, such policies remain less common and are generally less stringently implemented than those targeting forests (11–13).

For effective implementation of interventions aimed at reducing natural non-forest ecosystem loss, it is essential to have spatially explicit information on where conversion is occurring and which agricultural products are driving this process. While there has been extensive research on deforestation trends and drivers (14, 15), analyses addressing non-forest conversion, especially related to livestock production, remain scarce. This research gap partly stems from technical challenges, centering on the outsized role of livestock production in land-use change patterns. Global grazing land accounts for over two-thirds of total agricultural land (16) and includes intensively managed cultivated pastures (henceforth referred to as pasture) as well as native grasses grazed with minimal ecological impacts. Pasture comprises a substantial share of land use in major livestock-producing countries like Brazil (16) and its expansion plays a key role in driving ecosystem conversion (17). Nevertheless, it is difficult to distinguish pasture from natural grassland, especially when relying on scalable data sources like satellite imagery (18). Historically, most land cover products fail to classify pasture as a distinct category (19, 20) or are not based on earth observation and constrained by coarse spatial resolution (21, 22). Consequently, though previous studies have mapped global agricultural expansion (23) and agriculture-driven conversion of forest (24) and non-forest ecosystems (25), they either focused solely on cropland or lacked a comprehensive representation of pasture as well as its distinction from cropland, let alone the contribution of individual agricultural commodities to the conversion. This highlights the need for an integrated assessment of how both livestock and crop production affect natural non-forest ecosystems at global, commodity, and fine spatial scales.

In addition to identifying the primary agricultural products associated with non-forest conversion, it is also crucial to understand the underlying demand drivers, including their end uses (for food, feed, or other purposes) and final market destinations (domestic or export). This understanding is essential for designing demand-side strategies for sustainable land use and supply chains. For example, some crops such as soy and maize are often produced for animal feed and are often exported (16), illustrating how distant demand can indirectly drive natural ecosystem conversion (26). Recognizing such distinctions helps reveal where interventions, such as dietary shifts and trade regulations, can be most effective. Detailed supply chain information also supports further development of both corporate commitments and regulatory frameworks aimed at reducing natural land conversion, including integrating non-forest ecosystems into supply chain policies and environmental standards for traded commodities [e.g., the European Union Regulation on Deforestation–free Products (EUDR) (27)]. Several studies have examined how the expansion of internationally traded agricultural products affects forest ecosystems (28–30), yet comparable analyses for natural non-forest ecosystems are lacking, partly because of the absence of detailed data on non-forest conversion and its commodity-level drivers.

In this context, the paper aims to provide a global geospatial evaluation of the distribution and drivers of natural non-forest ecosystem conversion to both pasture and cropland between 2005 and 2020, structured around three research aims (Fig. 1). First, we mapped conversion at the grid, country, and ecoregion scales, to broadly inform regions of high conversion risk and conservation needs. Second, we identified the primary agricultural commodities associated with the conversion to facilitate targeted interventions in commodity-specific production. Finally, we analyzed the end uses and final markets of the conversion-linked primary agricultural commodities to reveal underlying demand drivers and support supply chain actions. Conversion was identified using two state-of-the-art 30 m resolution datasets: The recently published Global Pasture Watch (GPW) (31), which allowed us to distinguish pasture from natural grassland at a global scale, and the Global Land Cover and Land Use Change (GLCLUC) (32) for cropland (herbaceous crops only) and various natural land covers. These high-resolution, satellite-based datasets can improve the quality of land-conversion estimates compared to coarse or nonsatellite data, which often mask conversion of natural ecosystems within large grid cells. Conversion was then allocated to primary agricultural commodities by combining crop-specific nonspatial cropland expansion data from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) (16) with pixel-level crop distribution data from the Spatial Production Allocation Model (MAPSPAM) (33). End uses and final market destinations were identified using a physical trade accounting model that integrates FAO data on agricultural production, bilateral trade, and supply and utilization (16). Due to the lack of an accuracy assessment that would allow for reporting confidence intervals around any area estimates, we do not provide absolute area estimates but rather indicate estimates proportionally. Given the limitations of analyzing change with datasets that do not employ change detection techniques, we applied several techniques to minimize methodological limitations and ensure the robustness of the results, such as using multiyear data to define agricultural and natural land covers (Materials and Methods). We also discussed challenges in global-scale land conversion assessments and indicated key gaps where better data and local knowledge are most needed to enable more targeted and precise assessments.

Overview of the study framework.

We found that natural non-forest ecosystems were converted to pasture and cropland at an alarming rate, driven by demand from both domestic and international markets for diverse agricultural commodities (mainly staple, nuts, and oil crops). These findings highlight commodity-specific conversion risks across sourcing regions, the natural non-forest areas in need of protection, and key leverage points for supply chain interventions. Addressing these challenges requires integrated policies that avoid leakages across different ecosystems and sustainable development goals while also addressing demand-side drivers through internationally coordinated supply chain governance.

Results

Conversion of Natural Non-forest Ecosystems.

Natural non-forest ecosystem in this study refers to all noncropland, nonpasture, nonbare land areas with vegetation under 5 m tall [per FAO’s forest height threshold (34)] (see Materials and Methods for detailed definitions). Between 2005 and 2020, the conversion rate of natural non-forest ecosystems was almost four times that of lands with tree cover taller than 5 m, with both pasture and cropland expansion playing important roles (Fig. 2A). Considering the relatively small extent of wetland conversion, all subsequent analyses (in figures except Fig. 2A) focus on all natural non-forest land collectively.

Among the countries with the greatest conversion extent (hereafter referred to as top conversion countries, with the European Union analyzed as an aggregate), the top fifteen together accounted for nearly two-thirds of global total natural non-forest conversion (Fig. 2 B and C). Brazil represented 13% of the global total, more than twice that of the other top five countries: Russia, India, China, and the United States (each contributing about 6%). Pasture expansion was the main driver in Brazil (82% of total conversion), Australia (80%), Russia (74%), and Burkina Faso (73%), while cropland expansion dominated in Sudan (97%), China (94%), Tanzania (72%), Kazakhstan (67%), and India (66%). The top eight conversion countries, which together contributed approximately 50% of the global total, exhibited different temporal trends (Fig. 2D). Brazil and Russia saw a clear decline in conversion over time, primarily due to reduced pasture-driven conversion. Argentina showed a similar trend, with a slight rebound during 2015 and 2020, largely influenced by changes in cropland expansion. India was the only country with an overall increasing trend, driven by a sharp rise in pasture-driven conversion. China, the United States, the European Union, and Tanzania experienced modest increases during 2010–2015, followed by slight decreases.

Conversion-Linked Primary Agricultural Commodities.

The primary agricultural commodities associated with natural non-forest conversion are visualized in Fig. 3, with a further breakdown by ecoregion within the top eight conversion countries in Fig. 4. Only cropland-driven conversion was allocated, for lack of suitable methods and data to identify the contribution of specific livestock species. Crops can drive conversion directly by encroaching on natural lands or indirectly by occupying existing cropland or pasture and displacing other agricultural commodities into natural areas. By combining national-level cropland expansion statistics with pixel-level distribution data, our results integrated both direct and indirect influence. Around 1% of the observed conversion could not be attributed globally due to data inconsistencies between GLCLUC and MAPSPAM. Meanwhile, the attribution analysis focused on the entire 2005–2020 period and areas that remained cropland or pasture in 2020, to align with the available crop distribution data and reduce uncertainties related to land cover misclassification.

At a global level, cropland-driven natural non-forest conversion was predominantly linked to staple crops (mainly maize, wheat, and rice) and oil crops (mainly soybean, sunflower, and groundnut), although the composition of contributing crops varied greatly across countries. Among the top eight conversion countries, staple crops were the main contributors in five cases, with maize dominating in China, the United States, Argentina, and Tanzania, and wheat in Russia. Soybean played a particularly important role in Latin and North America, taking a clear lead in Brazil and contributing similarly to maize in Argentina. Other oil and nut crops accounted for a substantial share in Tanzania (mainly sunflower, groundnut, and sesame) and the European Union (mainly sunflower and rapeseed). In India, the leading contributing crops differed from those in other countries, with pulses, cotton, and rice being dominant. India and the European Union also exhibited higher diversity in contributing crops compared to other countries.

Conversion was concentrated in specific ecoregions in Brazil, Argentina, and Tanzania, while in other top conversion countries, it was more equally spread throughout the ecoregions. In Brazil, the Cerrado, one of the world’s most biodiverse savannahs, accounted for almost 40% of the national total, while the semiarid Caatinga accounted for another 17%. In Argentina, the Humid Pampas, historically one of the world’s most productive grasslands, and the Espinal, a transitional woodland-grassland mosaic, contributed over 40 and 20% of the national total, respectively. In Tanzania, Dry miombo woodlands and Southern Acacia-Commiphora bushlands and thickets, both supporting high wildlife diversity and pastoral livelihoods, each represented about 30%. For several ecoregions within Brazil, Russia, and India, over 20% of their natural non-forest vegetation present in 2005 was converted. Some conversion occurred within tree-dominated ecoregions, potentially reflecting either the loss of embedded non-forest ecosystems or reuse of previously cleared tree cover (see Discussion). The composition of contributing crops also varied across ecoregions.

Underlying Demand Drivers.

Cropland-driven conversion was tracked to the countries where associated commodities were consumed as food, feed, or other uses (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1), by converting processed commodities to their primary commodity equivalents based on a mass-balance approach. Pasture-driven conversion was allocated to ruminant meat as a proxy, which represents the major commodity of pasture-based livestock systems (16). Complementary results for allocation to milk, another primary pasture-derived product, show broadly similar patterns (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

Links between natural non-forest conversion in producing regions and end uses in final market destinations.

While some conversion-linked crops (e.g., rice and vegetables) were mainly consumed directly as food, other crops (e.g., soybean, maize, and rapeseed) were often used as animal feed, and still others (e.g., sugarcane, maize, soybean, and rapeseed) were used for other purposes such as biofuel. Globally, food consumption accounted for 54% of cropland-driven conversion and was the leading demand-side driver in India, Tanzania, and Russia (53 to 75%) (Table 1). Feed use contributed 34% of cropland-driven conversion, and this share reached over 50% (51 to 68%) in Brazil, Argentina, the United States, China, and the European Union. Other uses contributed only 12% to the global total, yet its relative importance was higher in the United States, China, and Brazil (16 to 28%), mainly due to their bioenergy demand (35–37).

Export-oriented production accounted for 20% of all global cropland-driven conversion and for 32% of cropland-driven conversion for feed (e.g., soy and maize), with markedly higher shares observed in several top conversion countries. In Brazil and Argentina, about 70% of cropland-driven conversion and about 80% of feed-related conversion were destined for foreign markets. This highlights strong consumption–production telecouplings to higher income and rapidly growing economies (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), similar to deforestation footprints (30). Although shares of exported food crops were lower relative to feed crops in these two countries, they still represented over 60% of food-related conversion. In the United States and Russia, roughly 40% of cropland-driven conversion was linked to export, but the exported shares were higher for food than for feed (57 vs. 51% in the United States and 46 vs. 32% in Russia). Globally, pasture-driven conversion was generally associated with domestic consumption; however, in Brazil, where pasture expansion was the main driver of conversion, 15% was related to exports. In Argentina, the United States, and the European Union, where pasture accounted for over one-third of conversion, export shares ranged between 7 to 11%. Overall, Latin America and the Caribbean were most affected by global markets, driven mainly by demand from East Asia, followed by Middle East and North Africa, Europe, and North America.

Discussion

This study utilized state-of-the-art global land cover and land-use datasets to provide a comprehensive assessment of agriculture-driven conversion of natural non-forest ecosystems. Our analysis underscores the critical need to expand the scope of conservation and restoration efforts beyond forests to valuable non-forest ecosystems, and offers actionable insights for policymakers, practitioners, and supply chain actors from both production and demand side.

This work presents a global analysis quantifying natural non-forest ecosystem conversion to both pasture and cropland. Land clearance for raising livestock is a leading driver of land-use change, and as this analysis shows, pasture-driven non-forest conversion is extremely widespread. However, this transition has not been well defined and requires the discrete classification of what is, in many cases, a continuous gradient of land-use intensity. GPW provides a starting point in identifying the nature and magnitude of these transitions. Further efforts will likely be required to refine and contextualize GPW methodology, like incorporating regional and context-specific datasets to support meaningful identification. In addition, conversion to pasture is sometimes a first step in a land-use change trajectory that leads to eventual conversion to cropland (17), so our findings should be also viewed in the context of broader land-use change dynamics.

The patterns of natural non-forest conversion are shaped by diverse country-specific contexts. Brazil experienced the highest level of conversion, with extensive agricultural expansion in the Cerrado and Caatinga regions. Our results reveal the significant impacts of livestock, soybean, and sugarcane production, which have long been recognized as major deforestation drivers and key targets of public land-use governance and no-deforestation initiatives (38, 39). The observed conversion highlights the potential leakage effects from such policies, many of which focus primarily on the adjacent Amazon region (4). Similar patterns were also observed in the broader Río de la Plata Grasslands area, where favorable soybean prices drove rapid expansion of soybean production area and in turn led to livestock intensification (40). In Russia, conversion was mainly found in steppe ecosystems, which rank among the least conserved biome by several metrics, and rising agricultural investment and land-use profitability have further driven the encroachment (41). In India, unlike other leading conversion countries, conversion is more evenly spread across ecoregions classified as forests. A challenge is that non-forest ecosystems are often misclassified as degraded forests or even deemed unproductive wastelands by the Indian government, a legacy of colonial interpretations and taxation systems (42). This misclassification increases the likelihood that non-forest ecosystems are converted for agriculture or targeted by tree-planting initiatives (43). China’s conversion was mainly driven by livestock feed demand in order to meet rising meat demand (44). Extensive cropland loss due to rapid urban expansion has also pushed agriculture into natural frontiers (45). For the United States, more recent farming policies coupled with high commodity crop prices for maize and soy (46), the two feed and bioenergy crops contributed the most, have driven continued conversion of the Great Plains ecoregion.

The identified patterns in natural non-forest conversion across these contexts point to both common and region-specific implications for public policies. Overall, our analyses suggest that conversion risks often arise from the undervaluing of non-forest ecosystems in both policy and public discourse, as well as broader economic and agricultural incentives. Addressing these challenges will therefore require enhanced education and outreach to strengthen recognition of the value of non-forest ecosystems, alongside coherent, cross-sectoral policy frameworks that reduce unintended land-use trade-offs and policy leakages. Raising public awareness has been considered a critical issue that policies could help to address in several contexts, most notably the Cerrado (47, 48). Reducing leakages will also require narrowing the disparities in public and private protections between forest and non-forest ecosystems, for example, by increasing representation of non-forest ecosystems in protected areas and promoting credible no-conversion commitments by private companies that go beyond forests. Currently, while many companies have committed to no-deforestation, few have extended this to no-conversion covering all natural ecosystems.

Our results also underscore a tension between domestic consumption and export-oriented production, highlighting the need for governance approaches across multiple scales: linking local land-use planning and production approaches with global demand-side and supply chain interventions. The findings indicate that it is the underlying demand rather than any one crop per se that drives conversion. Therefore, policies targeting individual commodities are unlikely to succeed in isolation, as restricting one commodity without reducing demand risks shifting production to alternative crops or locations (39, 49). We also found that both domestic and international demand influence these land dynamics, implying that effective responses depend on international cooperation and supply chain governance. Producer countries need support to reconcile local food security and economic growth with land conservation, while consumer countries and companies should share responsibility by promoting sustainable sourcing and reducing the land-use intensity of diets. Likewise, coordinated global initiatives, from traceable commodity supply chains to trade agreements that reward low-conversion production, could help translate these data-informed insights into practical mechanisms for reducing leakage and aligning economic incentives with conservation outcomes.

Compared with existing analyses of supply chain impacts on deforestation (i.e., deforestation footprints) (30), non-forest conversion showed both similar and contrasting geographic patterns. Consumption in high-income countries [as defined by the World Bank (50)] potentially drove 20% of global non-forest conversion, with 26% of their total conversion footprint and 37% of their cropland-driven conversion footprint occurring abroad, a pattern similar to the comparable analysis for deforestation. However, unlike deforestation footprints that were largely concentrated in tropical regions, influence of high-income countries on non-forest ecosystems was predominantly located in temperate and boreal regions. Accordingly, strategies for non-forest ecosystem conservation can draw on lessons from successful no-deforestation supply chain initiatives [e.g., the Amazon Soy Moratorium (51)], while calling for greater attention to supply chain impacts extending beyond tropical areas.

Moreover, our analyses point to several challenges for global-scale land conversion assessments and shortcomings of currently available data products:(1)Defining natural and non-forest ecosystems is conceptually challenging, especially at a global scale, and requires careful interpretation of the definitions used. Land cover classifications vary widely across existing literature (19, 20, 24). We used an exclusion approach to identify natural non-forest ecosystems, including all short vegetation pixels not labeled as cropland and pasture. Thus, our estimates may capture conversion from both natural and seminatural land (e.g., grazing land with natural vegetation and minimal human impact) toward intensive land use. A possible improvement is developing a discrete classification based on land-use intensity gradients. Additionally, our estimates may include conversion of regenerating short vegetation following forest clearing. The analyses at the ecoregion level can provide a complementary perspective (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), but it remains unclear whether observed changes reflect conversion of primary or secondary non-forest vegetation and whether previous clearing occurred recently or in the distant past.(2)Technical constraints may also bias the results, and context-specific knowledge and improved remote sensing techniques are essential to fully understand land-use dynamics. For example, historically large areas of communal or subsistence agriculture in India with low inputs, long fallow cycles, and minimal irrigation are harder to distinguish from natural ecosystems. Gradual intensification in these areas could have been classified as natural ecosystem conversion in our methodology (52). Nonetheless, previous studies have also confirmed agricultural expansion into Indian non-forest regions (53, 54). Similarly, the difficulty to differentiate tree plantations from natural tree cover may also cause misestimation. Moreover, improving conversion identification requires remote sensing products that support change detection (55), which are currently unavailable for non-forest ecosystems due to their undervaluation and technical challenges. Until such datasets become available, our study provides a foundation for understanding non-forest conversion patterns and identifies shortcomings of currently available data products and where better data and local knowledge are most needed for more targeted and precise analyses.(3)Our work underscores the need for more detailed, spatial, commodity-specific land-use data and a conceptually consistent integration with land cover data. We integrated land cover data with high-resolution, global-scale pasture data, while this integration would be further strengthened, if additional fine-scale information on livestock numbers and production systems becomes available. We also found discrepancies between remote sensing-based land cover data and crop-specific land-use data based on census data and expert solicitation. Aligning these approaches would advance the ability to confidently assess direct drivers of land conversion. This highlights the critical role of national agricultural censuses and statistical information, as current remote sensing-based land cover and land-use products lack comprehensive crop or livestock-specific details.

Materials and Methods

The methodological framework comprises three main steps. First, we established a consistent land-cover classification scheme and identified leading regions of natural non-forest conversion and their shares in the global total, using the GLCLUC (32) and GPW datasets (31). Second, we developed a commodity attribution model to allocate the conversion identified in Step 1 to individual primary agricultural commodities driving the conversion, combining MAPSPAM crop distribution data (33) with FAOSTAT crop expansion statistics (16). Third, we linked the commodity-specific results from Step 2 to a physical trade accounting model to trace primary agricultural commodities to their end uses and final market destinations, based on FAO data on production, bilateral trade, and supply and utilization (16). Because there are no suitable method and data to distinguish the impacts of different livestock species, and their use across commodities (e.g., meat and milk) cannot be reliably distinguished based on the mass-balance approach, unlike for crops, we allocated pasture-driven conversion to ruminant meat, which represents the major commodity in the livestock system according to FAO production data. Comparisons of export shares between ruminant meat and milk (another key livestock commodity) as well as results for allocation to milk are provided in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, which show broadly similar patterns.

Identifying Conversion of Natural Non-forest Ecosystems to Pasture and Cropland.

Natural land conversion was identified for areas classified as natural land initially and transitioned to cropland or pasture in subsequent years. Therefore, the types of land conversion (e.g., non-forest versus forest land conversion) depend on the land cover classifications of the datasets used. This paper mainly relied on the GLCLUC product (32), considering its comprehensive land cover classification, global coverage, wide temporal span, and fine spatial resolution. As it does not distinguish between natural and more heavily managed grassland, the GPW (31) dataset was used to differentiate natural and cultivated grassland (i.e., pasture). SI Appendix, Table S1 summarizes how the GLCLUC and GPW data were integrated. Land cover definitions vary greatly in existing literature and databases; therefore, the estimates should be interpreted with great attention to the definitions of terms. GLCLUC does not explicitly label forest, grassland, or wetland, but instead distinguishes between tree cover and short vegetation cover, which are further divided into subclasses by tree height and short vegetation cover density, respectively. In this study, natural non-forest land is defined via an exclusion approach. We first identified all areas in GLCLUC with vegetation less than 5 m in height [i.e., FAO’s threshold for forest tree height (34)] and more than 7% vegetation cover (GLCLUC cutoff between bare land and sparse vegetation), as well as land dominated by open surface water for less than 80% of the year (for wetland). We then excluded pixels also classified by GPW as cultivated grassland for the same year. The remainder was treated as natural non-forest land. Following FAO’s forest height threshold and previous studies (15, 32), conversion of lands with tree cover taller than 5 m was used as a proxy for deforestation and compared with natural non-forest ecosystem conversion.

Regarding agricultural land, pasture is defined as areas classified as cultivated grassland in the GPW dataset and short vegetation in GLCLUC. In cases where pixels are classified as cultivated grassland by GPW but not as short vegetation in GLCLUC, the GLCLUC classification was retained. Cropland is a discrete class in GLCLUC and was used directly. This class excludes tree plantations, which lack globally consistent datasets that provide year-of-establishment information and include all types of plantations, therefore, could bias some of the results.

While differencing maps produced annually is not best practice for identifying change (55), due to technical constraints, there are currently no global-scale change detection datasets that distinguish both cropland and pasture from natural grassland. To address potential biases resulting from this approach and ensure the robustness of the results, several measures were employed:(1)Cropland and pasture were classified using multiple years of data in order to reduce misclassification. The GLCLUC cropland data use a 4-y epoch, assigning the cropland class if there is cropland present in any of the 4 y. This allows for temporarily fallow land to be correctly mapped as cropland. To summarize, conversion was identified for areas that were consistently classified as natural non-forest land for two consecutive initial observations (both in 2000 and 2005) and later transitioned to cropland or pasture. The period of conversion reflects the first time the transition to cropland or pasture occurred. For example, conversion between 2010 and 2015 includes areas that remained natural non-forest land in 2000, 2005, and 2010, but appeared as cropland in 2015. The resulting classifications at 30 m resolution were aggregated to 10 km resolution (for visualization purposes and to ensure consistency with the MAPSPAM dataset), as well as the country and ecoregion level (as defined by ref. 56) for further analysis. We calculated tree cover conversion following the same methods, except using the tree cover class in the GLCLUC dataset instead of short vegetation.

Attributing Conversion to Primary Agricultural Commodities.

For pasture, no further attribution to different livestock species was performed, due to lack of suitable methods and data. For cropland, since there are no high-resolution, global-scale distribution maps that cover and differentiate all crops, we were not able to estimate direct encroachment of specific crops on natural non-forest ecosystems at 30 m resolution. Furthermore, crops can either drive conversion directly by encroaching on natural lands or indirectly by occupying existing cropland or pasture and displacing other agricultural commodities into natural areas. Therefore, we statistically allocated conversion to specific crops using available national-level data on cropland expansion provided by FAO (16) and the pixel-level crop distribution data (originally with a resolution of 5 arcminutes, projected to an equal area 10 km by 10 km resolution) provided by MAPSPAM (33). We attributed cropland-driven conversion to specific crops by assuming that conversion potentially associated with a specific crop would be in proportion to 1) its national-level cropland expansion between 2005 and 2020 (FAO data), and 2) its spatial distribution in accordance with the MAPSPAM data for 2020. Conversion was only allocated to herbaceous crops (see SI Appendix, Table S2 for included crops and item mapping) that showed expansion at the national level. For the presentation of the results, the European Union was treated as one entity, while for the calculation, each member state was considered individually.

We used 3-year averages in the calculation of crop expansion, i.e., FAO data for 2004, 2005 and 2006 for 2005 cropland area, and 2019, 2020, and 2021 for 2020 cropland area. All area values used in the above formula refer to physical area rather than harvested area. For lack of physical area data in FAO and lack of time-series consistent estimates in MAPSPAM, we estimated cropping intensity of each crop based on MAPSPAM 2020 physical and harvest area data and then applied these cropping intensities to convert FAO harvested area to physical area. Note that multiple cropping can also introduce bias. For example, the expansion of soy-corn double cropping is not captured by FAO data, which records increases in both crops’ harvested areas without distinguishing sequential planting. This can lead to an overestimation of their contribution to cropland expansion. However, quantifying such effects remains challenging due to the lack of globally consistent, spatially and temporally explicit data on multiple cropping. Integrating local knowledge can help assess the potential misestimation of contributing crops.

This approach revealed some inconsistencies between GLCLUC and MAPSAM datasets. For some areas, there was cropland according to the GLCLUC but less or even no cropland according to MAPSPAM. Based on the formula, the conversion can be allocated only when 1) MAPSPAM cropland distribution area is no less than the conversion area and 2) crops present within the grid cell concerned also show cropland expansion nationally. Globally, around 79% of the calculated conversion areas meet both conditions. For another 20%, the converted areas exceeded the total MAPSPAM cropland areas within the corresponding grid cell. For these grid cells, we assumed the share allocated to a specific crop can be applied to the entire converted areas within the grid. For the remaining 1%, no MAPSPAM data were available (i.e., condition 1 was not met). This unattributable portion was retained in the analyses, as GLCLUC, based on high-resolution satellite imagery, likely offers greater spatial accuracy than MAPSPAM, which relies on model-based estimates at a coarser resolution. To examine the robustness of our results, we additionally compared estimates based on MAPSPAM with those derived from an alternative crop distribution dataset, CROPGRIDS (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) (57), which showed highly consistent overall patterns and crop rankings.

Tracing End Use and Final Market Destination.

After attributing the conversion to agricultural land and allocating the conversion to specific agricultural commodities, we identified a) the shares of each commodity for domestic use and for export production and b) the shares of each commodity used directly as human food, as livestock feed, and for other purposes (such as biofuels and materials).

There are two widely applied methods to link producing countries experiencing land-use pressures with the countries where corresponding commodities are ultimately consumed, namely, the physical trade accounting model and the input–output analysis model, each with its own advantages and limitations (58). Input–output analysis is based on tables depicting all interindustry trade flows within the global economy, typically measured in monetary terms. It enables tracing environmental impacts across entire supply chains to highly processed commodities and final consumers, but often criticized for sectoral and country aggregation, which overlooks heterogeneities among products and countries, and for allocating land-use impacts in proportion to monetary value, which can cause distortion since land-use intensity is not necessarily linked to price (58, 59). Physical trade accounting utilizes data on production, bilateral trade, and supply and utilization of primary and relatively low-processed commodities in physical units. This approach provides higher commodity and country detail, and its mass-based allocation can better reflect land-use intensity, though it cannot capture highly manufactured products (i.e., truncation issues) (58, 60).

This study adopted the physical trade accounting model. This is because policy measures addressing upstream land conversion (e.g., EUDR) tend to focus on trade in raw or low-processed products, and we aim to facilitate targeted conservation strategies through commodity-specific supply chains, which requires high commodity details (provided in supplemental data). Moreover, this study focuses on agricultural products that are primarily used for food and feed rather than on manufactured goods, therefore, uncertainties due to truncation are likely smaller than those from sectoral or country aggregation and from monetary value-based impact allocation. The data for the physical trade accounting model are taken from the FAOSTAT database (16). The calculation procedures are described in Refs. 60–62. We here describe the general underlying approach, details on its implementation can be found in the original publications.

The model is balanced at the global level, so that global production equals global use (e.g. losses and seed uses are distributed proportionally among final uses). To achieve this, the model assumes that the imports and domestic production of a commodity contribute proportionally to its domestic consumption and to its exports, allowing to account for “re-exports” in the widest sense. For instance, if soybeans are produced in country A, processed in country B, and used as feed in country C for a livestock product consumed in country D, official trade statistics would count the flow originating from the same production several times. The model establishes a link between country A and country D and counts the flow only once, ensuring mass balance. From the model, we derive the amount of a product in primary equivalents (i.e. also accounting for processing) that is exported from the countries where the conversion occurs [(a) above]. In addition, based on FAOSTAT’s supply and utilization account data, we further split the uses into food, feed, and nonfood uses (proportionally assigning seed uses and losses [(b) above], irrespective if these uses occur domestically or in the importing countries.
