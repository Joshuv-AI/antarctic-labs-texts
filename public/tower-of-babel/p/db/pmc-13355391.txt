Loss of ATRAID in Late-Onset, Non-Syndromic Retinitis Pigmentosa
Authors: Roya Mehrasa, Ragnhild Wivestad Jansson, Hanah Kurosawa, Nita Singh, Per Morten Knappskog, Cecilie Bredrup, Lauren Surface, Ove Bruland, Eyvind Rødahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo identify and characterize a novel gene variant associated with late-onset, non-syndromic retinitis pigmentosa (RP).MethodsOphthalmological examination included Goldmann perimetry, optical coherence tomography, fundus autofluorescence and full-field electroretinography. Gene variants were identified using whole exome sequencing. Expression of ATRAID in fibroblasts was studied using immunoblot analysis and quantitative PCR. Lysosomes were identified in ATRAID−/− and SLC37A3−/− cells using immunofluorescence analysis. Uptake of fluorescent dextran was measured using immunofluorescence analysis and flow cytometry.ResultsWe first examined two siblings whose parents were second cousins. Ophthalmological examination revealed symmetrical midperipheral chorioretinal atrophy with bone spicules and vessel attenuation and a corresponding rod-cone dysfunction on full-field electroretinography consistent with RP. Whole exome sequencing identified a novel homozygous variant in the ATRAID gene (NM_001170795, c.120dup, p.Ser41Glufs*13) within a 12.45 Mb region of homozygosity, predicted to result in a premature stop codon. Immunoblot analysis confirmed protein absence, indicating a loss of function variant. Further analysis of individuals with similar clinical features identified this variant in a homozygous state in four additional affected individuals in three non-consanguineous families. ATRAID has been shown to interact with SLC37A3 in lysosomes. Recently, loss of SLC37A3 has been associated with non-syndromic RP with features resembling those seen in loss of ATRAID. Increased numbers of lysosomes and accumulation of fluorescein-labelled dextran were observed both in ATRAID−/− and SLC37A3−/− cells.ConclusionsWe propose that biallelic loss of ATRAID causes RP through the disruption of ATRAID and SLC37A3 interaction resulting in impaired endocytic trafficking and lysosomal function.

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) (MIM 268000) is a major cause of impaired vision and blindness. More than 1.5 million patients in the world suffer from RP.1 Its prevalence ranges between 1/3000 to 1/5000 (https://www.uptodate.com/contents/retinitis-pigmentosa-clinical-presentation-and-diagnosis) in the USA and Europe. Increased prevalence is seen in areas where consanguineous marriages are common.1–3 Approximately 70% to 80% of the RP cases are non-syndromic.1 The disease is characterized by a progressive degeneration of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, primarily affecting rods resulting in night blindness and concentric loss of peripheral visual field. Later, cone cells are affected accompanied by impaired color vision and reduced visual acuity.1,4 Among patients with known patterns of inheritance, autosomal dominant inheritance is seen in 30% to 40%, autosomal recessive in 50% to 60%, and X-linked in 5% to 15%.1 Sporadic cases of non-syndromic RP accounts for around 30% to 50% of the patients.5,6

So far, mutations in more than 150 genes associated with RP have been identified with more than 80 genes associated with non-syndromic RP (https://retnet.org).1,4 These mutations are in genes involved in various processes including the phototransduction cascade, canonical and non-canonical visual cycle, interphotoreceptor matrix, and cilia structure and transport.1 Defects in these pathways may result in oxidative, metabolic and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, as well as imbalance in autophagy and proteasome regulation that may eventually cause rod dystrophy.1,4 Moreover, cone death may result from secreted toxins from dying rod photoreceptors and release of cytotoxic factors from microglia.4,7–9

Despite extensive studies on genes involved in retinal integrity and maintenance, many patients with hereditary retinal disorders still lack a defined molecular cause. Here, we report the identification of a novel loss of function variant in a so far not described RP-gene in six affected adults from four families. They had all been diagnosed with late-onset, non-syndromic RP that remained genetically unresolved after retina gene panel analysis.

Material and Methods

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics, Western Norway (IRB# 00001872) (2017/2487). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study thus adhered to the Tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical Examination

Family members were subjected to an extended ophthalmological examination that included Snellen best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurements, Goldmann perimetry, iCare tonometry, slit-lamp examination, and fundoscopy. Color fundus photographs were obtained using a Canon CF-60 Dsi digital fundus camera combined with a Canon EOS-1D Mark II camera (Canon Inc, Tokyo, Japan). Blue-light autofluorescence images and posterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) was captured using a Heidelberg HRA-OCT Spectralis platform (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). A nine-channel full-field electroretinogram (ffERG) was recorded in accordance with the clinical ffERG standards of the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision10 with a Nicolet Viking IV D analysis system (Nicolet Biomedical Instruments, Madison, WI, USA). A bipolar Burian Allen contact lens electrode was used as described previously.11 For the patients from family B, C, and D, color fundus photographs and green-light autofluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss Clarus ultra-widefield fundus camera, while ffERG was obtained using the Diagnosys Espion system with Ganzfeld ColorDome LED stimulator and silver/nylon corneal thread electrodes (DLT plus electrode, Diagnosys LLC). Information on accompanying diseases was obtained from hospital records.

Exome and Sanger Sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood using the fully automated QiaSymphony instrument (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). For exome sequencing, DNA was mechanically sheared on a Covaris M220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris Ltd, Brighton, UK) and the exome captured by hybridization using the SeqCap EZ Medexome probe kit (Cat. no. 07681348001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Library construction was performed using the KAPA HyperPrep kit (Cat. no. 07962363001; Roche). Eight individual libraries were then pooled and sequenced on Illumina NextSeq500 (Illumina Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). Demultiplexing, alignment, and variant calling was performed using our in-house NGS pipeline based on GATK v.3.8, Broad Institute using their recommended best practice settings.12,13 Filtration and annotation were performed using Agilent Alissa Interpret (Agilent Technologies, Winooski, VT, USA). After interpretation and reporting, the variants located in stretches of homozygosity were manually extracted (see enclosed Excel file for a complete list of variants located in homozygous regions). Variants classified as benign or likely benign were discarded along with all intronic variants. The remaining variants were validated by Sanger sequencing using the primers listed in Table 1.

Genealogical Studies

Pedigrees were constructed based on information from digitalized parish records (https://www.digitalarkivet.no/en/parish), local community history books (https://www.genealogi.no/wiki/index.php/Liste_over_digitaliserte_bygdebøker), and MyHeritage (https://www.myheritage.no). These sources are in Norwegian only. The earliest parish records date back to the beginning of the 17th century.

Primary Fibroblast Culture

Primary fibroblast cultures were generated from punch biopsies of skin from individual IV-4 in family A and two healthy controls as described by Bredrup and co-workers.14 The healthy controls were hospital workers of approximately the same age as the affected individual with normal visual function and no family history of eye disease. Cells were cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium High Glucose with sodium pyruvate, without L-Glutamine (Cat. no. ECB7501L, Euroclone, Milan, Italy), supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Cat. no. F7524-500ML; Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA), penicillin 100U/mL streptomycin 100 µg/mL (Cat. no. DE17-602E; Lonza Bioscience, Basel, Switzerland ) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Cat. no. G7513; Sigma-Aldrich Corp.).

N-Deglycosylation Treatment

N-deglycosylation of protein lysates was performed as described previously.15 Acetone precipitated protein cell lysate was dissolved in 25 µL 20mM phosphate buffer pH 7.4 containing 1% sodium dodecyl sulphate and incubated at 100°C for two minutes. Then N-buffer containing 10 U N-glycosidase F (Cat. no. 11365169001; Roche) was added and the mixture incubated for one hour at 37°C. An additional 10 U N-glycosidase F was added, and the samples incubated at 37°C for one more hour. Finally, the sample was precipitated using acetone and the pellet was redissolved in 15 µL of RIPA lysis and extraction buffer containing 4× Laemmli Sample Buffer.

Immunoblot Analysis

After adding Laemmli Sample Buffer (Cat. no. 1610747; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) to the cell lysates, proteins were separated on 4% to 20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free gels (Cat. no. 4568095; Bio-Rad Laboratories) and transferred to Trans-Blot Turbo Mini 0.2 µm Nitrocellulose membranes (Cat. no. 1704158; Bio-Rad Laboratories). EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (5 mL; Cat. no. 12010020; Bio-Rad Laboratories) was used to block the membranes for 30 minutes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer, including rabbit anti-APR3 (Cat. no. PA5-62388; 1/1000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and rabbit anti-GAPDH (Cat. no. G9545; 1/5000 dilution; Sigma-Aldrich Corp.) overnight at 4°C. The membranes were washed 3 × 5 min in Tris-buffered saline solution pH 7.6 with 0.1% Tween 20 and incubated with HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (Cat. no. 7074; 1/2000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for one hour at room temperature. After a final 3- × 5-minute wash, the Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate (Cat. no. 1705062; Bio-Rad Laboratories) and Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Cat. no. 1705061; Bio-Rad Laboratories) were used for developing the membranes. The membranes were scanned using the ChemiDocTM MP (Bio-Rad Laboratories) Imaging System.

Quantitative PCR (TaqMan Analysis)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. no. 74104; Qiagen) following the manufacturer's instructions. Residual DNA was removed by treatment with RNase free DNase I (Cat. no. 25710; Nordic Biosite, Täby, Sweden), followed by RNA cleanup using the same RNeasy Mini Kit. RNA concentration was measured on a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Using 4 µg/µL of total RNA, cDNA was synthesized via SuperScript VILO Master Mix (Cat. no. 11755050; Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's protocol. TaqMan probes for ATRAID transcripts (Cat. no 4331182; Hs00211458_m1 (p458), Cat. no. 4351372; Hs00903942_m1 (p942), and Cat. no. 4351372; Hs00900703_g1 [p703]) and transcripts from the housekeeping gene ACTB (Cat. no. 4326315E; Hs99999903_m1) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. PCR was carried out in a 10 µL reaction volume. Each reaction contained 5 µL of TaqMan Universal Master Mix, 1 µL of cDNA and 250 nM of each TaqMan probe. Cycling was started by an initial two-minute activation at 50°C, followed by denaturation at 95°C for 10 minutes. The reaction was completed by 40 cycles of 15 seconds at 95°C and one minute at 60 °C. The quantitative data were processed using the ΔΔCt method in Excel with ACTB as the reference gene. The PCR reactions were performed in triplicates.

ATRAID−/− and SLC37A3−/− cells

ATRAID and SLC37A3 knockout HEK293T cells, previously reported by Yu and co-workers,16 were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and Gibco Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Cat. no. 15240096; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C and 5% CO2. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using Lonza MycoAlert mycoplasma detection kit (Cat no. LT07-318; Lonza Bioscience) or DAPI staining. All cell lines were free of mycoplasma contamination.

Immunofluorescence Analysis

Glass coverslips (no. 1.5 thickness) were coated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine solution for one hour at 37°C in six-well plates, after which the coating solution was aspirated, and 100,000 HEK 293 cells were plated into each well. One to two days later, cells were rinsed with PBS+ (containing calcium chloride and magnesium chloride) twice and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS+ for 15 minutes at room temperature, washed 3 × 5 minutes with PBS+, and blocked with PBS+ with 1% BSA for one hour at room temperature. Coverslips were stained overnight in a humidity chamber at 4°C using antibodies against LAMP1 (Cat. no. ab24170, Rb; 1:1000 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) and Na+/K+ ATPase (Cat. no. ab7671, Ms; 1:1000 dilution; Abcam) in blocking solution. Cells were then washed 3 × 5 minutes with PBS+ with 1% BSA, stained with anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 555 [Cat. nos. ab150073, ab150109, ab150114; Abcam], at 1:2000 dilution in blocking solution) for one hour at room temperature and mounted on coverslips with Vectashield mounting media (Cat. no. H-1000-10; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). For LysoTracker imaging, cells were incubated with 50 nM LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Cat. no. L7528; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for one hour at 37°C followed by a five-minute incubation in a 1:2000 dilution of stock Hoechst 33342 (Cat. no. H1399; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were then washed twice with PBS and imaged in cell culture media (DMEM with 10% FBS) immediately with a 63×/1.4 oil objective on an inverted Zeiss AxiObserver microscope equipped with a Hamamatsu Flash 4.0 v2 sCMOS camera. Images are single confocal slices of a Z-stack, processed using ImageJ software.

Determination of Lysosomal pH

To determine lysosomal pH in the HEK293T WT, ATRAID KO, and SLC37A3 KO cells, we followed the LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND160 (Cat. no. L7545; Thermo Fisher Scientific), microplate reader-based method described in Ma and co-workers.17 Specifically, cells were plated at 10,000 cells/well of a clear bottom, black-sided 96-well plate and assayed one day later. On the day of the experiment, cells were incubated with 5 µM LysoSensor in regular cell culture media (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics) for five minutes. Cells were then washed twice with PBS and then incubated in 100 µL phenol red-free DMEM with 10% FBS and fluorescence was read immediately via a Perkin Elmer Envision microplate reader with excitation at 300 nm and 380 nm and emission at 440 nm and 540 nm. The ratio of the fluorescence intensity at 440 nm/540 nm was calculated and compared to the pH calibration curve, which altogether reflected the average whole-cell intraorganellar pH labeled by LysoSensor. The pH calibration curve was determined simultaneously in separate cells. After washing twice with PBS, cells were washed again with pH calibration buffers (125 mM KCl, 24 mM NaCl, 10 µM Monensin, 25 mM HEPES (pH 7-7.5) or 25 mM MES (pH 3.5-6.5)), with pH buffers in 0.5 pH increments from pH 3.5-7.5. Cells were then imaged in 100 uL of the pH buffer. All readings for samples and pH calibrations were done in triplicate wells.

Dextran Uptake

Coverslips with live HEK 293 cells were incubated overnight in a humidity chamber at 4°C with Dextran-Alexa Fluor 488, 10000 MW, Anionic (Cat. no. D22910; 1:1000 in cell culture medium; Invitrogen). Cells were then washed twice for five minutes with PBS and chased with fresh media for two hours at room temperature and mounted on coverslips with Invitrogen SlowFade Diamond Antifade mountant with DAPI (Cat. no. S36964; Invitrogen). Cells were imaged with a 63×/1.4 oil objective on an inverted Leica Stellaris 5 microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with high-sensitivity Power HyD S detectors. Images are max projections of a Z-stack, processed using Fiji software.

Flow Cytometry

A total of 100,000 HEK293 cells were plated into each well of a 12-well plate. Cells were incubated with Dextran-AlexaFluor488, 10000MW, Anionic (1:1000 in cell culture medium; Cat. no. D22910; Invitrogen) overnight in a humidity chamber at 4°C or with 50 nM LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Cat. no. L7528; Invitrogen) for one hour at 37°C. Cells were then washed with PBS+, trypsinized, and resuspended in Hanks buffered salt solution with 5mM EDTA and stained with either SYTOX Green (Cat. no. S34860; Invitrogen) or Blue dead cell stain (Cat. no. S34857; Invitrogen) for 15 minutes at 37°C. Cells were analyzed with the Attune NxT Flow Cytometer. The cell counts were normalized using FlowJo software.

Results

Family A was first examined. The parents of the proband (V-4) and her affected brother (V-1) are second cousins (Fig. 1). The proband was first seen at 40 years of age. She reported a two-year history of reduced visual acuity and impaired night vision. Snellen BCVA was right eye (RE) 0: 0.5+, left eye (LE) +0,5, −0.75/20°: 0,8+. Goldmann perimetry (I4e) showed a pericentral ring scotoma and constriction of the superior visual fields in both eyes (Fig. 2). Intraocular pressure was normal; RE 12 mm Hg, LE 13 mmHg. Slit lamp examination of the anterior segment was normal. Fundoscopy revealed mild waxy pallor of the optic discs and multiple small, white dots organized in an almost striated manner around the optic discs and along the temporal arcades. There was fine granularity of the foveal RPE and loss of normal macular reflexes (Fig. 2). In the retinal mid periphery, outer retinal atrophy was seen with greyish flecks and narrowing of the arterioles, mottled RPE atrophy and scattered bone spicule-like pigmented lesions. The retina in the far periphery had a more normal appearance (Fig. 2). A 30° blue light fundus autofluorescence imaging (Fig. 2) demonstrated outer retinal and RPE atrophy in the peripheral macula with mottled hypo-and hyper-autofluorescence, and in the para- and perifoveal region, hyper-autofluorescence, consistent with ongoing retinal degeneration. A ring of high-density autofluorescence surrounded the fovea. Within this area the outer retina was better preserved (Fig. 2). OCT demonstrated a visible ellipsoid zone in the fovea with irregularity and diffuse hyperreflectivity in the foveolar region. In addition, a thin epiretinal membrane and mild foveal edema confined to the inner nuclear layer was seen. There was a gradual loss of outer retinal layers towards the periphery of the macula (Fig. 2). Full-field ERG10,11 displayed a marked attenuation and delay of rod responses and a milder attenuation and delay of the cone responses (Fig. 2) consistent with rod-cone dystrophy (RP). She was otherwise healthy and had normal bone density measurements.

The proband's brother (V-1) presented at 48 years of age with a three-year history of increasing nyctalopia, photopsias and photosensitivity, mild vision loss and clinical and electrophysiological findings consistent with RP, but with more extensive retinal degeneration than his younger sister (Fig. 2). The mother (IV-2) and the asymptomatic, youngest sister (V-5) had normal visual function.

Retina gene panel analysis (RetNet, www.retnet.org) of the proband V-4 did not reveal any pathogenic variants in genes known to be associated with RP. After obtaining written informed consent, whole exome sequencing was performed. A homozygous single nucleotide insertion in the coding region of the All-Trans Retinoic Acid Induced Differentiation Factor gene (ATRAID) (NM_001170795:c.120dup, p.Ser41Glufs*13) was identified and was also found biallelic by Sanger sequencing in her affected brother (V-1). The mother was a heterozygous carrier of the same variant, whereas the youngest healthy sister did not carry the variant.

Subsequently, we included ATRAID in retina gene panel analysis of individuals with late-onset, non-syndromic retinitis pigmentosa. An identical biallelic ATRAID variant was then detected by whole exome sequencing in four additional individuals coming from three different non-consanguineous families (II-3, family B, II-1, family C, and II-2 and II-3, family D; Fig. 1). The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing in all affected individuals. The complete list of variants located in homozygous regions of one of the individuals is provided in Supplementary Material.

Common features of all six affected individuals are a late-onset, non-syndromal, rod-cone dystrophy with mid-peripheral outer retinal degeneration, relatively good visual acuity (Snellen BCVA >0.5) and color vision at diagnosis, and a variable degree of retinal gliosis, epiretinal macular fibrosis and cystoid macular edema (CME). Four of the six affected individuals also exhibited localized, diffuse hyperreflectivity of the outer nuclear layer just above the external limiting membrane in the foveola on OCT. In gnomAD v.4.0.1, the variant is reported in a heterozygous state in one individual of European, non-Finnish ancestry (1/1,180,010 alleles). Several other loss-of-function variants in ATRAID are listed, but none have been identified in a homozygous state. Consequently, the pLi = 0 and the observed/expected ratio = 0.89. Haploinsufficiency is therefore not a likely disease mechanism for ATRAID; rather, an autosomal recessive inheritance is anticipated.

Estimation of the carrier frequency in the population was performed by running a query for the occurrence of the variant's genomic position in all samples of patients referred to Department of Medical Genetics, Haukeland University Hospital for exome sequencing for many different medical conditions. Screening of 13,989 in-house exomes revealed 15 individuals heterozygous for the ATRAID variant suggesting an allele frequency of approximately 0.0005 or 1/2000 in the Norwegian population.

We also determined the size of the region of homozygosity (ROH) surrounding the variant by querying the whole exome vcf for runs of homozygous or heterozygous genotypes. The size of the region was determined from the first to the last confident homozygous variant by manual inspection of the bam file. In individual V-4 from family A, the variant was in a 12.3 Mb ROH as expected from the close relationship between the parents. In individual II-3 from family B it was in a 5.9 Mb ROH, in individual II-1 from family C in a 9.7 Mb ROH, and in individual II-3 from family D, the variant was in a 4 Mb ROH, suggesting that the parents are more distantly related.

All affected individuals were of Norwegian ancestry and came from families living in the south-western part of Norway. We therefore looked for common ancestors by performing genealogical studies. Several family branches could be traced back to the beginning of the 17th century, but we were unable to find any common ancestors for all families. We were also unable to find common ancestors for the parents of the affected individuals except for family A where the parents are second cousins. We did, however, find some common ancestors between pairs of families from 7 to 11 generations back. These common ancestors were all born in the 17th century. Among others, individuals IV-1 and IV-2 in family A shared a common ancestor with I-1 in family D eight and nine generations back, respectively. Individual IV-1 in family A shared a common ancestor with I-2 in family D nine generations back for both families. Individual I-2 in family B shared two common ancestors with individual I-1 in family C 10 and 11 generations back in family B and eight and nine generations back in family C. Finally, we identified four common ancestors shared between individual I-1 in family C and individual I-2 in family D from seven to 11 generations back in both families.

The effect of the insertion at the protein level was predicted to result in a change in the reading frame after amino acid 41, leading to a stop codon 13 codons downstream, suggesting a total loss of function. We examined whole cell lysates of primary fibroblasts from two healthy and one affected individual by immunoblot analysis. In previous work, we have shown that native ATRAID is glycosylated and migrates as a fuzzy band with a higher molecular weight than that of the predicted core protein.15 After N-deglycosylation by treatment with N-glycosidase F, a band with the expected mw of almost 22 kDa was seen in extracts from healthy individuals. No band was detected in the lysates from the affected individual, showing that the variant results in complete loss of the ATRAID protein (Fig. 4).

Two ATRAID transcripts are listed in NCBI, NM_001170795.4 and NM_016085.5. NM_001170795.4 encodes full-length ATRAID (isoform C) whereas NM_016085.5 encodes a truncated form of ATRAID (isoform A). Isoform A is usually not detected since it is rapidly degraded by proteasomes.15 For qPCR analysis of fibroblast RNA from healthy and affected individuals, we used three different assays. Assay p458 will detect both transcripts, whereas p703 detects only NM_016085.5. Assay p942 will detect NM_001170795.4. The levels detected by assay p703 were nearly equal in samples from healthy and affected individuals indicating that NM_016085.5 was present at similar levels in both samples. This is as expected since the transcriptional start site of NM_016085.5 is located 3′ to the position of the variant. Lower levels were seen in affected individuals in assays p458 and p942, indicating that NM_001170795.4 may be degraded in affected individuals, possibly by nonsense mediated decay.18 Slightly higher levels were detected in assay p458 reflecting the contribution of NM_016085.5 (Fig. 4).

We have previously found that ATRAID and SLC37A3 form a complex that localizes to lysosomes and is required for releasing N-bisphosphonates (N-BPs) that have trafficked to lysosomes through fluid-phase endocytosis into the cytosol.16,19 To understand how ATRAID may affect endocytic and lysosomal trafficking, we used ATRAID and SLC37A3 knockout (KO) HEK293T cells, previously published by Surface and co-workers19 and Yu and co-workers.16 In both KO cell lines, we observed increased lysosomal abundance via immunofluorescence of lysosomal markers (Fig. 5A). To quantify this across a larger population of cells (∼20,000 cells/sample), we utilized LysoTrackerRed staining and detection via flow cytometry (Fig. 5B), confirmed by microscopy (Fig. 5C). Although lysosomal staining was increased in the KO cell lines, lysosomal pH remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). When we examined trafficking of dextran in KO cell lines, we also observed increased dextran staining in KO cells compared to WT via both microscopy and quantification by flow cytometry across a larger population of cells (∼20,000 cells/sample) (Figs. 5E, 5F), suggesting that ATRAID and SLC37A3 play a role not only in lysosomal function but also in the regulation of endocytic trafficking.

Discussion

We have identified a biallelic loss of function variant in ATRAID in six individuals from four Norwegian families that is associated with a late onset form of retinitis pigmentosa. While most symptoms1,4,5 and clinical findings1,20 were typical for RP, pigmented bony spicules were less prominent and the age at onset was relatively late ranging from 31 to 73 years of age. In a Norwegian cohort of retinitis pigmentosa patients, self-reported average (median) age at onset of RP was 24,4 (20) years of age.21 In Japanese patients with autosomal recessive RP, the average age at diagnosis was 36,2 years (median age 40 years).22

Based on data from in-house exome sequencing, we estimated an allele frequency of the ATRAID variant to approximately 1/2000 in the Norwegian population. Since most of the individuals subjected to exome sequencing came from Western Norway, this could represent an overestimation due to geographical clustering. It is likely, however, that there will be additional individuals with RP caused by loss of ATRAID in the Norwegian population.

Although short (<4 Mb), random ROHs are commonly found in outbred populations,23 the length of the ROHs surrounding the ATRAID variant suggested that the parents of the affected individuals in families B, C, and D were distantly related. We were unable, however, to find a common ancestor in genealogical studies going back to the beginning of the 17th century which corresponds to around 12 generations. The presence of the same variant surrounded by relatively long ROHs in all affected individuals suggests a founder effect.

ATRAID is expressed in the retina (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000138085-ATRAID/tissue) and in RPE cell lines. Among others it is localized in lysosomes and recycling endosomes.15 ATRAID has been shown to interact with SLC37A3.16,19 Interestingly, variants in SLC37A3 have recently been associated with RP.24–26 The clinical features such as age at onset and the pattern of retinal degeneration resemble those observed in patients with loss of ATRAID. Thus a similar phenotype is seen when either of the two interaction partners is missing.

Accumulation of lysosomes and increased levels of dextran in dextran uptake assays were seen in both ATRAID−/− and SLC37A3−/− HEK293T cells. These findings suggest an enhanced, possibly compensatory expansion of the lysosomal compartment that could reflect impaired endocytic trafficking, defective lysosomal degradation, or compromised export of degradation products from lysosomes.27 This is consistent with previous reports implicating ATRAID and SLC37A3 in endocytic trafficking and in the uptake and release of compounds to and from lysosomes.15,16,19 Although HEK293T cells differ functionally from retinal cells, the retina – particularly the RPE cells - is highly susceptible to disruptions in endocytic trafficking and lysosomal function.28,29 RPE cells are specialized phagocytes that clear and degrade shed photoreceptor outer segments and recycle their components back to photoreceptors.28,29 Consistently, pathogenic variants in genes encoding proteins involved in photoreceptor outer segment uptake, intracellular transport, and lysosomal uptake and degradation are a common cause of retinitis pigmentosa in humans.30–33

Conclusions

In six affected individuals from four families, we have identified a novel loss of function variant in ATRAID associated with autosomal recessive, late-onset, non-syndromic RP with clinical features resembling those seen in loss of SLC37A3. ATRAID has been shown to interact with SLC37A3. In both ATRAID−/− and SLC37A3−/− cells, we observed increased numbers of lysosomes and increased levels of dextran in dextran uptake assay suggesting that loss of ATRAID and subsequent loss of ATRAID - SLC37A3 interaction may result in lysosomal dysfunction and dysregulation of endocytic trafficking pathways in RPE cells that disturb the process of degradation and/or recycling in these cells. This could lead to degradation of RPE cells and subsequently also of photoreceptors resulting in the development of retinitis pigmentosa.
