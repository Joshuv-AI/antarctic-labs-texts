STING-ERO1 signaling exacerbates PARylation-mediated parthanatos in sepsis
Authors: Xuanheng Li, Qianzheng Zhou, Xufei Zhang, Yangguang Li, Yiyu Yang, Jie Wu, Qinjie Liu, Peizhao Liu, Haiyang Jiang, Juanhan Liu, Liting Deng, Zexing Lin, Cunxia Wu, Lei Wu, Yun Zhao, Jianan Ren, Xiuwen Wu

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by uncontrolled inflammation, oxidative stress, and aberrant cell death. Although the STING pathway is well established as a central mediator of innate immunity, its functions beyond interferon signaling remain largely undefined. Here, we investigated a noncanonical STING–ERO1–PARP1 axis that drives oxidative damage and Parthanatos during sepsis.MethodsA combination of in vivo and in vitro approaches was employed to dissect the STING–ERO1–PARP1 signaling cascade. A sepsis model was established via cecal ligation and puncture, and various genetic knockout mice (STING⁻/⁻, cGAS⁻/⁻, and IFNAR1⁻/⁻) were used to assess pathway specificity. In vitro studies in RAW264.7 and iBMDM cells involved STING agonist stimulation combined with genetic knockouts (STING⁻/⁻, TBK1⁻/⁻), targeted inhibitors (PJ34, EN460, NAC), and siRNA transfection to dissect signaling mechanisms. Molecular and cellular responses were evaluated by immunoblotting, immunofluorescence, ELISA, TUNEL staining, and ROS detection. Cell death, cytokine expression, and tissue injury were quantified via standard assays.Resultswe demonstrated that the STING signaling axis drives parthanatos and intestinal injury through endoplasmic reticulum (ER) oxidative stress. Mechanistically, STING directly interacts with ER oxidoreductase 1 (ERO1), inducing oxidative stress and cytosolic ROS accumulation, which triggers DNA damage, PARP1 hyperactivation, and excessive PARylation. This cascade promotes the nuclear translocation of apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) and Parthanatos. Notably, this mechanism occurred independently of the canonical cGAS–TBK1–interferon axis. Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of STING, ERO1, or PARP1 significantly reduces ROS levels, PARylation, and intestinal injury.ConclusionsWe identified a novel STING–ERO1–PARP1 signaling cascade that links innate immune sensing to redox dysregulation and cell death, providing new therapeutic targets for mitigating organ injury in sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02791-6.

Background

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide [1, 2]. Despite advances in supportive care, therapeutic options targeting the core pathological mechanisms of sepsis-induced organ injury, particularly the intricate interplay between dysregulated inflammation and cell death pathways [3, 4], are strikingly limited. A deeper understanding of the molecular drivers orchestrating the transition from systemic inflammation to irreversible cellular death in vital organs is paramount for the development of novel [5, 6], effective interventions.

The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway serves as a crucial cytosolic sensor for cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs), bridging innate immune recognition of pathogens or cellular damage to the production of type I interferon (IFN) and pro-inflammatory cytokines [7, 8]. While essential for host defense, persistent or excessive STING activation is increasingly recognized as a detrimental force in sterile inflammation and infection-associated pathologies, including sepsis [9]. Beyond its canonical IFN-centric signaling, emerging evidence implicates STING in modulating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress responses [10, 11], a pivotal yet underexplored facet linking immune hyperactivation to organellar dysfunction and cellular damage.

The ER, a central hub for protein folding, lipid synthesis, and calcium storage, is highly sensitive to perturbations in cellular redox balance [12]. Endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase 1 (ERO1) is a key ER-resident oxidase that facilitates disulfide bond formation during protein folding, inherently generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a byproduct [13]. Under physiological conditions, this activity is tightly regulated. However, under sustained ER stress, dysregulated ERO1 activity can drive pathological ER oxidative stress [14], overwhelming local antioxidant defenses and contributing to protein misfolding, calcium dysregulation, and the activation of maladaptive signaling cascades, ultimately threatening cell survival [15, 16].

While apoptosis and necrosis are well-characterized forms of cell death in sepsis, parthanatos represents a distinct, caspase-independent programmed necrotic pathway critically implicated in inflammatory and oxidative injury [17]. This death modality is initiated by hyperactivation of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), typically in response to severe DNA damage or oxidative stress. Excessive PARP1 activity depletes cellular NAD⁺ and ATP [18], leading to aberrant accumulation of poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) [19] and mitochondrial depolarization, the release of mitochondrial apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF), and its translocation to the nucleus with macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) [20], This ultimately leading to chromatin DNA fragmentation and parthanatos [21, 22]. Its role in sepsis-induced organ damage, particularly in the context of ER-derived oxidative stress, warrants further elucidation.

Here, we demonstrate that aberrant STING activation in sepsis serves as the primary trigger for hyperactivation of PARP1, culminating in parthanatos. Mechanistically, the interaction of STING with ERO1 in the ER induces severe oxidative stress, promoting DNA damage and excessive PAR production. This, in turn, triggers the nuclear translocation of AIF released from damaged mitochondria, leading to parthanatos. Crucially, we identified this ERO1-mediated ER oxidative stress as the key driver linking STING to PARP1 hyperactivation. Our study identifies ERO1 as a novel effector of STING signaling and defines a mechanistic cascade connecting innate immune sensing, endoplasmic reticulum–derived oxidative stress, and Parthanatos in sepsis progression. This axis represents a highly promising new therapeutic target for alleviating organ dysfunction in this lethal syndrome.

Methods

Human sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients with intra-abdominal infections at the Department of General Surgery, Jinling Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University. The collection of blood samples was approved by the Institutional Review Board Ethics Committee at Jinling Hospital (2018NZGKJ-022). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to blood collection.

Mice and sepsis model

All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. C57BL/6 wild-type (WT), STING⁻/⁻, cGAS⁻/⁻, and IFNAR1⁻/⁻ mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. and Huachuang Sino. Macrophage-specific Sting1 conditional knockout mice (STINGLysM⁻/⁻) and intestinal epithelial-specific Sting1 conditional knockout mice (STINGVil⁻/⁻) were obtained from the Transgenic Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. All animal experiments complied with ethical regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2012028).

The sepsis model was established via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery as previously described [9]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and subjected to laparotomy, and the cecum was ligated with silk sutures and punctured twice with a 21-gauge needle. Fecal contents were gently extruded, the cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was sutured. In sham-operated control mice, the cecum was exposed but neither ligated nor punctured. Intestinal and lung tissues were collected at specified time points for subsequent experiments. Survival was monitored for up to 14 days. PJ34 was administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg.

Cell culture and reagents

RAW264.7 (TIB-71, ATCC), iBMDM (M3-1001, Cyagen), HEK293T (CRL-3216, ATCC), RAW-Lucia™ ISG cells (rawl-isg, InvivoGen), RAW-Lucia™ ISG-KO-STING cells (rawl-kostg, InvivoGen), and RAW-Lucia™ ISG-KO-TBK1 cells (rawl-kotbk, InvivoGen) were cultured in DMEM medium (G4512, Servicebio), THP-1 cells (TIB-202, ATCC) were cultured in RPMI-160 medium (KGL1503-500, KeyGEN), all media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141, Gibco) and 100 units/mL penicillin/streptomycin (15140122, Gibco), at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The Cells were stimulated with DMXAA (25–50 µg/ml) or ADU-S100 (ADU, 10 µM) for the specified durations. For the inhibitor studies, the cells were pretreated for 1 h with PJ34 (10 mM), 3-Aminobenzamide (3AB, 50 mM), Olaparib (5 µM), N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC, 5 mM), Mitoquinone mesylate (mitoQ, 5 µM), GSK2795039 (10 µM), GLX351322 (10 µM), or EN460 (10 µM) prior to stimulation. DMXAA (HY-10964), ADU-S100 (HY-12885B), PJ34 (HY-13688 A), 3-Aminobenzamide (HY-12022), Olaparib (HY-10162), N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (HY-B0215), Mitoquinone mesylate (HY-100116 A), GSK2795039 (HY-18950), GLX351322 (HY-100111), and EN460 (HY-12837) were purchased from MedChemExpress.

Plasmid transfection and siRNA transfection

The Flag-STING plasmid was provided by Sino Biological. The following STING fragments were subcloned and inserted into the pCMV3-C-Flag vector: ΔTM (aa 147–379), ΔLBD (aa 1-146 + 341–379), and ΔCTT (aa 1-340) [9]. The Myc-ERO1 construct was provided by GENERAL BIOL (Chuzhou, China). The constructs were transiently transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000. PARP1 siRNAs used in this study were synthesized by GENERAL BIOL (Chuzhou, China), and ERO1 siRNAs were synthesized by Hippo Bio (Huzhou, China). The cells were transfected with siRNAs via Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L3000150, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences of the siRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot and immunoprecipitation

The cells were lysed in cell lysis buffer (P0013B, P0013, Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChem Express). The protein concentrations of the extracts were determined via the BCA method (Beyotime). Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation experiments were performed as previously described [23]. Antibodies used were as follows: STING (19851-1-AP, Proteintech), p-STING (72971, Cell Signaling Technology), p-TBK1 (5483, Cell Signaling Technology), TBK1 (38066, Cell Signaling Technology), p-IRF3 (29047, Cell Signaling Technology), IRF3 (4302, Cell Signaling Technology), PARP1 (9532, Cell Signaling Technology), AIF (sc-13116, Santa Cruz), pADPr (4335-MC-100, R&D Systems), Poly ADP-ribose (ALX-804-220-R100, ENZO Life Sciences), MIF (A11231, ABclonal), γH2AX (AP0687, ABclonal), β-actin (AC026, ABclonal), GAPDH (A19056, ABclonal), LC3 (A19665, ABclonal), Lamin B1 (AC057, ABclonal), ERO1 (67416-1-Ig, Proteintech), ERO1 (ab17716, Abcam), FLAG-tag (AE063, ABclonal; MA1-91878, Invitrogen), Myc-Tag (AE010, ABclonal; AE070, ABclonal).

Histological analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and mounted. Microscopic images were acquired for subsequent analysis. The extent of intestinal damage was assessed via Chiu’s scoring system [24], while the degree of lung tissue damage was evaluated with the lung injury score [25].

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissue or cell samples via RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA via the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (R323-01, Vazyme). cDNA from each sample was amplified using specific primers with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme). Data were normalized to GAPDH and fold changes in mRNA levels were calculated. The sequences of primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Commercially available ELISA kits were used to measure the concentrations of PARP1 (K4218-100, BioVision), D-Lactate (K667-100, BioVision), STING (ab315058, Abcam), AIF (SEB064HU, Cloud-Clone Corp), iFABP9 (JL12370, Jonln Bio), MIF (JL11770, Jonln Bio), and IFNβ (RK01630, ABclonal) in plasma samples according to each manufacturer’s instructions.

Evans Blue assay

Vascular permeability was assessed using Evans Blue (EB) extravasation [26, 27]. Briefly, 2% EB (2 mL/kg) was intravenously injected into anesthetized mice 1 h prior to transcardial perfusion with saline. Harvested tissues were homogenized in 50% trichloroacetic acid (1 mL) and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 620 nm. EB content was quantified using a standard curve.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed via a commercial CCK-8 kit (HY-K0301, MedChemExpress). After different stimulants or treatments, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1–4 h. The absorbance was measured via a microplate reader.

LDH assay

Cell death levels were measured via a commercial Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Activity Assay Kit (BC0685, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions after different stimuli or treatments. The released LDH was normalized to the total LDH content measured in indicator cell samples permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100.

Cell viability and cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability and cytotoxicity were assessed via a commercial Calcein-AM/PI Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (C2015, Beyotime). After different stimuli or treatments, cells were stained simultaneously with Calcein-AM (calcein) and PI (propidium iodide) following the kit protocol, and visualized via fluorescence microscopy.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated from cultured cells after different stimuli or treatments using a commercial Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0028, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, utilizing differential centrifugation.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were detected via a commercial Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (S0033, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions after different stimuli or treatments, via either fluorescence microscopy or a microplate reader.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining

TUNEL staining of intestinal tissue sections was performed via a commercial TUNEL Detection Kit (G1504, Servicebio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were acquired via fluorescence microscopy to assess the location and level of cell death within the intestinal tissue.

Immunofluorescence

Cells grown on glass coverslips were stimulated as indicated, fixed with immunostaining fixative (P0098, Beyotime), permeabilized with immunostaining permeabilization buffer (P0097, Beyotime), and blocked with immunostaining blocking buffer (P0260, Beyotime). Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γH2AX, AIF, STING, or ERO1. After being washed, cells were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio). For co-localization studies, cells were co-stained for STING and ERO1. Images were acquired via a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Statistical analysis

Comprehensive statistical analyses were performed via advanced features in GraphPad Prism 9 software. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were compared using two-tailed Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used for correlation studies. Survival differences were analyzed via the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Sepsis induces PARP1 overactivation, leading to intestinal barrier damage and inflammation

Our previous studies demonstrated that sepsis induces inflammation and tissue damage [9, 28, 29]. To further explore the progression of intestinal injury at different stages of sepsis, we established a time-gradient sepsis model for systematic exploration. Histopathological analysis revealed no significant villus damage in 6-hour septic mice compared to controls. By 12 h, focal vacuolization appeared at villus tips without epithelial barrier compromise, whereas 24-hour sepsis resulted in severe intestinal damage, characterized by villus disintegration, epithelial-subepithelial detachment, and extensive epithelial necrosis (Fig. 1A). Small intestinal injury scores were positively correlated with modeling duration (Fig. 1B). qPCR detected pronounced IL-6 upregulation at all timepoints, peaking at 6 h before gradual attenuation (Fig. 1C), indicating early inflammatory priming preceding barrier dysfunction. Western blot analysis revealed a time-dependent upregulation of PARP1 in septic mice, concomitant with progressively elevated PAR modification levels (Fig. 1D, E). Notably, MIF expression was significantly increased at 24 h (Fig.1F), whereas AIF remained unaltered (Fig. 1G). We further investigated tissue injury and signaling activation at various time points post-CLP. The results showed that tissue damage and PARP1/PAR signaling were markedly activated at 24 h, then gradually declined thereafter. This indicates that the 24-hour time point is critical for exploring the correlation between PARP1/PAR signaling and tissue injury (Supplementary Fig. 1A-D). These data collectively suggest that PARP1/PAR signaling plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of sepsis-associated intestinal injury.

Emerging evidence implicates aberrant PARP1 activation in diverse tissue and organ injuries [30, 31]. However, the mechanistic role of PARP1 in sepsis-induced intestinal damage remains poorly understood. To investigate this, we enrolled intra-abdominal infection patients and analyzed systemic biomarkers of PARP1 signaling and intestinal injury, including intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) and D-lactate [32]. Notably, circulating PARP1 levels were significantly positively correlated with both I-FABP and D-lactate levels (Fig.1H, I), suggesting a potential link between PARP1 activation and intestinal injury. Mechanistically, PARP1 activation triggers the cytoplasmic-to-nuclear translocation of AIF and MIF, culminating in parthanatos. Consistent with these findings, AIF and MIF levels correlated strongly with intestinal injury markers (Fig.1H, I), further supporting their involvement in the pathogenic cascade.

Our prior work identified STING as a critical mediator of intestinal barrier dysfunction in innate immunity [9, 28]. Strikingly, circulating STING levels were positively correlated with PARP1, AIF, and MIF (Fig. 1J), suggesting that there is crosstalk between STING signaling and PARP1-mediated parthanatos during septic intestinal injury. In contrast, interferon-β (IFN-β) levels were not significantly associated with these markers (Fig. 1K), highlighting pathway specificity. Consequently, a significant positive correlation exists between the STING signaling pathway and PARP1-dependent cell death in infection-induced intestinal injury, although the underlying immunomodulatory molecular mechanisms linking STING and PARP1 require further investigation.

STING knockout ameliorates sepsis-induced PARP1 activation and intestinal injury

STING plays a critical role in innate immunity. Our previous studies demonstrated that STING knockout (STING⁻/⁻) reduces ischemia-reperfusion injury, necroptosis or ferroptosis, and protects intestinal epithelial barrier function [9, 28, 29, 32]. Here, we investigated the role of STING in sepsis-induced PARP1 activation and intestinal injury. Wild-type (WT) mice presented significant intestinal villus damage, concomitant with lung injury and inflammatory cell infiltration following sepsis induction. Compared with WT mice, STING⁻/⁻ mice exhibited markedly ameliorated intestinal and lung injury (Fig. 2A-C, Supplementary Fig. 1E). TUNEL staining revealed significantly reduced intestinal epithelial cell death and improved intestinal barrier function in STING⁻/⁻ mice, relative to the substantial epithelial cell death and barrier disruption observed in WT septic mice (Fig. 2B). Immunofluorescence analysis of the intestinal epithelial tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin revealed a significant decrease following CLP. In contrast, this decline was remarkably restored in STING-knockout mice, indicating the pivotal role of STING in mediating intestinal epithelial injury (Supplementary Fig. 1F). With respect to intestinal inflammation, sepsis induced significant expression of the inflammatory cytokine IL-6, which was significantly suppressed by STING deficiency (Fig.2D). These results indicate that STING knockout ameliorates intestinal injury.

To further investigate whether the STING-modulated septic injury is associated with the PARP1 signaling pathway, we compared PARP1 signaling levels in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-treated WT and STING⁻/⁻ mice. STING knockout suppressed sepsis-induced PARP1 hyperactivation and reduced its poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation (PARylation) level (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that STING acts as an upstream signaling molecule that regulates PARP1 pathway activation and sepsis-induced intestinal injury.

To evaluate the specific roles of STING in intestinal macrophages and epithelial cells, we generated conditional STING knockout mice via Cre/loxP technology, which targets macrophages (STINGLysM⁻/⁻) or intestinal epithelial cells (STINGVil⁻/⁻) [9]. Compared with wild-type control mice, both STINGLysM⁻/⁻ and STINGVil⁻/⁻ mice presented significantly reduced intestinal and lung tissue injury post-sepsis (Fig. 2F-H, Supplementary Fig. 1G-H). Analysis of inflammatory cytokine expression further revealed that both conditional knockout mice reduced the expression of multiple inflammatory cytokines in intestinal tissue (Fig. 2I-J). Furthermore, both STINGLysM⁻/⁻ and STINGVil⁻/⁻ mice significantly suppressed sepsis-induced PARP1 hyperactivation and reduced its PARylation level. Additionally, these mice also exhibited significant inhibition of MIF expression (Fig. 2K-L). These results demonstrate that STING in both macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells plays crucial roles in mediating PARP1 activation, excessive inflammation, and tissue injury. Therefore, the STING pathway is critically important for the pathogenesis of sepsis.

STING activation induces PARP1-dependent cell death

To further explore the molecular mechanism linking STING and PARP1, we stimulated RAW264.7 cells with two classic STING agonists, DMXAA and ADU-S100 (ADU). Western blot analysis revealed that both agonists significantly activated the STING signaling pathway within 1 h (Fig.3A). Concurrently, STING activation markedly increased the levels of poly ADP-ribosylated proteins (Fig.3B), indicating hyperactivation of PARylation in macrophages following STING stimulation.

We next examined cytokine profiles upon STING activation. DMXAA treatment significantly increased the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFNβ, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, CCL2, and STAT1, while decreasing the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 3C). These findings demonstrate that STING activation enhances pro-inflammatory responses. Furthermore, STING activation increased its own mRNA level, suggesting potential amplification of inflammation. However, the mRNA levels of AIF and MIF remained unchanged within 6 h (Fig. 3C), indicating that STING does not directly regulate their transcriptional upregulation.

Previous studies have indicated that DNA damage from cellular stress or injury can activate PARylation [33]. To determine the mechanism of STING-induced PARylation, we stimulated RAW264.7 cells with DMXAA. This rapidly activated the STING-TBK1 pathway within 1 h, and significantly increased the levels of γH2AX, a nuclear DNA damage marker. γH2AX levels peaked at 3 h post-stimulation and subsequently declined (Fig. 3D), demonstrating a direct association between STING activation and nuclear DNA3damage. Consistent with these findings, PARylation was rapidly initiated following DNA damage, with significantly elevated expression levels (Fig. 3D). Fluorescence imaging confirmed a marked increase in γH2AX fluorescence intensity in nuclei after DMXAA treatment (Fig. 3E). We further stimulated cells with the TLR4 agonist LPS and the TLR3 agonist poly(I: C). The results showed that neither LPS nor poly(I: C) treatment induced significant DNA damage, and LPS treatment did not result in a significant increase in PAR levels. This contrasts sharply with the pronounced DNA damage and excessive PAR activation observed following STING activation (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). These results indicate that STING activation induces hyperactivation of PARylation via DNA damage. PARylation serves as a critical stress signal and determinant of cell fate, with excessive ADP-ribosylation ultimately driving cell death [22]. Given that STING activation significantly induces DNA damage and elevates PARylation levels, we hypothesized that STING is involved in PARP1-dependent cell death signaling. Cytotoxicity assays revealed a progressive increase in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into the supernatant of STING-activated cells. Cell viability assays also revealed a significant time-dependent decrease in viability (Fig. 3F), confirming substantial cell death. Western blot analysis revealed largely unchanged cytoplasmic AIF levels, but significantly increased nuclear AIF protein levels at 12 h post-stimulation (Fig. 3G, H). Fluorescence imaging further revealed elevated nuclear AIF levels following DMXAA treatment (Fig.3I). Cytosolic AIF binds to MIF and translocates to the nucleus, leading to genomic DNA fragmentation and cell death [34]. We further examined the cytoplasmic and nuclear distribution of MIF. The results showed that cytoplasmic MIF levels remained largely unchanged after stimulation, whereas nuclear MIF protein levels were markedly increased at 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 2C–D). These findings demonstrate that STING activation elevates ADP-ribosylation levels, induces AIF and MIF nuclear translocation, and triggers PARP1-dependent cell death.

STING-dependent PAR production occurs via a non-canonical pathway

Given that STING activation increases PAR modification, we investigated the underlying mechanism using STING-knockout (STING⁻/⁻) and TBK1-knockout (TBK1⁻/⁻) RAW264.7 cells. STING activation elevates PAR levels, induces nuclear DNA damage, and activates downstream TBK1 and IRF3. DMXAA treatment failed to induce TBK1 or IRF3 phosphorylation, PAR elevation, or nuclear DNA damage in STING⁻/⁻ cells. In contrast, DMXAA stimulation of TBK1⁻/⁻ cells, which are unable to activate STING or downstream IRF3, still triggered a significant increase in PAR levels (Fig. 4A). These findings indicate that STING activation promotes ADP-ribosylation independently of TBK1.

We further assessed the roles of STING and TBK1 in STING-activation-induced cell death. Calcein/PI immunofluorescence assays for cell viability and cytotoxicity revealed that STING knockout significantly suppressed PAR elevation and reduced cell death, which was consistent with the PAR levels. Although TBK1 knockout did not significantly inhibit PAR production and only slightly reduced cell death, substantial cell death still occurred (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that STING-activation-induced cell death results primarily from PAR hyperactivation. Further analysis revealed that TBK1 knockout failed to significantly inhibit the nuclear translocation of AIF, as confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4C, E). Collectively, these results implicate PAR as a key mediator of STING-regulated parthanatos.

Emerging evidence links ROS to nuclear DNA damage and subsequent ADP-ribosylation activation [35]. As STING is a key innate immune protein that is also involved in oxidative stress regulation [36], we explored its role in ROS generation during STING activation. Using H2DCFDA as a fluorescent probe for intracellular ROS, we observed that STING knockout significantly reduced basal ROS levels (Fig. 4F, G). Spectrophotometric analysis further quantified the relative intracellular ROS levels. Compared with controls, STING knockout markedly ameliorated cellular oxidative stress, whereas TBK1 knockout only partially reduced ROS levels, which remained significantly higher than those in STING⁻/⁻ cells (Fig.4H).

To investigate the role of PARP1 in STING-mediated oxidative stress regulation, cells were treated with the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34. PJ34 treatment did not attenuate ROS generation within 3 h but significantly suppressed the STING-induced increase in ROS observed at 5 h (Fig. 4I). These findings indicate that PARP1, which acts downstream of ROS, does not affect the initial ROS surge triggered by STING activation but can mitigate oxidative stress exacerbated by PAR overexpression.

ERO1 is a key endoplasmic reticulum target that mediates STING-activated ADP-ribosylation

Given the critical role of oxidative stress in STING-activated ADP-ribosylation, we investigated the source of ROS. As intracellular oxidative stress primarily arises from cytosolic ROS (cROS) and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) [37], we utilized specific inhibitors targeting each compartment to identify the major source of ROS during STING-induced ADP-ribosylation. Treatment with the mtROS inhibitor MitoQ failed to attenuate STING activation-induced DNA damage and hyperactivation of ADP-ribosylation (Fig. 5A, B). In contrast, scavenging cROS with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) did not affect STING-TBK1 pathway activation but significantly abrogated the associated DNA damage and ADP-ribosylation (Fig. 5C, D). Live/dead fluorescence staining showed that NAC treatment markedly reduced STING activation–induced cell death. Consistently, cell viability assays demonstrated that NAC significantly enhanced cell viability following stimulation (Fig. 5E, F). These results indicate that cROS is the primary source driving STING-activated ADP-ribosylation.

To identify the key oxidative stress regulator responsible for STING-induced cROS elevation in macrophages, we screened major cROS-associated proteins. Considering that STING, anchored to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) via its four transmembrane domains [38], regulates diverse immune responses, and that the ER—a central organelle for protein folding, lipid synthesis, and calcium storage—requires tight redox homeostasis for cellular function, we focused on core ER oxidative stress regulators [39]. Targeted inhibition of NADPH oxidases (NOX4 or NOX2) did not ameliorate STING-induced ADP-ribosylation hyperactivation (Fig. 5G). However, the inhibition of ERO1 significantly reduced STING-induced ADP-ribosylation and concurrently diminished STING and TBK1 activation. This effect was consistent when two distinct STING agonists were used (Fig. 5H, I). Furthermore, in immortalized iBMDM cells, ERO1 inhibition similarly suppressed STING pathway activation and the associated increase in ADP-ribosylation (Fig. 5J, Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). Knockdown of ERO1 in macrophages via siRNA also markedly inhibited STING activation and the elevation of ADP-ribosylation (Fig. 5K). On the basis of ERO1 knockdown, we further overexpressed ERO1. The rescue experiments showed that restoration of ERO1 expression normalized the STING activation–induced signaling pathway responsible for excessive PAR accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 3C-D).

The proton channel activity of STING and its ER-to-Golgi trafficking are known to play critical roles in STING-mediated signal transduction [40, 41]. On the basis of these findings, we further investigated whether these two functional properties of STING are also involved in the activation of ADP-ribosylation–related signaling pathways. We first employed C53 to block the proton channel activity of STING and subsequent LC3 lipidation (Supplementary Fig. 4A). The results showed that C53 did not attenuate STING-induced PAR accumulation or DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 4B). While C53 treatment significantly reduced cell death triggered by STING activation, it provided only partial rescue, as residual cell mortality remained observable (Supplementary Fig. 4C-D). We then used brefeldin A to inhibit STING trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus. Although brefeldin A markedly suppressed the phosphorylation of STING and TBK1, it failed to inhibit excessive ADP-ribosylation or DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 5A). In contrast, treatment with either an ERO1 inhibitor or a PARP1 inhibitor significantly reduced STING activation–induced excessive ADP-ribosylation and DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that neither the proton channel activity nor the ER-to-Golgi trafficking of STING is required for the activation of ADP-ribosylation–related signaling.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ERO1, a key ER oxidative stress protein, plays an essential role in mediating STING activation-induced cytosolic ROS elevation and the consequent hyperactivation of ADP-ribosylation.

STING interacts with ERO1 to promote ADP-ribosylation activation

STING and ERO1 are key regulatory proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) involved in innate immunity and oxidative stress. Using AlphaFold3, a tool for protein structural analysis and interaction prediction, we predicted an interaction between STING and ERO1 (Fig. 6A). The prediction suggests a potential interaction between STING and ERO1, with its potential binding sites primarily located within the CBD and CTT domains. To validate this, we transfected 293T cells with plasmids encoding STING and ERO1. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays confirmed their binding (Fig. 6B, C). We further investigated the interaction between endogenous STING and ERO1 following STING activation. Western blot analysis revealed that endogenous STING physically associated with ERO1 upon activation (Supplementary Fig. 6A-B). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that STING activation significantly increased its co-localization with ERO1 (Fig. 6D).

STING, an ER transmembrane protein, is composed of a transmembrane domain (TMD), a ligand-binding domain (LBD), and a C-terminal tail (CTT) [7] (Fig. 6E). To identify the STING domain responsible for ERO1 binding, we generated plasmids expressing STING mutants lacking specific domains. Co-IP assays in 293T cells co-transfected with ERO1 and these mutant plasmids demonstrated that deletion of the CTT markedly reduced the STING–ERO1 interaction (Fig. 6F, G). We further investigated the role of the CTT domain in ADP-ribosylation activation. Compared with full-length STING, deletion of the CTT domain markedly suppressed excessive ADP-ribosylation (Supplementary Fig. 6C-D). In parallel, we assessed the contributions of distinct functional residues of STING to ADP-ribosylation activation and found that the R238A and S366A mutants significantly attenuated ADP-ribosylation (Supplementary Fig. 6E-F). Collectively, these results indicate that STING located on the ER interacts with ERO1, and that the CTT domain is critical for this interaction and the activation of ADP-ribosylation signaling.

cGAS and IFNAR Knockout Fails to Suppress PARP1 Activation in Sepsis

To further elucidate the role of cGAS in sepsis, we generated a sepsis model using cGAS knockout (cGAS⁻/⁻) mice. Compared with WT mice, cGAS⁻/⁻ did not significantly alleviate intestinal or lung injury (Supplementary Fig. 7A-C). Survival curve analysis revealed that cGAS deficiency failed to improve sepsis survival, whereas the administration of a PARP1 inhibitor significantly reduced sepsis mortality (Supplementary Fig. 7D). Western blotting revealed that while STING⁻/⁻ markedly reduced PAR modification levels, cGAS⁻/⁻ did not decrease PAR levels (Supplementary Fig. 7E-F). These data indicate that cGAS deficiency does not affect PAR generation or sepsis-induced intestinal injury.

Activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway enhances type I IFN expression. Type I IFN exert their effects through the IFNAR receptor and downstream signaling [42]. However, the role of type I IFN in PARP1 activation remains unclear. We further investigated the function of the type I IFN receptor (IFNAR1) in sepsis. Compared with control mice, IFNAR1⁻/⁻ mice presented significantly improved histopathological alterations in the intestine and lung, indicating that IFNAR contributes to tissue damage in sepsis and that IFN inhibition mitigates injury (Supplementary Fig. 8A). However, Western blot analysis demonstrated that Ifnar1 KO did not suppress the increase in PARP1 protein levels or ADP-ribosylation modification (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C), suggesting that IFNAR1 deficiency does not affect PARP1 activation. We further measured inflammatory cytokine levels in intestinal tissues. Following sepsis induction, IFNAR1⁻/⁻ did not inhibit the expression of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2; conversely, their expression was further elevated in IFNAR1-deficient mice (Supplementary Fig. 8D). These results indicate that while IFNAR1⁻/⁻ ameliorates tissue damage to some extent, it fails to suppress PARP1 activation or the inflammatory response.

Overall, within the cGAS-STING-IFN pathway implicated in sepsis injury, the mechanism by which STING activates PARP1 represents a critical target for intestinal damage and inflammation in sepsis.

PARP1 inhibition mitigates STING activation-induced cell death

To further investigate the relationship between PARP1 and the STING signaling pathway and its downstream effects, we treated cells with the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 to assess the roles of STING signaling and ADP-ribosylation. PJ34 treatment significantly reduced STING activation-induced PAR polymer formation. Notably, PJ34 did not affect DMXAA-induced phosphorylation of STING and TBK1 (Fig. 7A, B). Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown of PARP1 in iBMDM macrophages yielded consistent results with PJ34 treatment: STING pathway activation was not significantly altered, but ADP-ribosylation levels were markedly suppressed (Supplementary Fig. 9A-B). These findings indicate that STING, which acts upstream, regulates the activation of PARP1 and its ADP-ribosylation function downstream.

Analysis of IFN reporter gene expression downstream of STING revealed that PARP1 inhibitors (PJ34, 3-AB, Olaparib) and the antioxidant NAC significantly reduced IFN-related gene expression (Fig. 7C), suggesting a critical role for PARP1 in STING pathway-mediated gene transcription. Investigating the link between PARP1 and the STING-associated DNA damage response, we observed that treatment with different PARP1 inhibitors (PJ34, 3-AB, Olaparib) followed by DMXAA stimulation significantly reduced STING-induced nuclear DNA damage in macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 9C). Time-course analysis further demonstrated that PJ34 specifically suppressed nuclear DNA damage at early time points after STING activation; thereafter, DNA damage levels were comparable between the treated and untreated groups (Supplementary Fig. 9D).

We next examined the effect of PARP1 inhibition on STING activation-induced cytokine expression. PJ34 significantly suppressed the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFNβ, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2. The expression of ISG15, a key STING-induced transcription factor, was also markedly reduced by PJ34. Furthermore, the mRNA levels of NOS2, a key regulator of reactive oxygen species, were significantly attenuated upon PARP1 inhibition (Fig. 7D). These results demonstrate that PARP1 plays a critical role in the downstream immune effector functions of the STING pathway.

To explore the role of PARP1 in STING-induced cell death, we stimulated cells with STING agonists (DMXAA, ADU-S100). Calcein AM/PI staining revealed that STING activation significantly increased cell death (PI-positive cells) and decreased viable cell numbers (Calcein AM-positive cells). Treatment with PJ34 or PARP1-siRNA substantially reduced the number of PI-positive cells (Fig. 7E; Supplementary Fig. 9E, 9 H). Consistent with these findings, cytotoxicity and cell viability assays revealed that STING activation significantly increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release and decreased cell viability. Treatment with PJ34 or PARP1-siRNA significantly reduced LDH levels and enhanced cell viability (Fig. 7F; Supplementary Fig. 9F-G, 9I), indicating that pharmacological inhibition of PARP1 mitigates STING-induced cell death and promotes cell survival.

Further investigation into the role of PARP1 in AIF nuclear translocation revealed that PJ34 treatment did not affect cytoplasmic AIF protein levels but significantly reduced nuclear AIF levels (Fig. 7G-H). Immunofluorescence confirmed that PJ34 markedly attenuated nuclear AIF fluorescence intensity, indicating that PJ34 prevents AIF nuclear translocation (Fig. 7I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PARP1 inhibition significantly reduces ADP-ribosylation activation, attenuates inflammatory cytokine expression, prevents AIF nuclear translocation, and ultimately diminishes cell death.

PARP1 inhibition attenuates intestinal inflammation and injury

To further investigate the critical role of the PARP1 pathway in tissue damage during sepsis, we utilized a murine sepsis model. Histopathological analysis revealed that PARP1 inhibition significantly attenuated intestinal injury, as evidenced by reduced pathological scores in the small intestine. Lung tissue damage and inflammatory cell infiltration were also alleviated (Fig. 8A-C). TUNEL staining of intestinal tissue revealed that PARP1 inhibition markedly ameliorated intestinal epithelial cell death and mitigated intestinal barrier dysfunction (Fig. 8B). Fluorescence imaging of the intestine revealed that CLP induced a pronounced disruption of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin, whereas treatment with the PARP1 inhibitor PJ34 effectively restored their localization and abundance, we next evaluated the expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 to assess intestinal epithelial regeneration. CLP-induced injury markedly impaired epithelial proliferation, whereas treatment with the PARP1 inhibitor robustly restored Ki67-positive cell populations, highlighting its potent role in promoting tissue repair and regeneration during sepsis. (Supplementary Fig. 10A). PJ34 significantly mitigated lung and intestinal damage, as evidenced by the reduced extravasation of Evans Blue dye (Supplementary Fig. 10B). Consistent with the observed intestinal injury, PCR results indicated that PARP1 inhibition significantly decreased sepsis-induced mRNA expression of the inflammatory cytokine IL-6, thereby ameliorating the intestinal inflammatory response (Fig.8D). We next evaluated the serum levels of key inflammatory cytokines and found that treatment with PARP1 inhibitor substantially attenuated CLP-induced elevations of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2, indicating its pronounced anti-inflammatory effect (Supplementary Fig. 10C).

In survival studies, PARP1 inhibition significantly reduced mortality induced by sepsis (Fig. 8E). Further analysis of PARP1 pathway activation and ADP-ribosylation levels revealed that PJ34 significantly suppressed the CLP-induced increases in PARP1 and ADP-ribosylation levels. Moreover, PJ34 effectively inhibited the CLP-induced increase in MIF levels (Fig. 8F, G). These findings indicate that targeted inhibition of the PARP1 pathway reduces PARylation levels and ameliorates sepsis-induced intestinal damage and inflammation.

Discussion

Sepsis-induced intestinal injury remains a major challenge in clinical therapy and involves complex mechanisms such as hyperinflammation, organelle dysfunction, and programmed cell death [5, 43]. In this study, we systematically revealed a previously underappreciated STING–ERO1–PARP1 signaling axis that centers on ROS-mediated PARylation to drive Parthanatos, leading to disruption of the intestinal epithelial barrier and exacerbated inflammation (Fig. 8H). This discovery not only elucidates a mechanistic intersection between innate immune sensing and cellular stress responses, but also offers a novel therapeutic target for organ dysfunction associated with sepsis.

While the current literature predominantly focuses on the role of STING in antiviral [44–46] and antitumor immunity [47, 48] through the canonical IFN pathway, our findings expand the understanding of the STING–PARP1 axis by revealing a non-canonical, cGAS-independent STING activation mechanism critical in sepsis. Conventionally, the cGAS–STING–TBK1–IRF3 axis is viewed as a linear cascade that mediates IFN-I production in response to cytosolic DNA [7, 8]. In contrast, our study departs from this paradigm and demonstrates that STING-induced overactivation of PARP1 and the subsequent Parthanatos pathway are independent of both cGAS and TBK1. These findings suggest a bifurcated function of STING in the inflammatory milieu of sepsis: one arm drives classical IFN responses, and the other regulates oxidative stress and ADP-ribosylation induced cell death.

Furthermore, our findings establish a previously unrecognized molecular link between STING and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Although STING activation has been reported to induce ER stress [10] and promote ROS production [36], its downstream mediators remain largely undefined. Here, we identified ERO1 as a direct binding partner of STING and a key amplifier of cytosolic ROS, thereby building the first molecular bridge between innate immune signaling and ER-originated redox stress. We show that STING interacts with ERO1 via its C-terminal tail (CTT) to induce ER oxidative stress, elevate cytosolic ROS, and cause nuclear DNA damage. This chain of events ultimately leads to PARP1 hyperactivation and triggers AIF-mediated nuclear translocation, culminating in Parthanatos. This non-canonical pathway not only enriches the known functions of STING in immune regulation but also highlights its emerging role in immunometabolic control. Targeting the STING–ERO1 interaction site may provide a strategy to attenuate tissue injury without compromising STING-dependent innate immunity. This selective intervention could help resolve the clinical dilemma between “immune protection” and “immune-mediated tissue injury” .

Recent studies have broadened the therapeutic landscape of PARP1 inhibitors beyond BRCA-deficient tumors [49, 50] to encompass various inflammatory diseases [51, 52]. In preclinical models of neurodegenerative disease [53, 54], lipid metabolism disorders [55], retinal degeneration [56], vascular injury [57], and sepsis [17], PARP1 inhibition has been shown to preserve cellular bioenergetics, reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and prevent cell death, thereby exerting protective effects on organ function. Inhibitors such as PJ34, 3-AB, and Olaparib [51] not only mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction but also significantly lower levels of TNF-α and IL-1β, improving survival. These observations support the notion that PARP1 serves as a pivotal node linking DNA damage repair with inflammation and cell death.

Importantly, our study extends the current understanding of Parthanatos in the context of infectious diseases. Although Parthanatos has been implicated in ischemia-reperfusion injury [58], neurodegeneration [34, 54], and radiation damage [59], its role in infection-driven tissue injury remains poorly characterized. We demonstrate that Parthanatos is a direct downstream consequence of ROS accumulation mediated by the STING–ERO1 axis. Notably, we revealed a central role for PARylation in this process. PARP1-catalyzed PARylation, initially a stress response for DNA repair, is shown here to be persistently hyperactivated, functioning as a metabolic switch and signal amplifier that governs cell fate. Excessive PARylation depletes NAD⁺, disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, and triggers AIF release [60] and nuclear translocation—hallmarks of Parthanatos [61]. Our findings suggest that abnormal PARylation is not a passive byproduct but rather a critical driver of cell death. Inhibiting this modification not only blocks AIF translocation but also dampens inflammatory cytokine release [62] and tissue damage. Thus, PARylation sits at the core of STING–ERO1-induced cytotoxicity and may serve as a convergence point of immune, oxidative, and metabolic signaling, representing a compelling therapeutic target.

Notably, compared with the partial protective effects of IFNAR or TBK1 deficiency, PARP1 inhibition confers significantly greater protection and survival benefits in tissue injury models. These findings indicate that Parthanatos may be the terminal effector mechanism through which STING drives organ damage under sterile inflammatory conditions, rather than merely promoting inflammatory cytokine expression. Hence, therapeutic targeting of PARP1 [51] or upstream ROS sources [63] may offer a strategic advantage for modulating STING signaling without compromising host defense. We further observed that PARP1 inhibition reduces the transcription of STING-dependent inflammatory mediators such as IFN-β, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2, suggesting that PARP1 may act not only as an executor of cell death [64] but also as a co-regulator of STING-driven transcriptional programs. These insights broaden our understanding of PARP1 function and provide a rationale for balancing immune homeostasis with tissue protection through fine-tuned modulation of the STING pathway.

This study focuses on defining the role of parthanatos in sepsis-associated tissue injury; however, cellular damage in sepsis reflects the coordinated activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways. In particular, pyroptosis has been established as a central driver of sepsis-induced tissue damage [26, 65, 66], yet its contribution was not fully explored here. The pore-forming executioner of pyroptosis, gasdermin D (GSDMD), can disrupt mitochondrial integrity and promote cytosolic release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), a process implicated in sustained cGAS–STING activation during sepsis-related pulmonary vascular injury [27]. As a damage-associated molecular pattern, mtDNA is sensed by cGAS, providing a plausible upstream trigger for STING activation, DNA damage, and excessive PARP1 activation observed in this study. These observations highlight the need for future work to delineate the coordinated crosstalk among pyroptosis, parthanatos, necroptosis, and ferroptosis in sepsis, thereby informing rational combinatorial therapeutic strategies targeting distinct cell death programs.

Despite these advances, several questions remain. While ERO1 has been identified as a central mediator of STING-induced oxidative stress, the broader metabolic consequences of STING–ERO1–PARP1 activation—such as NAD⁺ depletion and mitochondrial failure [67]—require further exploration. Detailed spatiotemporal mapping of immune signaling, redox imbalance, and cell death throughout the course of sepsis is warranted. It also remains unclear whether the STING–PARP1 axis intersects with other cell death pathways such as ferroptosis [68] or necroptosis [69], particularly under conditions of redox imbalance [70]. Moreover, the current study has certain scope limitations. Future work should incorporate organoid models, intestinal tissue sections, or single-cell multi-omics to validate the conservation and relevance of this axis in human samples, thereby paving the way for clinical translation.

Conclusions

This study identified a novel network of interactions between immune signaling, oxidative stress, and cell death, extending the pathological role of STING in sepsis beyond its classical interferon-driven function. By elucidating the STING–ERO1–PARP1 signaling pathway as a core driver of intestinal injury, we propose a multidimensional mechanism linking innate immune sensing, oxidative stress, and parthanatos. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying STING–ERO1–PARP1 signaling but also establish a conceptual and pharmacological basis for developing targeted therapies with improved specificity and safety. Further investigation of the structural, signaling, and therapeutic aspects of this pathway is crucial for advancing treatment strategies for inflammatory diseases.
