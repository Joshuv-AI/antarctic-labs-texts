The Japanese tits evaluate threat level based on the posture of a predator
Authors: Mingju E, Jiangping Jin, Yu Luo, KaiYan Li, Duo Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have shown that the threat level posed by a predator can vary with physical features. In this study, we tested whether the wing posture of a raptor could serve as a clue for bird threat assessment. We observed the behavioral response of Japanese tits Parus minor to taxidermy dummies Eurasian sparrowhawks Accipiter nisus with either spread wings and closed wings. The results showed that the response scores to sparrowhawks with spread wings were higher than those to sparrowhawks with closed wings. We suggested that Japanese tits can assess the predation risk associated with changes in wing posture of predators.

Introduction

Predation is a major source of mortality for most animals. The dynamic risk assessment hypothesis predicts that individuals modulate their responses according to the level of threat and alter their behavior to minimize personal risk1–3. Theoretically, both underestimating and overestimating the danger posed by predators can have adverse effects on individuals4. If individuals show antipredator responses against low predation threats, it will cost unnecessary expenditure of time and energy that could be allocated to other activities, conversely, individuals that fail to respond to high predation threats may face survival costs5–7. Rapid and accurate assessment of a predator’s threat level of a predator is crucial for the survival of prey.

To date, many studies have proved that prey have the ability to distinguish different predators and access their threat levels5,8,9. Visual cues are one of the main pathways for prey to assess predation risk. Assessing a predator’s features could allow prey to make more subtle judgments about risk, which could be important in reducing the overall costs of antipredator behavior10–12. Yet, the level of threat a predator poses can vary with physical features13, such as body size, beak shape and eye colour of the predators14–16. In addition, a predator with different behaviours or postures may also pose different threat levels to prey. For instance, a snake in a striking pose versus a coiled pose may represent different levels of aggression, and Rhesus macaques Macaca mulatta can use the posture of snakes as a cue in threat assessment as they responded more strongly to a snake model in a striking posture than in a coiled posture17. The careful and subtle distinctions about the immediate threat posed by a predator can be very beneficial to the prey18.

The wings of birds are integral to their ability to fly, a capability that is not only crucial for their survival but also for their ecological roles. For raptors, the posture of wings may reflect their behavioral intentions, as they may be in the process of hunting while in flight19. Therefore, avian predators in flight were considered to pose a greater threat to their prey than when they were perched4,20. However, despite the indispensable role of wings to birds, as far as we know, few studies have investigated the relationship between the wing posture of avian predators and the associated threat level.

The Japanese tit is a common passerine bird in our study area. Previous research has found that Japanese tits Parus minor performed low intensity response behaviours to taxidermic dummy Eurasian sparrowhawks Accipiter nisus, when displayed as standing with wings naturally closed21. Here, we aimed to compare the response behaviour of Japanese tits to Eurasian sparrowhawk dummies with spread wings (wing-spread sparrowhawk hereafter) and to sparrowhawk dummies with closed wings (wing-closed sparrowhawk hereafter, Fig. 1). We predicted that Japanese tits would show stronger antipredator behaviour to wing-spread sparrowhawks than to wing-closed sparrowhawsks.

Results

The response scores of tits differed significantly among treatments (CLMMs, χ2 = 18.819, df = 2, P < 0.001), while there were no significant effects of trial order (χ2 = 1.041, df = 1, P = 0.791), brood size (χ2 = 0.026, df = 1, P = 0.871) and nestling age (χ2 = 2.151, df = 1, P = 0.143). The response scores to wing-spread sparrowhawks were the higher than wing-closed sparrowhawks (adjusted P < 0.001) or Oriental turtle doves Streptopelia orientalis (adjusted P = 0.007) with wings closed (control, hereafter dove), while there was no significantly difference in the response scores of tits to wing-closed sparrowhawks versus doves (adjusted P = 0.127) (Fig. 2). When facing wing-spread sparrowhawks, the anti-predator behavior of Japanese tits is mostly hovering and skipping; while, when facing wing-closed sparrowhawks and doves, antipredator behavior is mostly skipping and observation.

Discussion

In line with our prediction, Japanese tits responded more strongly to wing-spread sparrowhawks than to wing-closed sparrowhawks. For perched raptors, the act of spreading their wings can significantly alter their perceived size and reveal additional feather markings or patterns. This behavior may also indicate an imminent takeoff or a preparation for an attack. In contrast, a closed-wing posture may suggest a resting state. When detecting perched raptors, passerine birds could adopt one strategy, which is driving the raptors off by performing strong antipredator behaviors (e.g., Wheatcroft and Price22); and another strategy is to “ignore” the raptors and observe them from a distance3. Faced with wing- spread sparrowhawks, Japanese tits might quickly recognize and assess them as highly threatening predatory threat and chooses more aggressive feints and vigilance behaviors to drive away predators. Then they displayed aggressive feints and enhanced their vigilance to deter the wing-spread sparrowhawks. When wing-closed sparrow-hawks were encountered, tits might not accurately identify the species and assess them as having low threatening. Subsequently, they adopted low-intensity vigilance and observation tactics to mitigate the risk of predation.

The sparrowhawk poses a high threat to Japanese tits, and there is a higher risk of injury or death in the process of defense. However, the threat level of predators can vary significantly if they are in different states23. Flexibility in response to variable predation risks will undoubtedly increase the survival fitness of birds6. The results showed that the Japanese tit can assess predation risk associated with changes in wing posture of predators, and make corresponding nest defense behavior responses. Here, we suggested that the wing postures of raptors could be an important visual cue for prey.

In our study, Japanese tits exhibited significantly lower response behaviors to doves compared to those displayed towards wing-spread sparrowhawks. Recognizing the intruder and accessing the actual threat it poses are critical prerequisites for birds to make appropriate responses4,24. It is essential for birds to minimize unnecessary nest defense investments against harmless intruders4,25,26. Consistent with the finding of Yu et al.21, the response behaviour of Japanese tits to doves were comparable to those elicited by wing-closed sparrowhawks. Sympatric non-predator large sized species can also trigger risk behaviors in smaller birds21,27. However, the distinct alarm calls produced by Japanese tits in response to sparrowhawks and doves suggest that the tits can discriminate between these species17. Here, our study supported that Japanese tits are able to discriminate between sparrowhawks and doves.

Conclusion

Japanese tits can assess predation risk and adjust response behaviour according to wing postures of a sparrowhawk. Birds accurately assess predation risk on the basis of changes in a predator’s postures, which helps birds to evaluate whether the predator is motivated to launch an attack. Our results suggested that changes in wing posture of a raptor, influence the antipredator behaviour of prey.

Materials and methods

Study area and subjects

The study was carried out in the Zuojia Nature Reserve (44° 1′–45° 0′ N, 126° 0′–126° 8′ E) in Jilin, China. The Japanese tits is common species that breeds in artificial nest boxes. The nest boxes were attached to trees approximately 3 m above the ground to attract Japanese tits. Our study plot contained approximately 400 nest-boxes per year12. We regularly checked the nest boxes to determine whether they were occupied by Japanese tits and monitor the reproduction activities at intervals of 5–7 days from March to June28.

Dummy-presentation experiment

During the nestling period of Japanese tits, we presented taxidermic dummies of wing-spread sparrowhawks and wing-closed sparrowhawks and control (wing-closed doves) on the nest box lids. Each nest received 3 dummy presentations in random order, with at least 1 h interval between presentations at each focal nest. Each set of experiments (3 dummy presentations) should be completed on the same day. To avoid the effect of nestling age on the response behaviour of tits, we conducted dummy experiments during the middle of the nestling period (ap-proximately 5–10 days old). To avoid pseudo replications, we used two specimens for each treatment type (including wing-spread sparrowhawks, wing-closed sparrowhawks and doves) and randomly selected one for use in each experiment.

Experiments were conducted between 8:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. on sunny days, from May 18th to June 18th, 2022. All experiments were completed by J.J. alone (n = 21). Behavioural responses of the Japanese tits were recorded for five minutes when the tits were observed within approximately 10 m of the dummy28,29. At the same time, video recorder was used to assist in recording the antipredator behaviours21,30. We scored the antipredator responses (hereafter response scores) of tits on a five-point scale: (i) entered the nest; (ii) produced alarm calls or observed from a stationary position; (iii) exhibited agitated skipping and flicking of wings; (iv) exhibited attack behaviour with no physical encounter, such as feint or hover near the specimen; and (v) exhibited attack behaviour with physical contact28,30. Moreover, we recorded the clutch size, incubation days, brood size and nestling age after the experiment.

Statistical analysis

We used cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs, clmm in R package ordinal) with logit-link function to analyze the response scores of Japanese tits. Two-tailed likelihood ratio tests were used to obtain P values. The response score was the dependent variable, treatment (dummy-presentation types), the order of treatment exposure, brood size and nestling age were treated as fixed terms, and individual bird nests were treated as random terms. When we found significant differences of treatment, we further applied post hoc pairwise comparisons by using least-square means (lsmeans in the R package lsmeans) and used false discovery rate correction to adjust P values31. All statistical tests were carried out in R 4.3.1 (https://www.r-project.org/), and the level of significance was set to 0.05.
