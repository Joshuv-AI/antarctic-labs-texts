Patient Perspectives on Electronic Communication of Cancer Diagnoses
Authors: Sheena Bhalla, Meera J. Patel, Lauren Abruzzo, Alana Christie, Ruchira Garg, Donglu Xie, Jeremy Louissaint, Song Zhang, Heather Kitzman, David E. Gerber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This survey study investigates patient experiences and preferences regarding the electronic communication of cancer diagnoses.

Introduction

The Information Blocking Provision of the 21st Century Cures Act Final Rule, implemented in April 2021, mandates that patients have timely, unrestricted access to their electronic health information.1 With penalties of up to $1 million per violation, to comply with this legislation many health systems have adopted immediate, automatic release of all test results through electronic patient portals. To date, few studies have explored how patients perceive immediate result release in oncology, particularly related to cancer diagnosis communication.2,3 We examined the experiences and communication preferences of patients who received a cancer diagnosis in the years preceding and following the Cures Act implementation.

Methods

English-speaking patients diagnosed with cancer at University of Texas Southwestern Harold C. Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, were invited to participate in a brief online survey in January 2025 (eAppendix and eMethods in Supplement 1). The survey assessed self-reported demographics, cancer history, use of the electronic patient portal (MyChart [Epic Systems]), and how patients experienced learning about their cancer diagnosis. We analyzed associations between these factors, method of cancer diagnosis delivery, and preferences for future communication using χ2 and t tests, with no adjustment for multiple comparisons. Analyses were conducted at the .05 significance level using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc). The study was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center institutional review board with a waiver of informed consent because patient identifiers were not collected. We followed the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guideline for survey studies.

Results

From 14 561 delivered surveys, we received 2412 responses (17%). Compared with nonresponders, responders were more likely to be older than 65 years of age (1271 [63%] vs 6096 [51%]), male (1186 [50%] vs 5460 [46%]), non-Hispnic White (1909 [80%] vs 6876 [66%]), and married or living with a partner (1783 [75%] vs 7266 [66%]) (all P < .001). Information on race and ethnicity was collected for this study to assess the generalizability of findings across diverse populations.

Most respondents 1923 (84%) received their cancer diagnosis through communication from their clinical team (1355 [59%] in-person and/or telemedicine visit; 568 [25%] telephone), while 170 (7%) learned their cancer diagnosis through the portal. In terms of future communication regarding new or recurrent cancer diagnoses, 1724 (75%) preferred clinician communication (1091 [48%] in-person and/or telemedicine visit; 625 [27%] telephone); 535 (23%) preferred portal delivery. Portal preference was more common among men and higher-frequency users (Table 1). Among individuals who initially received a cancer diagnosis from a clinician (n = 1923), 79% preferred this method for future diagnoses communication, while 20% preferred via the portal. Of those who initially received a portal-delivered diagnosis (n = 170), 54% preferred this notification method for a future cancer diagnosis, while 42% preferred initial clinician delivery.

For patients who initially received a cancer diagnosis via the portal, 71% were at home, and 59% were alone at the time (Table 2). These individuals sought more information in various forms, most commonly internet searches (48%).

Among 361 respondents (15%) who reported a cancer recurrence, 234 (67%) learned through clinician communication (204 [58%] in-person and/or telemedicine; 30 [9%] telephone) and 97 (27%) learned through the portal. For the portal group, 14% discussed the results with their clinical team within 1 day, 45% within 3 days, and 78% within 1 week.

Discussion

In this survey study of over 2400 individuals seen at an National Cancer Institute-designated Comprehensive Cancer Center, most respondents received their cancer diagnosis from a clinician. However, since 2021, an increasing proportion—particularly those with recurrent cancer—reported first learning of their diagnosis through the patient portal.

While general patient populations appreciate rapid electronic access to test results, even when abnormal,4,5,6 three-quarters of survey respondents preferred to learn about a new or recurrent cancer diagnosis directly from their clinical team. Nonetheless, there is unlikely to be a one-size-fits all approach, evidenced by preference differences we observed according to patient sex, frequency of portal use, and prior delivery of cancer diagnosis. To support flexibility, 3 states—California, Kentucky, and Texas—have enacted laws that allow delayed portal release of cancer-related and other sensitive results.7,8,9 Awareness of legal requirements and technical capabilities (such as disabling real-time notification of new test result availability in the patient portal, if preferred) among health care systems, clinical teams, and patients may also support matching communication practices to patient preferences.

In addition to response and recall bias, study limitations include the single-center design, modest response rate, restriction to English-speaking individuals, and a relatively educated population, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Further, we did not assess whether patients accessed portal results voluntarily or through automatic notifications, whether notification precounseling occurred, or how patients weighed result timeliness against notification method, all of which could influence patient experience. Nor did we capture information on the postnotification experience of patients receiving clinician-delivered cancer diagnoses. Future work could examine tiered results delivery, artificial-intelligence–generated plain-language summaries, and opt-in for immediate result delivery.10 Such tailored approaches may mitigate some of the unintended consequences of real-time access to potentially confusing, sensitive, and life-changing data such as new or recurrent cancer diagnosis.
