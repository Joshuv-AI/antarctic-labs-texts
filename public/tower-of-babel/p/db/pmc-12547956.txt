Use of awl for screw pilot hole creation increases strength of anterior cervical discectomy and fusion plate constructs in biomimetic model
Authors: Andrew B. Rees, Katherine Drexelius, Rebecca DeCarlo, Austin J. Allen, Samuel J. Chewning, Michael A. Bohl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) hardware failure can have serious complications for patients. Various advances in screw and plate design have increased the pullout strength of ACDF constructs, yet failure with screw pullout still occurs. One previously unexplored factor influencing pullout strength is the method of screw pilot hole creation. We aimed to compare pullout strength, stiffness, and strain of ACDF constructs in 3D-printed cervical spine vertebral bodies based on pilot hole creation with a drill versus an awl.MethodsSpine models were 3D-printed into uniform testing blocks to mimic cervical vertebrae, according to previously validated methods. Four pilot holes per block for eventual screw placement were made with a 3 mm drill (n = 20) or a 3mm awl (n = 20). Using a biomimetic model, a plate and four 3.5 mm screws were affixed to each testing block. Pullout strength, stiffness, and strain of the final construct were collected. Maximum axial pullout forces and forces over distance curves were recorded for statistical analysis.ResultsPullout strength of the construct was significantly greater in the awl group compared to the drill group (mean force during load-to-failure 810.8 N vs. 765.6 N, respectively; p = .002). No significant difference was observed in the stiffness (p = .434) or strain (p = .526) of the constructs based on method of pilot hole creation.ConclusionsThis study aimed to evaluate the influence of pilot hole creation technique on biomechanical characteristics of ACDF constructs in a high fidelity 3D-printed biomimetic model. The results—favoring creation of pilot holes with an awl—highlight a biomechanical benefit of a previously unexplored surgical technique for the placement of ACDF screws. This may guide surgeons in choosing the optimal surgical technique to improve the strength of an ACDF construct, potentially minimizing complications and improving fusion rates.

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is among the most common procedures performed in spine surgery and its use is increasing [1]. One reported method of failure of ACDF hardware is screw or plate pullout from the vertebral body [2]. Failure of hardware in this manner can lead to motion at arthrodesis sites, and ultimately contribute to pseudarthrosis and potentially worse clinical outcomes [3,4]. Even migration of hardware and esophageal perforation has been reported [5,6]. A variety of locking mechanisms have been developed to prevent screw migration independent of the plate construct [7]. While newer implants reduce the importance of purchase of a singular screw, screw purchase in bone and pullout strength of the combined screws in the construct remains vital for the development of a stable fusion mass and a successful outcome. There are a wide variety of factors that influence the pullout strength of ACDF screws, including bone density, screw length, screw size, angle relative to the plate, and insertional torque [2,[8], [9], [10]]. One unexplored potential influence on pullout strength is the technique used to create the pilot hole for screw placement, namely creation of the pilot holes with a drill or an awl.

An awl compresses the cancellous bone to the sidewalls of the pilot hole tract, as opposed to a drill, which removes cancellous bone via the flutes of the drill bit [11]. Studies have shown that predrilling pilot holes decreases pullout strength, but comparisons regarding additional alternative techniques such as the use of an awl for pilot hole creation are lacking. [12,13]. We hypothesized that use of an awl rather than a drill would allow for increased screw purchase and improved pullout strength. This study aims to evaluate the influence of pilot hole creation technique on biomechanical characteristics of ACDF constructs in a high fidelity 3D-printed biomimetic model. This may guide surgeons in choosing the optimal surgical technique to improve the strength of an ACDF construct.

Methods

This study did not require institutional review board approval given there were no human or animal subjects or samples from human or animal subjects. Spine models were 3D-printed into uniform testing blocks for this study in order to mimic cervical vertebrae. The methodology used to create these 3D-printed models has previously been reported and validated to create biomimetic models with analogous physical properties to cadaveric bone [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. A high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scan of a normal cervical spine at the C5-C6 level was converted into an “.stl” file using the Materialise Mimics software suite (Materialise, NV, Leuven, Belgium). This preliminary model was transferred to Meshmixer 3D graphical editing software (Meshmixer, Autodesk, Inc., San Francisco, California, United States). This allows for the testing block to have a superficial surface mimicking cortical bone and inner substance mimicking cancellous bone.

The simulated C5-C6 model was then embedded in a testing block and uploaded to Simplify3D printing software (Simplify 3D, Blue Ash, Ohio, United States). Other important design components of this testing block include a small 1.8 millimeter (mm) divot incorporated into the center of the cortical surface to ensure consistent centering of the ACDF construct during biomechanical analysis. Additionally, the outer cortical surface of the testing models was optimized to ensure the ACDF plate lay flat along the surface.

The FLASHFORGE Creator Pro 3D printer (Flashforge USA, City of Industry, California, United States) was used to print 40 models using acrylonitrile butadiene styrene filament (Fig. 1). The ACDF plate was placed on the block and aligned with the central divot, then 4 pilot holes were created on each block corresponding to openings for screws on the ACDF plate. These pilot holes were created using the awl technique in half of the models (n = 20) and the drill technique in the other half (n = 20). All were made by the same author. Both the awl and drill were calibrated to reach the same depth for each pilot hole. Pilot hole trajectories were standardized in perpendicular to the working surface at all times to minimize variability in technique. Both the awl and drill had a 3 mm diameter and were marked at a depth of 8.5 mm to ensure consistent size and depth of pilot holes across both groups.Fig. 1Cervical vertebral body testing block with outer surface cortical bone and inner portion cancellous bone.Fig 1:

After preparing the pilot holes, 2 sets of crimped steel cables were placed along the horizontal and vertical axis of the ACDF plate. These cable loops were carefully constructed so that the apex met in the center of the construct, allowing for even distribution of force during load-to-failure testing. The ACDF plate was then secured to the testing block using 4 screws, with the steel cables lying in grooves under the ACDF plate. Each screw placed in the ACDF plate was a cancellous self-tapping screw. Each screw was 12 mm in length, 2.4 mm in core diameter and 3.5 mm in total diameter. The plate used in each case was a Depuy Synthes Cervical Spine Locking Plate (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA). Once the ACDF plate with cable loops were secured to the block using 4 screws, the block was placed in the testing system for biomechanical analysis (Fig. 2B). The methodology of our biomechanical testing system has previously been described by Hani et al [18]. Briefly, the lab utilizes a MARK-10 model Taylor Spatial Frame with MARK-10 Series 5 advanced digital force gauge and MARK-10 Series RO1 tension and compression force sensor custom fitted with a vice to secure testing blocks. The testing device has been optimized to allow for precise centering of testing blocks in the unit using a custom-fitted laser pointer. Experimental data was captured in MARK-10 software integrated with the biomechanical testing equipment then transferred to Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA) for storage and analysis.

Pullout force measurement began when the load cell detected a force greater than 1 N and ended when the load cell detected a force that was 50% or less of the maximum recorded force, indicating hardware failure (Fig. 2C). Maximum axial pullout forces and forces over distance curves were recorded. Descriptive statistics including means, interquartile ranges, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation were collected. Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired 2-tailed t-tests, using an alpha value of 0.05.

Results

Forty total ACDF plate and screw constructs were studied using the model described above. The twenty ACDF plates attached with awled trajectories were determined to have a mean maximum pullout force during load-to-failure testing of 810.8 ± 40.1 N. The twenty ACDF plates attached with drilled trajectories had a mean maximum pullout force of 765.6 ± 43.4 N. Mean pullout force was significantly greater in the awled group than the drilled group (p = .002).

Mean screw stiffness in the awled group was 284.0 ± 30.1 N/mm versus 293.2 ± 41.3 N/mm in the drilled group (p = 0.434). Mean screw strain in the awled group was 1681.3 ± 375.4 Nmm versus 1614.4 ± 261.1 Nmm in the drilled group (p = 0.526). Discussion

This study demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in resistance to pullout of an ACDF construct with self-tapping screws when they are placed after creating pilot holes with an awl compared to a drill. This may be explained by the concept that an awl displaces bone during pilot hole creation – leaving behind more bone for screw purchase – rather than removing bone by the flutes of the drill.

While modern ACDF plates include locking screws to form fixed angle constructs and reduce hardware-related complications after ACDF [19,20], hardware failure still occurs in the form of screw loosening or failure of the plate’s locking mechanism [3,[21], [22], [23]]. Improved fixation techniques are not only important for minimizing hardware-related complications but also play a major role in providing stability that promotes successful fusion. The nonunion rate for single-level ACDF has been reported as up to 13% in some studies [24], and the rate for 3-level surgery can reach 60% [4,[25], [26], [27]]. However, the chronologic relationship between pseudarthrosis and hardware failure is not always clear. In some cases, inadequate screw purchase related to bone quality or screw malposition, metal fatigue, or locking-mechanism failure may lead to excess motion and subsequent pseudarthrosis [3,22,28,29]. In other cases, pseudarthrosis and motion at the attempted fusion site may produce strain on hardware and ultimately lead to failure [25]. These scenarios are also not always mutually exclusive.

A number of studies have highlighted this association between hardware complications and pseudarthrosis. Ning et al. evaluated over 2,000 patients who underwent ACDF with a locking plate and found that plate loosening or breakage occurred in 115 cases [29]. In their series, 62.5% of patients with loosening also had nonunion. Song et al. performed a study of 132 patients who underwent ACDF and found hardware failure to be one of the more common complications, with plate and/or screw loosening occurring in 26 cases [3]. They concluded that screw or plate loosening and breakage are associated with metal fatigue via pseudarthrosis [3].

While optimizing construct stability is important in promoting successful fusion, it is also important in limiting potentially fatal hardware complications. Yahanda et al. noted that in their 9 reported cases of hardware extrusion and pharyngoesphageal injury after ACDF, the majority (n = 5) of cases had definite or suspected pseudarthrosis. Finally, screw loosening or failure is one of the most common reasons for litigation following ACDF [30]. For the purposes of promoting fusion and minimizing complications and their repercussions, techniques that improve fixation and increase construct strength warrant ongoing investigation.

Although biomimetic models provide a reliable analysis of biomechanical performance, it is difficult to assess clinical outcomes with current methodology. This study is limited in its ability to determine if the statistically significant difference in pullout strength represents a clinically meaningful difference, with the mean improvement in pullout strength being 5.6%. There is a growing body of literature demonstrating that 3D-printed models of human bone can be inexpensive, have high fidelity to human bony tissue, and are highly controllable in terms of anatomy, size, bone quality, corticocancellous architecture, and overall morphology [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. In a study such as this, 3D-printed models allow us to directly compare 2 techniques while most effectively limiting all other variables.

In contrast, cadaveric specimens are costly, involve tissue handling restrictions, and impose a large number of uncontrolled variables to any biomechanical study. Whereas our system allows for the final ACDF construct to be fixed in the testing system for axial pullout force measurement, this step in cadaveric studies typically requires potting the specimens in polymethylmethacrylate or a similar compound, allowing time to cure, and then fixating the potted specimens into the testing system using a multiaxial vise grip. Not only can this process be cumbersome and time-consuming, but it introduces variability due to anatomic variation, variation in potting, or variation in the multi-axis vise grip fixation. In the system used for this study, a custom-fitted laser pointer allows for reproducible alignment of the ACDF construct, known to be perpendicular to the testing specimen as determined by the custom block for co-axial force measurement. Furthermore, the above-described testing blocks are designed such that they can fit exactly into the vise grip in the same location every time, thereby ensuring reliable specimen placement and alignment into the testing frame with every specimen.

Limitations of this study include variation with freehand screw placement, potentially yielding inconsistent insertion angles. Although this screw insertion was done as consistently as possible between groups, different screw insertion angles may impact the maximum pullout force [35]. Additionally, careful methodology with steel cables was developed in this study to distribute forces evenly across the ACDF plate during biomechanical testing. Despite this, subtle changes in location of these cables during ACDF construct testing may have yielded uneven load distribution across the 4 screws in each testing sample. Any uneven force distribution may have introduced premature failure and an artificially deflated pullout strength on load-to-failure testing. Both of these potential errors may be partly mitigated in that they would have been consistent across both the awl and drill group. Additionally, 12 mm length screws were used for testing in both the awl and drill groups, whereas screw length in clinical practice commonly includes 14 and 16 mm screws. Longer screws may increase pullout strength. However, our study aims to compare any differences in pullout strength between the groups rather than focus on the numerical load-to-failure values, and screw length was consistent throughout.

Future studies should incorporate a larger test sample to better understand the increase in mean maximum pullout force when preparing pilot holes using an awl. Investigating a correlation between biomechanical testing parameters and clinical outcomes would also be beneficial, namely for assessing rates of pseudarthrosis and hardware failure based on the methodology of pilot hole creation.

Conclusion

In this analysis of the biomechanical performance of ACDF plates instrumented into a biomimetic cervical spine model, mean maximum average pullout strength was significantly greater after preparing pilot holes for screw insertion with an awl rather than with a fluted drill. There were no significant differences in stiffness or strain of the construct based on the instrument used to create pilot holes. Future studies are needed to correlate whether this observed biomechanical difference translates to differences in clinical outcomes.
