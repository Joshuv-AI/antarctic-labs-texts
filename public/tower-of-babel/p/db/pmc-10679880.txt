Progress in patient-derived liver cancer cell models: a step forward for precision medicine
Authors: Zhengtao Zhang, Lijian Hui
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of effective precision treatments for liver cancers has been hindered by the scarcity of preclinical models that accurately reflect the heterogeneity of this disease. Recent progress in developing patient-derived liver cancer cell lines and organoids has paved the way for precision medicine research. These expandable resources of liver cancer cell models enable a full spectrum of pharmacogenomic analysis for liver cancers. Moreover, patient-derived and short-term cultured two-dimensional tumor cells or three-dimensional organoids can serve as patient avatars, allowing for the prediction of patients’ response to drugs and facilitating personalized treatment for liver cancer patients. Furthermore, the current novel techniques have expanded the scope of cancer research, including innovative organoid culture, gene editing and bioengineering. In this review, we provide an overview of the progress in patient-derived liver cancer cell models, focusing on their applications in precision and personalized medicine research. We also discuss the challenges and future perspectives in this field.Keywords: liver cancer, patient-derived cell lines, patient-derived organoids, precision medicine, immunotherapy

Introduction

Liver cancer remains a global health challenge, with incidence and mortality rates increasing worldwide. It is estimated that by 2025, more than 1 million individuals will be affected by liver cancer annually [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, is the most common type of liver cancer and accounts for 90% of all cases.

As the most frequent type of liver cancer, HCC represents a major health problem, causing more than 700,000 deaths annually worldwide [2]. Unfortunately, limited progress has been achieved in the clinical management of HCC in recent years. System therapies for HCC patients who are diagnosed at advanced stages offer minimal improvements in survival. Sorafenib, a multikinase inhibitor, was the first therapy approved by the FDA for patients with advanced-stage HCC, but it only improved median survival by three months [3]. Other multikinase inhibitors, such as lenvatinib and regorafenib, have been approved for advanced HCC, but they also provide minimal survival benefits of less than one year [ 4, 5] . The combination of atezolizumab (an anti-PDL1 antibody) and bevacizumab (an anti-VEGF antibody) was the first regimen to improve overall survival compared with sorafenib [6]. However, given that only a portion of patients respond, it is critical to develop biomarkers and stratification systems to predict which patients will benefit from the therapy. Notably, the limited success of current therapies in HCC patients may be attributed to the high genetic heterogeneity of HCCs [7]. Therefore, it is an urgent task to develop biomarker-guided precision treatments for HCC patients.

Recent efforts have comprehensively studied the genetic landscape of HCCs using genome sequencing [7]. TERT alterations are the most frequent genetic alterations in HCCs, accounting for 60% of cases. Other frequently altered genes in HCCs include TP53 (30% of cases), CTNNB1 (30% of cases), and chromatin remodelers ARID1A (10% of cases) and ARID2 (5% of cases) [8]. Unfortunately, these frequently altered genes remain undruggable [1], making it necessary to exploit these genetic alterations as biomarkers for drug response or to identify novel synthetical lethality approaches to these alterations. For example, Qiu e t al. [9] identified HDAC inhibitors that could target synthetical lethality to CTNNB1-activating mutations. It is worth noting that approximately 25% of HCCs carry potentially actionable mutations, although with a prevalence of less than 10% [1]. It is crucial to translate these genomic findings into clinical treatment options to improve patient care for HCC patients.

To bridge genomic profiling and precision medicine, it is critical to develop patient-derived models that can faithfully retain the genetic features of cancers and are amenable for high-throughput drug screening [8]. These patient-derived models could address two major challenges in precision cancer treatment: (1) building extensive pharmacogenomic knowledge banks by performing a full spectrum of pharmacogenomic characterization of patient-derived models at scale, which could enable the discovery of drug sensitivity-related biomarkers or potential therapies, and (2) using patient-derived models as patient avatars for real-time drug response prediction to help develop effective personalized treatments in the clinic.

Patient-derived tumor xenografts (PDTXs) are valuable in vivo models in basic and translational cancer research [8], which could be generated by directly implanting freshly resected tumor tissue pieces into immunodeficient mice [ 10, 11] . Since mice can recapitulate certain physiological environments of tumor cells in vivo, PDTXs can partially mimic the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ 10, 11] . However, PDTXs have several limitations, such as species differences in tumor-host interactions, the absence of adaptive immunity, and ethical issues. Importantly, establishing PDTXs is time-consuming and requires considerable manpower, materials, and financial resources, making it unsuitable for high-throughput drug screening ( Table 1) [ 8, 12] . Therefore, PDTXs may fall short in pharmacogenomic studies at scale and are not suitable for rapid evaluation of drug response within a limited clinical timeframe.

To achieve precision medicine in cancer treatment, it calls for new cancer models. Patient-derived cell lines (PDCs) are widely used in vitro cell models that can retain the genetic characteristics of cancers [13]. PDCs exhibit consistent phenotypes and genotypes, which are convenient for maintenance, expansion, and genetic manipulation, making it possible to conduct large-scale and high-throughput drug screening ( Table 1) [ 8, 12] . By utilizing PDCs on a sufficient scale, biomarkers of drug responses can be identified. Recent advancements in 2D culture systems enable the establishment of patient-derived and short-term cultured tumor cells in a few weeks. These cell models are suitable for genomic profiling and large-scale drug screening that can potentially help develop effective personalized cancer treatments.

Due to 2D culture in petri dishes, PDCs fail to preserve the pathological structures. To overcome this drawback, organoid cultures have been developed. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs), a self-organizing 3D cell culture model that recapitulates the pathological structures of original cancer tissues, have emerged as a promising tool for cancer research [14]. Importantly, PDOs can potentially bridge the gap between 2D PDCs and physiologically relevant PDTXs ( Table 1) [12]. The generation of PDO biobanks allows for a comprehensive spectrum of pharmacogenomic analysis, facilitating the identification of potential biomarkers. Furthermore, PDOs have shown promising results in predicting patient outcomes in clinical settings [15]. Notably, innovative organoid cultures offer new approaches to studying tumor-immune interactions, which can be applied in pre-clinical testing of immunotherapies [ 14, 16] .

A major challenge in cancer research is the poor efficiency with which cancer cell models can be established from tumor tissues [12]. However, recent advancements in generating liver cancer cell lines have improved the success rate and made it possible to develop a large number of new liver cancer cell line models [9]. Furthermore, significant progress has also been made in PDO generation for various cancers, including liver cancers [17]. Expanding the development of new liver cancer cell models will more accurately reflect the high genetic heterogeneity of human liver cancers. As a result, it will allow for a more comprehensive investigation of drug responses. This represents a significant step forward in precision medicine for liver cancers.

In this review, we summarize the progress made in developing new liver cancer cell models, including cell lines and organoids. Additionally, we will highlight the potential applications of these models in precision medicine research for liver cancers.

Progress in Developing Patient-derived Liver Cancer Cell Models

Both patient-derived cell lines and organoids are valuable in vitro cell models for basic and translational research in cancers. However, current cancer cell models do not adequately reflect the genetic diversity present in cancers. Significant efforts have been made to create novel in vitro cancer cell models through international collaborations, including the Human Cancer Model Initiative (HCMI) and Cancer Cell Line Factory (CCLF), to develop new PDOs and PDCs, respectively [ 18, 19] . In line with such effort, recent progress in culture protocols has generated over 100 new patient-derived cell models, including both PDCs and PDOs, greatly expanding the repertoire for liver cancer research. The expanded repository of the next generation of liver cancer cell models may overcome the major bottleneck that limits the translation of genomic data into the discovery of novel vulnerabilities or biomarkers.

Patient-derived liver cancer cell lines

Since the establishment of the first human cell line (HeLa cell line) almost 70 years ago [20], PDCs have been the mainstay of in vitro preclinical models in cancer research. However, there are only 30 liver cancer cell lines available to the community, which are insufficient to capture the heterogeneity of this disease. Unfortunately, it has recently been reported that many widely used HCC cell lines are contaminated by HeLa cells [21]. Therefore, to systematically analyze genetic heterogeneity and drug responses, it is urgent to generate new patient-derived liver cancer cell models.

To establish new PDCs, the success rate of establishing cell lines from liver cancer tissues needs to be improved, as the efficiency is usually too low (approximately 10%) under the current published protocols [ 13, 22] . Conditional cell reprogramming culture (CRC) involves propagating cancer cells on a layer of feeder cells combined with a Rho kinase (ROCK) inhibitor. The CRC system can rapidly and efficiently culture tumor cells and corresponding normal tissue cells from various epithelial tumor tissues, such as skin, prostate, lung, breast, and kidney [23]. Based on this culture system, Qiu et al. [9] found that the combination of the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 and the transforming growth factor-β inhibitor A83-01 could significantly improve the efficiency of generating liver cancer cell lines from freshly resected cancer tissues without feeder cells. Y-27632 facilitated the attachment of primary cells in vitro, while A83-01 inhibited mesenchymal cells and supported epithelial cell growth. With the optimized protocol, the success rate was increased to approximately 50%, resulting in the establishment of 50 new liver cancer cell lines, named the Chinese liver cancer cell line [9]. This culture protocol has the potential to allow for the large-scale establishment of the next generation of liver cancer cell lines. Importantly, PDCs can preserve the genomic and transcriptomic features of their parental cancer tissues. Remarkably, drug response profiles were highly similar in both low-passage patient-derived tumor cells and paired established cell lines (>20 passages) [9], highlighting their great potential in tumor cell-centered pharmacogenomic analysis.

Patient-derived liver cancer organoids

Organoids are 3D structures derived from (pluripotent) stem cells, progenitors, and/or differentiated cells that self-organize through cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions to recapitulate the architecture and function of native tissue in vitro [24]. A pioneering study by Sato et al. [25] successfully established an organoid culture system, realizing the 3D culture of single Lgr5 + ISCs to generate intestinal crypt-villus structures in vitro. This technique was then rapidly developed for the culture of other normal tissue organoids [ 24, 26] and for generating various types of cancer organoids [17], such as liver [ 27, 28] , pancreatic [29], bladder [29], and breast [30] cancer organoids.

Huch et al. [31] developed an organoid culture system for generating the first human liver organoids by isolating and expanding adult bile duct cells. By modifying the protocol, they further established liver cancer organoids [27]. Specifically, they removed Noggin, Rspo-1 and Wnt3a from the culture medium while adding dexamethasone to facilitate the expansion of tumor cell organoids, preventing contamination from nontumor cells [27]. Using this protocol, 7 liver cancer organoids were generated with a success rate of 37.5% [27]. Notably, liver cancer organoids can be successfully generated from tumor needle biopsy with an efficiency of 26% using a similar culture medium [28]. The ability to develop liver cancer organoids from needle biopsies overcomes a major challenge in personalized medicine, as most liver cancer patients receiving systemic therapies are those with intermediate or advanced tumor stages, who are no longer suitable for surgical resection.

These culture protocols were rapidly used to generate liver cancer organoids on a large scale. Recently, a large panel of 52 liver cancer organoids, consisting of 38 HCC organoids, 9 cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) organoids and 5 CHC organoids, was generated with a success rate of 29% [32]. Furthermore, liver cancer organoids can also be established from PDTX tissues. Fourteen PDTX-derived HCC organoids have been developed [33], demonstrating the possibility of high-throughput drug screening in liver cancer organoids and further validation of the efficacy and toxicity of candidate drugs in PDTX models.

Similar to cancer cell lines, 80%‒90% of the genetic mutations in cancer tissues were preserved in early and late passages of organoids. Liver cancer organoids also retain copy number alterations in original cancer tissues [ 27, 28] . However, compared to cell lines, liver cancer organoids are superior in maintaining the pathological histology of original tumor tissues. They resembled the pathological structures and growth pattern of the original tumor tissues while also maintaining tumor-matrix interactions. Although the efficiency of generating liver cancer organoids was not satisfactory and was influenced by the proliferation rate and differentiation status of primary tumors [ 27, 28] , liver cancer organoids provide new tools for precision medicine research.

Applications of Patient-derived Liver Cancer Cell Models in Precision and Personalized Medicine of Liver Cancers

Recent breakthroughs in the development of large-scale new liver cancer cell models have revolutionized the study of genetic heterogeneity and drug response profiles of liver cancers. Here, we outline the applications of patient-derived liver cancer cell models from 5 aspects. (1) The creation of biobanks of PDCs or PDOs can be used to define the pharmacogenomic interactions in liver cancers, which enables the identification of new therapeutic targets and molecular biomarkers to predict drug responses [19] ( Figure 1). (2) With the optimized culture protocols of PDCs or PDOs, patient-derived short-term cultured 2D tumor cells or organoids can be rapidly established and serve as patient avatars for drug testing within a clinical timeframe, which can guide personalized treatments in clinics ( Figure 2). (3) Organoid cocultures have allowed for modeling tumor-microenvironment interactions, such as tumor-immune interactions [34], and therefore could be used to analyze the effect of the tumor microenvironment on drug response and to identify novel therapies such as personalized immunotherapies for liver cancer patients. (4) Combining gene editing with organoid cultures allows for modeling the development of liver cancer by sequentially introducing oncogenes or tumor suppressors. (5) The combination of innovative bioengineering approaches and organoids provides new cancer models for precision or personalized medical research on liver cancer.

The creation of patient-derived cell model biobanks allows for defining the pharmacogenomic landscape in liver cancers

The aim of precision medicine is to identify personalized treatments with the greatest likelihood of clinical benefit by exploiting patient-specific vulnerabilities [35]. However, molecular diagnostic tests of patient tumors often fail to suggest effective treatments, due in large part to a lack of pharmacogenomic knowledge banks that establish associations between molecular alterations and drug response profiles. PDCs or PDOs can both define pharmacogenomic interactions and identify biomarkers of drug responses when used at a sufficient scale.

In the past few decades, cancer cell lines have been used to define a pharmacogenomic landscape in human cancers and build a data bank [19]. In 2012, two pioneering studies initiated by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and the Broad Institute reported that they used a large number of cancer cell lines to define drug response-related biomarkers, named the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) and Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC), respectively, which included 947 and 639 cancer cell lines [ 36, 37] . These two efforts demonstrated, for the first time, a collection of a large number of cancer cell lines allowing for the creation of a knowledge bank linking drug response to genomic data [ 36, 37] .

Because genomic profiles and drug responses vary greatly among different cancer types, several studies in breast [38], lung [ 39, 40] and melanoma cancers [41] have demonstrated the necessity of using tissue-specific cell models to establish the pharmacogenomic knowledge bank of specific cancers. In the field of pharmacogenomic study in liver cancer, PDCs have been used to study the interactions between genomic features and drug responses and to identify biomarkers. Caruso et al. [42] collected 34 public liver cancer cell lines and performed screening of 31 drugs and 4 drug combinations in these cell lines. Integrated analysis of genomic (genetic mutations, transcriptomic profiles and protein expression) and drug response profiles revealed unique drug response-related molecular biomarkers. By analyzing the transcriptomic and drug response profiles of 25 public liver cancer cell lines, an independent study identified that MYC pathway-activated S2-like cell lines showed higher sensitivity to the BET inhibitor JQ1 [43]. However, the cell lines used in the two studies were established decades ago and lacked clinical data. More importantly, the small number of cell lines falls short in reflecting the heterogeneity of liver cancers, as demonstrated by only S1 and S2 molecular subtypes of HCCs being covered by these cell lines [ 42, 43] . Recently, the Liver Cancer Model Repository (LIMORE) was created, which includes 50 newly established liver cancer cell lines from Chinese HCC patients and 31 public liver cancer cell lines. LIMORE has increased the number of cell models by nearly three times, and remarkably, the expanded repertoire of cell models captures all three molecular subtypes of liver cancers [9]. Interrogation of these genomically validated models provides a pharmacogenomic dataset in liver cancers that leads to the identification of unexpected potential therapies and biomarker candidates, such as CTNNB1 mutations-HDAC inhibitors and DKK1-sorafenib.

Recent studies have demonstrated that PDOs can also be high-throughput drugs screened in 3D format. Broutier et al. [27] performed a screen of 29 drugs in a panel of 6 PDOs and identified sensitivities to the ERK inhibitor SCH772984 in both PDOs and PDO-derived xenografts. Lim et al. [33] analyzed a total of 268 drugs, which included 165 FDA-approved drugs, in 14 PDTX-derived HCC organoids. Interestingly, they found that a combination of ixazomib (proteasome inhibitor) and dinaciclib (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor) could potentially be used as a treatment for HCC. Although these studies have demonstrated the ability of liver cancer organoids in high-throughput drug screening, a comprehensive analysis of genomic data and drug response profiles for PDOs is still lacking. Nevertheless, PDOs show the potential in defining the pharmacogenomic landscape in liver cancers, which waits for further systematic analysis of gene-drug associations in PDO biobanks.

Taken together, the creation of biobanks with large-scale PDCs or PDOs can be a functional platform that allows for a comprehensive investigation of gene-drug associations and the identification of drug response-associated biomarkers, which can be further translated into clinical treatments to select for responder patients ( Figure 1).

Patient-derived short-term cultures in real-time prediction of drug response

Personalized real-time prediction of drug response is an approach to identify therapeutic options for precision cancer treatment. To do this, robust and clinically relevant models are needed to serve as patient avatars for drug testing within a clinical time window ( Figure 2).

Patient-derived short-term cultured 2D tumor cells in real-time prediction of drug response

Compared with long-term cultured cell lines, the establishment of short-term cultures has the advantage of facile derivation. These patient-derived short-term cultured tumor cells can be rapidly obtained and easily tested for drug response in vitro. They have shown potential in predicting drug response for patients in a clinical time window [44]. Liu and colleagues [45] stablished short-term cultures of both tumor cells and corresponding normal tissue cells from a patient with recurrent papilloma using the CRC system. Further drug screening results showed that vorinostat significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation compared with normal cell controls. Importantly, the patient was treated with vorinostat for three months, and tumor progression was inhibited. Lee et al. [46] further demonstrated the potential of patient-derived short-term cultures in the prediction of patient responses in a larger patient cohort. They showed that the drug response of patient-derived short-term cultured tumor cells from 31 patients was reasonably consistent with the assessments of tumor response in the clinic. These data suggested that patient-derived short-term cultured tumor cells can serve as patient avatars for drug testing and predicting patient response, providing new therapeutic options for personalized treatment.

The encouraging results above suggest that patient-derived short-term cultured tumor cells could be used as a functional testing platform for drug screening, guiding personalized treatments. While such efforts are still in their infancy for liver cancers, recent and exciting breakthroughs in the derivation and propagation of liver cancer cell lines with a relatively high success rate have provided the possibility of applying patient-derived short-term cultured tumor cells in the personalized treatment of liver cancers ( Figure 2) [ 9, 47] .

Patient-derived organoids in real-time prediction of drug response

PDOs have emerged as promising personalized medicine platforms, allowing for functional testing of clinically approved drugs and high-throughput screening of candidate therapies ( Figure 2). They showed promising results in predicting the response of counterpart patients to the same agents, although in limited cohorts of patients who were retrospectively analyzed. In a pioneering study by Vlachogiannis et al. [48], organoids were generated from patients with metastatic colorectal and gastroesophageal cancer enrolled in phase 1/2 clinical trials, allowing a direct comparison between patient response and the derived organoid response to the same agent. By analyzing the drug responses (including paclitaxel, cetuximab, and TAS-102) of patients and the derived organoids, they demonstrated that PDOs could correctly reflect the drug responses of the patients from whom they were derived. These data are encouraging for the research community.

Later, more effort was put into determining whether cancer organoids can faithfully predict the drug response of patients from whom they were derived [14]. Tiriac et al. [49] demonstrated that PDOs can recapture the clinical response to chemotherapy in a retrospective analysis of a subset of 9 patients with pancreatic cancers from which PDOs were generated. In addition, several groups have further proven the power of PDOs in predicting drug responses to radiotherapy and chemotherapy in colorectal cancer patients [ 50‒ 52] . For example, Ye et al. [52] generated an organoid biobank from patients with locally advanced rectal cancer treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiation enrolled in a phase III clinical trial. By analyzing co-clinical trial data, they demonstrated that PDOs can predict chemoradiation responses in patients with 84% accuracy, 78% sensitivity and 91.97% specificity. These promising data further highlight the potential of PDOs in predicting patient response, and PDOs can represent a companion diagnostic tool in the real-time prediction of patient response to guide personalized therapies in a clinical time window.

Although clinical data on liver cancer organoids in predicting patient outcomes are not available, liver cancer organoids have been used as a drug testing platform to assess the sensitivity of clinically approved drugs. A recent study showed that PDOs could present a different response to sorafenib, a first-line drug with limited responses in HCC patients [28]. Therapeutic tests have been performed on CCA organoids, including sorafenib, gemcitabine, cisplatin and doxorubicin, which are commonly used in CCA treatments, showing variable responses [53]. These results provide the possibility of using PDOs to identify responders or non-responders to specific therapies and subsequently guide personalized treatments for liver cancer patients.

Organoid cocultures for modeling tumor-immune interactions and identifying immunotherapies

The TME evolves during tumor development [54]. It plays a key role in tumor initiation and progression and can modify the therapeutic response of tumors. Recent years have witnessed success in tumor therapies targeting the TME. One excellent example is immunotherapy [6]. However, 2D cell lines and simple cancer organoids generally lack immune cells, making it challenging to study tumor immunology. Excitedly, recent studies have demonstrated that organoid cocultures with immune cells provide a new method to model tumor-immune interactions ex vivo [ 14, 16] .

PDOs can be cocultured with peripheral blood mono-nuclear cells (PBMCs) or purified T cells, which show the ability to present variable sensitivity to immune cell cytotoxicity [55]. Such a platform could be used for predicting patient-specific sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) or screening new immunotherapeutic antibodies. PDOs also provide a practical strategy for neoantigen-peptide identification, which could be further exploited and translated into neoantigen-directed immunotherapies. Wang et al. [56] showed that liver cancer organoids preserved the neoantigen characteristics of original tumors by analyzing the immunogenetic potential of neoantigen peptides of 27 paired tumor tissues and organoids. Nine candidate immunogenetic peptides were identified, and an organoid killing assay confirmed the antitumor activity of these immunogenetic peptide-reactive CD8 + T cells. Additionally, PDO cocultures could potentially be applied to generate a large number of effector T cells for adoptive cell transplantation [ 56, 57] . When cocultured with peripheral blood-derived lymphocytes from the same patients, PDO cocultures allowed for expanding CD8 + T cell clones due to their recognition of putative neoantigens retained in organoids [ 56, 57] . Dijkstra et al. [57] showed that cocultures of autologous cancer organoids and peripheral blood lymphocytes could be used to enrich tumor-reactive T cells from peripheral blood lymphocytes, and they demonstrated that these T cells could be used to assess the efficiency of killing matched tumor organoids.

The above organoid cocultures could only integrate a few immune cell components, limiting their ability to recapture a realistic immune microenvironment in the original tumors. Air-liquid interface (ALI) culture offers a new approach to generating cancer organoids, which can maintain native stromal and immune components. In a pioneering study, Neal et al. [58] applied ALI cultures to generate cancer organoids. They demonstrated that ALI cultures could preserve a variety of immune cells, including T cells, B cells, NK cells and macrophages, with consistent proportions in original tumors. Furthermore, T cell clonal diversity in these ALI cultures mirrored the T cell diversity in the patient’s tumors, and those T cells retained in organoids could be maintained for more than 30 days [58]. ALI cultures have been widely used in generating organoids of different types of cancers, such as renal clear cell carcinoma [59] and colorectal cancers [60]. Importantly, ALI organoid cultures can be used as a functional platform to test immunotherapeutic responses [58]. Potentially, such an organoid technique could be further extended to liver cancer.

Overall, recent progress in cocultures of liver cancer organoids with immune cells has paved the way for liver cancer immunology research. These innovative organoid models could be used as functional test platforms to assess the efficacy of immunotherapy and optimize or identify novel immunotherapeutic strategies. This could be the first step towards personalized immunotherapy for liver cancer patients.

Liver organoids allow for modeling liver carcinogenesis and identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities

Due to the complex makeup of genetic alterations in PDOs, it is challenging to study the function of an individual cancer gene in cancer initiation and progression. In addition, the frequency of some cancer genes is remarkably low. For example, the frequency of many cancer genes in HCC is only approximately 5%, and there are often no corresponding patient-derived cell models [9]. The combination of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) technology and organoid cultures yields a tractable and feasible toolbox with which to generate human cancer models in a stepwise fashion [ 14, 61] . Several studies have demonstrated that the gradual introduction of cancer-related driver genes or tumor suppressor genes into normal tissue organoids via gene editing can mimic the initiation and progression of cancers such as pancreatic ductal [ 29, 62, 63] and colorectal cancer [ 64‒ 67] .

Gene editing of normal liver organoids allows the in vitro mutational modeling of liver carcinogenesis [61]. As a proof of concept, Sun et al. [68] introduced c-Myc, an important oncogene in human HCCs, into hepatic organoids derived from transdifferentiated hepatocyte-like cells and observed excessive contacts between the mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum membrane, which appeared to be an unrecognized oncogenic event in HCC initiation. Furthermore, they identified that RAS-induced organoids formed CCA after xenotransplantation into immunodeficient mice in vivo, suggesting that hepatocytes could be the cell of origin for CCA [68]. The function of other cancer genes was also investigated in normal liver organoids. The loss of the deubiquitinating enzyme BAP1 by CRISPR/Cas9 technology functionally disrupted the epithelial tissue structure and cell polarity of cholangiocyte organoids, which appeared to be critical for the acquisition of malignant features in human CCAs [69]. Murine CCA organoids were developed and further genetically edited by RNA interference and CRISPR/Cas9 technology to produce tractable CCA organoids that recapitulate the multiple steps in the development of CCA [70].

The combination of gene editing and organoid cultures holds great promise in studying the effect of individual genetic alterations on cancer phenotypes, including structural and functional changes during carcinogenesis. These engineered organoids provide a platform to exploit therapeutic vulnerabilities related to specific genetic alterations. For example, Sun et al. [68] identified that Mdivi-1, an inhibitor targeting DRP1 that operates in the interaction between the ER and mitochondria to facilitate mitochondrial fission, can significantly prevent c-Myc-induced HCC formation. These data suggested that excessive mitochondria-endoplasmic reticulum coupling could be a targetable vulnerability for c-Myc-related liver cancers. Collectively, these isogeneic organoid models generated by the genetic editing of any cancer gene could further expand our toolbox to identify new therapeutic targets for the precision treatment of liver cancers.

Innovative bioengineering approaches in organoid research

The conventional methods of organoid cultures rely on 3D culture of stem cells or differentiated cells in extracellular matrix. Although these methods have been widely used because of their simplicity, current organoid cultures fall short in controlling organoid formation and its local microenvironment, generating perfusable vascular networks and modeling multiorgan interactions [71]. In recent years, great progress has been made in innovative bioengineering approaches, such as 3D bioprinting and organ-on-a-chip technology. Researchers are exploring these technologies as platforms to address major technical challenges in organoid research.

Unlike the uncontrollable structural formation and local microenvironment in traditional organoid cultures, 3D bioprinting reproducibly reconstructs the TME in 3D cancer models [72] and allows user-controlled deposition of multiple biomaterials, cells, and biomolecules in a predefined architecture [73]. For example, Li et al. [74] generated a bioprinted cancer model characterized by a central zone composed of CCA cells and a peripheral zone containing tumor-associated stromal cells, including macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. They found that tumor cells in the heterotypic models showed higher proliferation and greater invasion compared to both 2D culture and 3D monoculture. Therefore, the combination of 3D bioprinting technology and cancer organoids will provide controllable and reproducible cancer models for analyzing the tumor-microenvironment interactions.

Organ-on-a-chip represents another innovative approach that meets enthusiasm by researchers from the organoid field. It is an active field, and integrating cancer organoids with organ-on-a-chip technology opens new frontiers in cancer research [71]. Organ-on-a-chip can introduce a perfusable vascular network in organoid cultures, which is a major challenge in traditional organoid cultures. Shirure et al. [75] developed a microfluidic device that supported vasculogenic self-assembly of endothelial cells in a 3D vascular network, and this microdevice allowed the vascularization of cancer organoids that supported long-term organoid cultures. Importantly, the vascularized cancer organoid-on-a-chip could be used for testing anti-vascular therapies [76]. Furthermore, vascularization-on-a-chip can be used as an in vitro model for cancer metastasis. Jeon et al. [77] developed a 3D microfluidic model that enabled the study of organ-specific human breast cancer cell extravasation into a human bone-mimicking microenvironment through a perfusable vascular network.

Notably, different organ-on-a-chip models can be fluidically connected to develop a multiorgan-on-chip system capable of modeling multiorgan interactions, which remains a major challenge in traditional organoid cultures [71]. For example, Skardal et al. [78] constructed a heart-lung-liver model by fluidically linking 3D-printed liver and heart organoids with engineered lung tissue in a modular fashion. One could imagine that the creation of a multiorgan-on-chip model by combining cancer organoids with other normal tissue organoids would more closely mimic the physiological environment in the body than cancer organoids alone. During clinical treatment, it would be necessary to conduct system-wide evaluation to gain a better understanding of the patient’s systematic response to the drug. These multiorgan-on-chip models could provide new platforms for systematically evaluating drug efficacy in tumor tissues and side effects on other normal tissues.

Challenges and Perspectives

Patient-derived cell models show great promise for precision medicine in the treatment of cancers. Recently established novel patient-derived cell lines and organoids expand our toolbox that will enable more comprehensive investigation of pharmacogenomics in liver cancers, which could lead to the identification of drug response-related biomarkers or therapeutic vulnerabilities. Additionally, patient-derived short-term cultured 2D tumor cells or organoids showed promising and encouraging results in predicting patient response to drugs, suggesting that they might serve as a companion diagnostic assay to direct treatment choices for individual patients. However, several challenges need to be addressed to improve liver cancer cell models.

One of the main challenges is the relatively low success rate of generating liver cancer cell models. The successful generation of PDOs can be biased by the proliferation and differentiation status of liver cancer tissues [ 27, 28] . Liver cancer organoids were all developed from poorly differentiated or highly proliferated liver cancers, while well-differentiated or poorly proliferated liver cancer cells are difficult to expand as organoids by the current protocols [ 27, 28] . Additionally, tumor size, vascular invasion and advanced tumor stage were also associated with the success rate of establishing HCC organoids [32]. Considerable efforts are needed to further optimize the current protocol to develop PDOs. Genetic profiling of the original tumors from both successful and unsuccessful organoids may be helpful in better understanding this process and improving the culture system. Furthermore, standardization of organoid cultures, including the cocktails of growth factors, extracellular matrix and devices, will enhance the reproducibility of organoid cultures.

As a pharmacogenomic research platform, the number of currently available cell models is insufficient to capture the high genetic diversity of liver cancers. Although the LIMORE platform included 81 liver cancer cell lines, covering 85% of cancer functional genes in HCCs, there are still some low-frequency cancer functional genes that are not covered [9]. Moreover, only 51% of cancer functional genes have three or more cell lines in which they are represented [9]. For liver cancer organoids, it is necessary to optimize the organoid culture system to improve the establishment efficiency, particularly for liver cancer tissues for which it is challenging to generate counterpart organoids [ 27, 28, 32] . Potentially, with the further development of organoid cultures, liquid biopsies could be used for extracting circulating tumor cells to generate organoids. The creation of a biobank with PDCs or PDOs established from a large number of liver cancer patients will better represent the high interpatient heterogeneity and increase the statistical power of pharmacogenomic analysis. Notably, current PDC or PDO protocols may select for specific cell clones; that is, these cell models do not represent intratumoral heterogeneity. It will be promising to generate PDCs or PDOs from several regions of one tumor and to estimate the intratumoral heterogeneity and drug response profiles.

Another challenge for liver cancer cell models is to reconstruct or maintain the complex TME in the parental tumors. Recent progress in organoid cocultures provides new methods to model tumor-immune interactions ex vivo. These organoid cocultures consist of a few immune cell components, falling short in mimicking the native TME in original tumors. Innovative organoid cultures, such as ALI cultures, are needed to generate novel liver cancer organoid models that can maintain the native TME. Including TME components in addition to immune cells, for example, vascular populations, will open additional research directions for organoid models. Cocultures of liver cancer organoids with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) can recapitulate crosstalk between tumor and endothelial cells by secreting angiocrine factors [79]. However, such organoid cocultures lack complex vascular structures. Microfluidic 3D cultures enabled the construction of a functional vascular system, which may be a practical way to develop a vascular system in the TME [ 71, 80] . Organoid cultures that combine immune cells and a functional vascular system will provide valuable preclinical models for evaluating the efficacy of combination therapy of immunotherapies and anti-vascular therapies, which are effective treatments approved for liver cancer [6]. Furthermore, innovative bioengineering approaches, such as 3D bioprinting and organ-on-a-chip technology, will provide new methods to reconstruct the TME in organoid cultures.

Importantly, to develop liver cancer PDOs for guiding prospective cancer therapy in clinical settings, innovation in organoid cultures should be achieved to further reduce the time required for PDO generation and drug testing. Remarkably, Ding et al. [81] developed droplet emulsion microfluidics with temperature control and dead-volume minimization to rapidly generate thousands of microorganospheres (MOSs) from clinical biopsy tissues, which enabled rapid drug testing within 14 days, a timeline suitable for guiding clinical treatment decisions. By employing an integrated superhydrophobic microwell array chip (InSMAR chip), Hu et al. [82] generated hundreds of lung cancer PDOs and finished drug testing in these organoids within 1 week after surgical operation or tissue biopsy. Notably, the one-week drug response profiles in PDOs were in good agreement with clinical outcomes, although in a small cohort of patients. These innovative organoid cultures should be further extended to liver cancer PDOs to accelerate the clinical application of liver PDOs.

Despite these challenges, liver cancer cell lines and organoids have emerged as promising in vitro models for translational applications and the development of precision cancer treatments for liver cancers. Patient-derived short-term cultured 2D tumor cells or organoids can be used for real-time drug testing and as a tool for functional diagnosis [ 14, 44] . One could envision that freshly resected liver tumors or needle biopsies are sequenced and expanded as short-term cultured 2D tumor cells or organoids in parallel. Drug screening and genomic profiling of these patient-derived short-term cultured 2D tumor cells or organoids could be performed to identify potential therapies. Organoid cultures with immune cells derived from patients will be used to test potential immunotherapies for liver cancer treatments. Notably, it will be essential that patient-derived 2D tumor cells or organoids can be generated and expanded efficiently to enable drug screening in a clinically accepted time window. Moreover, initial clinical studies should be designed to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of these cell models in reflecting the clinical outcomes in a large number of patients [14].
