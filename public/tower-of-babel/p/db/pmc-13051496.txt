Quantitative assessment and prognostic value of myocardial work indices in sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction
Authors: Xu Yanping, Yang Xiaojuan, Wu Jiali, Yang Xue, Pan Lu, Wang Xiaohong, Yang Ying, Wang Ying, Cao Wei, Na Lisha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigates the value of myocardial work indices (MWI) in quantitatively assessing left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) in patients with sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM-LVSD) and in predicting outcomes in sepsis.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 180 patients were categorized into sepsis and SICM groups based on echocardiographic findings. The patients further divided into survival and event groups based on the 28 days prognosis. The study assessed the diagnostic and prognostic value of MWI and various conventional echocardiographic parameters, including global work index (GWI), global constructive work (GCW), global wasted work (GWW), global work efficiency (GWE), global longitudinal strain (GLS), and left ventricular ejection fraction(LVEF). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the diagnostic value of MWI parameters for SICM-LVSD and their prognostic efficacy.ResultsMWI were significantly impaired in the SICM and event groups, with GWI, GCW, and GWE being significantly lower, while GWW was higher in the SICM compared to the sepsis group (all P < 0.01). GLS demonstrated the highest diagnostic value for SICM-LVSD (AUC = 0.81), followed by GWI and GCW (AUC = 0.77 for both). In terms of prognosis, GWI, GCW, GLS, and GWE were the most effective predictors of adverse events (AUC values of 0.75, 0.74, 0.72, and 0.70, respectively).ConclusionMWI can accurately assess LVSD in patients with SICM. GLS, GWI, and GCW demonstrate high diagnostic efficacy. While GWI, GCW, and GWE provide superior prognostic value for 28-day outcomes compared to traditional measures like LVEF. MWI represents a promising new echocardiographic approach that integrates myocardial strain with afterload, offering a more physiologic evaluation of myocardial performance.Graphical Abstract

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy(SICM) is a clinical syndrome characterized by transient and reversible myocardial dysfunction during sepsis [2, 3]. It typically manifests as acute single or bi-ventricular systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction that is not related to coronary artery disease [4]. New-onset left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) in sepsis, with no alternative cause, is widely recognized as a key diagnostic criterion for SICM [4–6]. Moreover, the presence of LVSD in sepsis is an independent risk factor for in-hospital mortality [7]. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is a common measure used to evaluate left ventricular systolic function in clinical practice. However, LVEF's diagnostic accuracy is restricted because it fails to sensitively and precisely reflect the inherent contractile state of the septic myocardium under conditions of changed preload, afterload, or cardiac geometry [8]. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) is a more sensitive indicator of left ventricular systolic function than LVEF [9]. Nevertheless, GLS is still influenced by afterload [10]. Myocardial work indices (MWI) is an innovative echocardiographic technique, offer a more precise assessment of myocardial function by combining left ventricular deformation and afterload parameters. This integration provides a more comprehensive evaluation of the myocardium and can detect subclinical dysfunction [11].

This study innovatively applies the MWI to the quantitatively assess SICM-LVSD in patients within the intensive care unit (ICU), thereby investigating its prognostic value in sepsis.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This prospective cohort study included 180 patients diagnosed with sepsis who were admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) at the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University from March 2023 to March 2024. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed on days 1, 3, and 5 after ICU admission. Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence of left and/or right ventricular dysfunction: the Sepsis group and the SICM group. The patients were further classified into the survival group (Sur group) and the event group (Eve group) based on their 28-day outcomes.

Inclusion criteria

Age between 18 and 85 years. Diagnosis of sepsis, meeting the Sepsis 3.0 criteria [12].

Exclusion criteria

(1) Pregnancy; (2) Post—cardiac surgery status; (3) Acute coronary syndrome; (4) Severe tricuspid regurgitation, moderate or greater aortic and/or mitral regurgitation, mild or greater valvular stenosis, and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction; (5) Metastatic malignant tumors; (6) Cases with unclear transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) images or suboptimal tracking in more than two segments during Speckle Tracking Echocardiography (STE) analysis.

Diagnostic criteria for ventricular dysfunction

LVSD was diagnosed if the LVEF was less than 50% [9, 13] or if the absolute GLS was less than 17% [14, 15].

Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction was diagnosed based on the following criteria: Left atrial volume index (LAVI) > 34 mL/m2. E/e' ratio > 14. Septal e' < 7 cm/s or lateral e' < 10 cm/s. Peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (VTR) > 2.8 m/s. The diagnosis is confirmed when more than two of these four criteria are met [10].

Right ventricular dysfunction was diagnosed if the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) was less than 16 mm [10].

Ethics approval

This study complies with medical ethics requirements and has been approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (Ethics Approval No.: KYLL-2023–0240).

Examination methods and echocardiography-related parameters

Comprehensive TTE examinations were conducted using a GE Vivid E9 ultrasound system equipped with an M5S probe (1.7–3.4 MHz). The examination protocol included two—dimensional (2D) imaging, M—mode echocardiography, spectral Doppler (both pulsed—wave and continuous—wave Doppler), color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI), and tissue Doppler imaging (TDI). Images were archived in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format for subsequent offline analysis using the proprietary GE Echo PAC software (version 203). At the conclusion of each TTE examination, brachial systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured using a cuff sphygmomanometer.

Conventional TTE parameters

Left atrial anteroposterior diameter (LADAP), superior-inferior diameter (LADSI), and left–right diameter (LADLR) were measured. Left atrial volume (LAV) was calculated using the formula LAV = LADAP × LADSI × LADLR × 0.523, and the left atrial volume index (LAVI) was derived as LAVI = LAV/BSA, where body surface area (BSA) was calculated using the formula: BSA (m2) = 0.0061 × height (cm) + 0.0128 × weight (kg)—0.1529. The left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd) and end-systolic diameter (LVDs) were also measured. Left ventricular end-diastolic volume (EDV), left ventricular end-systolic volume (ESV), stroke volume (SV), and LVEF were assessed using the modified biplane Simpson's method. Mitral inflow velocities, including the E and A waves, as well as septal e' (e septal) and lateral e' (e lateral) were measured. The average e' was calculated as (e' septal + e' lateral)/2, and the E/e' ratio was determined. Additionally, the peak velocity of tricuspid regurgitation (VTR) and the peak pressure gradient (PGTR) were measured. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and s' were measured in the four-chamber view, focusing on the right ventricle. The long-axis view of the inferior vena cava (IVC) was obtained, with the M-mode sampling line placed 2 cm distal to the junction of the IVC and right atrium, or distal to the hepatic vein confluence with the IVC. Data were acquired over three respiratory cycles, measuring the end-expiratory and end-inspiratory IVC diameters, calculating their respective averages, and assessing respiratory variation to estimate right atrial pressure. Finally, pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) was calculated using the formula PASP = PGTR + right atrial pressure.

Myocardial Work Indices (MWI) analysis

Two-dimensional grayscale images of the apical four-chamber, two-chamber, and three-chamber views were acquired over three consecutive cardiac cycles at a frame rate of 60 to 80 frames per second (fps) and subsequently stored. For the apical three-chamber views, clear visualization of the mitral valve (MV) and aortic valve (AV) was required, while the apical four-chamber and two-chamber views were expected to fully display the left atrium (LA).

Echo PAC software launches Myocardial Work analysis, measures GLS, determines MV and AV open and close times based on PW, and you can enter SBP and DBP and click the Advanced button to obtain MWI parameters, including ①GWI, ②GCW, ③GWW, and ④GWE.

Laboratory analysis

Venous blood samples were collected from all patients within two hours after the transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) examination. The samples were then sent to the central laboratory of Ningxia Medical University General Hospital for analyses. The analysis involved measurements of cardiac troponin I (cTn—I), N—terminal pro—B—type natriuretic peptide (NT—proBNP), a complete blood count, and evaluations of liver and renal functions.

Reproducibility assessment

An associate chief physician from the ICU screened all cases and supervised echocardiographic image acquisition. A chief physician completed the echocardiography examinations and conventional data measurements. Two independent physicians, who were blinded to patients’ clinical data and study group assignments, conducted the measurements of MWI parameters and subsequent data analysis.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (Version 4.2.0). Normally distributed continuous dose data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (-χ ± S), and independent-samples t-tests were used for inter—group comparisons. Non—normally distributed continuous data are reported as median (interquartile range) [M(IQR)], and non—parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test for two groups, Kruskal–Wallis H test for multiple groups) were utilized for intergroup comparisons. Categorical data are expressed as counts (%), and comparisons were performed using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher's exact test.

Based on the results of single-factor analysis, combined with echocardiographic guidelines and expert consensus on ventricular dysfunctio, multiple logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between MWI and SICM-LVSD.

To further investigate the correlation between MWI and clinical prognosis, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze the association between MWI and mortality risk. Relevant metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and optimal cutoff values, were calculated through diagnostic experiments, and a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated to evaluate the diagnostic utility of MWI in SCM—LVSD and its prognostic value in sepsis patients.

A two-tailded P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of clinical and TTE data between the sepsis and SICM groups

Patients in the SICM group were significantly older and had a higher incidence of supraventricular arrhythmias compared to the Sepsis group. Additionally, the SICM group showed elevated APACHE II scores, SOFA scores, NT-proBNP and cTn—I levels, Lac levels, and a higher 28-day mortality rate. Significant reductions were observed in SV, LVEF, lateral e' velocity, TAPSE, and s' velocity in the SICM group (all P < 0.01). Moreover, patients in the SICM group had lower values for GWI, GCW, GWE, and GLS, but higher values for GWW, PASP and PSD (all P < 0.01). Discussion

Sepsis remains the most common cause of death in critically ill patients, with the heart is one of the most frequently affected organs in sepsis. Ttransthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the primary tool for detecting associated cardiac dysfunction [16]. In this cohort of 180 sepsis patients, 102 presented with various forms of cardiac dysfunction, resulting in a SICM incidence rate of 56.7%. Our finding is consistent with the research conducted by WEN Yuqi et al. [17]. who reported a similar incidence of approximately 60%. The LVEF in the SICM group was measured at 62.00%, and LVEF demonstrated limited diagnostic effectiveness for SICM-LVSD, (AUC = 0.67). During the early stages of sepsis, patients ofen experience hypotension caused by vasodilatation, loss of vascular tone, and reduced catecholamine responsiveness. These factors can affect circulating blood volume, flow, and pressure, with consequent effects on myocardial perfusion and function [18]. As a result, even when myocardial function is impaired, LVEF may remain within normal limits. This underscores the limitations of relying solely on LVEF less than 50% for diagnosing SICM [19, 20].

The 28-day mortality rate was 7.7% in the sepsis group and 38.2% in the SICM group, the presence of SICM significantly increases the mortality rate of sepsis [21]. Therefore early detection of SICM and timely intervention are crucial for reducing sepsis-related deaths. GLS is the preferred method in current research for examining the association between left ventricular systolic function and prognosis in SICM patients [22]. In the SICM group, GLS was significantly lower than that in the sepsis group (−12.30% vs −17.25%), highlighting the ability of GLS to detect subclinical LVSD, even when LVEF remains normal. Additionally, GLS in the event group was significantly lower than in the survival group (−12.20% vs −15.80%), which corresponds to a significant increase in the risk of mortality [23].

Increased afterload reduces GLS values, which may lead clinicians to misjudge true myocardial contractility [24]. MWI enables the noninvasive construction of left ventricular pressure-strain loop, allowing for a quantitative assessment of left ventricular systolic function under varying afterload conditions. This integrated approach provides a more accurate assessment of intrinsic myocardial function and enhances the detection of subclinical dysfunction [11], demonstrating a strong correlation with established biomarkers such as NT-proBNP [25].

The MWI parameters include GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE, where GWE = GCW/(GCW + GWW) × 100%. GWI representing the area of PSL, denotes the stroke work of the left ventricle during systole. GCW is the work contributing to ejection, while GWW (calculated as GWI minus GCW) represents the work opposing ejection. GWE reflects the efficiency of myocardial work. GWI and GCW are considered indicators reflecting the overall efficiency of the left ventricle, with their decreased values indicating LVSD [26]. Notably, GWI is closely associated with myocardial oxygen consumption, whereas GCW exhibits a weaker correlation [26]. Compared to the normal population MWI data from 64 healthy adult volunteers reported by Ding Xueyan et al. [27], the GWI, GCW, and GWE values in our study's cohort of 78 sepsis patients were significantly lower. The heart is a high-energy-consuming organ, and the completion of its physiological activities relies on the supply of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) from myocardial cell mitochondria.In sepsis, the ultrastructure of myocardial mitochondria is damaged, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequently compromising the energy supply to cardiomyocytes. In this state of reduced energy production, sepsis serves as a protective mechanism by suppressing mitochondrial function to reduce oxygen consumption in cardiomyocytes, thus presenting as myocardial depression. [28].This discrepancy can be attributed to the fact that most patients with sepsis exhibit some degree of LVSD.

Our findings showed that GWI, GCW, and GWE were significantly lower in the SICM and event groups compared to the Sepsis and Survival groups, along with markedly increased levels of cTn-I and NT-proBNP. Furthermore, GWW was significantly higher in the SICM group. These changes reflect impaired myocardial function and energy efficiency, consistent with the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SICM. Several factors contribute to these findings: ⑴The older age of patients in both the SICM and event groups is associated with increased stiffness of the left ventricle, may decreasing myocardial compliance. The increased heart rate can shorten the filling time of the left ventricle, which increases the risk of myocardial injury, potentially resulting in a decrease in left ventricular stroke volume, GWI, and GCW.⑵The elevated requirements for norepinephrine, combined with lower systolic pressure, and significantly higher lactate levels in both the SICM and event groups, alongside an increased incidence of shock and reduced left ventricular end-diastolic dimensions observed in the event groups, indicatet impaired myocardial perfusion and ischemic injury. Collectively, these factors compromises cardiac energy supply. ⑶The elevation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) to 33 mmHg observed in the SICM group is associated with lung injury and hypoxemia resulting from infection. Even a minor increase in PASP can lead to a significant decrease in right ventricular stroke volume [29], as evidenced by the lower tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) observed in the SICM group. This reduction subsequently increases left ventricular filling pressure due to the phenomenon of ventricular interdependence [29]. ⑷Prolonged peak systolic longitudinal strain dispersion (PSD) observed in both the SICM and event groups reflects mechanical asynchrony. The work performed by the contracting myocardial segments is wasted on the asynchronous diastolic segments [27], which further compromises pump function [29].⑸Under conditions of disrupted lipid and glucose metabolism and stress responses, cardiac myocytes experience an energy deficit. This impairment in myocardial energetics may underlie the elevated GWW observed in SICM groups, which in turn contributes to the reduction in GWE. Therefore, MWI not only detects subclinical LVSD in SICM patients, but also provides mechanistic insights into myocardial energy metabolism, thereby elucidating the pathophysiology of SICM-LVSD [30].

Previous studies underscore the clinical significance of MWI. Hedwig et al. [25] posited that a GWI < 500 mmHg% serves as a predictor for severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and indicating a poor prognosis. Similarly, Chan et al. [26] demonstrated in their study on dilated cardiomyopathy that markedly reduced GWI and GWE suggest significantly impaired left ventricular myocardial systolic function. The logistic regression analysis conducted in this study revealed the higher values of GWI, GCW, and GWE were associated with a lower risk of SICM-LVSD, while higher GWW values indicated an increased risk of SICM-LVSD. In terms of diagnostic performance for SICM-LVSD, GLS exhibited the highest efficacy with an AUC of 0.81, followed by both GWI and GCW, which each had an AUC of 0.77. While the traditional indicator LVEF showed comparatively poor diagnostic performance. On the other hand, elevated values of GWI, GCW, and GWE are correlated with a reduced risk of 28-day mortality in sepsis. The GWI exhibited the highest prognostic power with an AUC of 0.75, closely followed by GCW with an AUC of 0.74, GLS at 0.72, and GWE at 0.70. LVEF demonstrated a significantly lower prognostic performance.

SICM is characterized by myocardial injury and disruptions in energy metabolism [28]. In our cohort of SICM patients, we observed significantly decreased GWI, GCW, and GWE, along with an elevated GWW. While GLS demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for identifying SICM—LVSD, GWI was the most robust predictor of 28—day mortality. As a novel measure of contractile efficiency, MWI offers enhanced clinical insight into left ventricular performance under varying load conditions and accurately reflects myocardial energy metabolism, myocardial perfusion and oxygen consumption [31].

This study presents several limitations: (1) As a single-center prospective cohort study, the sample size (n = 180) is relatively limited, which may reduce the statistical power of the tests and potentially lead to overfitting in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Furthermore, due to the single-center design, the generalizability of the study findings necessitates further validation. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the results(2) This study employed a composite diagnostic standard for SICM based on expert consensus, which includes reduced left ventricular systolic function, reduced left ventricular diastolic function, and reduced right ventricular systolic function, while excluding other etiologies. This approach may introduce case selection bias, as it does not account for other potential causes of myocardial dysfunction. (3)GLS, while more sensitive, also requires further validation to establish optimal diagnostic thresholds for SICM-LVSD. The MWI provides valuable insights into myocardial function, it relies on high-quality echocardiographic imaging, which can be challenging in critically ill patients due to factors such as poor acoustic windows and mechanical ventilation. Furthermore, this study utilized systolic blood pressure measured by a cuff at the brachial artery as a surrogate for left ventricular systolic pressure and indirectly assessed myocardial mechanics through this pressure measurement. This method fails to accurately reflect true intraventricular pressure fluctuations and neglects the effects of vascular compliance and waveform morphology, which may impact the study results. (4) In terms of methodological design, while the system evaluated core echocardiographic parameters, including left and right ventricular function, it did not incorporate a multi-organ dysfunction assessment system. Given that the prognosis of sepsis is closely related to the severity of organ dysfunction, this confounding factor may lead to an overestimation of the independent prognostic value of MWI parameters. (5)The study only considered norepinephrine as a vasoactive agent and did not account for the potential effects of other treatments, such as inotropic drugs or mechanical ventilation, on myocardial function and MWI measurements. Notably, some patients had already undergone inotropic drug therapy prior to the ultrasound examination. Although the study utilized stratified analysis to account for relevant confounding factors, there remains the possibility of residual confounding that has not been fully addressed. (6)This study established the primary endpoint at 28 days, consistent with the conventions of short-term prognostic research in sepsis. However, it did not examine the long-term trajectory of cardiac function in survivors of SICM or its impact on quality of life. Consequently, the predictive value of MWI for the long-term survival of sepsis patients requires further investigation. (7)This study primarily investigates the association between echocardiographic parameters and adverse outcomes in sepsis. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SICM are complex, encompassing metabolic disturbances, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and microcirculatory disorders. These alterations are closely linked to changes in cardiac function. Consequently, it is imperative to validate and enhance the reliability of this study's findings through multicenter, large-sample prospective research, further elucidating the relationship between SICM and alterations in echocardiographic parameters.
