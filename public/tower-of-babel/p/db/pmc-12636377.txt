Rediscovery of Vrikshayurveda: A nature-based vision for sustainability
Authors: Rajesh Kotecha, Deep Narayan Pandey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rediscovery of Vrikshayurveda principles offers a holistic framework to address contemporary environmental challenges and support sustainability goals. This analysis provides three foundational principles—Resource Utilization and Regeneration, No Harm to Nature, and Resource Conservation—rooted in the Atharva Veda and integrates ancient wisdom with modern ecological science. Through an exploration of Vedic texts and contemporary literature, these principles are shown to align with regenerative agriculture, natural forest regeneration, and biodiversity conservation, offering practical, scalable, and cost-effective solutions. Their applications extend to urban forestry, regenerative agriculture, and ecosystem recovery, demonstrating the potential to enhance soil health, improve carbon sequestration, and facilitate community-based conservation efforts. Furthermore, this approach also highlights how ancient practices, such as water harvesting and agroforestry, can inform modern strategies to achieve global restoration targets, environmental management, urban greening, and human health promotion. Future research should examine how Vrikshayurveda principles, in conjunction with contemporary science, can systematically be integrated into contemporary conservation policies, bridging cultural heritage with scientific advancements to create resilient ecosystems and sustainable societies through nature-based solutions.

Introduction

Vrikshayurveda, meaning “the science of plant life,” has its earliest conceptual roots in the Vedic period, particularly in the Atharva Veda, where plant care and ecological balance are emphasized. Over time, this knowledge evolved into a systematic discipline, with Surapala's treatise (circa 10th century CE) representing a key compilation of traditional practices. Vrikshayurveda covers soil management, plant propagation and pest control. Surpal's text describes various aspects of Vrikshayurveda [1,2]. While this ancient text is rich in the subjects it addresses [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]], there is a need to trace the roots of the fundamental principles of Vrikshayurveda. Here, we describe three foundational principles drawn from the Atharva Veda. The rediscovery of these principles addresses contemporary environmental challenges and offers solutions aligned with modern scientific understanding. Furthermore, we highlight future research areas that include non-pharmaceutical interventions for ecosystem sustainability and human well-being. Specifically, we emphasize their relevance to regenerative agriculture, urban and peri-urban forestry, and ecosystem restoration during the UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration. Additionally, we discuss the potential of Vrikshayurveda in contributing to mental and physical health through nature-based solutions, such as green exercise, stress reduction, and the prevention of mental health disorders.

The ancient Indian tradition of Vrikshayurveda is a repository of ecological wisdom. It emphasizes harmony between human activity and nature. Modern environmental challenges demand re-examining the potential of these age-old principles. This article explores three foundational principles of Vrikshayurveda, sourced from the Atharva Veda, that remain profoundly relevant today: Resource Utilization and Regeneration, No Harm to Nature, and Resource Conservation.

Previous discussions on Vrikshayurveda have largely focused on its historical, agricultural, or medicinal aspects, with limited attention to its ecological principles in the context of modern environmental science. Existing research addresses isolated practices but does not organize them into coherent frameworks linked to contemporary sustainability challenges. Our discussion provides a way forward by tracing ecological principles from the Atharva Veda, validating them through modern research, and proposing their application to various nature-based solutions for regenerative agriculture, urban forestry, ecosystem restoration, and human well-being.

The approach

Our approach here is to bridge ancient ecological wisdom with contemporary scientific perspectives. Vrikshayurveda describes numerous practices related to natural resource management but does not formalize them under a coherent framework of ecological principles. To address this gap, we conducted a focused search of primary Vedic texts, especially the Atharva Veda, known for its ecological insights, along with classical Vrikshayurveda texts. Secondary sources, including scholarly commentaries and contemporary research on environmental sustainability, were also consulted.

A purposive approach was used to identify verses encapsulating ecological themes such as resource utilization, conservation, and ethical interaction with nature. Sanskrit terms central to ecological concepts—such as prithvi (earth), vanaspati (flora), and jala (water)—were systematically traced across multiple texts to frame the selection. The collected textual data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis to extract principles of ecological relevance. These principles were then compared with findings from modern scientific research to highlight synergies and divergences. We selected only peer-reviewed journal articles, systematic reviews, and high-impact research reports published in the last fifteen years. These studies were selected based on their direct relevance to key ecological principles noted above. In this discussion, we have given priority to research aligned with the nature-based solutions for regenerative agriculture, ecosystem restoration, biodiversity conservation, and human well-being.

The rediscovery of three foundational principles of Vrikshayurveda—Resource Utilization and Regeneration, No Harm to Nature, and Resource Conservation—are rooted in the Atharva Veda and demonstrate their applicability to contemporary environmental challenges.

Resource Utilization and Regeneration

The principle of Resource Utilization and Regeneration is expressed in Atharva Veda 12.1.35: “Whatever I dig out from you, O Earth! May that have quick regeneration again; may we not damage thy vitals and heart.” [8] This verse underscores sustainable use of natural resources. It recognizes Earth as a living system whose vitality must be preserved. Ancient practices aligned with this principle through various nature-based solutions such as crop rotation, composting, and controlled resource extraction. Modern science supports these ideas through practices like regenerative agriculture [9,10], food security [11], ecosystem restoration [12], and global consequences of land use change [13], and the safe operating space for humanity [14]. Regenerative agriculture enriches soil biodiversity, reduces carbon emissions, and improves long-term productivity. Similarly, ecological restoration as a nature-based solution to climate change facilitates carbon sequestration, biodiversity and livelihoods.

This ancient guidance also aligns closely with modern approaches to agroforestry and natural regeneration. Agroforestry systems, by integrating trees into agricultural landscapes, enhance soil fertility, improve water conservation, and reduce dependency on natural forests [15,16]. These systems also act as significant carbon sinks, contributing to climate change mitigation by sequestering atmospheric CO2.

Natural forest regeneration is another critical strategy for ecological recovery. Globally, the potential for natural forest regeneration could sequester 23.4 gigatons of carbon over 30 years, making it a cost-effective and impactful solution for restoring degraded ecosystems [17]. Direct seeding, a method supported by traditional practices and modern research, has proven to be a highly efficient and affordable technique for regenerating tropical dry forests. This approach not only restores biodiversity but also strengthens the resilience of ecosystems to climate variability [18]. Participatory forest management further enhances ecological and social outcomes. By involving local communities in regeneration efforts, such programs improve livelihoods while fostering a shared responsibility for sustainable resource use [19].

These methods embody the regeneration principle by ensuring that resources are replenished and ecosystems restored. Contemporary environmental science highlights the necessity of these approaches to counteract resource depletion and habitat degradation [20]. These approaches particularly become relevant because we are living in an era of human dominated planet [21].

No harm to nature

The second principle, No harm to nature, is articulated in Atharva Veda 12.1.28: “Do not degrade the natural systems of Earth.” [8] This directive emphasizes minimizing ecological damage. Ancient Vrikshayurveda practices included ethical forestry, water conservation, and biodiversity protection. These align closely with contemporary understanding of ecosystem dynamics. Conservation biology emphasizes minimizing pollution, preserving biodiversity, and preventing habitat destruction [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. For example, industrial pollution and land-use changes have been linked to species extinction and ecosystem imbalance. Modern technologies, such as green infrastructure, pollution control mechanisms, and sustainable logging practices, directly address these issues. Ethical forestry practices in Vrikshayurveda parallel sustainable forestry certifications today, which ensure forest resources are harvested responsibly. Both ancient and modern approaches recognize that harming ecosystems undermines critical functions such as climate regulation, water purification, and soil health.

This tenet calls to avoid degrading natural systems underscores the necessity of sustainable practices to mitigate environmental harm. Deforestation, a major driver of biodiversity loss, disrupts soil ecosystems, reduces carbon storage, and diminishes essential ecosystem services [26]. Protecting intact forests is critical, as they absorb nearly half of global fossil fuel emissions annually and play a vital role in climate regulation [27].

Targeted conservation interventions have demonstrated success in reversing biodiversity declines and restoring ecosystem health. Strategies such as controlling invasive species, rehabilitating habitats, and creating wildlife corridors effectively enhance resilience and biodiversity [28]. However, conservation efforts must also address human-wildlife conflicts, which often arise from habitat destruction and overexploitation. Collaborative eco-development programs that balance ecological goals with the livelihood needs of local communities are essential for sustainable outcomes [29].

Resource Conservation

The Resource Conservation principle is highlighted in Atharva Veda 12.1.11: “O Earth! Pleasant be thy hills, snow-clad mountains and forests; O numerous coloured, firm and protected Earth! On this earth I stand, undefeated, unslain, unhurt.” [8] This verse conveys deep respect for Earth's stability and resources. Ancient practices promoted water conservation through stepwells and ponds, soil conservation through terracing and agroforestry, and the safeguarding of natural habitats. These approaches resonate with contemporary strategies for ecosystem conservation. Protecting forests, wetlands, and mountains is critical for maintaining biodiversity, regulating the water cycle, and preventing soil erosion. Renewable energy adoption and energy efficiency further exemplify modern conservation strategies by reducing dependence on fossil fuels. Traditional community-driven conservation efforts reflect participatory approaches in modern environmental management, such as community-based forest management and watershed protection programs.

The principle of Resource Conservation emphasizes the protection of Earth's landscapes and natural stability. Biodiversity hotspots, which house a significant proportion of the world's species, must be prioritized to achieve maximum conservation impact with limited resources [22]. Multifunctional forests offer a model for balancing ecological and societal needs, providing timber, carbon storage, and biodiversity conservation simultaneously [30].

Freshwater conservation is another cornerstone of sustainable development. Ancient rainwater harvesting practices, still relevant today, provide a reliable solution to water scarcity and enhance the hydrological balance of ecosystems. Such methods also support agricultural productivity, further aligning with conservation goals [31]. Preserving ecosystem resilience is crucial to preventing abrupt ecological shifts, that require proactive nature-based measures [24].

Scientific evidence supporting the effectiveness of Vrikshayurveda principles

To establish the relevance of Vrikshayurveda principles in modern environmental conservation and resource management, this section provides some studies that validate their effectiveness.

The integration of Vrikshayurveda principles with contemporary scientific approaches highlights their enduring relevance. Agroforestry, as a component of regenerative agriculture, enhances carbon sequestration, promote biodiversity, and provide economic benefits to rural communities, aligning with sustainable agricultural strategies [10]. Similarly, adaptive management frameworks for tropical forests incorporate local knowledge and societal learning to address ecological challenges effectively [32].

Educational initiatives that teach indigenous ecological knowledge embedded in Vrikshayurveda together with modern science facilitates understanding of sustainable practices. Such interdisciplinary approaches bridge the gap between traditional wisdom and contemporary environmental management. This enables innovative nature-based solutions for global ecological challenges [33].

These principles are also deeply interconnected. For example, restoration of soil organic carbon through regenerative agriculture practices, including no-tillage, cover cropping, and agroforestry, constitutes nearly 25 % of the potential of natural climate solutions globally [34], directly reflecting resource utilization and regeneration. Specifically, expanding agroforestry systems could substantially contribute to climate mitigation while improving biodiversity and farmer livelihoods [35].

Spring-shed restoration initiatives in Sikkim (Dhara Vikas Programme) operationalize Resource Conservation by protecting recharge zones through vegetative measures rooted in traditional nature-based solutions [36]. Urban forest restoration projects and modelled urban conservation globally, align with 'no harm to nature', while offering carbon sequestration and enhancing urban human well-being [[37], [38], [39]].

Similarly, the conservation and restoration of blue carbon ecosystems—including mangroves, tidal marshes, and seagrass meadows—play a vital role in both Resource Conservation and climate stabilization by sequestering large amounts of carbon [40]. Wetland restoration initiatives, particularly in peatlands and freshwater systems, have been shown to convert degraded landscapes back into net carbon sinks, although conservation of natural wetlands remains the most effective strategy [41].

Trophic rewilding initiatives—focused on restoring animal populations and their ecological roles—expand the potential of Natural Climate Solutions (NCS) by enhancing carbon storage through improved ecosystem functioning (Schmitz et al., 2023), reflecting an integrated application of all three Vrikshayurveda principles. The role of trees outside forests (TOF) has also been increasingly recognized as a scalable natural climate solution for carbon mitigation and landscape restoration, supporting both Resource Regeneration and Conservation.

Mapping and prioritizing forest-based NCS, especially in high-carbon-density regions, shows significant potential for climate mitigation through improved management and conservation [42]. Recent meta-analyses show that Natural Climate Solutions provide substantial co-benefits for biodiversity and human well-being, while also highlighting the need to manage trade-offs carefully to simultaneously achieve climate, ecological, and social goals [43,44]. Moreover, operationalizing Natural Climate Solutions successfully requires adherence to principles such as being nature-based, sustainable, measurable, additional, and equitable [45]—values deeply embedded in the Vrikshayurveda tradition.

Collectively, these cases and examples affirm that the rediscovered ecological principles of Vrikshayurveda remain vital not just as historical insights, but as living frameworks informing scalable, scientifically validated strategies and policies for climate resilience, biodiversity recovery, and sustainable human societies today. For example, these principles can also inform Aravalli Green Wall Landscape Programme, National Agroforestry Policy, Green India Mission, and other national initiatives.

Vision for sustainability

The rediscovery of Vrikshayurveda principles provides a valuable framework for addressing modern environmental challenges. For instance, integrating Kunapajala into agricultural practices has proven effective in increasing crop yields and reducing dependence on synthetic fertilizers. Recent studies emphasize regenerative agriculture's potential to address key ecological concerns through soil health improvement, biodiversity restoration, climate resilience, and sustainable food systems [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. Practices such as no-tillage, residue retention, integrated crop-livestock systems, and diversification directly promote resource regeneration and efficient utilization, aligning with the first rediscovered Vrikshayurveda principle of Resource Utilization and Regeneration. Simultaneously, the emphasis on reducing synthetic inputs, preserving soil microbiomes, and integrating natural ecosystem functions corresponds to the principle of 'no harm to nature', advocating a respectful and non-exploitative relationship with the environment [[52], [53], [54]]. Furthermore, frameworks promoting carbon sequestration, water cycling, and agroecological diversity inherently reflect the principle of Resource Conservation, ensuring long-term ecological stability while meeting human needs [47,50,55].

The tenet of 'no harm to nature' emphasizes the need for sustainable resource extraction and ecological preservation. This aligns with current conservation biology practices, including rewilding and pollution control. Projects like the restoration of riparian zones through low-intervention techniques have demonstrated success in improving biodiversity and stabilizing ecosystems.

The principle of Resource Conservation underscores the importance of preserving natural stability through efficient water and land management. Traditional methods, such as rainwater harvesting and stepwells, offer proven solutions for water scarcity and urban sustainability. Reviving these practices in urban planning can enhance climate resilience while addressing global goals like the Paris Agreement and the UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration.

First, the role of Vrikshayurveda in regenerative agriculture deserves special attention. Its principles can guide practices that improve soil health, enhance biodiversity, and increase agricultural sustainability. Recent scientific studies have begun to validate the traditional use of Kunapajala, as described in Vrikshayurveda, for enhancing soil health, plant growth, and microbial diversity. Field trials show that Kunapajala application improves crop performance in oilseeds like mustard [56] and medicinal plants such as sweet basil [57]. Nutrient profiling and microbial studies reveal that Kunapajala enhances soil nutrient availability, promotes beneficial rhizosphere bacterial communities, and contributes to crop resilience [58,59]. Broader reviews confirm that Kunapajala, alongside other traditional bio-inputs, offers a sustainable, ecologically sound alternative to chemical fertilizers in modern organic farming [60]. The research is still needed to up-scale these technologies.

Second, urban and peri-urban forestry is an emerging area where Vrikshayurveda can help create green spaces, improve air quality, and support urban biodiversity. In conjunction with rainwater harvesting, sorghum productivity has been found to increase in semi-arid regions [61]. Small-scale harvesting systems combined with irrigation have improved afforestation success in degraded mining areas [62,63]. Rainwater harvesting has also proven effective for mine spoil restoration, groundwater recharge, and enhancement of biomass production on degraded hills [63,64]. Furthermore, modern planning models now optimize rainwater harvesting site selection for agroforestry uses, balancing storage capacity with sustainability values [65,66].

Third, the non-pharmaceutical interventions of Vrikshayurveda hold immense potential. For example, future research can focus on how contact with forests and natural ecosystems enhances mental and physical health, confers happiness, and prevents mental health disorders [[67], [68], [69], [70], [71]]. Topics such as green exercise, the role of natural exposure in reducing stress and anxiety [68,69,72,73], and the physiological benefits of spending time in biodiverse ecosystems are critical areas for future research.

In a nutshell, the principles of Vrikshayurveda offer timeless solutions for achieving ecological harmony and societal well-being. By integrating ancient insights with modern scientific research in the nature-based solutions, societies can address the multidimensional crises of biodiversity, climate change and livelihoods effectively. Collaborative and community-driven conservation approaches ensure that these solutions are both sustainable and inclusive. The rediscovery and application of Vrikshayurveda principles highlight the interconnectedness of ecological and social systems, providing a pathway toward a resilient and equitable future where humans and nature coexist in mutual benefit.
