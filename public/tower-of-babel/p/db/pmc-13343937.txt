Defined bacterial consortium highlights the impact of intestinal bacteria on DNA methylation and tumorigenesis
Authors: Claudia Mercado-Rodriguez, Siddhi Chitre, Pyoung Hwa Park, Ye Yang, Anthony Pompetti, Raad Z. Gharaibeh, Jason O. Brant, Jean-Pierre J. Issa, Christian Jobin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States. While the gut microbiota has been shown to influence CRC development, the specific contribution of bacteria to DNA methylation and carcinogenesis remains underexplored.ResultsWe colonize two groups of GF ApcMin/+ mice with two consortia, one harboring a pks+ E. coli strain with "low-pks" activity (DSMZ) and the second with a clinical isolate exhibiting "high-pks" activity (UM149). These colonized mice are exposed to DSS-induced colitis, and analyzed for tumor burden, DNA methylation, and transcriptional changes. We find that colonization with C13-UM149 leads to more tumors, increased cell proliferation, and higher DNA damage compared to C13-DSMZ (p < 0.05). Methylation analyses show that C13-DSMZ causes extensive promoter hypermethylation and altered gene expression. Differential DNA methylation in mice colonized with either C13-DSMZ or C13-UM149 is associated with changes in pathways controlling tumor suppression, cell proliferation, inflammation, and Wnt signaling. In C13-DSMZ mice, hypermethylation is associated with gene expression involved in tumor suppression in both tumors and normal tissue, whereas hypomethylation is linked to expression of genes promoting Wnt signaling. In C13-UM149 mice, methylation changes were connected to genes involved in epithelial proliferation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and inflammatory responses.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that intestinal bacteria with distinct pks activities differentially modulate DNA methylation thereby influencing gene expression and tumor development. This highlights bacterial modulation of epigenetic responses as a potential mechanism underlying CRC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04183-y.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States, standing out as a major health concern [1]. The intestinal microbiota encompasses a group of microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses and fungi populating various body sites and cavities, and represents an important environmental factor to CRC pathogenesis, with some specific bacteria identified as tumor-promoting [2–6]. The mechanism by which microbiota impacts on carcinogenesis varies and includes modulation of immune response, xenobiotic metabolism, genotoxicity levels and dietary benefits [7–11]. Selective Escherichia coli strains carry a ~ 54-kb pks genomic island that enables production of colibactin, a genotoxic secondary metabolite increasingly implicated in CRC development [12, 13]. Genetic deletion of the pks genomic island abolished the ability of pks+ E. coli to promote neoplastic changes in pre-clinical models [12]. Colibactin causes direct DNA damage in colonic epithelial cells, including interstrand crosslinks and double-strand breaks [13, 14]. Importantly, repeated in vitro exposure of human intestinal colonoids to pks+ E. coli generates a unique mutational signature composed of single base substitution 88 and short insertion/deletion ID18 that has been identified in about 5% of CRC cases [11]. Moreover, this signature is enriched in early-onset CRC patients, being 3.3 times more common in individuals who were diagnosed before 40 years of age than in those over 70 years of age [15]. These findings highlight the important role of pks+ E. coli strains in colorectal initiation and progression. However, the relationship between carcinogenic bacteria and DNA methylation remains unclear.

A less developed area of research is the relationship between bacteria and epigenetics. DNA methylation is an epigenetic mark that plays an important role in the regulation of gene expression. Methylation involves the addition of a methyl (-CH3) group to cytosine (C) bases in cytosine-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides in the DNA [16, 17]. These CpG dinucleotides are typically found in clusters called CpG islands, particularly in the gene transcription start sites (TSS). When CpG islands become methylated, it leads to a series of chromatin conformation changes affecting the binding of transcription factors and other regulatory proteins, often leading to gene silencing or reduced gene expression [18]. DNA methylation affects the expression of genes involved in tumor development and progression, such as tumor suppressors or oncogenes, thus playing a direct role in tumorigenesis [19, 20]. Additionally, DNA hypermethylation represents a hallmark of CpG methylator phenotype (CIMP), which has been identified in several cancer types [21], including CRC [22], and is typically linked to microsatellite instability [23].

Previous studies have demonstrated a link between the microbiota and DNA methylation alterations [24–27]. For example, using germ-free (GF) and conventional mice, Yu et al., reported that the establishment of post-natal DNA methylation patterns in the intestine was dependent on the presence of intestinal microbiota [26]. Using the same mouse models, Ansari et al. showed that mice with or without microbiota presented different DNA methylation profiles, with microorganisms affecting the expression of genes involved in inflammation and homeostasis [27]. Despite these progresses, the contribution of bacteria to DNA methylation is not fully clear since these findings were based on complex microbial communities (fungi, viruses, bacteria, etc.). Likewise, although other groups have investigated the link between chronic infection with bacterial or viral pathogens and DNA methylation [28–30], the mechanisms remain uninvestigated. We established a 13-strain bacterial consortium (C13) representing major healthy murine gut phyla to study specific host responses, including the relationship between miRNA and bacterial gene expression and bacterial tissue location and spatial transcriptomic response following Campylobacter jejuni infection [31, 32].

In this study, we examined the exclusive contribution of bacteria to DNA methylation, gene expression and tumorigenesis by using mice colonized with a defined bacterial community of 13 species (C13) containing E. coli pks+ strains with different strengths to promote DNA damage.

Results

Differential host response to pks+ E. coli UM149 and pks+ E. coli DSMZ strain in DSS/ApcMin/+ mice

To investigate the exclusive contribution of intestinal bacteria to DNA methylation and host response, we assembled a defined consortium of 13 bacteria (C13) representing major mouse intestinal phyla. We previously used this consortium to study the impact of Campylobacter jejuni infection on bacterial ecosystem and host response [32]. This consortium includes the E. coli strain DSMZ 28618 (C13-DSMZ) or the human clinical isolate E. coli UM149 [33]. We first established the presence of the pks island in these two strains using PCR analysis of genomic DNA. Two sets of primers targeting different regions of the pks island confirmed the presence of the genomic island in both DSMZ and UM149 (Fig. 1A). We next tested the DNA damaging capacity of the two strains using the intestinal cells IEC-6. Interestingly, a weaker DNA damaging activity was observed in DSMZ- infected cells compared to E. coli UM149 infected cells as seen by western blot (Fig. 1B) and γ-H2AX immunofluorescence (Fig. 1C). To determine the impact of UM149 in neoplastic changes, we generated a clbP deletion mutant in the UM149 background (UM149 ΔclbP). ApcMin/+ mice were exposed to an antibiotic cocktail for 7 days, followed by a 1-day washout period before oral administration of UM149 or UM149 ΔclbP (108 CFU) for 2 weeks. Mice were then exposed to a one-week long treatment with 2.0% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) in drinking water (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A-C). Neoplastic changes were significantly reduced in ApcMin/+mice colonized with UM149 ΔclbP mutant compared to mice colonized with wild-type (WT) UM149, demonstrating that the presence of pks is critical for neoplastic changes in this model. At endpoint, mice colonized with C13-UM149 developed a significantly higher number of colonic tumors compared to the C13-DSMZ-colonized group (p = 0.046, Fig. 3A). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry analysis showed a higher level of DNA damaging marker γH2AX, Ki-67 expression, and nuclear β-catenin in mice colonized with C13-UM149 compared to C13-DSMZ (Fig. 3C). Changes in DNA methylation often lead to transcriptional alterations [18], and thus, RNA-seq was performed on RNA isolated from the same samples used for RRBS. Principal component analysis plot (PCA) shows significantly different clustering of the two groups, suggesting differences in gene expression profile between C13-DSMZ and GF mice (Fig. 4C). RNA-seq analysis showed 2,867 differentially expressed genes in C13-DSMZ colonized mice compared to GF mice (FDR < 0.05), with 1,632 upregulated and 1,235 downregulated genes (Fig. 4D). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) revealed significant upregulation of inflammation-related genes and pathways (Fig. 4E), such as the Th1 and Th2 pathways, the pathogen-induced cytokine storm signaling pathway, or the S100 family signaling pathway, which is involved in the interplay between inflammation and cancer. Additionally, upstream regulator analysis (Fig. 4F) identified pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, IFN-α TNF and IL-1β, as well as lipopolysaccharide as significantly predicted regulators of the observed gene expression changes after colonization with C13-DSMZ when compared with GF mice not exposed to DSS. This gene expression pattern was concomitant with a reduction in anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL10RA) and drugs (dexamethasone) expression.

Overall, these findings indicate that C13-DSMZ colonized ApcMin/+ mice showed a differential CpG methylation pattern compared to GF ApcMin/+ mice.

Even though RNA-seq and IPA analysis showed upregulation of pathways similar to those upregulated by C13-DSMZ (Fig. 5C-F), we found that stimulation of the WNT/β-catenin, interferon signaling, and production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine Il-15 were only upregulated in C13-UM149 and not C13-DSMZ when compared to GF mice not exposed to DSS (Figs. 4E and 5E).

We then sought to compare the effect of C13-UM149 and C13-DSMZ on DNA methylation and gene expression from both tumors and matched normal mucosa. Interestingly, 28,316 hypermethylated and 59,780 hypomethylated CpG sites (1.81% and 3.81% of all detected sites, respectively) (Fig. 7A) were observed in normal mucosa sites, with 120 hypermethylated and 216 hypomethylated promoters (1.04% and 1.87% of all detected promoters, respectively) (Fig. 7B). RNA-seq analysis revealed several differentially expressed genes with distinct functional roles. Aldoart1, a predicted retrogene homologous to Aldolase A (Aldoa), which functions in glycolysis, was upregulated in C13-UM149. Palm3, a membrane-associated adaptor protein involved in Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated NF-κB signaling and cytokine production, was also elevated in C13-UM149, indicating a potential role in immune regulation. In contrast, the C13-DSMZ sample showed an increased expression of Ccl9-ps1, a pseudogene related to the chemokine Ccl9, which may serve as a regulatory or epigenetic marker. Notably, Rmrp, a non-coding RNA essential for mitochondrial RNA processing and cell cycle control, was significantly upregulated in C13-DSMZ, consistent with its known roles in immune modulation (Fig. 7C). Colonization of ApcMin/+ mice with C13-UM149 led to tumors with 70,650 CpG sites hypermethylated and 65,024 sites hypomethylated compared to tumors obtained from ApcMin/+ mice colonized with C13-DSMZ (4.51% and 4.15% of all detected sites, respectively) (Fig. 7D). To gain higher resolution between DNA methylation and gene expression, we focused our analysis on promoter regions showing significant changes in methylation levels. This analysis showed that 192 promoters were hypermethylated and 220 were hypomethylated in C13-UM149 compared to C13-DSMZ-colonized ApcMin/+ mice (1.66% and 1.9% of all detected promoters, respectively) (Fig. 7E). RNA-seq analysis showed only seven genes showed differential expression in tumor samples (Fig. 7F). Among them, Il5ra, a receptor subunit for the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-5, was upregulated in tumor samples from mice colonized with C13-UM149 (logFC = 2.51; p-value = 6.34 × 10–6; FDR = 0.05). Another up-regulated gene, Galr1 (logFC = 5.25; p-value = 1.63 × 10–5; FDR = 0.07), has previously been involved in tumorigenesis, invasion and migration of cancer cells, as well as metastasis and cancer recurrence [34]. Interestingly, Minar1, described to behave as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer [35] was also upregulated (logFC = 1,73; p-value = 1,81 × 10–5; FDR = 0.07). Conversely, Tmed6 and Pcdhb19, which has been reported to be silenced in hepatocellular carcinoma [36] were found among the downregulated genes in the C13-UM149 tumor samples (logFC = −1,45; p-value = 1,99 × 10–5; FDR = 0.07 and (logFC = −1,4; p-value = 3,1 × 10–6; FDR = 0.05) (Fig. 7F).

Next, we performed correlation and integration analyses using the DIABLO framework to examine the relationship between differential DNA methylation and gene expression changes between the C13-DSMZ and C13-UM149 colonized mice. Due to the limited sample size and effect size, no statistically significant associations were detected. This is clearly illustrated by analyzing the seven differentially expressed genes with their corresponding promoter methylation pattern (Additional file 1: Table S1). Of the 7 differentially expressed genes (Fig. 7F), 4 lack promoter methylation data; no CpGs were detected in the promoter window for Pcdhb19 and Gm12708, while Il5ra (5 CpGs) and Tmed6 (2 CpGs) had insufficient coverage (< 100 merged reads) at any site. As a result, only three genes; C330024D21Rik, Galr1, and Minar1 have both expression data and reliable promoter methylation coverage. However, the relationship between methylation status and gene expression was unclear, given the limited sample size.

Overall, these findings indicate that replacing E. coli DSMZ with the cancer-promoting E. coli UM149 strain not only leads to a differential overall impact on DNA methylation, but it also induces a higher number of hypermethylated sites in the colon of ApcMin/+ mice.

To elucidate whether differential methylation induced by C13-UM149 could be associated with its tumorigenic effect, we specifically interrogated differentially methylated promoters in tumors derived from C13-UM149-colonized mice compared to C13-DSMZ-colonized mice. Overlap analysis revealed 10,692 shared sites that were hypermethylated in the C13-UM149 (Tumor vs. Mucosa) when compared to the C13-DSMZ (Tumor vs. Mucosa) group. Additionally, 86,969 sites were found to be hypomethylated in this comparison (Fig. 8A, B). We next focused our attention on the relationship between the promoters showing differential methylation in tumors from C13-UM149-colonized mice compared to C13-DSMZ-colonized mice as identified from Venn diagram of uniquely methylated promoters. We focused on genes closest to differentially methylated CpG islands and assessed whether changes in methylation correlated with transcriptional alterations. Hypermethylated promoters such as Cdh2 and Csrp1 exhibited higher expression in normal mucosa, suggesting a role in maintaining epithelial integrity, whereas Mmp9 and Mtarc1 were hypermethylated but upregulated in tumors, reflecting contributions to extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor progression. In contrast, promoter hypomethylation of Sema4c and Areg was associated with increased expression in tumors, consistent with previous reports linking Sema4c to colorectal and other cancers [37–39] and Areg to tumor growth and immune modulation [40, 41]. Additional genes, including Dusp26 and Il17rc, were hypomethylated and expressed predominantly in normal mucosa. These observations illustrate that colonization with a highly active pks+ bacteria (UM149) may drive both tumor-promoting and tissue-protective epigenetic changes, highlighting its impact on DNA methylation and transcriptional regulation in colorectal neoplasia.

In mice colonized with C13-DSMZ, Bdnf and Mecom displayed promoter hypermethylation accompanied by increased expression in tumors. Bdnf is known to support cell survival and has been implicated in colorectal tumor progression through regulation of proliferation and apoptosis [42, 43]. Mecom, a transcriptional regulator, has been associated with oncogenic signaling in multiple cancers [44, 45], and its upregulation may contribute to tumorigenic processes. Promoter hypermethylation of Lats2 and Hand2 was observed in normal mucosa consistent with their roles in maintaining epithelial homeostasis and restraining proliferation via the Hippo and developmental pathways, respectively. Conversely, hypomethylated promoters such as Cbx7 and Wnt7b showed increased expression in normal mucosa and tumors, respectively, reflecting contributions to chromatin remodeling and Wnt-mediated signaling in colorectal neoplasia. Together, these observations indicate that low-pks bacterial colonization modulates both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive pathways that are associated with epigenetic changes.

Overall, these findings indicate that a defined consortium of 13 bacteria modulates DNA methylation profile in ApcMin/+ mice, with a marked hypermethylating effect compared to GF. Replacement of E. coli with UM149 in the C13 consortium accentuates these changes in CpG sites and promoters, that may result in upregulation of pro-inflammatory and carcinogenic gene pathways and an increase in colonic tumor formation.

Discussion

Recent findings have established a relationship between the intestinal microbiota and DNA methylation [26, 27]. However, most of these findings were generated using microbiota containing complex microorganisms such as viruses, fungi and protozoa, making it challenging to elucidate a specific contribution of bacteria on DNA methylation. In the present study, we utilized a defined bacterial consortium comprising 13 species that represent the major intestinal phyla to interrogate how discrete bacterial members influence host DNA methylation, gene expression, and neoplastic progression in DSS/ApcMin/+ mice. Importantly, this model allowed us to compare the effects of a low-genotoxic pks+ E. coli DSMZ strain with the high-genotoxic pks+ E. coli UM149 strain within a defined bacterial consortium.

Exposure to the highly genotoxic UM149 strain induced pronounced DNA damage, as evidenced by increased γH2AX signals in immunofluorescence and western blot analyses, whereas the low-pks DSMZ strain elicited minimal DNA damage. This in vitro response is consistent with the in vivo phenotypes observed in C13-UM149 colonized mice, which exhibit higher tumor burden, increased Ki67 and β-catenin expression, and elevated γH2AX levels as compared to C13-DSMZ colonized mice, highlighting the potential of high-genotoxicity bacteria to drive both DNA damage and proliferative signaling. These results indicate that even a single genotoxic strain can perturb host genomic integrity and promote downstream pathways associated with tumorigenesis.

Colonization with the defined C13-DSMZ consortium elicited marked alterations in DNA methylation across the colonic mucosa, characterized predominantly by promoter hypermethylation as compared to GF mice. Consistent with these findings, our recent study demonstrated that tumor-infiltrating bacteria are enriched in CIMP-positive CRC, implying that microbiota may drive promoter CpG island hypermethylation in patients [46]. Together, these findings suggest that defined bacterial consortia in experimental models may recapitulate aspects of the promoter-hypermethylation patterns observed in complex tumor-associated microbiota in patients.

Methylation profiling further revealed that microbial colonization reshapes epigenetic regulation of key developmental and oncogenic pathways. Colonization with the C13-DSMZ consortium induced promoter hypermethylation of genes such as Bdnf and Mecom, which coincided with increased expression in tumors. Bdnf has been implicated in colorectal tumor progression through regulation of proliferation and apoptosis [42], while Mecom acts as a transcriptional regulator linked to oncogenic signaling [45]. In contrast, promoters of Lats2 and Hand2 were hypermethylated in normal mucosa, reflecting their roles in maintaining epithelial homeostasis via Hippo and developmental pathways. Conversely, hypomethylated loci, including Cbx7 and Wnt7b, displayed increased expression, consistent with their roles in chromatin remodeling and Wnt-mediated signaling during tumorigenesis. Cbx7 expression is frequently reduced in human CRC and correlates with poor prognosis; restoration of Cbx7 in cell lines reduces proliferation, underscoring its tumor-suppressive role [47]. Wnt7b is overexpressed in CRC, promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and enhances migratory capacity, with higher expression correlating with metastasis and poor patient outcomes [48]. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that gut microbiota can influence DNA methylation patterns in CRC by modulating oncogenes and tumor-suppressive genes [49].

Upon colonization with C13-UM149, the epidermal growth factor (EGF) ligand Areg is transcriptionally regulated through methylation and promoter demethylation, demonstrating methylation-dependent activation of oncogenic signaling. Notably, the EMT-associated gene Sema4c, linked to the CMS4 molecular subtype and poor prognosis CRC [39], exhibited promoter hypomethylation and increased expression in C13-UM149 colonized tumors, suggesting that bacterial genotoxicity can promote invasive phenotypes through selective epigenetic modulation. This finding is complemented with recent multi-omic analyses demonstrating that gut microbiota can reprogram host methylation by altering methyl-donor metabolism and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) availability [50]. In C13-UM149 colonized tumors, DUSP26, a dual-specificity phosphatase, regulates MAPK and Akt signaling, showing increased expression in normal mucosa compared to the tumors. In other tumor types, DUSP26 is downregulated in high-grade gliomas, and its overexpression suppresses proliferation and migration [51], highlighting its tumor-suppressive role. These observations raise the possibility that bacterial genotoxic stress may promote CRC progression by epigenetically modulating DUSP26 and downstream MAPK and Akt signaling, contributing to enhanced proliferation and invasive phenotypes.

Taken together, these findings underscore that bacterial genotoxicity and metabolic activity jointly shape the host epigenetic landscape, thereby reprogramming transcriptional networks that promote neoplastic progression. The defined 13-member bacterial consortium provides a reproducible, tractable model to study microbiota–host epigenetic interactions in CRC. Furthermore, the pronounced phenotypic and molecular differences elicited by substituting E. coli DSMZ with the highly genotoxic E. coli UM149 highlight how single-strain variation can recalibrate host methylation profiles and tumor susceptibility.

Our methylation profile revealed distinct patterns across colonization and tumor comparisons. Compared to GF, both C13-DSMZ and C13-UM149 resulted in increased global hypermethylation, indicating an induction of methylation by the defined consortium. When comparing C13-UM149 tumors to C13-DSMZ tumors, we observed global hypermethylation, with a notable exception where 1.9% of promoters were hypomethylated, as compared to 1.66% hypermethylation. These findings align with the literature, which shows that global hypomethylation can coexist with hypermethylation, leading to genomic instability and the activation of oncogenes [52, 53]. In contrast, a comparison of normal mucosa between C13-DSMZ and C13-UM149 revealed increased hypomethylation, when compared to their respective normal mucosa, indicating a hypomethylation bias. This data highlights that although hypermethylation is dominant in the tumor context, hypomethylation emerges in specific comparisons with normal or specific colonization conditions, which may reflect early epigenetic shifts.

Overall, our data show that microbiota can shift the methylome in two directions, depending on the context, with predominant hypermethylation in tumors versus hypomethylation in specific normal tissues. This dual pattern underscores the complexity of host microbiome epigenetic interplay.

Limitations of this study

Although our study establishes causal links between microbial composition, DNA methylation, and tumorigenesis, several limitations warrant consideration. The mechanistic cascade linking bacterial genotoxins such as colibactin to DNA methyltransferase activation remains unknown. Future work should integrate multi-omic strategies including targeted metabolomics, ChIP-seq, and single-cell methylome profiling to resolve how bacterial metabolites, DNA damage responses, and transcription-factor recruitment drive locus-specific methylation changes. Complementary studies using fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from patients with CRC or healthy controls to recipient mice will help assess the impact of microorganisms on methylation patterns and disease development.

Another limitation of this study is that we cannot identify a clear relationship between DNA methylation and gene expression due to the small number of differentially expressed genes with sufficient promoter methylation coverage. This may indicate that the effects we observe are indirect, limited to specific loci, or occur in regulator regions not well captured by RRBS. It should also be noted that RRBS primarily enriches for CpG-dense promoters and CpG islands and underrepresents enhancers, gene bodies and intergenic regions where key regulatory methylation changes frequently occur. The observed pairing of promoter hypermethylation with upregulation of genes such as Mmp9 and Bdnf may therefore reflect regulatory events at incompletely captured distal or intragenic elements. Thus, whole genome bisulfite sequencing will be necessary to fully understand the relationship between methylation and gene expression in our models. As a result, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than evidence of a direct causal link between methylation and gene expression. A further limitation is that RNA-seq libraries were generated in two sequencing batches, requiring batch correction using ComBat. This adjustment harmonized global expression profiles but may have reduced sensitivity to more subtle, strain-specific transcriptional differences.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that strain-specific bacterial genotoxicity drives DNA methylation reprogramming and transcriptional remodeling in the colon, thereby promoting tumorigenesis. Together, our data suggests that even a low pks E. coli DSMZ can induce detectable methylation changes in the gut, thus underscoring the sensitivity of host epigenetic responses to bacterial exposure. We further demonstrate evidence of dual epigenetic patterns, including both hypermethylation and hypomethylation, which may reflect a complex epigenetic landscape in CRC.

Methods

Bacteria strains and culture

Members of the C13-DSMZ bacteria consortium [32] were purchased from DSMZ (German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, Braunschweig, Germany). The bacterial strains were cultured individually according to their specific growth needs. Briefly, Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) (DSM 26127), Bacteroides acidifaciens (B. acidifaciens) (DSM 100502), Bacteroides caecimuris (B. caecimuris) (DSM 26085), Blautia caecimuris (B. caecimuris) (DSM 29492), Paraclostridium bifermentans (P. bifermentans) (DSM 29423), Thomasclavelia ramosa (T. ramosa) (DSM 29357), Parabacteroides goldsteinii (P. goldsteinii) (DSM 29187), Bacteroides uniformis (B. uniformis) and Parabacteroides distasonis (P. distasonis) were isolated by the Jobin’s laboratory from the healthy mice feces and cultured in a Coy Laboratories anerobic chamber at 37 °C in Mega Medium as previously described [54]. Ligilactobacillus murinus (L. murinus) (DSM 28683) and Enterococcus hirae (E. hirae) (DSM 28619) were cultured in microaerophilic conditions created by BD GasPak EZ Gas Generating Systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) at 37 °C in Mega Medium. Escherichia coli (E. coli) pks+ (DSM 28618) and Staphylococcus xylosus (S. xylosus) (DSM 28566) were cultured in aerobic conditions at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani broth (Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA).

Some experiments were conducted with C13 harboring Escherichia coli UM149 instead of Escherichia coli (DSM 28618). Escherichia coli UM149 harboring the pks+ island is a human clinical isolate obtained from an IBD patient [33].

Mouse models and husbandry

All mouse experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Florida (UF) and performed at UF Animal Care Facilities under IACUC Protocol #201909606. Mixed gender germ-free (GF) ApcMin/+ mice (129SvEv), 8–12 weeks old, were used for all experiments.

C13 consortium colonization models

Mice were transferred to gnotobiotic isolators and two days later orally gavaged with the C13-DSMZ or C13-UM149 consortium. Each species was cultured from a glycerol stock in their respective medium and oxygen requirements before CFU estimation via nanodrop for culture OD. Each strain was pooled using equal CFU amounts to reach a total of 1 × 108 CFU/mouse dosage in 200 µL of PBS (~ 7 × 107 CFU of each species). Two weeks post-gavage, mice were treated with 2% of dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) in sterile drinking water for one week and euthanized at 4 weeks following the end of DSS treatment. Stool samples were collected right before DSS treatment, the day after DSS treatment and at endpoint to quantify bacterial colonization. GF ApcMin/+ mice that were not colonized and did not receive DSS treatment were used as a control. Another group of GF mice was treated with the corresponding amount of DSS water 2 days after transfer to experimental gnotobiotic isolator, and euthanized 4 weeks following end of treatment, which served as DSS control. In-house breeding of GF ApcMin/+ mice was carried out by UF Animal Care Service staff, in sterile breeder isolators. At endpoint, the large intestine of each mouse was collected, flushed with sterile PBS using a sterile syringe and longitudinally cut open. Colonic tumors and adjacent off-tumor sites were collected from the distal colon at the same location in every mouse and snap frozen for DNA and RNA isolation. The remaining of the large intestine was swiss-rolled, fixed in 10% formalin, and submitted to the UF Molecular Pathology Core for paraffin-embedding, sectioning and H&E staining. All tissue harvesting, as well as stool collection at endpoint was sterilely performed in a BSL2 laminar flow hood. All samples were stored in sterile tubes at −80 °C until needed.

UM149 and UM149ΔclbP colonization models

SPF ApcMin/+ mice (10–12 weeks old) were pretreated with an antibiotic cocktail in the drinking water (2 g/L streptomycin, 1 g/L bacitracin, 0.5 g/L gentamycin, and 0.125 g/L ciprofloxacin) for a week, with water replaced once on day 4. After switching to regular drinking water for 24 h, mice were inoculated with 108 CFU E. coli UM149 or UM149ΔclbP via single oral gavage. One week after E. coli gavage, mice received 2.0% DSS (molecular weight 40 kDa, Thermo Fisher Scientific, J63606) in drinking water for 7 days. After switching to regular drinking water for 4 weeks, mice were euthanized and examined.

Generation of UM149ΔclbP

UM149ΔclbP was generated using the CRISPR-associated transposase (CAST) technique [55]. Briefly, crRNA targeting the 32-bp clbP coding sequence (attaagggggtacgtcagcgatttgtctacgg) was synthesized and ligated into pSC101-pSPINVchCAST (Addgene ID 160729). The constructed plasmid was then transformed into UM149 by electroporation, and the transformants were plated on spectinomycin-containing agar plates. Selective colonies were checked for genomic insertion of the chloramphenicol resistance gene payload from pSC101-pSPINVchCAST into the clbP coding sequence, by colony PCR using primers: forward, 5’ attaagggggtacgtcagcgatt 3’; and reverse, 5’ gccagtgttgaaaaaggcaatcct 3’. Successful mutagenesis was confirmed by Sanger Sequencing of the target clbP coding region.

Murine fecal DNA isolation and 16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

One-half of a fecal pellet per mouse was aliquoted into a 96-well PowerBead Pro Plate (QIAGEN), and DNA was extracted using the DNeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro QIAcube HT (QIAGEN). Firstly, fecal samples were homogenized in lysis buffer using the TissueLyser II (QIAGEN), and subsequently prepared using the QIAcube HT. After DNA isolation, the V1-V3 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA was amplified using primer pair 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 534R (5’-ATTACCGCGGCTGCTGG-3’). Both primers contained universal Illumina paired-end adapter sequences and were barcoded with unique individual 4 to 6 nucleotides between the primer sequence and the adapter sequence as previously described [56]. PCR products were sequenced in a single run using Illumina MiSeq (2 × 300).

Demultiplexed reads were fed to the DADA2 pipeline [57] for primer sequence removal, quality filtering, correction of Illumina amplicon sequencing errors and dereplication, pair merging followed by amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) generation and chimera removal. Taxonomic classification was then performed using DADA2 assignTaxonom and addSpecies functions utilizing an in-house database composed of SILVA reference dataset (v. 138.1), GTDB (v. bac120_arc122_ssu_r89) and RefSeq (v. 3).

ASVs were checked for possible contamination using decontam R package [58] using the prevalence method, and all ASVs that were detected as contaminants were removed. We then removed any ASV that was classified as non-bacterial and all singleton ASVs. This resulted in a total of 3,106,241 reads, with an average of 64,713 reads per sample (min = 50,284; max = 89,306). We agglomerated the ASVs at the species level using phyloseq package [59] and generated barplot representation of the percent relative abundance using R ggplot package.

DNA and RNA isolation from murine colonic tissues

DNA and RNA were simultaneously isolated from each tumor and adjacent off-tumor sample using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini kit (QIAGEN). Briefly, frozen samples were homogenized in 350 µL of RNA lysis buffer (RLT) buffer containing 10µL/mL of β-ME, in a sterile Eppendorf tube, using an electric pestle and subsequently passed through a 20-gauge needle fitted to an RNAse-free syringe 10 times. The homogenate was then centrifuged at maximum speed for 3 min in a tabletop centrifuge and processed according to the standard kit instructions. Isolated DNA was eluted in 100 µL of elution buffer, and isolated RNA was eluted in 50 µL of RNAse-free water.

Immunofluorescence detection of γH2AX in IEC-6 cells

IEC-6 rat intestinal epithelial cells were obtained from ATCC (CRL-1592). Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination using the Lonza MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (LT07-218). This cell line was not independently authenticated. IEC-6 cells were plated in 8-well chamber slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 5 × 104 cells per well and serum starved in 2% FBS medium overnight. Cells were treated with DMSO or the indicated MOI of E. coli UM149 or DSMZ for 4 h. After the infection, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with reduced medium + gentamicin for 20 h. Mitomycin (MMC) was used as a positive control and IEC-6 cells were treated with 3 µM of MMC for 24 h. Cells were then washed once with cold PBS and fixated in 4% methanol free formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min on ice, followed by washing with PBS and quenching of free aldehydes with a 50 mM NH4Cl solution for 10 min. Cells were permeabilized using 0.25% Triton-X 100 (Sigma Aldrich) for 5 min on ice, washed with PBS, and blocked with blocking solution (PBS containing 1% BSA and 5% normal goat serum) for 1 h on ice. Incubation with the Phospho-Histone H2AX (Ser139) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), 9718) was performed at 1:1000 dilution in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C, followed by PBS washing and incubation in the secondary Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) antibody (Life Technologies, A-11034) for 45 min at room temperature. Cells were then again washed with PBS and mounted with Vectashield mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Labs). Visualization of γH2AX immunofluorescence was performed using a Leica DM6000B upright microscope and counted as positive if more than five foci/nucleus were detected. 2 wells per treatment were used for visualization and quantification of γH2AX-positive cells. Five field of visions were captured to cover all the cells in each well, followed by quantification of the total number of cells as well as γH2AX-positive cells. The number of γH2AX-positive cells were represented as a percentage over the total number of cells.

γH2AX western blotting

IEC-6 cells were plated in 6-well plate at 200,000 cells per well and serum starved in 2% FBS medium overnight. Cells were treated with the indicated MOI for E. coli UM149 or DSMZ 28618 for 4 h. After infection, cells were washed with PBS and incubated in reduced medium + gentamicin for 20 h. MMC was used as a positive control and IEC-6 cells were treated with 3 µM of MMC for 24 h. Cells were collected after extensive washing in ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors followed by sonication at 20% amplitude for 5 s, twice, with a 10-s between pulses. Signals were detected using ECL chemiluminescence.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in 1X Citrate Unmasking solution (CST, 14746). Detection was performed using Single stain DAB substrate kit (CST, 8059) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS)

Reduced representation bisulfite sequencing was performed on tumor and adjacent off-tumor samples, following a previously described method [61]. Briefly, the mouse genomic DNA samples were digested with 100 units of MspI endonuclease (NEB, #R0106L) at 37 °C for 4 h. Blunt ends were created using Klenow DNA polymerase (NEB, #M0212) by filling in 5’ overhangs and generate 3’ dA overhangs. The size of the fragments was determined by using AMPure XP beads (Beckman, #A63881) at a volume of 2X compared to the DNA fragment. These selected fragments were then joined with methylated sequencing adapters (NEB, #E7535) using T4 DNA ligase (NEB #M0202) at 16 °C overnight. The hairpin loops of the adapters were removed using the USER enzyme (NEB #M5505). A dual size selection method was applied to isolate DNA fragments attached to the adapters, ranging from greater than 150 bp to less than 500 bp. Following this, the size-selected DNA fragments underwent bisulfite conversion using the EpiTect Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Qiagen, #59104) and were purified using 5X volume of 1:9 diluted AMPure XP beads. NEBNext Index i7 primers (NEB #7335L) were employed to amplify the libraries with EpiMark Hot Start Taq (NEB, #M0490). The concentrations of the amplified libraries were measured using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity kit (Thermo Fisher, #Q32851). The size distribution and molarity of the libraries were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent, #5067–4626). Subsequently, the libraries were pooled to a concentration of 10 nM and supplemented with 25% phiX (Illumina, #FC1103001) to ensure sequence diversity. Finally, the libraries were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument.

RRBS data processing and differentially methylated loci analysis

Sequenced RRBS libraries were trimmed using TrimGalore and aligned to the mm9 genome using the Bismark bisulfite read mapper and methylation caller [62] and bowtie2. Coverage files from Bismark output were read in using bsseq and samples were merged by microbiome and tissue group. After merging, CpGs were filtered for having at least 100 reads across all merged samples. Using the methylKit package in R. Using methylKit, differential methylation was performed on the CpG level using a Fisher’s exact test. Sites were selected for significance based on an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) less than 0.1 and an absolute methylation greater than 10%.

Differentially methylated promoter and promoter enrichment analysis

To understand which promoters are differentially methylated, the nearest gene TSS (defined as the first position of the gene annotation) was assigned to a CpG with its distance to the CpG. A CpG was considered within the promoter region of a gene if it was within 1000 base-pairs upstream and 500 base-pairs downstream of the TSS. The CpG sites falling within the promoter regions were selected for and the CpG closest to a gene’s TSS was assigned to the gene. If two CpGs had the same distance from a gene’s TSS, (e.g. 10 bp upstream and 10 bp downstream) the CpG with the smaller p-value & larger absolute methylation was selected. Significance was determined as an adjusted p-value less than 0.1 and an absolute methylation greater than 10%. Promoters were tested for enrichment within the “Hallmark” pathways from msigDB using the fgsea package in R. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed against the “Hallmark” pathways using the Ensembl ID of the genes. To generate a rank for gene set enrichment, the -log10 of the p-value was calculated and then multiplied by the sign of the methylation. A pathway was considered enriched if the adjusted p-value was less than 0.05.

Overlap analysis

To see which promoters overlapped in each analysis, the eulerr R package [63] was used. Significant promoters were isolated from each comparison and Ensembl IDs were used to identified genes overlapped. The statistical significance of the overlap was assessed using the hypergeometric test, and p-values were computed via Fisher’s exact test.

RNA sequencing library construction and sequencing

RNA samples were subjected to quality control on a 4200 Tapestation (Agilent), and concentrations were measured by the QUBIT fluorescent method (Life Technologies). For library construction, RNA input was normalized across all samples using a total RNA input of 250 ng (RIN > 7). PolyA selection was performed using the reagents provided in the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs, E7490L), and library construction was performed with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, E7760L) according to the manufacturer's user guide. Briefly, 250 ng of total RNA was used for mRNA isolated using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module. Then, the poly A enriched RNA was fragmented in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Buffer via incubation at 94 °C. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase and a random hexamer primer followed by ds cDNA synthesis, end-repair and dA-tailing. Illumina adaptors were ligated to the sample, amplified, and purified using AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter). The library quality and concentration were assessed using a High Sensitivity DNA1000 Screen Tape by Agilent TapeStation. Uniquely barcoded RNA-Seq libraries were normalized and pooled equimolarly for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus instrument, using a 2 × 150 cycles format (i.e., paired-end). Twelve samples were loaded on ~ 20% of a 25B flow cell lane, aiming for approximately 50 million reads per sample. All sequencing set up steps were carried out according to the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus sequencing protocol, using 130 pM loading concentration and 5% PhiX spike-in control. The instrument’s computer utilized the NovaSeq X Plus Control Software v1.2. FastQ files were generated using the BCL2fastQ function in the Illumina BaseSpace portal.

RNA-seq analysis

Reads were quality trimmed at Q20 (sequencing quality score of 20) and filtered to remove sequencing adaptors using Trimmomatic (v.0.39) [64]. This resulted in an average of 26,113,724 pairs of reads per sample that were used for subsequent analysis. Alignment and quantification were done by STAR (v.2.7.9a) [65] using mouse reference genome (GRCm39) with its corresponding annotation file obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI): https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_000001635.27. More than 80% of the input reads for each sample were uniquely mapped to GRCm39. Since the samples were run in two separate batches, we used ComBat-seq, a batch effect adjustment tool for RNA-seq data, from the sva (v.3.35.2) R package to account and correct for batch effect [66]. We set ComBat-seq’s option “batch” to batch number and option “group” to the treatment and tissue associated with each sample. Batch corrected gene counts were then imported into edgeR (v.4.0.16, R v.4.3.3) for normalization and differential expression analysis [67]. We considered a gene differentially expressed if its edgeR FDR adjusted p-value is less than 0.05. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was done using R prcomp function using the output of R’s VST (variance stabilizing transformation) function, which produces a matrix of transformed and normalized counts, with the option blind set to true. We tested for differences in samples clustering using gls (Generalized Least Squares model) function in R package nlme (v.3.1–163).

Statistical analysis

An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed to compare differences between groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
