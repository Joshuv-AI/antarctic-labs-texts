Effects of Bone Morphogenetic Protein Agonist Small Molecule, SB4, on Osteogenic Differentiation of Adipose-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells
Authors: Fatemeh Naghshnejad, Bahman Zeynali, Iman Shabani, Azita Parvaneh Tafreshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling is crucial for osteogenic (OS) differentiation, bone growth, and repair. However, the clinical use of BMPs is limited due to their cost and challenges in delivery. SB4, a potent compound of benzoxazole, is a newly introduced small molecule shown to activate the BMP signaling pathway. In this study, we investigated the effects of SB4 on OS differentiation of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs).MethodsWe first examined whether BMP signaling is active in osteoinduced ADMSCs. ADMSCs were treated with SB4 (5 and 10 μM) for 24 hr, and mRNA expression of BMP direct target genes (ID1 and ID3) was analyzed. ADMSCs were then cultured with or without SB4 (10 μM) for 7, 14, and 21 days, followed by Alizarin red staining and real-time polymerase chain reaction analysis of mRNA expression of OS markers RUNX2, BMP2, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and Osteopontin. To investigate the most effective time period of SB4 in osteogenesis, SB4 was applied every 3.5 days during the 21-day differentiation period.ResultsAfter 24 hr of treatment with SB4 (5 and 10 μM), increased mRNA expression of BMP direct target genes (ID1 and ID3) confirmed that SB4 acts as an active BMP agonist. SB4-treated ADMSCs showed enhanced bone matrix production and increased mRNA expression of RUNX2, BMP2, ALP, and Osteopontin. Increased mRNA expression of OS markers, bone matrix formation, and ALP activity during the first 7 days of SB4 treatment suggested the importance of SB4 at the early stage of differentiation.ConclusionsThese results indicate that SB4, with potential BMP-like activity, induces OS differentiation of ADMSCs, leading it to serve as a cost-effective BMP agonist for osteogenesis-related research as well as a potential therapeutic agent for bone repair and regeneration.Keywords: Bone morphogenetic proteins, Cell differentiation, Mesenchymal stem cells, Osteogenesis

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Osteoporosis is a serious global health concern especially during aging caused by the imbalance between osteoclastic bone resorption and osteoblastic bone formation. The current clinical approach for osteoporosis deals primarily with antiresorptive agents such as bisphosphonates,[1] calcitonin [2] and denosumab.[3,4] However, for individuals with substantial bone mass loss the only inhibition of bone resorption with anti-resorptive agents might not be adequate. Thus, developing new strategies which can stimulate osteoblast differentiation is essential. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), the precursors of osteoblasts, are multipotent cells with therapeutic potentials in bone-related diseases. These cells can be obtained from various sources such as bone marrow, adipose and dental tissues. [5–7] The differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts is influenced by various signaling pathways especially bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs).[8] BMP signaling pathway involves binding of BMP ligands to BMP receptors (type I and type II), which then transduce signals through the Smaddependent canonical pathway as well as non-Smad pathways such as the p38 MAPK pathway. This leads to the activation of transcription factors like Runx2 to promote osteoblast differentiation.[9–11] Multiple BMPs, including BMP2, BMP6, BMP7, and BMP4, have been shown to promote the differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts both in vitro and in vivo.[12–15] BMP signaling is necessary for normal postnatal bone formation and regulation of the balance between bone formation and resorption. Disruptions in BMP signaling can lead to various skeletal abnormalities.[16–18] Over the past several decades, recombinant human BMPs (rh- BMPs) have been the most extensively studied and widely used osteo-inductive agents for clinical bone repair.[19–25] However, the high cost of rhBMPs and the need for higher doses than those of the physiologicals, have demanded alternative strategies to promote osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. Small molecules, such as the newly introduced SB4, could be considered as potential modulators of BMP activity. SB4, a potent benzoxazole compound, has been shown to rapidly stimulate BMP signaling and to upregulate the expression of BMP target genes ID1mRNA and ID3mRNA in human renal cells (HEK293s).[26] It has also been reported that SB4 can activate BMP signaling independently of noggin and type I BMP receptors, indicating that its functionality through an alternative mechanism. Interestingly, it has been shown that BMP4 is replaceable by SB4 in an in vitro germ cell differentiation protocol even at a lower level.[27] To investigate whether SB4, can act as a powerful BMP4 stimulant and boost the osteogenic (OS) differentiation by triggering canonical BMP signaling, we have used SB4 as a cost-effective alternative to BMP in the osteo-induced adipose-derived MSCs (ADMSCs) which might be used for future stem cell therapy in disease context.

METHODS

Cell isolation and culture

Primary human ADMSC were isolated and cultured as previously described.[28] Briefly, abdominal liposuctioned subcutaneous fat tissues were obtained from female healthy donors with average ages of 30 to 36 years old. Tissues were then cut into small pieces and digested with 0.1% collagenase I (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 30 to 60 min at 37°C with gentle agitation. After being centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, the cell pellet was resuspended in culture medium containing Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) high glucose (0.3 mg/mL; G418) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and antibiotics (100 units/mL penicillin; Bioidea, Tehran, Iran), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Bioidea). The cells were seeded at a density of 200 to 500 cells/cm2 in tissue culture flasks at 37°C with 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 2 to 3 days. Being confluent at 80% to 90%, they were detached by using trypsin/EDTA (Bioidea) and passaged at a 1:3 ratio.

All procedures were performed according to the University of Tehran, College of Science Ethic Committee approval guidelines and regulations.

To induce the osteoblast differentiation, cells were cultured in an OS medium, containing complete medium with 5 mM β-glycerolphosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 25 μg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich). The medium was changed every three days.

Flow cytometry

ADMSCs express various cell surface antigens, known as clusters of differentiation (CDs), including CD90, CD73, CD105 and CD45. To analyze the expression of these cell surface markers, the following flow cytometry procedure was performed. Following the removal of culture media, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), trypsinized, and thoroughly washed with PBS thereafter. The washed cells were then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm, and the pellet was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 15 min, washed with PBS, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hr at room temperature, and then incubated with specific CD antibodies and the isotype control. Following the incubation, the pellet was washed and resuspended with PBS and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

Preparation of SB4 working concentrations

SB4 (2-4-Bromophenylmethylthiobenzoxazole; Sigma- Aldrich), a potent BMP4 agonist (EC50=74 nM), was stored as a beige solid powder at −20°C according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To prepare stock solutions, SB4 was dissolved in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) to a concentration of 10 mM by rapid stirring and gentle warming in a 45°C to 60°C water bath for complete solubilization, as recommended. The stock solution was aliquoted into sterile Eppendorf tubes (50–100 μL per aliquote) to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles and stored at −20°C for up to 1 month. Working dilutions (1, 2.5, 5, and 10 μM) were freshly prepared on the day of the experiment by serial dilution of the stock solution in complete culture medium (DMEM high glucose supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin). The final DMSO concentration in all working solutions and controls did not exceed 0.1% (v/v) to minimize potential solvent-related cytotoxicity. All manipulations were performed under sterile conditions in a laminar flow hood.

MTT cytotoxicity assay

The in vitro toxicities of SB4 (Tocris Bioscience, Ellisville, MO, USA) at different concentrations were examined on ADMSCs seeded at a density of 8×104 cells/ml in 96-well plates containing DMEM (DMEM high glucose; Gibco), supplemented with 10% FBS, L-glutamine (2 mM), penicillin (100 Units/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. The effect of SB4 was assessed using MTT (tetrazolium; Sigma-Aldrich), colorimetric method. SB4 was applied at four different concentrations (1, 2.5, 5, and 10 μM) to the wells, and the plates were incubated for 24, 48, and 72 hr. DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), the SB4 solvent, was used at the same volumes as those of SB4 as the control and examined in parallel. Working dilutions were freshly prepared on days of the experiment. To perform the MTT assay, firstly cell media were discarded and MTT was added to each well at a concentration of 0.5 μg/mL and incubated for 4 hr. The precipitates (formazan) were then dissolved in 100 μL of DMSO. The absorbance (A) was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Each experiment was repeated in triplicates. A formazan absorbance is inversely correlated with cell viability. The percentage of viability (%V) was calculated according to the following formula; where (A blank) indicates the average absorbance of DMSO treated cells, (A test) the average absorbance of the SB4 treated cells (24, 48, 72 hr), and (A control) the average absorbance in non-treated cells.

Alizarin red S staining for matrix mineralization

ADMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 4×104 cells per well, treated with or without SB4 at different time points and concentrations during the 21 days of OS induction. After the treatments, the cells were fixed with methanol (Jahan Shimi, Tehran, Iran) for 120 min on ice, and then stained with 2% Alizarin red solution (pH 4.1–4.3; Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 to 30 min at room temperature, and then washed to remove the unbound dye. Matrix mineralization was visualized by dark red staining of the mineral deposits under a phase contrast microscope. For quantitative assessment of the stains, dyes were extracted by using 10% acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), neutralized with ammonium hydroxid (Sigma-Aldrich) followed by measured absorbances at 405 nm, using a plate reader.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assay

ALP is considered as an early marker of the osteoblast activity. To evaluate the OS differentiation, we measured the ALP activity by analysing the formation of p-nitrophenol (pNP) from pNP phosphate (pNPP), according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided in the Pars Azmoon Kit. The kit components include ALP assay buffer, ALP substrate (e.g., pNPP), standard ALP and ALP stop solution. Initially, a standard curve was drawn at 405 nm absorbance, using the standard alkaline phosphatase. Cells were washed with 1xPBS and then trypsinized to be detached from the plate. Following neutralization of the trypsin, the cells were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 5 min. Cellular pellets were then lysed in a buffer containing 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) to release cellular contents. To perform the assay, both samples and the standards were prepared and protected from light. To initiate the enzymatic reaction, ALP assay buffer and ALP substrate (pNPP) were added. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at 37°C. Based on the levels of pNP in the lysates due to the reacted ALP in the samples with the phosphate substrate reagent, the ALP activity in the samples was measured in the dark at absorbance of 405 nm and quantified using the standard curve.

Quantitative reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

To assess the expression of lineage-specific genes and evaluate the OS differentiation of cells at transcriptional level, we performed qRT-PCR. Using the RNX plus reagent (a guanidine/phenol solution for total RNA isolation), total RNA was extracted from cultured cells at different groups. Then cDNAs were synthesized using using the reverse transcriptase enzyme (Yekta Tajhiz Azma, Tehran, Iran) according to manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicates using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Yekta Tajhiz Azma) and the real-time PCR system (Rotor-Gene instrument; Corbett Life Science, Sydney, Australia). The reactions were analyzed with Rotor-Gene Q Series software (version 2.3.5; Corbett Life Science). The expression levels of the candidate genes were normalized to that of β2-microglobulin, as a reference housekeeping gene, and then calculated relative to their corresponding controls. The primer sequences are provided in Table 1. The data were analyzed using the comparative ΔCT method through the freely available Relative Expression Software Tool (REST 2009).

Statistical analyses

All assays were repeated in triplicates per group. Data were expressed as mean and standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA to compare means between groups, with a P value of less than 0.05 as significant.

RESULTS

Characterization of human adipose-derived stem cells

Using flow cytometry, we showed that ADMSCs were highly positive for specific cell surface antigens of the MSCs including CD73, CD90, and CD105 (100%, 99.6%, and 99.9% respectively), but low for those of the hematopoietic stem cells such as CD45 (0.18%). This profile confirmed that our isolated ADMSCs were pure MSCs (Fig. 1).

Characterization of human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs). Flow cytometry analysis reveals that isolated human ADSCs are positive for the mesenchymal cell markers clusters of differentiation (CD)73, CD90, and CD105 while showing minimal expression of the hematopoietic stem cell marker CD45. The blue curves show the unlabeled cells, while the red ones show the stained cells.

ADMSCs viability and growth

To evaluate the effects of SB4 at different concentrations (1, 2.5, 5, and 10 μM) on viability and growth of ADMSCs, MTT cytotoxicity assay was used. Our results demonstrated that SB4 at 72 hr after treatment not only was not toxic but even proliferative, the highest effects were seen at 5 and 10 μM (Fig. 2A).

Activation of BMP4 Target Genes in ADMSCs by SB4

To evaluate if SB4 is active as a BMP agonist, the ADMSCs were cultured in DMEM and treated with different concentrations of SB4 (1, 2.5, 5, and 10) for 24 hr followed by real time PCR analysis of BMP4 target genes mRNAs, ID1 and ID3. The results showed that 10 μM SB4 was the most optimum concentration for increased expression of BMP target genes compared to the other concentrations (Fig. 2B). Altogether, the induction of ID1 and ID3 expression, as known direct targets of canonical BMP signaling [27] by SB4 might imply that it functions through the canonical SMAD-dependent BMP signaling pathway.

SB4 enhanced the early and late OS differentiation of ADMSCs

To investigate the effects of different concentrations of SB4 on OS differentiation of ADMSCs as well as on early and late OS markers, cells were cultured in OS medium with or without SB4 at 1, 2.5, 5, and 10 μM for 21 days. The OS differentiation was then evaluated by alizarin red staining followed by real time PCR analysis of early and late OS markers. The results of alizarin red staining showed that SB4 treatment significantly increased bone matrix production at 5 and 10 μM concentrations (Fig. 3A, B). The analysed expression of ALPmRNA and OPNmRNA as the respective early and the late OS makers revealed a significant increased expression of ALPmRNA on day 14 (using 5 μM SB4 in the osteo-induced ADMSCs) and increased expression of OPNmRNA on day 21 (using 10 μM SB4 treated ADMSCs; Fig. 3C, D). Altogether, these results showed that SB4 enhanced the OS differentiation of ADMSCs during early and late phases of osteoinduction.

The most efficient time frame for osteo differentiation potency of SB4 is early during osteogenesis

Having shown that SB4 increased the expression of both early (ALPmRNA) as well as the late OS marker (OPNmRNA), we next sought to find the most effective time period of SB4 treatment for an enhanced OS differentiation of ADMSCs. Therefore, 10 μM SB4 was applied every 3.5 days within a 21-day differentiation period, starting on days 0, 3.5, 7, 10.5, 14, and 17.5 (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 1), Alizarin red staining results showed that there were highest amounts of bone matrix production on 0–3.5 and 3.5–7 days, suggesting that the early stage of differentiation period is the most critical time for SB4 to enhance the OS differentiation of ADMSCs. Interestingly, when SB4 was administered after day 7, mineralization was significantly reduced even compared to the untreated OS control before day 7, suggesting that delayed exposure to SB4 may interfere with matrix maturation or loss of a critical commitment window (Fig. 4B). To further examine this result, SB4 was administered at two intervals, from day 0 to 7 and from day 0 to 21, after which the ALP assay and real time PCR analysis were performed. Our results showed that ALP activity in SB4 treated cells from day 0 to 7 was significantly higher than those treated from days 0 to 21 (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, analysis of real time PCR results showed that expressions of OS differentiation markers, including Runx2mRNA, ALPmRNA, OsteopontinmRNA, and BMP2mRNA were also significantly increased in SB4 treated cells during 0–7 days treatment compared to those of the 0–21 day period (Fig. 5). Together these results indicate that a short time (7 days) SB4 treatment would be sufficient to enhance the OS differentiation of ADMSCs.

DISCUSSION

BMPs have been recognized for long as key players in regulation of bone homeostasis, with their ability to induce the osteoblastic differentiation and bone formation, thus could be considered as candidates for therapies in bone disorders.[29,30] The inherent limitations of BMPs however, including their short half-lives, high costs, complex purification requirements, contamination issues and unwanted immunogenic responses of the host, have driven researchers to explore other alternative approaches.[31–33] Therefore, effective BMP agonists with more stability and costeffectiveness as well as ease in their production could be considered.

Recently, several small molecules have been identified to either activate or synergize with the BMP pathway.[34–39] While previous BMP agonist small molecules have shown promises,[39–42] some limitations such as off-target effects and inconsistent results among different cell types have been reported. Moreover, most of the BMP agonist showed relatively low activities and failed to induce maturation of the osteoblasts,[41] thus there is a strong need to identify efficient BMP activators to stimulate the osteoblast differentiation from the stem cells. Being introduced by Bradford and his colleagues [27], SB4 not only overcame the above mentioned shortcomings but also rapidly (in just one hr after the treatment) increased the level of p-SMAD-1/5/9 [27] and thus stimulated the BMP pathway. Insufficient knowledge on the OS effects of SB4 prompted us to treat ADMSCs with SB4. To our knowledge we are the first who showed that SB4 significantly enhanced the OS differentiation in ADMSCs in 21 days, verified by alizarin red staining and analysis of the expression of OS markers such as ALPmRNA and OPNmRNA. The osteo-induction by SB4 has also been previously shown by Yan and colleagues [43] in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. Their findings revealed that the suppressed bone formation by an autophagy inhibitor, 3-methyladenine, was rescued by SB4 through the activation of Smad1/5/8.[43]

Regarding the most efficient time point of SB4 in osteogenesis, we were also able to show that early and short treatment of the ADMSCs with SB4 (3.5 or 7 days) was sufficient to enhance the OS differentiation, verified by analysis of the ALP activity and the expression of OS markers such as ALPmRNA, Runx2mRNA, and BMP2mRNA. Accordingly, previous researchers have introduced the early days of osteoblast differentiation as the golden time.[44–48] Conversely, treatment with SB4 after this early window (e.g., after day 7) not only failed to promote osteogenesis but appeared to suppress mineralization relative to control, suggesting a critical dependency on early BMP pathway activation. Late exposure might either disrupt the ongoing maturation events or redirect the committed cells. Therefore, our findings suggest that short and early treatment with SB4 has the potential to promote osteogenesis through a BMP-like mechanism. Nevertheless, further direct comparative studies with rhBMPs are needed to determine whether SB4 can serve as an efficient replacement in clinical applications.

Mechanistically, we showed that SB4 increased expression of canonical BMP signaling target genes ID1mRNA and ID3mRNA in 24 hr which implies the activation of the canonical SMAD-dependent BMP signaling pathway by SB4.[27] It remains however to be examined if non-canonical BMP pathways, such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, also contribute to the OS differentiation by SB4. Furthermore, the possible mechanism through which SB4 as a BMP agonist regulate the osteoblastic differentiation might also be through its interaction with other signaling pathways, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and Wnt pathways. Based on our recently published data, we showed that TGF-β and Wnt pathways cooperatively inhibited the osteogenesis.[28] The dampening effect of BMP on TGF-β pathway has also been pointed out elsewhere by other researchers.[49–53] Considering our previous findings [28] alongside with present data, we speculate that the required inhibition of Wnt and TGF-β for the OS differentiation might have been induced by BMP and its agonist e.g. SB4 to generate a permissive OS environment. However, further validation is needed to clarify the precise involved signaling mechanisms.

In summary, we have shown that SB4 has a canonical BMP-like activity promoting the OS differentiation of ADMSCs at early stages similar to what has been shown for OS activity of BMP. Further studies will be essential to determine the mechanism of action of SB4 for its proposed role in promoting OS differentiation and for its effectiveness compared to human recombinant BMP in bone regenerative strategies. At present however, SB4 should be regarded as a research tool with a possible future therapeutic potential, rather than a clinically validated agent.
