Chronic refractory ulcer caused by Edwardsiella tarda: a case report and literature review
Authors: Qingdan Gu, Da Ma, Yi Wu, Wenxuan Wu, Jiazhen Zou, Chunlei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-traumatic Edwardsiella tarda (E. tarda) infections usually have an acute course. Chronic infection is rare. Introduction

Edwardsiella tarda (E. tarda) is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic bacterium in the family Enterobacteriaceae [1]. It is a zoonotic pathogen that mainly infects aquatic animals, including fish, reptiles, and amphibians. Human infection is uncommon [2]. Ingestion of contaminated aquatic products can cause intestinal infection, usually as self-limiting gastroenteritis [3]. E. tarda can also cause extraintestinal infections, including skin and soft tissue infection, cholangitis, urinary tract infection, and septic shock from hematogenous spread. These severe infections have reported mortality rates of up to 38%-50% [4–7].

Most published clinical reports of E. tarda infection describe acute disease, with either rapid recovery or rapid deterioration. Chronic wound infection in immunocompetent hosts is less well described. Here, we report a healthy 32-year-old male with E. tarda skin and soft tissue infection after a freshwater stingray injury. This case shows that E. tarda can follow a chronic course and highlights key points in diagnosis, wound management, and antibiotic selection.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old male professional diver was admitted with a non-healing wound on the right knee for more than 20 days. He had no chronic disease. During work, he sustained a puncture wound to the anterior right knee from a freshwater stingray barb. Initial treatment at another hospital included wound irrigation, analgesia, prophylactic antibiotics, and tetanus immunization. The wound later developed persistent swelling, pain, exudation, and erythema. His symptoms did not improve despite repeated outpatient dressing changes and a self-administered one-week course of oral cefaclor.

On admission, physical examination showed a 1 × 2-cm skin ulcer, 1.5 cm deep, on the anterior right knee (Fig. 1A). The ulcer base contained purulent exudate. Exploration identified an 8-cm sinus tract extending toward the suprapatellar region (12 o’clock position, supine anterior view). The local skin temperature was elevated, with mild tenderness. Laboratory tests showed slight increases in white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a sinus tract extending into the suprapatellar soft tissues, with mild joint effusion. There was no bone marrow edema or cortical destruction. These findings supported a diagnosis of deep soft tissue infection (Fig. 2).

Because a sinus tract had formed and conservative treatment had failed, the patient underwent serial radical debridement. The first procedure was performed on the day of admission. It included debridement and negative pressure wound therapy (Fig. 1B). Further debridements were performed on postoperative days 11 and 25. In each procedure, the ulcer opening was enlarged, the sinus tract was laid open, and necrotic tissue was removed until viable tissue was exposed.

Intraoperative tissue from the ulcer base and wound secretions were sent for pathology and microbiology. Histopathology showed ulceration with necrotic tissue overlying granulation tissue, consistent with a chronic infected ulcer. Microbiological testing confirmed the diagnosis. Blood agar and MacConkey agar both grew pure grayish-white, moist, smooth colonies with regular margins. Gram staining showed Gram-negative bacilli/coccobacilli. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) identified the isolate as E. tarda. Susceptibility testing showed that the strain was susceptible to all tested agents, including cefuroxime. Peripheral blood cultures were negative.

The patient received intravenous cefuroxime sodium (1.5 g every 8 h) according to the susceptibility results. After one week, the infection had clearly improved. Swelling and pain decreased, the sinus tract became shallower, and early epithelial growth appeared at the wound edge. Antibiotics were therefore temporarily stopped. Between operations, wound care included continuous negative pressure wound therapy and topical antiseptics. Topical recombinant human epidermal growth factor gel and oral agents aimed at improving microcirculation were also used to support healing.

After combined antimicrobial treatment and aggressive surgery, the infection was controlled and the wound healed well. A later tissue culture showed no bacterial growth. At discharge, the wound was re-epithelialized and left a 0.5 × 2.0-cm hypertrophic scar. The interval from injury to complete wound healing was about 3 months.

Discussion

E. tarda is a Gram-negative bacillus found in aquatic environments. Humans are mainly infected by eating undercooked aquatic products or by exposing wounds to contaminated water. The clinical spectrum ranges from gastroenteritis to necrotizing fasciitis [8]. Most infections are acute and fulminant; chronic progression is uncommon. Severe acute infection is more likely in patients with chronic liver disease, especially hepatitis C or alcohol abuse [9], malignancy [10, 11], diabetes mellitus [11, 12], or immunosuppression such as chemotherapy [10]. However, disease can vary even in patients without major immunosuppression. Some develop chronic osteomyelitis [13], while others recover rapidly after appropriate treatment [14]. Host factors and treatment strategy therefore both influence the clinical course.

Our patient was immunocompetent but developed chronic infection after a freshwater stingray injury. The E. tarda isolate was susceptible to all tested antimicrobial agents. Persistent infection was therefore unlikely to be caused by drug resistance. A more plausible explanation is the nature of stingray trauma. The barb can carry bacteria deep into soft tissue and devitalize surrounding tissue. This may create a protected focus that systemic antibiotics cannot clear alone. Recovery depended on combined treatment. Serial radical debridement removed the 8-cm sinus tract and necrotic tissue. It also improved local drug delivery and reduced the inflammatory burden. MALDI-TOF MS provided rapid species identification. This allowed cefuroxime to be selected according to susceptibility testing and avoided unnecessary broad-spectrum exposure. Negative pressure wound therapy and topical growth factors then supported perfusion and epithelialization after source control.

This case supports the view that E. tarda soft tissue infection is unlikely to resolve with antibiotics alone when deep tissue is involved. Previous reports also support the importance of source control in E. tarda bone and soft tissue infections. Antibiotics are necessary, but they may be insufficient when abscesses, necrotizing infection, sinus tracts, or chronic osteomyelitis are present [7, 12, 13, 15]. In such settings, clinicians should combine early empirical coverage for aquatic trauma with prompt pathogen identification and de-escalation according to susceptibility results [7, 10–13]. At the same time, they should actively assess whether the wound contains retained foreign material, necrotic tissue, deep extension, or inadequate drainage. The patient also received oral horse chestnut seed extract (HCSE) as an adjunct, not as primary treatment. HCSE has anti-inflammatory and anti-edema effects and may reduce swelling in chronic wounds. However, current evidence does not show that it independently accelerates healing [16, 17]. In the HAVLUT trial, oral HCSE reduced wound slough but did not improve overall healing rates in venous ulcers [18]. There is no direct evidence for HCSE in E. tarda infection. In this case, it was used only to support debridement and targeted antibiotics. Future studies should focus on symptom relief, such as pain or edema, rather than cure rate alone.

In summary, this case shows successful treatment of chronic E. tarda soft tissue infection in an immunocompetent host. Clinicians should suspect unusual pathogens when wounds follow aquatic exposure. Early microbiological diagnosis and susceptibility-guided antibiotics are important. Thorough surgical debridement remains the key step for controlling chronic infectious foci. This case may help guide the management of similar chronic infections. Future work should assess adjunctive therapies and monitor emerging antimicrobial resistance.
