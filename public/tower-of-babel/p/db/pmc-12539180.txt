Grounded large language models for diagnostic prediction in real-world emergency department settings
Authors: Alexandre Niset, Ines Melot, Margaux Pireau, Alexandre Englebert, Nathan Scius, Julien Flament, Salim El Hadwe, Mejdeddine Al Barajraji, Henri Thonon, Sami Barrit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate predictive diagnostic performance of open- and closed-source large language models (LLMs) in emergency medicine, addressing the urgent need for innovative clinical decision support tools amid rising patient volumes and staffing shortages.Materials and MethodsWe generated 2370 AI-driven diagnostic predictions (Top-5 diagnoses from each of 6 model pipelines per patient), using data from 79 real-world emergency department cases collected consecutively during a 24-hour peak influx period at a tertiary care center. Pipelines combined open- and closed-source embedding models (text-embedding-ada-002, MXBAI) with foundational models (GPT-4, Llama3, and Qwen2) grounded via retrieval-augmented generation using emergency medicine textbooks. Models’ predictions were assessed against reference diagnoses established by expert consensus.ResultsAll pipelines achieved comparable diagnostic match rates (62.03%-72.15%). Diagnostic performance was significantly influenced by case characteristics: match rates were notably higher for specific versus unspecific diagnoses (85.53% vs 31.41%, P < .001) and surgical versus medical cases (79.49% vs 56.25%, P < .001). Open-source models demonstrated markedly superior sourcing capabilities compared to GPT-4-based combinations (P < 1.4e-12), with MBXAI/Qwen2 pipeline achieving perfect citation verification.DiscussionDiagnostic accuracy primarily depended on case characteristics rather than the choice of model pipeline, highlighting fundamental AI alignment challenges in clinical reasoning. Low performance in unspecific diagnoses underscores inherent complexities in clinical definitions rather than technological shortcomings alone.ConclusionOpen-source LLM pipelines provide enhanced sourcing capabilities, crucial for transparent clinical decision-making and interpretability. Further research should expand knowledge bases to include hospital guidelines and regional epidemiology, while exploring on-premises solutions to better align with privacy regulations and clinical integration.Keywords: large language models, emergency medicine, diagnostic prediction, retrieval-augmented generation, clinical decision support, open-source models

Introduction

Background

Emergency medicine (EM) has faced a persistent crisis since the early 2000s, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic.1–4 Emergency departments face staff shortages and patient surges amid intensifying technical demands and administrative burdens.2 In these high-paced environments, emergency physicians must make critical, time-sensitive decisions.5

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLM), shows promise as a supportive tool,6–9 though deploying LLM in EM presents technical, ethical, and regulatory challenges.10 While commercialization has made state-of-the-art AI widely accessible,11–13 many studies rely on simplistic paradigms using convenient benchmarks, overlooking real-world complexities and risking data contamination and overfitting.14,15

In parallel, regulatory frameworks increasingly mandate AI interpretability16 while also governing personal data confidentiality, particularly regarding data sharing with private entities or across borders.17,18 Commercial solutions could potentially leverage user interactions for undisclosed purposes such as model training or business intelligence,19 raising privacy concerns that must be carefully managed.20,21

Importance

While large-scale proprietary efforts offer significant promise, their complexity and limited transparency could restrict physician autonomy.22 In response, several open-source-friendly initiatives have developed resource-conscious approaches for domain adaptation, such as parameter-efficient tuning and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). These approaches may provide alternative pathways that potentially enhance transparency and autonomy, without excessive computational and economic demands.23 However, they still present technical challenges.

Here, we leverage a user-centered system to specialize LLM in EM through grounding methods aimed at enhancing the models’ contextual integration of queries and the verifiability of their responses. Using this system, we deployed closed- and open-source models to investigate their predictive diagnostic performance and sourcing capabilities in generating early diagnoses from limited, initial clinical data. These predictions were compared to reference diagnoses established through expert consensus based on comprehensive, conclusive clinical data from a siloed, real-world dataset of a tertiary-care ED.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary ED in Belgium. We retrieved all admissions during the 24-hour peak influx period on a single day in 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (ID 187/2023; Supplementary File S1), and all procedures adhered to applicable regulatory and ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation 2016/679. All data were stored in a secure research database inaccessible to external entities, preventing data leakage and contamination into current or future AI training datasets.

AI system

Neura24 deploys LLM with custom configurations through advanced RAG.23 We used 2 embedding models (text-embedding-ada-002 and MXBAI) with 3 foundational models (GPT‐4 and open‐weight Llama 3 and Qwen 2 in the 70B‐class variants), deployed within the same RAG pipeline and prompting schema. Our prototype corpus consisted of 4 textbooks: 3 on EM25–27 and 1 on critical care.28 A fully detailed breakdown illustrating prompts, inputs, and outputs for one representative clinical case is available in the Supplementary Files (Supplementary File S2). For each AI run, we logged the model’s time to answer (API request dispatch to receipt of the full response). We did not use few‐shot exemplars or chain‐of‐thought demonstrations. Evaluation paradigm

Data were extracted from electronic health records to construct a comprehensive dataset, the “full clinical information,” encompassing all clinical information recorded at the conclusion of each patient’s management in the ED. This dataset included patient demographics, vital signs, anamnesis (ie, presenting complaints, past medical and surgical history, allergies, and current treatments), physical examination findings, as well as workup results and management decisions with procedural details from complementary examinations (ie, examination type, location, findings, specialist involvement, and timestamps) of each case. In parallel, the conclusive “real-world diagnosis” of the attending EP in clinical practice was recorded. The complete list of extracted parameters and definitions for each field is available in a data extraction sheet (Table S1).

Three experienced EPs conducted an adjudication process to establish a “reference diagnosis” for each case. First, 2 EPs independently reviewed the “full clinical information” (which included the “real‐world diagnosis”). If they agreed, the case was labeled concordant and that agreement defined the reference diagnosis. If they disagreed, the case was labeled discordant, and a third EP resolved the diagnosis.

“Early clinical information” was defined as a subset of the “full clinical information”, comprising only initial presentation parameters available at the onset of patient evaluation in the ED—ie, anamnesis and physical examination findings. Based on this limited information, the AI was prompted to generate the Top 5 most likely diagnoses, ranked by decreasing probability.

To evaluate AI performance, 3 independent investigators, distinct from the adjudicators and the attending EP, assessed whether any of the 5 AI-generated predictive diagnoses aligned with the reference diagnosis. For cases with correct alignment, they determined the rank of the matching diagnosis. Independently, the investigators classified each case as medical or surgical based on the “reference diagnosis.” Additionally, each “reference diagnosis” was categorized as either “specific” (ie, ICD-10-specific) or “unspecific” (ie, nonspecific according to ICD-10 or outside the ICD-10 classification).29 Agreement between the 2 investigators confirmed the results for alignment, ranking, and classification (Figure 2).

Diagnostic evaluation framework comparing AI-driven and clinical diagnostic pathways: (A) AI pathway: Initial clinical presentation data is input into a specialized AI system that combines grounding techniques (RAG using emergency medicine textbooks), parametrization, and prompting to generate Top-5 most likely diagnostic predictions. (B) Clinical pathway: Full clinical information, including laboratory tests, imaging studies, and other diagnostic workup, is assessed by the attending EP to establish a real-world diagnosis, which is then independently reviewed by expert EP adjudicators to determine the reference diagnosis. (C) Evaluation process: Independent investigators analyze the alignment between AI-generated diagnoses and the reference diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic match was defined a priori as a binary outcome indicating whether the reference diagnosis appeared within the model’s Top‐5 predictions. We additionally summarize Top‐1 and Top‐3 match to contextualize the choice of list length. The match rate was calculated as the proportion of cases with diagnostic match out of the total number of cases. Diagnostic rank was measured on an ordinal scale (1-5), representing the rank at which the reference diagnosis was provided—based on this position, we assigned 5 points for first place, decreasing by 1 point per rank down to 0 if absent and summed all points obtained per model combination across all cases to constitute a total ranking score. Diagnostic sourcing was assessed as a binary outcome, indicating whether verifiable citations substantiated the diagnosis.

Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables for pairwise comparisons, and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare ordinal between 2 groups. To assess overall differences in ordinal across multiple groups, we used the Kruskal-Wallis test. The Chi-square test of independence was applied to evaluate associations between categorical variables. McNemar’s test was utilized for within-model comparisons of paired categorical data.

We implemented multiple regression models tailored to each outcome’s characteristics. For binary outcomes (diagnostic match and sourcing), we employed logistic regression models. For analyzing ranking performance, we developed an ordinal score transformation and used ordinary least squares regression. Detailed mathematical expressions, estimation methods, and comprehensive model diagnostics are provided in the Supplementary Files (Supplementary File S3).

Python 3.10 was employed using pandas (v1.5.3) for data manipulation, scipy (v1.10.1) for statistical testing, matplotlib (v3.7.1), seaborn (v0.12.2) for data visualization, and statsmodels (v0.14.0) for statistical modeling. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

In 2023, the peak ED influx occurred on a summer day. We retrieved 79 cases. The 2 independent adjudicators initially agreed in 61/79 (77.2%) cases; 18/79 (22.8%) required resolution by a third adjudicator. Of all cases, 53 were classified as specific and 35 as medical. In the subgroup analysis of cases with poor match performance, we found that (i) no-match cases (n = 16) showed a significant predominance of unspecific diagnoses (87.5%, OR = 33.33, P < .0001) and medical presentations (75%, OR = 3.75, P = .0482), while concordance rates remained comparable; and (ii) mixed-match cases (n = 25) showed no significant differences in characteristics (all p > .05, Fisher’s exact tests) (Figure 5).

Ranking analysis showed total scores ranging from 214 to 245 points and mean ranks between 1.01 and 1.39, with no significant differences in distribution across model combinations (Kruskal-Wallis test) or pairwise comparisons (Mann-Whitney U tests).

Regarding diagnostic sourcing, LLaMA3-based and QWEN2-based combinations demonstrated significantly higher rates compared to GPT-4-based combinations (OR: 33.92 to ∞ for MBXAI/QWEN2 achieving perfect sourcing rate; P < 1.4e-12), with no other significant differences between model combinations (Fisher’s exact tests) (Figure 6). Regression analyses revealed that case characteristics, not model selection, were the primary determinants of diagnostic performance. For diagnostic match, the logistic model showed significant effects for case-specific factors: specific diagnosis (β = 2.66, P < .001, relative importance: 52.07%), surgical discipline (β = 1.23, P < .001, relative importance: 24.05%), and adjudicator concordance (concordant vs discordant; β = 0.72, P = .008). Model comparisons (embedding and foundation models) showed no significant impact (all p > .05). The model demonstrated strong performance metrics (accuracy = 0.81, F1-score = 0.87).

Similarly, for diagnostic ranking, specific diagnosis (β = 2.20, P < .001), surgical discipline (β = 0.53, P = .003), and adjudicator concordance (β = 0.63, P = .003) were significant predictors, while model combinations remained nonsignificant.

For diagnostic sourcing, only the foundation model choice showed significant effects, with LLaMA3 and QWEN2 significantly outperforming GPT-4 (both β = 4.23, P < .001, relative importance: 48.11% each), while case characteristics showed no significant impact.

Discussion

In this real-world evaluation, both open- and closed-source models achieved comparable predictive performance (62%-72% diagnostic match rates) with no significant differences among model combinations. When models included a diagnosis, they consistently ranked it among top proposals (mean ranks 1-1.4). The predominant use of proprietary commercial systems in biomedical literature may reflect factors such as ease of access, product maturity, or integration rather than inherent technological superiority.12 Diagnostic performance was primarily determined by case characteristics, with regression analysis identifying specific diagnosis and surgical discipline as strongest predictors (52% and 24% relative importance). ICD-10-specific diagnoses and surgical/concordant cases demonstrated higher match (71%-85%). Analysis of no-match cases revealed predominance of unspecific diagnoses (87.5%) and medical presentations (75%), suggesting LLM limitations reflect fundamental AI alignment challenges in medical reasoning. Open-source models showed significantly superior sourcing capabilities, underscoring advantages in transparency and verifiability.

As AI performance patterns across different case types demonstrate how technology adapts to the complexities of medical diagnosis, addressing these challenges requires us to go beyond mere technical metrics. We must critically assess diagnostic reasoning, recognize its inherent uncertainty and nonspecificity, and create clinically relevant solutions. Additionally, we should explore how various clinical scenarios might support or confront these systems. Yet, medicine lacks a unified, unequivocal clinical code—a longstanding challenge predating the advent of biomedical AI.30–33 Against this complex backdrop, evaluating AI diagnostic capabilities requires precise definitional frameworks and robust comparative standards, which are currently lacking.34 For instance, the poor model performance on unspecific diagnoses suggests that ambiguity in clinical definition and communication should be acknowledged, studied, and mitigated rather than simply viewed as a failure of either artificial or human intelligence. This may, in part, also reflect limitations in terminology mapping—the process of aligning unstructured clinical narratives with structured taxonomies like ICD-10 or SNOMED CT.32–34 Such mappings are often brittle in the face of vague, polysemous, or context-dependent terms, and current LLM may lack the embedded ontological reasoning or symbolic anchoring needed to bridge this gap reliably. Consequently, ambiguity in clinical communication may expose underlying weaknesses in how LLMs represent and generalize medical knowledge, suggesting a need for hybrid approaches combining LLMs with structured medical knowledge graphs or terminology servers.33,34

Here, an AI-generated Top-5 shortlist was used as a pragmatic dual proxy for (i) emergency physicians’ early differential and (ii) a clinically relevant contribution to this endeavor, while acknowledging that this simplification cannot capture the full clinical gestalt. This a priori choice balances comprehensiveness with clinician cognitive load in a high-throughput ED context. Because Top-3 match rates closely approached Top-5 across pipelines (Figure 6), a Top-3 default may retain most benefit while reducing choice overload and alert fatigue, whereas longer lists (eg, Top-10) would likely add distraction for limited incremental value35,36; prospective studies should optimize the number of differential diagnoses offered to the physician against usability and safety endpoints.

Moreover, in emergency medicine, the rapid identification of relevant diagnoses based on limited information occurs in a continuously dynamic assessment, where identifying or ruling out time-sensitive and potentially severe conditions often take precedence over diagnostic probability.5,37,38 We also employed a structured adjudication process involving 3 experienced EPs who reviewed full clinical data to establish a final reference diagnosis. As this approach allows validation against ground truth, it differs from identifying relevant diagnoses at any given moment—a process that may encompass a broad range of possibilities, confine to subjectivity, and ultimately diverge from practical clinical endpoints.39,40

In this study, multi-level grounding approaches could achieve verifiable predictions without prohibitive computational resources by combining RAG with parameterization and prompting. These benefits manifested particularly in open-source architectures, which demonstrated marked superiority in sourcing capabilities, LLaMA3-based and Qwen2-based pipelines displaying substantially higher rates of verifiable references than GPT-4-based ones, with one open-source combination, MBXAI/Qwen2, achieving a perfect sourcing rate. Interestingly, embedding models showed no significant influence on sourcing, suggesting that proprietary API deployments of foundational models involve opaque back-end processing that may impede custom grounding. This lack of transparency extends beyond technical considerations but involves ethical and regulatory issues on model interpretability and accountability in clinical settings. In emergency medicine, where decisions must be both rapid and defensible, verification of AI recommendations against a curated knowledge base enables controlled and effective clinical oversight. This transparency reinforces physician autonomy by providing direct control over AI assistance while establishing clear lines of medical responsibility and liability.41–43 However, providing sources does not ensure their verification in practice—a time-consuming and cognitively demanding task—potentially making sourcing features counterproductive by fostering unwarranted trust. In parallel, the mere use of AI does not inherently enhance physician diagnostic reasoning.44,45 This reality demands thoughtful integration of AI through intuitive interfaces that guide optimal human-computer interactions (Figure S1) alongside semi-automated LLM-driven reference verification.45,46 Such verifiability, while essential for maintaining physician agency, must extend beyond technical features to promote appropriate trust and build scientific and technological literacy—crucial elements in an increasingly AI-augmented clinical environment.

We fixed capacity to the ∼70B class for open‐weight models to reduce size‐related confounding. In general, performance increases with both parameters and training tokens under compute‐optimal scaling, but realized gains depend on data quality and objective, not parameter count alone.47 For knowledge‐intensive tasks, RAG can reduce dependence on sheer parameter count by leveraging curated and indexed context at inference.23,48 Future research would benefit from benchmarking small (≈7-8B) versus large (∼70B) models—with and without RAG—under latency/cost constraints while evaluating clinically meaningful outcomes. Small models merit particular attention as they enable on-premise and edge deployment, addressing critical health data protection requirements.

Several recent studies have investigated the diagnostic reasoning capabilities of LLMs, with results varying considerably depending on study design and dataset characteristics. Off-the-shelf models such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 have demonstrated high accuracy when evaluated on curated case vignettes and challenge sets. For instance, Eriksen et al reported that a selected version of GPT-4 correctly solved 57% of complex diagnostic cases sourced from published clinical vignettes, outperforming online medical journal readers, who achieved 36%.49 However, this methodology is susceptible to overfitting—where a model excels on familiar or previously encountered data but fails to generalize to novel cases—50 as well as data contamination, since such vignettes are publicly available and may have been part of the model’s training data. Furthermore, the comparator group of “medical journal readers” was not clearly defined and may have included a heterogeneous population, ranging from trained physicians and medical students to casual or nonclinical readers. In a subsequent evaluation, GPT-4 achieved approximately 61% accuracy on previously unpublished, diagnostically challenging cases—compared to ∼49% for experienced internists—and perfectly identified all common diagnoses in a standard vignette set, likely further reflecting overfitting.51 Similarly, a 2025 study found that advanced models such as Anthropic Claude 3.5 outperformed specialist gastroenterologists across 67 challenging gastrointestinal cases.52 Collectively, these findings suggest that in controlled scenarios with fully synthesized case information, current ungrounded LLMs may approach or even surpass expert-level diagnostic performance.

However, evaluations based on real-world clinical data and workflows present a more cautious perspective. These studies consistently highlight that LLMs perform optimally when provided with complete, structured case summaries—conditions that contrast sharply with the fragmented and evolving nature of EM practice. Hager et al, for instance, assessed GPT-based models using authentic ED records and observed inferior diagnostic performance compared to physicians, including frequent omissions of essential diagnostic steps.53 The models often failed to interpret raw clinical data accurately and overlooked necessary examinations, raising safety concerns.53 In one randomized trial, physicians tasked with diagnosing complex cases—either with or without GPT-4 support—achieved similar accuracy (∼75%), despite GPT-4 alone scoring 92% when retrospectively provided with full case data.54 This discrepancy underscores the challenges of embedding LLMs into the dynamic, iterative nature of clinical reasoning.

In contrast to prior vignette-based evaluations, our study is grounded in real patient encounters and early clinical information available at the point of care. This approach provides a structured and standardized evaluation of LLM contributions to clinical reasoning by assessing their ability to predict the Top-5 final diagnoses from preliminary data—offering insight into how such tools might contribute to physician decision-making and bolster diagnostic workflows in real-world emergency care.

Future research will benefit from prospective, multicenter, and multilingual trials that evaluate LLM-driven diagnostic support in diverse practice settings across larger and more heterogeneous patient populations. Integrating iterative data updates—eg, findings from laboratory and imaging examinations, specialist consultations, and clinical evolution—into real-time LLM-driven diagnosis would better emulate the dynamic nature of emergency medicine. We should examine how LLM-based tools streamline ED triage by supporting physician decision-making. Critical outcomes include the prioritization and acceleration of critical care delivery for time-sensitive and high-risk cases while facilitating the primary care of benign conditions—specifically measuring how these tools enhance the quality of care provided but also reduce, rather than add to, physician cognitive load and administrative burden.55–57 Furthermore, expanding and specifying the AI knowledge base—such as by incorporating hospital guidelines or regional epidemiology—could enhance the relevance of such diagnostic support. In this regard, open-source approaches hold particular promise for on-premises deployments. They may mitigate privacy risks and align with strict data-sharing regulations. Building upon these advantages, purpose-built LLM-based agentic infrastructure could contribute to patient management workflows—from triage and clinical assessment to treatment planning and discharge—with explicit reasoning traces and self-correction capabilities, enabling transparent processes that adapt to evolving clinical information while maintaining accountability across healthcare providers’ interactions.58,59 These solutions could offer a viable path for institutions seeking technological independence with granular control over data governance and model interpretability.

Limitations

While we utilized real-world, siloed ED data from a tertiary center amid a peak influx of patients and applied an independent adjudication framework to strengthen ecological validity and methodological robustness, several limitations persist. First, the monocentric and retrospective design during peak 24-hour limits broader generalizability. Second, although using clinical vignettes mimics a natural form of medical communication, this step inevitably introduces the risk of language biases. Third, we deployed RAG on a prototype curated corpus consisting of only 4 textbooks. Fourth, it is also important to note that diagnostic performance, sourcing abilities, and interpretability might further be influenced by factors such as neural network architecture, model training parameters, and pre-training data specifics—variables not systematically explored in this study. Fifth, Llama 3 was originally restricted to an 8192-token context window therefore requiring positional embedding interpolation60—which may have impacted performance compared to Qwen2 and GPT-4, both of which natively support 128 000 tokens. Since then, Meta’s Llama 3.1 and 3.2 with extended context windows, along with newer OpenAI’s series of reflective models with integrated chain-of-thought (CoT) and Alibaba’s Qwen2.5, have been released—each potentially altering relative performance if re-evaluated under similar conditions.

Because physician time-to-analysis was not collectable in this retrospective design, direct human-model timing comparisons were not performed; moreover, contrasting a model’s Top-5 list generated from preliminary/early clinical data with a clinician’s final differential derived from full and evolving information is not a peer-to-peer outcome and would be methodologically unsound. Evidence that clinician diagnostic reasoning unfolds over minutes within complex, multi-step ED workflows44,61 underscores the noncomparability of computational answer times and clinical task times and motivates prospective, workflow-integrated studies (time-motion, time-to-decision, interruption costs, cognitive load, safety) to determine whether rapid model output improves care.

We did not explore few-shot or CoT prompting. While these approaches can improve complex reasoning7,62 and show gains in medical QA,63,64 they increase prompt length, surface nonverifiable intermediate reasoning, and complicate privacy review. Given our emphasis on RAG-based grounding and verifiable citations, we prioritized concise prompts. Comparing zero-shot versus few-shot/CoT with self-consistency remains for future work.

In addition, we did not investigate Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models (eg, Mixtral-8 × 7B).65 Although MoE systems offer high effective capacity at constant per-token compute—improving throughput/latency over dense models66,67—they add routing and systems complexity. Evaluating MoE pipelines and RAG + MoE hybrids against dense baselines on match accuracy, verifiability, and real-time throughput and computing demand would be of interest.

In summary, in this real-world ED setting, open- and closed-source LLM pipelines demonstrated promising and comparable performance in generating Top-5 predictive diagnoses. Case characteristics emerged as the dominant influence of diagnostic performance—elucidating AI alignment challenges extending beyond technological optimization to fundamental aspects of clinical reasoning patterns. In this study, open-source pipelines demonstrated superior sourcing capabilities compared to proprietary models, suggesting significant advantages for interpretability in clinical decision support. As emergency departments contend with heightened patient volumes and staffing shortages, such specialized AI-driven tools may become a valuable asset for supporting timely, well-informed clinical decision-making. Continued research exploring larger-scale, multi-centric efforts, including real-time applications and human-computer interactions, as well as benchmarking against clinical outcomes, workflow efficiency, and reference verification, will be key to delineating the full potential of grounded LLM-driven diagnostic assistance in emergency medicine.

Data availability

We have elected not to make our original patient-level dataset publicly available. As this dataset originates from real-world emergency department cases, it contains sensitive clinical information despite de-identification. We are particularly concerned that sharing these data may lead to unintended contamination and dissemination among external models used for training purposes. Such dissemination could compromise the integrity of the dataset and preclude its future use in further research. Consequently, we have decided to restrict access to the data to preserve its unique value and to ensure that its subsequent application in research remains controlled and ethical.

We are particularly concerned that sharing these data may lead to unintended contamination and dissemination among external models used for training purposes. Such dissemination could compromise the integrity of the dataset and preclude its future use in further research. Consequently, we have decided to restrict access to the data to preserve its unique value and to ensure that its subsequent application in research remains controlled and ethical.
