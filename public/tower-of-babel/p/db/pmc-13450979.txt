Validation of the English Social Attunement Questionnaire: Associations with Substance Use, Impulsivity, and Sensation Seeking across the Lifespan
Authors: Emese Kroon, Karis Colyer-Patel, Christophe Romein, Janna Cousijn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOur social environment strongly influences behaviors like substance use and sensation seeking. While most research focuses on group pressure and conformity, the role of social attunement – the tendency to align with one’s social environment for social reward without explicit pressure – has been largely overlooked.MethodsThis study evaluated the English version of the Social Attunement Questionnaire (SAQ) and examined its links to age, alcohol and cannabis use, impulsivity, and sensation seeking (N = 447; 54.5% female).ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis showed acceptable to good fit of the originally proposed two factor structure, with the one factor solution showing a substantially worse fit. Internal consistency was good across the full scale (α = 0.82) and acceptable for the Cognition subscale (α = 0.75) and the Behavior subscale (α = 0.78). The SAQ also showed similar scale and subscale scores and measurement invariance across sex. Higher social attunement was observed in younger individuals. SAQ scores – particularly the behavior subscale – were positively associated with alcohol and cannabis use and with impulsivity. Age moderated these associations: among younger individuals, higher social attunement was linked to greater alcohol use and sensation seeking, while these associations reversed with age.ConclusionThese findings suggest that social attunement may act as both a risk and protective factor for risk-taking behaviors, depending on developmental stage. The English SAQ demonstrated solid psychometric properties across its two subscales – Cognition and Behavior – while also proving suitable for use as a single composite scale, supporting its flexibility as a reliable measure of social attunement.

Introduction

Substance use – especially alcohol and cannabis use – is very common [1, 2]. Most individuals initiate use during adolescence, with substance use peaking during young adulthood, after which most people moderate use without formal intervention [3]. Historically, this process of “maturing out” has often been proposed to be primarily guided by brain development, with increased behavioral control resulting in an increased ability to moderate use [4]. However, any theory relying primarily on brain development as an explanation of substance use trajectories ignores a crucial component: the social component of substance use. Over the last 2 decades, more studies have attempted to evaluate the role of social influence in substance use trajectories [5], and more recent theories of substance use have attempted to integrate neurocognitive with social factors in explaining initiation, escalation, and reduction of substance use [6].

The social plasticity hypothesis proposes that social attunement – the extent to which one adapts to and harmonizes with the social environment – and its interaction with brain developmental plays an important role in substance use trajectories [6]. Most adolescents start experimenting with substances in social settings; the social value of substance use, and alcohol use specifically, is generally high among peer groups in this age range. During mid to late adolescence, we usually see high social attunement to the peer group (e.g., risk-taking behavior in the presence of peers). Together with brain processes supporting high learning flexibility and suboptimal behavioral control, which are characteristic for this age group, social attunement can result in the escalation of substance use, especially when the peer environment encourages substance use [6, 7]. While transitioning to adulthood, the social value of substance use generally decreases – for example, reflected by a change in drinking norms [8] – while behavioral control improves [4]. During this period, social attunement to the environment – first partially responsible for increases in substance use – can facilitate de-escalation of use. For example, one would expect social attunement to be positively associated with alcohol use during adolescence where the social value of alcohol is high, while this association would become more negative with increasing age and social devaluation of alcohol use [6]. Similar mechanisms are to be expected regarding other substances with a large social component during adolescence, such as cannabis.

To be able to explore these theories, we developed the 11-item, 2-subscale Dutch Social Attunement Questionnaire (SAQ; [7]). The 5-item Cognition subscale assesses the extent to which people think about their behavior and how others perceive that behavior. The 6-item Behavior subscale assesses the extent to which people change their behavior to harmonize with the social environment. Our results using the Dutch SAQ are initial but have been largely in line with the stated hypotheses. We consistently showed that social attunement is negatively associated with age [7, 9, 10]. Full-scale SAQ scores have also been positively associated with some implicit social attunement outcomes (i.e., the increase in willingness to drink when peers indicate higher willingness to drink in ambiguous drinking situations) [10] and play a mediating role in the association between age and alcohol use [10]. Furthermore, social attunement appeared to be differentially associated with prospective alcohol use, AUD symptomology, and fMRI social alcohol cue reactivity in adolescent and adult male drinkers [9]; the association between social attunement and alcohol use outcomes was positive during mid-adolescence, but these associations were negative during adulthood. Hence, social attunement might be a risk or protective factor for alcohol use depending on the developmental period. Although the SAQ might overlap with other questionnaires evaluating different aspects of social influence, previous results show a substantial diversion from social conformity [7]. Furthermore, the SAQ distinguishes itself by the positively phrased items, limited number of items, and the lack of specific examples in the questions (e.g., party settings, social influence on buying something), potentially enabling broader generalizability over settings (i.e., not restricted to substance use) and age ranges.

To expand these efforts beyond the Netherlands, a validated English translation of the SAQ is crucial. Also, samples with a larger age range (i.e., including individuals over 35 years old) are needed to evaluate the role of social attunement across the full (adult) lifespan, and the inclusion of other substance use outcomes such as cannabis use can help us to assess the generalizability of these effects. Furthermore, previous studies did not assess the association – and differentiation – of social attunement with other behaviors such as impulsivity and sensation seeking which have often been associated with risk-taking behavior, especially in adolescents [11, 12]. Exploring how social attunement might interact with these factors is important to get a more complete overview of the role of social attunement in risk-taking behaviors such as substance use.

Hence, the aims of this study were twofold. First, we aimed to assess the psychometric properties – specifically the factor structure, internal consistency, and measurement invariance to sex – of the English translation of the SAQ. Based on the original Dutch version [7], we expected an acceptable to good structural fit, acceptable to high internal consistency, and good measurement invariance to sex, indicating similar performance of the SAQ in males and females. Second, we wanted to explore the associations between the SAQ, alcohol use, cannabis use, impulsivity, and sensation seeking and assess whether these associations were dependent on age. We expected SAQ scores to be higher in younger individuals and expected the association between SAQ scores and substance use to depend on age, showing positive associations in younger individuals that would become more negative with increasing age. No specific hypotheses regarding the association of SAQ with impulsivity and sensation seeking and the role of age therein were formulated.

Methods

Translation

The Social Attunement Questionnaire (SAQ; [7]) was originally developed and validated in Dutch. For this study, the Dutch version was translated in English using the following approach. First, one author – fluent in both English and Dutch (E.K.) – translated all 11 SAQ items. Second, two members of the extended research team – fluent in both English and Dutch (including J.C.) – reviewed the items before sending them back to the original translator (E.K.) to implement the suggestions. Finally, the translation was reviewed by a native English researcher for clarity and interpretation of the items, which was cross-checked with the intended interpretation of the Dutch items. The final version of the English SAQ can be found in online supplementary A (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553273).

Procedure and Sample

This study was part of a larger international online study intended to assess the role of social processes in substance use. Participants were recruited via social media (i.e., advertisements and personal contact), Prolific (www.prolific.com), and the student research website of the Erasmus University Rotterdam. In line with the goals of the larger international online study, recruitment focused on individuals that used cannabis and/or alcohol, were proficient in either English or Dutch, and were at least 16 years old. Participants received an online information letter, had the opportunity to contact the researchers with questions, and signed online informed consent before participation. The full experiment took about 1 h to complete and included both questionnaires (including the SAQ) and several computer tasks. At the end of the experiment, participants could opt to enter a raffle in which 1 in 5 participants received a 10-euro gift card and 4 out of all participants received a 50-euro prepaid credit card as compensation. The Ethics Review Committee of Erasmus University Rotterdam (ETH2122-0311) approved all procedures. A total of 557 participants completed the English version of the experiment. Given the online nature of the experiment, various measures were implemented to ensure data quality and a total of 108 were excluded based on (1) failure to correctly complete at least one of the three included catch questions (requesting a specific answer) (N = 17) or (2) inconsistency in reporting across duplicate questions in which participants were asked whether they had ever used alcohol, cannabis, or tobacco (N = 91), resulting in an analysis sample of N = 449.

Measurements

Sample Characteristics

Participants reported on their age, sex, and nationality using single-item questions. Highest completed education was assessed using a multiple choice question with textbox option (personal description) to account for differences in education systems across countries. The question was recoded into 3 categories: (1) less than high school, (2) (pre-)vocational/(pre-)college, and (3) (pre-)university education.

Social Attunement Questionnaire (SAQ)

The newly translated 11-item English SAQ was used to assess social attunement, including the 5-item Cognition and 6-item Behavior subscales. The full version of the original Dutch [7] and translated questionnaire can be found in online supplement A.

Additional Outcomes

Additional outcomes were included to assess the associations of the SAQ with substance use and impulsivity and sensation-seeking tendencies. Alcohol use (AUDIT-C: item 1–3) and related problems (AUDIT-P: 4–10) were assessed using the alcohol use disorder identification test (AUDIT; [13]). Cannabis use (CUDIT-C: item 1–3) and related problems (CUDIT-P: item 4–8) were assessed using the cannabis use disorder identification test (CUDIT-R; [14]). Impulsivity (7 items) and sensation seeking (12 items) were assessed using the 19-item impulsive sensation seeking scale (ImpSS; [15]).

Data Analysis

Factor Structure

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with a diagonally weighted least squares estimator – accounting for non-normality associated with the ordinal nature of the items – was used to assess whether the English translation of the SAQ fit well within the factor structure proposed for the Dutch version. We used KMO values (>0.70 as sufficient) and Bartlett’s test (assessing sphericity assumption) to determine suitability of the data for this approach. Model fit was assessed by evaluating the chi-square test, absolute fit indices (RMSEA <0.08 acceptable, <0.05 good [16]), and comparative fit indices (TLI [17], CFI [18] >0.90 acceptable, >0.95 good).

Internal Consistency

Internal consistency of the full scale and the subscales was assessed by evaluating Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega (alpha, omega >0.70 acceptable, >0.80 good [19]).

Measurement Invariance

A separate CFA was conducted in males and females, before group CFA was used to assess configural (equal factor structure), metric (equal factor loading), scalar (equal indicator intercepts), and strict factorial (equal indicator residuals) invariance to sex [16]. Model fit was considered not significantly worse when ΔRMSEA <0.015 and ΔTLI and ΔCFI <0.010 [20]. Mann-Whitney U tests – accounting for non-normality – were used to assess sex differences in SAQ scores.

The Role of Age in the Associations between SAQ, Substance Use, and Impulsivity

Spearman correlations – accounting for violations of normality – were used to assess the associations of the full SAQ and the Behavior and Cognition subscales with age, AUDIT (split over C and p scores), CUDIT-R (split over C and p scores), and ImpSS. Then, multiple linear regression analysis with age as a moderator – using a product term with centered predictors – was used to assess whether the associations between full SAQ scores and AUDIT (split over C and p scores), CUDIT-R (split over C and p scores), and ImpSS were dependent on age. A tertiary split was used to visualize the continuous moderation and evaluate the direction of the effect.

Software

All analyses were conducted in JASP version 0.19.3 [21].

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 449 participants – 54.5% female – were included in the analysis (Table 1). The two participants (0.4%) indicating nonbinary defined sex at birth were excluded from all analyses including sex as a variable (resulting N = 447). Participants were highly educated, age ranged from 16 to 61 with an average age of 33.86 (SD = 12.04). Participants held a variety of nationalities, with the majority (N = 341; 76%) being European (see online suppl. B). Most participants reported lifetime use of alcohol (96.2%) and about two third of the participants reported lifetime cannabis use (67.9%). AUDIT and CUDIT-R scores showed a large range, but average scores were indicative of moderate-/low-risk use. SAQ and ImpSS scores showed a full range of responses, with a small right skew in ImpSS and a small left skew in SAQ scores.

Factor Structure and Internal Consistency

CFA of the two-factor model (Table 2) showed a significant chi-square test (Χ2 [43, N = 449] = 132.044, p < 0.001), but all other indices showed acceptable to good fit (RMSEA = 0.068, 95% CI: 0.055–0.081; CFI = 0.959; TLI = 0.947) and factor covariance indicated sufficient discriminant validity (COV = 0.656, SE = 0.044). Internal consistency of the Cognition (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.748; McDonald’s omega = 0.754) and Behavior subscale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.782; McDonald’s omega = 0.784) was acceptable. CFA of the one-factor model – combining the subscales – showed a substantially worse fit than the two-factor model (Χ2 [44, N = 449] = 216.999, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.094, 95% CI: 0.081–0.106; CFI = 0.920; TLI = 0.900) but did show higher internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.823; McDonald’s omega = 0.826).

Separate CFA in males and females showed acceptable to good fit indices comparable to each other and the full sample model fit (Table 3). Assessments of changes in RMSEA and CFA when conducting CFA and constraining factor structure (configural), factor loading (metric), intercepts (scalar), and residual variance (strict) showed good measurement invariance to sex differences (Table 3). Looking at SAQ scores, no sex differences were observed for the full scale (U = 23,962.50, p = 0.519) or the subscales (Cognition: U = 22,562.50, p = 0.094; Behavior: U = 25,465.50, p = 0.646).

Correlations between SAQ, Age, Substance Use, and Impulsivity

Highest correlations were observed between subscales of the same questionnaire (e.g., AUDIT-C and AUDIT-P), and substance use outcomes were positively associated with each other, and with impulsivity and sensation seeking (Fig. 1). Age showed a negative correlation with all included scales and subscales except the Cognition subscale of the SAQ. The SAQ Cognition subscale did not show a significant correlation with any of the included variables, suggesting substance use and impulsivity might be primarily associated with the SAQ Behavior subscale. SAQ full-scale scores were positively correlated with AUDIT (guided by AUDIT-P), CUDIT-R (guided by both subscales), and the impulsivity subscale of the ImpSS. The SAQ Behavior subscale showed a similar pattern but was also positively correlated with the total ImpSS score.

The Moderating Role of Age

Moderation analyses, using the full SAQ score to reduce multiple comparisons, showed no moderating role of age in the associations between SAQ and cannabis use outcomes (online suppl. C). However, age moderated the association between AUDIT-C and SAQ scores (B = −0.003, SE(B) = 0.002, t(445) = 1.34, p = 0.036; online suppl. C). It must be noted that this effect would be nonsignificant when performing a within-outcome Bonferroni correction (p = 0.108). There was a positive association between social attunement and alcohol consumption in younger individuals, but this association became more negative with age (Fig. 2). Furthermore, age moderated the association between SAQ and ImpSS (Fig. 2), guided by sensation seeking (B = −0.003, SE(B) = 0.001, t(445) = 2.481, p = 0.013), showing a similar pattern. Where SAQ was positively associated with sensation seeking in younger individuals, this association was more negative with increasing age.

Discussion

The English version of the SAQ showed good psychometric properties and appeared to perform similarly to the original Dutch version. The two-factor structure – distinguishing the Cognition and Behavior subscale of the SAQ – had an acceptable to good fit of the data, which was superior to the fit of the single factor structure. Also, both the subscales and the full SAQ showed acceptable internal consistency, the full scale showed good internal consistency, and the SAQ showed excellent measurement invariance to sex. Furthermore, our results replicate previous results showing that younger age and higher alcohol use are associated with higher SAQ scores [7, 9].

Unlike earlier studies, we put an additional focus on assessing associations with the subscales of the SAQ. The use of the SAQ subscales rather than the full scale appears to be relevant depending on the outcome. While the full scale shows higher reliability and better reflects our conceptual model of social attunement [7], some associations might be largely dependent on one subscale or the other given their clear scoring distinction (correlation; r = 0.46). This became evident in our results: most associations of social attunement with substance use and impulsive sensation seeking outcomes were guided by the Behavior subscale of the SAQ (the Cognition subscale was only associated with age; Fig. 1). This may be explained by the nature of the investigated measures, with a primary focus on externalizing behaviors (i.e., substance use, impulsivity and sensation seeking). However, these results need to be replicated and additional measures – including measures of social cognition [22]), self-monitoring [23], and peer influence [24] – should be studied in relation to social attunement to evaluate convergent and divergent validity. Furthermore, the higher internal consistency of the full SAQ compared to the subscale and the lack of associations of the Cognition subscale with the currently included measures (except age) highlight the need for replication and the possibility that the SAQ is better suited as a single factor scale in some situations or samples.

Looking at impulsivity and sensation seeking, associations with social attunement appeared to depend on age. The simple correlations showed small but significant associations between ImpSS and SAQ, especially between the SAQ Behavior subscale and impulsivity. These results indicate that those with higher impulsivity are more likely to socially attune their behavior, which might in turn affect substance use if alcohol use is expected to improve social status [25]. The lack of an interaction effect with social attunement and age in the prediction of impulsivity suggests that this association could be relatively stable with age. Conversely, no direct associations between sensation seeking and social attunement were observed, but this association appeared to depend on age: younger individuals showed a positive association between SAQ and sensation seeking, but this association became negative with increasing age. This highlights a potentially less stable association with age, with the associations disappearing over time, and that the associations of impulsivity and sensation seeking with social attunement are not identical. This is in line with the social plasticity hypothesis [6]; sensation seeking is socially devaluated over time, with sensation seeking being less appreciated as an adult, resulting in a reversed association with social attunement. Studies with longitudinal designs are needed to evaluate the stability and change of social attunement, as well as associations with social attunement during the aging process. Furthermore, these results should be replicated in samples that were not recruited for their substance use to get insight into these associations in the general population.

Similar results were observed for alcohol consumption. No direct associations between alcohol consumption and social attunement were observed, with the direct association between SAQ and AUDIT being guided by alcohol use problems. However, the association between social attunement and alcohol consumption appears to depend on age: in younger individuals social attunement was related to higher alcohol consumption, but this was not the case in older individuals. High prevalence of heavy alcohol use has historically been associated with adolescence and young adulthood [26] and might be of highest social value in this group [27, 28]. However, the potential influence of social processes and contexts on alcohol consumption in older age groups should not be ignored in future studies [29, 30]. Hence, high consumption might be less of a risk factor for long-term alcohol related problems in those with high SAQ, if they do not experience severe use-related problems. Again, longitudinal studies are needed to assess the stability of social attunement and the associations with alcohol use characteristics.

Interestingly, these age dependent effects were not observed in relation to cannabis use. While SAQ does also show a direct association with CUDIT-R scores (all scales), age did not moderate this association. It should be noted that cannabis use was relatively low in this sample and results should be replicated in heavier use samples. However, it could also be that social influence on cannabis and alcohol use differs and might therefore show different associations with age. There are indications that use trajectories for alcohol and cannabis differ and are complicated. For example, while use of both substances is very common in adolescence, very heavy substance use in this period has been associated with using a single substance (i.e., cannabis or alcohol, not both) and heavy use of cannabis in this period appears more persistent [31]. Also, lower social motives for alcohol use have been associated with higher likelihood of heavy cannabis use [32], but higher social motives for cannabis use appear to be associated with higher likelihood of co-using alcohol on a given day [33]. Future research should include measures of alcohol [34] and cannabis [35] motives to assess how motives influence the relationship between social attunement and use across development. Also, the development of a measure focusing on the socially rewarding value of alcohol and cannabis over time would add to our understanding of socially driven goal-directed consumption.

Some limitations should be noted. First, most participants were highly educated and were lifetime alcohol (96.2%) and cannabis users (67.9%), which is not representative of the use levels in the general population. Second, we did include individuals with a wide range of ages, but most participants were still below 40 (50th percentile (median) age = 31, 75th percentile age = 43). Future research should focus on the recruitment of individuals in this older age range that go through social change – for example, those that just retired or just experienced their children moving out – to assess whether social attunement affects substance use and other behaviors during these periods [36]. Furthermore, we included individuals aged 16–17, which is below the legal drinking age in most of the countries of residence of the included participants, to capture underage drinking and substance use. However, to capture the role of social attunement in initiation of substance use, studies in younger samples are required. Third, next to the social processes, we try to capture with the SAQ, cultural influences are paramount in explaining substance use patterns. Hence, future studies should include additional measures on use norms, perceived peer use, and ease of access to substances to capture this additional sociocultural component. In line with this, country-specific recruitment might be required to capture and evaluate these patterns in samples with sufficient power. Fourth, we only included measures of externalizing behaviors to be associated with social attunement. Based on our results, with social attunement cognition showing limited associations but being negatively associated with age, we invite future studies to include other measures such as social cognition, inhibition, or more implicit social outcomes to assess their association with social attunement cognition. Fifth, our research was focused on the validation of the English SAQ; hence, results and interpretations beyond questionnaire validity should be considered exploratory. In line with this, observed correlations were small, and future research should include additional outcomes that might explain additional variance in the association between social attunement and substance use.

All in all, the English SAQ is a promising measure with good psychometric properties that can be used to assess the role of social attunement in a variety of behaviors from substance use to more general traits such as impulsivity. Importantly, as proposed in the social plasticity hypotheses, social attunement appears to play a role in both the increase of alcohol use in adolescence and the reduction of use – potentially guided by reduced sensation seeking – when maturing. Hence, high social attunement in those with heavy alcohol use might actually be a protective factor rather than a warning sign in the long run.
