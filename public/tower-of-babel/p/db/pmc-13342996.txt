Training humans to detect AI-generated faces
Authors: Amy Dawel, Tanya George, Eric Y. Mah, James D. Dunn, Clare A. M. Sutherland, Nick Argument, James W. Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As AI-generated faces become indistinguishable from real ones, deepfake technology poses escalating threats to information integrity and security. While algorithms can detect deepfakes, they suffer from opacity and critical vulnerabilities—and training humans to identify specific visual artifacts has proven largely ineffective. Here, we introduce a fundamentally different approach that harnesses people’s global impressions of faces. Building on findings that AI and human faces evoke systematically different perceptual impressions (Miller et al., 2023), we trained participants to attend to these distinguishing qualities without explicit instruction on how to use them. Using a rigorous pre–post design with untrained test faces, we demonstrate that all participants (N = 45) improved, with mean accuracy nearly doubling. High performers achieved near-perfect detection, and participants developed metacognitive insight, showing appropriate confidence calibration only after training. A test–retest control study ruled out practice effects as an explanation for training gains, and an online replication demonstrates the scalability of our approach. As the technology advances, targeting systematic biases that are inherent to generative AI, and which manifest in global features, may offer a more durable defense than approaches reliant on image artifacts alone.

AI-generated deepfake faces are now so realistic that human observers can no longer reliably discriminate them from real people (1, 2) (Fig. 1A), fueling misinformation and AI-related fraud projected to cost $40 billion by 2027 (3). While algorithms offer one solution, their decision-making processes remain opaque (the “black box” problem) (4) and recent benchmarking reveals serious vulnerabilities (5). Lacking an AI answer to the deepfake problem, we urgently need to improve human AI-detection abilities.

Previous efforts have focused on training people to identify visual artifacts in AI images, such as mismatched earrings or anatomical abnormalities. These approaches produce disappointing results: either no improvement (6) or modest gains of approximately 10% (2, 7–10). They also increase false positives, causing real faces to be misclassified as AI (6, 7, 9, 10). Most problematically, real-life fraudsters can easily screen or edit out obvious artifacts.

We propose a fundamentally different approach. Recent research by Miller et al. (1) revealed that people’s global impressions are implicitly sensitive to systematic differences between AI and human faces. This sensitivity exists because generative algorithms are inherently biased toward the mathematical average of the tens of thousands of faces on which they are trained. Consequently, AI-generated faces are perceived as more typical in appearance than real human faces (1, 11). People automatically detect these differences, rating AI faces as more symmetrical, well-proportioned, and attractive than human faces, but less distinctive, memorable, and expressive (1) (Fig. 1B).

The critical insight is that, without training, observers fail to use these implicit sensitivities effectively or misinterpret their significance for AI-detection (1, 11). Our training method addresses this gap by drawing attention to six distinguishing perceptual qualities (Fig. 2A). Crucially, we do not instruct participants how to use these differences. Instead, we let them discover the patterns through guided experience rating AI and human faces on these qualities.

(A) Study design. (B) Results for untrained faces show robust improvements in AI-detection from pre- to posttraining. To verify their engagement with the training, participants also completed a version of the singles task testing trained faces and an old/new face recognition task (SI Appendix).

Our design addresses two additional key issues in AI-detection research. First, because people are overconfident about their AI-detection abilities (1), we measured decision confidence to assess whether training improved metacognitive insight. Second, given racial bias in both AI generation (favoring White faces) and human perception (favoring own-race faces) (1), we tested both East Asian and White participants on both face races to assess generalizability. We employed a rigorous pre–post training design using two AI-detection formats (triplet selection and single face judgment; Fig. 2A) to assess robustness of training gains. The pre–post design allowed each participant to serve as their own control, an ideal approach given there are only small practice benefits of task repetition in AI-face detection, as established by a test–retest control study without the training intervention (SI Appendix).

Results

Data Analysis.

Primary dependent variables for the AI-detection tasks, calculated for each participant (N = 45), were percent correct for AI and real faces and median RTs for correct trials, plus d’ (sensitivity) for the single faces task. We also calculated confidence scores for correct and incorrect trials for the single faces task. Primary analyses were mixed ANOVAs, with training phase (pre and post) and face race (Asian and White) within subjects and participant race (Asian and White) between subjects. Post hoc t tests were Holm-corrected. See SI Appendix for details and task reliability analyses.

Triplets Task.

Training produced dramatic improvements for untrained faces (Fig. 2B). Average accuracy nearly doubled from pre- (41.4%) to posttraining [81.1%, F(1, 43) = 199.54, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.82]. Median RTs decreased (P < 0.001), indicating no speed–accuracy trade-off. Training was effective for Asian [48.6 to 85.6%, t(43) = 12.17, P < 0.001] and White faces [34.2 to 76.6%, t(43) = 14.38, P < 0.001], with better overall performance for Asian faces [F(1, 43) = 60.12, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.58]. Participant race showed no significant effects (all ps > 0.211).

Single Faces Task.

Results for untrained single faces replicated the triplets findings. Accuracy improved for both real [51.6 to 78.0%, t(44) = 9.65, P < 0.001] and AI faces [47.1 to 80.6%, t(44) = 12.37, P < 0.001]. Response times decreased (P < 0.001). Sensitivity improved from chance [d’ = −0.04 vs. chance = 0, t(44) = 0.36, P = 0.724] to strong discrimination [d’ = 1.67, F(1, 43) = 195.42, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.82]. Training was effective for Asian [d’ 0.14 to 1.88, t(43) = 12.23, P < 0.001] and White faces [d’ −0.29 to 1.45, t(43) = 11.30, P < 0.001], with better overall performance for Asian faces [F(1, 43) = 20.51, P < 0.001, η2p = 0.32]. Participant race showed no significant effects (all ps > 0.066).

Metacognitive Insight.

Training improved performance awareness. At posttraining, participants showed higher confidence for correct (M = 2.19/3) than incorrect (M = 1.89) decisions on the single faces task [t(44) = 7.62, P < 0.001]. Pretraining confidence did not differ between correct (M = 1.77) and incorrect (M = 1.80) decisions [t(44) = 0.91, P = 0.367].

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that human AI-face detection is highly trainable through an approach targeting global impressions. Large effect sizes, with high performers approaching near-perfect accuracy, confirmed substantial training gains that meaningfully advance beyond the limited success of artifact-based methods. Benefits generalized robustly across test formats, face races, and participant races. Training also improved metacognitive insight, with confidence aligning with accuracy only posttraining. Although universal training uptake may be unrealistic, targeted deployment is feasible given the protocol’s brevity and online scalability (demonstrated in an independent replication study where we observed near-identical training benefits). Broader public education about the difficulty of AI detection may also be achieved through media coverage and gamified training tools.

Why is this approach so effective? Global facial impressions are formed rapidly and intuitively (12) and are sensitive to the systematic biases created by the statistical averaging inherent in generative algorithms (1, 11). However, people typically fail to leverage these impressions for AI-detection without training (1). We speculate that, by directing attention to global impressions, our training enabled participants to become attuned to how these holistic qualities distinguish AI from human faces. The faster posttraining response times further support this interpretation, suggesting participants adopted an efficient global processing strategy.

Our approach also offers practical advantages over purely algorithmic solutions. Combating AI-generated content is inherently a cat-and-mouse endeavor; yet, because generative biases are difficult to eliminate altogether, and because our training targets these biases rather than specific artifacts, the approach can adapt as generation techniques evolve simply by updating the training images. Aggregating judgments from multiple trained observers could ensure robust performance (Fig. 2B, bottom right), while combining human and algorithmic detection could leverage their complementary strengths (13). Maintaining humans in the loop also supports trust in detection decisions, as trained participants can articulate their reliance on impression cues (see qualitative section in SI Appendix).

Several promising directions warrant investigation. Research should establish optimal training protocols and assess long-term retention of AI-detection skills. Hybrid approaches combining global impression training with artifact detection, or incorporating additional rating dimensions such as trustworthiness (2), may further boost performance. Finally, given the rapid advancement of AI-generation techniques beyond StyleGAN, future work should explore whether training generalizes to other formats, including audio and video deepfakes, leveraging global impressions formed across multiple modalities (14).

Participants and Materials

Participants.

Data are from 19 East Asian and 26 White adults tested in-person, recruited from an Australian university pool (35 women, 10 men; Mage = 20.6 y, SD = 3.6, range 18 to 40).

Face Stimuli.

AI faces were generated using StyleGAN3 (15) and screened for visual artifacts (SI Appendix). Faces were balanced across apparent sex and racial groups (Asian and White). Real faces from the FFHQ database were matched to AI faces on apparent sex, race, and age.

AI-Detection Tasks and Training.

Fig. 2A illustrates the triplets and single faces AI-detection tasks and training method (SI Appendix). Face stimuli, data, and analysis files are provided at https://osf.io/r3syv

Ethics Protocol.

This research was approved by the ANU HREC (protocol: 2019/970). Participants provided informed consent by proceeding into the study after reading the participant information.
