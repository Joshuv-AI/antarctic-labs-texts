Microcatheter delivery of embolic protection devices for acute atherosclerotic tandem occlusion: patient series
Authors: Wang Chen, Xianjun Wang, Hongxing Han, Qinjian Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAcute atherosclerotic tandem occlusions of the internal carotid artery (ICA) present significant technical challenges for endovascular intervention. Optimal strategies for the efficient and safe deployment of embolic protection devices (EPDs) in this context remain to be established, as conventional approaches often necessitate multiple device exchanges, prolonging procedural time and potentially increasing risk.OBSERVATIONSIn this retrospective series, 16 consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke due to ICA occlusion underwent endovascular therapy utilizing a microcatheter-facilitated EPD (MICRO-EPD) delivery technique. This novel approach, leveraging a 0.027-inch microcatheter for direct EPD deployment, eliminated the need for standard exchange maneuvers and reduced procedural complexity. Technical success was achieved in all cases, with a median puncture-to-recanalization time of 55 minutes. At 90 days, 56.3% of patients achieved favorable functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score ≤ 2). Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and mortality each occurred in 1 patient (6.3%). No device-related technical failures or unanticipated complications were observed.LESSONSThe MICRO-EPD technique demonstrates a promising advance in the management of acute atherosclerotic tandem occlusions, offering a simplified, safe, and effective method for EPD deployment. Tandem occlusions, defined as simultaneous blockage of the intracranial and ipsilateral extracranial carotid arteries, are most often caused by large artery atherosclerosis and account for 10%–15% of all large vessel occlusions.1 Endovascular treatment has shown efficacy in these patients comparable to that in isolated intracranial occlusions.2–6

Several studies have demonstrated that performing acute extracranial carotid placement during intracranial thrombectomy can enhance recanalization success and improve functional outcomes,1,7–9 with benefits observed whether stent insertion precedes or follows thrombectomy.10,11

Despite extensive research, clinical guidelines offer no consensus on the optimal sequence for treating tandem occlusions.12,13 Some interventionalists favor an intracranial-first approach for faster reperfusion, while others prioritize treating the extracranial lesion to enhance collateral flow and facilitate access. Current evidence does not clearly favor one strategy over another.5,14,15

In response, various specialized techniques have been developed.16–21 The anterograde approach commonly uses embolic protection devices (EPDs), but this often prolongs procedure time due to multiple device exchanges. To address these limitations, we introduce a simplified technique—microcatheter delivery of EPD (MICRO-EPD)—which utilizes a 0.027-inch microcatheter for direct device deployment. This strategy minimizes equipment exchanges and may enhance both procedural efficiency and safety. The present report describes this technique in detail, aiming to provide a practical solution for challenging tandem lesions.

Study Description

Study Design and Patient Selection

This was a retrospective study of patients with acute ischemic stroke due to internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusions treated at our center between December 2023 and January 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) age ≥ 18 years; 2) prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≤ 2; 3) baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score ≥ 6; 4) symptom onset ≤ 24 hours; 5) digital subtraction angiography (DSA)–confirmed C1 segment ICA occlusion due to atherosclerosis, with or without concomitant intracranial occlusion; and 6) treatment using the MICRO-EPD technique. Atherosclerotic lesions were identified based on DSA findings, particularly the presence of calcification and “slim waist” morphology during balloon dilation.22

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Linyi People’s Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because this was a retrospective analysis of fully anonymized data without any patient-identifiable information, and the study posed no additional risk to the patients; all patients had provided informed consent at the time of surgery.

MICRO-EPD Technique

Endovascular treatment was performed under local anesthesia with intravenous propofol or dexmedetomidine. The procedure began with the insertion of an 8-Fr short sheath (Terumo) into the femoral artery. Diagnostic angiography was conducted using a 5-Fr VER catheter (Cordis). On confirmation of ICA occlusion (Fig. 1A), an 8-Fr guiding catheter (Boston Scientific) was advanced to the distal common carotid artery with the aid of a 5-Fr MPA catheter (Cordis).

C: After microwire removal, an EPD is deployed via the microcatheter. D: Balloon dilation is performed while the EPD wire remains in place. E: A carotid stent is deployed at the stenotic site. F: The guiding catheter is advanced near the EPD, which is retrieved under continuous manual suction using a 50-ml syringe. G: Intracranial thrombectomy is performed using a stent retriever or aspiration device.

A 0.014-inch micro-guidewire was carefully navigated through the cervical segment of the ICA, accompanied by a 0.027-inch microcatheter (Fig. 1B). Following microwire removal, microcatheter angiography was performed to confirm the position within the true lumen and assess the patency of the intracranial artery. Subsequently, the SpiderFX EPD (ev3) was deployed using a modified delivery technique (Fig. 1C). Specifically, the original SpiderFX delivery sheath was not advanced as a whole through the microcatheter, which would be technically impossible given the outer diameter requirements. Instead, the hub of the SpiderFX delivery sheath was aligned with the tail of the 0.027-inch microcatheter in a “hub-to-hub” fashion. The Spider filter was then gently pushed from its original sheath into and through the microcatheter lumen, with the dedicated sheath functioning only as a short pusher rather than as a full delivery catheter (Fig. 2). This modification allowed direct deployment of the SpiderFX through the microcatheter without the need to withdraw the microcatheter and exchange it for the standard delivery sheath, thereby avoiding additional exchanges. The C1 segment of ICA was then dilated using a balloon catheter (Invatec, Medtronic) while maintaining the EPD in place (Fig. 1D). Repeat angiography was performed to evaluate residual stenosis, followed by the placement of a carotid WALLSTENT (Boston Scientific) at the site of stenosis (Fig. 1E).

Schematic demonstration of the hub-to-hub technique. The distal hub of the original SpiderFX delivery sheath is aligned with the tail of the 0.027-inch microcatheter, allowing the filter device to be gently advanced from its native sheath into and through the microcatheter lumen. In this modified approach, the dedicated sheath serves only as a short pusher rather than as a full delivery catheter, thereby enabling direct microcatheter-based deployment of the SpiderFX EPD without additional exchanges.

If the intracranial artery remained patent, the EPD was retrieved and the guiding catheter was withdrawn, concluding the procedure. In cases in which the intracranial large artery was occluded, the 8-Fr guiding catheter was advanced through the stent near the EPD. Continuous manual suction was applied via the guiding catheter using a 50-ml locked syringe, while the EPD was withdrawn directly through the guiding catheter without the use of its bundled retrieval sheath (Fig. 1F). Thrombectomy was then performed with stent-like equipment or a combined aspiration instrument at the thrombus site (Fig. 1G).

In summary, the MICRO-EPD technique involves the following steps: 1) advancing a microwire and 0.027-inch microcatheter through the lesion, 2) deploying the Spider EPD via the microcatheter, 3) performing angioplasty and stent implantation, 4) retrieving the Spider EPD directly through the guiding catheter, and 5) conducting thrombectomy as necessary. It is crucial that the inner diameter of the microcatheter used in this technique be at least 0.027 inches, as this was determined to be the optimal size after extensive testing with various combinations, including a 0.021-inch microcatheter and the FilterWire EZ (Boston Scientific) EPD in vitro. This simplified sequence eliminated the need for microcatheter-guidewire exchanges, which may reduce procedural complexity and embolic risk in suitable patients.

Periprocedural Antithrombotic Strategy

Perioperative antithrombotic management followed institutional protocols tailored for emergent tandem occlusions. Heparin was not routinely used. Instead, tirofiban was administered once balloon angioplasty or stent insertion was deemed necessary, with an intravenous loading dose of 0.4 μg/kg/min for 30 minutes followed by a continuous infusion of 0.1 μg/kg/min for 24 hours. If no intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) was observed on 24-hour noncontrast CT, dual oral antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg once daily) was initiated 4 hours before tirofiban discontinuation.

Imaging and Outcome Assessment

Pial collateral status was assessed by DSA using the ASITN/SIR (American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology) grading scale, with scores ≤ 2 defined as poor collaterals.23 Recanalization success was evaluated using the expanded Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (eTICI) score, with scores of 2b and 3 considered successful.24

The primary clinical outcome was defined as favorable 90-day mRS scores of 0–2. Safety endpoints included any ICH, symptomatic ICH (per Heidelberg criteria25), and stent reocclusion, evaluated within 72 hours postprocedure using CT, CTA, or vascular ultrasound.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data following a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while nonnormally distributed data are presented as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are reported as count and percentage. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM).

Clinical and Procedural Results

A total of 16 patients were treated with the MICRO-EPD technique. The mean age was 68 years, and 75% were male. The most common vascular risk factors were hypertension (56.3%), smoking (25.0%), and hypercholesterolemia (18.8%). The mean baseline NIHSS score was 15. The median Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) was 7 (IQR 4–7) (Table 1). Among these patients, 10 (62.5%) had tandem occlusions involving both the C1 segment and an intracranial large artery, while 6 (37.5%) had isolated C1 occlusions. A representative case is shown in Fig. 3. The median time from arterial puncture to recanalization was 55 minutes (IQR 45–64 minutes), and the median time from symptom onset to recanalization was 412 minutes (IQR 330–493 minutes). Successful recanalization (eTICI ≥ 2b) was achieved in all cases (Table 1).

Complications and Outcomes

As summarized in Table 2, complications were minimal. Distal embolism occurred in 1 patient (6.3%), attributed to intracranial thrombus retrieval via a guiding catheter. Stent reocclusion was observed in another patient (6.3%). At the 90-day follow-up, 9 patients (56.3%) achieved a favorable outcome (mRS score ≤ 2). ICH occurred in 4 patients (25.0%), including 1 case (6.3%) of symptomatic ICH. All 4 cases occurred in patients with tandem lesions (1 with C1+terminal ICA, 2 with C1+M1, and 1 with C1+M2), while no ICH was observed in isolated cervical ICA occlusion cases. The mortality rate was 6.3% (1 patient). No device-related complications or unexpected technical failures were observed during the application of the MICRO-EPD technique, supporting its procedural consistency in this limited cohort.

Discussion

Observations

This study demonstrates that the MICRO-EPD technique is a feasible and efficient strategy for managing acute atherosclerotic tandem occlusions. By allowing direct EPD deployment via a 0.027-inch microcatheter, our approach streamlines the antegrade workflow, potentially reducing both procedural time and complexity while maintaining safety.

Notably, our median puncture-to-recanalization time (55 minutes) closely mirrors the efficiency reported with other routine EPD-based approaches such as the PEARS (protect-expand-aspiration-revascularization-stent) technique.16 The elimination of multiple device exchanges may help mitigate microembolic risk, as well as procedural complications.26 In our cohort, only a single case of distal embolism occurred, and it was associated with intracranial thrombus retrieval rather than cervical manipulation.

Traditional functional scales like the mRS can underestimate subtle neurological sequelae, including cognitive or executive dysfunction, which are increasingly recognized through advanced imaging and structured outcome assessment.17–20,27,28 Silent or minor infarction, likely due to microembolism, may arise even when gross clinical outcomes appear favorable. As such, the routine use of embolic protection—whether by EPDs or balloon guide catheters (BGCs)—remains advisable in these high-risk procedures.

Although antegrade flow is compromised in patients with tandem occlusion and concomitant intracranial large vessel blockage, embolic protection during cervical angioplasty and stent insertion may still be beneficial. First, pial or perforator collaterals may maintain partial distal perfusion, rendering downstream territories vulnerable to embolic insult. Second, since intracranial thrombectomy is planned immediately after cervical revascularization, any additional embolic burden generated during angioplasty or stent placement could accumulate on the existing thrombus, potentially increasing clot burden and reducing first-pass recanalization success. Third, the EPD itself serves as a stabilizing anchor, facilitating precise device deployment in cases of unstable plaque. Lastly, even in the absence of overt antegrade flow, microemboli may enter small distal vessels through residual channels or collaterals, contributing to silent infarction or delayed deficits. For these reasons, we consider embolic protection an important precaution even when distal occlusion is present.

While embolic protection remains a widely accepted strategy during carotid stent placement, its application in the acute stroke setting varies. Some operators opt not to use EPDs in favor of BGCs with continuous aspiration, which can also reduce the risk of distal embolism. However, the use of BGCs may be technically challenging in patients with tortuous anatomy or when navigating through complex cervical lesions. In this context, the MICRO-EPD technique offers an alternative method of achieving distal protection while simplifying the antegrade workflow. Future studies directly comparing outcomes between EPD, BGC, and nonprotection strategies in acute tandem occlusion would be valuable to further guide practice.

The best procedural sequence for tandem occlusion intervention continues to be debated, with both antegrade and retrograde approaches showing comparable outcomes in prior studies.10,11 Our findings suggest that, particularly in cases with challenging cervical anatomy or unstable plaque, the MICRO-EPD–facilitated antegrade strategy may provide procedural advantages by securing protection prior to intracranial access.29–31

Additionally, a notable subset of our cohort consisted of patients with isolated cervical ICA occlusion. Recent literature emphasizes the value of timely revascularization in such cases, highlighting the potential benefit of direct and simplified endovascular strategies like MICRO-EPD for selected patients with moderate to severe or evolving strokes.32–34

Lessons

The experience from this series underscores the importance of device selection and technical planning in implementing the MICRO-EPD technique. Our in vitro testing confirmed that a microcatheter with a 0.027-inch inner diameter is essential for safe and effective Spider EPD delivery; other EPDs such as the FilterWire EZ remain incompatible with this approach. This device limitation, while currently restrictive, points to an opportunity for future innovation and expanded applicability.

For cases involving highly calcified lesions or no ICA stump, a staged approach—starting with a smaller microcatheter and wire before upgrading to the MICRO-EPD system—may enhance procedural success. Furthermore, routine aspiration during EPD retrieval appears to further lower the risk of embolic complications, a finding in line with prior technical reports and experimental studies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which limits the external validity and generalizability of our findings. Device compatibility constraints and lack of a comparator arm are additional weaknesses. Nevertheless, the consistently high recanalization rate, favorable safety profile, and operational simplicity observed here suggest that MICRO-EPD may represent a meaningful advance in the endovascular treatment of complex tandem occlusions.

In summary, our study provides initial evidence that MICRO-EPD deployment is both feasible and safe, and may streamline workflow in selected patients with acute atherosclerotic tandem occlusions. Further prospective and multicenter validation is warranted to fully define its role in contemporary stroke intervention.
