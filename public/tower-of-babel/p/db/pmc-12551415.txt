Therapeutic efficacy of Yangyin Shugan capsules against premature ovarian insufficiency: results from a multicenter, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Xiaojing Cao, Lei Song, Jiarui Hou, Siyu Mao, Qiaolin Du, Fangping Cheng, Daning Feng, Jingyan Zhu, Qiuxia Chen, Xiaoyu Li, Jian Liu, Wenwei Ouyang, Xuchun Huang, Xiaoyun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:This multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of Yangyin Shugan (YYSG) capsules in women with premature ovarian insufficiency (POI).Methods:This study enrolled 548 Chinese women with POI (August 2016-June 2021) from 13 Guangdong hospitals. After screening, 333 participants were randomized (2:1) to receive YYSG capsules or placebo for 12 weeks. Primary outcomes included the modified Kupperman Index (KI) and serum basal follicle-stimulating hormone, basal E2, and anti-Müllerian hormone levels. Secondary outcomes included psychological status scores (Self-Rating Depression Scale, Self-Rating Anxiety Scale) and ovarian markers (antral follicle count, peak systolic velocity).Results:YYSG demonstrated superior efficacy versus placebo across all endpoints (P < 0.01), including greater reductions in KI scores (−22.2 vs −6.0) and basal follicle-stimulating hormone levels (−23.7 vs −10.4 IU/L) and greater increases in anti-Müllerian hormone levels (2.5 vs 0.8 ng/mL). The YYSG group exhibited larger antral follicle count gains (4.3 vs 1.1 follicles) and bilateral peak systolic velocity improvements (left: 10.8 vs 4.9 cm/s; right: 10.0 vs 4.4 cm/s) and greater reductions in psychological scores (Self-Rating Depression Scale: −12.9 vs −1.3; Self-Rating Anxiety Scale: −15.7 vs −3.7).Conclusions:YYSG capsules significantly improved ovarian reserve function and emotional disorders in women with POI, demonstrating good efficacy and safety. These findings support the potential application of YYSG capsules in clinical practice.

Globally, premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) affects 3.5% of women. By region, the prevalence was highest at 11.3% in North America (these numbers may have been skewed, as many of the women had either HIV or cancer). In addition, the prevalence was higher in developing countries (5.3%) than in developed countries (3.1%). The prevalence of POI has increased over the past 20 years.1 POI significantly affects reproductive health and overall well-being, and it is characterized by the cessation of ovarian function before 40 years of age.1,2 The condition is often accompanied by symptoms such as amenorrhea, infertility, and various menopausal symptoms, severely affecting the quality of life of women. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying POI involve accelerated follicular depletion and dysfunction, resulting in decreased estrogen production and elevated gonadotropin levels.3

Current treatment options for POI primarily include hormone therapy (HT), fertility treatments, and lifestyle modifications.4 HT aims to alleviate menopausal symptoms and mitigate long-term health risks by restoring estrogen levels, whereas fertility treatments are designed to assist women who wish to conceive.5 However, these therapies are associated with limitations and challenges, including incomplete symptom relief, potential side effects, and limited efficacy in restoring fertility.6 Consequently, there is an urgent need for alternative treatments that can comprehensively manage POI symptoms and long-term health outcomes.

Yangyin Shugan (YYSG) capsules, rooted in these principles, have been historically utilized in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) to address a variety of women’s health issues, particularly those related to reproductive and hormonal imbalances. YYSG capsules are derived from the classic TCM formula “Yiguan Jian,” originally documented during the Qing Dynasty. The effective components of YYSG capsules include Rehmannia glutinosa (1030 parts), Paeonia lactiflora (1020 parts), Bupleurum chinense (515 parts), Curcuma aromatica (1015 parts), Cyperus rotundus (515 parts), Dioscorea opposita (1030 parts), and Paeonia rubra (1015 parts). This medication is cost-effective and readily available, and it effectively regulates participants’ basal endocrine levels, increases the number of antral follicles, and improves ovarian blood flow.7

Our unpublished data from patent-protected research (Chinese Patent No. ZL201910387573.7) demonstrated YYSG capsules’ ability to enhance ovarian reserve and function in murine models. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the treatment downregulated apoptosis-related proteins, including Bax (−42%, P < 0.01) and caspase-3 (−38%, P < 0.05), and increased Bcl-2 expression (55%, P < 0.01) compared with controls.8 These findings were validated through standardized pharmacodynamic and toxicological evaluations conducted under Good Laboratory Practice conditions at Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine. In addition, YYSG capsules effectively improve the thymus index, phagocytic index, and phagocytic index in mice; increased hippocampal dopamine and 5-hydroxytriptamine levels and the swimming and hypoxia tolerance times; and reduced the tail suspension immobility time. Furthermore, small-scale human trials reported improvements in menstrual regularity and hormonal profiles among participants with POI.7

Therefore, this multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial rigorously evaluated the efficacy and safety of YYSG capsules in a large population of menopausal women.

METHODS

Research design

This investigation was executed as a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial to assess the therapeutic efficacy and safety of YYSG capsules in participants diagnosed with POI. The study enrolled participants from the outpatient departments of 13 hospitals across Guangdong Province, China, from August 2016 to June 2021. To ensure robustness and eliminate bias, participants were randomly assigned to receive either the YYSG capsules or placebo for a 12-week period, with both participants and researchers blinded to group assignment. The 12-week intervention period was selected on the basis of our pilot pharmacokinetic study, which demonstrated that the plasma concentrations of the bioactive components of YYSG (eg, paeoniflorin, catalpol) peaked by week 10, suggesting biological plausibility for a 12-week assessment.

The study received ethical clearance from the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. B2015-115-01). This study strictly adhered to the guidelines of Good Clinical Practice (GCP)9 and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring ethical conduct throughout the research process. The trial was officially registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn,identifier ChiCTR-IOR-16007694, date: January 4, 2016). Compliance with the CONSORT guidelines for TCM was maintained throughout the study.10 Informed consent was obtained in writing from all participants before their enrollment in the trial.

Participants

The diagnosis of POI followed international consensus criteria requiring the following: (1) amenorrhea or oligomenorrhea (cycle length >35 d) for ≥4 consecutive months and (2) two elevated basal FSH measurements (>25 IU/L) obtained ≥4 weeks apart under standardized conditions (early follicular phase for cycling women, random timing for amenorrheic subjects).

The exclusion criteria included hormone therapy use within 3 months before screening or participation in other clinical trials affecting menstrual cycles. Participants with amenorrhea or irregular menstruation resulting from surgical or medical interventions, such as chemotherapy, pelvic radiotherapy, or various ovarian surgeries, were excluded. In addition, those with chronic or severe diseases, such as gastrointestinal, renal, or liver diseases, treated diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, cancer, autoimmune diseases, thrombosis, or phlebitis, were not eligible. Individuals with known allergies to the study medication or a history of severe allergic reactions were also excluded.

Sample size

The sample size calculation was based on our pilot study demonstrating a 2.5-fold increase in the antral follicle count (AFC) with YYSG capsule treatment (effect size = 0.8, Cohen d).7 Using PASS 15.0 (NCSS, LLC), we estimated 288 participants (192 YYSG:96 placebo) would provide 90% power (β = 0.1) to detect this difference at α = 0.05 (2-tailed). The 2:1 allocation ratio was strategically implemented to facilitate comprehensive safety monitoring of the novel intervention, optimize participant enrollment given inherent participant preferences for active treatment arms, and preserve statistical validity while addressing logistical complexities inherent in multicenter trial coordination. Accounting for a 15% attrition rate (based on prior TCM trials), the final target was 333 participants (222 YYSG:111 placebo), distributed proportionally across 13 centers according to their enrollment capacities.

Recruitment and randomization

Participants for the study were recruited through advertisements across 13 hospitals in Guangdong Province, including Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine, Baiyun TCM Hospital, Bao’an TCM Hospital, Conghua TCM Hospital, Futian TCM Hospital, Huangpu TCM Hospital, Huizhou TCM Hospital, Liwan TCM Hospital, Nanhai TCM Hospital, Yangjiang TCM Hospital, Fangcun TCM Hospital, Chancheng Central Hospital, and Conghua Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital. In total, 548 individuals were screened, and 339 were deemed eligible and randomized to receive either YYSG capsules (n = 228) or placebo (n = 117). Six participants were excluded because of nonadherence to treatment or unavailable data, resulting in a final analysis of 333 participants (222 in the YYSG group and 111 in the placebo group). The dropout rate was lower in the YYSG group (2.7%) than in the placebo group (5.4%), albeit without significance (P = 0.15, Fig. 1).

The randomization employed a stratified block design with a block size of 8 to ensure an even distribution of participants based on age and basal FSH (basal follicle-stimulating hormone) levels. An independent statistician generated the random allocation sequence using SAS software. Both participants and researchers were blinded to the treatment assignments to maintain objectivity, with medications identically packaged and labeled with codes. Emergency envelopes containing the randomization codes were prepared for serious adverse events or urgent unblinding situations.

Treatment protocols

Participants in the intervention group received YYSG capsules, whereas those in the control group were administered a placebo. Both groups were instructed to take 4 capsules 3 times daily, resulting in a total daily dosage of 6 g for the YYSG group. Each YYSG capsule contained 0.5 g of the herbal formulation.

YYSG capsules (Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Guangzhou, China) were derived from the traditional Chinese medicine formula Yiguan Jian, and they included the following ingredients: Rehmannia glutinosa, Paeonia lactiflora, Bupleurum chinense, Curcuma aromatica, Cyperus rotundus, Dioscorea opposita, and Paeonia rubra. YYSG capsules have received clinical trial approval from the National Medical Products Administration of China (2023LP01281). The placebo capsules were made to match the appearance, taste, and smell of the YYSG capsules, ensuring participant blinding. Both the YYSG and placebo capsules were identically packaged in medicinal plastic bottles, each containing 36 capsules. These bottles were uniformly labeled with the drug name, observation period code, manufacturer, usage instructions, dosage, and storage conditions.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome measures of this study included the modified Kupperman index (KI)11,12 and serum basal FSH, basal estradiol (basal E2), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels. These metrics were assessed at baseline and after the 12-week treatment period to evaluate the impact of YYSG capsules on menopausal symptoms and ovarian function. FSH was specifically chosen because of its role as a key biomarker for ovarian function, with elevated levels indicating reduced ovarian reserve.

Secondary outcome measures encompassed the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS)13 and Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)14 to assess emotional well-being, the AFC to evaluate ovarian reserve, and ovarian peak systolic velocity (PSV)15 to measure blood flow to the ovaries. In addition, adverse events were closely monitored throughout the study to ensure the safety of the interventions.

Data collection and laboratory analysis

Blood samples were collected from participants at baseline and at the end of the 12-week treatment period. These samples were used to measure basal FSH, basal E2, and AMH levels. All blood tests were performed at the central laboratory of the Medical Laboratory Department of Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine. To ensure consistency and accuracy, samples from the same participant were analyzed using the same batch of reagents.

The Roche Cobas 8000 (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Baden-Württemberg, Germany) automated biochemical analyzer was employed for the blood sample analyses, providing reliable and standardized results. In addition, color Doppler examinations were conducted to assess ovarian function and blood flow. Transvaginal ultrasound was used for AFC and PSV assessments. The procedure was performed by 2 certified sonographers, ensuring precise measurement. This comprehensive data collection and analysis approach ensured the robustness and validity of the study’s findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) in accordance with a predefined analysis plan. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.10 threshold). For primary outcomes (modified KI, basal FSH, AMH), mixed-effect models for repeated measures were implemented, incorporating treatment group, time, and the group-time interaction as fixed effects, with baseline values and study center as covariates, using an unstructured covariance matrix. Secondary outcomes employed appropriate methods as follows: analysis of covariance with baseline adjustment for parametric data (ovarian PSV), the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables (AFC), and Fisher exact test for categorical safety outcomes. Multiple comparisons were addressed through Hochberg’s step-up procedure to control type I errors. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen d (parametric) and Hodges-Lehmann estimators (nonparametric), accompanied by 95% CIs. Missing data were primarily handled through last observation carried forward, with sensitivity analyses conducted through multiple imputation by chained equations and complete case analysis. Two-tailed testing with α = 0.05 was applied throughout the study, except where adjusted for multiplicity. Clinical significance thresholds were prespecified as a KI reduction of ≥30%, FSH decline of ≥50%, and 2-fold increase of AMH from baseline to contextualize statistical findings.

Safety assessments

Routine examinations were conducted after 12 weeks of treatment to evaluate various parameters, including routine blood, urine, and stool tests, liver and kidney function tests, electrocardiogram, gynecologic ultrasound, and breast ultrasound. These assessments were completed both before and after treatment to identify any abnormalities in biochemical indicators or abnormal proliferative changes in the uterus and adnexa (eg, endometrial thickening, ovarian cysts, uterine fibroids) and breast tissue (eg, breast nodules, cysts). Adverse events were closely monitored and reported throughout the study. Participants were asked to report any discomfort or side effects, with specific attention to any unexpected symptoms.

Primary outcome assessment

Modified KI and hormone levels were significantly improved in the YYSG group compared with those in the placebo group after 12 weeks of treatment. In the YYSG group, the mean total modified KI decreased markedly from 35.6 ± 5.2 to 13.4 ± 7.9 (P < 0.01), whereas that in the placebo group declined from 33.4 ± 4.7 to 27.3 ± 7.8 (P < 0.01). Significant reductions in the modified KI were observed in both groups, but the magnitude of improvement was markedly greater in the YYSG group (62.4% reduction vs 18.0% in placebo, P < 0.01). Similar trends were observed across all domains (vasomotor symptoms, insomnia, depression, sexual dysfunction), with YYSG demonstrating superior efficacy (all P < 0.01, Table 2, Fig. 3).

Similarly, significant improvements were noted in the serum levels of basal FSH and AMH. In the YYSG group, basal FSH levels decreased from 31.6 ± 4.0 to 8.0 ± 3.8 IU/L, whereas AMH levels increased from 0.5 ± 0.5 to 3.0 ± 1.5 ng/mL (both P < 0.01). Secondary outcome assessment

The secondary outcomes, including SDS, SAS, and PSV, were significantly improved in the YYSG group compared with the findings in the placebo group after 12 weeks of treatment. The mean SDS score in the YYSG group decreased from 46.0 ± 6.4 to 33.1 ± 6.0 over the treatment period, whereas the placebo group exhibited a smaller reduction from 46.6 ± 6.9 to 45.4 ± 9.8 (P < 0.01). Similarly, the SAS score in the YYSG group declined from 58.8 ± 7.7 to 43.1 ± 8.2, whereas that in the placebo group decreased from 58.9 ± 8.5 to 55.2 ± 12.5 (P < 0.01). These results indicate that YYSG treatment was significantly more effective in alleviating the symptoms of depression and anxiety in participants with POI (Table 3, Fig. 4).

In addition, ovarian PSV was substantially improved in the YYSG group. Left ovarian peak systolic velocity (LEFPSV) increased from 8.3 ± 5.2 to 19.1 ± 7.5 cm/s, and right ovarian peak systolic velocity (RIGPSV) increased from 10.1 ± 6.4 to 20.1 ± 7.2 cm/s (both P < 0.01). YYSG treatment significantly improved menstrual resumption rates compared with placebo [62.1% (138/222) vs 37.8% (42/111); χ2 = 8.731, P < 0.01], with an absolute risk reduction of 24.3% (95% CI: 14.2-34.4) and number needed to treat of 4.1 (95% CI: 2.9-7.0). Safety assessment and medication adherence

No significant differences in safety parameters were observed between the groups, and no severe adverse events were reported. Routine evaluations after 12 weeks of treatment revealed no abnormalities in blood tests, including complete blood count, renal function, and liver function tests, as well as urine analyses and electrocardiogram. Breast and uterine examinations also revealed no anomalies. Key parameters such as complete blood count (white blood cells, red blood cells, and hemoglobin), renal function (serum creatinine and blood urea nigtrogen), and liver function (alanine transaminase and aspartate aminotransferase) data were within normal ranges for both groups, with no significant differences observed. Both treatments were well-tolerated, and no severe adverse events necessitating hospitalization were reported. In addition, no adverse events were documented in either group.

Overall, treatment adherence was high among participants. Analysis of the remaining medication capsules indicated that participants took at least 95% of the prescribed doses. Adherence rates were similar across both treatment groups, demonstrating that participants consistently followed the treatment protocol.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrated that following treatment with YYSG capsules, women with POI experienced significant improvements in vasomotor symptoms. In addition, notable enhancements were observed in participants’ mood, sleep quality, and vaginal discomfort, indicating that YYSG capsules had a substantial effect on tonifying the kidneys and soothing the liver.16

After 12 weeks of treatment, we found that YYSG capsules significantly improved ovarian function in women with POI. This was evidenced by a marked reduction in basal FSH levels from 31.74 ± 4.05 to 8.04 ± 3.77, and a significant increase in AMH levels from 0.54 ± 0.49 to 2.99 ± 1.47. Compared with placebo, the YYSG formulation effectively increased the AFC and ovarian PSV, aligning with our previous findings.7 These improvements underscore the potential of Chinese medicine in effectively managing POI symptoms and enhancing ovarian reserve.

The absence of estradiol elevation despite improved FSH/AMH suggests that YYSG can restore ovarian function through FSH receptor modulation rather than direct steroidogenesis, consistent with TCM mechanisms prioritizing systemic regulation over isolated hormone stimulation.17 This profile could offer clinical advantages by reducing estrogen-related risks during prolonged POI management. Further investigation is needed to determine whether extended treatment elicits differential E2 responses.

Comparison with previous studies

In clinical practice, women with POI often experience negative emotions because of decreased ovarian function.18 To assess the emotional impact of POI, we utilized the SDS and SAS scores.19 Our findings revealed that after 12 weeks of treatment, the YYSG group exhibited significant improvements in anxiety and depression compared with the placebo group. The SDS score decreased from 45.99 ± 6.40 to 33.07 ± 6.02, and the SAS score declined from 58.80 ± 7.67 to 43.11 ± 8.15. This suggests that YYSG alleviates negative emotions in women with POI by modulating neuroendocrine pathways (eg, serotonin/dopamine balance) and improving ovarian hemodynamics, consistent with its TCM mechanism of soothing liver Qi stagnation and tonifying kidney Yin deficiency.8

These results are consistent with the existing literature, which also highlights the positive impact of TCM on ovarian reserve and emotional well-being in participants with POI.20,21 Previous studies reported similar improvements in ovarian function and emotional health following the administration of YYSG capsules.7 The observed increase in AMH levels and reduction in basal FSH levels align with the findings of earlier research, further validating the efficacy of YYSG in improving ovarian reserve. In addition, the significant reductions in anxiety and depression scores underscore the holistic benefits of YYSG, offering a comprehensive approach to managing both the physiological and psychological aspects of POI.

Justification for psychological assessment tools

The selection of the SDS and SAS for evaluating psychological outcomes in this study was intentional, given their relevance to the specific emotional challenges faced by participants with POI. Depression and anxiety are the most common mental health issues in POI, being driven by the sudden loss of ovarian function and associated hormonal changes.22,23 Although other assessment tools, such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and SF-36 Quality of Life Questionnaire, could have offered a broader perspective on sleep and general well-being,24 our aim was to focus primarily on the emotional domains that most affect participants’ day-to-day lives. The SDS and SAS were chosen for their sensitivity to intervention effects in these domains, providing a focused approach to evaluating the impact of YYSG treatment on emotional health.

Biological mechanisms of Yangyin Shugan

The observed beneficial effects of YYSG capsules in this study might be attributable to several underlying biological mechanisms. Pharmacodynamic and toxicological research demonstrated that YYSG can enhance ovarian reserve and function by modulating the expression of key pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic markers. Specifically, YYSG has been demonstrated to decrease the expression of the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax16 and caspase-3 and increase that of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2.16 This modulation of apoptotic pathways likely contributes to the improved ovarian function observed in participants with POI.

The individual components of YYSG play crucial roles in its overall efficacy. For example, Rehmannia glutinosa and Paeonia lactiflora are known for their ability to nourish the blood and regulate the endocrine system, which can help restore hormonal balance.25 Bupleurum chinense 26 and Curcuma aromatica have been found to possess anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating effects, which might further support ovarian health. In addition, Cyperus rotundus 27 and Dioscorea opposite 28 are traditionally used to alleviate liver Qi stagnation and kidney deficiency, which are often implicated in POI. These combined effects enhance ovarian blood flow, increase the number of antral follicles, and improve overall reproductive health, as evidenced by the significant changes in basal FSH and AMH levels observed in this study.

A new hope for menopausal symptoms

The findings from this study underscore the potential of YYSG capsules as a well-tolerated intervention for treating POI. The significant improvements observed in menopausal symptoms, ovarian reserve, and emotional well-being highlight the effectiveness of YYSG in addressing the multifaceted challenges faced by participants with POI. This suggests that YYSG could be a valuable addition to the current treatment options for POI, providing a non-hormone alternative that is both effective and safe.

The efficacy of YYSG in alleviating menopausal symptoms also suggests its potential applicability beyond participants younger than 40 years with POI. The promising results indicate that YYSG might serve as an innovative alternative to HT for managing menopausal symptoms in older women who are either unsuitable for or reluctant to undergo hormone therapy. Given the observed benefits in this study, future clinical trials are warranted to further explore the potential of YYSG in improving symptoms in postmenopausal women.29,30

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths, including its multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled design, which enhanced the validity and reliability of the findings using YYSG therapy compared with previous trials.7 The rigorous methodology, including stratified block randomization and comprehensive blinding procedures, ensured that the results were not influenced by biases. The large sample size and the use of standardized outcome measures further strengthened the study, which provided robust evidence of the efficacy of YYSG capsules in treating POI.

However, the study also had some limitations. First, the absence of an HT comparator limited direct comparisons with the current first-line treatment, though this reflects participant preferences for non-hormone alternatives. Second, the lack of long-term follow-up precluded conclusions about sustained efficacy after treatment discontinuation. In addition, there was potential for self-selection bias, as most participants expressed a strong preference against hormone therapy, and they had high expectations for the efficacy of TCM during the intake interviews.31 Finally, the study relied on clinical and hormonal endpoints without directly measuring biomarkers of liver/kidney Qi or blood circulation (eg, inflammatory cytokines and neurotransmitter levels), which could have provided mechanistic insights into YYSG’s effects aligned with TCM theory.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings of this multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial indicate that YYSG capsules significantly improve ovarian reserve function and alleviate emotional disorders in women with POI. Over a 12-week treatment period, YYSG capsules led to substantial reductions in modified KI and basal FSH levels, along with notable increases in AMH levels, AFC, and ovarian PSV. These findings endorse the broader clinical application and promotion of YYSG capsules in managing POI.
