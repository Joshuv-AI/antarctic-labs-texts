Postpartum Long-Acting Reversible Contraception Use Among People With Sickle Cell Disease in the United States
Authors: Preetha Nandi, Amy Luo, Anne Burke, Lydia H. Pecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immediate postpartum long-acting reversible contraception use is high among people with sickle cell disease, a population with high acuity in reproductive health outcomes.

People with sickle cell disease (SCD) have high rates of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as miscarriage, stillbirth, preeclampsia, preterm birth, cesarean delivery, and maternal mortality.1–3 These risks make patient-centered, evidence-based counseling and accessibility of contraception critical in optimizing maternal and fetal well-being.4 Postpartum contraception, especially immediate postpartum long-acting reversible contraception (LARC), is a highly effective strategy to achieve this for medically complex patients, including those with SCD.5–10 People with SCD may benefit from immediate postpartum LARC for many reasons: LARC offers effective contraception during postpartum recovery; hormone-containing LARC can treat comorbid dysmenorrhea or heavy menstrual bleeding; and hormone-containing LARC is aligned with the most recent U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use SCD-specific recommendations.6,10 Although medical societies recommend immediate postpartum LARC as an effective and safe contraceptive method for high-risk populations, patterns of immediate postpartum LARC use among people with SCD are unknown.3,6

People with SCD face unique barriers to achieving contraceptive care.4 Previous research suggests lower LARC use and a higher rate of unintended pregnancy among people with SCD compared with the general population.4,11–14 Adolescents with SCD have lower contraception use compared with their healthy peers, with contraceptive uptake associated with presence of severe disease (defined by study authors as having at least three pain crises per year).15,16 This pattern may be related to a lack of contraceptive counseling despite many medical encounters or intersectional factors that limit how this population accesses reproductive health care, such as insurance churn, systemic racism, geography, and historic reproductive coercion.17–21 Providing immediate postpartum LARC can help mitigate known structural and administrative barriers to contraceptive access, especially because many patients are motivated to use contraception in the immediate postpartum period.8,9 Therefore, this study aimed to measure immediate postpartum LARC uptake among individuals with SCD compared with the general population using a national dataset and to determine whether SCD status is independently associated with immediate postpartum LARC uptake.

METHODS

We performed a cross-sectional study using data from the Agency for Healthcare Quality and Research 2012–2019 National Inpatient Sample (NIS), a nationally representative sample of inpatient, all-payer claims from U.S. hospitals. This dataset accounts for approximately 97% of the U.S. population.22 We identified all live birth and stillbirth delivery hospitalizations to people 12–55 years of age using International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification and Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-CM) codes (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E733). We excluded delivery encounters with nonexistent ICD codes, that were flagged by the NIS, or that contained codes for miscarriage or abortion.

We defined deliveries among people with SCD as those with at least one SCD ICD-9 or ICD-10 code and no sickle cell trait code. This approach has a greater than 90% positive predictive value in identifying people with SCD in administrative datasets.23 We defined deliveries among people without SCD as those with no SCD ICD-9 or ICD-10 code. People with sickle cell trait were included in the non-SCD group.

The primary outcome was immediate postpartum LARC insertion, defined as placement of contraceptive implant or intrauterine device (IUD) during the delivery admission. Consistent with other contraception studies using the NIS, we excluded deliveries coded with more than one contraception procedure (ie, LARC and permanent contraception) from the primary outcome (n=235 unweighted deliveries).24,25

We reported the rates of immediate postpartum LARC use as cumulative incidence of LARC per 1,000 deliveries for people with and without SCD. We additionally conducted a difference in trends analysis during our study period. Descriptive analyses estimated the rate of immediate postpartum LARC use for people with and without SCD among all NIS deliveries, separated by the covariates of age, severe maternal morbidity (SMM), delivery mode, insurance, hospital type, median household income, and region. Differences in rates within analytic groups were calculated with design-adjusted Pearson χ2 statistic, corrected for survey design.26 Next, we calculated a univariate logistic regression to calculate the unadjusted odds (OR) and 95% CIs of immediate postpartum LARC use between deliveries in people with and without SCD. We then used multivariate logistic regression models to conduct this analysis using covariates that were sequentially included through forward stepwise selection based on previously published relevant factors and clinical expertise and application.1,6,17 The final multivariate model adjusted for maternal age, mode of delivery, presence of SMM, insurance type, median household income by ZIP code, region, hospital type, and calendar year. We defined SMM as deliveries that included one or more of a set of 21 conditions defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; SMM conditions of blood transfusion, vaso-occlusive crises, and hysterectomy were excluded because of association with SCD, as well as poor predictability of blood transfusion for SMM.17,27–29 Because characteristics of the delivery may affect immediate postpartum LARC use, we assessed potential effect modification by SMM and mode of delivery using interaction terms in the final multivariate model and evaluated their significance using design-adjusted Wald tests.

Sickle cell disease affects mostly Black Americans. Because of the well-recognized disparities in maternity outcomes among Black women in the United States, we performed a subanalysis using the methods above limited to deliveries among Black individuals to delineate trends by SCD status within this population.30 In the NIS, the race variable is reported from source hospital discharge records and is collected according to individual hospital protocols. We used race as a proxy for the influence of structural racism and other social factors on health equity and outcomes.31 We therefore conducted analyses with two population sets: all deliveries within the NIS sample (primary analysis as described previously) and deliveries coded with Black race only.

To generate nationally representative inferences, we applied hospitalization discharge sampling weights provided by the NIS to all descriptive and regression analyses. These weights account for the stratified sampling design of the survey, consistent with NIS guidance (Fig. 1).32 We conducted a complete case analysis given low missingness (less than 6%) among covariates with missing information (race, insurance type, and median household income, Appendix 2, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E733). All analyses were design based to account for complex survey design. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided α<0.05. We performed analyses in STATA Be 17.

RESULTS

Between 2012 and 2019, there were a total of 29,822,518 deliveries: 18,860 among people with SCD and 29,803,658 among people without SCD.1,17 Luo et al17 and Early et al1 have previously reported unadjusted crude rates of delivery volume and demographic characteristics of interest from the NIS sample by SCD status, and we highlight relevant findings here that informed our subsequent analyses. Deliveries among people with SCD had higher overall acuity: SMM excluding SCD-related events occurred at a sevenfold higher rate in deliveries among people with SCD compared with people without SCD deliveries (adjusted OR [AOR] 7.22, 95% CI, 6.25–8.34). In addition, cesarean delivery was higher among people with SCD compared with people without SCD (45.4% vs 32.5%). The delivery rates among people with SCD were higher than those among people without SCD when deliveries occurred at an urban teaching hospital (80.6% vs 63.6%), with public insurance coverage (66.0% vs 43.7%), and or in the South (54.4% vs 38.8%).1,17

Figure 2 shows the rates of immediate postpartum LARC use stratified by SCD status and race over the study period. Immediate postpartum LARC use increased over time among all groups, but the rate of increase was steeper for deliveries among people with SCD compared with people without SCD, including when stratified by race (differences in trends, P<.0001). The overall rate of immediate postpartum LARC use in the study period was more than threefold higher among people with SCD compared with people without SCD (16.4 per 1,000 deliveries vs 4.7 per 1,000 deliveries, P<.001). Among Black individuals with and without SCD, the rate of immediate postpartum LARC uptake was 1.8-fold higher among people with SCD (17.1 per 1,000 deliveries) compared with people without SCD (9.8 per 1,000 deliveries, P<.0001).

Significant factors in differential rates of immediate postpartum LARC uptake across all analytic groups included median household income, hospital location and teaching status, SMM, and patient age. Specifically, for people with SCD, deliveries with SMM had a threefold higher rate of immediate postpartum LARC uptake compared with deliveries without SMM (49.3 vs 14.6 per 1,000 deliveries, respectively, P<.05), and this effect persisted among deliveries among Black individuals with SCD (40.5 vs 15.7 per 1,000 deliveries, respectively, P<.05). These factors were included as relevant covariates for our regression model.

DISCUSSION

Using the NIS dataset, we identify that people with SCD receive immediate postpartum LARC at higher rates than people without SCD, including when the analysis was limited to only those of Black race. This association persisted after adjustment for clinical acuity indicators as well as demographic and hospital factors. These findings suggest that the immediate postpartum period is a time of high contraception use by this medically complex population.

In general, patients who request IUD placement or sterilization in immediate postpartum period are more likely to receive their desired method than at an interval time point.8 Our findings complement previous work identifying high rates of immediate postpartum sterilization among people with SCD and contrast with previous studies that report low LARC uptake among people with SCD compared with those without SCD.11,12,15,16 People with SCD have higher acuity deliveries, and our findings support that SMM and SCD are associated with higher immediate postpartum LARC use compared with their healthy counterparts.4 Contraception choice is a dynamic process that encompasses many values and circumstances that shape decision making, including recent postpartum status. Although our study cannot directly address the motivations for immediate postpartum LARC uptake, we hypothesize that contributing factors may include increased access to care around pregnancy, contraception counseling, increased SMM, patient preference, or practitioner influence or coercion.

Our findings corroborate previous research showing that hospital demographics and household income are associated with immediate postpartum LARC uptake.5,33 In this study, regardless of SCD status or stratification by race, people delivering in urban teaching hospitals and those with the lowest household incomes consistently had higher rates of immediate postpartum LARC use than those delivering in other contexts or with higher household incomes. However, even after adjustment for these factors, people with SCD still experienced higher immediate postpartum LARC use. This population is more susceptible to reproductive health disparities because of reliance on public insurance, systemic racism, regional accessibility of care, and elevated maternal mortality.1,4 Clinicians should ensure adequate and noncoercive access to immediate postpartum LARC as a safe and effective contraceptive method.10

Immediate postpartum LARC provision is an evolving practice, particularly during our study period. In 2012, only one state had Medicaid coverage for immediate postpartum LARC insertion outside of the global delivery fee (commonly referred to as “unbundling” the payment) compared with 43 states and Washington, DC, in 2020. During this time, clinician education and training as well as patient awareness and acceptability of immediate postpartum LARC also increased.8,34,35 As immediate postpartum LARC safety and acceptability by patients and clinicians increase, immediate postpartum LARC rates may continue to rise nationally, and disparities in immediate postpartum LARC use may change. Furthermore, limited data are published on disease-specific immediate postpartum LARC uptake among medically complex patients with high pregnancy morbidity. It would be prudent to replicate this work using national data for other conditions to evaluate whether people with SCD have differential use of this postpartum contraceptive strategy. Given our findings that this medically complex patient population has high use of immediate postpartum LARC, public health efforts should support equitable access to these methods in the postpartum period.

A strength of our study includes the use of a nationally representative dataset to capture trends in immediate postpartum LARC use, improving the generalizability of our results. Our study also adds to the small literature of contraception patterns among people with SCD and specifically addresses a gap in evidence in postpartum contraception use.17

This study also has limitations. We could not adjust for all potential confounders, including education level, engagement in prenatal care, obesity, emergency delivery, or hospital religious affiliation. Many hospitals do not offer immediate postpartum LARC because of billing limitations, stocking issues, or gaps in clinician training.8,36 Coding limitations may reflect lack of availability of these services as opposed to nonuse of immediate postpartum LARC. Our study period also intersects with the time period of unbundling payments for immediate postpartum LARC provision, a policy change that may have differentially affected hospital systems at a regional level or had a stronger consequence based on insurance makeup of the comparative patient populations. Our study period additionally included immediate postpartum LARC rates from 2012 to 2019, which represents an older dataset that may not reflect current practices. This analysis cannot address how postpartum contraception decision making may vary across the life span or in the context of complex SCD treatment decisions.4

In the recent NIS, immediate postpartum LARC uptake among people with SCD is higher than among people without SCD, including after adjustment for clinical and structural factors that contribute to differential LARC use. Plausibly, patient preference or values, safety considerations, health practitioner counseling, accessibility and convenience, insurance coverage, or other aspects of high-risk pregnancy care for people with SCD may factor into contraceptive decision making during this period. Given the observed increased uptake of highly effective immediate postpartum contraception options, including LARC, among people with SCD, this study invites the opportunity for future research and implementation work to evaluate whether this contraceptive strategy represents noncoercive, high-quality contraceptive care.
