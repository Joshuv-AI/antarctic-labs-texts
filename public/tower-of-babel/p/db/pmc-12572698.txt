The Hidden Influence: Impacts of Residual Dimethylformamide in NDSB‐211 on icIEF Separation for Monoclonal Antibodies
Authors: Delaney Doran, Matthew Myers, Katarina Moravcevic, Steven LaBrenz, Pinger Wang, Xiaoying Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMonoclonal antibodies (mAbs) present analytical challenges due to their inherent heterogeneity and susceptibility to post‐translational modifications (PTMs) during production and storage. Monitoring of charge heterogeneity profiles by imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (icIEF) has been aided by the use of non‐detergent sulfobetaines (NDSBs), particularly NDSB‐211, to enhance protein solubility and stability. When used in a quality control laboratory setting, NDSB‐211 has shown performance variability over time due to residual manufacturing impurities that impact the capillary isoelectric focusing separation. Dimethylformamide (DMF) was detected using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) in several lots of NDSB‐211 that simultaneously produced poor resolution of basic variants of a mAb on icIEF. A parallel spiking study using DMF at 1%–10% (v/v) on 1H NMR and icIEF illustrates the interference in charge heterogeneity separation when a large amount of DMF is present in the NDSB‐211 reagent. As DMF is not found on the NDSB‐211 certificate of analysis from any vendor, more traditional additives for icIEF are suggested to limit the potential interference for analytical methods.Keywords: charge heterogeneity | imaged capillary isoelectric focusing | monoclonal antibody | non‐detergent sulfobetaine (NDSB)‐211

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) as protein therapeutics present complex analytical challenges because of the inherent heterogeneity of protein expression. These challenges are further compounded by the multitude of post‐translational modifications that can occur over the course of manufacturing and storage, some resulting in charge variants. The total net charge on the protein depends on a variety of modifications, such as C‐terminal lysine clipping [1, 2], glycation [3, 4], and deamidation [5, 6], some even impact the antigen binding affinity and can lead to loss of efficacy. Variation of local charge distribution through processes, like methionine oxidation [7, 8] and isomerization [9, 10], can also alter biologically relevant structural features and cause a loss of overall bioactivity. Additionally, chemical and physical degradation from oxidation, deamidation, and aggregation during storage can impact overall product quality and shelf life [11]. Characterization and monitoring of mAb charge variants from development through production can assist in maintaining the integrity, efficacy, and safety of the biotherapeutic [12, 13]. Additionally, charge variants represent critical quality attributes (CQAs) that must be assessed throughout development and commercial product lifecycle to meet the expectations of health authorities.

When it comes to analytical methods to monitor charge variants, both chromatographic and electrophoretic techniques have their advantages. Ion‐exchange chromatography (IEX) provides analysts with more control over the overall separation; however, long separation times, potential interference from buffer components, and low response of minor peaks are all still present issues for mAb analysis [14]. In recent years, applications of capillary electrophoresis have gained popularity as a technique for monitoring charge variants because of inherent high‐throughput capability, high reproducibility, and reagent‐sparing quality [15]. Specifically, imaged capillary isoelectric focusing (icIEF) results in high resolution, rapid sample analysis time, low limits of detection, and the ability to easily measure by whole image UV detection at 280 nm [16, 17, 18]. Additionally, because icIEF profiles are generated by migration and focusing of isoforms through a self‐assembled pH gradient and are dependent on the total intrinsic charges of the protein, the measurement does not rely on the assumed stability of protein conformation over the duration of the assay and need not be concerned with potential impact from column performance or interactions as in IEX [19]. Although there is an understanding of the inherent absorption tendency of mAbs to the capillary wall in CE‐based separations, additives, like methyl cellulose (MC), are used and capillary coatings have been developed to reduce the generation of electroosmotic flow along the capillary [20, 21]. Although permanent coatings like fluorocarbon have become standard in industrial icIEF capillaries to aid long‐term reproducibility, the coating can deteriorate over multiple injections and lead to more interactions between proteins and the uncoated capillary surface; this often leads to a deterioration in resolution of icIEF profiles [22].

Another drawback of isoelectric focusing is the issue of protein precipitation. Proteins at their pI already have poor solubility as a result of having little to no charge; further concentrating a protein at its pI in a narrow range in a small capillary at a low ionic strength only exacerbates the chance of precipitation and aggregation [23]. Aggregation of this kind can cause poor peak shapes and irreproducible profiles because of artificial spikes, fluctuating currents, and variable migration times as the counterions are stripped from the protein [15, 24]. The precipitation can be mitigated by use of traditional additives, such as zwitterionic or neutral surfactants (Triton‐X, Tween, or (3‐((3‐cholamidopropyl)dimethylammonio)‐1‐propanesulfonate) [CHAPS]), organic modifiers like ethylene glycol, polyols like sucrose, or chaotropic agents, such as urea [25]. One such class of additives, non‐detergent sulfobetaines (NDSBs), was first synthesized in 1994 as an additive for protein crystallization [26]. Several molecules of this family have since been used for other applications, such as protein stabilization [27] and in vitro protein renaturation [28, 29]. This zwitterionic small molecule was quickly seen to be advantageous to icIEF, as it could be used over a wide range of pH values, would not raise the conductivity of the overall solution, and does not form micelles and therefore diffuses easily in the capillary, while also maintaining and stabilizing the native condition of the protein [30, 31].

Although a few NDSB molecules have been synthesized, NDSB‐195, NDSB‐201, and NDSB‐211 do not absorb at 280 nm, which makes them prime candidates for icIEF applications (Figure S1). The mechanism for enhanced solubilization has been speculated to hinge on the charge screening properties of the dipole created by the opposite charges on either end of the molecule, as well as the presence of a large hydrophobic motif that can abolish nonspecific protein–protein interactions [29, 30]. This potential dependence on the hydrophobic portion of the molecule may explain why NDSB compounds impact protein renaturation and stability with varying degrees of success.

Herein, we focus on NDSB‐211, which has been previously qualified as an in‐house icIEF additive. Although NDSB‐211 has been shown to work well at enhancing the resolution of especially difficult to separate protein charge variants in the highly concentrated icIEF microenvironment, the additive showed performance issues over time. There exists a dearth in the literature regarding the long‐term performance of commercially available NDSB‐211 for icIEF applications, and therefore, we report variability in peak resolution and baseline irregularities during routine release and stability testing. Structural studies utilizing 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) were done to show a correlation between the purity and stability of NDSB‐211 and the complementary icIEF performance to illuminate the intricate complexities of using this molecule as an icIEF assay additive.

Materials and Methods

Materials

All mAbs were provided by Janssen Research & Development, LLC (Malvern, PA, USA). Carrier ampholytes (Pharmalyte 3–10, Pharmalyte 5–8, Pharmalyte 8–10.5) were purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA). MC solutions (1% and 0.5%), 0.8 M H3PO4 in 0.1% MC anolyte solution, 0.1 M sodium hydroxide in 0.1% MC catholyte solution, iCE3 cartridges, and pI markers (6.14, 7.65, 9.22, 9.46) were purchased from Bio‐Techne (Minneapolis, MN, USA). iCE3 cartridges are fluorocarbon‐coated, 50 mm in length, with 100 µm (I.D.) and were purchased from Bio‐Techne (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Carboxypeptidase B (CPB) was obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All solutions were prepared with deionized water filtered through a Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA) Milli‐Q Advantage A10 system (R = 18.2 MΩ cm at 25°C, TOC ≤ 5 ppb).

NDSB‐211 was sourced from multiple vendors to aid in this study: Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) (N0665), Soltec Bio Science (Beverly, MA, USA) (SB211), Hampton Research (Aliso Viejo, CA, USA) (HR2‐793), and Anatrace (Maumee, OH, USA) (ND2115GM). In each case, NDSB‐211 was prepared at a concentration of 2 M in Milli‐Q water before adding to the stock solution. Dimethylformamide (DMF) was obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Methods

icIEF Assays

Protein mAb samples (starting concentrations: mAb A, 25.2 mg/mL; mAb B used undiluted at 10.2 mg/mL; mAb C, 11.4 mg/mL) were diluted to 10 mg/mL in water and treated with CPB to minimize the impact of C‐terminal lysine manufacturing variability (see Supporting Information Note 1) on the icIEF profile. Samples were run with an anolyte solution of 80 mM phosphoric acid in 0.1% m/v MC and a catholyte solution of 100 mM sodium hydroxide in 0.1% m/v MC. The working mAb concentration in all samples analyzed was ∼0.23 mg/mL, and each icIEF matrix contained 40% NDSB‐211, 0.35% MC, 1% each of low and high pI marker, and 4% ampholyte or ampholyte mixture of Pharmalyte 3–10, Pharmalyte 8–10.5, or Pharmalyte 5–8. mAb A used an ampholyte ratio of 1:3 Pharmalyte 5–8:Pharmalyte 3–10, and mAb B and mAb C used an ampholyte ratio of 1:3 Pharmalyte 3–10:Pharmalyte 8–10.5. All samples were used immediately after preparation and maintained at 4°C during analysis.

icIEF was performed on a Bio‐Techne iCE3 whole‐capillary imaging system using fluorocarbon‐coated capillary cartridges. Cartridges were installed and conditioned per instrumentation guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The cartridge UV light intensity check was performed before each run. Cartridges were rinsed between each sample injection for 90 s, and a final rinse was performed at the end of the batch, followed by the short‐term shutdown procedure. Cartridges not used for more than 24 h were washed with the long‐term shutdown procedure, and electrolyte tanks were washed and refilled with water. Fresh 0.5% MC wash solution was added to the instrument before each run. The instrument was run at focusing conditions of 1500 V for 1 min followed by 3000 V for 9 min with the autosampler maintained at 4°C for the duration of the assay. iCE3 CFR software was used for data acquisition at 280 nm and calibration, and electropherograms were imported into Empower software for further data analysis.

1H NMR Experiments

1H NMR data were collected on a Magritek (Malvern, PA, USA) SpinSolve 80 instrument using 7 in., 5 mm NMR tubes. Samples for 1H spectroscopy were dissolved in D2O at 0.2 M concentration without pH adjustment. Spectra were collected in 1H decoupled mode using the preset method in the SpinSolve software, PROTON CDEC. Spectra delay was set to 3 s, and 64 scans were collected for each sample at a spectral resolution of 0.08 Hz.

All resulting data were analyzed using Mestrelab Mnova software. For 1H data, the spectra chemical shift was normalized to the HDO signal in the data using a value of 4.80 ppm. Integration areas were normalized to the resolved NDSB‐211 signal at 4.1 ppm and adjusted to two protons.

Results and Discussion

Improved icIEF Profiles by NDSB‐211 Addition

During method development, the addition of solubilizing additives is occasionally necessary to achieve well‐resolved protein peaks, and NDSB‐211 was ultimately incorporated into the final methods for three mAbs. As shown in the red boxes in Figure 1, NDSB‐211 helped to better resolve the broad peak seen at pI 7.50, resolved the two basic species at ∼pI 7.8, and more clearly resolved the third basic peak at ∼pI 7.9. Only one protein is presented for this example (mAb A), but similar improved profile features were also seen in the other two proteins of interest to this study (Figure S2). A small shift towards more acidic pI values is seen for all mAb A variants (∼0.2 pH units) when NDSB‐211 is added. A shift to more basic pI values is seen for mAb C with the addition of NDSB‐211, but interestingly, no pI shift is seen for mAb B (Figure S2).

Recent discussion of NDSB‐211 negatively impacting the linearity of the pH gradient has been noted [32], but in the present case, the addition of NDSB‐211 to the sample solution improved the linearity of the gradient with this specific Pharmalyte mixture. A pI marker spiking study of nine markers using the Pharmalyte mixture for mAb A (Figure 1) showed that NDSB‐211 improved the slope of the gradient from an r 2 of 0.94 to r 2 of 0.97 (Figure S3). Furthermore, the pH gradient was shown to be very linear in the sample solutions for mAb B and mAb C with the inclusion of NDSB‐211 (Figure S4).

Increased Noise Spikes

In an imaged capillary system, noise or sharp peaks can appear due to capillary cartridge manufacturing errors, dust within the detector cell, and/or poorly resolving species within the analyzed sample. Noise spikes that encroach upon the protein profile make it difficult to discern if the peaks are from protein charge variants or from interference. Maintaining clean baselines by limiting the amount of noise ensures that all peaks seen in charge profiles belong to the protein of interest and enhances the reliability of detection of additional charge species. During the initial phase of testing with the method presented in Figure 1, it was observed that NDSB‐211 was introducing random and unpredictable noise spikes in certain regions of the electropherograms (Figure S5).

For mAb A and mAb B the noise in all sample replicates appears in the same pI region (pI 8.0–8.8) as sharp peaks, but the noise profiles vary between individual replicates. The location and height of the interference peaks are not consistent, and these experiments have produced similar results when repeated on multiple instruments and utilizing different icIEF capillary cartridges, eliminating the possibility of physical interference (like dust on capillary window) or electrical interference from an instrumental malfunction. For both mAbs, passing the NDSB‐211 solution through a 0.22 µm PES syringe filter eliminates the extraneous background noise in the profile (black trace in Figure S5A,B).

Once filtered, the profiles maintain the same intensity and separation resolution, indicating that the noise‐causing components filtered out are not essential to the mechanism of protein focusing. Although the noise does not directly interfere with the profile resolution of these two molecules, the consistent presence of the interference indicates additional method variability that is protein agnostic.

Ampholyte Variability

Developing a robust icIEF method requires creation of the most appropriate pH gradient for the specific mAb charge variant separation. The combination of ampholytes must enrich the relevant regions to effectively resolve the populations of mAb charge species. Carrier ampholytes are usually a diverse mixture of polyamines, carboxylic acid derivatives, dipeptides, and other species with distinct and variable pI values, which makes them very complicated to manufacture [33]. The industry standard is to use a commercially available proprietary product, such as Ampholine, AESlytes, Servalyt, or Pharmalyte carrier ampholytes [34, 35, 36].

Representative electropherograms of mAb A from method development are shown in Figure 1. Commercial ampholytes are used routinely without issue for other molecules; however, in this application, when the method also contained NDSB‐211, the lot‐to‐lot variability of the ampholyte mixture altered the resolving power. Illustrative of this effect, Figure 3 shows mAb A samples tested with the same NDSB‐211 Lot 1 and different lots of Pharmalyte 3–10. The ampholyte mixture for mAb A has a final concentration of 3% (v/v) of Pharmalyte 3–10 and 1% (v/v) of Pharmalyte 5–8.

The difference in Basic Peak 2 resolution between Figures 1 and 3 is clear: The two distinct peaks in Figure 1 now appear as a peak and a shoulder in Figure 3A. A slight pI shift is observed for all mAb variants between the three lots (∼0.04), normalized by flanking pI markers, potentially indicating variable strength or amount of ampholytic species in the mAb resolving region [33]. Although the optimized ampholyte solution for this assay contains a ratio of 3–10 ampholytes to 5–8 ampholytes (3:1), variability in the 5–8 ampholyte lot had less of an effect on the icIEF profile (Figure 3B).

The 3–10 ampholyte lot has a larger effect on resolution of the basic species, most likely due to greater prominence in the mixture. However, neither 5–8 ampholyte lot resulted in a profile comparable to the reference in Figure 1. The main difference is that Figure 1 utilized NDSB‐211 Lot 2, whereas Figure 3 utilized NDSB‐211 Lot 1.

Certain lots of NDSB‐211 have resiliency against the ampholyte lot resolution effects. Figure 4 shows a comparison of three 3–10 Pharmalyte lots with different NDSB‐211 lots. The experiment performed with no NDSB‐211 looks very similar to NDSB‐211 Lot 1, whereas NDSB‐211 Lot 2 shows good separation of the basic peaks. The observed pI shift between no NDSB‐11 (Figure 4C) and NDSB‐211 (Figure 4A,B) is expected as the additive slightly impacts the linearity of the pH gradient (Figure 1).

This raises important questions about the quality of NDSB‐211 Lot 1 because the electropherograms look similar to the electropherograms without the additive altogether. NDSB‐211 Lot 1 is significantly worse at solubilizing and aiding in focusing than NDSB‐211 Lot 2, despite both lots originating from the same vendor. It appears that when NDSB‐211 is either not effective or poor at solubilizing, the ampholyte lot has an effect on the resulting profile, as the basic peaks are better resolved with Pharmalyte Lot 2 in NDSB‐211 Lot 1. When the NDSB‐211 lot is effective at solubilizing and aiding focusing (NDSB‐211 Lot 2), the ampholyte lot has very little impact on the resulting profile; all Pharmalyte lots used showed good resolution of the basic peaks.

Although variability of some properties of these carrier ampholytes, such as the slope of the pH gradient and the buffering capacity, must fall within manufacturing specifications, there are clearly other interactions that affect the focusing of certain mAbs on icIEF. This could be related to differences in composition of carrier ampholytes and depend on the relative distribution of ampholytic species across the range [33]. In this case, the lot of 3–10 ampholyte used for testing had an impact on the resolution, but the lot of NDSB‐211 used had a much greater ability to impact the quality of the resolution.

To better understand the composition of the NDSB‐211 material, a structural analysis was performed to determine if poor purity could be causing the observed charge variant profile variability.

Investigating Structural Differences

NDSB‐211 Analysis by NMR

NDSB‐211 Lot 2 most closely resembles the theoretical spectrum (Figure S7). When the other profiles are compared to NDSB‐211 Lot 2 as an experimental control, two specific regions of interest are observed. In the spectrum of NDSB‐211 Lot 1 (Figure 5A), a small shoulder peak at 2.89 ppm was detected; this peak is also seen to a lesser extent in NDSB‐211 Lot 5 (Figure 5E) profile. Notably, these two profiles also have a small peak at 8.0 ppm, downfield of the range of the peaks expected for NDSB‐211 (Figure 5G). The same peak at 2.89 ppm in NDSB‐211 Lot 6 (Figure 5E) was significantly larger than all other samples and was distinguishable from the peak at 2.95 ppm. NDSB‐211 Lot 6 also showed the novel peak at 8.0 ppm at a greater intensity than in the other samples (Figure 5G). The purity differences observed in this set of NMR data generally trend with the ability of the different lots and vendors to properly resolve mAb A on icIEF (Figure 2). The worst resolving icIEF profile was with NDSB‐211 Lot 6, NDSB‐211 Lot 5 and Lot 1 profiles resolved somewhat poorly, and NDSB‐211 Lot 2 resolved mAb A the best. Therefore, if the purity on NMR correlates to effectiveness of the specific NDSB‐211 as an icIEF additive, then the extra peaks downfield in the NMR spectra may be indicative of the cause leading to the loss of resolution on icIEF for mAb A. However, it should be noted this trend is not reflected for the pI range of mAb C (Figure 2B) as NDSB‐211 Lot 1 produces a well‐resolved profile and NDSB‐211 Lot 2 does not produce a well‐resolved profile.

Identifying an Impurity

Further investigation was carried out to determine if the additional peak seen on 1H NMR was an impurity overlapping in the same region of chemical shift <4.2 ppm. One vendor of the NDSB‐211 reagent had previously communicated that several batches of the reagent had to undergo repurification because of the presence of up to 10% residual DMF. DMF is a solvent used in the manufacturing process for NDSB‐211, but residual levels are not routinely tested before batches are released. The only purity criterion on the certificate of analysis for NDSB‐211 is a 1H NMR that “conforms to structure” with no quantitative values for acceptance criteria [37]. A sample of pure DMF in D2O shows a 1H NMR spectrum with three key peaks: two methyl groups at 2.88 and 3.03 ppm and a single amide CH signal at 7.95 ppm (Figure S8). These peaks are comparable to the impurity peaks seen in the NDSB‐211 NMR spectra in Figure 5 and match those documented for DMF [38].

A spiking experiment was conducted to confirm that the extraneous NMR peaks were resulting from residual DMF. When 10 µL (30.0%) and 20 µL (44.2%) of DMF were spiked directly into the NDSB‐211 solution, the peak at 8.0 ppm grew proportionally. The same trend was seen in the 3.0 ppm region as the split peaks in the 0% panel were completely enveloped by the methyl DMF peaks in the 30.0% level and the 44.2% level. Figure 6 shows that the DMF spike did not interfere with any other area of the spectrum. This evidence strongly supports the hypothesis that the manufacturing process of NDSB‐211 leaves considerable residual DMF in the final product. Reanalysis of Figure 5 with this understanding raises concerns about the reliability of NDSB‐211 as an additive for icIEF. Residual DMF in NDSB‐211 samples varied among vendors (Figure 5A,D,E,F), indicating inconsistency in the manufacturing process; notably, NDSB‐211 Lot 6 had the greatest amount of DMF detected at 6.5%. Additionally, even within a single vendor (Figure 5A–C), the NDSB‐211 lots exhibited variability in levels of residual DMF.

A spiking experiment with DMF in NDSB‐211 was also performed with mAb A on icIEF to examine if DMF could be directly attributed to the resolution issues observed. icIEF profiles of mAb A when the sample solution was spiked with increasing levels of DMF are shown in Figure 7. The spike levels were designed as percentages of the NDSB‐211 concentration in the final sample, whereas all other components were held consistent, and the final total volume was maintained. The DMF concentrations in the levels are 0.6 mM (1%), 1.9 mM (3%), 3.1 mM (5%), 4.3 mM (7%), and 6.2 mM (10%).

It is evident that as the DMF spike level increased, the profile of mAb A lost resolution of Basic Peak 2. Where the two basic peaks were separate and identifiable in the control and at the 1% level, by the 5% level, the peaks have lost resolution and Basic Peak 2 appeared as a shoulder on Basic Peak 1. Additionally, at greater DMF spike levels, variable noise peaks in the profile increased in count and intensity (Figure S9). Although this increase in noise occurs in a different region of the electropherogram than previously discussed (Section 3.2), it may correlate to the earlier observation of random noise spikes seen in the poorly resolving (confirmed to contain DMF) NDSB‐211 lots (Figure S5).

The reported results for this experiment did not vary greatly across the spike levels. The ranges of percent area results observed for sum of acidic peaks (19.3%–21.1%), main peak (64.4%–67.2%), and sum of basic peaks (13.0%–14.6%) are well within the validated method variability. However, the lack of profile robustness that results when the NDSB‐211 additive has residual DMF still presents a method development issue. The loss of profile resolution can lead to invalid assays as analysts attempt to integrate the profile to match method examples that contain clearly defined basic peaks.

Together, the data shown demonstrate that DMF is present in certain lots of NDSB‐211 as a residual impurity and contributes to the lack of basic peak resolution for the icIEF charge variant profile of mAb A.

Concluding Remarks

On the basis of the data provided of the complex and interlinked method performance issues that arise from using NDSB‐211 for cIEF applications, the recommendation is to choose an alternative solubilization additive if one is needed. There is variability in effectivity of NDSB‐211 as an additive across vendors and even discrepancies between lots of the same vendor. Charge variant profiles with resolution affected by the residual DMF will be difficult to trend over long‐term stability, as new lots of NDSB‐211 are manufactured and distributed. Additionally, even profiles with resolution unaffected by residual DMF may still benefit from manually filtering NDSB‐211 stock before using it in the parent solution for sample preparation to minimize random noise spikes. For promising early phase mAb programs, the potential variability introduced by NDSB‐211 is too great to ensure a smooth icIEF method transfer to manufacturing facilities and reliable long‐term method performance unless this variability can be addressed, controlled, or mitigated.
