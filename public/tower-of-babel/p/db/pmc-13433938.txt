Real-time qPCR evaluation of amino acids buffered sodium hypochlorite gel as an adjunct to non-surgical periodontal treatment: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Ewa Dolińska, Aleksandra Starosz, Kamil Grubczak, Katarzyna Golińska, Małgorzata Pietruska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of non-surgical periodontal therapy (SRP) and SRP with an amino acid-buffered hypochlorite solution (NaOCl/AA + SRP) on subgingival plaque bacteria. Quantitative changes in Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Campylobacter rectus and Pan Bacteria 3 assay were determined by real-time qPCR from GCF obtained from deep periodontal pocket in twelve weeks observation period. Both treatment strategies led to significant clinical improvements over time. During the first week, we observed a significant twofold decrease in the levels of P. gingivalis and C. rectus following SRP treatment. Similarly, at 12 weeks, the between-group difference in log₂ P. gingivalis DNA levels (SRP vs NaOCl/AA + SRP) was −0.79 (95% CI −1.35 to −0.23; p = 0.006), indicating a significantly greater reduction following SRP alone. The adjunctive application of NaOCl/AA gel to SRP also resulted in reduced levels of P. gingivalis, C. rectus, or F. nucleatum; however, not as pronounced. Additionally neither treatment strategy significantly affected the total amount of bacterial DNA. In patients with low PD, a significant reduction in P. gingivalis expression was observed during the first week of therapy across both study groups. Conversely, in patients with high PD, a significant reduction in bacterial DNA expression was evident only in the SRP group. In conclusion, a single 30 s NaOCl/AA gel application did not demonstrate additional benefit over SRP alone under the applied protocol.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54584-z.Keywords: Periodontitis, Periopathogenes, Non-surgical periodontal therapy, Sodium hypochlorite, Amino acids buffered sodium hypochlorite gelSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

The first symptom of periodontal disease is gingival inflammation caused by plaque bacteria1. Gingivitis is limited to the soft tissues located above the alveolar ridge and, if untreated, leads to loss of connective tissue attachment and alveolar bone, thus progressing to periodontitis. This disease can lead to loss of dentition and is therefore a major socio-economic problem2,3.

The unequivocal role of bacteria in the pathogenesis of periodontal diseases was confirmed in the 1960s through the studies of Löe and Theilade4,5. However, the prevalence of periopathogenic microorganisms in the community is much higher than the incidence of the diseases they cause. This indicates that other important factors related to the host organism also influence the development of periodontitis. It is mainly the individual immune response to the bacterial agent that is responsible for tissue destruction and disease progression6. Host susceptibility is heritable but can be modulated by numerous environmental, acquired and behavioral factors7.

The oral cavity is a complex microbial environment populated by more than 700 microbial species. Each human hosts approximately 100–200 species, resulting in a high degree of individual variability. Groundbreaking work of Sockransky and colleagues illustratively divided the plaque microorganisms into five colour-coded bacterial complexes. The most pathogenic of which is the red complex consisting of Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), Tanerella forsythia (T. forsythia) and Treponema denticola (T. denticola). The complex is detected in deep periodontal pockets and is associated with active inflammation. Its occurrence is preceded by colonisation of the periodontal pocket with bacteria of the orange complex (Fusobacterium nucleatum, Campycobacter rectus, C. gracilis, C. showae, Prevotella intermedia, P. nigrescens, Parvimonas micra, Peptostreptococcus nodatum, Streptococcus constellatus)8. Due to its numerous virulence factors, P. gingivalis is considered a key pathogen with the ability to transform symbiotic flora into dysbiotic one. These factors include proteolytic enzymes (gingipains), a capsule, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), fimbriae, nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDK), ceramide and outer membrane vesicles9. P. gingivalis is classified BPB- black- pigmented Bacterioides and is the most virulent bacterium of this group. The BPB complex is responsible for the development of gingivitis, periodontitis, dental abscesses and endodontic infections10. F. nucleatum is another important Gram-negative, non-motile, anaerobic bacterium with several virulence factors, such as adhesins, endotoxins and serine proteases, which trigger periodontal disease11. Outer membrane proteins and sensory molecules enable F. nucleatum to co-aggregate with both early and late colonizers during plaque formation, thereby promoting the progression of periodontal disease11,12. This aggregation acts as a critical scaffold for microbial growth and development, facilitating the formation and maturation of the dental biofilm13,14. F. nucleatum is an important component of the subgingival plaque and may serve as a metabolic anchor for other members of the community15. If it is absent, the number of ‘late plaque colonisers’ is low.15. Campylobacter rectus, a Gram negative, microaerophilic, and motile bacterium also plays a role in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. Surface components, surface layer (S-layer), and cytotoxin, have been proposed as possible virulence factors of the microorganism16.

It is currently believed that it is not individual species but changes in the microenvironment lead to excessive growth of certain species, which begin to act as opportunistic pathogens. A vicious circle also emerges where inflammation drives dysbiosis and dysbiosis increases inflammation17,18. In 2020, a new theory of the etiology of periodontitis emerged called the IMPEDE (Inflammation-Mediated- Polymicrobial- Exacerbation) model. This model assumes that it is the inflammation that leads to dysbiosis which fuels the transition of oral health to disease19.

The bacteria responsible for the development of periodontitis are part of the supragingival and subgingival biofilm that is additional challenge to the host20. Its three-dimensional exopolymer structure, firmly adhering to the tooth surface, creates physical protection against immune responses and antimicrobial drugs. The plaque bacteria, through the development of community-like interrelationships, can exhibit synergism in action in which multiple determinants of their virulence are expressed21. Given the high resistance of microorganisms in the biofilm the first step in eradicating biofilm-associated bacteria is their physical removal. Plaque, unlike other biofilms, is readily accessible to mechanical procedures such as oral hygiene, scaling and root planing (SRP) or periodontal surgery20. Basic periodontal therapy (SRP) can be supported by the use of antiseptics. Chlorhexidine, iodopovidone or sodium hypochlorite in low concentrations are recommended for professional use.

Sodium hypochlorite is one of the strongest antiseptics with a broad spectrum of action. It inhibits enzymes essential for the metabolism of microorganisms, denatures proteins and inactivates nucleic acids. It exhibits strong oxidising and chlorinating effects22. The minimum concentration of the agent that in vitro inactivates bacteria is 0.01%, and the highest bactericidal activity has been reported at a concentration of 2.25%. At low concentrations, sodium hypochlorite is used as a topical antimicrobial agent to clean wounds and skin ulcers without inhibiting fibroblast function22. Perisolv® (sodium hypochlorite amino acids buffered gel) is a two-component system containing low-concentration sodium hypochlorite (0.95%) and appropriately selected amino acids (glutamic acid, lysine and leucine). When the two components of the preparation are mixed, short-acting chloramines are obtained23. Sodium hypochlorite in low concentrations can be a very good antiseptic for the use in periodontics, but its use is not widespread and its effect on the subgingival microbiome in clinical settings is little understood. The preliminary, in vitro findings suggest that using amino acid buffered sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl/AA) gel could be an effective way to chemically destroy the bacterial biofilm and make its mechanical removal easier24,25. However, at present, the data on the impact of a local application of NaOCl used in conjunction with subgingival mechanical instrumentation on subgingival biofilm is still limited. We hypothesized that application of low concentrated sodium hypochlorite formulation to the periodontal pocket will exhibit direct antimicrobial activity and reduce the burden of subgingival bacteria.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to assess subgingival plaque bacteria after non-surgical periodontal therapy (SRP) and non-surgical periodontal therapy with amino acid buffered hypochlorite solution (NaOCl/AA). Quantitative changes in P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum and C. rectus, as representatives of bacterial species with different functions in the biofilm, were determined as primary outcomes of the study. The analysis of clinical parameters of sampling site was a secondary objective.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a randomized clinical trial. The specimens for microbiological analysis were obtained from 40 (32 woman and 8 men, mean age 48.2) generally healthy, non-smoking periodontal patients (stage II-III periodontitis26) during the study assessing the impact of a low-concentration sodium hypochlorite formulation on the clinical condition of the periodontium registered under ISRCTN 11287170 (retrospective registration date 22/07/2025, because of the Covid-19 pandemic). The study complies with the Helsinki Declaration with further amendments and was approved by the Bioethics Committee Medical University of Bialystok, Poland (APK.002.269.2020).

The participants’ enrollment

Subjects were recruited among patients of Outpatient Clinic of the Department of Periodontal and Oral Mucosa Diseases, Medical University of Bialystok, Poland. All participants gave their written informed consent to participate in the study. All patients were anonymized for this analysis. Due to the inability to calculate the sample size based on microbiological data, a priori sample size calculation was performed, assuming a standard deviation of 1 mm in CAL change and aiming to detect a mean difference of 1 mm with 80% test power on 32 subjects. However, we accounted for possible dropouts and therefore decided to recruit and randomize 40 patients. We included adult patients with periodontitis (stages II and III) who had at least four pockets with PD ≥ 5 mm and had not undergone professional hygiene procedures in the last six months and no systemic antibiotic therapy in the last three months. We excluded patients with general contraindications to any periodontal therapy; patients who were immunosuppressed or immunologically incompetent; smokers; patients with uncontrolled diabetes; pregnant or breastfeeding women; patients who were dependent on alcohol and/or drugs; and patients who did not have the opportunity to participate in the programme for 6 months.

Periodontal therapeutic intervention

Patients were randomly assigned (computer generated list) to two groups (20 patients each): the study group (sodium hypochlorite/amino acid gel + SRP (NaOCl/AA + SRP)) and control group (SRP). Study group patients were treated by SRP (scaling and root planing) preceded by application of Perisolv® (Regedent AG, Zurich, Switzerland) (NaOCl/AA) in the pockets 5 mm or deeper (PD ≥ 5 mm). NaOCl/AA gel was introduced into the pocket with blunt cannula for 30 s. This was followed by a total subgingival scaling with root planning (SRP) using an EMS Piezon ultrasonic scaler (EMS, Electro Medical Systems, Nyon, Switzerland) with a PS (Perio Slim) tip. The control group was treated only by SRP. Periodontal treatment was done by one experienced periodontist (ED). Patients received standardized oral hygiene instruction with the recommendation not to use additional antiseptics.

Sample collection

At the first visit, one deep pocket with a PD > 5 mm was selected for the collection of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) for microbial analysis. The tooth with the pocket was isolated from saline with the cotton swabs. GCF was collected using sterile paper filter, which were placed in the pocket for 30 s. The filter was then placed in an Eppendorf 2.0 ml (Eppendorf DNA LoBind Tubes, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) with buffer (RLT Buffer; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany ). After 15 min, the tubes were placed for 15 s in a vortex MX-S shaker (DLab Scientific Inc. Rowland, CA, USA). The paper filter was removed from the tube to avoid contamination. After collection, the material was prefrozen in −20 °C and then frozen at −80 °C until analysis.

Clinical periodontal parameters measurement

Clinical measurements were done by one experienced dentist (KGo) by means of UNC-15 (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) periodontal probe. The examiner was blinded. The following measurements were taken from the pocket selected at the first visit: pocket depth (PD), gingival recession (GR) and clinical attachment level (CAL). Plaque Score (PS, dichotomously designated as present or absent in six points, expressed as percentage) and bleeding on probing (BOP, dichotomously designated as present or absent in six points, expressed as percentage) were also determined at the tooth with examined site. Clinical measurements and GCF samples were collected at baseline, one week, two weeks, 12 weeks and 24 weeks after treatment. Clinical outcomes were assessed to 24 weeks and microbiological endpoints were assesses to 12 weeks.

Bacterial DNA isolation

QIAamp UCP Pathogen Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for the bacterial DNA isolation from swabs preserved in the lysis buffer consistent with RLT Buffer (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). The procedure was accomplished according to the manufacturing protocol, including 10 min digestions with the Proteinase K at 56 °C degree in the first step. The amount of obtained DNA was measured on NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA) by measuring the absorbance of the width 260 and 280 nm. The purity of DNA was established via A260/A280 ratio evaluation at a range of 1.8–2.2.

RT-qPCR

Real-time qPCR was performed for the evaluation of three chosen bacteria species associated with periodontal disease: P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum and C. rectus. Additionally, for every patient, we also performed the Pan Bacteria 3 assay, which consists of a mix of 7 main phyla (Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Euryarchaeota, Firmicutes, Fusobacteria, Proteobacteria, Spirochaetes, Tenericutes) for ensures the positive signal of Bacterial DNA. We used assays, P. gingivalis (BPID00271AR Microbial DNA qPCR), F. nucleatum (BPID00160AR Microbial DNA qPCR), C. rectus (BPID00088AR Microbial DNA qPCR), and for the Pan Bacteria 3 (BPCL00362AR Microbial DNA qPCR). One single assay includes 5 ng of bacterial DNA, Microbial qPCR Mastermix (ROX), Microbial DNA qPCR assay, and Microbial DNA-Free Water. The total volume of the reaction sample was 25ul. The stages of reaction are following according to the manufacture protocol: 1 cycle of initial PCR activation step (95 °C, 10 min), and 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 15 s) and annealing with extension (60 °C, 120 s,) respectively. The assays were performed using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System device (Applied Biosystems; Foster City, CA, USA) and analyzed using manufacturing software (StepOnePlus software v2.3). Data was presented as a normalized expression log2 (2^-ΔΔCt), and gene expression values (Ct) were used for correlation.

Statistical analysis

Biostatistical analysis of the collected data was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 statistical software (GraphPad Prism Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables included clinical periodontal parameters (e.g., probing depth, clinical attachment level, and bleeding on probing) and bacterial DNA levels expressed as Log2(-ΔCt) values. These variables were analyzed to evaluate differences between treatment groups and at different therapy time points. Since the analyzed continuous variables showed a non-Gaussian distribution, we used two-way ANOVA with Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test. This allowed us to assess statistically significant differences between treatment groups and follow-up time points. Categorical variables represented bacterial load categories (high vs. low bacterial DNA levels), and we analyzed these using the Chi-square (χ2) test to evaluate differences in frequency distribution between the groups. For this analysis, samples were classified into low- and high-bacterial-DNA groups based on cycle threshold (Ct) values, using a predefined cut-off of Ct < 30. Here, Ct ≤ 30 indicated high bacterial load and Ct > 30 indicated low bacterial load. Results were shown graphically as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In the figures, significance was indicated by asterisks or exact p-values: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Correlations between clinical periodontal parameters and bacterial DNA levels were evaluated using the nonparametric Spearman rank correlation test. Correlation strength was expressed as Spearman’s correlation coefficient (R), with statistical significance indicated by asterisks or exact p-values.

Microbiological analysis

In microbiological analysis, firstly, we detected the presence of selected bacteria (P. gingivalis, C. rectus and F. nucleatum) in the gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) throughout the study period in both analyzed groups (NaOCl/AA + SRP and SRP). Interestingly, during the first week, we observed a significant 2-fold decrease in the levels of P. gingivalis and C. rectus following SRP treatment. Similarly, at the 12-week point, a comparable reduction in P. gingivalis levels was noted after SRP treatment. The adjunctive application of sodium hypochlorite/amino-acid gel to SRP also resulted in reduced levels of P. gingivalis, C. rectus, or F. nucleatum; however, these changes were not as pronounced as those observed with SRP treatment alone. To assess the overall bacteria levels in the periodontal pockets, we measured the Pan Bac3 (mix representing the 7 main phyla) expression. We found that neither treatment strategy significantly affects the total amount of bacterial DNA. (Fig. 3A–D).

Discussion

Scaling and root planing (SRP) is an effective nonsurgical treatment of periodontitis. It reduces the inflammation (measured as a reduction in BOP index), shallows periodontal pockets and enables clinical attachment gain27. Gingival recession as an inevitable consequence of SRP is hardly desirable28. Both of the treatment strategies that were compared in our study were clinically effective, as proven by the changes in the aforementioned clinical indices. A very small change in the height of gingival recession were also recorded, despite the marked shallowing of the pockets examined. We also observed a decrease in the number of bleeding on probing (BOP) sites in both groups during the observation period. There was a strong decrease in BOP after 24 weeks in the study group, whereas BOP in the control group increased slightly from two weeks until the end of therapy. In summary we achieved desired clinical outcomes in deep (PD ≥ 5 mm) periodontal pockets in both groups.

SRP also has a beneficial effect on the periodontium in terms of microbiological aspect. Subgingival instrumentation results in a reduction in the number of Gram negative anaerobic bacteria (including periopathogens). This provides an opportunity for microorganisms compatible with healthy periodontium to grow. The main aim of SRP is therefore not only to eliminate specific periopathogens, but to stabilize a healthy subgingival ecosystem by changing the number and composition of the microbial community29. The effectiveness of SRP depends on many factors, including the initial pocket depth, the type and shape of the tooth, the extent of furcation involvement, and the operator’s experience30. The deeper the pocket, the less effective the SRP. That is why, various antimicrobial strategies are regularly used in dental clinical practice to reduce the microbiota and improve the outcome of periodontal therapy. The most commonly used treatments include antibiotics (applied topically or systemically), antiseptics and photodynamic therapy. Pharmaceuticals such as antibiotics and antiseptics have bactericidal or bacteriostatic effects, but they are not able to remove calculus and plaque biofilm. That is why SRP remains the gold standard of treatment. However, it can be supplemented with chemotherapy31. The combination of mechanotherapy and pharmacotherapy (topically applied sodium hypochlorite, povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, or systemic antibiotic therapy) in the treatment of periodontal disease can reduce the number of pathogens to below the levels found in active periodontitis32.

In the case of P. gingivalis, periodontal therapy is able to reduce its amount in a relatively short period of time up to six weeks after treatment33. Our study confirms those observations. In a deep periodontal pocket P. gingivalis was reduced up to 12 weeks after treatment in both groups. The sharpest decrease was noted 1 week after therapy and it was more marked for SRP group. In our study F. nucleatum decreased in both groups a week after therapy to return to baseline values after 12 weeks. Its changes did not reach statistical significance. F. nucleatum is described as a core species which prevalence is similar in periodontal health and disease15. The amount of Campylobacter rectus DNA in the sampled material decreased in both groups after therapy but significant decrease was noted only in control group after a week and 2 weeks. There were also differences between groups in 1 week and 12 weeks observation period. The decreases in the test group were not as pronounced as in the control group. This illustrates that a single subgingival application of NaOCl/AA gel does not increase the potential of SRP in eliminating C. rectus. The literature reports that photodynamic therapy in addition to SRP is more effective in reducing C. rectus in subgingival biofilm samples than SRP alone. It can be explained by the mechanism of action of photodynamic therapy (PDT), which involves the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that have the potential to inactivate pathogenic bacteria, including Gram-negative species34.

SRP non-specifically reduces the number of microorganisms and leads to a decrease in bacteria such as T. forsythia, P. gingivalis, T. denticola and an increase in non-periopathogenic bacteria: Actinomyces spp, Veilonella parvula, Capnocytophaga spp or non-periopathogenic streptococci. Quantitatively, after just 3–7 days, as many microorganisms will be present in the pocket as before SRP. However, qualitative changes of the microflora takes longer, as the ‘early colonisers’ occupy the free habitat20. This is in line with finding of our study where Pan Bac 3 (mix representing the 7 main phyla) expression revealed that neither treatment strategy significantly affects the total amount of bacterial DNA in the samples.

The ability of chloramines to effectively kill pathogens (bacteria, fungi, viruses, protozoa) makes their use as anti-infectives advisable, especially as they have other beneficial properties such as toxin destruction, biofilm degradation, anticoagulant and anti-inflammatory properties23. In earlier in vitro studies, the MIC of the NaOCl/AA gel was the same as that of the CHX solution against Gram-negative bacteria especially species associated with periodontitis. Moreover, its component NaOCl is able to alter biofilm matrices24. In a new in vitro study, chloramines demonstrated antimicrobial effect in all methods tested, with convincing results in the kinetic study and broth dilution and their efficacy may provide an alternative to chlorhexidine solutions commonly used in periodontics35. A key role in periodontal healing is played by achieving a biocompatible tooth surface. This influences migration, adhesion and proliferation of periodontal stem and progenitor cells. In an in vitro study in which the dentin surface (discs prepared from bovine dentin) was evaluated after air-polishing or the application of NaOCl/AA gel, a smooth dentin surface was observed. In addition, 40% more PDL (periodontal ligament cells) were found after application of NaOCl/AA gel and air-polishing than in other cases25. Another in vitro study focused on the NaOCl/AA gel efficacy dependance on time of mixing the two components, pH changes and the possibility of replacing amino-acids with hyaluronic acid. These study confirmed the efficacy of the gel up to 20 min after mixing the ingredients. A decrease in pH resulted in a decrease in efficacy of the formulation. An exchange of the amino acid component by hyaluronic acid did not decrease its properties36. It proves that the formulation should be prepared just before the application and so it was in our study.

Since periodontitis is a biofilm-induced chronic infection, the use of low concentrations of sodium hypochlorite buffered with amino acids in order to increase disinfection is explainable and proven in above mentioned in vitro studies. Unfortunately, despite numerous clinical studies37–40, reports of direct effects of the tested formulation on oral microbiota remain scarce. In the study of Radulescu et al. numbers of five pathogens: Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (A.a.), P. gingivalis (P.g.), P. intermedia (P.i.), T. forsythia (T.f.), and T. denticola (T.d.) were assessed using the semi-quantitative analysis. The intra-group comparison revealed a significant decrease in detection scores between baseline and the 12-month timepoint for P.g., P.i., T.f., and T.d. and no statistically significant differences among the groups (chlorhexidine, sodium hypochlorite gel, placebo gel)41. These intragroup microbiological results compare favourably with those obtained in a similar study in which repeated applications of NaOCl/AA gel were used38 and with our study where no intragroup differences were noted. In contrast, a microbiological evaluation of the efficacy of sodium hypochlorite/amino acid gel with additional pocket sealing with hyaluronic acid was carried out by Ramanuskaite et al., and showed better properties of SRP with tested preparations than of SRP alone. The comparison of the test and control groups revealed statistically significant differences in detection frequency for P. gingivalis, T. denticola and P. intermedia after 6 months, in favour of the test group. This study differs methodologically from ours. The NaOCl/AA gel was introduced for 60 s into the pocket and the procedure was repeated 2–3 times. Additionally hyaluronic acid was applied to the pocket as a sealant42.

Our microbiological results have not confirmed the additional benefit of using low-concentration sodium hypochlorite gel in deep periodontal pockets in the tested in our study protocol. Non-surgical therapy alone (SRP) seems to be sufficient method to achieve the clinical objective and to reduce the number of selected periopathogens in the pockets. Despite its excellent physicochemical properties and proven in vitro efficacy, the NaOCl/AA preparation did not demonstrate strong bactericidal activity in vivo in our study protocol. One possible reason for this is the sensitivity of sodium hypochlorite to organic barriers, which leads to its deactivation. Such barriers include blood, pus, and tissue debris, which are often present in deep periodontal pockets43. Therefore, longer and repeated application of the preparation may be clinically desirable.

Our study has been carried out with the utmost care but it has limitations. One is the lack of patient blinding what carries a risk of bias. Another is that only material from one deep pocket in each patient was examined. It was not technically or financially feasible for us to examine biological material from all deep pockets into which the gel was applied. That is why our microbiological results cannot be extrapolated to changes in bacterial load throughout the entire oral cavity or other shallower pockets. Our evaluation is also based on a single application of the test preparation. It is possible that leaving the gel in the pocket for longer or repeating the procedure more than once would have given better results. This leaves room for further research on the possibility to effectively support SRP in the elimination of pathogens from periodontal pockets. Broader sequencing based studies are also needed to complement our results and to assess restoring microbial homeostasis in periodontium This leaves room for further research on the NaOCl/AA formulation and its possibilities to effectively support SRP in the elimination of pathogens.

We can conclude that under the tested protocol (single 30 s NaOCl/AA application into a deep periodontal pocket prior to SRP), no additional microbiological benefit was demonstrated versus SRP alone. We showed a reduction in the burden of P. gingivalis and C. rectus following SRP treatment one week after SRP therapy. The adjunctive application of NaOCl/AA gel to SRP also resulted in reduced levels of P. gingivalis and C. rectus. These changes were not as pronounced as those observed with SRP treatment group. However, further research is needed before significance of NaOCl/AA gel administration to the deep pockets can be dismissed because of limitations of our study (site-specific sampling, limited taxa, potential underpowering for between-group microbiological differences).
