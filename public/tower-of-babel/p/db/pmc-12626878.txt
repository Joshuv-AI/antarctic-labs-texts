DVFGCDR: a dual-view fusion graph neural network for cancer drug response prediction
Authors: Weihong Huang, Shengwei Qin, Haohao Li, Hancan Zhu, Yuhua Yao, Zhong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prediction of cancer drug responses (CDRs) holds considerable relevance for the guidance of patient-specific clinical treatments. However, the inherent cellular heterogeneity among cancers introduces substantial challenges to such predictions. This paper presents a novel molecular graph convolutional model, named DVFGCDR, developed for predicting CDRs. This innovative model amalgamates both 2D chemical and 3D geometric drug properties to derive a more representative drug embedding. It also includes the capacity to integrate gene expression data from single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing data associated with hundreds of cancer cell lines, thereby enhancing the accuracy of drug response predictions. Experimental comparisons highlight the superior performance of the DVFGCDR model in CDR classification and regression tasks, achieving a Pearson score of 0.959, surpassing current state-of-the-art models. This superiority underscores the model’s powerful predictive capability.

Introduction

Cancer, characterized by abnormal cell differentiation and proliferation, presents an enduring threat to human health due to its significant incidence and lethality. Consequently, accurate prediction of cancer drug response (CDR) has surfaced as a pivotal task for enhancing anticancer drug design and deepening the understanding of cancer biology [1]. For the past three decades, the primary approach to drug discovery has been target-based drug discovery (TDD). While TDD facilitates designing drug screening trials due to clearly defined targets, it falls short when confronted with diseases possessing unspecified targets. Currently, only 11% of patients treated with precision oncology progress to clinical trials, and a mere 5% derive benefit from such precision oncology [2]. Hence, the accurate prediction of oncology patients’ responses to specific anticancer therapeutic agents is critical to improving targeted therapy success rates.

With the advancement of high-throughput technologies, a wealth of data has become available to bolster drug response prediction. For instance, Iorio et al. [3] utilized cancer drug sensitivity genomics (GDSC) to investigate drug responses across a multitude of cancer cell lines, with the half-inhibitory concentration (IC50) emerging as a common metric to gauge drug response. Multi-omics data are integral to these endeavors, with databases like the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [4] offering researchers extensive cancer profiles with multi-omics data including genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and epigenomic data. Among these, transcriptomics is extensively leveraged to characterize cell and tissue phenotypes [5]. However, traditional RNA sequencing approaches fall short in addressing the challenge posed by intra-tumor heterogeneity. The advent of single-cell sequencing technology has enhanced our comprehension of intra- and inter-tumor heterogeneity across different cancer types. Consequently, single-cell transcriptome data have been incorporated into CCLE for improved CDR prediction [6]. With the voluminous information encapsulated by drug molecules and the corresponding omics information from cancer cell lines, efficient knowledge extraction still remains a challenge for researchers.

Numerous computational models have been proposed for CDR prediction, which can broadly be classified into three main categories. The first category comprises network-driven approaches that analyze data derived from drug-drug similarity and cancer cell line similarity. This approach hinges on constructing a similarity-based model and assigning the sensitivity distribution of a known drug to a new drug if structural similarity exists. Wang et al. [7], for instance, employed the similarity-regularized matrix factorization (SRMF) to predict anti-CDRs using drug chemical structures and baseline gene expression levels in cell lines. Zhang et al. [8] developed a dual similarity network for CDR prediction based on gene expression and drug chemical structure in cancer cell lines. Yuan et al. [9] applied the trace norm regularization and efficient alternating direction method of multipliers optimization algorithm to predict CDR using multi-task learning. Zhang et al. [10] proposed a HNMDRP method for CDR prediction, integrating multiple heterogeneous networks, including cell line similarity networks and drug similarity networks.

The second category includes machine learning methods that analyze profiles directly from large-scale drug and cancer cell lines. Dong et al. [11] used SVM and SVM-RFE (Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination) learning models to predict CDR, while Menden et al. [12] amalgamated neural networks and random forest algorithm for CDR prediction.

In recent times, various deep learning models, forming the third category of computational methods, have been proposed for CDR prediction tasks. Chang et al. [13] used the molecular fingerprints of drugs and genomic mutations as cancer cell profiles for CDR prediction using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Liu et al. [14] proposed a method called twin Convolutional Neural Network for drugs in SMILES format (tCNNS) that uses two convolutional networks to learn the SMILES information of drugs and genomic mutation data. Sharifi-Noghabi et al. [15] inputted multi-omics data (genomic mutation, copy number, and gene expression) into an encoder neural network and introduced the triplet loss for training a CDR prediction model. Liu et al. [16] suggested DeepCDR, a deep learning method that integrates the multi-omics features of cancer cells and adopts a uniform graph conventional network (UGCN) to probe the intrinsic chemical structure features. Jiang et al. [17] proposed a DeepTTA method, which employs a Transformer model for drug representation learning and a multilayer neural network for predicting anti-CDRs using transcriptomic data. Notably, most of these models rely on features extracted from the SMILES sequence information and the 2D molecular graph information of the drug. However, the 3D geometric information of the drug, which is of significant importance, is generally overlooked. In recent years, advancements have been made in learning the 3D geometric information of molecules. For instance, Schütt et al. [18] proposed a SchNet model, utilizing continuous-filter convolutional layers to model local correlations of atoms. Unke and Meuwly [19] constructed a neural network, Physnet, that uses the distance between atoms as a key feature for predicting energies, forces, dipole moments, and partial charges.

In this paper, we introduce a novel deep learning model, DVFGCDR, for predicting CDRs. Our model offers several advantages. First, it incorporates the 3D geometric feature of drug molecules and combines it with the 2D chemical feature to construct a dual-view fusion graph convolution network, thus obtaining a more meaningful molecule embedding. Second, it can extract the gene expression from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to generate the embedding of cancer cells, thereby enhancing the precision of CDR prediction. The two embeddings are concatenated and inputted into a fully connected network to obtain the final predicted value. When compared with existing cutting-edge models, our DVFGCDR model achieves superior performance in CDR prediction.

Materials and methods

Our model’s training and testing datasets are derived from the publicly accessible GDSC database (www.cancerrxgene.org Rxgene.org) [3]. This expansive resource offers a wealth of information on molecular markers of drug sensitivity and drug responses in cancer cells, including IC50 values for pairings of cancer cell lines and drugs. Our analysis employs the most recent version of this database, GDSC2, encompassing 135 242 IC50 pairs comprising 809 distinct cell lines and 198 chemical compounds, where IC50 values are log-transformed. The cell lines represented span 31 different cancer types. On average, each cell line interacts with 167 drugs, and each drug is tested against 683 cell lines.

In this part, we present DVFGCDR, a novel deep learning framework designed for the prediction of CDRs. A depiction of the model’s neural network architecture can be found in Fig. 1. This model primarily consists of two modules: drug feature extraction and cell feature extraction. The drug feature extraction module constructs 2D and 3D molecular graph structures of drugs and introduces a new graph convolution module, DVFGConv, to comprehensively understand the structural attributes of drug molecules. In particular, this module initially extracts 2D chemical and 3D geometric features from drug molecule graphs. These features are then fused in a parallel manner and globally pooled, yielding more biologically significant drug embeddings. The cell feature extraction module first applies principal component analysis (PCA) for dimension reduction and regularization on cancer cell gene expressions from scRNA-seq or RNA-seq. It then generates feature embeddings of cell lines via multilayer perceptron (MLP). Finally, the embeddings of the drug and cell are concatenated and passed through the MLP once more to compute the predicted IC50 values.

Data preprocessing

Drug feature processing

For drug data processing, we fetch the relevant information using the PubChem ID registered in the GDSC database [3] via the Python package pubchemy, resulting in the SMILES sequence information for 154 drugs. Subsequently, we utilize the RDKit software [20] to interpret the SMILES notation for each drug and construct 2D and 3D molecular graphs. In the 2D molecular graph, we exhaustively extract the chemical feature information of the drug. Whenever a chemical bond is present between two atoms, we add a corresponding edge. The initial node feature vector of an atom includes five atomic features: atomic symbol, count of adjacent atoms, count of neighboring hydrogens, implicit valence of the atom, and the aromaticity status of the atom [21]. Moreover, we integrate chemical bond type information as initial edge feature data in the model, taking into account whether the bond is single, double, triple, cyclic, aromatic, or conjugated. In the 3D molecular graphs, atom feature information is represented by the 3D coordinates of atoms. The 3D molecular structures are generated via the stochastic optimization algorithm of the Merck Molecular Force Field (MMFF) [22].

Gene expression data processing

The characterization of cancer cell lines is achieved using transcriptome data (gene expression matrix). We incorporate two unique transcriptome datasets obtained via scRNA-seq or RNA-seq technologies. The scRNA-seq data, sourced from Kinker et al. [6], are integrated after a series of regularization and averaging operations. We selected transcriptional data from GDSC2, which were analyzed using the Human Genome U219 96-Array Plate on the Gene Titan MC instrument (Affymetrix). Intensity values for each locus were established using the robust multiarray analysis algorithm [23]. The scRNA-seq data, sourced from Kinker et al. [6], are integrated after a series of regularization and averaging operations.

Comparative experiment

To gauge the effectiveness of our proposed model, we first construct dataset samples from 102 629 instances of 153 drugs and 805 cell lines recorded in the GDSC database. We then randomly allocate 95% of these instances as the training dataset and the remaining 5% as the test dataset. For cell line feature representation, RNA-seq gene expression data serve as the consistent data embedding. We pit our model, termed DVFGCDR, against six state-of-the-art methods, executing each model test five times to yield an average result.

Among these methods, CDRscan [13] deploys a two-step CNN architecture to extract the gene mutation fingerprints and drug molecular fingerprints features of cell lines, respectively. tCNNS [14] uses a CNN model to predict CDR by extracting SMILES sequence features of drugs and gene mutation features of cancer cell lines, respectively. Multi-omics late integration (MOLI) [15] employs type-specific coding sub-networks to learn features of each histology type (i.e. somatic mutations, copy number aberration, and gene expression data) and integrates them for drug response prediction. DeepCDR [16] comprises a hybrid graph convolutional network, integrating multi-omics features of cancer cell lines and exploring the intrinsic chemical structure of drugs to predict CDR. DeepTTA [17] uses the gene expression data and learns the chemical substructure of a drug via the Transformer model to predict drug response, resulting in more reliable predictions among these methods.

As depicted in Table 1, DVFGCDR not only achieves the highest PCC and SRCC (0.959 and 0.941, respectively) but also records the lowest RMSE (0.795)—0.157 lower than the second-best method DeepTTA, highlighting the superiority of our method.

Ablation experiment

To authenticate the efficacy of each component of our proposed model, we conduct an ablation experiment. We adhere to the data division process outlined above and replicate each model test five times. The 2D and 3D molecular graph convolution layers proposed in our model are designated as DVFGConv2d and DVFGConv3d, respectively, and are compared against prevalent graph convolution models (GIN, GAT, GCN). As reported in Table 2, DVFGConv2d and DVFGConv3d show improvements relative to these commonly used graph convolutional models. Furthermore, the superior performance of DVFGConv3d over DVFGConv2d demonstrates the vital role of 3D geometric features in characterizing molecules.

Upon merging DVFGConv2d and DVFGConv3d to form a comprehensive model (DVFGCDR) and comparing it against DVFGConv2d and DVFGConv3d, we find that DVFGCDR effectively integrates useful feature information from 2D and 3D molecular graphs to gain a performance advantage. This experiment not only validates the importance of the 3D geometric information of molecules but also underscores the superiority of DVFGCDR, which can predict drug response more accurately than traditional graph convolutional networks.

Analysis of predicted drug response outcomes

To investigate the predictive effectiveness of this method across different TCGA cancer types or various drugs, we randomly select 80% of the 102 629 drug response instances among 30 cancer types contained in the GDSC dataset for model training. We use the remaining 20% of the instances to predict the CDR corresponding to different cancers or various drugs. Subsequently, we examine the Pearson correlation between the IC50 observed in the GDSC dataset and the values predicted by our model using the test set.

The PCC scores range from 0.919 to 0.978 across all cell lines, indicating the superb performance attained by our method. Notably, among the best-performing cancer types, brain lower grade glioma (LGG) achieves 0.978, and among the most severe cancer types, breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) achieves 0.919 (Fig. 2). These predicted results for different drugs show a PCC range from 0.462 to 0.953—this fluctuation may be due to the limited types of drugs included in the dataset. The drugs with the best and worst predicted effects are irinotecan and sepantronium bromide, respectively, as depicted in Fig. 3.

Cancer drug response prediction for novel nodes

In an effort to evaluate the efficacy of our method in predicting drug responses for new drugs or cell lines (i.e. new drug or cell line nodes that lack corresponding instances of drug response), we first segregate 20% of the cell line node sensitivity information from the 102 629 instances as the test set. From each cell line in the test set, we eliminate all known instances of drug responses linked to that specific cell line. Then, we utilize the remaining 80% of the cell line nodes correlating to the drug response instances as the training set. This methodology ensures that the cell line nodes in the test sample represent unknown new nodes, and we adopt the same approach for new drugs. Each prediction model is tested five times to compute average results. DeepTTA [17], a superior method for predicting drug response, is utilized as a benchmark for comparison. The corresponding results are depicted in Fig. 4. The outcomes indicate that the performance of DVFGCDR in the independent cell line nodes test is on par with that of DeepTTA (Fig. 4A). This is primarily due to both models using the gene expression matrix as input. The advantage of DVFGCDR is more pronounced in the interval of PCC > 0.89, while DeepTTA excels in the interval of PCC < 0.89. In the independent drug nodes test, it is evident that the performance of DVFGCDR significantly surpasses that of DeepTTA (Fig. 4B). DVFGCDR’s PCC index is 19% higher than that of DeepTTA, which may be attributed to our proposed DVFGconv layer’s superior ability to extract the intrinsic feature information of drugs.

Unknown cancer drug response prediction

Since the number of instances in the GDSC database is limited and a portion of the CDRs are unknown, our goal is to predict these unknown CDRs based on known instances. We apply all 102 629 recorded instances to the model for training, then predict 20 478 missing CDRs in the GDSC database, accounting for ~17% of all possible pairs in the complete GDSC database. The average IC50 values for each drug are illustrated in a ranking shown in Fig. 5, where 809 cancer cell lines covering 31 cancer types in the GDSC database are analyzed. It presents the “top sensitive drugs” and “tolerant drugs” that are identified by calculating the average predicted IC50 value for reflecting the overall degree of each CDR. In the predicted outcomes of DVFGCDR, bortezomib is ranked first, indicating its potent therapeutic effect, aligning with the results from DeepTTA and DeepCDR. Bortezomib, the first anticancer proteasome inhibitor approved by the FDA [26], has demonstrated activity against a variety of malignancies in vitro and in vivo, including myeloma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, and colon cancer [27]. Sepantronium bromide (YM155), a small imidazolium-based compound, was originally developed to counter the anti-apoptotic protein survivin [28]. Preclinical studies involving sepantronium bromide demonstrate inhibition of survivin at both the mRNA and protein levels, displaying considerable anticancer activity in mouse models [29]. Furthermore, a combination treatment with sepantronium bromide and docetaxel has been shown to exert a synergistic antitumor effect [30]. Note that many drug targets have tissue-specific expression and gene–gene interactions, and many cell lines have different mutations, gene deletions, and amplifications. Thus, many cell lines likely have opposite or at least inconsistent response to drug treatment. Therefore, this experimental result is just a relative reference of CDR.

The role of single-cell sequencing data in predicting drug response

While RNA-seq data can be employed to compare expression differences between various tissues, it remains insufficient for more heterogeneous systems (such as complex tissues like tumors). Single-cell sequencing technology provides a solution to this issue by constructing the expression profile of each cell, reflecting the heterogeneity among cells. To validate the effectiveness of our method, we follow the Precily method by Chawla et al. [31] and use their data set to do the comparison. By integrating pathway scores and bulk RNA-seq data, our model can leverage tumor scRNA-seq data to predict drug response at sub-clonal resolution. Comparing the test results of DVFGCDR and Precily, we find that the Pearson coefficient of DVFGCDR surpasses Precily by 2.4%, as illustrated in Fig. 6, verifying our method’s superior performance. Additionally, to validate the effectiveness of scRNA-seq data in predicting CDR, we separately use scRNA-seq data and RNA-seq data as the input of transcriptomics data in the model and contrast their prediction results. During this process, the CCLE/GDSC data experiments are performed by randomly selecting 95% as the training dataset and 5% as the testing dataset. The experimental results indicate that gene expression data based on scRNA-seq produces significantly better results compared to gene expression data based on RNA-seq, with a Pearson correlation coefficient higher by 0.7% as depicted in Fig. 7. This confirms the potential of single-cell transcriptome data in predicting drug response.

Binary classification experiment for drug sensitivity prediction

Additionally, we conduct a binary classification experiment concerning drug sensitivity. Following the method outlined by DeepCDR [16], we binarize the IC50 of each drug using the threshold value of each drug as provided by Iorio et al. [3]. Subsequently, drugs without a defined threshold are eliminated, leading to a dataset comprising 7488 positive instances of drugs sensitive to the respective cancer cell line and 52 210 negative instances of drugs resistant to the cancer cell line. Given the unbalanced data ensuing from the binarization process, we balance the ratio of positive to negative samples at 1:1, while maintaining a training to test set ratio of 4:1. Following this, we employ the indicators of AUROC and AUPR to compare our model with other different methodologies (DeepTTA and DeepCDR). As illustrated in Fig. 8, DVFGCDR achieved the highest AUROC and AUPR, realizing an improvement of 2% and 1.7%, respectively, when compared to DeepTTA.

Comparison of drug sensitivity prediction results with different methods. (A) ROC curve comparison of various methods. (B) PR curve comparison of various methods.

We also juxtapose the results obtained from different ratios of positive to negative samples to evaluate the influence of the number of positive and negative samples on our model’s performance. We consider positive to negative sample ratios of 1:1, 1:3, 1:5, 1:7, 1:9, and 1:11, respectively, in order to scrutinize their effect on our model (where 1:11 approximates the maximum ratio). Furthermore, we create a control group in different ratios by omitting a small quantity of positive samples, setting the proportion of positive samples to {100%, 95%, 90%, and 85%} of the original positive samples, respectively. The test results are presented in Fig. 9. We observe that our model’s performance improves with an increased number of positive samples, possibly because an increased quantity of positive samples augments the message passing in the prediction model. Additionally, we observe that when the ratio of positive samples ranges between 85% and 100%, the performance of the model remains stable and slightly improves with an increase in the proportion of negative samples. This is likely due to the dual influence of the increase in the number of training samples and negative samples.

Discussion

In this study, we propose DVFGCDR, a dual-view fusion-based model for predicting CDR. This model extracts and merges 2D chemical and 3D geometric features of drug molecules respectively to create a more comprehensive drug molecule embedding. Concurrently, gene expression data (sourced from RNA-seq or scRNA-seq data) are employed to extract cell line embeddings via an MLP. These embeddings are subsequently concatenated and processed through MLP again to derive the predicted IC50 values. Benchmark comparison outcomes demonstrate that our proposed model attains improvements in metrics such as RMSE, PCC, and SRCC across multiple test sets compared to existing predictive models. This has significant potential to aid the discovery of anticancer drugs by accurately predicting CDR.

Despite the utility of our method as a potent tool for predicting CDR, there are aspects of our work that can be enhanced in future studies. For instance, we exclusively studied the CDR of individual drugs, which may have inherent limitations. In future endeavors, we will broaden our focus to include the CDR of combination drugs. Moreover, we will integrate spatial multi-omics technologies (particularly spatial transcriptomics) to model tumor–stroma–immune interactions and tissue architecture. These enhancements will capture microenvironmental determinants of drug response, ultimately improving clinical translation.
