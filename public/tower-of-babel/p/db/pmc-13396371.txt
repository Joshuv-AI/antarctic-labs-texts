Sleep architecture differences and predictive markers of sleep perception impairment in depression
Authors: Hongjuan Li, Jiong Chen, Meng Qi, Leilei Wang, Shuangjiang Zhou, Jingxu Chen, Song Gao, Yue Leng, Shuping Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep perception impairment (SPI) characterized by subjective-objective discrepancies in sleep, is common among patients with depression. Its neurophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated associations between polysomnography (PSG)-derived sleep macro- and micro-architecture features and SPI in depressed patients. We enrolled 63 adults (aged 18–65) with DSM-V major depressive disorder. The participants were divided into two groups: the SPI (n = 26) and non-SPI (n = 37). All underwent overnight PSG and completed clinical assessments. We analyzed sleep macro-architecture and EEG micro-architecture, including sleep temporal entropy (STE), reflecting fragmentation of sleep-stage transitions. Logistic and linear regression models assessed predictors of SPI, adjusting for demographics, clinical, and sleep-related covariates. Despite comparable objective sleep duration, SPI patients significantly underestimated their sleep duration based on their post-PSG subjective sleep time estimates, reported poorer subjective sleep quality, exhibited lower EEG total power (median 9.7 vs. 12.5 kµV²; p = 0.003), decreased interhemispheric EEG symmetry (0.50 vs. 0.51; p = 0.02), and elevated high-frequency relative to slow-wave EEG activity. Higher EEG total power (OR = 0.35 per 1000 µV² increase) and greater EEG symmetry (OR = 0.47 per 0.01 increase) independently predicted reduced odds of SPI in adjusted models. Introduction

Sleep disturbances are a hallmark of major depression, with ~ 75% of depressed patients experiencing insomnia symptoms1–5. Objective polysomnography (PSG) studies have documented characteristic macro-architectural abnormalities in depression, including impaired sleep continuity (prolonged sleep latency, frequent awakenings), reduced slow-wave sleep (SWS), and REM sleep dysregulation4,6. For example, depressed patients tend to show shortened REM latency and increased REM density, reflecting a disinhibition of REM sleep generation1,2,6. These alterations not only serve as biomarkers of depression but may also contribute to daytime impairment4,7. Paradoxically, some depressed patients subjectively report extremely poor or insufficient sleep even when their objective sleep appears relatively intact7. This phenomenon—often termed sleep state misperception —has been extensively described in primary insomnia8,9. Emerging evidence further indicates that similar patterns of sleep misperception also occur in depression, with patients frequently underestimating total sleep time while overestimating sleep latency and wake after sleep onset6,7,10. In such cases, individuals typically underestimate total sleep time (TST) while overestimating sleep latency and wake after sleep onset (WASO)10,11. Subjective–objective discrepancies are common in sleep assessment. Prior studies have demonstrated that conventional PSG metrics explain only a modest proportion of variance in subjective sleep quality, even in healthy individuals. Thus, subjective sleep perception and objective sleep physiology represent partially dissociable constructs rather than interchangeable measures, with insomnia and depression characterized by a more pronounced and systematically negative dissociation12–14. Importantly, insomnia and depression are characterized not by the mere presence of discrepancy, but by its magnitude and systematic negative bias, particularly marked underestimation of sleep duration and overestimation of nocturnal wakefulness15–17. Accordingly, sleep perception impairment has been operationalized in research using empirical thresholds, most commonly a subjective–objective TST discrepancy of ≥ 60 min (or alternatively a ≥ 15% difference in sleep efficiency), to capture clinically meaningful deviations beyond normal perceptual imprecision16,17. Recent evidence indicates that sleep misperception also occurs in depression and may impede recovery1,10. However, the mechanistic basis for sleep perception impairment (SPI) in depressed populations is not fully understood. SPI refers to the discrepancy between subjective estimates of total sleep time reported after the PSG night and objective polysomnographic measures. Because definitions of sleep misperception and paradoxical insomnia vary across studies18, we operationalized SPI as a subjective–objective discrepancy in total sleep time (TST) ≥ 60 min, a commonly used and clinically meaningful threshold for sleep duration misperception.

One hypothesis is that depressed patients with SPI experience a state of cortical hyperarousal during sleep, analogous to that seen in chronic insomnia disorder. EEG spectral studies in insomnia disorder suggest a pattern of cortical hyperarousal during NREM sleep, characterized by relatively increased high-frequency activity—particularly in the beta range—and reduced low-frequency (delta) power19. This reflects a failure to achieve the normal synchronized cortical slowing of deeper sleep, potentially leading to a subjective sense of light or restless sleep despite normal sleep duration. Similarly, hyperarousal has been proposed to manifest as increased sleep fragmentation or instability at a microstructural level, potentially contributing to subjective experiences of wakefulness during objectively scored sleep20,21. We speculate that depressed individuals with SPI have subtler micro-architectural anomalies – for instance, an excess of fast oscillatory activity or diminished amplitude of slow waves – that differentiate them from depressed patients with concordant sleep perception.

Beyond conventional PSG metrics (sleep stages, latency, etc.), advanced EEG analytics provide quantitative “digital biomarkers” of sleep physiology that could shed light on SPI22–24. Spectral power analysis across frequency bands can capture shifts in the balance of slow versus fast activities. Spectral ratios (e.g. beta/delta or alpha/theta ratios) summarize this balance and have been linked to arousal levels19,22. Sleep spindles and their characteristics (frequency, density, amplitude) reflect thalamocortical dynamics; spindle asymmetry between hemispheres might indicate uneven sleep depth and has been posited as an insomnia marker22,25. Entropy-based measures of sleep EEG (e.g. sleep temporal entropy, permutation entropy) quantify the complexity and fragmentation of sleep architecture26,27. For instance, entropy analyses have shown that insomnia patients display altered complexity patterns – with lower EEG entropy during wake and higher entropy during deep sleep compared to healthy sleepers27– suggesting these metrics track the brain’s arousal and stability states. Such measures, alongside traditional PSG variables, may improve our ability to detect the subtle physiological signatures of sleep misperception.

In this study, we compare a comprehensive set of sleep architecture features between depressed patients with vs. without SPI. We integrate standard macro-architecture indices (TST, sleep latency and continuity measures) with EEG-derived microarchitecture features including band-limited spectral power, spectral ratios, spindle metrics, EEG asymmetry, and entropy. We hypothesized that SPI patients would have relatively normal objective total sleep time but perceive it as shorter (subjectively underestimate their sleep) and show evidence of hyperarousal in their EEG and possibly lower sleep temporal entropy. We further hypothesized that these microarchitecture markers would significantly associate with SPI status even after controlling for depression severity and other clinical factors. By elucidating these differences, our study seeks to advance the understanding of sleep perception impairment in depression and explore novel EEG biomarkers that could eventually aid in clinical assessment of subjective-objective sleep discrepancy.

Results

Characteristics of Participant

A total of 63 patients (mean age 38.8 ± 15.4 years; 59% male) completed the study (see Table 1 for demographics and clinical metrics). Among these, 26 (41%) met criteria for Sleep Perception Impairment (SPI), reporting worse sleep than measured. Demographic factors (age, sex, marital and employment status) were similar between SPI and No-SPI groups. Notably, 27% of SPI patients were categorized as mildly depressed (HAMD ≤ 13) compared to 8% of No-SPI, whereas severe depression was more prevalent in No-SPI (73% vs. 42% in SPI; group difference p = 0.037; Table 1). In terms of subjective sleep quality, SPI patients reported poorer subjective sleep quality than the No-SPI group. The PSQI global score was significantly higher (worse) in SPI (median 22 [21–22]) than No-SPI (21 [19–22], p = 0.004). HAMD scores did not differ significantly between two groups. Additional sensitivity analyses comparing individual HAMD items and factor domains between SPI and non-SPI groups are shown in Appendix E (Figures E1–E3).

Despite these subjective and clinical disparities, objective sleep macro-architecture was strikingly similar between groups on most measures. Total Sleep Time (TST) did not significantly differ. Sleep latency and REM latency were slightly shorter (n.s.) in SPI (both p > 0.2). The proportion of time in each sleep stage (N1, N2, N3, REM) showed no significant differences. WASO and arousal indices likewise did not differ: median wake after sleep onset was ~ 33 min in both, and the wake index (Wake index was defined as the number of full awakenings per hour of sleep, as scored according to AASM criteria. This differs from the arousal index, which reflects brief EEG arousals that do not necessarily result in full awakenings.) was ~ 3.8 in SPI vs. 3.7 in No-SPI (p = 0.44). Respiratory events were mostly minimal; mean AHI was low in both groups (~ 3/h, p = 0.94), and PLMI did not differ (p = 0.28).

Thus, conventional PSG macro-architecture measures did not meaningfully differentiate SPI from No-SPI in this sample. Figure 1a demonstrates this paradox: despite equivalent PSG-measured sleep durations, SPI patients reported far worse sleep quality (PSQI scores).

Global spectral power

SPI patients exhibited significantly lower total EEG power across the night. The mean Total absolute power (integral of 0.5–40 Hz power) was 9730 ± 2767 µV^2 in SPI vs. 12,482 ± 4179 µV^2 in No-SPI (p = 0.0026). This ~ 22% reduction in total power in SPI is evident in Fig. 1d. Notably, delta power (the dominant component of total power) tended to be lower in SPI, although median relative delta fraction (~ 0.70) was similar between groups.

EEG Symmetry

The SPI group showed a small but significant asymmetry in EEG power between hemispheres. The EEG symmetry index (where 0.50 indicates perfect symmetry) was slightly lower in SPI (0.50 ± 0.01) than No-SPI (0.51 ± 0.01), p = 0.021. Figure 1c depicts this difference.

NREM sleep spindles

Despite the asymmetry finding, basic spindle counts and characteristics in stage N2 were not significantly different between groups. N2 spindle count was high and comparable (SPI 1362 ± 502 vs. No-SPI 1342 ± 447 spindles/night, p = 0.87). Spindle properties (mean frequency ~ 13.1 Hz, duration ~ 0.83 s, RMS amplitude ~ 12 µV) did not differ (all p > 0.3).

Band-specific power – NREM

Stage-specific spectral analysis revealed shifts in the power spectrum of SPI patients during NREM sleep. In stage N2, SPI patients showed lower alpha-band power (median 0.04 vs. 0.05, p = 0.017) and higher theta power (mean 0.08 ± 0.01 vs. 0.07 ± 0.01, p = 0.008), indicating a redistribution of spectral activity toward slower frequencies within the NREM spectrum. In stage N3 (slow-wave sleep), no significant group differences were observed in delta power (the dominant component of N3), with both groups showing similarly high delta fractions (median ~ 0.77 of N3 power, p = 0.99). SPI patients showed a non-significant trend toward higher theta power in N3 (0.08 vs. 0.07, p = 0.21), while alpha activity remained minimal in both groups. During REM sleep, no significant group differences were found in traditional spectral bands (REM theta ~ 0.08 in both groups, p = 0.20; REM sigma ~ 0.02–0.03, p = 0.10). Both groups demonstrated the typical REM spectral profile characterized by elevated theta and alpha activity and low delta power.

Importantly, complementary analyses of fast-to-slow spectral ratios (e.g., beta/delta and gamma/delta ratios) suggested relatively greater high-frequency components in SPI patients, which is consistent with the hyperarousal hypothesis reported in insomnia-related literature.

Wake EEG

Interestingly, PSD differences were also emerged in the pre-sleep wake EEG (recorded during eyes-closed rest before sleep onset). SPI patients had significantly lower alpha power during wake (median 0.04 vs. 0.05, p = 0.007), and non-significantly lower wake beta (0.06 vs. 0.07, p = 0.20).

Spectral ratios

The ratios of fast-to-slow band power showed some of the most pronounced differences, particularly in lighter sleep stages. In stage N1, SPI patients had significantly elevated ratios of sigma, beta, and gamma relative to delta power: N1 Sigma/Delta ratio was higher in SPI (mean ~ 0.42) vs. No-SPI (~ 0.33), with fully adjusted linear model β ≈ +45 (relative units), p < 0.01 (Table 2 and Appendix Table A3). Logistic regression likewise indicated a strong association (OR ~ 1.6 per 0.1 increment; see Appendix). N1 Beta/Delta and N1 Gamma/Delta ratios were also significantly higher in SPI (e.g., N1 Gamma/Delta: SPI median 0.13 vs. 0.10, p ~ 0.01; linear model p = 0.01; OR ~ 2.3 in logistic).

Sleep temporal entropy

We observed a trend toward lower entropy of sleep stage durations in SPI patients, though this did not reach significance. The Overall STE for the full night was slightly lower in SPI (mean 5.08 ± 0.73) than No-SPI (5.51 ± 0.88), p = 0.062. Figure B6 (Appendix B) illustrates the entropy values.Stage-specific entropy measures (NREM STE, REM STE) were not significantly different, except REM STE in fully adjusted linear regression was lower in SPI (β ≈ − 45, p = 0.04; see Appendix A1).

Correlation heatmap of SPI features

To better understand interrelationships among key variables, we generated a correlation matrix heatmap (Appendix C) including SPI status, PSQI score, sleep stages, and selected EEG markers. Briefly, SPI status was negatively correlated with total power (Pearson r ≈ − 0.45) and symmetry (r ≈ − 0.30), and positively correlated with PSQI (r ≈ + 0.50) and N1 Gamma/Delta ratio (r ≈ + 0.40). Interesting secondary correlations were noted: for example, total power correlated positively with N3% (r ≈ + 0.25) and symmetry (r ≈ + 0.4), suggesting patients with more slow-wave activity also had more symmetric EEG – a plausible link since high delta power often occurs bihemispherically. Meanwhile, PSQI had only weak correlation with objective measures except SPI. Sleep duration and efficiency

In linear regression analyses (Model 3, fully adjusted), each additional hour of objectively measured PSG total sleep duration significantly corresponded to approximately 84 min smaller subjective-objective discrepancy in sleep duration (β = − 83.71, 95% CI − 109.92 to − 57.50, p < 0.001), highlighting that shorter PSG-measured sleep strongly predicted greater sleep perception impairment (SPI). Although logistic regression suggested each additional hour of PSG sleep duration was associated with 31% lower odds of SPI, this finding was not statistically significant (OR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.32–1.48, p = 0.34). Sleep efficiency similarly predicted greater subjective-objective discrepancy, with SPI patients exhibiting an approximately 46% lower sleep efficiency than their counterparts after adjustment (β = − 46.0, 95% CI − 81.59 to − 10.41, p = 0.01).

Sleep Continuity (Latency and Fragmentation)

Sleep latency did not significantly predict subjective-objective sleep discrepancies in linear regression models (β = 12.95 min, p = 0.44), though logistic models indicated shorter sleep latency significantly predicted SPI status (OR = 0.35 per 10-min increase, 95% CI 0.14–0.90, p = 0.03, Model 3). Neither REM latency nor wake after sleep onset (WASO) were significant predictors in linear or logistic models. However, the frequency of awakenings (Wake index) was significantly higher in SPI patients after adjustment (β = 40.50 events/hr, p = 0.02, Model 3).

EEG markers

Total EEG absolute power was among the strongest objective predictors of subjective-objective sleep discrepancy. In fully adjusted linear regression (Model 3), increased total absolute EEG power significantly predicted smaller subjective-objective discrepancy (β = 33.98, 95% CI 2.19–65.77, p = 0.04), while logistic regression supported this result, indicating lower odds of SPI with higher EEG power (OR = 0.34, 95% CI 0.14–0.83, p = 0.02, Model 3).

Interhemispheric EEG symmetry also predicted SPI, with greater asymmetry associated with larger sleep discrepancies. In fully adjusted linear regression, symmetry showed a trend towards a significant negative relationship (β = − 29.83, p = 0.07, Model 3), and logistic regression confirmed increased asymmetry significantly associated with SPI (OR = 0.43, 95% CI 0.20–0.92, p = 0.03, Model 3).Band-specific powers (alpha, beta, theta, etc.) did not independently predict subjective-objective sleep discrepancy significantly after adjustments.

Spectral ratios, particularly fast-frequency band ratios within NREM sleep, were robust predictors of SPI. Higher N1 and N2 Beta/Delta ratios were strongly associated with larger discrepancies in linear models (N1: β = 42.67, p = 0.01; N2: β = 37.92, p = 0.02; Model 3), and logistic models similarly supported this relationship (e.g., N2 Theta/Beta ratio OR = 1.97, p = 0.05, Model 3).

Sleep Temporal Entropy (STE) measures were generally not independently significant in adjusted models, suggesting limited unique predictive value beyond traditional sleep measures, though lower REM STE was marginally predictive in linear regression (β = − 45.82, p = 0.04, Model 3).

Subjective and clinical covariates

Adjusting for subjective sleep quality (PSQI total) and depression severity (HAMD score) improved model fit, but these variables themselves did not significantly drive the subjective-objective discrepancy. HAMD total scores were similar between groups, suggesting that the observed EEG differences are not simply attributable to differences in measured depression severity. However, this finding does not exclude the possibility that depression-related mechanisms contribute to sleep perception impairment. Other factors not fully captured by the HAMD scale—such as anxiety, cognitive hyperarousal, rumination, or heightened attention to sleep-related sensations—may also contribute to both sleep perception impairment and EEG spectral alterations.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses adjusting for antidepressant medication use yielded largely consistent results. Specifically, after additional adjustment for medication status, the associations between key EEG markers—including total EEG absolute power, interhemispheric symmetry, and stage-specific spectral ratios—and sleep perception impairment remained similar in both direction and magnitude. In linear regression models, macro-sleep variables (particularly PSG-derived sleep duration and sleep efficiency), as well as microstructural EEG markers, continued to show significant associations with sleep misperception (minutes). The consistency of findings across both logistic and linear models suggests that the observed EEG correlates are unlikely to be attributable solely to medication effects (Appendix Table E1).

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that state-based and trait-based sleep misperception measures were significantly correlated, indicating that the two approaches capture related but not identical constructs. Reclassification analyses showed a moderate level of agreement between definitions. As illustrated in the reclassification matrix and alluvial flow diagram, most participants remained within the same classification category, while a subset of individuals transitioned between SPI and non-SPI groups across definitions. Detailed results of these analyses are presented in Appendix Figure E3–1.

Discussion

In this study, we identified distinct differences in micro-sleep architectures between depressed patients with Sleep Perception Impairment (SPI) and those without, despite otherwise similar macro-sleep architecture. To our knowledge, this is one of the first investigations to comprehensively characterize EEG microarchitecture correlates of sleep misperception in depression. Our findings suggest that, within depressed patients, SPI may be accompanied by measurable EEG micro-architecture differences that are not captured by conventional stage-based metrics – specifically, a pattern of diminished slow-wave activity, elevated high-frequency EEG activity during NREM sleep, and subtle interhemispheric EEG asymmetry. Given the sample size and the operational definition of SPI, these associations should be considered exploratory and require replication. However, these features mirror what has been observed in primary insomnia patients with sleep state misperception, pointing to shared underlying mechanisms.

Sleep perception impairment in depression

Previous work has noted that a subset of depressed patients experiences a discrepancy between how they feel they slept and what objective measures show10. Our study extends those observations with quantitative EEG evidence. We found that SPI patients slept objectively about as long as non-SPI patients (~ 7.3 h on average), yet they consistently underestimated their sleep duration subjectively, highlighting their sleep misperception, with no increase in conventional awakenings or reduction in sleep efficiency28–30. Yet, subjectively, they were just as distressed about their sleep as chronic insomniacs (PSQI ~ 21–22 out of 24). This aligns with prior reports that misperception can occur even when total sleep time is normal10,31,32. Notably, in a recent study of depressed patients, reported that factors like higher N1% and N2% were associated with misperception of WASO10. We similarly observed a non-significant trend of SPI patients spending more time in N1 (median 7% vs. 9.5%) and less in N3 (though our sample overall had little N3). Taken together, these findings suggest that analyses based solely on macro-sleep architecture may be insufficient to fully capture the neurophysiological differences underlying SPI. Therefore, to better characterize the mechanisms of SPI, we further investigated micro-sleep architecture features.

Building on this, rather than indicating a simple redistribution of N1 or N2 sleep, our results point to differences in the electrophysiological characteristics of these stages. Specifically, N1/N2 sleep in SPI patients tended to exhibit spectral profiles enriched in relatively faster EEG frequencies, which may reflect a comparatively lighter or less stable form of sleep. Such micro-architectural features could contribute to the subjective experience of wakefulness or restless sleep during objectively scored sleep periods, offering a potential explanation for sleep misperception.

Interestingly, we found that SPI patients tended to have lower depression severity on average (fewer were in the “severe depression” category). This is counterintuitive – one might assume worse depression yields worse sleep complaints10,33. One interpretation is that sleep misperception is somewhat independent of core mood symptoms. It may stem more from trait factors like anxiety, attentional bias to bodily sensations, or specific neurophysiological arousal profiles34,35. It is known in insomnia literature that patients with sleep perception impairment often have elevated anxiety and sleep-related attentional bias, monitoring for awakenings10. In depression, those who are less severely depressed might still have significant anxiety or ruminative traits that predispose to misperceive sleep. Our finding invites future research into the personality or cognitive factors that differentiate SPI vs. non-SPI depressed patients (e.g. anxiety levels, obsessional traits, somatic hypervigilance).

Hyperarousal and EEG fast activity

The hyperarousal model of insomnia posits that patients suffer from excessive CNS arousal that persists into sleep20,27,36,37. This model has robust support: for example, A recent systematic review and meta-analysis confirm insomniacs have elevated beta and sigma power during NREM and reduced delta power19. Our SPI patients – who could be considered “insomnia within depression” – exhibited a very similar signature. They had 22% less total EEG power, which likely reflects reduced delta power given delta dominates the spectrum. Concurrently, they showed higher relative beta/gamma, captured in the significantly higher fast/slow ratios in N1-N2. This suggests that physiologically, their brains were not downshifting into deep synchronous activity as effectively37,38. They remained in a more alert EEG state, especially in early sleep. Notably, one frequency band that was lower in SPI was alpha (8–12 Hz) during N2 and wake. Alpha can represent relaxed drowsiness; insomniacs often paradoxically show less alpha when awake (due to tension) and more alpha intrusions during sleep27. Our SPI group’s lower wake alpha might indicate difficulty relaxing with eyes closed, consistent with heightened somatic/cognitive arousal.

The implications of this are important: even though a polysomnographer scoring their data saw “7 hours of sleep, normal sleep efficiency,” the EEG reveals that much of that sleep was light and probably unrefreshing39–41. The lower total spectral power observed in SPI patients may reflect differences in EEG signal power across frequencies rather than a direct reduction in slow-wave amplitude. In the present study, we quantified spectral power rather than slow-wave amplitude per se, and we did not directly assess physiological markers of sleep restoration. Therefore, we interpret this finding as an EEG microarchitectural correlate of sleep misperception rather than direct evidence of impaired restorative sleep. This aligns with reports that sleep misperceivers have more intra-sleep awareness and memory of being awake40,42.

We also observed an intriguing REM sleep finding in regression analyses: lower REM temporal entropy in SPI. In recent years, REM sleep has increasingly been studied in relation to various adverse health outcomes, and REM-related features also revealed some interesting patterns in our current study26,43. While REM spectral power differences were minimal, the entropy result might hint that SPI patients had a more uniform REM EEG (perhaps indicating less phasic REM activity or fewer arousals within REM). This could be speculative, but one possibility is that SPI patients, being hyperaroused, transition out of REM sleep less, effectively “locking in” to REM episodes (hence lower duration entropy). Future work with higher-resolution measures (e.g. REM density, micro-arousals) could explore this.

Spindle asymmetry and local sleep

A novel finding in our study is the lower interhemispheric spindle symmetry in SPI patients. Sleep spindles are thalamocortical oscillations important for sleep stability. Typically, spindles occur fairly symmetrically across hemispheres44. Our SPI group had a consistent asymmetry (often right-dominant). However, the absolute difference between groups was small (0.50 vs. 0.51), and therefore the clinical relevance of this finding should be interpreted with caution. Although the statistical comparison suggested a group-level difference, the magnitude of asymmetry remains modest and may partly reflect measurement variability or natural heterogeneity in spindle expression.

Given this relatively small absolute difference, the interpretation of spindle asymmetry in the present study should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. This could reflect local sleep deficits – perhaps one hemisphere, possibly the left, remained in a more wake-like state. However, the current data do not directly demonstrate such localized wake-like activity, and this interpretation remains speculative. Such local or regional sleep discrepancies have been documented: for instance, on the first night in a new environment, one hemisphere’s default-mode network stays more alert44,45. In chronic insomnia, there is evidence of localized cortical hypermetabolism. Nevertheless, whether the modest asymmetry observed in our study reflects similar mechanisms in depression-related sleep perception impairment remains uncertain. Our findings therefore suggest a potential association between sleep perception impairment and subtle hemispheric differences in spindle expression, but further work is required to determine the physiological basis and clinical relevance of this observation.

This spindle asymmetry finding, while needing replication, opens an interesting direction: could targeted interventions (like auditory stimulation or transcranial stimulation) be used to enhance bilateral slow-wave/spindle activity in SPI patients? If one could “even out” sleep oscillations across hemispheres, perhaps patients would feel more fully asleep46,47. At present, however, these possibilities remain speculative and require confirmation in larger studies with more detailed neurophysiological characterization.

Clinical relevance and biomarker potential

Our results have several implications for clinical sleep medicine in depression. First, they suggest that subjective sleep complaints should not be dismissed in depressed patients simply because a PSG appears normal4,48. The complaints may stem from real microarchitectural differences that are invisible to standard sleep staging. In fact, using advanced EEG analysis could help identify SPI patients. For instance, a depressed patient with normal sleep duration but low EEG power and high beta activity may warrant further assessment at risk for SPI. This might inform treatment: these patients might benefit from therapies targeting arousal (e.g. cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia – CBT-I – or possibly low-dose sedating medication)49–51 even if their PSG “looks okay.”

Second, the identified EEG markers (low total power, high beta/gamma ratios, spindle asymmetry) could serve as objective endpoints in trials52,53. If an intervention truly helps a misperceiving patient sleep more restfully, we would expect to see total power go up, beta/delta ratio go down, etc., alongside improved subjective sleep reports24,52. This provides a way to quantitatively assess improvement in sleep quality beyond just asking the patient.

Third, the concept of digital biomarkers emerges. Measures like entropy and spectral ratios can be computed from home EEG devices or even certain headband sleep monitors1,54. As wearable EEG technology advances, one can envision monitoring these subtle EEG signatures in outpatients. A persistent high beta/delta ratio could alert clinicians to an underrecognized sleep perception issue in a depressed patient who might otherwise attribute fatigue solely to depression.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant caution. Our sample size was moderate (n = 63) and all patients had depression; we lacked a non-depressed control group. Thus, we cannot say which EEG features are unique to depressed SPI vs. primary insomnia. However, our results align strongly with known insomnia physiology19, suggesting overlap. In addition, the relatively modest sample size in relation to the large number of EEG-derived variables examined increases the possibility of type II errors and limits the stability of multivariable findings. Therefore, the present results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Exploratory analyses involving multiple EEG spectral measures may also increase the probability of statistically significant findings occurring by chance, even when applying correction methods such as the FDR. Therefore, interpretation of the results should focus not only on statistical significance but also on the magnitude of the observed effects and the consistency of findings across sleep stages and spectral bands. Isolated or marginal findings should be interpreted with caution until replicated in larger independent cohorts. Based on the observed effect sizes of key EEG markers, preliminary planning estimates suggest that future confirmatory studies may require approximately 60–230 participants in total depending on the specific EEG outcome, with larger samples needed for spectral ratio measures such as beta/delta power ratios.

Another limitation is that PSG recordings were obtained from a single night without an adaptation night. Sleep misperception is known to be particularly susceptible to the first-night effect, in which sleeping in an unfamiliar laboratory environment may alter sleep architecture and exaggerate discrepancies between subjective and objective sleep measures. Therefore, it is possible that first-night adaptation effects influenced the observed subjective–objective discrepancies in our cohort. Future studies using multiple-night recordings or adaptation-night protocols may help further clarify the relationship between sleep perception impairment and EEG-derived sleep features. An additional limitation relates to the potential influence of the first-night effect. Sleep perception impairment is generally considered a trait-like phenomenon, whereas our PSG-based assessment reflects a single-night measurement that may be susceptible to environmental adaptation effects. In the present study, we attempted to address this issue by incorporating both a state-based measure (post-PSG subjective sleep duration) and a trait-based measure derived from the PSQI (reflecting sleep perception over the preceding month). These measures were highly correlated in our sample, suggesting partial convergence across temporal scales. However, this approach does not fully eliminate the potential bias introduced by first-night effects. Future studies integrating multi-night or home-based sleep monitoring aligned with longer-term subjective measures (e.g., PSQI) may help better characterize chronic sleep perception impairment while minimizing laboratory-related artifacts.

Another limitation is our definition of SPI – there is no universal standard. We defined SPI based on the discrepancy between subjective total sleep time estimated after the PSG night and PSG-derived total sleep time, rather than using questionnaire-based sleep estimates such as PSQI. It’s possible some misclassification occurred (e.g., a patient who actually slept a bit less may have been labeled SPI or vice versa). Nonetheless, the observed group differences suggest that our operational definition identified a subgroup with distinguishable EEG features, but misclassification remains possible. Because SPI definitions vary across studies, our operationalization based on TST discrepancy should be interpreted as one possible approach to capturing sleep perception impairment rather than a formal diagnostic definition. Future studies could use a more quantitative approach, like the Misperception Index (subjective minus objective sleep divided by objective) to grade SPI severity55. In the present study, SPI was primarily defined using PSG-night–based subjective–objective sleep discrepancy. Although trait-based measures such as the PSQI have been proposed to capture habitual sleep perception, our sensitivity analyses indicate that state- and trait-based measures are related but not interchangeable. Methodologically, state-based estimates provide better temporal alignment with PSG-derived physiology, enabling more precise assessment of the relationship between EEG microarchitecture and perceived sleep, whereas trait-based measures may introduce temporal mismatch. Importantly, the consistency of findings across both definitions supports the robustness of the identified EEG markers and suggests that sleep perception impairment in depression reflects underlying neurophysiological alterations rather than an artifact of classification.

Also, many EEG measures were intercorrelated; our regression models, while adjusted, could suffer multicollinearity, although our variance inflation factor (VIF) assessments suggested multicollinearity was not a major concern. We mitigated this by focusing on conceptually distinct features (power vs. symmetry vs. ratios) in separate models. Our findings of specific predictors (like power, symmetry) likely highlight the underlying dimension of “hyper-aroused vs deep sleep” rather than independent additive factors.

We did not measure physiological arousal markers like heart rate or cortisol, which could further underscore hyperarousal in SPI. Some research finds insomniacs have elevated heart rate and metabolic rate at night56,57. In depression, those data are mixed. It would be interesting to see if our SPI group had higher nocturnal heart rate or sympathetic tone; unfortunately, our study design did not capture detailed autonomic metrics beyond basic ECG line length (which showed a trend toward higher in No-SPI, possibly due to a few with minor arrhythmias, see Table 2).

Lastly, while we identified statistical predictors, the real-world application would require refining thresholds. For example, how low must total power be to signify SPI? In our data, a cutoff around 10,000 µV^2 separated many SPI from No-SPI. Similarly, a spindle symmetry index < 0.505 was common in SPI. These thresholds would need validation in larger samples.

Conclusion

In depressed patients, sleep perception impairment represents a phenotype where the complaint of insomnia belies relatively normal sleep duration Our study shows that the brains of these patients exhibit quantifiable signs of not achieving deep, symmetrical sleep. This convergence of subjective and objective evidence underscores that sleep misperception is a genuine neurophysiological phenomenon. It highlights the importance of looking beyond sleep duration and considering EEG markers when a patient’s experience and the polysomnographic data don’t align. Importantly, the main findings remained largely unchanged in sensitivity analyses adjusting for medication use, suggesting that antidepressant medication is unlikely to fully account for the observed EEG alterations associated with sleep perception impairment.

From a clinical perspective, our findings encourage clinicians to treat the complaint, not just the numbers. On the research front, the identified EEG differences (especially total power and high-frequency ratios) could be targeted in neuromodulation studies – for instance, could enhancing slow waves via auditory stimulation reduce misperception? Or could neurofeedback training to increase alpha relaxation help these patients transition into deeper sleep?

In summary, our study provides evidence that SPI in depression has measurable correlates in the brain’s sleep activity. It bridges a gap between the subjective and objective in sleep medicine, suggesting that advanced EEG metrics can help underscore patients’ experiences and possibly guide more personalized treatment. Future research should explore interventions that modify these EEG markers and determine if such changes correspond to improved sleep perception and overall outcomes in depression.

Methods

Participants

Participants were adults (age 18–65) with a diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD) according to DSM-5 criteria, recruited from an academic medical center’s mood disorders clinic between May 2022 and December 2023. Key inclusion criteria were: current depressive episode (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, HAMD ≥ 8), self-reported insomnia symptoms (difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep) of at least one month duration, and ability to undergo overnight sleep monitoring. Exclusion criteria included other sleep disorders (e.g. obstructive sleep apnea with apnea–hypopnea index > 15; severe restless legs), active substance use disorder, current benzodiazepine or hypnotic medication use (due to EEG effects), and serious medical or neurological conditions affecting sleep.

We operationally defined Sleep Perception Impairment (SPI) as a significant subjective-objective sleep discrepancy. After PSG, patients were asked to estimate their total sleep time. Patients were classified as SPI if they underestimated their sleep duration by ≥ 1 h compared to PSG (i.e., subjective TST was at least 60 min less than objective TST). Patients who did not meet this criterion were classified as No-SPI. This yielded 26 patients with SPI (“mis-perceivers”) and 37 without SPI (“accurate perceivers”).

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Huilongguan Hospital. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations.Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to any study procedures.

Study procedure

Demographic information, including age (years), sex, family history of psychiatric disorders, marital status, occupation, and educational level, was collected using a standardized questionnaire. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17)58, which evaluates multiple symptom domains such as depressed mood, psychic and somatic anxiety, somatic symptoms, hypochondriasis, and insight. The total HAMD-17 score ranges from 0 to 52, with higher scores indicating greater depressive severity. Consistent with established conventions, scores of 8–17, 18–24, and ≥ 24 were used to classify mild, moderate, and severe depression, respectively. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)59, a self-report questionnaire developed by Buysse et al. that evaluates sleep quality over the preceding month. The PSQI comprises seven components, including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality.

All participants completed a sleep diary and the PSQI prior to the PSG night. The sleep diary recorded daytime naps, bedtime, pre-sleep medication use, lights-off time, sleep latency, number and duration of nocturnal awakenings, final awakening time, get-up time, and perceived difficulty initiating sleep. The PSQI assessed subjective sleep quality over the preceding month. Following the PSG night, participants completed the sleep diary again.

Participants underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) for one night in a dedicated sleep ward using a SOMNOscreen™ plus system (Somnomedics GmbH, Randersacker, Germany). Lights-off timing was individually adjusted according to each participant’s habitual sleep schedule. Participants were instructed to turn off mobile phones throughout the recording period and to refrain from using sleep-related medications. Device sensitivity and signal quality were verified using the monitoring software before and during data acquisition. The recording ended in the morning after participants awakened and the equipment was removed. No external disturbances were permitted during the recording period.

Overnight polysomnography (PSG) was conducted in accordance with the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) standards60. Recordings included electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography (EOG), chin and leg electromyography, electrocardiography (ECG), airflow (nasal pressure and thermistor), respiratory effort (thoracic and abdominal belts), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), pulse rate, body position, and movement. EEG was recorded using standard international 10–20 system placements, with signals referenced to the contralateral mastoid (M1 or M2), including frontal (F3–M2, F4–M1), central (C3–M2, C4–M1), and occipital (O1–M2, O2–M1) derivations. All PSG recordings were scored by the same registered polysomnographic technologist (RPSGT) using standard AASM scoring criteria.

From PSG scoring, we derived macro-sleep architecture measures, including total sleep time (TST, minutes), sleep efficiency (% of time in bed spent asleep), sleep latency (minutes to sleep onset), REM latency (minutes to first REM episode), wake after sleep onset (WASO, minutes), wake index (number of awakenings per hour), and the percentage of TST spent in each sleep stage (N1, N2, N3, and REM). The Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) and Periodic Limb Movement Index (PLMI) were calculated to quantify sleep-disordered breathing and periodic limb movements, respectively.

EEG feature extraction

EEG signals were sampled at 512 Hz. Prior to spectral analysis, signals were band-pass filtered between 0.3 and 45 Hz and visually inspected for artifacts. Power spectral density (PSD) was computed using Welch’s method with 4-s windows and 50% overlap. For EEG micro-architecture, we performed quantitative analysis on artifact-free EEG signals (focusing on mastoid-referenced central derivations C3–M2 and C4–M1 unless otherwise specified). Prior to spectral and spindle analyses, EEG signals were quality-controlled at the epoch level. Data were segmented into 30-s epochs, and epochs containing gross artifacts—defined by excessive peak-to-peak amplitude (> 500 µV) or near-flat signals—were excluded using predefined criteria applied uniformly across participants. Artifact-free segments were concatenated for subsequent analyses. Both absolute and relative band power were computed to characterize overall signal magnitude as well as spectral composition.

Key EEG features included:

Band Power: We computed the power spectral density within standard frequency bands – delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), sigma (12–15 Hz, spindle range), beta (15–30 Hz), and gamma (30–40 Hz) – using Welch’s method on NREM epochs. Relative spectral power was estimated using Welch’s method as implemented in the YASA Python package61, with fixed-length sliding windows of 4 s and 50% overlap, yielding a frequency resolution determined by the sampling frequency and window length. Power was expressed both in absolute terms (µV^2) and as fraction of total power (%). We also calculated Total Absolute Power (integral of the 0.5–40 Hz range).

Statistical analysis

Group differences between the SPI vs. No-SPI groups were first examined with descriptive statistics. The study size (n = 63; SPI = 26, No-SPI = 37) reflected all eligible patients during the recruitment window; the sample size was therefore determined pragmatically by cohort availability rather than by a priori power calculation, and multivariable analyses were interpreted as exploratory with attention to effect sizes and confidence intervals. Continuous variables were assessed for normality; appropriately, we used independent t-tests for normally distributed measures (reported as mean ± SD) or Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normal measures (reported as median [IQR]). Categorical variables (e.g. sex, depression severity category) were compared via chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 (two-tailed) was applied for group comparisons.

To identify independent predictors of SPI, we employed both logistic regression (treating SPI as a binary outcome) and linear regression, with the outcome defined as the difference between subjective sleep duration (self-reported) and objective sleep duration (measured by PSG), quantifying the magnitude of sleep perception impairment. While logistic models yield odds ratios (OR) for binary SPI status, linear models allowed us to estimate how each predictor contributes quantitatively to the extent of subjective-objective sleep discrepancy. For each domain of features, we built incrementally adjusted models: Univariate (unadjusted), Model 1 (adjusted for basic demographics: age, sex, marital status, education, employment), Model 2 (additionally adjusted for clinical factors: PSQI total score, cognitive function (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA score), and depression severity (HAMD score)), and Model 3 (further adjusted for relevant health factors: AHI, PLMI, general health status, and any medical comorbidities). Separate regression tables were prepared for (A) sleep macro-architecture variables, (B) EEG micro-architecture variables (global spectral measures), and (C) stage-specific EEG measures. Results are reported as OR with 95% confidence interval (CI) for logistic models and as unstandardized β (95% CI) for linear models. Variance inflation was checked to avoid multicollinearity in multi-predictor models. Specifically, we computed variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all covariates in Model 1 through Model 3, and none exceeded conventional thresholds (VIF < 5), suggesting that multicollinearity was not a significant concern. Given the large number of EEG and sleep-related variables examined, we controlled for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini–Hochberg). All p values reported for EEG micro-architecture features and regression analyses represent FDR-adjusted values unless otherwise specified.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings under alternative model specifications and operational definitions. To evaluate whether the observed associations were influenced by antidepressant medication use, we conducted sensitivity analyses by additionally adjusting for medication status in Model 3. This adjustment was applied to both logistic regression models predicting SPI status and linear regression models predicting sleep misperception (minutes). All EEG-derived markers were reanalyzed under this extended model, and results are presented in Appendix E.

To evaluate the robustness of our SPI classification, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis comparing state-based and trait-based measures of sleep misperception. State-based misperception was defined using post-PSG subjective total sleep time (TST), as described in the primary analysis. Trait-based subjective sleep duration was obtained from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; Question #4), reflecting average nightly sleep duration over the preceding month. Trait-based sleep misperception was calculated as the difference between PSQI-reported TST and objective PSG-derived TST. Participants were then reclassified into a trait-based SPI group (Trait-SPI) using the same ≥ 60-minute underestimation threshold applied in the primary (state-based) definition. To assess concordance between these two operationalizations, we computed Spearman’s rank correlation for continuous misperception measures and constructed a 2 × 2 reclassification matrix. In addition, classification flow was visualized using an alluvial diagram to illustrate transitions between state- and trait-based SPI categories.
