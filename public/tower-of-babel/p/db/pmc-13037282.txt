m6A-mediated stabilization of Bhlhe22 suppresses osteogenesis in osteoporosis by activating the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling axis
Authors: Qiaonan Ye, Qianke Tao, Xuemei Long, Fangzhi Lou, Jingxiang Li, Xiaobing Li, Dan Tan, Jingang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaired osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) contributes to osteoporosis (OP) pathogenesis. While RNA modifications such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) regulate stem cell differentiation, their role in OP remains unclear. Transcriptomic analysis revealed upregulated basic helix-loop-helix family member e22 (Bhlhe22) in OP-BMSCs. This study aimed to investigate whether dysregulated m6A modification contributes to OP by upregulating Bhlhe22, thereby impairing the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.MethodsFemale Sprague-Dawley rats were subjected to ovariectomy (OVX) to establish an OP model. BMSCs were isolated, characterized by flow cytometry and trilineage differentiation, and subjected to osteogenic induction. Bhlhe22 expression was modulated via lentiviral overexpression, siRNA-mediated knockdown, and adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated shRNA knockdown in vivo. Gene expression was assessed by immunofluorescence, quantitative PCR (qPCR), western blotting, and RNA sequencing. Osteogenic differentiation was evaluated via alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alizarin red S (ARS) staining, and osteogenic marker expression. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and histology were used to assess bone structure. BHLHE22 repressed osteogenesis by directly transactivating phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 3 (Pik3r3), activating the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β pathway. Crucially, m6A modification at position 2023 stabilized Bhlhe22 mRNA, increasing its expression. Mutation of this m6A site reduced the inhibitory effect of Bhlhe22.Conclusionsm6A modification stabilizes Bhlhe22 mRNA, leading to its overexpression in OP. Elevated BHLHE22 protein suppresses BMSC osteogenesis by transcriptionally activating Pik3r3 and the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling axis. Background

Osteoporosis (OP), a prevalent skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deteriorated microarchitecture, poses a significant global health burden, particularly in aging populations, as it leads to increased fracture risk and morbidity [1–4]. The key pathogenic mechanism underlying OP involves the dysregulation of bone remodeling, which is characterized primarily by impaired bone formation [5–7]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), the progenitors of osteoblasts, are central to bone formation and homeostasis [8, 9]. Impairment in the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs is increasingly recognized as a fundamental contributor to the diminished bone formation observed in OP [10–12].

Accumulating evidence points to profound epigenetic dysregulation as a key contributor to the defective osteogenic potential of BMSCs in OP [13–15]. Epigenetic regulation, encompassing DNA methylation, histone modifications, and RNA modifications, represents a crucial layer controlling gene expression programs during stem cell differentiation, including BMSC osteogenesis [16–18]. Notably, among RNA modifications, N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant internal mRNA modification in eukaryotes, dynamically regulates mRNA processes (splicing, stability, translation, and decay), playing a vital role in biological processes such as stem cell self-renewal and lineage commitment [19–28]. Dysregulation of m6A modifiers—writers, erasers, and readers—has been implicated in several diseases, including bone metabolic disorders such as OP [29–32]. However, the specific mechanisms by which m6A-mediated posttranscriptional regulation governs key osteogenic transcriptional regulators and thereby impairs BMSC osteogenesis in OP remain undefined.

Transcriptomic analyses in our study revealed a significant upregulation of basic helix-loop-helix family member e22 (Bhlhe22) in BMSCs isolated from osteoporotic rats (OP-BMSCs) compared with those from controls. BHLHE22, a transcription factor belonging to the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family, is implicated in diverse physiological and pathological processes, including neurogenesis, immune responses, and cancer progression [33, 34]. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that Bhlhe22 may influence bone metabolism through an immunosuppressive mechanism [35]. Furthermore, recent research has indicated that Bhlhe22 regulates the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells [36]. Hypermethylation of the Bhlhe22 gene promoter or gene body has been associated with increased proliferation and migration of endometrial cancer cells [34]. While direct evidence connecting Bhlhe22 to m6A modification is currently limited, it is well-established that multiple bHLH transcription factors are modulated by m6A-mediated posttranscriptional regulation [37]. Given its significant overexpression in OP-BMSCs and the recognized role of dysregulated posttranscriptional modifications in OP, we aimed to investigate whether Bhlhe22 acts as a negative regulator of BMSC osteogenesis, potentially under the control of m6A-dependent mechanisms.

To mechanistically dissect this pathway, our experimental approach was designed to first confirm the functional role of Bhlhe22 in inhibiting BMSC osteogenesis through gain- and loss-of-function studies. Subsequently, to validate the physiological relevance and therapeutic potential of our findings, we employed an in vivo intervention using adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated knockdown of Bhlhe22 specifically in the bone marrow cavity and assessed its impact on bone formation and OP prevention in our rat model. Then, aiming to delineate the downstream signaling pathway through which BHLHE22 exerts its inhibitory effect, we focused on the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β axis based on preliminary data. Finally, we sought to identify whether its dysregulated expression is governed by an m6A-mediated mechanism, utilizing methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP), RNA stability assays, and site-directed mutagenesis of the identified m6A site.

The experimental objectives required an animal model that faithfully recapitulates the key features of human postmenopausal osteoporosis, particularly the imbalance in bone remodeling leading to low bone mass and deteriorated microarchitecture. The Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat ovariectomy (OVX) model is the most extensively validated and widely accepted preclinical model for this purpose [38, 39]. OVX in SD rats induces an estrogen-deficient state that leads to accelerated bone turnover with resorption outpacing formation, resulting in significant bone loss, trabecular microstructure deterioration, and reduced bone strength over time—pathological changes that closely mirror the progression of human postmenopausal osteoporosis. The use of female SD rats is therefore highly appropriate as their hormonal response and bone metabolism are relevant to human biology. Furthermore, the relatively large size of rats, compared to mice, facilitates procedures such as serial blood sampling, bone marrow extraction, and local intra-bone marrow AAV injection, which were critical for our mechanistic and therapeutic investigations.

In this study, we demonstrated that Bhlhe22 functions as a potential suppressor of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs. Mechanistically, we revealed that m6A modification at a specific site on the Bhlhe22 mRNA enhances its stability, leading to dysregulated accumulation in OP-BMSCs. Elevated BHLHE22 protein levels subsequently target phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 3 (Pik3r3), thereby activating the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling pathway to inhibit osteogenesis. Critically, mutation of this specific m6A site reduces the ability of Bhlhe22 to inhibit osteogenesis. Furthermore, we provide in vivo evidence that adeno-associated virus (AAV) mediated knockdown of Bhlhe22 in the bone marrow of osteoporotic rats effectively prevents osteoporosis, accompanied by enhanced markers of bone formation, underscoring its therapeutic potential. This work identifies a novel m6A-Bhlhe22-PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis as a critical epigenetic mechanism underlying impaired BMSC osteogenesis in the context of osteoporosis.

Methods

Experimental design and sample size

A study protocol detailing the research question, experimental design, and analytical plan was prepared prior to the commencement of the study as part of the funding approval process but was not publicly registered. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were pre-established: only rats with successfully induced OP (confirmed via micro-CT) were included; those that failed were excluded. Sample sizes (n) for each group are indicated in the figure legends. The study started with 17 CON rats and 19 successfully modeled OP rats. These animals were allocated to specific experimental cohorts as follows: model verification and initial differential gene screening utilized femurs from 5 CON and 5 OP rats; preliminary gene expression profiling, early-stage osteogenic differentiation, and methylation analysis used 3 CON and 3 OP rats; 3 CON rats were allocated for overexpression experiments and 3 OP rats for siRNA knockdown experiments; therapeutic efficacy testing in vivo involved 5 OP rats; an additional 3 OP rats were used for pathway rescue experiments, 3 CON rats for ChIP assays, and 3 CON rats for m6A mechanism investigation.

The primary outcome measure for this hypothesis-testing study was bone microarchitecture parameters assessed by micro-CT analysis in the rat osteoporosis model; secondary outcome measures included histological bone morphology (H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining), mRNA and protein expression levels of osteogenic markers (Runx2, Osx, Opn), and Bhlhe22, as well as in vitro assessments of BMSC differentiation (ALP and ARS staining), RNA stability, and other related measures. The experimenters performing animal allocation and interventions were not blinded to group assignment, whereas outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to this information. Randomisation was used to allocate animals to control and treatment groups. To minimise potential confounders, the order of treatments and measurements, as well as animal cage locations, were systematically varied across groups.

Establishment of the OP model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest Medical University (20230703-016). Eight-week-old (adult developmental stage) female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, weighing 200–220 g, were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Southwest Medical University and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 24 ± 2℃ and 55 ± 5% relative humidity. The rats were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg body weight). Animals were randomly assigned to either sham surgery (n = 5) or ovariectomy (OVX; n = 5) group. The rats in the OVX group underwent bilateral ovariectomy, whereas those in the sham group received a sham surgical procedure involving laparotomy without ovary removal.

Cell culture

BMSCs were isolated from rat bone marrow by flushing with α-MEM (HyClone, Pittsburgh, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, Pittsburgh, USA). The cells were cultured at 37℃ in 5% CO₂ and passaged at 80% confluence. Passage 3 (P3) cells were used for flow cytometry analysis of the following surface markers: anti-CD14, anti-CD29, anti-CD31, anti-CD45, anti-CD90, and anti-CD105, with isotype controls for background correction.

Multilineage differentiations

Osteogenic differentiation: BMSCs were cultured in osteogenic medium (PD-008, Pricella) for 14 days, and then stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS) to visualize mineralized nodules. Adipogenic differentiation: Cells were induced in adipogenic medium (PD-010, Pricella) for 3 days, followed by maintenance medium for 1 day, cycles of 3–5 cycles, and Oil Red O staining for lipid droplet detection. Chondrogenic differentiation: BMSC pellets were cultured in chondrogenic medium (PD-009, Pricella) for 21 days, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with alcian blue to assess proteoglycan deposition.

Alkaline phosphatase and alizarin red staining

BMSCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (4 °C, 20 min). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, indicating early osteogenesis, was assessed via a commercial kit (Beyotime, C3250S) with staining performed at 37℃ for 10 min. Late-stage osteogenesis, characterized by mineralized nodule formation, was evaluated via ARS staining at room temperature for 5 min. Images of ALP-stained and ARS-stained mineralized nodules were acquired via an inverted light microscope.

Transfection and viral infection

Transient transfections were performed via the riboFECT™ CP Transfection Kit (RiboBio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with the oligonucleotide sequences listed in Table 1. Lentivirus constructs for genes overexpression were generated by Heyuan Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Table 2). Lentiviral transduction was initiated when the cells reached approximately 30% confluency. The MOI was determined 48 h post-transduction by assessing cell morphology and fluorescence intensity via fluorescence microscopy. The transduction efficiency was confirmed 48 h post-transfection via quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis.

Western blot analysis

BMSCs were lysed via a cell protein extraction reagent (KEYGEN Biotech, Nanjing, China). The lysate was mixed with loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v; lysate: buffer) and boiled for 10 min, after which the proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoretic transfer onto a polyvinylidenedifluoride (PVDF) membrane, the membrane was blocked with blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies (Table 4). The next day, after washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) was performed in BMSCs overexpressing 3×Flag-tagged Bhlhe22. The cells were crosslinked, and the chromatin was sheared prior to immunoprecipitation with an anti-Flag antibody (Sigma, F1804). The immunoprecipitated DNA was subsequently sequenced by SeqHealth Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). ChIP-qPCR validation was conducted via primers targeting the Pik3r3 (forward: 5′-GGGTCTTGCTAAGTATCCTCGT-3′; reverse: 5′-GTGCCCCACACCTCTAATCC-3′). IgG served as the negative control.

m6A MeRIP-qPCR assays

m6A-modified RNA fragments were enriched via the EpiQuik™ CUT&RUN m6A RNA Enrichment Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Putative m6A sites were predicted via the SRAMP (http://www.cuilab.cn/m6asiteapp/old) web tool. cDNA was synthesized with HiScript® III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper), followed by quantification of target transcripts via ChamQ™ Universal SYBR® qPCR Master Mix and gene-specific primers (Table 5).

RNA m6A dot blot assays

Two micrograms of total RNA were denatured at 95℃ for 3 min and UV-crosslinked to a nitrocellulose membrane via a crosslinker (1200 mJ, 50 s × 2 cycles), followed by immunodetection with an m6A-specific antibody (Synaptic Systems). Dot blot signals were quantified via ImageJ software.

RNA stability assays

To reduce m6A levels globally, BMSCs were treated with cycloleucine (500 µg/ml) for 48 h [40]. To assess RNA stability, these m6A-inhibited BMSCs were subsequently treated with actinomycin D (ActD, 5 µg/ml) for 0, 4, or 8 h [41], followed by total RNA isolation. The relative level of Bhlhe22 mRNA was then quantified via qPCR, normalized to Gapdh and analyzed via the decay constant (t1/2) to assess RNA stability.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Plasmids encoding wild-type Bhlhe22 or Bhlhe22 mutants (A-to-T substitutions at predicted m6A sites) were constructed (Fig. 7I), generating three mutant variants: Mut1172, Mut2023, and Mut1172&2023. m6A-depleted BMSCs were cotransfected with firefly luciferase reporter plasmids containing the Bhlhe22 3’UTR, Renilla luciferase control plasmids, and either wild-type or mutant Bhlhe22 cDNA plasmids via Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At 24 h post-transfection, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured via a dual-luciferase reporter assay system, and the Renilla-to-firefly luciferase activity ratio was calculated for normalization [42].

Animal experiments

Four weeks after establishing osteoporosis via bilateral ovariectomy in SD rats, adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) encoding either nontargeting control shRNA (shRNA-NC; pAAV-U6-shRNA (NC)-CMV-MCS-WPRE) or Bhlhe22-targeting shRNA (shRNA-Bhlhe22; pAAV-U6-shRNA (Bhlhe22)-CMV-MCS-WPRE) were injected into the contralateral femoral bone marrow cavities (shRNA-NC in the right femur vs. shRNA-Bhlhe22 in the left femur, or vice versa) [43]. Eight weeks post-injection, the rats were euthanized by deep anesthesia with isoflurane (RWD; R510-22-10), followed by bilateral thoracotomy. Then femurs were harvested for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and Masson’s trichrome staining to evaluate the effects of Bhlhe22 knockdown on osteoporotic progression. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Micro-CT analysis

The femoral samples were scanned using a SCANCO µCT 50 scanner (SCANCO Medical AG, Switzerland). Prior to scanning, each sample was secured in a dedicated specimen holder to ensure the region of interest was centered within the scanning field of view. Scanning was performed at a tube voltage of 70 kV and a tube current of 114 µA, with an integration time of 300 ms per projection and 1000 projections acquired over 180°. A 0.5 mm aluminum filter was applied to reduce beam-hardening artifacts. The acquired two-dimensional projection data were reconstructed using µCT Ray software. Reconstruction was conducted with a standard filtered back-projection algorithm to generate isotropic three-dimensional images with a voxel size of 10.0 μm.

For trabecular bone morphometric analysis, the distal growth plate was used as an anatomical landmark. The region of interest (ROI) was defined as a cubic volume located 1.0 mm distal to the growth plate and extending 2.0 mm along the longitudinal axis of the diaphysis. Bone and non-bone tissues were segmented using a global thresholding method in the SCANCO image analysis software (µCT Evaluation Program), with a segmentation threshold of 220. Within this ROI, the following trabecular microstructure parameters were automatically calculated and exported: bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Histological analysis

Bone specimens from the treatment groups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA for 4 weeks, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3 μm thickness. Sections were stained with H&E and Masson’s trichrome to assess tissue morphology and structural components, respectively. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining was performed on bone sections with an anti-BHLHE22 antibody (Invitrogen). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining was conducted to evaluate the expression of RUNX2 and OPN.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 9.1.1. Unpaired or paired Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups. Comparisons among three or more groups utilized one-way or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate, followed by post hoc tests. Prior to these analyses, the assumptions of each statistical test were assessed. For t-tests and ANOVA, normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. In cases where the data did not meet the assumption of normality, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (for unpaired comparisons) or Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for paired comparisons) was employed instead. For ANOVA comparisons with violated variance homogeneity, Welch’s correction was applied. The data are presented as the means ± SDs. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Impaired osteogenesis in OP-BMSCs is associated with altered m6A methylation

An osteoporosis (OP) rat model was established via bilateral ovariectomy (OVX). Micro-CT analysis revealed fewer bone trabeculae in the OVX group than in the sham group (Fig. S1A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significantly lower trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV) and trabecular number (Tb.N), along with greater trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) in OVX rats (Fig. S1A), confirming successful OP model establishment. H&E and Masson staining of femurs further demonstrated reduced trabecular bone in the bone marrow cavity of OVX rats (Fig. S1B), which was consistent with an osteoporotic phenotype. We successfully isolated and cultured BMSCs. Cultured BMSCs underwent trilineage differentiation into osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic lineages (Fig. S1C), confirming their mesenchymal stem cell identity. Flow cytometry analysis of passage 3 (P3) primary rat BMSCs revealed negative expression of CD14, CD31, and CD45, and positive expression of CD29, CD90, and CD105 (Fig. S1D), validating successful isolation and culture of BMSCs from Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat bone marrow.

In the control (CON-BMSCs) and OP groups, compared with the CON-BMSC group, the OP-BMSC group exhibited significantly impaired osteogenic differentiation, as demonstrated by qPCR and western blot analyses (Fig. 1A, B). Consistently, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining confirmed reduced mineralization and osteogenic capacity in the OP group (Fig. 1C). To investigate the role of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) in OP and osteogenesis, we assessed global m6A levels. Dot blot assays revealed decreased global m6A levels in OP-BMSCs under basal conditions, which increased upon osteogenic induction (Fig. 1D, E). Further analysis of the gene expression of key m6A regulatory enzymes showed that, compared with CON-BMSCs, the expression of the methyltransferases Methyltransferase-like 3 (Mettl3) and Methyltransferase-like 14 (Mettl14) were significantly downregulated in OP-BMSCs, while the expression of the demethylases Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (Fto) and AlkB homolog 5 (Alkbh5) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 1F).

Bhlhe22 expression is elevated in both the femurs and BMSCs of osteoporotic rats

RNA sequencing identified differentially expressed genes in OP-BMSCs (Fig. 2A). Among these genes, Bhlhe22 emerged as a gene of interest due to its marked upregulation in OP-BMSCs compared with controls (Fig. 2B). This increase was corroborated in the femurs of OP rats (Fig. 2C). Further confirmation via qPCR confirmed significantly increased Bhlhe22 mRNA levels in OP-BMSCs (Fig. 2D), whereas western blotting revealed a 1.5- to -2.0-fold increase in BHLHE22 protein levels (Fig. 2E). Following the osteogenic induction of BMSCs, both the mRNA and protein levels of Bhlhe22 were downregulated in both groups; however, its expression remained persistently higher in OP-BMSCs than in CON-BMSCs throughout the osteogenic differentiation process (Fig. 2F, G). This pattern not only suggests an inverse relationship between Bhlhe22 expression and osteogenic differentiation, but also further confirms its sustained overexpression in the osteoporotic context. Taken together, the persistent overexpression of Bhlhe22 across multiple experimental modalities, spanning transcriptomic screening, tissue validation, mRNA quantification, and protein analysis, alongside its suppression during osteogenic differentiation, underscores its potential functional significance in osteoporosis pathogenesis. These findings suggest that Bhlhe22 is a competing candidate for further mechanistic investigations into bone homeostasis dysregulation.

Bhlhe22 modulates the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs

To investigate the role of Bhlhe22 in the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we established a Bhlhe22 overexpression (OE) system via lentiviral transduction. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed an optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 60 for efficient gene delivery (Fig. S2A). Subsequent qPCR and western blot analyses verified that Bhlhe22 was upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels. Following stable OE of Bhlhe22, the mRNA and protein expression of osteogenic markers—Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), Osterix (Osx), and Osteopontin (Opn)—were significantly downregulated (Fig. S2B, C). Consistent with these findings, ALP and ARS staining revealed reduced alkaline phosphatase activity and mineralized matrix deposition, respectively, indicating impaired osteogenic differentiation (Fig. S2D). Conversely, we generated Bhlhe22-knockdown BMSCs via siRNA. Efficient knockdown was confirmed by qPCR and western blotting (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast to the OE results, Bhlhe22 depletion markedly enhanced osteogenic differentiation, as evidenced by elevated expression of Runx2, Osx, and Opn (Fig. 3A, B) and increased ALP/ARS staining (Fig. 3C, D). Among the three siRNAs tested, siRNA-Bhlhe22-2 demonstrated the strongest knockdown efficiency and osteogenic promotion. Therefore, this sequence was selected for animal experiments and subsequent pathway validation. Together, these data demonstrate that Bhlhe22 negatively regulates the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

Reduced Bhlhe22 expression enhances osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs by suppressing PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling pathway activity

The present study has established (as shown in Figs. 3 and 4) the pivotal role of Bhlhe22 in inhibiting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and bone formation. Next, the underlying molecular mechanism was determined. RNA sequencing analysis was performed to compare BMSCs transfected with Bhlhe22-targeting siRNA (siRNA-Bhlhe22) with those transfected with a nontargeting control siRNA (siRNA-NC). Bioinformatic analysis of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways revealed significant enrichment of DEGs specifically within the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 5A, B). To validate this finding, the protein expression levels of key pathway components were measured via western blotting. This analysis confirmed decreased phosphorylation of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (p-PI3K) and protein kinase B (p-AKT), along with increased phosphorylation of glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (p-GSK3β), in the siRNA-Bhlhe22 group compared with the siRNA-NC group, indicating reduced activity of the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β pathway upon Bhlhe22 knockdown. Treatment with the PI3K activator recilisib (10 µM; Fig. S3A) effectively reversed the reduction in the phosphorylation levels of these pathway components induced by siRNA-Bhlhe22 (Fig. 5C). qPCR analysis revealed that Bhlhe22 knockdown significantly increased the mRNA expression of the osteogenic markers Runx2, Osx, and Opn. Crucially, this increase was alleviated by recilisib treatment (Fig. 5D). These findings were corroborated at the protein level for RUNX2, OSX, and OPN (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, functional assays for osteogenic differentiation, namely ALP and ARS staining, demonstrated enhanced mineralization upon Bhlhe22 knockdown, which was similarly suppressed by the PI3K-Akt pathway activator recilisib (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these data indicate that Bhlhe22 knockdown suppresses the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling pathway while promoting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

BHLHE22 directly regulates Pik3r3 to modulate the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling axis

To identify direct transcriptional targets of BHLHE22 that mediate its functional effects, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq). Compared with those of the input controls, the enrichment peaks for BHLHE22 binding were readily detectable genome wide (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, global RNA sequencing analysis revealed significant gene expression changes in the OE Bhlhe22 BMSCs compared with the negative control (NC) BMSCs (Fig. 6B). Integration of these datasets identified 154 genes that were both bound by BHLHE22 via ChIP-seq and significantly upregulated in OE Bhlhe22 BMSCs (Fig. 6C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these 154 overlapping genes revealed highly significant enrichment of PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 6D). GSEA revealed that elevated Bhlhe22 in OP-BMSCs may inhibit osteogenesis by activating the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. S3B). Several key components of this pathway were among the overlapping DEGs (Fig. 6E). Examination of the ChIP-seq profiles specifically at the phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 3 (Pik3r3) genomic locus revealed pronounced BHLHE22 binding enrichment (Fig. 6F). Pik3r3 encodes the p55γ regulatory subunit of PI3K. We validated this direct binding to the Pik3r3 via ChIP-PCR. Compared with the IgG control, BHLHE22 exhibited significantly enriched binding (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these results indicate that BHLHE22 transcriptionally regulates the PI3K-Akt signaling, at least in part, through the direct targeting and upregulation of Pik3r3.

m6A-mediated upregulation of Bhlhe22 impairs the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs

Although the role of Bhlhe22 in the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs has been established, the mechanism underlying its aberrant expression in OP remains unknown. Given the differences in global m6A methylation levels between OP- and CON-BMSCs, we investigated whether m6A modification regulates the stability and expression of target mRNAs. To identify potential regulatory sites, we predicted Bhlhe22 mRNA m6A modification sites via the SRAMP website (http://www.cuilab.cn/m6asiteapp/old), which revealed four high-confidence sites at nucleotide positions 1172, 1600, 2023, and 2047 (Fig. 7A, B). Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)-qPCR validation revealed overall elevated m6A levels in OP-BMSCs, with significant increases specifically at sites 1172 and 2023 (Fig. 7C), indicating a potential role for Bhlhe22 m6A modification in OP pathogenesis.

To further validate the specific regulatory role of m6A modification on Bhlhe22 expression, we employed a genetic approach by knocking down the core m6A methyltransferases Mettl3 and Mettl14 (Fig. S3C, D). Consistent with the pharmacological effect observed with the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) synthesis inhibitor cycloleucine (Fig. 7D-F), which broadly suppresses SAM biosynthesis and consequently inhibits the catalytic activity of the METTL3/14 complex, the knockdown of the Mettl3/Mettl14 complex in BMSCs specifically reduced the m6A modification level (Fig. 7G). This intervention resulted in significant suppression of both Bhlhe22 mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 7H, I). This suppressive effect occurred at the post-transcriptional level by reducing mRNA stability, indicating that m6A modification normally enhances the stability of Bhlhe22 transcripts (Fig. 7J). To further explore the mechanism underlying m6A-mediated transcriptional regulation of Bhlhe22, we conducted luciferase reporter assays coupled with mutagenesis. Compared with control BMSCs, cycloleucine-pretreated BMSCs presented substantial decreases in luciferase activity (Fig. 7K). However, this decrease was not observed in the BMSCs transfected with a plasmid bearing a mutation at the 2023 site (Mut2023 plasmid). These data suggest that the m6A modification site at position 2023 is essential for regulating Bhlhe22 expression.

Finally, transduction of BMSCs with lentiviral vectors overexpressing wild-type (OE WT) Bhlhe22 cDNA inhibited osteogenesis. Importantly, this inhibition was partially rescued by Mut2023 (OE Mut) lentivirus (Fig. 8A-C). Collectively, these findings establish that m6A modification at nucleotide 2023 of Bhlhe22 mRNA enhances its stability and expression, thereby suppressing BMSC osteogenesis in the context of osteoporosis.

Discussion

This study revealed an epigenetic axis that governs BMSC osteogenic dysfunction in OP. We identified Bhlhe22 as a potential suppressor of osteogenesis, whose dysregulation in OP-BMSCs is mechanistically driven by m6A-mediated mRNA stabilization, ultimately impairing bone formation via activation of the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling axis. Our findings provide novel insights into RNA modification-directed control of bone homeostasis and offer a promising therapeutic target for OP intervention.

Accumulating evidence highlights epigenetic dysregulation as a cornerstone of OP pathogenesis, particularly involving impaired BMSC differentiation [44]. While DNA and histone modifications have been extensively studied, the role of RNA epitranscriptomic marks, especially m6A, in OP remains less defined [45–47]. Our data demonstrate that global m6A levels are dynamically altered in OP-BMSCs and decrease under basal conditions but increase upon osteogenic induction. It is crucial to emphasize that the impact of m6A modification on osteogenic differentiation exhibits high target specificity and functional complexity. While numerous studies indicate that elevated global m6A levels can promote osteogenesis [48–51], this study reveals that for certain osteogenesis-disrupting factors (e.g., Bhlhe22) increased m6A modification leads to inhibitory functional outcomes. This finding aligns with reports implicating m6A writers (METTL3/14) and erasers (FTO/ALKBH5) in skeletal development and OP [48–51], and is consistent with our observed upregulation of both Bhlhe22 expression and its m6A modification level in OP. However, the specific mRNA targets through which m6A affects OP-BMSCs are largely unknown. Our transcriptomic profiling revealed Bhlhe22 as the most significantly upregulated transcription factor in OP-BMSCs, a finding validated in vivo in OP rat femurs.

Functional studies established Bhlhe22 as a potential inhibitor of BMSC osteogenesis. The knockdown of Bhlhe22 restored the osteogenic capacity of OP-BMSCs, whereas its overexpression suppressed the differentiation of OP-BMSCs. This finding expands the known roles of Bhlhe22 beyond neurogenesis and cancer [52, 53], revealing a direct function in skeletal biology. Mechanistically, we linked its overexpression to aberrant activation of the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β pathway. This pathway is a known regulator of osteoblastogenesis [54, 55]. Constitutive PI3K-Akt activation promotes bone formation or reduces bone loss through mechanisms that paradoxically involve the suppression of osteogenic differentiation, promotion of adipogenic commitment, and/or enhancement of osteoclast activity [56–59]. Our data show that Bhlhe22 knockdown suppresses PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling directly by targeting Pik3r3 (a PI3K regulatory subunit), and critically, pharmacological inhibition of PI3K reduced the pro-osteogenic effects of Bhlhe22 knockdown, establishing this pathway as the primary downstream effector.

This study’s most significant advance lies in revealing the epitranscriptomic regulation of Bhlhe22 by m6A. We identified a specific m6A site (position 2023) within the Bhlhe22 mRNA that is hypermodified in OP-BMSCs. This modification enhances mRNA stability leading to BHLHE22 protein accumulation. Mutation of this site (Mut2023) alleviated both the m6A-dependent stabilization of Bhlhe22 and its inhibitory effect on osteogenesis, demonstrating the functional necessity of this precise modification. This may add BHLHE22 to the growing list of transcription factors (e.g., RUNX2 and OSX) regulated by m6A in bone [60, 61], uniquely positioning it as an upstream suppressor controlled by site-specific mRNA methylation. We recognize that the osteoporotic bone marrow niche involves a complex interplay of signals, including inflammation and cellular senescence, which may also influence BMSC function [62, 63]. While the present study establishes m6A-mediated stabilization of Bhlhe22 as a distinct and causally important regulatory mechanism, future work investigating its interaction with these broader pathological cues will provide a more comprehensive understanding of OP pathogenesis.

Therapeutic relevance was demonstrated in vivo: AAV-mediated Bhlhe22 knockdown in the bone marrow of OP rats significantly reversed trabecular bone loss and improved microarchitecture, suggesting its potential for preventing OP. This approach avoids broad manipulation of the m6A machinery, which could have pleiotropic effects, by focusing on a specific pathogenic effector downstream of the epitranscriptomic mark.

Although our study established the m6A-Bhlhe22-PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, Bhlhe22 is not the sole relevant target gene. RNA-seq revealed numerous differentially expressed genes, thus comprehensively elucidating the functions of these genes in BMSCs will deepen our understanding of OP pathogenesis. Furthermore, despite its low baseline expression in BMSCs, Bhlhe22 significantly inhibits osteogenic differentiation, indicating that bone tissue is particularly sensitive to its dysregulation. This sensitivity may stem from Bhlhe22’s ability to amplify downstream inhibitory effects through activating the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β pathway. Future studies could explore its pathological significance as a “threshold sensor”.

Additionally, while our current findings are derived from rat models and in vitro-cultured BMSCs, the conservation of both m6A modification machinery and the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling pathway across mammals, including humans, suggests a strong potential for translational relevance. The Bhlhe22 gene is also evolutionarily conserved, and its regulatory role in osteogenesis is likely to extend to human biology. However, further validation using human BMSCs, or clinical samples will be essential to confirm the generalizability of this axis in human osteoporosis. Species-specific differences in gene expression patterns, metabolic rates, or bone remodeling dynamics may influence the exact role and weight of Bhlhe22 in the disease process. Therefore, future investigations should prioritize examining Bhlhe22 expression and m6A modification status in BMSCs from OP patients to solidify its pathophysiological and therapeutic relevance in humans.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we elucidated a novel regulatory axis in which site-specific m6A modification stabilizes Bhlhe22 mRNA, leading to its accumulation in OP-BMSCs. Elevated BHLHE22 protein represses osteogenesis by targeting Pik3r3 and thereby activating PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling. Disrupting this axis—via m6A site mutagenesis or Bhlhe22 depletion—rescues osteogenic defects. This work advances our understanding of epigenetic dysregulation in osteoporosis and identifies Bhlhe22 as a promising therapeutic target.
