Intraoperative evaluation of median arcuate ligament syndrome using a transit-time flowmeter: a case report
Authors: Daisuke Koike, Hiroyuki Kato, Masahiro Shimura, Kazuma Horiguchi, Hiroki Tani, Yoshiki Kunimura, Takahiko Higashiguchi, Toki Kawai, Hironobu Yasuoka, Takayuki Ochi, Takahiro Tashiro, Yukio Asano, Masahiro Ito, Yutaro Kato, Tsunekazu Hanai, Akihiko Horiguchi

Background

Median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS) is a rare surgical condition caused by external compression of the celiac artery by a fibrous band, leading to malperfusion of the upper abdominal organs and symptoms including abdominal pain, weight loss, nausea, and rarely, intra-abdominal hemorrhage [1, 2]. This malperfusion results from the stenosis of the celiac axis (CA), with compensatory blood supply from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) to the organs normally perfused by the CA. This increased blood flow can also lead to the formation of a pseudoaneurysm in the pancreatico-duodenal region [3]. Diagnosis of MALS is made based on clinical symptoms and radiological findings, such as CA stenosis (hook sign) observed via enhanced computed tomography (CT), as well as high blood flow velocity due to stenosis seen with Doppler ultrasonography (US) [4–6].

Surgical intervention is the first-line therapeutic option, which involves dissection of the fibrous band compressing the CA [7]. Intraoperative surgical success is typically evaluated based on visual findings alone, except in some case series that used ICG fluorescence imaging [8]. However, given reported surgical failure rates of up to 20%, novel intraoperative evaluation methods are urgently needed [2, 9].

Herein, we present a case of MALS surgery, using an intraoperative ultrasonic blood flowmeter to evaluate surgical success. Flowmeters, which are widely used in various surgical procedures, have been implemented in gastroenterological surgeries to evaluate common hepatic artery (CHA) blood flow [10]. This report aims to demonstrate the utility of the blood flowmeter as an intraoperative evaluation tool for MALS surgery.

Case presentation

Preoperative evaluation and imaging

A 74-year-old man was referred to our hospital with a diagnosis of early gastric cancer. His medical history included diabetes, hypertension, and stable angina pectoris. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed early gastric cancer in the distal stomach. Enhanced CT showed no evidence of metastasis from the gastric cancer. It revealed severe compression at the root of the CA, demonstrating the characteristic hook sign of MALS with a minimum luminal diameter of 1.8 mm (Fig. 1). A mild increase in the caliber of the artery in the pancreatic arcade from the SMA indicated that the SMA was compensating for blood flow to the CA perfusion area. The angle between the superior border of the pancreas and the origin of the CA was sharp (33 degrees), indicating a technically challenging surgical approach. There was no evidence of an aneurysm in the pancreatico-duodenal region. Abdominal US failed to detect CA compression due to enteric gas in the upper abdominal region. The diagnosis was MALS with gastric cancer.

The transit-time flowmeter (TTFM)

The TTFM is an intraoperative device that measures real-time blood flow using ultrasound transit-time differences between upstream and downstream transducers. It provides quantitative, non-invasive assessment, unaffected by flow profile or vessel diameter within the calibrated range and has been validated in cardiovascular and transplant surgery with high reproducibility and minimal operator dependence. The device provides flow measurements with minimal offset error and low observer variability due to its automated measurement principle. For each measurement in this case, the device automatically performed calibration and zero adjustment at startup.

Surgical technique and intraoperative findings

Based on these findings, we planned laparoscopic surgery for both gastric cancer and MALS. After gastrectomy, the procedure for MALS was initiated. First, the CHA was dissected away from the pancreas within the anatomical triangle formed by the CHA, gastroduodenal artery, and the superior border of the pancreatic head. It was then encircled with a vessel loop to allow atraumatic handling (Fig. 2). This step took only several minutes because the CHA was already exposed during lymph node dissection for gastric cancer. A Transonic Vascular Flow probe (6 mm) with Optima Flow-QC monitor (Transonic Systems Inc., Ithaca, NY, USA), which operates as a TTFM, was used to evaluate CHA blood flow. The flowmeter probe has a hook-shaped configuration, allowing dorsal access to the artery; it was inserted through the port site after removal of the 12-mm trocar and was positioned around the CHA using vessel loop manipulation. Flow was measured in real time, and the final value was recorded after both the waveform and readings stabilized over several seconds. The initial measurement revealed a mean flow of −119 mL/min. To reduce the measurement error, the vessel loop was released after the probe was introduced and saline was applied to eliminate air interference. This confirmed retrograde flow within the CHA, indicating that the celiac territory was being perfused via collateral circulation from the SMA through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade (Fig. 3). “Retrograde flow” in this context refers to arterial flow running in the opposite direction to the normal physiological flow from the celiac artery toward its branches. In contrast, “antegrade flow” indicates arterial flow proceeding in the expected physiological direction. This reversed flow pattern, along with the markedly reduced CHA flow value (–119 mL/min), confirmed a functional obstruction at the celiac origin. These findings, in combination with preoperative imaging, guided the decision to proceed with MAL dissection. Decompression of the MALS was then initiated. The crura of the diaphragm were cut, and the frontal surface of the aorta was exposed. An additional port was placed in the epigastric area, as manipulation and visualization of this region were difficult due to the sharp angle between the upper pancreas and the root of the CA. The CA was exposed from the periphery to the root, and the median arcuate ligament (MAL) was dissected (Fig. 2). After the MAL was dissected, CA stenosis was resolved, as confirmed by surgical findings. Re-evaluation of the CHA showed antegrade blood flow, with a mean flow of 317 mL/min (Fig. 3).

Postoperative course and follow-up

The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged on postoperative day 21. Postoperative CT showed enlargement of the CA; however, the root lumen diameter remained smaller than that of the midsection of the CHA (Fig. 4). CT at 6 months confirmed preservation of the CHA diameter, which had expanded to 6.7 mm at its narrowest point, and no additional symptoms were observed during the 9-month follow-up period. CT imaging and clinical consultation will be performed every 6–12 months for ongoing follow-up of both MALS and gastric cancer.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of MALS surgery in which a TTFM (Transonic Systems) was used to provide real-time, quantitative assessment of blood flow in the CHA and to evaluate intraoperative surgical success. The flowmeter allowed us to assess retrograde blood flow, indicating malperfusion in the CA region, which enhanced the diagnosis of MALS. After dissection of the MAL, the TTFM clearly demonstrated the return of antegrade blood flow in the CHA, confirming the surgical success of MALS surgery with relief from the compression caused by the MAL.

In this case, recovery of blood flow was confirmed intraoperatively using the TTFM. In many reported cases, the surgical success of MALS has been evaluated based on intraoperative visual findings and postoperative radiological examinations. The high surgical failure rate has been reported, which may be caused by the strategy solely relying on intraoperative visual findings without reliable and reproducible measurement [7]. The challenges also arise from the difficulty of manipulation and visualization in MALS patients with a sharp angle between the pancreas and the CA origin, which occurs in about half of MALS cases, similar to our case [11]. Intraoperative US including both intracorporeal and transabdominal use is other modalities used to assess surgical success [8, 12]. However, intraoperative US is more commonly used to confirm CA diameter enlargement rather than to evaluate flow recovery in the CHA, due to its limitation of measuring flow velocity rather than flow volume. TTFM provides an objective, quantitative volumetric flow measurement (mL/min), enabling real-time assessment of hepatic arterial perfusion and confirmation of surgical success. In contrast, Doppler US measures flow velocity (cm/s), is more operator-dependent, and is therefore less suited for direct quantitative assessment. While Doppler can be applied without vessel encirclement, TTFM offers reproducible measurements once the target vessel is exposed, with a minimal learning curve. Hybrid techniques with real-time angiography have also been reported [13], but require substantial capital investment, femoral artery puncture or cutdown, and sometimes stenting, which carries additional risk. The TTFM has demonstrated a high correlation with other measurement methods, minimal variability, and validated clinical efficacy, particularly in cardiovascular surgery [14–16], may serve as a viable intraoperative alternative to visual assessment.

The evaluation of the TTFM holds potential advantages for determining the indication for MALS surgeries. In this case, retrograde blood flow was clearly detected intraoperatively. The indication for MALS surgery has typically been based on ruling out other illnesses with upper abdominal symptoms, alongside radiological findings, however, it remains controversial [4, 6, 7, 17]. Although a reference value for CHA flow in MALS has not been established, the marked retrograde flow provided strong intraoperative evidence for diagnosis and surgical indication [18, 19]. In liver transplantation, average hepatic artery flow has been reported as 202 ± 102 mL/min in donors using a flowmeter [20], and a flow of 100 mL/min has been proposed as a threshold for acceptable outcomes in recipients [21]. In our case, the flow increased by 436 mL/min (from − 119 to + 317 mL/min) after MAL division, indicating a change from retrograde to antegrade flow. This substantial improvement in hepatic arterial perfusion suggests that integrating intraoperative observations with TTFM measurements could help guide surgical decision-making and avoid unnecessary interventions. The reversed CHA flow directly indicates organ malperfusion in the CA area, and this finding could improve diagnostic accuracy, help determine the indication for MALS surgeries, and predict the therapeutic outcome.

In our case, the hook sign was reduced on postoperative CT, but the CA root diameter did not fully enlarge compared to the midsection of the CA. This finding was also reported in other cases [22, 23]. This discrepancy between anatomical restoration and intraoperative flow normalization, suggests that relying solely on postoperative CT may lead to underestimation of the effectiveness of MAL release. Normalization of CHA perfusion may precede CA anatomical remodeling due to chronic compression effect. In our case, follow-up CT at 6 months showed minimal change in the CA morphology. The clinical significance of residual narrowing despite normalized flow remains unclear, and no direct postoperative flow measurements (e.g., TTFM or Doppler US) were obtained; therefore, the durability of intraoperative flow improvement could not be confirmed. The potential association of residual narrowing with recurrence or complications is uncertain, warranting continued long-term imaging and clinical follow-up beyond the early postoperative period.

Based on this case, we propose incorporating TTFM into an advanced surgical protocol (Fig. 5). While preoperative imaging can suggest anatomical compression, intraoperative flowmetry offers real-time functional confirmation of hemodynamic significance. This integration may help avoid unnecessary decompression in patients without impaired hepatic arterial flow. Intraoperative TTFM measurements were performed on the CHA before and after MAL division using a 6 mm probe, following a standardized approach: vessel loop release after circumferential dissection, stable hemodynamic conditions, and multiple consecutive readings with mean values recorded. Adequate flow restoration was defined as ≥ 100 mL/min or a marked improvement in waveform pattern, consistent with liver transplantation criteria. Although a single stabilized measurement was obtained at each time point in this case, multiple consecutive measurements are recommended in future cases to improve reproducibility. Inter- and intra-observer variability were not assessed in this single case; however, the device’s automated measurement principle likely minimizes operator variation. Potential sources of error in MALS surgery include vessel tortuosity caused or accentuated by encircling with a vessel loop, and changes in vessel diameter after decompression. These anatomical factors could influence the accuracy of intraoperative flow measurements, although the impact of these factors in MALS has not been systematically evaluated. Further validation of this approach in MALS-specific applications is required.

In our institution, the flowmeter and probe were already available for other surgical applications and reused without additional cost, similar to intraoperative Doppler US, which also incurs no additional cost and requires minimal training and is easy to integrate into the workflow when already available in an institution. However, in other settings including low-resource situations, equipment availability, cost-effectiveness, and maintenance may limit adoption. TTFM may vary across institutions, particularly where additional equipment purchase is required, and accessibility may be further limited in resource-limited settings due to cost and maintenance constraints. As noted above, unlike Doppler US, TTFM requires vessel encirclement for probe placement but provides quantitative volumetric flow measurement with minimal operator dependence. Contraindications include anatomical variations that prevent encirclement, adhesions or inflammatory changes around the CHA, excessive dilation of the CHA that makes the probe unfit, and significant arteriosclerosis. The procedure carries risks such as vascular or pancreatic injury, but it is occasionally performed in pancreatic and gastric surgery and can be completed within several minutes once the artery is exposed, as in previous cases [24, 25]. In non-simultaneous MALS surgery, it may require additional dissection time but generally remains minimally invasive. The accuracy of flowmeter readings can also be influenced by technical and physiological factors, and both false-positive and false-negative results have been reported in other surgical fields [26]. Although it allows straightforward use and intuitive assessment, TTFM should be complemented by other modalities, such as Doppler US or angiography, when necessary to ensure diagnostic reliability due to the lack of validation against other intraoperative flow measurement techniques. In the present case, concomitant gastrectomy, although a potential confounder of the findings, was unlikely to have materially affected the flow measurements, given the absence of hepatic arterial variants and the measurement site being distinct from the gastrectomy field.

Given the single-case design, the reliability and reproducibility of the findings are limited, the results cannot be generalized, and no control group or statistical analysis was possible. The patient was asymptomatic pre-operatively; therefore, no patient-reported outcome measures or quality-of-life assessments could be obtained, and correlation between flow values and symptom improvement was not feasible. This case did not include other intraoperative flow measurement techniques for direct comparison; intraoperative US can assess vascular morphology, but preoperative CT was considered sufficient for anatomical evaluation. Doppler US can measure respiratory variation in flow velocity, but intraoperative reference values for MALS are lacking. Quantitative correlation between flowmeter readings and visual or imaging findings was not performed, and no direct comparison with flow values from other MALS procedures is possible, as this is the first report of TTFM use in this context. Future studies are needed to establish reference values and assess reproducibility. As noted above, CHA encircling is feasible and minimally invasive when the artery is adequately exposed, although it carries certain risks. TTFM could serve as a first-line intraoperative assessment tool. Additional investigations are warranted to confirm the clinical applicability of this technique.

In this patient, who remained asymptomatic with a stable celiac axis diameter up to 9 months postoperatively, the long-term durability of symptom relief and flow improvement remain uncertain due to the lack of standardized protocols and validated CHA flow reference ranges.

Conclusion

In this case, a transit-time flowmeter (Transonic) was used to provide real-time, quantitative assessment of hepatic arterial flow during MALS surgery. This allowed for intraoperative identification of retrograde flow and confirmation of restored antegrade flow after decompression. The use of a transit-time flowmeter may serve as one of the most reliable methods for both determining the indication for surgical intervention and evaluating its success intraoperatively.
