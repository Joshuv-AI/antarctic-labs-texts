Impact of simulation-based learning on the academic performance of medical students during pediatric clerkships
Authors: Abdelaziz Elamin, Maryam Fuad Ali, Amer Almarabheh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSimulation-based learning (SBL) is a structured educational method that enables learners to practice clinical, technical, and decision-making skills in a safe, immersive, and controlled environment. It has become a cornerstone of modern medical education.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the effect of SBL on the academic performance of medical students at the pediatrics end of the clerkship examination.MethodsWe analyzed the academic performance of 2,251 fifth-year medical students over 12 academic years, from September 2012 to June 2024, at the end of the pediatric clerkship. The period includes six years before and six years after the integration of simulation-based learning into the pediatric curriculum. ResultsThe performance of the students in the three components of the end of the clerkship examination, written, clinical, and continuous assessment, after the utilization of SBL was much better than their performance in the era before the SBL. The difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe study highlights the positive impact of incorporating SBL into pediatric education for medical students in Bahrain. These findings support the wider integration of simulation as a teaching strategy across various clinical specialties in medicine and other sciences worldwide. Introduction

Simulation-based learning (SBL) is an educational approach that utilizes simulated setups and scenarios to replicate real-world experiences, enabling learners to practice and refine their skills in a safe environment without incurring adverse consequences. In medicine, it has become an essential tool for teaching clinical, procedural, and decision-making skills. Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of simulation in improving both technical skills, such as surgery and clinical procedures, and non-technical skills, including communication and decision-making [1–5]. For SBL to be truly effective, essential elements must be in place: clear learning objectives, an appropriate venue, suitable equipment and software, skilled instructors, structured debriefing sessions, constructive feedback, and motivated students actively participating in hands-on practice [6]. Simulation is not only useful for training but also for evaluating students’ academic performance. In 1990, George Miller introduced an assessment framework that involved evaluating four hierarchical levels: knowledge acquisition, application of knowledge, clinical competence, and real performance at the workplace [7]. Clinical competency, the third level, can be assessed through simulation, including Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) that utilize standardized patients and high-fidelity mannequins [8]. At the College of Medicine and Health Sciences (CMHS) of the Arabian Gulf University (AGU) in Bahrain, the Medical Skills and Simulation Center (MSSC) was established in 2017. Starting in the academic year 2018–2019, simulation-based learning was officially integrated into the clinical curricula of Pediatrics, Obstetrics & Gynecology, Internal Medicine, and Surgery. Previously, pediatric training at CMHS depended mainly on lecture-based instruction and bedside clinical rotations. The integration of SBL into the pediatric curriculum aimed to improve experiential learning and better prepare students for real-world clinical encounters. The MSSC is equipped with advanced simulation technology, including high-fidelity mannequins and virtual patient platforms. These tools encourage learners to interact with digital avatars in realistic clinical scenarios, providing immediate feedback on communication, history taking, physical examinations, reasoning, and decision-making. Repetition and guided supervision build students’ confidence and competence.

The CMHS curriculum is a six-year blended program divided into three phases [9]. Phase I (Year 1) centers on subject-based, lecture-driven learning. Phase II (Years 2–4) emphasizes problem-based learning of core medical sciences, supplemented with additional professional skills lab training. Phase III (Years 5–6) includes hospital-based clinical clerkships. Since 2018, Phase III has incorporated SBL sessions using high-fidelity manikins, standardized patients, and realistic equipment. The fifth year consists of 40 weeks of clinical training, with 20 weeks in internal medicine and 10 weeks each in pediatrics and obstetrics & gynecology, arranged into four rotations. Students complete pediatric rotations either before or after their internal medicine training, depending on their schedule. The pediatric clerkship ends with a written exam (type A MCQs and extended matching questions), a clinical exam (OSCE at CMHS and a long case at the hospital), and ongoing evaluation through a logbook. Despite the increasing use of SBL, no research has yet examined its specific impact on pediatric medical education at CMHS, AGU. This study aimed to assess the effect of SBL on the academic performance of medical students at the pediatrics end of the clerkship exam.

Methods

This is a retrospective analysis of 2,251 fifth-year medical students, over 12 academic years from September 2012 to June 2024, comprising six years before and six years following the incorporation of simulation-based learning into the pediatric curriculum. The academic performance of the students was analyzed. Performance metrics were based on the three components of the end-of-clerkship examination: the written exam, the clinical exam, and the continuous assessment. As fifth-year medical students complete their pediatric clerkship in four equal rotation groups across each academic year, performance outcomes were also compared across these rotation groups and subjected to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered into Excel sheets and processed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 29.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). The means and standard deviations were calculated, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

The difference in the number of students in the two cohorts is attributed to the increase in the number of students who are admitted to the CMHS, AGU after 2018. Discussion

Globally, medical education is transitioning toward practical hands-on training. Yet, implementing this approach with real patients may raise legal and ethical concerns. As a result, simulation-based learning has become an invaluable alternative, and its use has increased significantly in recent years [10].

This training method has proven to be a valuable tool in medicine and surgery, not only for clinical procedures and time-sensitive resuscitation training, but also for non-procedural aspects, such as information gathering and counseling [11].

This study demonstrated a significant improvement in academic performance among medical students following the integration of simulation-based training into the pediatric curriculum. Compared to the pre-simulation cohort, students exposed to SBT achieved higher mean scores across all assessment components, including written examinations, clinical evaluations, and continuous assessments. The failure rate decreased markedly, while the proportion of students attaining excellent grades increased. This trend was observed consistently across both genders, and it was most pronounced among students who completed the pediatric rotation after their internal medicine training, suggesting that prior clinical exposure further enhances the effectiveness of simulation-based learning.

The study highlighted that integrating simulation-based learning into the pediatric curriculum had a statistically significant positive effect on students’ academic performance, as evidenced by improvements in both written and clinical examination scores. These findings further support the growing recognition of SBL as an effective and valuable educational strategy in medical education.

“These observed benefits align with findings from numerous previously published studies, further supporting the integration of simulation-based learning into undergraduate medical curricula. In a meta-analysis of 15 studies, Yan-Yuan et al. reported that high-fidelity simulation training was associated with a statistically significant improvement in objective knowledge examination scores among nursing students [12]. Likewise, a study involving emergency medicine residents found that incorporating high-fidelity simulation into the residency curriculum significantly enhanced clinical performance in real-world settings [13].”

Moreover, a recent survey conducted among 50 medical students who participated in realistic simulation-based learning revealed highly favorable feedback. Most students reported that simulation-based training was a more effective method for achieving learning objectives. Notably, 94% of participants also indicated that they found this approach to be more engaging and enjoyable compared to traditional learning methods [14].

An increasing number of recent studies have focused on optimizing simulation-based learning by exploring strategies to enhance its structure, consistency, and educational impact. Educational researchers are investigating key instructional elements, including scenario realism, levels of learner engagement, effectiveness of feedback mechanisms, and opportunities for deliberate practice as components associated with improved skills acquisition and retention [15]– [16]. Moreover, metric‑based simulation frameworks that define performance benchmarks and align scenarios with expert standards have been shown to improve consistency and instructional efficiency in SLB [17].

Conclusion

The study highlights the positive impact of incorporating SBL into pediatric education for medical students in Bahrain. These findings support the wider integration of SBL as a teaching strategy across various clinical specialties in medicine and nursing worldwide. They also highlight its potential to progressively complement and, in some cases, partially replace traditional classroom-based instruction.

Limitations of the study

A recognized limitation of this study is its reliance on data from different cohorts of medical students over 12 years, six years before and six years after the implementation of simulation-based learning (SBL) in the pediatric curriculum. However, all cohorts shared consistent characteristics, including identical admission criteria, similar male-to-female ratios, comparable academic performance during the first four years, standardized curriculum objectives and outcomes, and uniform theoretical and clinical training. The only distinguishing factor was the introduction of SBL, whose impact on academic performance was the focus of our evaluation. Although the ideal research design would involve randomly assigning students within the same cohort to SBL and non-SBL groups, such an approach is ethically unacceptable in this context.
