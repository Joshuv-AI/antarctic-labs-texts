Ancestry-associated social and genomic hallmarks linked with inferior outcome in elderly acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Lacey S Williams, Alexis Williams, Olivia Wilkins, Sarah Mudra, Paul Sackstein, Garret Diltz, Rachel Zemel, Divya Lakshmi Padmanabhan, Gary M Kupfer, Purnima Sravanti Teegaparavu, Kimberley Doucette, Catherine Lai, Martha Mims, Anne Renteria, Gustavo Rivero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent literature reports inferior outcomes for patients of African ancestry (AA) who develop acute myeloid leukemia (AML). We herein present a cohort of 396 patients with 116 patients of AA (29%) who show inferior survival outcomes for patients of African ancestry ≥ 60 years (p = 0.008). Our cohort also demonstrated significantly increased rates of monosomy 7 and 7q deletions in the patients of AA (p = 0.04, p = 0.03), suggesting association with genomic instability and loss of chromatin stabilization protein EZH2, DNA repair function mediated by CUX1, and SAMD9L anti-proliferation effect. Increased genomic instability may be mediated by accelerated aging related to environmental stressors or inherited genomic patterns. We also assessed the influence of neighborhood vulnerability. Patients of AA were more likely to live in census tracts with more people living below the 150% poverty level (p < 0.01). Undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic cellular transplant (allo-HCT) provided improved outcomes to patients of AA, highlighting the need to optimize access and supportive care for allo-HCT for minority patients. In multivariate analysis, race was not retained as an independent predictor of survival. Instead, adverse ELN22 risk and higher socioeconomic vulnerability scores were independently associated with inferior outcomes. These findings suggest that disparities in survival may be largely driven by the intersection of biological risk and social determinants of health, rather than ancestry itself acting as an independent prognostic factor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14777-7.Keywords: Leukemia, Racial disparity, Genomic abnormalities

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a cancer involving the cells of the bone marrow, blood, and other tissues with abnormal differentiation of the myeloid lineage of the hematopoietic system. Malignant clonal expansion occurs in myeloid progenitor cells affected by impaired differentiation and proliferation. AML incidence is near 4 cases per 100,000/year, comprising the most common form of acute leukemia, favoring older individuals with median age of 68 years at diagnosis [1]. AML carries poor prognosis overall with a 5-year overall survival (OS) of 24%.1 Outcomes have improved in recent years with novel therapies and advances in allogeneic hematopoietic cellular transplant (allo-HCT), with 5-year OS nearing 60% in patients that undergo allo-HCT [2]. However, outcomes remain dire for older patients with a 2-year OS of only 5–15% for patients ≥ 65 years [3].

There is a remarkable heterogeneity of risk factors that contribute to the varied outcomes of AML. Recent reports highlight the influence of African ancestry (AA) mediating inferior outcomes. The SEER database from 1986 to 2015 demonstrated worse survival for patients of AA compared to White ancestry, especially among patients < 60 years [4]. These findings were disproportionate to poverty status and urban vs. rural residence [4]. The Alliance trial cohort from the same period also found inferior survival outcomes among patients of AA under 60 years, notably in a trial cohort in which patients did not proceed to allo-HSCT in first complete remission (CR) [4]. Among patients with NPM1 mutations, their cohort identified a dramatically worse 3-year OS (17%) for patients of AA compared to Caucasian patients (47%).4 Molecular profiling in the Alliance trial population revealed that patients of AA carried lower rates of normal karyotypes (19% vs. 40%) [5]. A consortium of universities in Chicago also examined disparities in AML outcomes. They found that structural racism, indicated by census tract measures for racial and economic segregation, significantly accounted for disparities in AML outcomes, with the study analysis adjusted for age, gender, and study site [6]. In addition to social factors contributing to inferior outcomes, their analysis identified higher rates of p53 mutations and complex karyotypes in the patients of AA [6].

Taken together, these reports highlight worse outcomes for patients of AA with AML mediated by social determinants of health as well as suggest a variability in disease biology, whether inherited or developed in response to unique environmental stressors. Detailed characterization of disadvantaged populations including those of AA has been challenging due to limited clinical trial enrollment affected by geographic availability, time and transportation resources necessary for participation, and historical distrust from minority groups [7]. In the United States, the annual population incidence of AML is 2.9 cases per 100,000/year in people of AA, and 3.7 cases per 100,000/year in people of Caucasian ancestry [8]. In the populations described in the literature, the most well-characterized Alliance cohort contains a relatively low number of patients of AA, with 72 patients of AA < 60 years (8.5%) included in one analysis, and 89 patients of AA (13.6%) in another analysis [4, 5]. Among older patients, the Alliance cohort included 23 patients of AA (5%) [4]. The Chicago consortium included 15% patients of AA [6]. These cohorts highlight the need for the investigation of real-world AML populations that include patients of AA. In our study we present the outcomes of diverse patient populations with AML that were treated in the District of Columbia (DC) metropolitan area and Houston, Texas (TX), with a special emphasis on the elderly AML sub-cohort. We aimed to investigate the hypotheses that genomic instability aberrations, social determinants, and metabolic factors contribute to outcomes in patients with AML in association with ancestry [9].

Methods

Patient selection

We conducted retrospective analysis of 396 patients with AML from Baylor College of Medicine Houston, TX 2013–2023, and Medstar Georgetown University Hospital and Medstar Washington Hospital Center in Washington, DC from 2015 to 2024, that included their demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, race, BMI, comorbidities, smoking, treatments, and outcomes). Patients with the diagnosis of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) were excluded. Patients were treated according to standard of care approaches at the given time of diagnosis for their clinical situation including best supportive care in cases of very poor functional status. Patients were treated urgently upon diagnosis, with treatment initiation within one week.

Preparation of clinical variables

Genomic factors were considered in the context of genomic instability. We assessed these genetic alterations based on single nucleotide polymorphisms collected from available next-genome sequencing (NGS), characteristic regions of deletions and duplications obtained from fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH), and large chromosomal aberrations, i.e. aneuploidy identified from cytogenetics. For NGS data available, we completed variant annotation with assessment for frequencies. We stratified patients by European LeukemiaNet 2022 classification (ELN22). We used Pearson correlation to assess the association of age and number of karyotypic abnormalities. In patients with extended NGS, we analyzed individual mutational events with regard to biologic mechanisms (epigenetics, DNA repair, etc.) for association with ancestry using chi-square analysis. We utilized the Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (SIFT) score algorithm to assess available nonsynonymous or missense variants in the Baylor cohort for their likelihood for protein deleteriousness, with score < 0.05 indicating potential for protein damaging effect. We also collected nucleotide substitutions based on previous Sanger Institute data which demonstrated imprinted mechanisms that correlated with endogenous and exogenous processes [10].

Subgroup analyses

We completed subgroup analyses for patients </≥ 60 years, according to chromosome abnormalities that characterize AML, based on albumin levels, and with respect to BMI levels. For the DC cohort, we included analysis of social determinants based on census tract. Measures of the CDC’s Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) were used for each patient’s local census tract to assess their neighborhood vulnerability [11]. Chi-square and t-test evaluated differences in categorical and continuous variables respectively. Logistic binary regression was used to detect predictors of survival based on subgroups. Kaplan-meier survival curves demonstrated survival analysis. SAS Studio software was used.

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to evaluate factors associated with overall survival (OS) among patients older than 60 years. Variables included in the model were selected based on clinical relevance, univariate significance, and investigator recommendations. The candidate variables included: (1) Race (AA vs. Non-AA, forced into the model as a variable of interest), (2) ELN 2022 risk classification (Favorable, Intermediate, Adverse), (3) a poverty indicator based on estimated proportion of people living below 150% poverty level by census tract, (4) socioeconomic vulnerability by census tract (SVI theme 1), (5) minority status by census tract (SVI theme 3) and (6) aggregate social vulnerability score. A backward selection procedure was used (significance level of p < 0.10 for retention). Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Proportional hazard assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

Results

Outcomes for patients of African ancestry in real world cohort

Our cohort had 396 patients with 116 patients of AA (29%). Mean age was 63.8 (+/- 14.3) years and age was balanced between patients with and without AA. Patients of AA ≥ 60 years were well-represented (23%). 45% of the cohort underwent induction with intensive anthracycline-based therapy, 24% were treated with with hympomethylating agent/venetoclax combination, 13% with azacitadine alone, 4% with other attenuated chemotherapy (including cytarabine and clofarabine), and 14% with best supportive care. Karyotypic abnormalities

Among patients 60 years and older, we found that 11/23 (48%) and 22/82 (27%) of patients of AA and non-AA harbored complex typical karyotype (CK; p = 0.05) [12]. We examined OS among older AML cases with or without CK. Inferior outcomes were more pronounced for patients with complex karyotype (Fig. 2). Notably the patients without AA ≥ 60 years with normal karyotype demonstrated significantly better survival compared to those with AA with either normal or complex karyotypes, who experienced more similar outcomes (Fig. 2, p = 0.005). In fact, investigation of chromosomal abnormalities among the entire cohort revealed significantly increased rates of monosomy 7 and 7q deletions in the patients of AA (p = 0.04, p = 0.03). Protein loss of function

We investigated whether loss of function due to somatic mutations contributed to the dismal AA AML outcomes using the SIFT score algorithm, with score < 0.05 indicating amino acid substitution ability for protein deleteriousness. One hundred and twenty-five pathogenic variants were screened from the Houston cohort (extracted from 12 AA, 37 white, 16 h, 3 Asians = 68 pt total). SIFT score means were 0.01, 0.09, 0.02 and 0.01 in White, AA, Hispanic and other ancestry, respectively (p = 0.03).

Mutational profiles in patients with AA

Based on abnormalities demonstrating enrichment for genomic losses involving ch 7 and inferior prognosis for patients with CK, we investigated the role of gene mutations contributing to genomic instability. When considering the mutational events in patients undergoing extended mutational analysis (n = 120), the instances of DNA repair defects evidenced by mutations in CHEK2, RAD21, FBXW7, MUTYH, and ATM or ch 7q deletions involving CUX1 demonstrate enrichment in the patients with AA (p < 0.01, Fig. 3, Table 3). Mutational events involving biotypes maintaining genomic stability, including epigenetics, chromatin, tumor suppressors, splicing, DNA repair, cohesin, and telomeres or 7q/17p deletions indicating haploinsufficiency of CUX1 and TP53 were also disproportionately more present in the patients of AA (p < 0.01, Table 3). Analyzed at the patient level for the whole cohort, there was also increased rate of DNA repair defects in patients of AA, based on presence of either ch 7 abnormalities or mutations in CHEK2, RAD21, FBXW7, MUTYH, and ATM (p = 0.03, Table 2).

Investigating metabolic dysfunction in AML

We assessed factors that may contribute to the inferior outcomes experienced by patients of AA. We analyzed patients’ body mass index (BMI) in association with the observed genomic abnormalities. We found that the incidence of certain chromosomic abnormalities were disproportionately higher in patients of AA with elevated BMI (Table 4). We found that some chromosomal abnormalities which did not show significant association with ancestry in the whole population, did show increasing association with ancestry at higher BMI levels. There were higher rates of deletion 5q, trisomy 8 and deletion 20q in patients of AA with higher BMI in the overall cohort and in patients ≥ 60 years (Table 4). Analyses in patients ≥ 60 years were limited by fewer than 20 patients of AA with BMI ≥ 32 (Table 4). Based on annotated variants from patients’ NGS results, we investigated the nucleotide changes in mutations related to DNA damage and oxidative stress, the C to A and G to T substitutions, and found them overrepresented in the AA patients with high BMI (33% vs. 11%, p = 0.06) [13, 14].

Albumin levels in AML

A subgroup analysis demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia, manifested as moderate (2.5–3.4 g/dL) and severe (< 2.5 g/dL), identified patients with AA with worse OS in Kaplan-Meier analysis (p = 0.04). However, this association was not significant in non-AA patients. We did not detect linear association between BMI and hypoalbuminemia. However, AA patients with BMI ≥ 34 were more likely to have severe hypoalbuminemia (p = 0.046), not demonstrated in the rest of the cohort.

Social vulnerability in the AML population in DC

We conducted a subanalysis of patient social vulnerability indicated by the Center for Disease Control’s Social Vulnerability Index (CDC SVI) by census tract for the patients of the DC metropolitan area. This subcohort comprised 174 patients with AML: 71 of AA (AA) and 103 of non-AA. A higher proportion of patients of AA lived in census tracts with more people living below the 150% poverty level than patients of non-AA (p < 0.01). Among patients who did not undergo an allo-HCT, patients from census tracts with greater than 90th percentile vulnerability based on racial and ethnic status had a trend towards lower overall survival (p = 0.09). However, this trend was improved when patients who proceeded to allo-HCT were included. Similarly, among patients not proceeding to allo-HCT, those with AA had lower OS when compared to patients of non-AA (p = 0.045, Fig. 1B). This difference in survival was not observed when comparing patents of AA and non-AA who also underwent allo-HCT. These analyses assessed for the impacts of age, ancestry, BMI, albumin level, and CDC SVI parameters.

Univariate and multivariant analyses

In univariate Cox models restricted to patients 60 years and over, the following variables were each associated with inferior OS as a continuous variable: African ancestry, adverse ELN22 risk, lower albumin, estimated proportion of people living below 150% poverty level by census tract (poverty indicator), and greater social vulnerability for (1) socioeconomic status, (2) house composition and diversity, (3) and racial and linguistic minority status. The 30- and 60-day early mortality events also trended higher among AA patients, although statistical significance was not formally assessed in univariate survival models due to being time-fixed events.

In the multivariate Cox regression model with backward selection, only ELN22 and low socioeconomic status (SVI theme 1) were retained as independent predictors of overall survival. Both intermediate and adverse ELN22 categories were associated with significantly reduced survival compared to favorable risk (HR = 0.47, p = 0.02 for intermediate; HR = 0.14, p = 0.0017 for adverse). A trend was observed for socioeconomic vulnerability, where low socioeconomic status was associated with worse survival (HR = 2.18, p = 0.0876). Notably, race, and estimate of persons under 150% of the poverty level, and other social determinants were not retained in the final model (Tables 5, 6 and 7).

Discussion

Our real world cohort highlights the inferior survival experienced by patients of AA, supporting previous reports in the literature [4–6]. The DC metropolitan area reflects diverse backgrounds of patients of AA, including individuals who have lived in the United States for many generations as well as recent immigrants from many areas of the African continent and other regions resulting from the diaspora. Patients of AA from the greater Houston area include families that have lived in the region for several generations, immigrants, and is enriched with families also with Hispanic heritage. The Houston cohort also includes patients from the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), typically exposed to unique occupational stressors that are likely to influence malignancy pathogenesis [15]. Within this heterogenous real-world cohort, our data revealed inferior outcomes for patients of AA, particularly among patients ≥ 60 years. Patients of non-AA ≥ 60 years with a non-CK experienced superior OS compared to all patients of AA (Fig. 2). Patients of AA with non-CK shared similar survival outcomes with patients of non-AA with CK, while patients of AA with CK experienced significantly worse survival outcomes when compared to the other groups. This data suggest that the AA AML genome is compromised by adverse mutagenesis and/or epiphenomenon beyond structural and segregation chromosomal abnormalities.

Advanced age and pre-existing myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) are known biologic factors associated with genomic instability. However, with advances in sequencing technology, frequent mutations in DNA repair, mitotic spindle apparatus regulation, and cohesion suggest complex interaction between mutation acquisition and karyotypic complexity. Our cohort showed increasing karyotypic complexity with age, with corresponding trend in the patients of AA. We also previously reported, albeit in a small patient cohort, that the number of chromosomic abnormalities detected in metaphase karyotyping increases with aging in the AA AML genomes (R2 = 0.63, p = 0.02), a finding not observed in other ancestry groups [12]. Indeed, our observation that higher than expected median SIFT score for pathogenic variants in the AA AML genome implies not only lack of protein loss of function from mutational defects, but also that AA leukemogenesis alternatively prioritizes uncharacterized genomic instability pathways leading to higher-than-expected aneuploidy. It remains challenging to assess protein deleteriousness for reported variants in patients of AA since population genetic polymorphisms are less well characterized.

The noted enrichment for monosomy 7 and 7q deletions in patients of AA suggest leukemogenic mechanisms related to aging and myelodysplasia, therapy-related diseases, and a potentially higher incidence for impaired DNA repair mechanisms. Our cohort revealed that patients of AA ≥ 60 years carried an inferior OS, in contrast to SEER and Alliance data indicating disproportionately worse outcomes for patients of AA under 60 years. This may reflect an increased proportion of elderly patients treated at our sites. Even if the median age for AML is 68 years at diagnosis [1], many academic centers have higher rates of younger patients, while the elderly patients, frequently less fit for intensive therapies, are less likely to have access to tertiary academic centers [16]. Increased rates of ch 7 abnormalities in patients of AA in our elderly cohort indicates an increased presence of MDS-disease ontology. This may indicate an accelerated aging process since MDS-related disease is expected to increase with chronological age, in keeping with the suggestion that social disadvantage accelerates biological aging [17]. Patients of AA did not have higher rates of prior malignancy or lines of therapy, suggesting a therapy-independent factor driving the strong association with ch 7 abnormalities in this group. With the inclusion of patients from the VHA in our cohort, it is possible that military-related occupational exposures contribute to the genetic changes detected, though our analysis was not powered for this assessment.

Chromosome (ch) loss in the 7q region contributes to haploinsufficiency of the chromatin stabilization protein EZH2, DNA repair pathway function mediated by CUX1, and SAMD9L anti-proliferation effect. Fanconi anemia, a well-known germline defect associated with failure to repair DNA, also frequently retain loss of chromosome 7. It is possible that enrichment for these chromosomal abnormalities reflects a leukemogenic process that prioritize MDS ontogeny and DNA repair defects.

Genomic instability (GI) in AML encompasses changes in chromosomal number and structure, telomere shortening, and smaller genetic changes in microsatellite patterns that may explain adverse outcomes [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients of AA diagnosed with prostate cancer develop tumorigenesis characterized by high genomic instability [18, 19]. Significantly higher frequencies of deletions were present in the regions of 2p22.2, 4q34.3, 2q22.1–2, 15q15.1, 6q15, 8p21.2, and 13q14.2, and significantly more frequent gains in the region of 8q24.21 in AA than in Eastern European/White men, and resulting in more aggressive prostate cancer [18]. Indeed, 89% of AA/Black males developed tumors with at least one mutation in nucleotide excision repair pathway genes, whereas > 40% of tumors had mutations in base excision repair pathway genes in Caucasians, suggesting that DNA repairome expression is racially induced in sporadic prostate cancer [19]. These observations emphasize that detecting a similar higher level of genomic instability present in AA AML genome may suggest that genomic instability plays a contributing factor in AA leukemogenesis in myeloid malignancies. Next steps in this work will require defining genomic instability variation among patients from different backgrounds, with emphasis on identification of unstable genomic regions and neoantigens.

Metabolic dysregulation related to BMI and hypoalbuminemia may reflect various clinical states that impair patients’ immune system response to malignancy, and their tolerance to treatment. Obesity syndromes predispose to clonal hematopoiesis, oxidative DNA damage and chronic inflammatory states, which may potentiate myeloid leukemic transformation [20, 21]. Annotated mutations in our cohort revealed nucleotide changes representative of oxidative stress specifically in the AA population with higher BMI, emphasizing unique metabolic vulnerability in this group. Albumin levels also reflect an underlying inflammatory state and have also been linked to cachexia [22]. Conversely, other studies demonstrate that obesity and morbid obesity are associated with hypoalbuminemia in patients without chronic liver or renal disease [23]. In our cohort, low BMI did not segregate with low albumin levels; in fact we observed disproportionate severe hypoalbuminemia (albumin < 2.5 g/dL) in patients with high BMI ≥ 34 in the patients of AA. This suggests alternative inflammatory process for hypoalbuminemia, most notable in the patients of AA. This highlights potential of additional undefined mechanisms of inflammation and premature biologic aging. In fact, the increased rates of chromosomal abnormalities at increased BMI in our cohort suggest that lower BMI may function as a protection against clonal hematopoiesis and leukemogenesis, consistent with previous reports in the literature [24]. Hypoalbuminemia has been identified as a predictor of inferior outcome in AML and other malignancies [22, 25–28]. In our cohort, patients of AA with moderate (2.5–3.4 g/dL) and severe (< 2.5 g/dL) hypoalbuminemia demonstrated worse OS in Kaplan-Meier analysis (p = 0.04), which was not seen for patients of other ancestry.

The analysis of the DC area patient census tract locations with the CDC Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) showed that a higher proportion of patients of AA lived in census tracts with more people living below the 150% poverty level than patients of non-AA (p < 0.0001). In addition, among patients who did not undergo allo-HCT, patients from census tracts with greater than 90th percentile vulnerability based on racial and ethnic status had a trend towards lower OS (p = 0.09). These emphasize that racial minority status and socioeconomic vulnerability were closely related for many patients, and are likely to play a role within heterogeneous biological factors. OS differences based on race and ethnicity are mitigated when patients of AA with AML are able to undergo allo-HCT, which has also been observed in other studies [29, 30]. This highlights the need to address barriers to access to allo-HCT for patients of AA. Because there was no association between social vulnerability and OS when allo-HCT patients were included, there may be factors outside of socioeconomic status at play. There also remain significant questions about access to allo-HCT for patients of AA and selection processes that may disproportionately exclude them, including the availability of suitable donors.

There are several limitations related to the retrospective nature of our study. While our patient cohort includes very distinct medical centers which supports its generalizability, it also carries unaccounted differences among diagnostic and treatment practices that could have differentially impacted our patients’ outcomes. Further, retrospective data collection includes patients with a short follow up time in patients recently diagnosed and in those who discontinued treatment at a participating center. The retrospective nature of our data analysis introduces the potential for multiple comparisons, increasing the likelihood that some statistically significant p-values may have occurred by chance. Although such limitations are inherent in retrospective analyses, we present our findings in a confirmatory framework, reinforced by multiple, hypothesis-driven analyses.

When considered in multivariate analysis, race, estimate of persons under 150% of the poverty level, and other social determinants were not retained in the final model, suggesting that the observed racial disparity in univariate analysis may be accounted for by differences in molecular risk and social vulnerability.

Conclusion

Differences in genomic, metabolic, and social determinants of health for patients of AA diagnosed with AML highlight the need for tailored therapeutic approaches. Our data emphasizes increased genomic instability among patients of AA, with resulting inferior outcomes for those patients ≥ 60 years. The links between genomic instability, metabolism and chronic inflammation highlight therapeutic opportunities that may have positive impacts. Better understanding of their role in myeloid malignancy may also inform effective supportive care strategies. Physical fitness and/or therapeutic interventions such as GLP-1 inhibitors throughout management present opportunities to study the metabolic impact on chemosensitivity, relapse, and long-term outcomes.

Our data highlights the benefit of allo-HCT in patients of AA with AML as an opportunity to ameliorate their otherwise inferior outcomes. Patient support strategies and health-related education should be optimized in this vulnerable population. Despite ongoing progress, allo-HCT access remains limited among elderly patients ≥ 60 years. This patient population showed greatest disparity in outcomes in our cohort. It is possible that accelerated biological aging processes mediate increased genomic instability and therefore inferior outcomes in patients of AA, and may negatively impact fitness required for undergoing an allo-HCT. This biological aging requires differentiation from chronological aging.

In this retrospective analysis of older adults with AML, we observed that both molecular risk and social vulnerability were significant contributors to overall survival disparities, with implications for understanding racial differences in outcomes. Consistent with prior studies, patients of AA exhibited higher rates of adverse ELN22 molecular risk and greater social vulnerability as measured by established metrics such as socioeconomic status (SVI theme 1). It remains unclear why patients of AA harbor higher rates of adverse molecular risk disease, and whether this is mediated by inheritance, environmental exposures, social determinants, or some combination.

Interestingly, in multivariate analysis, race was not retained as an independent predictor of survival. Instead, adverse ELN22 risk and higher socioeconomic vulnerability scores were independently associated with inferior outcomes. These findings suggest that racial disparities in survival may be largely driven by the intersection of biological risk and social determinants of health, rather than race itself acting as an independent prognostic factor.

Future studies should focus on validating these findings in larger, multi-institutional cohorts and exploring whether targeted supportive care or social resource interventions can mitigate the adverse impact of social vulnerability on AML outcomes. Additionally, the role of social determinants and unique molecular patterns that influence treatment selection, early mortality, and post-remission outcomes warrant further investigation.
