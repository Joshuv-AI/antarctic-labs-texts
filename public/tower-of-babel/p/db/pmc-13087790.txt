Distinct molecular characteristics of humic-like substances (HULIS) in cloud water under land and marine influences
Authors: Xinghui Liu, Huiting Mao, Xiaoling Nie, Jiebo Zhen, Ping Du, Tao Li, Xinfeng Wang, Likun Xue, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHumic-like substances (HULISs) are a major fraction of cloud-water organic matter that modulate aqueous reactivity and light absorption. We sampled Mt. Tai cloud water (North China Plain) in summer 2021 under land-influenced (LI) and marine-influenced (MI) air-mass trajectories, isolated HULIS by solid-phase extraction, and characterized them using ultrahigh-resolution FT-ICR MS. HULIS averaged 3.1 ± 2.1 mgC L−1 (39 ± 16% of WSOC). LI concentrations were ∼2× MI, whereas the HULIS/WSOC fraction was higher in MI. We assigned 7,104 molecular formulas with substantial overlap but distinct unique pools (1,879 LI-unique; 787 MI-unique). LI-unique formulas were enriched in mid-high-mass CHOS and reduced carbon oxidation state signatures, while MI-unique formulas featured a low-mass, highly oxygenated diacid-like pool and aromatic/unsaturated character. These results show that air-mass provenance leaves persistent molecular fingerprints in cloud-water HULIS, informing source-to-cloud processing links and cloud-phase chemistry.

Introduction

Cloud water represents a dynamic multiphase medium in the atmosphere where chemical reactions, long-range transport, and cloud-aerosol interactions intersect.1 Among the carbonaceous constituents of cloud water, brown carbon (BrC), operationally defined by its near-UV/visible light absorption, plays a pivotal role in climate forcing by absorbing ultraviolet radiation, enhancing radiative warming, and mediating atmospheric photochemistry.2,3 Humic-like substances (HULISs), an operationally defined fraction isolated by chemical extraction (e.g., solid-phase extraction) and exhibiting UV absorption and fluorescence properties similar to those of aquatic and soil humic substances,4 have been found to account for over 40% of the solar radiation absorption attributed to water-soluble BrC,5 underscoring their significance in atmospheric processes. Beyond their optical significance, HULIS substantially influence cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) activity,6 mediate trace metal complexation,7 and contribute to the formation of secondary organic aerosols (SOAs).8

Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) enables detailed molecular characterization of cloud-water dissolved organic matter by resolving thousands of distinct elemental formulas.9,10,11 Studies have shown that the water-soluble organic matter (WSOM) in cloud water comprises primarily CHO and CHON species, with lesser contributions from CHOS and CHONS groups.12,13 For example, summer 2021 measurements at Mt. Tai, China, revealed that CHON formulas accounted for 63% of the total signal, of which nearly 75% arose from the nucleophilic addition of ammonia (NH3) to carbonyl intermediates formed during biogenic VOC oxidation.8 Along the South China coast at Tianjin Mountain, CHON abundances ranged from 43.6% to 65.3% and CHO from 13.8% to 52.1%, with nocturnal NO3-driven pathways favoring nitroaromatic CHON formation (e.g., dinitrophenols) and daytime photolysis shifting composition toward more oxidized CHO species.13 At France’s Puy de Dôme station, cloud waters influenced by urban-polluted Paris air masses exhibited an expanded molecular diversity,12 and follow-up analyses linked lipid-like formulas predominantly to marine biogenic emissions.14 Although land-influenced (LI) and marine-influenced (MI) cloud-water compositions clearly differ, comprehensive side-by-side comparisons of HULIS molecular signatures under both regimes at a single site remain rare. In summer 2021, Mt. Tai experienced alternating southwest (LI) and southeast (MI) flow conditions, making it an ideal natural laboratory to address this gap.

In this study, cloud water was collected from the summit of Mt. Tai during summer 2021. LI and MI samples were gently filtered and subjected to a simplified solid-phase extraction procedure optimized for HULIS. The extracted fractions were analyzed by FT-ICR MS in negative-ion electrospray mode to assign molecular formulas within the CHO, CHON, CHOS, and CHONS groups. We then directly compared LI and MI using van Krevelen diagrams and O/C and H/C elemental ratios to assess how continental versus marine air masses shape HULIS composition in Mt. Tai cloud water. This side-by-side comparison is important because LI and MI air masses represent contrasting precursor pools and in-cloud processing environments, which can imprint different HULIS molecular fingerprints. Such regime-dependent composition is expected to influence cloud-water light absorption and photosensitized/oxidative chemistry, thereby affecting the atmospheric evolution of water-soluble organic matter. Our results, therefore, provide observational constraints for interpreting aqueous-phase secondary formation and the cloud-processing contribution to brown-carbon evolution.

Results

Concentrations of HULIS and major compounds

The average concentration of HULIS in Mt. Tai cloud water during the sampling period was 3.1 ± 2.1 mgC L−1, accounting for 39 ± 16% of water-soluble organic carbon (WSOC). This HULIS/WSOC fraction is comparable to values reported for atmospheric aqueous samples, where HULIS has been reported to account for ∼32–50% of WSOC in fog water,4 indicating a substantial contribution of HULIS to the dissolved organic pool in cloud droplets. Major ionic species were dominated by secondary inorganic components, with mean concentrations of SO42−, NO3−, and NH4+ each exceeding 10 mg L−1 (Table S2), while other ions such as Na+, Cl−, K+, and Mg2+ remained below 1 mg L−1. A statistically significant positive correlation between HULIS and several ionic species (Figure S1) further supports the interpretation that HULIS and secondary inorganics share common emission sources and atmospheric processing pathways.

The site was influenced by two distinct air masses: LI trajectories originating from southeastern inland regions and MI trajectories transported from the western Pacific (Figure 1A). Cloud-water composition differed substantially between these regimes (Figure 1B). Overall, the concentrations of most chemical species in LI cloud water were nearly twice those in MI, reflecting the stronger impact of continental emissions. For instance, SO42−, NO3−, and NH4+ averaged 18.2 ± 18.39, 24.8 ± 32.50, and 13.2 ± 13.26 mg L−1, respectively, in LI events, compared to 7.9 ± 6.9, 8.8 ± 7.0, and 5.2 ± 4.3 mg L−1 in MI events (Table S2). HULIS concentrations followed a similar pattern, reaching 3.67 ± 2.41 mgC L−1 in LI samples versus 1.88 ± 0.90 mgC L−1 in MI samples. The only notable exception was sea-salt-related ions. Concentrations of Na+ (0.51 ± 0.43 vs. 0.74 ± 0.70 mg L−1) and Cl− (1.39 ± 1.50 vs. 1.44 ± 1.08 mg L−1) were comparable between LI and MI samples. Na+ was detected in only 6 of 23 LI events, compared with 9 of 13 MI events, and the higher detection frequency in MI was statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, two-tailed, p = 0.0168). In contrast, crustal and biomass-burning tracers such as Ca2+ (3.8 vs. 1.3 mg L−1) and K+ (0.64 vs. 0.32 mg L−1) were more abundant in LI cloud water, consistent with stronger terrestrial influence. Although MI samples contained lower absolute HULIS concentrations, they exhibited a larger HULIS fraction relative to WSOC, with average values of 46.9 ± 17.2% compared with 33.9 ± 12.4% in LI samples. HULIS has been suggested to be formed and further transformed through secondary processes in the atmosphere.15,16 In this study, MI events exhibited substantially lower overall solute and WSOC loadings than LI events (Table S2), indicating weaker inputs of fresh, regionally sourced organics. Under such conditions, the HULIS/WSOC ratio may increase because the fresh WSOC pool is reduced (i.e., a denominator effect), potentially together with secondary formation during MI transport. Field and laboratory evidence further shows that BrC in both continental and marine air masses undergoes rapid photobleaching and chemical transformation on timescales of hours to days.17,18,19 Here, photochemical bleaching refers to sunlight-driven aqueous-phase oxidation in cloud/fog droplets that attenuates light-absorbing chromophores (e.g., via oxidation/fragmentation of conjugated structures) and produces more oxygenated, less light-absorbing products. Previous studies have confirmed that LI HULIS generally exhibits stronger UV-visible light absorption than MI HULIS.20,21 To clarify the chemical basis of these contrasts, the molecular composition of LI and MI HULIS was subsequently examined using FT-ICR MS.

Differences in element composition and basic parameter display

The LI and MI HULIS mass spectra (Figures 2A and 2B) showed comparable peak structures, featuring dense clusters concentrated at m/z 200–400 and high-abundance CHON homologues (e.g., C35H70NO8) observed across the mass range, indicating compositional convergence likely promoted by cloud processing. In total, 7,104 molecular formulas were assigned to cloud-water HULIS, of which 4,438 were shared between LI and MI, 1,879 were unique to LI, and 787 were unique to MI (Figure 2C). This yields a Jaccard similarity of 0.625 and a Sørensen-Dice coefficient of 0.769, with shared formulas representing 70.3% of the LI set, and 84.9% of the MI set, indicating substantial molecular commonality alongside source-specific distinctions. Class-level contributions were led by CHON and CHO, which together accounted for the largest shares of total signal intensity (35–39% and 30–38%, respectively), whereas sulfur-containing classes (CHOS and CHONS) contributed less (10–20% each by intensity). By formula counts, CHON and CHO represented 41–45% and 23–25%, respectively, with S-containing classes combined (CHOS and CHONS) contributing 30–35%. To isolate source-specific features, we examined unique molecular formulas (Figure S2). In LI unique HULIS, CHON, and CHONS each contributed 31.3% of total intensity and accounted for 32.9% and 30.1% of assigned formulas, respectively, indicating widespread multifunctional molecules containing both nitrogen and sulfur. CHOS species were also substantial (20.9% by intensity; 18.1% by count), whereas CHO-only species constituted the smallest fraction (16.5% by intensity; 18.9% by count). In MI unique HULIS, nitrogen-rich and sulfur-free classes dominated: CHON contributed the largest share (39% by intensity; 45% by count), followed by CHO (28% by intensity; 27% by count). Sulfur-containing compounds were comparatively scarce: CHONS contributed 22% of intensity, but only 17% of formulas, and CHOS contributed 11% of intensity and 12% of formulas. This indicates that secondary processing in MI is mainly reflected by oxidized CHO/CHON products, whereas the enrichment of CHOS/CHONS requires sulfur-containing precursors and is more favored under the sulfur-rich/polluted conditions characteristic of LI.

These differences are consistent with distinct emission profiles and processing pathways in continental versus marine environments. The LI regime likely reflects mixtures of terrestrial organics from biomass burning, fossil-fuel combustion, and biogenic sources.23 Biomass burning emits abundant N-containing HULIS (e.g., aromatic nitro-compounds and organonitrates formed under high-NOx), while urban/industrial pollution (coal combustion, vehicle exhaust) supplies SO2/NOx, enabling aqueous-phase formation of organosulfur species.24 Prior studies have reported higher S-bearing formula abundance in coal/urban aerosols relative to typical biomass-burning HULIS, consistent with the elevated CHONS/CHOS shares in LI unique formulas here.

By contrast, MI HULIS reflects contributions from oceanic precursors (e.g., dimethyl sulfide, isoprene, amino acids, and polysaccharides) introduced via sea spray and marine biogenic emissions. While dimethyl sulfide oxidation forms methanesulfonic acid and related CHOS species characteristic of marine air,25 the comparatively low sulfur content in MI's unique formulas suggests that pure marine organosulfates constitute a modest fraction relative to land-derived sulfur organics transported aloft. The dominance of CHO/CHON in MI unique HULIS points to the extensive photochemical aging of marine volatile organic compounds (VOCs), with longer solar exposure over the ocean favoring the formation of small, highly oxidized acids (e.g., dicarboxylics).26 Reactive nitrogen (NH3/amines from seawater or colonies; nitrate from continental outflow or lightning) further contributes to CHON formation via aqueous reactions of carbonyls (e.g., glyoxal/methylglyoxal) to yield N-heterocycles and CHON oligomers.27 Field observations indicate enhanced CHON diversity in clouds versus non-cloud aerosols.8 Under summer conditions, N-heteroaromatic formation via ammonium-mediated pathways may be favored, offering a mechanistic explanation for the high CHON fraction observed in MI unique formulas.

Discussion

Oxidation-state metrics reveal distinct LI-MI redox regimes

As mentioned above, in-cloud processing produces substantial molecular commonality while preserving source-specific differences, so bulk averages over all assigned formulas do not resolve contrasts between air masses (Table S3). The following comparisons, therefore, use unique formulas to probe LI-MI differences (Table S4). The O/C and OSc indices were used to evaluate the oxidation level of carbon atoms in cloud-water HULIS. The weighted average O/Cw value of LI formulas was higher than that of MI formulas 0.54 vs. 0.45), with the CHONS group contributing the most prominent difference (0.64 vs. 0.37). These patterns imply that LI HULIS contains more oxygenated functionalities, including nitro (–NO2), nitrate (–NO3), and sulfonate (–SO3), which elevate O/C.24 However, O/C alone can overestimate oxidation when heteroatoms such as N and S are abundant, since O associated with N or S does not necessarily increase the oxidation state of carbon.

To correct for this bias, the OSc index was used as a more conservative descriptor of carbon oxidation.28 The abundance-weighted OScw of LI unique HULIS was more negative than that of MI unique HULIS (−0.82 vs. −0.64), which indicates that, despite the higher O/Cw, carbon in LI remains overall more reduced. The contrast was stronger within CHONS, where LI OScw = −1.16 and MI OScw = −1.57. These values reflect a balance between higher oxygenation in LI CHONS and stronger N/S enrichment in MI CHONS. In practical terms, LI CHONS likely derive from continental precursors that undergo extensive aqueous-phase oxidation and form highly functionalized yet partially reduced molecules such as organonitrates or nitrooxy sulfates, whereas MI CHONS retain lower OSc because of a higher proportion of reduced N and S functionalities that are consistent with marine or biogenic sulfur precursors.

The O/Cw values reported here for unique formula sets are comparable to those from the Rocky Mountain region (0.61–0.62) and Mt. Tianjing (0.52), and they are higher than those at Mt. PUY (0.30), where anthropogenic influence is lower.12,13,29 These elevated O/Cw values distinguish the present cloud-water HULIS from typical primary emissions such as biomass burning, coal combustion, and vehicle exhaust, which commonly occupy lower O/C regions (often <0.4). The H/Cw values were similar between LI unique and MI unique HULIS (1.42 vs. 1.41). Sulfur-containing classes showed consistently higher H/Cw (1.45–1.72) than CHO and CHON (1.22–1.31), which indicates more saturated backbones. These values are higher than those reported for primary sources such as biomass burning (1.08–1.25) and coal combustion (0.83–0.90),24 pointing to extensive atmospheric aging and cloud-phase transformation.23 The DBEw values for major classes ranged from 5.26 to 5.46, exceeding those for PM2.5-derived HULIS from the East China Sea (3.49–3.78).30 Elevated DBEw suggests greater oligomerization and structural complexity in cloud droplets, consistent with the mechanism proposed by31 in which adding one to two carbon atoms per oligomer unit increases DBE by approximately one.

Distinct molecular weight (MW) distribution and composition characteristics

Building on the oxidation-based differences for the unique formula sets, we examine molecular weight (MW) distributions because mass captures size-dependent outcomes of cloud-phase aging and oligomerization.29 In both regimes, the unique formulas span 100–800 Da (Figures 4A and 4B) with a broad maximum at 500–600 Da, which accounts for about 30% of formulas. The LI unique distribution is skewed toward mid to high MW constituents, whereas the MI unique distribution contains more low MW species and a slightly stronger high MW tail. Formulas below 200 Da represent about 2% of LI unique compared with about 6% of MI unique, and the 600–800 Da fraction is about 11% for LI unique versus about 15–16% for MI unique. At the upper end (600–800 Da), the class composition diverges. LI unique formulas are dominated by CHOS (organosulfates, about 50% of intensity), whereas MI unique formulas are dominated by CHONS (more than 50% of intensity) (Figure 4C). The LI cloud water, therefore, contains abundant organosulfate oligomers, consistent with aqueous reactions of biogenic precursors such as isoprene in sulfate-rich polluted conditions.33 In contrast, the MI high MW end is enriched in dual N- and S-containing species, indicative of nitrooxy organosulfates formed through nocturnal NO3/N2O5 chemistry and heterogeneous uptake of nitric acid in sulfate-rich marine air.34

The O/C versus MW patterns support these interpretations (Figure 4A). In both regimes, O/C decreases as mass increases, and large HULIS molecules near 750 Da typically exhibit O/C about 0.3–0.4. The MI unique set contains several low MW, very high O/C formulas (about 1.0–1.2) with strong signals that are consistent with diacids produced by marine photochemical processing.35 The LI unique set shows substantial signal from high MW, lower O/C formulas up to 800 Da, indicating efficient oligomerization and accretion in continental cloud water.

Taken together, MW resolved fingerprints of the unique formula sets highlight pronounced contrasts between air masses. LI cloud organics are skewed toward mid to high mass and are characterized by abundant large organosulfates (CHOS) together with high-mass, lower-O/C oligomers, consistent with sulfate-rich continental processing. MI HULIS, in contrast, combines a larger low-mass, very high-O/C component (diacid-like species) with a high-mass pool dominated by CHONS, consistent with nitrooxy organosulfate formation under NO3/N2O5 chemistry. In short, LI is characterized by CHOS-rich oligomer accumulation at high mass, whereas MI is characterized by diacid-rich low-mass together with CHONS-rich high mass.

Van Krevelen fingerprints and cluster endpoints distinguish LI and MI HULIS

Seven distinct groups of molecular formulas were categorized depending on their O/C and H/C ratios (Figures 5A and 5B, and Table S5), following established van Krevelen classification criteria.36 Van Krevelen mapping provides a concise fingerprint of functionality and origin, allowing us to identify which compound classes carry the air-mass imprint in the LI unique and MI unique sets. Compared with LI unique, MI unique exhibits stronger aromatic signatures, with lignin-like and tannin-like classes more abundant (51% vs. 36% and 16% vs. 12%), consistent with polyphenolic inputs and oxidative aging in marine environments.37,38 In contrast, LI uniquely carries more nitrogenous biopolymer-like features, reflected by higher protein or amine-sugar-like and carbohydrate-like fractions (31% vs. 16% and 12% vs. 2%), which aligns with terrestrial plant and soil contributions and biomass-burning phenolics.39,40 MI unique also contains a somewhat larger lipid-like component (11% vs. 7%), consistent with sea spray delivery of fatty acids and algal or microbial lipids.41Figure 5Van Krevelen diagrams and compound class distributions of molecular formulas in LI-unique and MI-unique HULIS(A) Van Krevelen diagram of LI-unique molecular formulas.(B) Van Krevelen diagram of MI-unique molecular formulas.(C) Compound class distribution of LI-unique molecular formulas.(D) Compound class distribution of MI-unique molecular formulas. These two major elemental groups exhibit broadly similar internal distribution patterns (Figures 5C and 5D). For example, CHON formulas are dominated by lignin-like compounds in both LI and MI HULIS (accounting for 47.0% and 48.6% of CHON formulas, respectively), with comparable contributions of protein or amine sugar-like species and carbohydrate-like components. These patterns are consistent with oxidized phenolic and biopolymer signatures produced from plant-derived precursors and combustion emissions.39,42 However, subtle differences are observed in the CHO fraction between LI and MI. MI HULIS features a higher proportion of aromatic lignin-like (52.3%) and polyphenolic tannin-like (16.1%) CHO compounds compared to LI HULIS (49.5% and 12%, respectively), whereas LI shows a greater fraction of protein/amine-sugar-like CHO species (30.7% vs. 22.9% in MI). Carbohydrate-like compounds remain only a minor subset of CHO formulas in both cases (∼2.8% in LI and ∼3.2% in MI). Overall, both LI and MI HULIS share a common dominance of lignin-derived (aromatic) structures in their CHO and CHON components, but the MI sample is enriched in aromatic/polyphenolic CHO compounds, while the LI sample contains relatively more protein-like CHO material.

The largest contrasts occur in sulfur-bearing classes. In CHOS, LI unique is dominated by protein or amine-sugar-like (43.6%) and carbohydrate-like (31.5%) formulas with smaller lignin-like and tannin-like components (9.8% and 10.9%), in line with sulfate esters of saccharides and related biopolymer fragments formed in sulfate-rich conditions.43,44 In MI CHOS, carbohydrate-like formulas are most abundant (39.3%), followed by protein or amine-sugars (25.1%), with higher aromatic shares than LI (17.6% lignin-like and 14.6% tannin-like), consistent with polyphenolic metabolites from marine biota undergoing oxidative aging.38 For CHONS, LI is skewed toward carbohydrate-like (42.5%) with smaller fractions of protein or amine-sugars (26.5%) and tannins (14.7%), whereas MI CHONS shifts toward lipid-like (6.3% vs. 1.0% in LI) and tannin-like (21.1% vs. 14.7%) with a lower carbohydrate share (29.4%). The MI CHONS pattern is consistent with surface-active N,S-containing organics in sea spray, including amine or amide sulfates and their oxidized derivatives, while LI CHONS reflects glycosidic organosulfates and glycoprotein-related components tied to soil and vegetation.41

To test whether the class-level contrasts are robust at the molecular-pattern level, we applied PCA-K-means clustering in van Krevelen space and compared the occupancy of five clusters (C0–C4) between air masses (Figure S3 and Table S6). The clusters span a gradient from lipid-like, low-O/C/high-H/C compositions (C0) through moderately oxidized and unsaturated groups (C1–C2) to highly oxidized small acids/sugars (C3), with C4 representing a transitional, moderately to highly oxidized pool. Two clear enrichments emerge. First, the aromatic/unsaturated cluster C2 is relatively more frequent in the MI set (19.9% vs. 16.4% in LI; odds ratio, OR = 1.27; Fisher p = 0.029), with a centroid of O/C = 0.526, H/C = 0.909, AImod = 0.522, consistent with the higher tannin-/protein-like fractions discussed above for MI. Second, the extremely oxidized cluster C3 is enriched in the LI set (12.3% vs. 7.0% in MI; OR = 0.53; p = 2.8 × 10−5), with a centroid of O/C = 0.887, H/C = 1.729, AImod = −0.884, mirroring the larger carbohydrate/protein signatures and higher O/C seen for LI-unique formulas. By contrast, the lipid endpoint C0 and the transitional oligomeric pool C4 contribute similarly in both regimes (C0: 22.2% vs. 22.0%; C4: 30.7% vs. 31.8%; p > 0.6), indicating a shared background of sea-spray lipids and moderately oxidized oligomers. Considered jointly, the clustering statistics corroborate the VK-class analysis: MI tends toward aromatic/unsaturated endpoints, whereas LI more often reaches a highly oxidized small-acid endpoint, even though both air masses exhibit signatures of extensive in-cloud aging.

Taken together, van Krevelen analysis shows that marine influence enhances aromatic polyphenolic and lipid-like signatures and shifts CHONS toward lipid- and tannin-rich compositions, while continental influence enhances protein or amine-sugar-like and carbohydrate-like signatures and shifts CHOS and CHONS toward saccharide-derived functionalities. These endpoints are consistent with source profiles and processing pathways, where marine emissions and sea spray contribute polyphenols and surface-active N,S-containing organics, and continental outflows supply plant- and soil-derived biopolymers and combustion phenolics that undergo further aqueous-phase transformation into organosulfates and related products.

This study shows that air-mass provenance exerts first-order control over the molecular composition of cloud-water HULIS at Mt. Tai. Although continental and marine regimes share a substantial molecular core, their unique components diverge toward distinct chemical endpoints that persist through in-cloud processing. LI HULIS is skewed toward mid-to high-mass oligomers enriched in CHOS and exhibits higher nominal oxygenation alongside more reduced average carbon states, reflecting different balances of nitrogen and sulfur functionalities. MI HULIS combines a larger pool of low-mass, highly oxygenated diacid-like species with a high-mass tail dominated by CHONS and occupies a more aromatic, unsaturated region in Van Krevelen space, whereas the land regime carries stronger biopolymer signatures with greater protein/amine-sugar and carbohydrate character. Multivariate pattern analysis reinforces these contrasts, indicating the preferential enrichment of aromatic-unsaturated clusters in marine air and of extremely oxidized small-acid clusters in continental air, while lipid-like and transitional oligomeric pools remain broadly comparable across regimes, consistent with a shared sea-spray and moderately oxidized background.

Viewed mechanistically, these fingerprints imply that continental outflow promotes the formation and accretion of CHOS-rich oligomers at high mass and sustains partially reduced CHONS despite higher O/C ratios, whereas marine influence favors two complementary pathways: the production of very high-O/C small acids at low mass and accumulation of CHONS-rich, likely nitrooxy-sulfated compositions at high mass, with sea-spray-derived lipids providing a common baseline. In both air masses, the signatures of extensive in-cloud aging are evident, yet the direction of chemical evolution remains source-conditioned, driving the system toward compositionally distinct endpoints.

Limitations of the study

First, FT-ICR MS provides elemental formulas rather than unambiguous molecular structures; therefore, functional assignments (e.g., organosulfates or nitrooxy organosulfates) remain inferential and should be interpreted as compositional fingerprints. Second, the LI and MI samples were analyzed as composites to obtain sufficient volume for HULIS isolation and ultrahigh-resolution MS measurements. While this approach improves signal coverage and highlights robust regime-level differences, it may dampen event-to-event variability and obscure low-abundance, episodic formulas. Third, ESI(−) ionization and SPE isolation introduce selectivity toward more polar/acidic compounds, and MS signal intensity is semi-quantitative; thus, relative abundance patterns may not directly translate into concentration-weighted composition in bulk cloud water. Future work combining event-resolved samples and orthogonal structural constraints (e.g., tandem MS or NMR, where feasible) would further strengthen source-process attribution.

Method details

Sample collection

During 26 June – 5 August 2021, 36 samples of cloud water were collected from the summit of Mt. Tai located in the North China Plain (latitude 36°16′N, longitude 117°06′E, altitude 1545 meters, Figure 1A). Samples were collected over a period of one to several hours using a single-stage Caltech Active Strand Cloud Collector to ensure sufficient volume for subsequent analysis. A fog monitor (Model FM-120) was placed next to the sampler to measure liquid water content (LWC). The samples were preserved at -4°C in pre-cleaned HDPE bottles for later use. Detailed information for each individual sample is available in Table S1.

Purification of HULIS

For HULIS quantification, each individual cloud-water sample was extracted separately using a 50 mL aliquot. For FT-ICR MS analysis, cloud-water samples were additionally pooled into two composites: one representing marine-influenced events (MI; 13 events, 10 mL from each event) and one representing land-influenced events (LI; 23 events, 10 mL from each event). A 50 mL aliquot of each composite was then extracted following the same procedure described below. Each individual cloud event was categorized as marine-influenced (MI) or land-influenced (LI) based on backward-trajectory analysis (NOAA HYSPLIT; see “Backward trajectory of air masses” below and Figure 1A). Briefly, 36-h trajectories were calculated for each sampling event and grouped using cluster analysis into two dominant regimes. Events characterized by southeasterly transport pathways passing over the western Pacific were classified as MI, whereas those dominated by inland/southwesterly transport were classified as LI. The classification is supported by chemical indicators, with higher sea-salt ions (Na+ and Cl-) in MI and higher terrestrial tracers (e.g., Ca2+ and K+) in LI (Figure 1B and Table S2). Event-level classification is provided in Table S1. Each 50 mL aliquot was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove insoluble particles, then acidified to pH 2 with hydrochloric acid. HULIS were isolated from cloud-water samples using a solid-phase extraction (SPE) procedure with Oasis HLB cartridges following established protocols.5 Oasis HLB cartridges (30 μm particle size, 60 mg sorbent; Waters, USA) were activated by passing 5 mL of HPLC-grade methanol through the cartridge, followed by equilibration with 5 mL of acidified ultrapure water, and then dried under a gentle nitrogen flow. The acidified cloud water sample was loaded onto the cartridge at approximately 1 mL min-1, after which the sorbent was washed with 5 mL of acidified ultrapure water and again dried under N2. HULIS were eluted with 2 mL of 2 % (v/v) ammonia in methanol, and the eluate was concentrated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream. For TOC quantification, the dried HULIS residue was re-dissolved in ultrapure water (Milli-Q). For FT-ICR MS analysis (MI/LI composite extracts), the residue was re-dissolved in 1.5 mL of 50:50 methanol/water (v/v). The extraction protocol, which was validated against the Suwannee River Fulvic Acid standard, achieved recoveries above 90%.

HULIS-C analysis and chemical species analysis

Cloud-water samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to chemical analyses. WSOC was determined on the filtered cloud-water aliquots using a Shimadzu TOC-L analyzer. Water-soluble inorganic ions were measured by ion chromatography (Metrohm 940), and low-molecular-weight organic acids (carboxylates) were analyzed using a Dionex ICS-5000+, both using the same 0.22 μm–filtered cloud-water samples. For HULIS-C quantification, the SPE-isolated HULIS fraction was concentrated to dryness and re-dissolved in ultrapure water (Milli-Q), and the carbon concentration was measured by the TOC-L analyzer.

(ESI-) FT-ICR MS analysis

The sample solutions were introduced into the ESI ion source in the negative ion mode at a controlled flow velocity of 120 μL per hour with the aid of a syringe pump. Mass spectra signals spanning from 100 to 1600 m/z were carefully recorded in high-resolution 4M 32-bit format, with the time-domain signal averaged 300 times to boost the signal-to-noise ratio significantly. The mass spectrometry calibration process was meticulously executed utilizing a 10 mmol L-1 solution of formate sodium. Subsequently, the spectral data underwent re-calibration employing an internally developed reference mass spectrum.

Mass spectral data underwent calibration using well-defined CHO-like compounds found within the DOM, and molecular formula matching was meticulously executed with specific parameters. Peaks with a signal-to-noise ratio greater than 4 were chosen for elemental analysis. Elemental attributions were confined within defined ranges: 1–100 for 12C, 1–200 for 1H, 0–20 for 16O, 0–2 for 14N, and 0–1 for 16S, thus generating potential molecular formulae. Following calibration, the mass error for detected peaks was consistently less than 1 ppm, ensuring high accuracy in formula assignment. Subsequently, the results underwent further refinement based on stringent criteria: molecular formulae with 0.2 ≤ H/C ≤ 2.3, 0 ≤ O/C ≤ 1.2, and –10 ≤ double bond equivalent (DBE) – O ≤ 10 were retained for analysis. In instances where multiple plausible molecular formulae corresponded to a specific m/z value, preference was given to the formula containing the least number of heteroatoms. In the event of persistent non-uniqueness among molecular formulae, the formula exhibiting the smallest error value was prioritized. The analysis of mass spectral peaks was conducted within the m/z range of 100–800 Da.

DBE quantifies the total amount of rings and double bonds within a molecule.45 The calculation method for each formula (CcHhOoNnSs) is represented by Equation 1:(Equation 1)DBE=(2c+2-h+n)/2

The oxidation state of carbon atoms (OSc) was determined using Equation 2, following the estimation approach outlined in46:(Equation 2)OSc≈2×oc−h/c−5×n/c−6×s/c

Aromaticity index (AImod) was calculated using Equation 347:(Equation 3)AImod=(1+c-0.5o-s-0.5h)/(c-0.5o-s-n)

The final parameter results were expressed as a relative abundance-weighted average, denoted with w, to represent the overall level of compounds using Equations 4, 5, 6, and 7:(Equation 4)O/Cw=∑(O/Ci×inti)/∑inti(Equation 5)H/Cw=∑(H/Ci×inti)/∑inti(Equation 6)DBEw=∑(DBEi×inti)/∑inti(Equation 7)OScw=∑(OSci×inti)/∑intiwhere inti denotes the mass spectral intensity corresponding to each unique molecular formula i.

Backward trajectory of air masses

Backward trajectories were computed with the NOAA HYSPLIT model over 36 h to trace the sources and transport pathways of air masses reaching Mt. Tai. All trajectories were initialized at 1545 m (the sampling altitude) and subjected to cluster analysis.

Multivariate analysis (PCA and clustering)

To evaluate whether class-level differences persisted at the molecular-pattern level, PCA and K-means clustering were performed on the LI-unique and MI-unique molecular formula sets. Assigned formulas (m/z 100–800, S/N > 4, mass error < 1 ppm) were described by O/C, H/C, DBE, OSc, AImod, and nominal molecular mass. After z-standardization, PCA was conducted on the correlation matrix, and the retained PC scores were used for clustering analysis. K-means clustering was performed using k-means++ initialization with multiple random starts, and k = 5 was selected according to the average silhouette score, the Calinski–Harabasz index, and chemical interpretability. The robustness of the clustering results was evaluated by varying the feature set, scaling method, and cluster number. LI and MI enrichment within each cluster was assessed using odds ratios derived from 2 × 2 contingency tables and two-sided Fisher’s exact tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied where appropriate.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Detection frequencies (detected vs. not detected) between LI and MI events were compared using Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed). Differences in concentrations between LI and MI were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test (two-tailed), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
