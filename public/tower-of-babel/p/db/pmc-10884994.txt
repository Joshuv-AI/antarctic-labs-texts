Children at high familial risk for obesity show executive functioning deficits prior to development of excess weight status
Authors: Alaina L. Pearce, Kyle Hallisky, Barbara J. Rolls, Stephen J. Wilson, Emma Rose, Charles F. Geier, Hugh Garavan, Kathleen L. Keller
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objective:The objective of this study was to determine whether children with healthy weight who vary by familial risk for obesity differ in executive functioning.Methods:Children (age 7–8 years) without obesity (n = 93, 52% male) who differed by familial risk for obesity (based on maternal weight status) completed go/no-go and stop-signal tasks to assess inhibitory control and an N-back task to assess working memory. Dual energy x-ray absorptiometry measured adiposity. Linear and mixed-effect models assessed unique effects and relative importance analysis-quantified relative effects of familial risk and percent body fat.Results:Children at high compared with low familial risk showed worse inhibitory control; however, child adiposity was not associated with inhibitory control. Both high familial risk and greater child adiposity were associated with worse N-back performance when cognitive demand was high (2-back), but not low (0- and 1-back). The relative effect of familial risk on executive functioning was 2.7 to 16 times greater than the relative effect of percent body fat.Conclusions:These findings provide initial evidence that deficits in executive functioning may precede the development of obesity in children at high familial risk for this disease. Additional family risk studies are needed to elucidate the pathways through which maternal obesity influences child executive functioning and risk for obesity.

INTRODUCTION

The presence of obesity in childhood increases risk for numerous physical and psychosocial sequalae [1], including deficits in cognitive functioning [2]. In particular, childhood obesity has been associated with impaired executive functioning [2]. Executive functioning refers to a set of cognitive processes that support self-regulation, including cognitive flexibility (i.e., the ability to switch between tasks or rule sets), inhibitory control (i.e., the ability to suppress a prepotent or dominant response), and working memory (i.e., the ability to hold and manipulate information in the mind) [3–5]. Childhood obesity has been associated with deficits in all three domains [2], but the current literature is unclear on whether these deficits are present prior to the development of excess adiposity. To advance the field, family risk studies are needed to characterize executive function in children with healthy weight who are at risk for developing obesity in the future.

Extant literature has suggested that the relationship between executive functioning and obesity is bidirectional such that worse executive functioning is associated with the development of obesity [6] and obesity is associated with declines in executive functioning [6]. However, it remains unclear how this “viscous cycle” is set in motion [7]. A challenge to the interpretation of longitudinal studies in this area is that it is often not possible to disentangle the temporal relationship between executive functions and obesity. Indeed, many studies that have shown associations between poorer executive functioning and future obesity have also reported associations between these variables at the first timepoint (i.e., baseline) [8–10]. Although these baseline associations make it difficult to determine the order of effect, it has been theorized that poorer executive functioning increases susceptibility for overconsumption [11, 12], which, over time, leads to accumulation of excess weight. Therefore, it is possible that the “viscous cycle” originates with poor executive functioning and is maintained by chronic inflammatory or other processes related to excess adiposity that negatively impact cognitive function [13, 14]. However, to date, there is a lack of empirical evidence showing that deficits in executive functioning precede development of obesity.

In order to understand temporal relationships between executive function and obesity, it is necessary to study children who differ in risk for obesity before they develop the disease themselves. Compared with children whose mothers have body mass index (BMI) in the normal weight range, children of mothers who have obesity are twice as likely to develop obesity themselves [15] and, therefore, are considered to be at high familial risk for the disease. Although no previous studies to our knowledge have examined whether children at high familial risk for obesity exhibit deficits in executive functioning, supportive evidence can be drawn from family risk studies in adolescence. Prior to the development of obesity, adolescents with high familial risk for obesity showed reduced prefrontal cortex activation in response to food cues [16, 17] compared with those at low familial risk. Because prefrontal cortex supports executive functioning [4], it is reasonable to theorize that, prior to the development of obesity, children at high familial risk might differ in how they engage self-regulatory abilities.

To advance research on the relationship between executive functioning and obesity, the present study examined the association between executive functioning and familial risk for obesity based on maternal weight status among a cohort of children aged 7 to 8 years who had primarily healthy weight status. Inhibitory control and working memory are most frequently targeted in cognitive training interventions in children [18]; therefore, this study focused on these two domains with the hypothesis that children at high risk for obesity would show poorer executive function abilities than those at low risk despite currently having healthy weight.

METHODS

Design

Children in this study were participants in a seven-visit longitudinal study (6 baseline visits with a 1-year follow-up visit) aimed at identifying neurocognitive determinants of pediatric obesity (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03341247). Primary assessments included body composition, parental feeding practices, cognitive function, laboratory eating behaviors, neural food cue reactivity, and actigraphy. The present study used data from four separate baseline visits. Child assent and parental consent were obtained in accordance with the Institutional Review Board of the Pennsylvania State University. Families were compensated for each study visit completed.

Participants

Participants included 93, 7- to 8-year-old (mean [M] = 7.9, standard deviation [SD] = 0.63) children along with the parent who reported primary responsibility for household feeding. Children were classified by familial risk for obesity such that those at high risk (n = 40) had a biological mother with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (+/− 1 BMI), whereas those at low risk (n = 53) had a biological mother with a BMI ≤ 25 kg/m2 (+/− 1 BMI). All children did not have obesity (BMI-for-age < 90th percentile) by study design. Inclusion criteria included the biological mother meeting criteria for one of the familial risk groups and children being in good health based on parent self-report, with no learning or neurodevelopmental disabilities, psychological conditions, or prescribed medications known to influence appetite or cognition. Children were excluded if they were colorblind, could not read at grade level, were not fluent in English, or had magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contraindications (e.g., metal in the body; study functional MRI data to be reported separately). See Table 1 for demographic information by risk group.

Protocol

For each visit, children arrived at the laboratory after fasting for at least 3 h. On the first visit, parents reported demographic information, the height and weight of parent and child were measured, and children’s body composition was assessed (see Anthropometrics). Children’s executive functioning was assessed using three behavioral tasks, which were completed after consumption of a meal or snack to reported fullness. These included the go/no-go task (GNG), the N-back task, and the food-related stop-signal task (SST), which were completed on the second, third, and sixth visits, respectively.

Anthropometrics

Parents and children were weighed and measured in duplicate, without shoes and in light clothing, on a standard scale (Scale-Tronix 5002, Welch Allyn, Inc., Skaneateles Falls, New York; precision to 0.01 kg) and stadiometer (precision to 0.1 cm). The means of these measurements were used to determine parental and child BMI. Child BMI percentile was determined using age- and sex-specific growth charts from the Centers for Disease Control. Of the 93 children, 81 (87%) had their mother accompany them to the first visit to have their height and weight objectively measured. For the remainder of families (n = 12), maternal height and weight were recorded by a proxy (e.g., the father).

Children also underwent whole-body dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Hologic Inc., Marlborough, Massachusetts) for measurement of body composition. DXA uses low-energy x-rays to determine amounts of various tissues in the body, including total lean tissue, fat mass, and bone density. Total percent body fat was the variable of interest for the current study.

Demographic questionnaire

Parents completed a demographic questionnaire assessing race, ethnicity, yearly family income, and parental education. Family income and maternal education were used as proxies for socioeconomic status (Table 1) [19].

Intellectual functioning

The Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI)–II [20] was used to assess intellectual functioning. Children completed the vocabulary and matrix reasoning scales to calculate full-scale intelligence quotient (IQ) to ensure that risk groups did not differ in intellectual functioning (Table 1), because intelligence is related to some executive function domains [5].

Executive function

GNG

A child-friendly GNG was administered using E-Prime 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Inc.) (details in online Supporting Information). Children were told that the animals at the zoo had gotten out and that the orangutans were helping the zookeeper get all of the animals back in their enclosures. They were instructed to press the spacebar when they saw an animal from the zoo (i.e., “go” stimuli) to help bring the animals home but refrain from pressing if they saw an orangutan (i.e., “no-go” stimuli; Figure 1A). After practice, children completed five blocks of 40 trials (10 no-go). Performance was assessed with percent correct “go” responses, percent false alarms (i.e., response to no-go stimulus), and average correct “go” reaction time (RT). Additionally, sensitivity to targets (i.e., go stimuli) was assessed using d’, a measure of sensitivity from signal detection theory, with higher d’ values indicating better performance and negative values or values near zero indicating chance performance [21].

SST

Children completed a food-related SST that was administered using Matlab2018b and Psychtoolbox3 [22] and followed recommended best practices [23, 24]. Plates of food with a triangular napkin placed on either the right or left side of the plate were presented, and children were instructed to sort the plates as quickly as possible by pressing the right or left arrow key based on which side of the plate the napkin was on (go signal). The energy density and amount of food depicted (referred to as portion size throughout) were varied for consistency with research questions related to the larger project. This resulted in a 2 × 2 design with participants completing all four conditions (Figure 1B; details in online Supporting Information). All food images were adapted from a standardized data set [25] and presented across four blocks of 64 trials. Children were told not to respond when the plate was covered by a silver cloche (stop-signal). The silver cloche (stop-signal) was presented on 25% of trials after a variable stop-signal delay (SSD), which was determined using an adaptive stepwise procedure that increased or decreased SSD by 50 ms after each successful or unsuccessful stop, respectively. In order to prevent response slowing, which is a known issue for some participants during this task [26], children were encouraged to respond quickly during the task with the prompt “FASTER” if their response was 1.5 SDs below their average response time from practice.

Stop-signal RT (SSRT), reflecting the relative speed of the “stop” process compared with the “go” process, was computed using the integration method after replacing omitted (i.e., missed) “go” responses with the participants’ slowest go-trial RT [24]. SSRT was only computed if the assumptions of the racehorse model were met (i.e., mean go-trial RT > mean unsuccessful stop-trial RT) [24]. Given the recommendation to have at least 50 stop-trials, primary analyses examined SSRT and SSD across all trials (n stop-trials = 64) [24]. In order to explore the influence of energy density and portion size on inhibitory control, SSRT and SSD were also calculated for each condition.

Working memory

A verbal N-back task with three working memory loads (0-back, 1-back, and 2-back) was administered using E-Prime 2.0. Loads were presented in a fixed order starting with 0-back (lowest load) and ending with 2-back (highest load; Figure 1C). During the 0-back load, children were instructed to press the space bar if the letter “X” (i.e., target) was presented. For the 1- and 2-back loads, children were instructed to respond when the current letter matched the letter that was presented n trials ago (i.e., target; 1-back: “R”-“L”-“L”; 2-back: “B”-“N”-“B”). Letters were serially presented consonant letters so that children could not make words to support meaning (e.g., “D”-“O”-“G”; details in online Supporting Information). Prior to each load, children completed a practice round specific to the upcoming load. Immediately following the practice, children completed two blocks of 30 trials (n targets = 16; 26.7%) for a total of 60 trials per load. Working memory performance was assessed for each load through percent false alarms (i.e., response to non-target) and balanced accuracy, which is computed by averaging the percent of correct responses with the percent of correct rejections (i.e., not responding to non-targets). Similar to GNG, d’ was used as a measure of sensitivity [21].

Data analysis

All analyses were completed in R, and all data and analysis code are publicly available at the following link: https://osf.io/ynjqw/.

Descriptive statistics are reported by risk group, with differences among groups tested using Student t tests for continuous variables (e.g., age, percent body fat) and χ2 for categorical variables (e.g., sex, income). Although none of the children had obesity, there was variability in percent body fat (Table 1). Therefore, the effects of both familial risk and total percent body fat on task performance were examined for each task, with all models adjusted for maternal education, yearly family income, sex, and age. General linear regression was used for all GNG and SST performance metrics. Mixed-effects models, with participant treated as a random effect, were used for SST to determine whether energy density or portion size moderated the effect of familial risk or percent body fat on inhibitory control performance. Similarly, mixed-effects models were used for the N-back task to determine whether working memory load moderated the effect of familial risk or percent body fat on working memory performance. For any performance metrics showing a significant effect of familial risk or percent body fat, exploratory analyses tested the relative influence of the two variables by including them both in the same model and performing a relative importance analysis [27]. Sensitivity analyses tested whether restricting the sample to children with healthy weight (5 children with BMI percentile ≥ 85th percentile) would impact the pattern of results. Additionally, sensitivity analyses tested whether the inclusion of BMI z score attenuated the effect of familial risk on executive functioning.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Children at high and low familial risk did not differ by age, sex, or IQ (p > 0.085). Although the majority of children had mothers who completed a degree beyond high school, children at low risk were more likely to have mothers who completed education beyond a 4-year degree than those at high risk (Fisher’s exact p = 0.008). Similarly, compared with children at high risk, those at low risk were more likely to come from families with yearly income > $100,000 (χ2[2] = 10.37, p = 0.006). Children at high risk had higher BMI percentiles (t[85] = −2.81, p = 0.006) and percent body fat (t[77] = −4.14, p = 0.004) compared with those at low risk (Table 1).

GNG

Children at high risk had higher rates of false alarms than those at low risk (β[standard error (SE)] = 7.76 [3.84], p = 0.047; Table 2, Figure 2A) but did not differ for percent correct responses (p = 0.923), d’ (p = 0.104), or RT (p = 0.826). In contrast, total percent body fat was not associated with percent false alarms (p = 0.334), percent correct responses (p = 0.716), d’ (p = 0.440), or RT (p = 0.934; Figure 2B). This indicates that, compared with children at low risk, those at high risk had more difficulty inhibiting responses but did not differ in overall attention to the task or speed of responding. When both familial risk and percent body fat were added to the model for percent false alarms, the pattern of results was similar but attenuated for familial risk (β[SE] = 7.31 [4.10], p = 0.078) and the same for percent body fat (p = 0.740). Relative importance analysis showed that the relative effect of familial risk was 2.7 times greater than percent body fat. Therefore, the effect of familial risk on children’s inhibitory performance was independent of child adiposity.

SST

Children at high risk had slower SSRT (β[SE] = 69.07 [27.52], p = 0.015) and shorter SSD (β[SE] = −52.11 [22.60], p = 0.025) than children at low risk (Table 3, Figure 3A,C). Total percent body fat was not associated with SSRT (p = 0.168) or SSD (p = 0.052; Figure 3B,D). Exploratory analyses showed no interaction between risk group or percent body fat with either the energy density (p > 0.362) or the portion size (p > 0.229) of the food images for either SSRT or SSD (Table S1; full model in online Supporting Information). This indicates that children at high risk had more difficulty inhibiting responses than those at low risk regardless of the energy content or portion size of the food depicted in the image.

Exploratory analyses, including both familial risk and percent body fat in the model for SSRT, showed a consistent pattern of results such that children at high risk had slower SSRT than those at low risk (β[SE] = 62.79 [28.99], p = 0.034), but there was no relationship between SSRT and percent body fat (p = 0.478). The pattern of results for SSD was similar but attenuated, with children at high risk showing longer SSD than children at low risk (β[SE] = −42.28 [23.54], p = 0.078), whereas no relationship was seen between percent body fat and SSD (p = 0.173). Relative importance analyses indicated that the relative effect of familial risk was 5.5 and 3.6 times greater than percent body fat for SSRT and SSD, respectively. This indicates that familial risk for obesity was associated with worse inhibitory performance, independent of children’s level of adiposity.

N-back task

Across all models, there was a significant effect of working memory load such that all children performed worse for 2-back compared with both 0-back (balanced accuracy: p < 0.002; d’: p < 0.001) and 1-back (balanced accuracy: p < 0.001; d’: p < 0.001), but there was no difference between 0-back and 1-back (balanced accuracy: p > 0.285; d’: p > 0.175). Although there was no difference by risk group when increasing from 0- to 1-back (balanced accuracy: p = 0.128; d’: p = 0.092), there was a significant load-by-risk status interaction when increasing from 0- to 2-back for both balanced accuracy (β[SE] = −7.300 [2.39], p = 0.003) and d’ (β [SE] = −0.54 [0.19], p = 0.005; Table 4, Figure 4A,C). Children at high risk had worse balanced accuracy (t[218] = 2.26, p = 0.025) and d’ (t[207] = 2.13, p = 0.034) than those at low risk during 2-back, but there were no differences by risk status during 0-back (balanced accuracy: p = 0.998; p = 0.158) or 1-back (balanced accuracy: p = 0.765; d’: p = 0.496) conditions. Together, this indicates that children at high risk showed worse working memory compared with children at low risk, but only when cognitive load was high.

Similar to familial risk, there was no difference in association between balanced accuracy and percent body fat when increasing from 0- to 1-back (p = 0.515), but there was a significant load-by-percent body fat interaction when increasing from 0- to 2-back (β[SE] = −0.65 [0.28], p = 0.021). Percent body fat was negatively associated with balanced accuracy during 2-back (β [SE] = −0.45 [0.22], p = 0.041), but not during 0-back (p = 0.919) or 1-back (p = 0.083) conditions. In contrast, working memory load did not moderate the effect of percent body fat on d’ (p < 0.343; Figure 4B,D). There was also no association between percent body fat and d’ (p = 0.578). These findings suggest that overall performance was worse in children with greater adiposity when working memory load was high.

In exploratory analyses including both familial risk and percent body fat, the interaction between working memory load and percent body fat was no longer significant for balanced accuracy (p = 0.183) and remained nonsignificant for d’ (p = 0.948). In contrast, the pattern of interactions between working memory load and familial risk remained the same after adjusting for percent body fat such that increasing from 0- to 2-back more negatively impacted performance for children at high than low risk (balanced accuracy: β[SE] = −6.07 [2.56], p = 0.019; d’: β[SE] = −0.55 [0.20], p = 0.008). Although the interactions remained significant after accounting for percent body fat, the post hoc tests for group difference during 2-back were attenuated for balanced accuracy (p = 0.109) and d’ (p = 0.059). Relative importance analyses indicated that the relative effect of the interaction between block and familial risk was 16 times greater than the interaction with percent body fat. Therefore, the association between familial risk for obesity and poorer working memory was attenuated after adjusting for child adiposity, but the direction of effects remained similar.

Sensitivity analyses

After removing children with overweight (n = 5), the effect of familial risk on executive functioning remained significant for all models except GNG false alarms (p = 0.108; see online Supporting Information for full models). Including BMI score as a covariate did not impact the observed pattern of results for SSRT or N-back performance metrics. However, the inclusion of BMI z score did attenuate the effect of familial risk on GNG false alarms (p = 0.114) and SSD (p = 0.063; see online Supporting Information for full models).

DISCUSSION

This study was novel in its ability to identify deficits in executive functioning in children at high familial risk for obesity prior to the development of the disease itself. Although children were within BMI guidelines for their age and sex, those at high familial risk for obesity exhibited deficits across multiple domains of executive functioning compared with those at low risk. These relationships were independent of child adiposity, indicating a unique component of maternal obesity on child executive functions. The present findings suggest that deficits in executive functioning may precede the development of obesity because children at high risk are twice as likely to develop obesity in the future compared with their counterparts at low risk [15].

Familial risk for obesity, but not child adiposity, was uniquely associated with worse executive functioning. Although inhibitory control is often discussed as a single domain of executive functioning [3, 5], proactive (i.e., inhibition prior to activating motor response) and reactive inhibitory control (i.e., inhibition after motor response is activated) are driven by distinct mechanisms [28, 29]. In this study, proactive inhibitory control was assessed using the GNG, whereas reactive inhibitory control was assessed using the SST. Familial risk for obesity was associated with deficits in both proactive (i.e., GNG) and reactive (i.e., SST) inhibitory control. Similarly, both high familial risk and greater adiposity were associated with worse performance when children’s working memory capacity was taxed, which extends findings from children and adolescents with obesity who showed worse N-back performance at higher cognitive loads [30]. When examining their relative influence, familial risk, but not adiposity, was uniquely predictive of inhibitory control and working memory performance. These findings show that maternal obesity was associated with poorer executive functioning in children prior to the development of excess weight; however, the mechanisms remain unclear.

Although future work is needed to elucidate the mechanisms through which maternal obesity influences child risk for obesity and cognitive function, two possible pathways include genetic contributions and fetal programming. As executive functions are among the most heritable cognitive traits [31, 32], it is possible that there are genetic influences driving the association between familial obesity risk and poorer executing functioning. Indeed, a prior study suggested that up to 80% of the association between greater BMI and worse executive functioning in childhood is due to genetic influences [33]. Additionally, there is evidence that maternal pre-pregnancy BMI is negatively associated with early-childhood cognitive development, potentially due to increased inflammation, suboptimal metabolic state, or nutritional status during pregnancy [34, 35]. However, pre-pregnancy BMI is also associated with lower socioeconomic status and child weight gain, both of which can impact cognitive development [34]. Although risk groups in the current study differed in socioeconomic status (indexed by maternal education and income) and adiposity, the effect of familial risk status on executive functioning was independent of these factors. Longitudinal studies are needed to better characterize the possible pathways through which maternal obesity may influence child executive functioning and risk for obesity.

This study was uniquely designed to examine executive functioning in children who varied by familial risk for obesity prior to the development of excess weight; however, there are some limitations to consider. Although the sample matched the demographic characteristics of central Pennsylvania, there was limited ethnic and racial diversity, which limits the ability to generalize to more diverse or more urban samples. Additionally, although this sample had generally higher income, as is typical of university-based studies, children at high risk had lower income and maternal education than those at low risk. Although socioeconomic status was not related to executive function in this study, samples with a wider range of socioeconomic status and/or food insecurity may show different patterns of results. Additionally, given that all children did not have obesity by design, the range of percent body fat was limited in the sample. Therefore, the pattern of results for adiposity and executive functioning may change in a sample of children with broader range in adiposity. Because familial risk was defined based on only mother’s BMI, the influence of paternal weight status should also be considered in future studies.

These findings provide initial evidence that deficits in executive functioning precede the development of obesity in children who are at high familial risk for obesity. However, longitudinal studies are needed to determine the relative influence of familial risk and executive functioning on future weight status. Similarly, because pre-pregnancy BMI can influence both cognitive development and childhood obesity [34], it is important to distinguish the effects of pre-pregnancy and current maternal BMI. Additionally, given that executive functions are among the most heritable cognitive traits [31, 32], and given that there is evidence for a genetic overlap between executive functioning and obesity in children [33, 36], it is possible that heritability of executive functioning contributes to familial risk for obesity. Because BMI has the highest heritability during adolescence [37], middle childhood may provide an optimal window for prevention and intervention efforts.
