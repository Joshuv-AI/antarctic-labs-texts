The Pathological Roles of Cationic Amino Acid Transporters (CATs) in Cancer
Authors: Thant Thant Myo Oo, Yasuhiro Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCationic amino acid transporters (CATs), encoded by the SLC7A family, play essential roles in the uptake of arginine and other cationic amino acids, thereby regulating cellular metabolism, signaling, and immune responses. Among SLC7A transporters, SLC7A1–4 constitute the classical CAT family and have emerged as important regulators of tumor biology. Arginine is increasingly recognized as a critical metabolic resource within the tumor microenvironment. Consequently, dysregulated CAT expression can affect both cancer cell fitness and tumor–immune interactions. In this review, we summarize current knowledge regarding the biological and pathological roles of SLC7A1–4 in cancer. SLC7A1 is broadly associated with enhanced tumor growth, metabolic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance through increased arginine uptake. In contrast, SLC7A2 exhibits context‐dependent functions, displaying both tumor‐promoting and tumor‐suppressive activities depending on tumor type and immune context regulation. Emerging evidence also implicates SLC7A3 in metastatic progression through transcriptional programs that increase arginine availability, whereas the physiological role of SLC7A4 remains largely unresolved. Despite its structural similarity to other CAT family members, convincing evidence for its membrane localization, cationic amino acid transport activity, and cancer‐associated functions is still lacking. We further discuss the mechanistic pathways linking CAT to cancer progression and tumor–immune metabolic competition. Finally, we highlight key unresolved questions, including functional redundancy among CAT family members, context‐dependent biological effects, and the uncertain role of SLC7A4. A deeper understanding of CAT biology may provide new opportunities for therapeutic strategies targeting amino acid metabolism in cancer.

Introduction

Amino acid transporters are essential regulators of cellular metabolism, growth, and signaling. To meet the increased metabolic demands associated with malignant transformation, cancer cells frequently reprogram amino acid transport systems to support proliferation, survival, and adaptation to environmental stress [1]. Among the diverse transporter families involved in amino acid uptake, the solute carrier (SLC) superfamily contains multiple transport systems that govern the cellular acquisition of essential and non‐essential amino acids. Within this superfamily, the SLC7 family represents a major group of plasma membrane amino acid transporters that play critical roles in both cancer cell metabolism and tumor‐host interactions. The SLC7 family comprises two major transporter subgroups: the L‐type amino acid transporters (LATs) and the cationic amino acid transporters (CATs). Importantly, the designation “L‐type” is part of the transporter nomenclature and should not be confused with the stereochemical designation “L” used for amino acids. While LAT transporters primarily mediate the transport of large neutral amino acids, CATs preferentially transport positively charged amino acids, including L‐arginine, L‐lysine, and L‐ornithine. Among the LAT family members, SLC7A5 (LAT1) and SLC7A11 (xCT) have been extensively investigated and are now recognized as key regulators of tumor metabolism, mTOR signaling [2], oxidative stress responses [3], and therapeutic resistance [4, 5]. Consequently, LAT family transporters have become established therapeutic targets and biomarkers across multiple cancers [6, 7].

In contrast, the cationic amino acid transporter (CAT) family remains comparatively understudied despite its central role in amino acid homeostasis. The CAT family consists of five members, SLC7A1–4 (CAT1–4) and SLC7A14, which share a conserved multi‐pass membrane transporter architecture characteristic of SLC7 proteins [8] but exhibit distinct tissue distributions and regulatory properties. While SLC7A1–3 have been relatively well characterized as functional cationic amino acid transporters, the physiological roles of SLC7A4 [9, 10] and SLC7A14 remain poorly understood, and whether they function as conventional amino acid transporters in vivo remains controversial. CATs are widely regarded as the principal route for cellular uptake of cationic amino acids in mammalian cells, particularly L‐arginine.

Arginine occupies a unique position in cancer biology because it functions not only as a substrate for protein synthesis but also as a precursor for nitric oxide and polyamine biosynthesis [11, 12], both of which contribute to tumor progression. Furthermore, dysregulation of urea‐cycle enzymes, including ASS1 deficiency, renders many tumors partially dependent on extracellular arginine [13]. Beyond cancer cells, arginine availability critically influences T‐cell activation [14], macrophage polarization [15], and anti‐tumor immunity [16]. Consequently, arginine represents a key metabolic resource shared by tumor and immune cells, and competition for arginine within the tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as an important determinant of cancer progression and therapeutic response. Consequently, alterations in CAT‐mediated arginine transport have the potential to influence both tumor cell behavior and the function of surrounding immune cells.

Recent studies suggest that the biological significance of CATs extends beyond simple nutrient acquisition. Within the tumor microenvironment, cancer cells and immune cells compete for limited metabolic resources [17]. Because L‐arginine serves as a substrate for both tumor growth and anti‐tumor immune responses, CATs may function as key regulators of arginine allocation among competing cellular populations. In this context, CAT family members may influence not only metabolic adaptation but also immune remodeling, intercellular communication, and therapeutic responsiveness. Thus, CATs can be viewed as metabolic gatekeepers that shape the balance between tumor‐promoting and tumor‐suppressive activities within the TME (Figure 1).

Despite growing recognition of amino acid transporters in cancer biology, current knowledge remains heavily centered on LAT family members, whereas evidence regarding CATs is dispersed across different tumor types and biological contexts. Consequently, a unified framework explaining how CAT‐mediated arginine transport contributes to tumor progression and tumor–immune interactions is still lacking. Although particular attention is given to breast cancer, where the pathophysiological roles of CAT family members remain poorly defined, the concepts discussed in this review are broadly relevant across multiple malignancies. Particular attention is warranted in breast cancer, where the biological and clinical significance of CAT family members remains incompletely understood despite growing evidence linking amino acid metabolism to disease progression and therapeutic response. Understanding how CATs regulate arginine availability may therefore provide new insights into both tumor biology and therapeutic vulnerability.

In this review, we summarize the structural characteristics, transport functions, and biological activities of SLC7A1–4 and discuss the pathological roles of CATs in cancer. We further propose a conceptual framework in which CATs act as regulators of arginine allocation within the tumor microenvironment, thereby integrating metabolic signaling, immune regulation, and cancer progression. This perspective highlights CAT family members as emerging therapeutic targets and provides a foundation for future studies investigating amino acid transporter‐dependent tumor ecology.

Overview of CAT Family

Structural analysis using the SMART (Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool) revealed that SLC7A1–4 (CAT1–4) and SLC7A14 share a highly conserved membrane transporter architecture (Figure 2). All five proteins are approximately 600–770 amino acids in length and contain 14 predicted transmembrane domains, consistent with their classification as multi‐pass membrane transporters within the SLC7 family. The transmembrane domains are similarly distributed throughout the protein sequences, reflecting strong structural conservation and suggesting a common evolutionary origin. Despite this overall similarity, variations in exon organization, intracellular regions, and low‐complexity sequences may contribute to functional diversification among individual family members.

Members of the CAT family mediate the transport of cationic amino acids, including L‐arginine, L‐lysine, and L‐ornithine. Among these substrates, L‐arginine is of particular biological importance as mentioned above. Although CATs exhibit overlapping substrate specificity, differences in transport kinetics, tissue distribution, and regulatory mechanisms suggest that each family member fulfills distinct physiological functions.

CATs are predominantly localized to the plasma membrane, where they facilitate the uptake of extracellular cationic amino acids (Table 1). SLC7A1 (CAT1) and SLC7A2 (CAT2) are broadly expressed across multiple tissues and are considered the principal transporters responsible for cellular arginine uptake. In contrast, SLC7A3 (CAT3) displays a more restricted expression pattern, with predominant expression in the thymus, brain, ovary, and testis. SLC7A4 (CAT4) and SLC7A14 are the least characterized members of the family. Although SLC7A4 and SLC7A14 share substantial structural similarity with other CATs, their physiological function remains incompletely understood, and whether they function as bona fide cationic amino acid transporters under physiological conditions remains controversial. Therefore, we do not discuss the details of SLC7A4 and SLC7A14 in this review.

Together, the CAT family exhibits a conserved structural framework while displaying considerable diversity in expression patterns, transport activity, and biological functions. These shared and distinct characteristics suggest that individual CATs may play both overlapping and specialized roles in cancer biology. The following sections summarize the current understanding of each CAT family member and discuss their emerging contributions to tumor metabolism, tumor–immune interactions, and cancer progression.

SLC7A1 (CAT1)

SLC7A1 (CAT1) is the predominant transporter responsible for cellular uptake of cationic amino acids, particularly arginine, and is strategically positioned at the interface between metabolic adaptation and immune regulation in cancer (Figure 3).

Accumulating evidence indicates that SLC7A1 supports tumor progression by maintaining intracellular arginine availability under nutrient‐restricted conditions [18, 19]. Arginine depletion activates the amino acid stress sensor GCN2, which triggers the eIF2α–ATF4 integrated stress response and promotes compensatory upregulation of SLC7A1, thereby helping to restore intracellular amino acid availability [19]. This adaptive response may allow cancer cells to withstand metabolic stress within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Consistently, elevated SLC7A1 expression has been associated with activation of proliferative programs, including MYC, E2F, and Notch signaling pathways [20], whereas genetic suppression of SLC7A1 inhibits tumor growth and invasive behavior in multiple cancer models [20, 21]. Furthermore, the high levels of SLC7A1 showed poor clinical prognosis in ovarian cancer [22].

Beyond its cell‐autonomous functions, SLC7A1 appears to influence the composition and activity of the TME. Increased SLC7A1 expression has been associated with M2‐like macrophage polarization and the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [23]. Furthermore, recent CRISPR‐based studies demonstrated that SLC7A1 cooperates with SLC38A2 to regulate CD8+ T‐cell fate through mTORC1 signaling [24], promoting effector differentiation while limiting memory formation. These findings suggest that SLC7A1 regulates not only nutrient acquisition by tumor cells but also the availability of arginine to immune populations.

The dual involvement of SLC7A1 in both tumor growth and anti‐tumor immunity highlights a potential therapeutic dilemma. While inhibition of SLC7A1 may restrict tumor cell proliferation through arginine starvation, it could simultaneously impair T‐cell function. Therefore, the biological outcome of SLC7A1 targeting is likely to depend on the relative contributions of tumor‐intrinsic and immune‐mediated effects in individual tumor contexts.

Collectively, current evidence supports a model in which SLC7A1 functions as a metabolic gatekeeper that coordinates arginine allocation within the TME, thereby linking nutrient availability to cancer progression, immune regulation, and therapeutic responsiveness.

SLC7A2 (CAT2)

Among CAT family members, SLC7A2 exhibits the most context‐dependent functions in cancer. SLC7A2 generates two alternatively spliced isoforms, CAT2A and CAT2B, which differ in their N‐terminal sequences and regulatory characteristics [8, 25]. Experimental studies have suggested that these isoforms may also differ in transport kinetics, substrate affinity, expression patterns, and inducibility. In particular, CAT2B has been reported to be induced under inflammatory conditions and may facilitate adaptive cationic amino acid uptake in response to increased metabolic demand [8, 25]. However, the functional distinction between CAT2A and CAT2B, especially in humans, remains incompletely characterized.

Recent studies have identified several tumor‐promoting mechanisms downstream of SLC7A2 (Figure 3). In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, SLC7A2‐mediated arginine uptake facilitates mTORC1 activation and promotes invasive behavior [26]. In glioblastoma stem cells, enhanced lysine transport through SLC7A2 contributes to lysine catabolism and subsequent histone crotonylation, resulting in suppression of interferon signaling and promotion of immune evasion [27]. These findings suggest that SLC7A2 can couple amino acid transport to metabolic and signaling pathways that support tumor progression.

In contrast, multiple studies support a tumor‐suppressive role for SLC7A2 (Figure 3). Loss of SLC7A2 promotes the accumulation of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), enhances immune suppression, and accelerates hepatocellular carcinoma progression [28]. Similarly, reduced SLC7A2 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and therapeutic resistance in non‐small cell lung cancer [29], whereas restoration of SLC7A2 suppresses lymphatic metastasis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [30].

Collectively, these seemingly contradictory observations indicate that the biological functions of SLC7A2 are highly dependent on cellular and microenvironmental context. Factors such as tumor type, inflammatory status, immune cell composition, and extracellular amino acid availability may influence the consequences of SLC7A2 activity. Unlike SLC7A1, which predominantly exhibits tumor‐promoting functions, SLC7A2 may either promote or restrain tumor progression depending on the balance between cancer cell metabolic demands and immune regulatory effects within the tumor microenvironment. Future studies distinguishing the respective contributions of CAT2A and CAT2B, as well as their functions in tumor and immune cells, will be essential for understanding this complexity.

SLC7A3 (CAT3)

Compared with SLC7A1 and SLC7A2, the role of SLC7A3 in cancer appears to be more specialized. Current evidence primarily links SLC7A3 to metastatic progression through enhanced arginine utilization (Figure 3).

In osteosarcoma, SIRPα has been reported to promote metastatic progression through transcriptional regulation of SLC7A3. Mechanistically, SIRPα stabilizes the transcription factor SP1, which binds to the SLC7A3 promoter and enhances its transcription. Increased SLC7A3 expression subsequently elevates arginine uptake, supporting metabolic adaptation associated with cell migration and metastasis. These findings suggest a SIRPα–SP1–SLC7A3 signaling axis that links transcriptional regulation of amino acid transport to metastatic behavior [31]. These findings position SLC7A3 as a downstream effector connecting extracellular nutrient availability to metastatic behavior.

However, evidence from breast cancer suggests a more complex role. Higher SLC7A3 expression correlates with favorable prognosis in early‐stage disease [32], indicating that its biological effects may vary according to tumor stage or microenvironmental conditions. The mechanisms underlying this apparent functional switch remain unclear.

Notably, genetic ablation of SLC7A3 does not substantially impair hematopoiesis, leukemogenesis, or T‐cell development [33]. This observation highlights a fundamental feature of CAT biology: functional redundancy. Because multiple CATs can mediate arginine uptake, inhibition of a single transporter may be insufficient to disrupt metabolic adaptation. Therefore, the therapeutic significance of SLC7A3 is likely to depend on transporter expression patterns within specific tumor contexts.

SLC7A4 (CAT4)

SLC7A4 remains the least understood member of the CAT family. Although it shares substantial structural similarity with other CATs, convincing evidence supporting its plasma membrane localization and robust cationic amino acid transport activity remains limited. Indeed, reports suggesting predominantly cytoplasmic localization raise questions as to whether SLC7A4 functions as a canonical amino acid transporter. Consequently, it remains unclear whether SLC7A4 acts as a bona fide transporter, exhibits context‐dependent localization, or fulfills alternative cellular functions independent of amino acid transport.

Current evidence linking SLC7A4 to cancer is largely restricted to correlative studies. In breast cancer, higher SLC7A4 expression has been associated with favorable clinical outcomes, including prolonged overall and relapse‐free survival [34]. However, functional and mechanistic studies are currently lacking, precluding definitive conclusions regarding its biological significance or mode of action.

The discrepancy between the structural conservation of SLC7A4 and the limited evidence for its localization and transport function highlights one of the most important unresolved questions within the CAT family. Determining whether SLC7A4 functions as a transporter, possesses non‐canonical regulatory activities, or exhibits context‐dependent cellular localization will be essential for understanding its physiological role and potential contribution to tumor progression and tumor–immune interactions.

Emerging Concepts and Unresolved Questions

Although individual CAT family members have been investigated in different cancer types, several common themes emerge from the current literature. Collectively, these findings suggest that CATs function not merely as amino acid transporters but as critical regulators of arginine allocation within the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Arginine Allocation as a Determinant of Tumor–Immune Interactions

Arginine is an essential metabolic substrate for both cancer cells and immune cells. While tumor cells require arginine to sustain protein synthesis, polyamine production, and mTORC1 activation, immune cells utilize arginine for proliferation, activation, and effector functions. Consequently, CATs may influence the outcome of tumor–immune competition by regulating cellular access to extracellular arginine.

Current evidence indicates that elevated SLC7A1 expression enhances tumor growth and promotes an immunosuppressive microenvironment through M2‐like macrophage polarization. Conversely, SLC7A1‐mediated arginine uptake is also required for optimal CD8+ T‐cell differentiation and activation. These observations highlight a fundamental challenge in targeting arginine transporters therapeutically: inhibition of arginine uptake may simultaneously suppress tumor growth and anti‐tumor immunity. Therefore, future studies should evaluate CAT function within multicellular TME models rather than cancer cells alone.

Context‐Dependent Functions of CATs

Another recurring observation is the context‐dependent nature of CAT family members. Although SLC7A1 predominantly exhibits tumor‐promoting activity, SLC7A2 and SLC7A3 have been reported to exert both tumor‐promoting and tumor‐suppressive effects depending on cancer type and disease stage.

For example, SLC7A2 promotes mTORC1 activation and immune evasion in pancreatic cancer [26] and glioblastoma [27], whereas loss of SLC7A2 enhances MDSC accumulation and accelerates tumor progression in hepatocellular carcinoma [30]. Similarly, SLC7A3 promotes metastasis in osteosarcoma [31] but is associated with favorable prognosis in early‐stage breast cancer [32]. These findings suggest that the biological consequences of CAT activity are shaped by tumor‐specific metabolic demands and immune contexture. Defining the determinants of these context‐dependent effects remains an important challenge for the field.

Functional Redundancy and Therapeutic Limitations

The therapeutic targeting of CATs may be further complicated by functional redundancy among amino acid transport systems. Experimental evidence demonstrates that loss of SLC7A3 alone does not significantly impair hematopoiesis, leukemogenesis, or T‐cell development, indicating that alternative transporters can compensate for reduced arginine uptake.

This redundancy suggests that inhibition of a single transporter may be insufficient to achieve durable metabolic suppression in tumors. Future therapeutic strategies may therefore require simultaneous targeting of multiple arginine transport pathways or combination approaches integrating metabolic therapy with immunotherapy.

Knowledge Gaps and Future Perspectives

Among CAT family members, SLC7A4 remains the least characterized despite its strong structural similarity to other transporters. Whether SLC7A4 functions as a bona fide amino acid transporter, a regulatory protein, or a context‐specific metabolic sensor remains unknown. Resolving this question may reveal previously unrecognized mechanisms linking amino acid transport to tumor progression and immune regulation.

More broadly, most current studies rely on transporter expression analyses or gain‐ and loss‐of‐function experiments, whereas direct measurements of arginine flux and nutrient competition within the TME remain limited. Integration of metabolomics, spatial transcriptomics, single‐cell technologies, and functional immunology will be essential to define how CATs coordinate metabolic communication among tumor cells and immune cells.

Taken together, current evidence supports a conceptual framework in which CAT family members regulate the distribution of arginine within the TME, thereby influencing tumor progression, immune responses, and therapeutic sensitivity. Understanding this metabolic network may provide new opportunities for precision therapies targeting amino acid utilization in cancer.

Conclusion

The CAT family transporters SLC7A1–4 have emerged as important regulators of cancer biology through their ability to control cellular uptake of arginine and other cationic amino acids. Among these family members, SLC7A1–3 are established arginine transporters that influence diverse processes involved in tumor progression, including metabolic adaptation, mTORC1 activation, immune cell polarization, and metastatic dissemination. These observations highlight the growing importance of amino acid transport systems as integral components of the metabolic networks that shape tumor behavior and therapeutic responses.

A recurring theme emerging from current studies is that CATs function not merely as nutrient transporters, but as regulators of arginine allocation within the tumor microenvironment. By controlling access to a critical metabolic resource, CAT family members can influence both tumor‐intrinsic programs and tumor‐extrinsic processes involving immune cells. This concept may help explain the context‐dependent functions reported for individual CATs. While SLC7A1 predominantly exhibits tumor‐promoting activities, SLC7A2 and SLC7A3 have been reported to exert either tumor‐promoting or tumor‐suppressive effects depending on the cellular and immunological context. These findings underscore the complexity of nutrient competition within the tumor microenvironment and suggest that the biological consequences of CAT activity extend beyond cancer cell metabolism alone.

Despite these advances, several important questions remain unresolved. Most available studies rely on correlative analyses or phenotypic changes following transporter knockdown or overexpression, whereas the molecular mechanisms linking CAT‐mediated amino acid transport to downstream signaling pathways remain incompletely defined. Furthermore, the relative contributions of cancer cells and immune cells to CAT‐dependent phenotypes have not been systematically evaluated. Functional redundancy among CAT family members may represent an additional challenge, as compensatory transport systems could maintain arginine uptake despite inhibition of individual transporters. The biological function of SLC7A4 also remains largely unknown despite its structural similarity to other CAT family members, representing a major knowledge gap in the field.

Future studies integrating cancer metabolism, immunology, and transporter biology will be required to establish a comprehensive understanding of CAT family function across tumor types. Such efforts may identify context‐specific vulnerabilities and facilitate the development of therapeutic strategies that target amino acid utilization while preserving anti‐tumor immunity. Ultimately, defining how CATs coordinate arginine allocation within the tumor microenvironment may provide a conceptual framework for understanding metabolic competition in cancer and reveal new opportunities for precision cancer therapy.
