Research progress on pathogenic genes of dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Xiao Zhang, Xinyu Zhang, Yunru Chen, Na Huan, Chenglong Wang, Dawu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a primary myocardial disorder characterized by progressive ventricular dilatation and impaired myocardial systolic contractility, and it represents the most common form of cardiomyopathy globally. DCM drives a substantial worldwide disease burden, thus presenting a formidable and persistent challenge to global public health systems. The pathogenesis of DCM is marked by extreme etiological heterogeneity: 30%–50% of cases have a familial origin, with genetic determinants serving as the core driver of disease onset and progression. With the rapid advancement and widespread application of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, a growing repertoire of DCM-causative genes has been successfully identified. These genes encode key functional proteins that regulate multiple core physiological processes in cardiomyocytes, including sarcomeric structure maintenance, intracellular signal transduction, and myocardial energy homeostasis. DCM-causing genes can be classified into multiple categories according to their functions. Sarcomeric protein genes (such as TTN, MYH7, and TNNT2) disrupt sarcomere assembly and contractile function through mechanisms such as haploinsufficiency and the toxic peptide hypothesis; mutations in nuclear membrane protein genes (such as LMNA and EMD) cause abnormal nuclear structure and disordered mechanotransduction signals; ion channel genes (such as SCN5A, CACNA1C, and RYR2) affect electrophysiological balance and calcium handling; desmosome-related genes (such as DSC2 and DSP) are associated with abnormal cell junctions and dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway; KLF13, ETS1, and BMP10 are possible candidate genes for DCM with limited research; loss of function of RBM20 leads to abnormal splicing of TTN, CamkIIδ, RyR2, etc. and causes nuclear import defects as well as cytoplasmic RBM20-RNP granule toxicity, thereby driving ventricular dilation. These genes drive myocardial remodeling through common signaling pathways (such as ERK and TGF-β). Potential treatment strategies include gene-level interventions, targeted pathway inhibitors, and myosin activators. However, genetic heterogeneity results in a narrow applicable population for single-gene therapies. Future research needs to shift from targeting individual genes to improving the common pathological environment to achieve broad-spectrum treatment. Exploring upstream prevention of mutations or activation of endogenous repair mechanisms provides new directions for the treatment of DCM.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, internal medicine, cardiovascular medicine

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a myocardial disease characterized by ventricular enlargement and impaired myocardial contractility. Its etiology requires exclusion of secondary ventricular dysfunction caused by coronary artery disease, hypertension, valvular disease, or congenital heart disease.1 In 2018, among individuals aged ≥36 years in the United Kingdom, DCM was the most prevalent cardiomyopathy phenotype, with an overall prevalence of 4.3 per 10,000.2 Analysis of national electronic health record data from the United States 2017–2019 revealed a DCM prevalence rate of 11.8 per 100,000. Extrapolating this to the 2019 U.S. general population, the DCM burden was estimated at approximately 388,000 individuals3; survey data from China 2017–2021 indicate that DCM and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) collectively constitute the core clinical presentations of cardiomyopathy, accounting for over 90% of all outpatient and inpatient cardiomyopathy cases.4 However, DCM patients represent approximately twice the proportion of HCM patients among hospitalized cases,4 directly reflecting the more severe clinical symptoms and greater urgency for hospitalization among DCM patients. In summary, DCM presents a high disease burden globally, posing significant challenges to public health systems worldwide.

The etiology of DCM exhibits high heterogeneity, encompassing both genetic and non-genetic factors. Approximately 30%–50% of DCM cases are considered familial, indicating that genetic factors play an important role.5 With the continuous advancement and innovation of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology, bioinformatics analysis, and functional research methods, an increasing number of disease-causing genes associated with DCM have been discovered and identified. These genes play indispensable roles in key physiological processes of cardiomyocytes, including structural composition, signal transduction, and energy metabolism.6 Different disease-causing genes, through their unique molecular mechanisms, synergistically or independently induce cardiomyocyte dysfunction, leading to myocardial remodeling and impaired cardiac function. A 2023 review of DCM published in The Lancet systematically examined the genetic and acquired causes of the disease, specifically noting that pathogenic gene variants may still exist in acquired DCM. Therefore, diagnosis and treatment should simultaneously consider a combined model of “genetics+triggers”7; in 2024, Ramone Eldemire et al. conducted a systematic review on DCM diagnosis, imaging, genetics, and emerging therapies, emphasizing that precision medicine has become the forefront of DCM treatment8; a 2025 review in Nature Reviews Cardiology centered on DCM’s monogenic causes, acquired etiologies, inflammation-mediated pathomechanisms, and the “second hit” hypothesis of gene-environment interactions, noting a comprehensive shift toward genotype-centered precision medicine.9 Despite numerous excellent reviews, most focus on providing an overview of DCM and its developmental trajectory, with limited exploration of specific mechanisms corresponding to particular genotypes. Therefore, this paper systematically reviews research progress on DCM-causing genes from multiple dimensions—including pathogenic gene classification, molecular mechanisms, and clinical translation—based on the function and action pathways of gene products. It aims to provide a solid theoretical foundation and novel research perspectives for DCM treatment and prognostic assessment.

Sarcomere protein genes

Sarcomere is the fundamental unit of myocardial contraction, composed of ordered arrangements of thick and thin filaments. Its structural and functional integrity directly determines myocardial contractility. Mutations in genes encoding sarcomere proteins can disrupt normal sarcomere assembly and contraction mechanisms, leading to myocardial contractile dysfunction. This represents one of the key pathogenic mechanisms underlying DCM.

TTN

The titin (TTN) is located on the 2q31.1, spanning 280 kb in the human genome and comprising 364 exons.10 TTN encodes the largest protein in cardiomyocytes, extending from the Z-disc to the M-line (Figure 1D). It serves as the core structural protein maintaining sarcomere integrity and stability.10 TTN possesses multiple functional domains, enabling it to fulfill diverse roles in cardiomyocytes: (1) structural scaffolding: precisely positions thick filaments at the sarcomere center, ensuring ordered assembly and structural stability11; (2) molecular spring function: TTN’s I-band region is rich in extensible tandem immunoglobulin-like domains and PEVK-enriched regions, conferring passive tension and elasticity to cardiomyocytes, which is crucial for cardiac diastolic function12; (3) signaling hub: specific domains interact with proteins to regulate sarcomere signaling, gene expression, and protein turnover, enabling responses to mechanical stress and modulating cardiac growth and remodeling.13 TTN truncation variants (TTNtv) represent the most common genetic cause of DCM, present in 15% of outpatient DCM patients and 25% of end-stage or familial DCM patients.14 Recent studies indicate that TTNtv is not the result of a single factor but rather a complex process triggered by initial protein dosage deficiency, cascading and amplifying into myofibrillar dysfunction, organelle homeostasis disruption, and signaling network dysregulation.

Haploinsufficiency and the toxic peptide hypothesis of TTN

Currently, haploinsufficiency is widely recognized as the primary pathogenic mechanism of TTNtv.15 Most TTNtv mutations introduce a premature termination codon (PTC). The cellular surveillance system—specifically, nonsense-mediated mRNA degradation (nMD)—recognizes and degrades mutant TTN mRNA containing PTCs.16 This results in a significant reduction of full-length, functional TTN protein produced by the mutant allele, ultimately leading to a total TTN protein level in cardiomyocytes of approximately only 50% of the normal level.17 This reduction in TTN dosage directly disrupts sarcomere balance, initiating a cascade of subsequent pathological changes.18 In contrast, the “toxic peptide” hypothesis proposes that a fraction of mutant mRNA may evade NMD surveillance, translating into a truncated TTN protein fragment. If this “toxic peptide” persists stably and integrates into the sarcomere, it may interfere with normal TTN function or interact abnormally with other sarcomeric proteins, thereby producing a dominant negative effect.19

Recent studies increasingly support the haploinsufficiency hypothesis. Shahnaz et al. found that CRISPRa increased levels of truncated TTN protein (previously thought to potentially exacerbate toxic peptide effects), yet cardiac function was fully restored without cellular stress toxicity from protein overload.20 This result demonstrates that cardiac function recovery stems from compensating for the deficiency of full-length TTN, rather than eliminating toxin peptides. Thus, haploinsufficiency represents the primary pathogenic mechanism of TTNtv. Separate studies revealed that I-band variants cause only haploinsufficiency of full-length TTN, whereas A-band variants generate stable truncated proteins. These toxic peptides integrate into newly formed myofibrillar structures, disrupting sarcomere formation and impairing contractile function.19 This demonstrates that the toxic peptide hypothesis is not entirely incompatible with haploinsufficiency. Quentin et al. first definitively detected truncated TTN in human TTNtv-DCM hearts and confirmed reduced full-length TTN protein levels.21 This demonstrates that TTNtv-associated DCM is a disease co-driven by haploinsufficiency and toxic peptides: reduced full-length TTN establishes the disease foundation, whereas truncated TTN proteins further disrupt sarcomere function. Both mechanisms synergistically cause DCM onset and progression.

It should be noted that as of February 2026, the NCBI ClinVar database has cataloged 702 pathogenic variants and 5,078 likely pathogenic variants within the TTN gene. Among these, 39 pathogenic/likely pathogenic missense mutations are explicitly annotated, confirming the pathogenic classification of non-truncating TTN mutations. When missense mutations occur within the core folding regions of TTN protein domains, they induce domain conformation instability and misfolding. This not only directly disrupts the molecular spring function of TTN but also leads to accumulation of misfolded proteins within cardiomyocytes, potentially triggering proteotoxic stress and adverse myocardial remodeling.10 However, non-truncating mutation-associated DCM accounts for less than 5% of cases and predominantly occurs in rare familial or sporadic cases.10

Impairment of sarcomere assembly, structural integrity, and contractile function

As the template and core scaffold for sarcomere assembly, reduced TTN protein dosage directly leads to structural and functional defects in sarcomeres.22 Firstly, TTN deficiency severely impairs sarcomere formation and assembly. Studies indicate that TTNtv disrupts the critical balance between sarcomere formation and adaptive remodeling, resulting in sarcomere assembly defects and structural irregularities.23 In induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte (iPSC-CM) models from TTNtv patients, pronounced ultrastructural alterations—including disorganized myofibril arrangement and abnormal Z-disk architecture—are observable.24 Secondly, reduced TTN compromises sarcomere structural integrity and mechanical stability, impairing the heart’s capacity to adapt to hemodynamic stress.25 Gramlich et al. first discovered that TTN translocation mutation mouse models exhibited normal baseline phenotypes, but prolonged exposure to angiotensin or isoproterenol environments led to cardiac dilation, reduced ejection fraction, and myocardial fibrosis changes.26,27 Subsequent studies confirmed that cardiomyocytes harboring TTNtv mutations demonstrated decreased contractility, abnormal calcium handling, and weakened responses to β-adrenergic stimulation.22 This indicates that TTN variants represent only a genetic predisposition, requiring additional triggers to induce the DCM phenotype. This may also explain why carriers in the general population remain asymptomatic. Ultimately, these structural defects inevitably lead to myocardial contractile dysfunction.

Organelle dysfunction

TTNtv not only affects the sarcomere itself but also triggers widespread organelle dysfunction, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy.28,29 TTNtv-mutated cardiomyocytes exhibit significant mitochondrial dysfunction, manifested as decreased oxygen consumption rate, reduced ATP production, impaired electron transport chain activity, and abnormal mitochondrial structure.29 RNA-sequencing analysis of myocardial tissue from TTNtv patients further confirms significant alterations in pathways related to cardiac energy metabolism. Gene expression for all electron transport chain complexes (I–V) is markedly upregulated, yet left ventricular mass is reduced.30 This TTNtv-induced reprogramming of mitochondrial energy metabolism renders cardiomyocytes incapable of meeting the energy demands of high-intensity contractile activity, serving as a key driver of DCM progression. Accumulation of autophagy proteins and increased autophagosome numbers in TTNtv cardiomyocytes indicate an imbalance in autophagosome formation and degradation processes, triggering severe mitochondrial dysfunction with heightened mitochondrial protein ubiquitination and acetylation.28 Impaired mitochondria should be cleared via mitophagy, but disrupted autophagic flux leads to massive intracellular accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria. This further exacerbates oxidative stress and cellular damage, creating a vicious cycle.

Therefore, TTNtv is the primary genetic cause of DCM, with pathogenicity centered on haploinsufficiency as the core mechanism. Toxic peptides exert synergistic effects, further triggering sarcomere assembly abnormalities, contractile dysfunction, and disorders in organelles, such as mitochondria and autophagy. The multi-mechanistic synergy and variant-specific differences in TTNtv pathogenesis suggest that DCM treatment requires individualized precision strategies. Combining compensation of full-length troponin with degradation of truncated toxic peptides during therapeutic planning may enhance the potential efficacy of gene therapy while reducing off-target effects. Non-genetic stressors like obesity amplify the “dose-dependent defect” of full-length myosin by increasing myocardial energy demand and mechanical load, thereby accelerating sarcomere disassembly and mitochondrial dysfunction. This underscores the need for TTNtv carriers to combine genetic screening with lifestyle interventions to prevent disease onset and progression.

MYH7

The MYH7 gene is localized on the 14q11.2, with a total length of approximately 22.9 kb and containing 40 exons. The cardiac β-myosin heavy chain (β-MHC) encoded by the MYH7 gene is a major component of the sarcomere thick filament in cardiac muscle (Figure 1C) and serves as the “molecular motor” driving cardiac contraction.31 Traditionally, MYH7 mutations have been considered the most common genetic cause of HCM.32 However, a growing body of clinical genetics research confirms that distinct MYH7 mutations can also cause DCM, exhibiting significant phenotypic heterogeneity.33 A study of an Indian cohort of DCM patients identified shared genetic variants in exons 7, 12, 19, and 20 of the β-MYH7 gene across both HCM and DCM patients. Variants in HCM were predominantly heterozygous, whereas variants at the same loci in DCM were consistently homozygous.34 This phenomenon reveals the complexity of MYH7 mutation-induced pathology, where identical variants in the same gene can lead to diametrically opposed myocardial remodeling patterns under different genetic backgrounds or environmental conditions.

The β-MHC encoded by the MYH7 gene is located at the center of cardiac myofibrils. Its head domain possesses ATPase activity and actin-binding sites, responsible for converting chemical energy into mechanical work to drive myocardial contraction (Figure 1C).35 Therefore, the mechanism by which MYH7 mutations initiate DCM most directly stems from their disruption of myofibrillar structure and function.

Haploinsufficiency and the toxic peptide hypothesis of MYH7

DCM-associated MYH7 missense mutations primarily exert their effects through a “toxic protein” or “dominant-negative” mechanism. The mutant β-MHC protein is incorporated into the thick filaments of the sarcomere, where its aberrant conformation or function disrupts the stability and cooperative function of the entire sarcomeric complex. Recent bioinformatic and structural modeling studies indicate that MYH7 missense mutations can alter the three-dimensional structure and function of the protein.36 These structural alterations can directly impair core functions of the myosin motor head, affecting ATP binding or hydrolysis rates, leading to inefficient energy conversion and diminished power supply for myocardial contraction.37 They may also modify myosin affinity for actin on thin filaments or alter cross-bridge cycling dynamics, resulting in reduced contractile force or abnormal contraction/relaxation processes.38 Chronic impaired force generation directly drives the DCM phenotype. To maintain effective pumping function, the heart initiates compensatory mechanisms, such as increasing preload via the Frank-Starling mechanism, leading to progressive ventricular dilation and ultimately forming the hallmark pathology of DCM.39 Beyond toxic protein effects, another potential mechanism is “haploinsufficiency.” Certain MYH7 frameshift mutations may cause degradation of the mutant messenger RNA (mRNA) or production of nonfunctional truncated proteins.36 This results in insufficient total β-MHC protein produced by the healthy allele to sustain normal sarcomere function, ultimately leading to diminished myocardial contractility. Although less common than toxic effects in DCM, this mechanism may still contribute in specific mutation types.

Dysregulation of downstream cellular signaling cascades triggered by MYH7 mutations

Primary sarcomere dysfunction subjects cardiomyocytes to prolonged mechanical and metabolic stress, thereby activating a series of complex downstream signaling pathways. Chronic dysregulation of these pathways constitutes a key pathophysiological mechanism driving the progression of DCM. The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, particularly its downstream extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) branch, serves as a pivotal hub regulating cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis and stress responses.40 Under physiological conditions, the ERK signaling pathway plays a role in cardiac development and adaptive hypertrophy; however, its persistent abnormal activation drives pathological cardiac remodeling.41

Lingyu Wang et al. unequivocally demonstrated that MYH7 mutations induce marked and sustained activation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway.42 The sarcomeric dysfunction caused by MYH7mutations generates abnormal intrinsic mechanical tension within the cardiomyocyte.42 This persistent endogenous mechanical stress is sensed by intracellular mechanosensors, leading to the activation of upstream kinases and culminating in the phosphorylation and activation of ERK1/2.42 This process promotes cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, upregulates inflammatory cytokine expression, and may ultimately induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis.42 This transition from compensatory hypertrophy to decompensated dilation represents a core feature of DCM progression, with excessive ERK pathway activation considered a key driver of this vicious cycle.43 Notably, in DCM models with LMNA mutations, targeted inhibition of the ERK pathway has been shown to improve cardiac function and delay disease progression,44 suggesting the significant potential of the ERK pathway as a therapeutic target for MYH7-associated DCM.

Cardiac fibrosis is one of the primary pathological features of DCM, characterized by excessive deposition of collagen in the myocardial interstitium and perivascular spaces. This leads to increased ventricular stiffness, impaired diastolic function, and ultimately exacerbates systolic dysfunction.45 Transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and its mediated Smad signaling pathway represent the most prominent drivers of the fibrotic process.46,47 Recent research evidence indicates that MYH7 mutations serve as a critical upstream trigger for activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway within the heart. This activation promotes the release of multiple pro-fibrotic factors, including TGF-β, from cardiomyocytes and infiltrating immune cells.42,48 This initiates the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of downstream Smad2/3. Once in the nucleus, Smad2/3 act as transcription factors to activate a series of pro-fibrotic genes, such as collagen and fibronectin. This drives the transformation of cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, exacerbates extracellular matrix deposition, and creates a vicious cycle ultimately leading to irreversible structural damage to the heart.49,50

Oxidative stress refers to an imbalance between the production and clearance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells, leading to excessive ROS accumulation that damages intracellular proteins, lipids, and DNA.51 In the pathophysiology of DCM, oxidative stress is a key factor contributing to cardiomyocyte apoptosis and dysfunction.52 Research reveals that MYH7 mutations specifically activate the ROS-generating enzyme NADPH oxidase 4 (Nox4), thereby elevating intracellular ROS levels.42 Excessive ROS directly oxidizes sarcomeric proteins, impairing their contractile function, while simultaneously attacking mitochondria, leading to energy metabolism disorders and apoptosis.53 ROS further activates pathways such as MAPK/ERK and TGF-β, forming a vicious positive feedback loop of “stress-oxidation-inflammation/fibrosis” that greatly amplifies the harm caused by the initial mutation.54,55 NF-κB, a central transcription factor regulating inflammatory responses, undergoes Nox4/ROS-mediated activation. This promotes the production of numerous inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α or IL-6), recruits immune cell infiltration, and exacerbates inflammatory damage to myocardial tissue.56

MYH7 mutations can lead to both DCM and HCM, indicating that DCM is not solely determined by mutation type but rather results from the synergistic interaction between mutation sites and genetic modification backgrounds. This perspective currently remains a scientific hypothesis and has not yet been adopted as standard clinical practice. Future clinical studies may explore the application value of combined mutation site detection and modified gene profiling in the precise prediction of disease phenotypes.

TNNT2

The human TNNT2 gene is located on chromosome 1q32 and consists of 17 exons distributed across 17 kb.57 The cardiac troponin T (cTnT) encoded by the TNNT2 gene is a key subunit of the myosin-troponin complex in cardiac myofibrils (Figure 1B), playing an irreplaceable role in cardiac excitation-contraction coupling (ECC).24 It anchors the entire troponin complex to tropomyosin and regulates actin-myosin interactions through calcium ion concentration changes, thereby precisely controlling myocardial contraction and relaxation.58,59 Consequently, even minor variations in the TNNT2 gene may disrupt this intricate regulatory mechanism, leading to severe cardiomyopathy phenotypes.

Alteration in myofilament calcium sensitivity

The core mechanism underlying the contrasting effects of TNNT2 mutations on DCM and HCM lies in the opposite alterations in myosin Ca2+ sensitivity.58 HCM-associated TNNT2 mutations (e.g., I79N and R92Q) increase filament calcium affinity, triggering excessive filament activation and myocardial hypersystole.60,61,62 This activates hypertrophic signaling pathways alongside ECC abnormalities, microvascular dysfunction, and fibrosis, ultimately leading to myocardial hypertrophy60,61,62; conversely, DCM-associated TNNT2 mutations (e.g., R141W and K217) decrease myosin calcium affinity, resulting in reduced contractility and impaired diastolic function.61,63 They exacerbate ventricular dilation and adverse remodeling by affecting pathways such as Hippo/YAP-TAZ.64,65 Although both involve electrophysiological instability and transcriptional alterations, they respectively result in the hypertrophic phenotype of HCM and the dilated phenotype of DCM due to sarcomere overcontraction or contractile failure. Specific regions of the TNNT2 protein are crucial for stabilizing the conformation of the troponin complex.66 Mutations in TNNT2 alter the local and overall structure of cTnT, thereby affecting its interactions with troponin I, troponin C, and myosin.67 These altered interactions disrupt the conformational change relay chain triggered by calcium ion binding to cTnC. Consequently, fewer actin-myosin cross-bridges form at the same intracellular calcium concentration, manifesting macroscopically as reduced myocardial contractility.68,69 To compensate for this reduced contractility, the heart increases ventricular volume through mechanisms such as the Frank-Starling response. Over time, this leads to pathological ventricular dilation, ultimately forming the classic phenotype of DCM.

Disruption of sarcomere-mitochondrial communication and energy metabolic dysregulation

The heart is a high energy-demanding organ, and its continuous pumping function relies on the substantial ATP production from mitochondria.70 Research has revealed that cTnT serves not only as a structural component of sarcomeres but also as a “messenger” connecting sarcomeres and mitochondria.24 In normal cardiomyocytes, cTnT interacts with 14-3-3 proteins to maintain the stability of the sarcomere-mitochondria communication pathway. However, the TNNT2p. K185E mutation weakens cTnT’s binding capacity to 14-3-3 proteins, abnormally activating the downstream RAS/RAF1-p44/42-DRP1/MFF signaling pathway. This leads to mitochondrial dysregulation, manifesting as excessive mitochondrial fission and dysfunction.24 Dysfunctional mitochondria fail to efficiently produce ATP while generating increased ROS, exacerbating cellular damage. This indicates that TNNT2 mutations not only compromise the mechanical components (engine) of the muscle fibers (myofibrils) but also disrupt the “fuel supply” (ATP) to the engine, delivering a dual blow to the heart’s pumping function.

The protective role of XIN protein and its loss

The functional stability of sarcomeres depends not only on the integrity of their core components but is also regulated by numerous auxiliary proteins and cytoskeletal proteins. Research in 2013 revealed that XIN deficiency leads to disorganized myosin arrangement and abnormal Z-disk structure, suggesting its fundamental role as a “molecular scaffold” for sarcomere assembly.71 In 2021, Bin Li et al. first proposed that “XIN deficiency” represents a key synergistic mechanism in TNNT2ΔK210 mutation-induced DCM. XIN loss further exacerbates mutation-induced filament disorganization, reduces cardiomyocyte contractility, and suppresses expression of contraction-related genes, such as TNNT2, ACTN2, and MYH7, thereby accelerating DCM pathology.64 This indicates that in the presence of TNNT2 mutations, the XIN protein exerts a certain compensatory protective effect, delaying disease progression. The potential mechanism may lie in the XIN protein’s ability to stabilize damaged sarcomere structures through its unique domains.72

In summary, different mutation sites in TNNT2 exert quantitatively distinct effects on myosin filament calcium sensitivity. Mutations such as R141W, which cause a significant reduction in calcium affinity, are clinically more likely to result in contractile dysfunction and can serve as prognostic stratification markers. Leveraging the protective role of XIN protein, combining XIN protein agonists with calcium sensitivity modulators enables more precise restoration of filament structure and calcium regulatory function, offering a possible novel therapeutic approach for TNNT2-related DCM.

Nuclear envelope protein genes

LMNA

The LMNA gene is located on human chromosome 1q21.2-q21.3, spans approximately 25 kb, and consists of 12 exons. Through alternative splicing, it produces two main lamin isoforms: lamin A and lamin C.73 These two proteins exhibit high structural homology, differing only at their C termini. Lamin A undergoes a series of complex post-translational modifications, including farnesylation, methylation, and protease cleavage, to mature into a functional protein, whereas lamin C does not require these processes.74 The LMNA-encoded protein belongs to the type V intermediate filament protein family and constitutes a major component of the nuclear lamina (Figure 2). The nuclear lamina is a reticular structure beneath the inner nuclear membrane (INM) that provides mechanical support for the nuclear envelope and participates in various nuclear activities.75 The LMNA protein comprises three major functional domains: the N-terminal head domain, the central α-helical rod domain, and the C-terminal globular tail domain.76 It plays multiple critical roles in maintaining nuclear structure and function: (1) providing mechanical stability to the nucleus, protecting genetic material from mechanical stress damage77; (2) regulating gene expression through interactions with chromatin via its laminin-related domain78; and (3) participating in various critical biological processes including DNA replication, DNA damage repair, cell differentiation, cell cycle regulation, and signal transduction.79

LMNA mutations account for 0.5%–5% of DCM cases, with prevalence increasing to 10% in familial DCM and up to 33% in DCM associated with atrioventricular block.80 Mutation types in LMNA-related DCM are diverse, with most DCM-associated mutations concentrated in the central rod-like domain and C-terminal region.74 Common DCM-associated mutations include R321X, E161K, R249Q, L35P, and others.81,82,83 LMNA-related DCM exhibits distinct clinical features: (1) it typically presents initially with conduction system disease, preceding overt ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction84; (2) it exhibits high penetrance, with approximately 69% of mutation carriers developing overt cardiac manifestations before age 6085; (3) prognosis is poor, characterized by a high incidence of sudden cardiac death86; and (4) it frequently involves skeletal muscle involvement, presenting as myasthenia gravis or muscular atrophy.87

Aberrant nuclear architecture

LMNA mutations cause structural abnormalities in lamin A/C proteins, preventing them from assembling into a stable, resilient nuclear lamina network.88 In cardiomyocytes—cells subjected to continuous intense mechanical contractions—fragile nuclei cannot effectively resist mechanical stress, leading to nuclear membrane rupture and abnormal nuclear morphology, which in turn trigger cellular damage and death.89 Abnormal nuclear morphology is commonly observed in iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes.90 Applying in vitro mechanical stretching to cells harboring LMNA mutations exacerbates nuclear damage and apoptosis. Physical disruption of the nuclear envelope not only directly threatens genomic integrity but may also trigger abnormal mixing of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins, activating non-specific cellular stress responses, such as calcium signaling and inflammatory pathways, ultimately leading to apoptosis and fibrosis.90,91,92 Although normal nuclei possess a degree of flexibility to accommodate cellular deformation, the effect of LMNA mutations on nuclear mechanical properties presents a paradoxical state. On one hand, the mutant lamina renders the nucleus macroscopically fragile and prone to rupture under mechanical stress.90,91,92 On the other hand, nuclei carrying certain LMNA mutations exhibit a significant increase in local stiffness.93 Furthermore, structural defects in the nuclear lamina inevitably cause disruption of its interaction with chromatin. Widespread chromatin reorganization is observed in LMNA-mutant cells, including the redistribution of heterochromatin and its detachment from the nuclear periphery. The anchoring of lamin-associated domains (LADs) is compromised, leading to aberrant expression of genes that are normally repressed in these regions.94 Studies also indicate altered interactions between mutant lamin A/C and euchromatin. These alterations may directly affect transcription factor binding or indirectly modulate gene expression by changing chromatin accessibility.95 Therefore, the epigenomic alterations and transcriptional dysregulation initiated by the disruption of nuclear architecture represent a critical aspect of the pathological progression in LMNA-DCM, effectively linking structural defects to functional impairment.

Abnormal mechanotransduction signaling

The heart is an organ subjected to constant mechanical stress. Cardiomyocytes convert physical force signals into biochemical signals through a precise mechanical-to-electrical transmission mechanism to adapt to physiological demands. One of the core pathomechanisms of DCM caused by LMNA mutations is the severe disruption of this process.

The LINC complex: A mechanosensor linking the nucleoplasm and cytoplasm

Mechanical signals are transmitted from the extracellular matrix to the cell nucleus via a molecular bridge spanning the nuclear membrane-the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex.96 The LINC complex primarily consists of SUN proteins and Nesprin proteins (Figure 2).97,98 Through a continuous protein chain-cytoskeleton-Nesprins-SUN proteins-nuclear lamina-the LINC complex establishes a physical connection between the nucleus and cytoplasm, enabling direct transmission of mechanical forces between them.99 In LMNA-DCM, mutant lamin A/C proteins fail to assemble into a stable nuclear lamina network and cannot effectively anchor SUN proteins. This compromises the stability of the LINC complex, potentially leading to its disassembly and resulting in a “mechanical uncoupling” of the nucleus from the cytoskeleton. This uncoupling has two major detrimental consequences: (1) aberrant mechanotransduction: residual, unstable LINC complexes may transmit mechanical forces in an aberrant manner, applying cytoskeletal tension unevenly onto the fragile nuclear envelope and exacerbating nuclear damage. A key finding in LMNA mutant mouse models is that knocking out SUN1, which further disrupts the LINC complex connection and paradoxically ameliorates nuclear damage and cardiomyopathy phenotypes.100 This “negative-negative-makes-positive” phenomenon strongly demonstrates that it is precisely this abnormal, detrimental force transmission—rather than the mere absence of connection—that is a key driver of pathogenesis; (2) loss of mechanosensing: normal mechanical signals cannot be efficiently transmitted into the nucleus, rendering the nucleus incapable of mounting appropriate physiological responses to the external mechanical environment.101

Downstream pathological signaling pathways abnormally activated by defective mechanotransduction

Upstream physical injury and disrupted mechanical signaling ultimately converge on the abnormal activation of a series of key downstream signaling pathways. These pathways collectively drive cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, apoptosis, and functional decline in DCM. The MAPK pathway, comprising ERK1/2, JNK, and p38 branches, is a key pathway repeatedly demonstrated to be abnormally activated in LMNA-DCM studies.102,103 ARRY-371797, a selective p38α MAPK inhibitor, was investigated based on evidence from animal models and patient myocardial biopsies showing p38 MAPK pathway overactivation in LMNA-DCM. Although phase 2 trials and a 144-week long-term extension study showed improvements in a 6-min walk test (6MWT), reductions in NT-proBNP levels, and a favorable safety profile,104 the subsequent phase 3 clinical trial found that ARRY-371797 did not demonstrate superior efficacy compared to placebo. None of the key endpoints (6MWT, KCCQ, NT-proBNP, and composite endpoint) reached statistical significance.105 The AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a core regulator of cell growth, proliferation, and autophagy. In LMNA mutation models, its activity is also dysregulated, contributing to pathological myocardial remodeling processes.106 As mTOR inhibitors, everolimus and NV-20494 demonstrate potential therapeutic value for LMNA-mutated mouse models of DCM. They improve cardiac function, prolong survival, and exhibit no severe toxicity; however, they fail to halt the progression of myocardial fibrosis.107

The TGF-β pathway is a primary driver of tissue fibrosis. In LMNA-DCM, activation of this pathway promotes fibroblast activation and excessive extracellular matrix deposition, leading to increased myocardial stiffness and impaired diastolic function—hallmark pathological features of DCM.95 The platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) pathway has recently emerged as another key player. Studies indicate that PDGF receptors are aberrantly activated in hearts with LMNA mutations, potentially contributing to both fibrosis and arrhythmogenesis.103 Furthermore, fragile nuclei undergo repeated rupture under mechanical stress, causing DNA double-strand breaks that activate DNA damage response pathways and downstream p53 signaling. p53 activation induces cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, accelerating cardiomyocyte loss.108 Additionally, LMNA/C deficiency leads to accelerated degradation of the silent information regulator 1 (SIRT1), which in turn suppresses the expression of downstream mitochondrial-related genes. This initiates mitochondrial dysfunction, causing an increase in ROS levels. The elevated ROS further exacerbates sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ leakage, predisposing to cardiac arrhythmias.90 These signaling pathways intertwine to form a complex pathological network, transforming the initial physical defects caused by LMNA mutations into progressive, lethal myocardial dysfunction.

The failure of phase III trials with MAPK inhibitors suggests that single-pathway intervention is insufficient. Combining inhibitors that target the TGF-β pathway may simultaneously block myocardial injury and fibrosis, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Given that conduction system disorders are the initial manifestation, early implementation of electrocardiographic monitoring combined with SIRT1 activator intervention in mutation carriers can delay the onset of mitochondrial dysfunction and arrhythmias, improving prognosis.

EMD

The EMD gene is located on the long arm of the human X chromosome at position Xq28. It is a relatively small gene comprising 6 exons and 5 introns.109 The EMD gene encodes the emerin protein, a type II INM protein with a molecular weight of approximately 34 kDa.110 Emerin exhibits a unique topological structure, with a full-length amino acid sequence of approximately 254 residues.109 It serves as a critical molecular link between the nuclear lamina and the nuclear cytoskeleton (Figure 2). Emerin directly or indirectly interacts with lamin A/C and lamin B, forming a robust protein network that collectively maintains nuclear morphology and mechanical resilience.111

Traditionally, mutations in the EMD gene have been considered the primary cause of X-linked Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy (EDMD).112 EDMD patients typically develop joint contractures in the elbows, neck, and Achilles tendons during childhood or adolescence, followed by progressive brachioradialis muscle weakness and atrophy.113 Cardiac involvement represents the most lethal complication of EDMD, potentially progressing to severe DCM and heart failure, which constitute the primary cause of mortality.113 One study identified a missense mutation in the EMD gene (c.23C>G p.Ser8Trp) in a DCM family.114 Male patients in this family exhibited severe DCM without the typical skeletal muscle weakness or joint contractures seen in EDMD, showing significantly reduced or absent emerin protein expression on cardiac nuclear membranes.114 This finding is considered definitive evidence directly linking EMD mutations to isolated DCM.

Furthermore, other studies have further indicated that DCM patients carrying EMD gene variants face an extremely high risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.115 This suggests that the EMD gene should be considered during genetic screening and risk stratification for DCM, particularly for patients with X-linked inheritance patterns or those presenting with severe arrhythmias. Although studies suggest that EMD variants may contribute only marginally to the overall DCM population,116 they hold irreplaceable value for diagnosis and prognosis assessment within specific families.

Cardiomyopathies caused by EMD and LMNA mutations exhibit significant overlap in clinical phenotypes, both presenting as conduction system disorders and DCM.114 This arises because emerin and lamin A/C physically interact at the nuclear envelope, functionally cooperating to maintain its stability and function.117 Consequently, research into the mechanisms of EMD-associated cardiomyopathy has long drawn heavily upon findings from LMNA-related DCM studies.103 However, distinctions exist: LMNA-related DCM typically presents as isolated cardiac involvement, characterized by left ventricular dilation, reduced systolic function, and conduction system disorders; whereas, EMD patients usually develop skeletal muscle symptoms first, with cardiac involvement occurring later.118 Recent studies, however, have revealed that missense mutations in EMD genes can also cause isolated DCM,114 indicating heterogeneous pathogenic pathways between EMD and LMNA mutations.119 Thus, understanding the independent pathogenic mechanisms of EMD mutations is crucial for achieving precise diagnosis and personalized treatment.

Ion channel genes

SCN5A

The SCN5A gene is localized on human chromosome 3p21 and is the only gene encoding the Nav1.5 protein. Its genomic DNA spans approximately 83.6 kb and contains 28 exons and undergoes complex alternative splicing to produce multiple isoforms.120 This diversity provides a molecular basis for the functional variety of Nav1.5 in different cardiac tissues and developmental stages. Its primary role is to mediate the rapid depolarization phase (phase 0) of the cardiac action potential.121 Mutations in SCN5A are estimated to account for approximately 1.7%–3% of all familial DCM cases.122 A subset of DCM-associated SCN5A mutations cause impaired channel inactivation, leading to a significant enhancement of the late sodium current (I_Na,L).123 This persistent influx of Na+ results in elevated intracellular Na+ concentration. This sodium overload subsequently promotes reverse-mode operation of the sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX), causing an increase in intracellular Ca2+ concentration.124 Chronic calcium overload can activate calcium-dependent proteases, which degrade sarcomeric and cytoskeletal proteins.125 Concurrently, it induces mitochondrial dysfunction, reduces ATP production, increases ROS generation, and activates apoptotic and necrotic signaling pathways, ultimately leading to ventricular dilation and impaired contractility.126

Another subset of mutations exhibits a loss-of-function (LOF) phenotype, characterized by a substantial reduction in the peak sodium current (I_Na,P).127 A decreased I_Na,P directly slows the action potential upstroke velocity and cardiac conduction velocity. Severe conduction slowing can cause intra- and inter-ventricular electromechanical dyssynchrony. Chronic ventricular contraction dyssynchrony leads to uneven mechanical stress on cardiomyocytes and low energy efficiency, activating pathological remodeling signaling pathways that ultimately result in ventricular dilation and functional deterioration.128 Therefore, whether through a “gain-of-function” (GOF) or “LOF” mechanism, persistent electrophysiological abnormalities can, via distinct pathways, converge on the same pathological outcome: the phenotypic manifestation of DCM.

Furthermore, specific mutations in the voltage-sensing domain (VSD) of Nav1.5 can disrupt the interaction between the fourth transmembrane segment (S4) and the gating charge transfer center. This disruption creates an abnormal, non-selective ion leak pathway within the channel protein, known as a “gating pore”.129 The resulting gating pore current disrupts myocardial ion homeostasis, particularly affecting Ca2+ handling, and can further contribute to the development of arrhythmias and DCM.130,131

Calcium channel genes

CACNA1C

The CACNA1C gene is localized on human chromosome 12p13.3, with a total genomic DNA length of approximately 93.0 kb, encompassing 55 exons, and its dysfunction is closely associated with DCM. The protein encoded by CACNA1C, Cav1.2, is a large transmembrane protein that forms the core subunit of the L-type calcium channel complex.132 In healthy cardiomyocytes, the transmembrane flux of calcium and its intracellular storage/release are tightly regulated to maintain ECC and energy metabolism. Dysfunction of CACNA1C disrupts this equilibrium through two primary mechanisms: (1) LOF: microarray analyses have shown significant downregulation of CACNA1CmRNA in DCM patients.133 This leads to a reduced L-type calcium current (I_Ca,L), which diminishes myocardial contractility due to insufficient activation of ryanodine receptor (RyR2) and subsequent decreased calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Concurrently, the abbreviated action potential duration triggered by reduced I_Ca,L can induce long-term electrophysiological remodeling.134,135 (2) GOF: although GOF mutations are more frequently associated with arrhythmias and HCM,136,137 chronic, mild GOF can lead to an excessively strong or prolonged I_Ca,L due to slowed channel inactivation. This results in calcium overload, which exerts direct cardiotoxic effects.132 However, compared to established DCM genes such as TTN and LMNA, the detection rate of CACNA1C mutations in DCM cohorts is relatively low. Reported cases where CACNA1C mutations act as a primary, monogenic cause of typical DCM remain limited.

RYR2

RYR2 is one of the largest genes in the human genome. It is located on 1q43, with a total genomic length of approximately 791 kb and encompassing 105 exons, encoding the cardiac RyR2, which is the largest known ion channel protein. Its core function is to mediate calcium-induced calcium release (CICR), the fundamental mechanism underlying ECC in cardiomyocytes.138 In 2021, Costa et al. reported a novel heterozygous early truncation variant in RYR2 (c.294G>A; p.Trp98Ter) associated with autosomal dominant familial DCM.139 Further supporting its role, Zhou et al. demonstrated in 2023 that a heterozygous LOF RYR2 variant caused by a 1q43 deletion (coupled with an ACTN2 variant) led to DCM combined with left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC).140 These findings confirm that dysregulation of RyR2 function contributes to DCM pathogenesis by impairing the maturation of cardiomyocyte calcium handling. In the context of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD)-associated DCM, RyR2 undergoes aberrant post-translational modifications, including hyperphosphorylation by PKA, S-nitrosylation, and oxidative modifications.141,142 These alterations reduce the binding of RyR2 to its stabilizing subunit calstabin 2 (FKBP12.6), resulting in a persistent diastolic Ca2+ leak during diastole.141,142 This Ca2+ leak has multiple detrimental effects: it not only depletes sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ stores, thereby impairing contractile force but also activates the NCX, predisposing to arrhythmias.143 Simultaneously, the aberrant Ca2+ signaling activates pathways, such as calmodulin-Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII), and calcineurin, accelerating pathological myocardial remodeling.144

The common pathophysiology of ion channel gene-related diseases involves ion homeostasis disruption, characterized by GOF or LOF mutations that ultimately trigger DCM through electrophysiological abnormalities and calcium overload. The distinction lies in SCN5A-mediated Na dysregulation, while CACNA1C and RYR2-mediated Ca2+ imbalance. This allows stratified intervention by ion type: sodium channel modulators for Na+-related mutations, combined with calmodulin inhibitors for Ca2+ abnormalities, enhancing targeting specificity. All three activate ROS pathways; adding antioxidants can mitigate myocardial injury. Integrating genetic testing with early electrophysiological monitoring enables precise arrhythmia risk prediction for mutation carriers, delaying disease progression.

Desmosome-related genes

DSC2

The DSC2 gene is localized on human chromosome 18q12.1, with a total genomic DNA length of approximately 97.7 kb and containing 17 exons. It is a key component of the transmembrane core of desmosomes.145 In the heart, DSC2 is highly expressed at the intercalated discs. Its primary function is to mediate mechanotransduction between cardiomyocytes, enabling them to withstand the immense shear forces generated during cardiac contraction and diastole, thereby ensuring synchronized and efficient heartbeats. Missense, nonsense, or frameshift mutations in the DSC2 gene can lead LOF or a dominant-negative effect in its protein product. This results in aberrant protein synthesis and trafficking, weakened intercellular adhesion, and ultimately cardiomyocyte dissociation and death.146,147 Traditionally, DSC2 mutations have been recognized as a classical cause of arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC).148 Both ACM and DCM share core features of myocardial tissue damage, structural remodeling, and cardiac dysfunction, involving shared signaling pathway abnormalities and rare genetic overlaps.149 Research has revealed significantly reduced DSC2 protein expression in cardiac tissue from DCM patients. Notably, this downregulation is not necessarily accompanied by abnormalities in other desmosomal proteins. Serum DSC2 levels are significantly decreased in DCM patients with biventricular systolic dysfunction but show no significant change in those with isolated left ventricular systolic dysfunction.150 This indicates that DSC2 may serve as a specific molecular marker and a potential pathogenic factor for DCM presenting with concomitant right ventricular systolic impairment.

DSP

The DSP gene is localized on 6p24.3, with a total genomic DNA length of 162.6 kb and containing 24 exons. Through alternative splicing, the DSP gene can produce two protein isoforms, DSP-I and DSP-II, with DSP-I being the predominant form in cardiac muscle and most epithelial tissues.151 The core function of DSP is to link desmosomes with the intermediate filament network. The absence or mutation of DSP disrupts its role as a core component of desmosomes at the intercalated discs of cardiomyocytes, leading to abnormalities in desmosome structure and the mechanical connections and signaling functions between cardiomyocytes.152 Consequently, this disruption triggers significant dysregulation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.153 It is also accompanied by downregulation of the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway and upregulation of the Hippo/YAP-TAZ pathway. The combination of this structural damage and signaling dysfunction collectively results in cardiomyocyte degeneration and death, which in turn induces pathological changes such as myocardial fibrosis and fatty infiltration. These pathological alterations manifest as the functional abnormalities characteristic of DCM.154

Other genes

KLF13

The Krüppel-like factor family (KLF) comprises transcription factors possessing conserved zinc finger DNA-binding domains. In mammals, this family includes KLF1–KLF17.155 Expression of these factors commences during the early stages of embryonic cardiac development and persists throughout the entire cardiac development process.156,157 KLF13 is localized on 15q13.3, with a genomic length of 8.0 kb and 3 core exons. The first key discovery came from whole-exome sequencing studies of familial DCM patients, which identified KLF13 gene mutations cosegregating with the DCM phenotype in the 15q13.3 region,158 establishing KLF13 as a susceptibility gene for DCM. Further studies revealed that KLF13 mutations can also cause sporadic DCM cases,159 indicating that KLF13 mutations not only transmit within families but may also arise as de novo mutations leading to sporadic cardiomyopathy. KLF13 is a positive transcriptional regulator of MYH7, a key structural gene for myocardial contraction. Mutations in KLF13 result in loss of transcriptional activation capacity in the encoded protein, preventing normal regulation of MYH7 expression.158 This directly causes abnormal expression of myocardial contractile proteins, leading to impaired myocardial contractility and ventricular remodeling. Furthermore, KLF13 exerts a protective effect on cardiomyocyte survival. Studies reveal that downregulating KLF13 expression suppresses cell viability and promotes apoptosis,160 whereas overexpressing KLF13 protects cardiomyocytes by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing apoptosis.161 The long non-coding RNA FTX may mitigate cardiomyocyte injury by regulating the miR-150/KLF13 axis,162 further supporting KLF13’s role in myocardial stress protection. Despite significant progress, substantial knowledge gaps remain in the field of DCM caused by KLF13 mutations. Direct evidence regarding the specific mechanisms by which KLF13 regulates downstream target genes, particularly whether it directly controls calcium-handling-related genes, is still lacking.

ETS1

ETS1 is located on 11q24.3, with a total genomic length of 11.5 kb and 13 core coding exons. ETS1 is essential for normal cardiac structural development.163 Knockout or LOF of ETS1 leads to structural abnormalities including ventricular morphology defects, depletion of endocardial tissue within the cardiac chambers, absence of the ventricular outflow tract septum, and uneven myocardial wall thickness.164 This directly reflects ETS1’s critical role in cardiomyocyte differentiation and cardiac morphogenesis. Although ETS1’s role in cardiac development is well established, its pathogenicity in DCM remained unclear for a long time. A recent study of familial DCM identified a novel ETS1 gene heterozygous variant through whole-exome sequencing, which co-segregated with the familial DCM phenotype.165 This discovery first established ETS1 as a novel susceptibility gene for DCM. Nonsense mutations in the ETS1 gene result in loss of transcriptional activation capacity for key cardiovascular target genes Claudin 5 (CLDN5) and activin receptor-like kinase 1 (ALK1) through a molecular mechanism of ETS1 haploinsufficiency.165,166

CLDN5 is a tight junction protein essential for maintaining the structural integrity and function of cardiomyocytes.167 Downregulation of CLDN5 caused by ETS1 mutations may compromise the structural barrier of cardiomyocytes, rendering them more susceptible to damage, apoptosis, or pathological remodeling under mechanical stress, ultimately contributing to ventricular dilation. ALK1, a type I receptor within the TGF-β superfamily, is downregulated by ETS1 deficiency, thereby amplifying TGFβ1/SMAD2 signaling pathways.166,168 This imbalance in receptor expression may increase endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition, promoting fibrosis and vascular abnormalities. Impaired adaptive remodeling of blood vessels to hemodynamic changes results in myocardial hypoperfusion and microcirculatory dysfunction, promoting DCM development. Furthermore, ETS-1 knockout reduces angiotensin II-induced cardiac fibrosis and hypertrophy,169 suggesting ETS1 positively regulates the fibrotic response under pressure overload. Despite significant progress, the precise mechanism by which ETS1 mutations cause DCM remains unclear.

BMP10

The BMP10 gene is located on 2p13.3, with a total genomic length of 10.5 kb and 2 core coding exons. As a member of the BMP branch of the TGF-β superfamily, BMP10 exhibits distinct atrial bias in cardiac expression. Multiple clinical cohort and mechanistic studies have characterized it as a cardiogenic protein primarily secreted by atrial cardiomyocytes,170,171 which enters the circulation and reflects atrial stress/remodeling states. A 2023 study first identified direct human genetic evidence for BMP10 as a causative gene for DCM.172 Recent research indicates that BMP10 activates STAT3 phosphorylation via non-canonical pathways173; mitochondrial STAT3 is recognized as a critical myocardial protective signal influencing mitochondrial ROS and energy homeostasis.174 By promoting STAT3 phosphorylation and entering mitochondria, BMP10 reduces mitochondrial ROS, enhances ATP production, and improves electron transport chain function,175 suggesting its capacity to regulate mitochondrial-energy metabolism. This finding suggests that when BMP10 function is compromised, cardiomyocytes may become more susceptible to a vicious cycle of “energy deficiency-oxidative stress-cell death,” thereby driving cardiac dilation and contractile dysfunction. A zebrafish study of BMP10 mutations revealed that the IL-6/p-STAT3 inflammatory pathway is a critical node influencing cardiac iron metabolism and injury progression,176 suggesting that BMP10 deficiency can enter the inflammatory STAT3 network and disrupt cardiac homeostasis. Large-scale phosphoproteomics studies demonstrate that BMP10 activates signaling modules related to MAPK and GADD45β in endothelial cells,177 suggesting BMP10 also couples stress and inflammation-related pathways. In summary, BMP10 mutations weaken the mitochondrial STAT3 axis, leading to increased ROS, decreased ATP, and heightened iron homeostasis/ferroptosis susceptibility. Concurrent activation of inflammation-related pathways further promotes myocardial fibrosis and injury. However, direct evidence linking BMP10 mutations to DCM remains limited in both population studies and mechanistic research, necessitating further investigation to elucidate the precise interaction mechanisms between the BMP10 signaling pathway and DCM.

RBM20

The RBM20 gene is located on 10q25.2, with a total genomic length of 196.2 kb and 14 core coding exons. Large-scale genetic epidemiological studies indicate that RBM20 mutations account for approximately 3% of familial DCM cases.178 Compared to other forms of hereditary DCM, RBM20-associated DCM patients exhibit earlier onset age, higher arrhythmia risk, and often faster disease progression, potentially rapidly advancing to end-stage heart failure.179 Some studies suggest male patients tend to develop the condition earlier and have lower survival rates.180

Splicing-dependent pathogenic mechanisms

The core mechanism by which RBM20 mutations cause DCM is the loss or alteration of its RNA splicing regulatory function. RBM20 mutations reduce its ability to inhibit TTN splicing, leading to abnormal expression of the fetal N2BA isoform in cardiomyocytes.181 This disrupts passive tension and the Frank-Starling mechanism, promoting ventricular dilation and reducing contraction efficiency. RBM20 regulates splicing of multiple calcium-handling genes; its mutation causes widespread calcium-handling disruption. RBM20 LOF causes abnormal splicing of genes involved in calcium handling and ion regulation (e.g., CamkIIδ and RyR2), leading to increased L-type calcium currents in cardiomyocytes.182 This results in intracellular calcium overload, abnormal elevation of sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium content, increased arrhythmogenic spontaneous calcium release, and the development of malignant ventricular arrhythmias. This mechanism operates independently of TTN abnormal splicing-mediated myocardial remodeling and heart failure. Notably, some models suggest that splicing defects alone may not produce phenotypes consistent with human RBM20 variants. For instance, RRM-related variants can alter target gene splicing but do not necessarily cause typical DCM remodeling,183 indicating the presence of a more critical secondary mechanism.

Non-splicing-dependent pathogenic mechanisms

Previously, the prevailing view within the field held that the core mechanism by which RBM20 mutations cause DCM was loss of nuclear splicing function, particularly abnormal splicing of the TTN gene.181 However, current research confirms that RBM20 mutations not only cause splicing dysfunction but also disrupt nuclear localization signals. This leads to massive cytoplasmic accumulation of RBM20 and the formation of abnormal RNP particles, disrupting mRNA metabolism, translation processes, cell-cycle regulation, and stress responses in cardiomyocytes.184 This GOF toxicity is the core cause of lethal neonatal DCM, supplementing the key pathogenic mechanism beyond splicing deficiency. Further studies demonstrate that reducing RBM20 expression via oligonucleotides to decrease cytoplasmic RNP particles improves/reverses cardiac function and ventricular dilation in mice.185 Crucially, these benefits occur even without fully restoring correct splicing of RBM20 target genes. This finding strongly supports RNP particles as a key pathogenic driver.

In summary, the pathogenic genes of DCM exhibit significant heterogeneity. Based on the function and signaling pathways of their gene products, they can be categorized into myosin genes, nuclear membrane protein genes, ion channel genes, and desmosome-related genes. These diverse genes trigger myocardial remodeling and impaired cardiac function by disrupting key physiological processes in cardiomyocytes, though their core mechanisms share both commonalities and specificities (Table 1 and Figure 3). Based on currently available public data, evidence supporting KLF13, ETS1, and BMP10 as single-gene causes of DCM remains relatively limited. Therefore, these genes are considered emerging candidate genes for DCM, with their pathogenicity and expressivity likely highly dependent on genetic background and environmental/stress factors.

Potential therapeutic approaches for DCM

Precision intervention at the genetic level

Gene supplementation and editing

The CRISPR-Cas9 system is the most rapidly advancing gene editing technology in recent years, demonstrating significant potential in the gene therapy of DCM.186 This system employs a specifically designed single-stranded guide RNA to direct the Cas9 nuclease to generate double-strand breaks at targeted DNA locations. Subsequently, the cell’s intrinsic DNA repair mechanisms repair these breaks, enabling gene knockout, knock-in, or precise editing.187 Research indicates that CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) can upregulate the expression of full-length TTN protein, compensating for the core pathogenic mechanism of “haploinsufficiency”. Even without eliminating the potential “toxic peptide,” this approach can fully restore cardiac function and does not induce a stress response from protein overexpression. This strategy’s efficacy has been validated in models of TTNtv-associated DCM.20 Precision gene editing can also correct pathogenic LMNA mutations, restoring normal assembly of nuclear lamin A/C, reversing nuclear structural abnormalities and mechanical conduction defects, and achieving correction of nuclear morphology and gene expression in cellular models.83

AAV vector-mediated functional protein delivery

Utilizing an AAV9 viral vector carrying the TNNT2 promoter to specifically drive expression of the XIN protein subtype XINB in cardiomyocytes can stabilize damaged sarcomere structure, upregulate contraction-related genes such as ACTN2 and MYH7,64 and significantly reverse myofilament disorganization and contractile dysfunction caused by the TNNT2ΔK210 mutation, providing a protein replacement strategy for TNNT2-related DCM.64

Short-term protein supplementation via modified mRNA

In a DCM model with DSC2 deficiency, intravenous administration of modified DSC2 mRNA restored the expression of DSC2 protein at the cardiac intercalated discs, enhanced intercellular adhesion, and improved biventricular systolic dysfunction. This approach, which avoids genomic integration, is suitable for the short-term correction of desmosomal protein deficiency.150

Targeted inhibition of pathological signaling pathways

Although the pathogenic genes responsible for DCM have diverse functions, they often converge on the activation or inhibition of core signaling pathways, creating a “different routes, same destination” pathological effect. The dysregulation of these pathways represents the critical nexus linking genetic variations to organ-level pathology (Table 2 and Figure 4). For example, PD98059 (an ERK inhibitor), which can suppress the sustained ERK1/2 phosphorylation induced by MYH7 mutations44; ARRY-371797 (a p38α inhibitor), which improved the 6MWT and reduced NT-proBNP levels in a phase 2 trial for LMNA-associated DCM.98 Although its subsequent phase 3 trial did not meet primary endpoints, it provided proof of concept for pathway intervention99; everolimus and NV-20494 (mTOR inhibitors), which improved cardiac function and prolonged survival in LMNA mutation mouse models without serious toxicity. Although they could not halt fibrosis progression, they alleviated core pathological processes107; fibroblast growth factor-18 (FGF18), which downregulates Nox4 expression, reduces ROS production, protects the mitochondrial electron transport chain, and mitigates oxidative stress damage caused by multiple genetic defects188; astragaloside IV(a natural compound), which inhibits TGF-β1-mediated Smad2/3 phosphorylation and attenuates myocardial fibrosis155; the transcription factor Yin Yang 1 (YY1), which inhibits the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby reducing cardiac fibrosis and inflammation.189

Signaling pathways in DCM

Signaling pathways in DCM

DCM is not caused by a single gene or pathway. Instead, various pathogenic genes converge through an intricate network of intersecting signaling cascades. This multi-step process ultimately drives cardiomyocytes toward common pathological endpoints, including apoptosis, inflammation, cytoskeletal disarray, fibrosis, and mitochondrial damage. These cellular events culminate in the organ-level phenotype of DCM, characterized by left ventricular dilation and impaired myocardial contractility. (Created in BioRender. x, Z. Activators of β-myosin heavy chain

Omecamtiv mecarbil and danicamtiv are both activators targeting β-MHC in cardiac myofibrils. The GALACTIC-HF study demonstrated that omecamtiv mecarbil improves NT-proBNP levels in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and reduces the incidence of the composite primary endpoint of heart failure events or cardiovascular death.199,200 Phase 2a trial results for danicamtiv demonstrated improved left ventricular systolic function and reduced left atrial minimum volume index.201 Although these clinical trials exclusively enrolled HFrEF patients, they suggest potential for omecamtiv mecarbil and danicamtiv in treating DCM. Clinical trials evaluating danicamtiv for DCM are ongoing.202 Yang Li et al. found that omecamtiv mecarbil enhances myocardial contraction by upregulating genes associated with cardiac cell contraction (C10orf71), while exerting minimal effects on fatty acid metabolism, calcium cycling/sarcoplasmic reticulum, and ion channel genes.203 Therefore, both agents target only the “contractile dysfunction” symptom of DCM without addressing its underlying pathogenesis or halting the core pathological processes driving disease progression.

Limitations and prospects of the study

This paper focuses on exploring the mechanisms of familial hereditary DCM. In addition to familial hereditary DCM, acquired DCM also exists in clinical practice. This type of DCM is primarily caused by environmental factors acting on a potential genetic predisposition, such as infectious DCM, which accounts for approximately 95% of acquired DCM cases.204 It is important to emphasize that acquired DCM differs significantly from familial hereditary DCM. This section will exclusively address familial hereditary DCM. DCM disease-causing genes exhibit high heterogeneity, with significant variations in the proportion of patients affected by different genes. This results in “single-gene mutation-targeted drugs” covering only a very small fraction of patients. Even for TTN, the most prevalent gene, such drugs cover only 25% of end-stage/familial DCM patients. The proportion of patients affected by other genes is even lower, meaning “single-gene drugs” inherently face the problem of a “narrow target population” from the outset. This strict “gene-mechanism-drug” correspondence limits each drug class to serving only a tiny patient subgroup, making large-scale clinical application difficult. Furthermore, merely improving downstream symptoms cannot halt the core pathological processes driving disease progression. Therefore, we can shift our research focus upstream to explore the common pathological environment that leads to mutations in relevant disease-causing genes, the likelihood of staged gene mutations, or improve the internal environment to activate the body’s gene repair mechanisms for treating DCM. Therefore, future therapies should not only target single-gene treatments but also explore how to improve the shared pathological environment. By reversing the “soil” in which gene mutations take effect, we can achieve broad-spectrum intervention for polygenic DCM.
