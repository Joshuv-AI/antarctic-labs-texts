Core-genome Single-nucleotide Polymorphism Analysis of Streptococcus pyogenes M1 Clone from Korea During the Pre-COVID-19 Era
Authors: Takashi Takahashi, Takahiro Maeda, Hyoshim Shin, Sunjoo Kim
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Streptococcus pyogenes, which harbors the carbohydrate A-antigen in its cell wall, is a major gram-positive coccus in human clinical settings. It commonly causes skin and soft tissue infections, including erysipelas, contagious impetigo, and necrotizing soft tissue infection, and, in rare cases, primary pneumonia/empyema. Some patients develop streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, leading to septic shock and multi-organ failure. During 2019–2020, S. pyogenes M1UK emerged in the UK, Canada, the USA, and the Netherlands [1–4]. This M1T1 variant possesses toxigenic and hypervirulent properties including enhanced production and secretion of streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin A (SpeA, also known as scarlet fever or erythrogenic toxin A) and has been associated with an increased incidence of scarlet fever among children. Similar to contemporary and traditional strains (M1global), M1UK harbors several important genes encoding virulence factors involved in adherence, immune escape, and pathogenicity.

Friães, et al. [5] recently reported features of S. pyogenes causing invasive infections among adults in Portugal during 2016–2019, including the prevalence of M1global, M1intermediate, and M1UK clones, and noted the pre-COVID-19 expansion of the M1UK (sub)lineage. Yu, et al. [6] reported the detection of S. pyogenes M1UK in China in 2018.

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are widely used molecular markers for analyzing genome-wide distribution because of their low analysis cost. Kim and Lee [7] have recently reported SNP genotyping for the rapid detection of vancomycin-intermediate resistance in Staphylococcus aureus.

In this retrospective study, we conducted core-genome SNP analysis of S. pyogenes M1 clones from Korea during the pre-COVID-19 era, ending with data that was available by the end of 2018. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of core-genome SNP results for Korean S. pyogenes M1 strains.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kitasato University Medical Center (Institutional Review Board approval No. 2024036). Shin, et al. [8] had conducted a comparative genome analysis of invasive S. pyogenes strains detected over a 20-yr period in Korea. We utilized the whole-genome sequence data of Shin, et al. and extracted data on 17 Korean S. pyogenes M1 clone (emm1 lineage) strains with whole-genome data available during the pre-COVID-19 period from the US National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) website, which hosts the article [8]. Table 1 summarizes the data on the 17 Korean M1 strains (GCH79–GCH174). The dominant emm subgenotype was emm1.0, followed by emm1.30, emm1.76, and emm1.18. The major exotoxin gene profile was speA–speB–smeZ (N=16); only one strain had speA–speB–speC–smeZ. The dominant sequence type (ST) was ST28, with one strain being ST785. This M1 population consisted of isolates from 1997 (N=3), 2006 (N=3), 2011 (N=1), 2012 (N=2), 2015 (N=2), 2016 (N=2), 2017 (N=3), and 2018 (N=1). The isolation sources included samples collected from the throat (N=9, non-invasive strains) and blood (N=8, invasive strains). Genome sequences of the 17 strains were retrieved from the NCBI Nucleotide database (GenBank nucleotide accession Nos. are provided in Table 1).

To construct phylogenomic and evolutionary trees based on the maximum-likelihood method and Tamura three-parameter model [9], we conducted a core-genome SNP analysis using PhyML (http://www.atgc-montpellier.fr/phyml/) and MEGA v.11 (https://www.megasoftware.net/dload_win_beta) [10], as reported [11]. Publicly available whole-genome sequences of M1UK (M127snps or more) and M1intermediate (M113snps/M123snps) were incorporated into the tree as comparators, along with the reference genome of M1global strain MGAS5005 (accession No. CP000017.2). Fig. 1 shows that nearly all pre-COVID-19 Korean M1 genomes clustered around the M1global genome (except for GCH79–GCH97–GCH169), and all were genotypically distinct from the M1UK/M1intermediate genomes.

A major limitation of our study is the small number (N=17) of S. pyogenes genomes analyzed. To search for genome information on Korean S. pyogenes isolates, we queried PubMed by entering the search terms “Streptococcus pyogenes, Korea, whole genome” but retrieved only one report [7]. When we queried the NCBI Nucleotide database using the same search terms, we retrieved only two BioProjects, registered by Gyeongsang National University: “S. pyogenes strains GCH, whole-genome shotgun sequencing project” and “S. pyogenes strain GNUCH_SPyo_81, whole-genome shotgun sequencing project.” In the future, more pre-COVID-19 Korean S. pyogenes M1 genomes should be registered in the NCBI database.

In conclusion, our findings revealed that pre-COVID-19 Korean S. pyogenes strains were of the M1global and neither the M1UK nor M1intermediate lineages, indicating the hypervirulent strains had not been yet found. In the future, we need to clarify the presence of M1UK and M1intermediate during the post-COVID-19 era via whole-genome shotgun sequencing.
