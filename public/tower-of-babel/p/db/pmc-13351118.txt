The Estrogen-Mitochondria Axis in Bipolar Disorder: Promise, Complexity, and Future Directions
Authors: Marzieh Majd, Pamela B Mahon, Katherine E Burdick
Published: 2026

While bipolar disorder (BD) is reported to occur at comparable rates in males and females, differences in both clinical course and symptomatology have been observed, with females more frequently experiencing rapid cycling, mixed states, and depressive symptoms. In their review recently published in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, Zachos et al. (1) propose the estrogen-mitochondria axis as a mechanistic framework for understanding sex differences in the clinical course and symptomatology of BD. The central hypothesis advanced is that sex differences may be influenced by bioenergetic processes associated with estrogen fluctuations and their regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis (MB). Across the female lifespan, the article links key reproductive transitions—including puberty, pregnancy, postpartum, and perimenopause—to hormonal variation, alterations in hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis regulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, contributing to mood vulnerability.

This review highlights several compelling aspects to the consideration of estrogen-mitochondrial mechanisms in BD, as well as potential challenges. The proposed role of mitochondrial dysfunction in BD is a compelling aspect of the review, as is the lifespan framework highlighting reproductive transitions in females. While estrogen-mitochondria interaction has been demonstrated through preclinical work, a key challenge for the proposed framework, discussed further below, is the bidirectional relationship, which adds complexity to the identification of underlying causal mechanisms. Relationships with other sex hormones and neuroactive steroids also warrant additional consideration within this framework. Future large-scale studies using longitudinal and sex-stratified designs will be essential to clarify causal relationships. A strength of this review is the detailed mechanistic insight into mitochondrial biology. There is growing evidence that abnormalities in mitochondrial energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial DNA regulation are present in psychiatric conditions, including BD (2). The article provides a comprehensive overview of mitochondrial processes, including oxidative phosphorylation, reactive oxygen species regulation, mitophagy, and MB. The discussion of key regulatory pathways such as PGC-1α, NRF1/2, SIRT1, and AMPK (adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase) provides mechanistic support for the involvement of energy metabolism in psychiatric conditions. The review appropriately highlights the potential clinical relevance of targeting mitochondrial function in BD. Emerging pharmacological and nutraceutical approaches, including PPAR (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor) agonists, antioxidants, and metabolic interventions, are discussed as potential modulators of MB. While these strategies are promising, most remain in early stages of investigation, and their efficacy in clinically meaningful end points for BD is yet to be established. The proposal to integrate reproductive stage and hormonal fluctuations into clinical monitoring is important and aligns with precision medicine approaches.

The review highlights bidirectional interactions between estrogen signaling and mitochondrial function. The authors describe a reciprocal relationship in which mitochondria contribute to steroidogenesis through pregnenolone synthesis, while estrogen in turn promotes MB and oxidative phosphorylation. Estradiol is proposed to act via ERα/ERβ-mediated nuclear receptor signaling to regulate the expression of PGC-1α, the master regulator of mitochondrial gene expression, alongside key regulatory factors including NRF1, NRF2, PPARs, and estrogen-related receptors. The review also notes indirect pathways through SIRT1 and AMPK activation, further linking estrogen signaling to MB and metabolic adaptation. Together, this reciprocal relationship provides a coherent framework for exploring endocrine-metabolic interactions in neuropsychiatric disease, although much of the supporting evidence remains derived from preclinical models rather than clinical studies.

Another key strength of this review lies in its integrative, lifespan-oriented framework, which links sex-related differences in BD to endocrine transitions and associated changes in mitochondrial function across key reproductive life stages. This framing allows for a more nuanced consideration of sex-related differences in psychiatric vulnerability and aligns with growing interest in precision psychiatry.

The authors link puberty to hormonal surges that may influence neuroplasticity and stress regulation via the HPA axis, while cyclic reproductive stages are associated with phase-dependent mood variation. The postpartum period is characterized as a window of heightened relapse risk due to abrupt hormonal shifts and sleep disruption, and perimenopause is similarly framed as a period of increased vulnerability linked to declining estrogen and altered endocrine feedback. The presented associations between menstrual cycle phases and mood states in BD are derived from limited evidence and should therefore be interpreted as preliminary. However, although not presented in the review, increased rates of premenstrual dysphoric disorder have been observed in BD, further supporting a relationship between menstrual phase in females and BD (3). The extension of the estrogen-mitochondria axis framework to sex-related differences in the symptomatology of BD represents an intriguing area for further investigation. Although differences in clinical presentation between males and females with BD have been reported, findings remain heterogeneous across studies, and the mechanisms that underlie these patterns are not well understood. This may also be relevant to behavioral domains such as impulsivity and inhibitory control, although findings regarding menstrual cycle influence on cognitive performance have been mixed. Inconsistencies in menstrual cycle effects on cognition have been attributed to limited statistical power, as well as methodological and biological heterogeneity across studies. Studies examining inhibitory control have reported both null and phase-dependent effects (4), potentially depending on the specific component of inhibitory control assessed and the task used. Among studies incorporating both menstrual cycle phases and male-female comparisons, Colzato et al. (5) provides one example of phase-dependent findings. They examined inhibitory control using the stop signal task in healthy males and across 3 phases of the menstrual cycle in healthy females. Females in the late follicular phase, when serum estradiol is high, displayed poorer inhibitory control than males, whereas no differences were observed during the early follicular or midluteal phases. Higher estradiol was associated with poorer inhibition. Such findings suggest that behavioral variability may fluctuate across hormonal transitions rather than reflecting fixed sex-based symptom differences. Further studies examining how sex hormones interact with mitochondrial function across reproductive stages may therefore help clarify the relationship between hormonal variation and symptom expression in BD.

The review emphasizes estrogen-mediated mechanisms, with progesterone-related pathways discussed to a lesser extent. This is notable given that pregnenolone, which is discussed as a mitochondrial product and steroidogenic precursor, gives rise not only to estrogen but also to progesterone and related neuroactive metabolites. Progesterone and its neurosteroid derivatives, particularly allopregnanolone, have effects on GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) signaling and mood regulation (6), suggesting that progesterone-related pathways may also provide an additional mechanistic link between mitochondrial function and affective instability that is not fully incorporated within the proposed framework. This is particularly relevant in reproductive transition periods such as postpartum and perimenopause, where abrupt shifts in progesterone-derived neurosteroids have been implicated in mood vulnerability. This is further supported by the development of neurosteroid therapeutics such as brexanolone and zuranolone, which target the progesterone-derived allopregnanolone pathway and have demonstrated efficacy in postpartum depression. This perspective can be complemented by findings from Brown et al. (7) in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of pregnenolone in bipolar depression. A treatment effect on depressive symptoms was observed, with a stronger statistical signal in women (F = 2.68) compared with men (F = 0.14), suggesting potential differences between women and men in response to pregnenolone, possibly involving downstream neurosteroid pathways. While preliminary, these findings suggest that progesterone-related neurosteroid mechanisms may also be relevant to sex-related variability in the phenotypic expression of BD and warrant further integration into the estrogen-mitochondria framework proposed by the authors.

Conclusions

Overall, this review presents a conceptually rich and integrative framework linking estrogen signaling, mitochondrial biology, and BD across the female lifespan. The estrogen-mitochondria axis represents a promising hypothesis-generating framework that may help guide future research in sex-specific psychiatry. Nevertheless, substantial empirical work, particularly longitudinal human studies, is required before this model can be considered explanatory rather than exploratory. Integrating hormonal, metabolic, and psychosocial factors in a multilevel framework is likely to be essential for advancing understanding of BD in females. While these types of studies have been few and far between in BD, one promising platform that will soon offer the opportunity to test these questions is the recently launched Integrated Network of Breakthrough Discoveries for thriving with Bipolar Disorder (BD2) (8, 9, 10).
