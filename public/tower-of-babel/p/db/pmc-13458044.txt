Comparative evaluation of artificial intelligence-deep learning-based cine short-axis segmentation and manual post-processing for left ventricular function and structure in cardiac magnetic resonance
Authors: Yanghao Lin, Wenwei Zhong, Junfeng Yao, Jinliang Zeng, Zheng Zhong, Qimin Li, Ting Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is the reference standard for evaluating left ventricular (LV) function and structure. Manual post-processing is time-consuming, operator-dependent, and limited in reproducibility. Deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI-DL) offers automated image segmentation with expert-level accuracy, but requires clinical validation against manual analysis. This study aimed to directly compare two AI-DL segmentation strategies—simplified (trabeculae and papillary muscles included in blood pool) and standard (included in myocardium)—with manual analysis to determine which approach yields superior clinical agreement.MethodsWe retrospectively studied 104 patients who underwent CMR. Using both AI-DL (simplified/standard) methods and traditional manual post-processing software, we quantified LV parameters including ejection fraction (EF), end-diastolic volume and index (EDV/EDVI), end-systolic volume and index (ESV/ESVI), stroke volume and index (SV/SI), cardiac output and index (CO/CI), and end-diastolic LV-mass and index (M/MI). Analysis time, correlation, and agreement with manual results were compared.ResultsAI-DL markedly reduced analysis time compared with manual post-processing following standard method (median 50.0 vs. 217.0 s; P<0.001). The AI-DL simplified method underestimated EF (54.2 vs. 60.0), mass indices (131.45 vs. 156.00, 76.45 vs. 91.25) and overestimated volumetric indices (148.10 vs. 118.00, 88.70 vs. 69.80, 62.85 vs. 46.65, 40.00 vs. 28.70, 69.90 vs. 63.30, 41.70 vs. 37.83, 5.35 vs. 4.80, 3.15 vs. 2.80) relative to manual analysis (all P<0.001). In contrast, the standard method showed no significant differences from manual values (58.3 vs. 60.0, 122.80 vs. 118.00, 72.70 vs. 69.80, 46.30 vs. 46.65, 27.75 vs. 28.70, 64.70 vs. 63.30, 37.85 vs. 37.83, 4.90 vs. 4.80, 2.90 vs. 2.80, 163.05 vs. 156.00, 93.55 vs. 91.25) (all P>0.05). Both methods demonstrated strong correlations with manual results, with the standard method achieving higher intraclass correlation coefficient (0.798–0.978 vs. 0.690–0.928) and correlation coefficients (r=0.772–0.952 vs. 0.726–0.944) (all P<0.001) as well as narrower Bland-Altman limits of agreement (LOA) (the differences were smaller and not statistically significant, all P>0.05), whereas the simplified method had wider LOA (the differences were larger and statistically significant, all P<0.001).ConclusionsAI-DL enables rapid and reliable quantification of LV function and structure in CMR. Although both simplified and standard methods show strong agreement with manual analysis, the standard method provides better agreement and narrower limits of difference, making it more clinically acceptable. These findings highlight the potential of AI-DL as a practical tool for routine cardiac functional assessment.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has remained the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the world over the past few decades (1,2). Targeted and sensitive diagnostic methods are essential for early diagnosis and prevention. For left ventricular (LV) assessment, accurate evaluation of structural and functional parameters, including myocardial mass, volume, and ejection fraction (EF) is critical for accurate diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic decision-making in CVD (3). Therefore, a rapid and reliable cardiac examination of functional and structural parameters is of great significance for disease diagnosis, treatment, follow-up evaluation, and risk estimation (4).

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) provides high-resolution and multi-parametric assessment of cardiac function and morphology, and is regarded as the gold standard for LV evaluation (5). Compared with CMR, other imaging methods such as computed tomography (CT) and ultrasound have inherent limitations in image quality and safety (6,7). Cine CMR enables accurate quantification of LV functional and structural parameters without the need for gadolinium contrast. However, conventional post-processing relies on manual contour delineation, which is labor-intensive and prone to inter-observer variability, particularly in basal and apical slices where the anatomy is often ambiguous (8). These limitations have motivated the development of automated segmentation methods based on artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), which can accelerate analysis and reduce operator dependency (9,10).

Recent advances in cine short-axis (SAX) segmentation algorithms have introduced classifier-guided two-stage networks combined with all-slice fusion transformers. This framework mimics the radiologist’s annotation workflow by first classifying anatomical presence and then performing standard segmentation, thereby integrating both intra- and inter-slice contextual information. These methods address the shortcomings of traditional single-stage approaches, which often struggle with complex cardiac morphology and yield suboptimal performance in challenging slices (11). By improving contour accuracy and visual consistency—reducing artifacts such as holes or fragmented regions—cine SAX algorithms enable more robust and reliable quantification of LV mass, volumes, and EF without the need for contrast agents. This progress not only enhances the accuracy of AI-DL post-processing but also facilitates broader clinical translation of automated CMR analysis.

Despite these developments, evidence remains limited regarding direct comparisons between AI-DL methods that differ in their treatment of trabeculae and papillary muscles (TPM). The simplified method assigns TPM to the blood pool, whereas the standard method assigns TPM to the myocardium (12,13). Determining the optimal AI-DL strategy therefore requires validation against manual post-processing to assess which approach—simplified or standard—achieves better agreement with expert analysis. However, few studies to date have compared the performance of AI-DL for cardiac functional structural analysis between the simplified and standard CMR methods.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed clinical CMR data from patients and compared LV functional and structural parameters obtained from AI-DL (simplified and standard methods) with those from manual post-processing, with the aim of evaluating the clinical applicability of AI-DL for CMR analysis. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0364/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

A single-center, retrospective study was conducted in the Department of Radiology, The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University Yuedong Hospital. A total of 120 patients who underwent 3.0 Tesla (T) CMR scans from February 2024 to August 2025 were screened. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) clear consciousness with the ability to cooperate during CMR scanning; and (II) cine images of sufficient image quality to delineate endocardial and epicardial contours. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) inability to cooperate due to claustrophobia; and (II) patients with pacemakers and aneurysm clips in their bodies. A total of 104 patients were ultimately included in the study. Figure 1 shows a detailed flowchart illustrating the above process. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University Yuedong Hospital (No. II2026-004-01) and all patients provided written informed consent.

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition

Imaging was performed either on a Philips Ingenia Elition 3.0 T scanner (Philips, Heerlen, Netherlands) with an 18-channel phased-array body coil or a GE SIGNA Architect 3.0T (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with a flexible 30-channel phased-array body coil. Among the 104 included patients, 90 (86.5%) were examined on the Philips Ingenia Elition 3.0T and 14 (13.5%) on the GE SIGNA Architect 3.0T. Patients were scanned in the supine position using electrocardiogram (ECG) gating with breath-hold instruction. Sequences were acquired sequentially in the following order: a three-plane localizer (axial, coronal, sagittal), a black-blood sequence covering the region from the aortic arch to the diaphragmatic surface, and one or more sets of 2D SSH BIFE sequences at the same anatomical location. Subsequently, two-chamber (2ch), four-chamber (4ch), and a stack of short-axis images (10–15 slices) of the left ventricle were obtained. The typical imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) 3.0–3.1 ms; echo time (TE) 1.0–1.5 ms; flip angle (FA) 40–50°; field of view (FOV) 300–400 mm2; matrix (172–180) × (180–288); slice thickness 8 mm; slice gap spacing 1–2 mm; pixel size ~1.8×1.6 mm2.

Manual post-processing

The cine images were analyzed on vendor-provided Philips IntelliSpace IX/LX Workstations (No. 105554601) or GE Advanced Workstation (version 4.7) by two radiologists (each with >5 years of CMR experience) who independently performed semi-automated segmentation using vendor software, followed by manual adjustment. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Readers were blinded to all AI-DL outputs during manual analysis. Semi-automatic segmentation followed by manual correction was performed for the LV endocardium and epicardium at end-systole and end-diastole on short-axis stacks with TPM assigned to the myocardium by semiautomatic threshold selection using the vendors’ cardiac function analysis software (Figures 2,3). The following LV parameters were obtained: EF, end-diastolic volume and index (EDV/EDVI), end-systolic volume and index (ESV/ESVI), stroke volume and index (SV/SI), cardiac output and index (CO/CI), and mass and index (M/MI). The analysis time for each case was recorded.

AI-DL analysis (simplified and standard methods)

The same cine image was processed using United Imaging Intelligent CMR Image Processing Software (United Imaging Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Version 20250130sp1), which performs automatic LV segmentation and quantitative analysis based on DL algorithms. The AI-DL segmentation pipeline was fully automated without any user intervention. End-diastolic and end-systolic phases were automatically identified as the first phase after the ECG R-wave trigger and the phase with minimum LV cavity area on the volume-time curve, respectively. Phase selection was visually confirmed to match the phases used in the clinical reference analysis for each case. The analysis time was recorded.

The AI-DL pipeline produced two sets of results depending on the treatment of TPM: the simplified method (Figures 2C,2D,3C,3D) with TPM assigned to the blood pool; the standard method (Figures 2E,2F,3E,3F) with TPM assigned to the myocardium, consistent with contemporary post-processing consensus (12,13). Phase selection was visually confirmed to match the phases used in the clinical reference analysis for each case. Any discrepancy in phase selection (observed in 17.3% of cases) was manually corrected prior to comparative analysis to avoid bias.

Endpoints and measurements

Primary endpoints were between-method differences, agreement, and correlations for left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), EDV/EDVI, ESV/ESVI, SV/SI, CO/CI, and M/MI among manual, AI-DL simplified, and AI-DL standard outputs. Analysis time served as a secondary endpoint to quantify workflow efficiency.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc 20.022 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables. The AI analysis time was compared with the manual analysis time. The parameters of LV function and structure calculated based on AI-DL (simplified method/standard method) and manual method were compared to analyze the difference, consistency, and correlation between the two methods. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using paired-sample t-tests. For skewed continuous variables, data were expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR)], and paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson correlation for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation for non-normally distributed data. Agreement between methods was evaluated by intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman analysis. Correlation strength was defined as follows: r≤0.2, weak; 0.2<r≤0.4, mild; 0.4<r≤0.6, moderate; 0.6<r≤0.8, strong; and 0.8<r≤1.0, excellent. ICC was interpreted as ≤0.40, poor; 0.40<r≤0.75, moderate; and >0.75, good. For all statistical analysis, P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 104 patients who underwent CMR were included, comprising 80 males (76.9%) and 24 females (23.1%), with a median age of 57.5 years (IQR, 47.5–66.0 years). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 24.5±3.82 kg/m2, the median heart rate was 75 beats/min (IQR, 66–87 beats/min), and the mean body surface area (BSA) was 1.74±0.19 m2. The CMR diagnosis included: atrial septal defect (n=2, 1.9%), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (n=3, 2.9%), hypertensive heart disease (n=6, 5.8%), coronary heart disease (n=57, 54.8%), dilated cardiomyopathy (n=14, 13.5%), myocarditis (n=1, 0.9%), arrhythmia (n=4, 3.8%), valvular disease (n=3, 2.9%), heart failure (n=3, 2.9%), and others with no significant abnormality (n=11, 10.6%).

Inter-observer reproducibility of manual analysis

To assess inter-observer variability, we randomly selected 20 patients (10 with normal LV dimensions, 10 with dilated ventricles) and compared the initial independent measurements of the two radiologists before consensus. The ICC were: EF =0.930 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.834–0.971], EDV =0.931 (95% CI: 0.835–0.972), ESV =0.948 (95% CI: 0.876–0.979), CO =0.773 (95% CI: 0.510–0.904), and SV =0.853 (95% CI: 0.668–0.939), all indicating good reproducibility.

Analysis time

The median analysis time was significantly shorter for AI-DL than that of the manual method as listed in Table 1. The AI-DL methods analyzed in 50.0 seconds (IQR, 49.0–52.0 seconds), whereas the manual method needed 217.0 seconds (IQR, 211.5–223.0 seconds). This difference was statistically significant (Z=–8.855, P<0.001), demonstrating markedly improved efficiency with AI-DL.

Discussion

Evaluation of cardiac function and structure is essential for patient monitoring, risk estimation, disease characterization and treatment assessment in cardiovascular medicine (9). The left ventricle plays a pivotal role in maintaining circulation and its parameters such as EF, SV, CO, ESV, and EDV, and mass (M) have great clinical value (14). With the rapid progression of AI, particularly DL, automatic segmentation and quantitative analysis of medical images have become feasible, efficient, and accurate, offering a powerful alternative to traditional manual approaches (9,15-17).

Clinical and imaging characteristics

The patient cohort was representative of typical clinical practice, consisting predominantly of middle-aged and elderly males, with coronary heart disease as the most common diagnosis—consistent with the known epidemiological distribution of CVD (1,2). The median resting heart rate was 75 beats/min (IQR, 66–87 beats/min), which is within an acceptable range for high-quality cine CMR acquisition without the need for pharmacological rate control.

AI-DL simplified versus standard methods

At present, CMR analysis mainly relies on manual or semi-automated software, which is labor-intensive and operator-dependent (18-20). AI refers to the use of computer technology to simulate cognitive skills similar to humans (21), whereas DL belongs to an important branch of AI, defined as the machine learning algorithm for processing neural networks, with deep structure or multiple hidden layers of neural networks, so it is also called deep neural networks (9). Automated analysis reduces operator error and time cost, allowing measurements obtained within seconds (19,22). In our study, AL-DL reduced analysis time from nearly 4 minutes manually to 50 seconds (Z=–8.855, P<0.001), consistent with previous literature reports, reflecting the high efficiency of AI (23).

The Society of Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (SCMR) recognizes two main methods for quantifying cardiac functional and structural parameters in CMR: the simplified method and the standard method. There is still no uniformly accepted convention on the inclusion of trabecular tissue and papillary muscles in the myocardial mass (12,13). Manual analysis based on the simplified method remains widely used (24); some can achieve the standard methods, but it is time-consuming, operator-dependent, difficult to accurately annotate, and often requires manual correction (25,26). Given the irregular morphology and variability of TPM, especially at basal and apical slices, manual delineation can be challenging (27). The simplified method attributes TPM to the blood pool, leading to overestimation of ventricular volume and underestimation of myocardial mass. In contrast, the standard method classifies TPM as myocardium, improving anatomical consistency and aligning with expert consensus (4). Our findings demonstrate that AI-DL can implement both strategies automatically with minimal correction, thus overcoming key limitations of manual approaches.

Comparison, consistency, correlation, and agreement with different methods

It is sometimes difficult to ensure the consistency and accuracy of the manual method in practice, especially when dealing with complex anatomical structures. DL has high accuracy and efficiency in image detection, segmentation, registration, and feature classification (28). Accurate segmentation of medical images is the foundation for accurate diagnosis, whereas Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) can achieve the same segmentation accuracy as expert doctors in left and right ventricular cardiac membrane tracking (27). The previous literature (4,9,19,23,27,29) has mainly focused on technical metrics such as Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), average symmetric surface distance (ASSD), and Hausdorff distance (HD), as well as clinical indicators such as LV volume, mass, and EF to evaluate the value of AI in CMR analysis. These studies have demonstrated that AI is capable of fast, accurate, robust, and reliable automatic segmentation of cardiac images. The quantitative measurements of cardiac functional structure analysis obtained using AI show good consistency and correlation with the real value or manual measurement.

Compared with manual standard analysis, the AI-DL simplified method underestimated EF and mass indices, while overestimating volumetric indices, with significant statistical differences (all P<0.001). By contrast, the AI-DL standard method showed no significant differences from manual results (all P>0.05), indicating greater similarity. As described in Results, the standard method exhibited visually better contour consistency than the simplified method.

Our data indicate that TPM have a significant impact on the quantification of LV function and structure. This observation aligns with previous work. Riffel et al. (13) found that standard analysis yielded smaller EDV and ESV but higher EF and LV-M compared with the simplified method by manual post-processing (all P<0.001): LV-EDV: 149.7±32.5 vs. 160.5±35.0 mL; LV-ESV: 48.7±14.5 vs. 56.4±16.7 mL; LV-EF: 67.7%±5.4% vs. 65.1%±5.6%; LV-M: 122.9±32.0 vs. 111.5±29.1 g. There was a strong correlation between the results for LV functional structural parameters between the two methods (all r>0.9), with all results corroborated by our AI-based validation. In this study, AI-DL confirmed the differences between the two methods. The AI-DL simplified method showed moderate to good consistency (ICC =0.690–0.928) and strong to excellent correlation (r=0.726–0.944), whereas the standard method showed higher consistency (ICC =0.798–0.978) and stronger correlation (r=0.772–0.952). Bland-Altman plots showed that most of the differences between the AI-DL (simplified method/standard method) and the manual method were within the 95% LOA, indicating good agreement. Assadi et al. (29) found in a multi-center CMR cohort study of about 814 patients that LV parameters showed an excellent correlation between AI automatic and manual analysis [left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), r=0.98; left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), r=0.94; left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV), r=0.94; LVEF, r=0.91] and the difference was small (LVEDV −3.2 mL; LVESV 1.9 mL; LVSV −5.1 mL; LVEF −1.5%). However, in a study of 600 cases conducted by Bai et al. (19), based on short-axis cardiac cine images, the mean absolute differences between AI automatic and manual analysis measurements were: LVEDV 6.1 mL, LVESV 5.3 mL, and left ventricular mass (LVM) 6.9 g, respectively. The above results show that the automatic and manual analysis of LV function and structure parameters have good correlation and consistency, and the repeatability is high, which is consistent with the results of this study. The r values (0.931, 0.927, 0.936) and difference values (0.376%, –0.630 mL, –2.710 mL) of EF, EDV, and ESV of the standard method and manual method were similar to those reported in the above literature, and the ICC and r values of the standard method parameters were higher than those of the simplified method. The difference was significantly smaller and the 95% LOA was narrower, so the difference was more easily accepted in clinical practice.

Future technical improvements

Future technical improvements may further enhance the clinical utility of AI-DL in CMR. These include training on multi-vendor and multi-field-strength datasets to improve generalizability, incorporating attention mechanisms to better handle basal slice ambiguity, and extending the pipeline to include right ventricular and atrial segmentation. Additionally, integrating AI-DL analysis with emerging sequences such as 4D flow and myocardial strain imaging could provide a comprehensive cardiac assessment in a single automated workflow.

Future work may also incorporate uncertainty estimation or quality control modules to automatically identify cases requiring manual correction, thereby improving reliability in real-world clinical deployment.

Conclusions

Cine CMR represents the gold standard for the quantitative assessment of LV functional and structural parameters and is critically important in clinical practice (30). The traditional manual method is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and less reproducible. In contrast, AI-DL significantly shortens analysis time while maintaining strong correlation and consistency with manual results. Notably, the standard method achieves smaller parameter differences and narrower LOA than the simplified method, making it more clinically acceptable. Therefore, both the simplified and standard AI-DL methods can provide accurate, reliable, and efficient quantification of LV function and structure, with the standard method offering superior concordance. These findings underscore the clinical value of AI-DL in assisting CMR-based cardiac functional and structural analysis.
