One-year multicenter surveillance of Fosfomycin resistance Enterobacterales: the rise of FosA3-producing P. mirabilis
Authors: Katerina Chudejova, Vittoria Mattioni Marchetti, Vita Zaccaria, Stepanka Kanova, Anna Sramkova, Marcela Krutova, Lenka Ryskova, Gabriela Kroneislova, Beata Horvathova, Renata Tejkalova, Eva Krejci, Iva Vagnerova, Zuzana Zemanova, Ibrahim Bitar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•One-year multicenter survey of fosfomycin-resistant Enterobacterales in Czech Republic hospitals.•FosA3-producing Proteus mirabilis predominates.•FosA3-positive P. mirabilis ST185 forms a local clonal cluster while E. coli clones are heterogeneous.•Transporter defects (GlpT/UhpT) are widespread; most fosA strains carry ESBL genes.•Molecular surveillance is crucial to monitor and contain emerging fosfomycin resistance.

Introduction

The global rise in antimicrobial resistance continues to undermine the effectiveness of standard therapies, with multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative pathogens increasingly implicated in severe, difficult-to-treat infections. As treatment options narrow, attention has turned not only to novel antibiotics but also to older antimicrobials with in vitro activity against some MDR pathogens (Paranos et al., 2022).

One of these newly rediscovered antibiotics is fosfomycin (FOS). Its broad-spectrum activity is explicated by irreversible inhibition of MurA (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvyl transferase), involved in peptidoglycan synthesis. For this reason, FOS is a valuable treatment against MDR organisms, including, among others, ESBL-producers, AmpC (hyper)producers, and some carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (Falagas et al., 2019; Putensen et al., 2019). Clinically, FOS plays an increasingly important role not only in the treatment of uncomplicated and complicated urinary tract infections (Rouphael et al., 2023), but also in life-threatening infections caused by MDR organisms, mainly as part of combination therapy (Antonello et al., 2020). Unfortunately, with the more frequent use of FOS in clinical practice, the FOS resistance level is also increasing.

FOS resistance arises via several well‑characterized molecular mechanisms. The first is reduced drug uptake: mutations or deletions of FOS transporters, glycerol-3-phosphate transporter (GlpT) and/or the glucose-6-phosphate transporter (UhpT), as well as in their regulators (e.g., uhpA, uhpB, cyaA, ptsI) (Ohkoshi et al., 2017; Nilsson et al., 2003). These alterations of transporter/regulators lead to reduced cell permeability, resulting in FOS minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) increase. Second, target modification can be detected: although rare in clinical isolates, point mutations of the active site Cys115 of MurA can reduce FOS binding and thus its efficacy (Cao et al., 2019). The third is enzymatic inactivation mediated by FosA enzymes. FosA is a glutathione-S-transferase that catalyzes the opening of the FOS epoxide ring, leading to FOS inactivation (Zurfluh et al., 2020). Currently, 14 genetically related fosA variants on chromosomes and plasmids in members of the genus Enterobacterales have been reported in the GenBank database.

The prevalence of fosA-type genes mediated by plasmids is increasing globally. Findlay et al., in a comprehensive study, demonstrated a high prevalence of fosA3 localized on conjugative plasmids of various Inc-types in an international collection of E. coli isolates. Less frequently detected fosA-types in this study were four fosA4 (Findlay et al., 2023). Similar conclusions were reached in Portuguese national study, where Grilo et al. also described the dominance of the transferable fosA3 followed by fosA4 in E. coli (Grilo et al., 2023). There are also reports of fosA3 in Citrobacter freundii: a recent Italian clinical carbapenemase producer (KPC-2, VIM-1) with the presence of the fosA3 gene, carried on a large conjugative plasmid (Mattioni Marchetti et al., 2024). Many of these plasmids carrying fosA also harbored ESBL or carbapenemase genes, underscoring the mobility and clinical risk of fosA-mediated resistance, as well as the potential for spreading resistance to additional antibiotics.

National surveillances on FOS resistant strains are scarce in Europe due to the limited diagnostic approaches for MICs and for rapid FosA enzymes’ identification. In the Czech Republic, FOS has not been available until 2014 and only one multicentric national surveillance on fosA genes incidence among carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales dated back to 2023 (Mattioni Marchetti et al., 2023). This study aims to assess the prevalence of FOS resistance among Enterobacterales in Czech health-care settings and to provide a comprehensive characterization of the dominant molecular resistance mechanisms in these isolates.

Materials and methods

Study design, bacterial collection, susceptibility determination and FosA detection

In this study nine different catchment tertiary care hospitals, spanning throughout the Czech Republic, have been invcluded: University Hospital Pilsen (FNP), General University Hospital in Prague (VFN), University Hospital Hradec Kralove (HK), University Hospital Brno (FNB), St. Anne's University Hospital in Brno (FNUSA), University Hospital Ostrava (OST), University Hospital Olomouc (OL), Motol University Hospital in Prague (MOT), and Na Homolce Hospital in Prague (HOM). Participating hospitals were requested to collect FOS-resistant isolates of E. coli, Proteus spp., and Citrobacter spp. identified during the year 2024. Resistance to FOS was defined according to the EUCAST breakpoint criteria (version 14.0). Other Enterobacterales species, as Serratia spp. and Klebsiella spp., were excluded due to the intrinsic chromosomal presence of the fosA gene. To avoid duplicate and clonally related isolates, only one isolate per patient was included. In addition, isolates originating from the same hospital department and sharing identical antibiograms were excluded to minimize the inclusion of potential clonal strains. FOS susceptibility testing was performed using the EUCAST-recommended agar dilution method. Alternatively, gradient diffusion (E-test) or disk diffusion methods were accepted as screening approaches when performed and interpreted according to EUCAST standards.

Identification of strains was confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) with MALDI Biotyper software (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). FOS MICs were confirmed using Agar-dilution method (ADM) and interpreted according to EUCAST clinical breakpoints v 15.0, whereas the production of FosA-like and FosC2 enzymes was detected by the PPF test (Nakamura et al., 2014). All FosA producers’ strains underwent fosA-like gene amplification via Polymerase-chain reaction (PCR), as reported elsewhere (Freire et al., 2023).

Carbon-source growth test (CSG)

The activity of GlpT and UhpT transporters was investigated by carbon-source growth test (CSG), as reported elsewhere (Huang et al., 2021). Each strain was investigated in duplicate and E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as positive control. Absence of growth in the presence of G3P and/or G6P is related to functional deficiency of the transporters GlpT and UhpT, respectively (Huang et al., 2021).

Whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the 14 fosA-positive isolates using NucleoSpin Microbial DNA kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany). Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Initial paired-end reads were quality trimmed using the Trimmomatic tool v0.40 (Bolger et al., 2014) and then assembled by use of the de Bruijn graph-based de novo assembler SPAdes v4.2.0 (https://github.com/ablab/spades). Specification on the reads’ quality are available on Supplementary table S1. Assembled sequences were annotated using the RAST (Rapid Annotation using Subsystems Technology) server. In-silico multilocus sequence typing (MLST) evaluation of the strains, when applicable, was accomplished through PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org). Reconstruction of the resistome, plasmidome, and virulome of the isolates was accomplished using ResFinder v4.7.2, PlasmidFinder v2.1, and the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB) via ABRicate (github.com/tseemann/ABRicate).

Phylogenetic analysis

In order to track strains transmission path and, thus, to understand their microevolution, SNP-based core genome phylogenies of each STs were accomplished on the FosA-producing strains using parsnp v1.2 (https://github.com/marbl/parsnp). Genomes were retrieved from EnteroBase (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk) for E. coli and from PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/proteus-spp) for Proteus spp. In detail, a total of 127 P. mirabilis ST135 and 27 P. mirabilis ST185 were compared with fosA3-harboring P. mirabilis from this study using P. mirabilis HI4320 (AM942759.1) as reference. Referring to E. coli, a total of 169 E. coli ST550 and 220 E. coli ST1308 genomes were compared, using E. coli TUM9803 (CP135671.1) as reference genome. Considering E. coli ST69 and ST58, due to large datasets, a genome selection was applied, including all genomes harboring fosA-like genes or genomes from the Czech Republic, obtaining the following datasets: n=80 E. coli ST58 and n=131 E. coli ST69. For both E. coli and Proteus spp., a SNP cutoff of ≤20 was used for defining close clonal relationship, as reported elsewhere (de Been et al., 2015). The built datasets were additionally evaluated for the resistome and plasmidome. A graphic illustration of the trees was built with the Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) (https://itol.embl.de/).

Protein mutations

To molecularly investigate the presence of amino acid mutation in MurA, GlpT and UhpT transporters, BLAST alignment was conducted using the following amino acid reference sequences:

MurA E. coli (AAA24187.1), GlpT E. coli (CAA68598.1), UhpT E. coli (AAA24723.1), MurA P. mirabilis (WP_004245292.1) , GlpT P. mirabilis (WP_004245406.1). The effects of amino acid alterations on the biological function were predicted using the online Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant tool (SIFT)(https://sift.bii.a-star.edu.sg). The SIFT involves searching for similar protein sequences, creating an alignment, and calculating a score for a given substitution. A score below 0.05 indicates a "deleterious" substitution that likely affects protein function, while a score of 0.05 or higher suggests the change is "tolerated".

The collection included 149/211 P. mirabilis, 57/211 E. coli, 3/211 P. vulgaris, 1/211 P. hauseri, and 1/211 C. amalonaticus (Fig. 2C). All species strains were confirmed through MALDI-TOF MS. Increased FOS MICs were highlighted in all Proteus spp. (FOS MIC > 64 mg/L), while the FOS resistance phenotype was confirmed in all E. coli strains using ADM (FOS MIC > 8 mg/L). The PPF test pointed out the probable production of FosA enzymes in 34/211 strains (P. mirabilis n = 29, E. coli n = 5). Moreover, all 211 isolates were subjected to a carbon-source growth test (CSG test), which revealed impairment in the FOS transporters’ systems. Among Proteus spp. (n = 153), 93.46 % exhibited reduced growth in the presence of G3P, indicating impaired GlpT transporter function. In the remaining 10 isolates (6.54 %), no alteration in GlpT activity was detected; notably, five of these isolates harbored the fosA gene, which may explain the observed FOS resistance (Fig. 2D). The activity of the UhpT transporter was not assessed in Proteus spp. due to the natural absence of the corresponding gene. Among E. coli and C. amalonaticus isolates, 98.28 % demonstrated impaired GlpT activity. In addition, all isolates (100 %) showed reduced growth in the presence of G6P, consistent with impaired UhpT transporter activity. Impairment of both GlpT and UhpT transporters was observed in 98.28 % of these isolates, while in 1.72 %, only one transporter impairment was implied (Fig. 2E). These transporter impairments were associated with increased FOS MICs in most of the tested strains.

Molecular and WGS analysis

All strains with a positive PPF test were subsequently investigated through PCR for the presence of fosA genes, confirming in 14/34 strains (9/14 P. mirabilis and 5/14 E. coli). All 14 fosA-carrying strains underwent WGS and displayed substantial genomic diversity. In detail, seven out of nine P. mirabilis belonged to ST185 and the remaining two to ST135. This variety increased among the five FosA-producing E. coli, where ST69 (n=2), ST58 (n=1), ST1308 (n=1) and ST550 (n=1) were identified (Table 1). FosA3 emerged as the leading allelic variant (85.7 %), followed by fosA4 (14.3 %). Interestingly, fosA4 was uniquely detected in E. coli strains (n=2 ST 69 and n=1 ST58), while fosA3 reached both E. coli and P. mirabilis (Table 1). Moreover, except for fosA4, all strains were co-harboring blaCTX-M-like genes (n= 8 blaCTX-M-65, n= 1 blaCTX-M-55 and n=1 blaCTX-M-3). All fosA4-carrying E. coli co-harbored blaTEM-215 ESβL. Differently, plasmidome analysis depicted a more homogeneous distribution of replicons. In fact, IncF replicons emerged in five out of 12, Col type in two and IncHI2 in one (Table 1). P. mirabilis ST185

Seven FosA-producing strains carried the allelic variant fosA3 (Table 1, Fig. 3A). These seven strains all belong to ST185. Based on the SNP-based core genome phylogeny, all seven ST185 P. mirabilis clustered together, revealing high-level relatedness (SNPs ≤ 11). Interestingly, this Czech cluster included strains obtained from three different Czech regions: Hradec Kralove (n= 4/7), Olomouc (n=1/7) and Prague (n=2/7). P. mirabilis ST135

Two FosA3-producing P. mirabilis (FNP2 and VFN10) were assigned as ST135. The strains were obtained from different patients: FNP2 was collected on 12th January 2024 at the University Hospital Pilsen, while VFN10 on 22nd March 2023 at the General University Hospital of Prague. Despite the different date and place of origin, FNP2 and VFN10 manifested a close genomic link (SNPs = 15), clustering in the same branch (Fig. 3C). As well as P. mirabilis ST185, FNP2 and VFN10 were the only representative strains from the Czech Republic and showed a distant evolution path to other ST135 genomes (SNPs ≥ 1,000).

E. coli ST550

The only case of E. coli ST550 (VFN13), an emerging MDR Extraintestinal Pathogenic E. coli, belonged to phylogroup B2, harbored the fosA3 variant and was collected at the Neurology ward of General University Hospital in Prague on 22nd April 2024. The core genome phylogeny highlighted a more homogeneous relation with other European E. coli ST550 genomes. In detail, VFN13 clusterized with a wide branch including several North European E. coli ST550 of clinical origin, as “RC_6272AA” from Norway (SNPs = 58) and “XB_5121AA” from Denmark (SNPs = 59) (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, based on the metadata analysis, none of the included genomes owned any fosA variants. E. coli ST1308

HOM20 was obtained from the Cardiology ward of Na Homolce Hospital in Prague on 15th July 2024. Based on the WGS analysis, HOM20 was assigned to ST1308 and carried the fosA3 variant. The core genome phylogeny and the available metadata revealed a worldwide circulation of ST1308 mainly in the animal ecosystem (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, HOM20 disclosed relevant linkage with a USA cluster, collected from livestock animals (Fig. 4B). No genomic relatedness was indicated with other fosA3-carrying E. coli ST1308. This evidence supports the hypothesis of a link of diffusion with farm animals of US origin and of a subsequent plasmid-mediated acquisition of the fosA3 gene.

E. coli ST58

MOT4 was identified on 3rd February 2024 from the Gynecology department of Motol University Hospital in Prague. WGS analysis recognized the presence of fosA4 variant and the association with ST58 (clonal complex 155). From a phylogenetic perspective, MOT4 is evolutionarily distant from other fosA-like-carrying ST58 isolates circulating in different ecological niches within the Czech Republic (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, MOT4 showed the highest genomic similarity with ZC_3535AA, collected in the USA from an unknown source (Fig. 5A). E. coli ST69

The remaining FosA-producing E. coli, VFN24 and VFN28, were both obtained from the Gynecology ward of General University Hospital in Prague. Moreover, VFN24 and VFN28 shared the fosA4 variant and belonged to ST69. Unexpectedly, this strain resulted genomically distant, carrying more than 2,600 SNPs differences, suggesting different evolution and diffusion path. This divergence was reflected in similarities with the compared genomes. In fact, VFN24 manifested close linkage with ZC_6749AA (SNPs= 22), obtained from human niches in the USA, and other relevant likeness (SNPs = 40-55) with ST69 of human origin from China. VFN28 exhibited a different evolutionary path, showing similarities with human isolates from China, but with a higher phylogenetic distance (SNPs = 170-181) (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, in both clusters VFN24 and VFN28 were the sole representatives of fosA4-carrying. The data further suggest connections with different ecological niches in the United States and China but also highlight a difference in the acquisition of FOS resistance genes, favoring the stabilization of fosA4.

Single amino acid mutations

Among the 14 sequenced strains, GlpT disclosed the wider pattern of single-point mutations not affecting protein function (Table 1). Among E. coli, E448K was the predominant, followed by L297F, T348N, E443Q and Q444E. Interestingly, VFN28 presented the mutation E299K, predicted as affecting protein function. No altering mutations were detected in GlpT among P. mirabilis strains. No mutations with a SIFT score <0.05 were identified within MurA. These findings show a multifactorial involvement of FOS resistance, which may contribute to a reduced sensitivity to the drug also following loss of fosA genes.

Discussion

The use of FOS has gained optimistic interest in the treatment of serious infections caused by MDR and carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales. However, its recent reuse in clinical practice has highlighted a series of limitations that make difficult not only to evaluate it but also to study the resistance phenomena associated with it. Both EUCAST and CLSI recognize ADM as the reference method for FOS MIC evaluation (Chudejova et al., 2024). Faster alternatives can be found in disc diffusion methods but applicable on E. coli only (Chudejova et al., 2024).

In this study PPF test revealed a positivity percentage of 16.11 %, against a 6.63 % of detected fosA genes, with a 58.82 % of false positive results. Based on our results is PPF test applicable on E. coli only (100 % specificity), but with limited use for other Enterobacterales due to reduced specificity. This phenotypic performance is in line with literature results, where a reduction in sensitivity for species as Proteus spp. can occur due to naturally lack of UhpT transporter (O’May et al., 2009). For this evidence, the molecular approach may provide the most reliable results.

Contrary to what was reported in the literature so far, FOS-resistant P. mirabilis was the leading encountered species in our study and the one most associated with fosA3 genes. P. mirabilis is an opportunistic pathogen that commonly causes catheter-associated urinary tract infections, wound infections, gastroenteritis and, in some cases, bacteremia. In recent years, unusual cases of Proteus spp. infections have emerged with increasing regularity on a large scale (Su et al., 2025). However, the literature is still scarce on this phenomenon in Europe. Loras and co-authors evidence the presence of only two FosA3-producing strains among 623 FOS-resistant P. mirabilis, marking the low incidence of fosA3 in P. mirabilis (Loras et al., 2023). The evident epidemiological gap of FOS-resistant P. mirabilis requires greater attention at the clinical level, using both phenotypic and molecular approaches, in order to avoid misidentifications.

The ST135 lineage includes international clones that are emerging in Asian continent due to its potential in acquiring various carbapenemase genes, as blaOXA-48, blaVIM-78, and blaNDM-1 (Mavroidi et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). The association P. mirabilis ST135 and FosA3 is rare but already pointed out in China by Guo and co-authors from human urine and feces (Guo et al., 2025). Similarly, Su and colleagues highlight the circulation of blaCTX-M-65 + fosA3 P. mirabilis ST135 in the Chinese veterinary niche (Su et al., 2025). In our study both ST135s showed clonal relatedness, while no significant similarities have been identified with other ST135s, suggesting the emergence of a clonal ST135 group uniquely within the Czech Republic.

The ST185 is a not-well characterized P. mirabilis group that is rarely reported on a global level. Among Chinese isolates it has been associated with blaCTX-M-65 and fosA3 in clinical health-care settings (Guo et al., 2025). In our study the ST185 represented the largest ST and the most widespread within the Czech hospitals. As well as ST135, the seven P. mirabilis ST185 exhibited the blaCTX-M-65 and fosA3 co-harboring, suggesting the implication of horizontal transferability. The seven ST135 emerged in three different locations and times: in Hradec Kralove in February-March 2024, in Olomouc in March 2024, and in Prague from May to September 2024. This geographical diversity seems also reflected in genetic variability, with the Prague clones being genetically more distant from those of Hradec Kralove and Olomouc.

E. coli ST69 is a notorious extraintestinal pathogenic strain and the leading lineage causing urinary tract infections (Mattioni Marchetti et al., 2021). It has been implicated in horizontal acquisition of several hydrolyzing enzymes, such as CTX-M and KPC (Giacobbe et al., 2015). The occurrence of fosA4 within E. coli ST69 is a rare event that has been reported only in Pakistan from wild birds’ feces and in Australia from humans (Munir et al., 2025; Mowlaboccus et al., 2020).

In this study, we highlighted the occurrence of the fosA3 in E. coli ST1308, a strictly veterinary clone, genomically connected with livestock animals from the USA (Elias et al., 2019). The use of FOS is worldwide but heterogeneous, with a restricted use to human niches in European countries and an expanded usage in both human and animal niches in Latin America countries (Juscele et al., 2025). According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United States, the use of FOS is allowed in treating E. coli-related diarrhoea and salmonellosis in cattle, pseudotuberculosis in Perciformes, and other various bacterial infections in broiler chickens and pigs. For this reason, FOS is classified as a highly important veterinary antimicrobial agent (https://www.fao.org/faoterm/viewentry/en/?entryId=175085).

From December 2018 to February 2022 a genomic surveillance on FOS resistance among carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales was conducted in the Czech Republic. In fact, the overall rate of FOS resistance Enterobacterales was around 29 %, with impairment in transporters as leading mechanisms (97.6 %) against FosA production (2.4 %) (Mattioni Marchetti et al., 2023). In this study, the share of FosA enzyme-producing strains rose to 6.6 %, underlining an increase in the trend that is approaching the global estimated share (Seok et al., 2020). This increase can be explained by several factors. Firstly, the Czech Republic has witnessed an increased use of the FOS molecule in clinical practice and, in combination, against severe infections due to the increased rates of general antibiotic resistance (Mattioni Marchetti et al., 2023; Fajfr et al., 2017). Secondly, during the COVID-19 pandemic and post-pandemic period, the Czech Republic experienced an increase in tourism and international travel, especially in Pilsen and Prague, with an annual increase of 4.4 % (https://www.czechtourism.cz). Moreover, as reported by the Czech National Bank (CNB), the Czech Republic population has increased significantly due to an influx of refugees from Ukraine following the 2022 invasion, with Ukrainians making up the largest group of the more than one million foreign nationals now living in the country (https://www.cnb.cz).

According to Research Institute for Labour and Social Affairs (RILSA), the largest population increases in the Czech Republic after the start of the Ukraine war have been concentrated in Prague, followed by Central Bohemia, South Moravia, and Pilsen and Hradec Kralove regions (https://www.rilsa.cz/). In our study, those regions were the most affected by fosA-like genes circulation. In this historical period characterized by an ever-increasing number of intracontinental movements, it is essential to strengthen molecular surveillance systems for those molecules that were previously considered minor, such as FOS.

Conclusion

A depiction of the current circulation of FOS-resistant Enterobacterales in clinical settings in the Czech Republic has been provided. The results raise concerns about the increase in strains harboring acquired fosA genes and the limitations of culture-based methods available in clinical practice. The molecular approach needs to be implemented and complemented with culture-based methods, to monitor and contain the phenomenon of FOS resistance. Multifactorial approaches in FOS-resistant phenomenon are essential for the timely and specific detection of FOS resistance and for the optimal management of antimicrobial stewardship programs, which aim to preserve the effectiveness of FOS.
