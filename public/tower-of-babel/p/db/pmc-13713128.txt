Exploring surgical failures in primary hyperparathyroidism
Authors: Azza Mediouni, Maamoun Kriaa, Maha Mejbri, Rihab Lahmar, Maissa Lajhouri, Houda Chahed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction: Parathyroidectomy is considered the most cost-effective treatment for primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), with a relatively low incidence of complications. Nonetheless, 5-10% of patients do not achieve cure after initial parathyroidectomy. We hereby report our experience to determine the causes of our uncured patients and explore factors that could be associated with failure.Methods: We conducted a retrospective case series study and reviewed all the patients who underwent surgery for PHPT from 2004 to 2020. Cure was defined as re-establishment of normal calcium homeostasis lasting a minimum of 6 months. Persistent PHPT (perPHPT) was defined as ongoing hypercalcemia at any time within the 6 months after surgery.Results: The overall failure-to-cure rate was 6%. Of the 165 patients, 155 underwent successful surgical treatment of PHPT at the initial operation and perPHPT occurred in 10 patients. Baseline factors including age, sex, preoperative laboratory values, imaging, and surgical approach did not differ significantly between cured and non-cured groups. The sensitivity of intraoperative PTH measurement (iOPTH) as an indicator of operative success was 88,5%. Of the 10 patients with perPHPT, multi-gland disease (MGD) was present in 7 and ectopic adenoma in one. The lack of iOPTH use contributed to failure in 2 cases.Conclusion: Our study underscores the critical role of MGD as a significant cause of perPHPT. Other etiologies may include ectopic glands or technical problems during surgery. The use of iOPTH is crucial in reducing the incidence of perPHPT and should be a routine practice.

INTRODUCTION

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the third most common endocrine disorder, and the most common cause of hypercalcemia, with a prevalence of 0.1% to 1% of adults in the general population (1). Parathyroidectomy (PTx) is considered the most cost- effective treatment for PHPT, with a relatively low incidence of complications. Nonetheless, 5-10% of patients do not achieve a long-term cure after initial parathyroidectomy (2). Causes of persistent PHPT (perPHPT) include failure to localize a pathologic gland during the first surgery or incomplete resection due to the presence of multi-glandular disease (MGD) (3). The purpose of this study is to investigate surgery failure in our experience, explore uncured patients’ characteristics, and review relevant literature.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective study of patients who underwent initial PTx between 2004 and 2020. Patients with secondary, or tertiary hyperparathyroidism were excluded, as well as those who had prior parathyroid surgeries, and those with post-operative follow-up of less than 6 months. The information gathered covered demographics, preoperative and postoperative laboratory values (ionized calcium, parathyroid hormone (PTH), 25-OH vitamin D, alkaline phosphatase…), preoperative imaging results, intraoperative findings, intraoperative PTH (iOPTH) values, the extent of surgery, the final pathology, and long-term results. The normal ranges of Ca and PTH were defined as 85–104 mg/L and 15–65 ng/L, respectively. Preoperative imaging consisted of neck ultrasonography (US) and a Technetium (99m Tc) sestamibi scintigraphy (MIBI). We considered MIBI and US concordant if they both localized the adenoma on the same side of the neck. In addition, a CT scan was performed when the lesions were difficult to locate. The use of intraoperative tools (frozen section and iOPTH) was variable and left to the treating surgeon's judgment (considering that iOPTH monitoring has been introduced in our department since 2012). Frozen section was used to confirm the nature of the excised tissue. To monitor PTH intraoperatively, two serum samples were collected. These were taken from a venous line 10 min after the induction of general anesthesia and positioning of the patient (baseline), and 10 minutes after the removal of the abnormal parathyroid tissu. According to the established Vienna criteria, intraoperative success was defined as a decay of greater than or equal to 50% from baseline (4). The surgical strategy was divided into two categories: unilateral (UNE) and bilateral neck exploration (BNE). In the preoperative evaluation, if the pathologic gland was localized, unilateral PTx was performed; otherwise, bilateral exploration was performed. Three or three and a half gland excision was undertaken when parathyroid hyperplasia was clinically diagnosed at operation. The final diagnosis incorporated both histopathologic and iOPTH criteria: Single adenoma (SA) was defined as one histologically abnormal gland with a corresponding decrease in iOPTH after excision. Double adenoma (DA) refers to the presence of two glands that exhibit histological abnormalities with an appropriate iOPTH decrease after excision. Four-gland hyperplasia (HP) was defined as four histologically abnormal glands. Post-operative PTH levels and serum calcium levels were measured on postoperative day one and at the first outpatient follow-up appointment, which typically occurred one month after surgery. Long-term follow-up consisted of ionized calcium, PTH, and vitamin D levels obtained at 3, 6, and 12 months, and then annually. Cure was defined as re-establishment of normal calcium homeostasis lasting a minimum of 6 months. PerPHPT was defined as ongoing hypercalcemia at any time within the 6 months after surgery. Records of patients not cured by initial surgery were reviewed to explore factors underlying failure to cure.

RESULTS

We included a total of 165 initial PTx. The mean age was 56 years [27-78] and 148 patients (89,7 %) were female. The incidence of family history of hyperparathyroidism or personal history of multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) was low: 2 patients. On biochemical evaluation, the mean preoperative serum calcium was 118.36 mg/L (90-217 mg/L), and the mean preoperative PTH was 574,27 pg/mL (90-8890 pg/ ml). Ten patients (6%) had normocalcemic PHPT. All but one patient had US and MIBI. One or more enlarged glands were identified in 115 patients (70%) for US and 138 patients for MIBI (84%). Eighty-five patients (51,8 %) had lateralized and concordant imaging. After comparing surgery, histopathological and biochemical data, the results were as follows: 90,3 % of patients (n=149) presented with a SA, 3,6 % had DA (n=6), and 4,8 % (n=8) had HP. Two patients (1,2 %) had parathyroid carcinoma (PC). The median biochemical follow-up time was 13,8 months [6-96]. The overall failure-to-cure rate was 6%. Of the 165 patients, 155 (94 %) underwent successful surgical treatment of PHPT at the initial operation. PerPHPT occurred in 10 patients. After re-exploring and treating most of these patients, the failure rate has been reduced to 2,4 % (2 patients). Baseline factors including age, sex, preoperative laboratory values, and imaging did not differ significantly between cured and non-cured groups. IOPTH was documented for 62.4% (103/165) of all primary operations. Mean post-induction and post-excision PTH were 723,1 pg/mL (91-5400) and 155,8 pg/mL (17-1062), respectively. Mean post-excision PTH decrease was 66%, and PTH level decreased to a normal level in 34 patients. In 15 cases, post-excision iOPTH levels fell by <50% from baseline levels. Among patients receiving curative procedures (n=96), iOPTH levels decreased by 50% or more in 88.5% of the cases (85/96), correctly predicting success (true positives), while in 11 cases (11.5%) the level fell by less than half (false negatives). Intra-operative PTH level was measured in 7/10 cases of surgical failure and was reduced by >50% in 3/7 cases, falsely suggesting operative success (false positives). Overall, the sensitivity and specificity of IOPTH were 88.5% and 57.1%, respectively, with a Positive Predictive Value (PPV) of 96.59% and a Negative Predictive Value (NPV) of 26.67%. The immediate post-surgery mean calcium level was 92,5 mg/L: postsurgical hypercalcemia was evident in 16 patients (9,7%) but only 10 presented perPHPT. Analysis of patients in whom first surgery failed revealed that MGD accounted for 7/10 of perPHPT, and 5 of them were BNE. In one case (case 4, table 2), MGD was recognized preoperatively but the initial surgery was incomplete. Five other patients had failure due to a suspected or confirmed second adenoma. Another patient (case 5, table 2) did not undergo standard imaging and was operated on based on a CT scan suggesting a DA. The other cause of failure besides MGD was the inability to detect a single adenoma. The use of iOPTH or frozen section could have prevented two of these failures (case 1 and 2, table 2). DISCUSSION

When a parathyroidectomy is performed successfully, serum calcium levels typically return to the normal range, effectively addressing the metabolic issues associated with hypercalcemia. This normalization may also lessen symptoms and enhance quality of life (5). Despite a success rate exceeding 95% in experienced centers, the most common complication today remains surgical failure (6). Persistent primary hyperparathyroidism is defined as the persistence or reappearance of hypercalcemia within the first six months post-parathyroidectomy. This condition generally arises due to either a missed pathological parathyroid gland or an incomplete resection of hyperfunctional parathyroid tissue (7). Detailed causes of perPHPT after parathyroidectomy may include:

1/ Failure to detect a solitary adenoma due to surgical technical issues, often related to the surgeon's lack of experience (7), and in our cases, the absence of intraoperative tools. Missed solitary adenomas are common in low-volume centers, and these adenomas can be located in normal or ectopic positions (8). The incidence of ectopic glands can reach up to 16%, typically located in the neck (11.6%) or mediastinum (4.3%) and can cause perPHPT by missing a solitary adenoma (9). Supernumerary glands, although rare, can also contribute to perPHPT, especially in secondary or hereditary hyperparathyroidism. In a meta-analysis, it was found that 4.9% of healthy individuals and 6.3% of patients with hyperparathyroidism had five or more parathyroid glands (9). We advocate the use of intraoperative aids since iOPTH could have prevented two of our failures. In the presented series, the sensitivity of iOPTH as an indicator of operative success was 88,5%. This compares with a 92% sensitivity reported by Westerdahl in a study of 269 PTx followed up for an average of 3.6 years (10). Other benefits of iOPTH include cost benefits, avoidance of extensive dissection, and the correct and early identification of MGD (11).

2/ Incomplete resection of MGD is a significant cause of perPHPT, especially in high-volume centers (8). In a recent study of 3,779 subjects (8), MGD was identified in up to 15% of patients with PHPT. Among those with MGD, 12% had double adenomas, while the remainder presented with four-gland hyperplasia. In our study, we observed a rate of 3,6% for DA and 4,8% for HP, which falls within the reported range of rates (2,12). Inadequate resection in patients with DA often leads to perPHPT. This is typically due to prematurely ending the surgical exploration, as the condition is initially assessed as a SA during the preoperative phase. Because there is also a risk of recurrent disease in DA, some authors suggest that DA might indicate asymmetric or asynchronous HP (13).

Differentiating between DA and asymmetric HP is crucial for patient management. Patients with genuine DA can be effectively cured by removing both abnormal glands, whereas those with asymmetric HP remain at elevated risk of persistence or recurrence. Several recent studies have focused on this subject; however, data is limited and current criteria for the diagnosis of DA are insufficient to separate true DA from asymmetric HP (12). Overall, MGD is linked to a higher risk of intraoperative failure, persistent hyperparathyroidism, and recurrent disease (2).

3/ Additionally, parathyroid cancer, although rare, can lead to perPHPT if not completely resected, with persistent disease suggesting residual or metastatic involvement (14). The gold standard for PC is “en bloc” resection, which involves removing the parathyroid lesion along with surrounding fat tissue, the ipsilateral thyroid lobe, and possibly the recurrent laryngeal nerve and ipsilateral central neck compartment lymph nodes (15). Studies show this technique yields better outcomes than local excision by reducing R1 margins and the risk of locoregional recurrence or residual disease (16).

The failure rate in the current observational study is 6% which is similar to other published results (17), but we did not find any predictive factors apart from the established iOPTH. In a multivariate analysis of 2,239 patients treated for PHPT, independent risk factors for perPHPT were identified. These included age >70 years, low volume centers, failure to locate enlarged glands on US or MIBI, higher preoperative calcium levels, lower preoperative parathyroid hormone levels, failure to meet iOPTH criteria, and lower weight of excised glands (18). These risk factors ultimately align with the predictive MGD factors (19,20), which remains the most critical factor in perPHPT and can further guide surgical decision-making in parathyroidectomy. Normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism was also found to be associated with a higher incidence of perPHPT due to a higher incidence of MGD (21). - Imaging is crucial for exploring PHPT, and all patients should undergo preoperative combined imaging with high-definition ultrasound and Technetium (99m Tc) sestamibi scintigraphy (17,22). This approach aims to minimize the risk of missing adenomas, especially in cases of MGD or ectopic adenomas. While lateralized and concordant imaging has shown high accuracy (95- 100%) for single adenomas (23), the sensitivity for MGD is significantly lower, with rates dropping to less than 50% (24). Negative preoperative studies are strong indicators of MGD (25), and neglecting standard imaging may lead to missed diagnoses, as demonstrated in one patient who underwent surgery based on a CT scan without prior imaging. - Achievement of iOPTH criteria is also a known predictor of operative success, though the final target iOPTH level is not universally agreed upon. Existing research suggests that a drop of 50% is insufficient to demonstrate intraoperative cure in both DA and HP. In their study of iOPTH monitoring which included 271 patients with MGD, Richards et al. (26) demonstrated that using only the criterion of a 50% decrease in iOPTH would lead to perPHPT in more than 20% of patients with MGD. Similar results were found for DA by Goodsell et al. (12). This raises the question of adopting stricter iOPTH criteria when MGD is suspected, such as the Rome or Halle protocols, which require iOPTH normalization and reportedly enhance intraoperative sensitivity for detecting MGD (27,28). In contrast, the more balanced Miami and Vienna criteria may be better suited for straightforward cases without MGD suspicion (27). Foley et al. (29) used ROC analysis to determine that a 72% decrease in iOPTH (using the Vienna criteria) was optimal for identifying MGD, though this threshold had low sensitivity and specificity. Ultimately, a unanimous threshold or criterion is lacking, and sound clinical judgment remains vital. While iOPTH is more sensitive than localization studies (28), further research is needed to investigate the kinetic variations in IOPTH between SA and MGD, and to establish more effective criteria for identifying MGD. Moreover, combining IOPTH with preoperative imaging has proven to be the most reliable method for predicting MGD (30). - In conjunction with these methods, researchers have developed preoperative scoring systems to predict MGD and guide surgical decisions. The CaPTHUS score (31) and Wisconsin index (32) are the most commonly used tools, combining biochemical markers and imaging results to assess the likelihood of MGD. Studies confirm these scores perform well, but thresholds do not fully rule out MGD (33). Additional scoring models, such as the 4D-CT MGD score (34), incorporate imaging data and biochemical markers, enhancing specificity. Emerging machine learning techniques, also known as artificial intelligence, have demonstrated significant potential (35), with models such as RandomTree achieving high accuracy and JRip optimizing predictive value (36). These advancements provide innovative approaches for identifying MGD when traditional methods yield inconclusive results.

After curative parathyroidectomy, normocalcemia is achieved in the majority of patients within 48–72 hours postoperatively. Approximately 10% of patients may experience delayed normalization of calcium levels, which typically resolves within 1–2 weeks (37). This early transient persistent hypercalcemia is not predictive of perPHPT and should not be mistaken for persistent disease. Evaluating these patients postoperatively requires time, especially for those with an adequate decline in PTH levels, and early reintervention should be avoided in these cases. Secondary surgery should be delayed until at least 3 months postoperatively, as the surgical field is significantly affected by inflammatory scarring from the initial surgery for several months (38). Surgical indications for perPHPT are similar to those for PHPT and are recommended when patients meet the established guidelines for surgery, provided there are no contraindications (39). However, deciding if and when to operate is complex, as it requires balancing a decreased cure rate with the increased risk of complications such as vocal cord paralysis, permanent hypoparathyroidism as well as tracheal and cervical esophageal injury (40). Parathyroid reoperations should involve a multidisciplinary team discussion and should be conducted by experienced surgeons (those who perform at least 50 parathyroidectomies per year are considered by most endocrine surgeons to be experienced (39)). A targeted operation is also imperative for surgical success in re-operative surgery; thus, having specific imaged glands is essential. First-line imaging modalities include ultrasound and scintigraphy, followed by advanced techniques such as four-dimensional (4-D) CT, dynamic 4D-MRI, and positron emission tomography (PET) or PET/CT with 18F-fluorocholine (7,40). These imaging techniques are crucial for accurately locating the parathyroid glands and planning the surgical approach. This study was limited by its small sample size and its retrospective nature, which inherently comes with associated challenges. Some patient data were missing due to unavailable medical records. The use of preoperative imaging and intraoperative tools was variable. Given the retrospective design and limited access to patient records, distinguishing between SA and MGD relied on reviewers’ judgment based on clinical and histopathological records.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study highlights the essential role of multiglandular disease as a major contributor to persistent primary hyperparathyroidism and stresses the importance of identifying risk factors associated with MGD. Both imaging and iOPTH measurements are crucial in this context, though iOPTH offers greater sensitivity, and the optimal final value of iOPTH remains a topic of discussion. Ultimately, combining these methods, particularly using scoring systems, can aid in minimizing therapeutic failures.
