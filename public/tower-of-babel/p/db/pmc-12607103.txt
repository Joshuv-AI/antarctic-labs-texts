Joint associations of handgrip strength and functional disability with cognitive impairment in older adults
Authors: Yuejun Li, Xueming Han, Sirui He, Xingying Chen, Lan Liu, Peiyi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHandgrip strength (HGS) and functional disability may reflect interconnected biological processes contributing to functional decline. However, the extent to which their co-occurrence predicts cognitive impairment in aging populations remains unclear. This study investigated the joint association of low HGS and functional disability with cognitive impairment in a nationally representative cohort of older Chinese adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2020), including participants aged ≥ 60 years. Based on baseline HGS and functional disability status, participants were categorized into four groups. Logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine cross-sectional and longitudinal associations with cognitive impairment, respectively. Cognitive trajectories were assessed using linear mixed-effects models, and subgroup analyses evaluated the consistency of associations across demographic and clinical strata.ResultsA total of 2423 participants (mean age 65.4 ± 4.7 years; 44.1% female) were included. In cross-sectional analyses, compared with participants without low HGS or functional disability, the adjusted odds ratios (OR) for cognitive impairment were 1.62 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.23–2.13; P < 0.001) for functional disability alone, 1.81 (95% CI 1.35–2.41; P < 0.001) for low HGS alone, and 2.81 (95% CI 2.07–3.83; P < 0.001) for both conditions. Over nine years of follow-up, the corresponding adjusted hazard ratios (HR) for incident cognitive impairment were 1.52 (95% CI 1.16–1.99), 1.63 (95% CI 1.21–2.20), and 1.73 (95% CI 1.16–2.59), respectively. Linear mixed-effects models indicated significant differences in cognitive trajectories among groups, with participants having both conditions consistently maintaining the lowest standardized cognitive scores. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent associations across stratified analyses.ConclusionThe joint presence of low HGS and functional disability was strongly associated with an elevated risk of cognitive impairment. Incorporating combined assessments of muscle strength and functional status into routine geriatric evaluations may enable earlier risk stratification and inform comprehensive interventions to preserve cognitive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03353-1.Keywords: Handgrip strength, Functional disability, Cognitive impairment, Older, CHARLS

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is an escalating public health concern among older adults, characterized by progressive declines in memory, attention, and executive function. These deficits substantially diminish quality of life and increase the risk of dementia and long-term care dependency [1–3]. With the rapid aging of populations, particularly in countries such as China, the prevalence of cognitive impairment is projected to rise steadily. According to World Health Organization (WHO) estimates, individuals aged 60 years or older will comprise 35.1% of China’s total population by 2050. Given the insidious onset and poor prognosis of cognitive impairment, there is an urgent need to identify accessible and objective risk indicators that enable early detection and timely intervention.

Handgrip strength (HGS) is a widely recognized indicator of muscle strength and physiological reserve, reflecting nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and neurological integrity. Multiple studies have demonstrated that low HGS is significantly associated with cognitive impairment, dementia, and increased mortality risk [4–7]. Potential underlying mechanisms include impaired neuromuscular activation, chronic inflammation, and neurodegenerative structural changes in the brain [8–10]. Owing to its practicality and non-invasive nature, researchers increasingly regard HGS as a valuable tool for identifying older adults at elevated risk of cognitive impairment. Functional disability is another common manifestation of aging, typically assessed through activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL). Existing evidence suggests that functional disability often precedes the clinical onset of cognitive impairment and may accelerate cognitive decline by reducing physical activity, increasing depressive symptoms, and limiting social engagement [11–13]. Functional disability frequently coexists with chronic conditions, underscoring its importance in the early identification of individuals at risk for cognitive impairment.

Emerging evidence indicates a strong interrelationship between low HGS and functional disability [14–16]. HGS reflects muscular strength and physiological reserve, and functional disability reflects limitations in daily living shaped by multiple factors. They have independent prognostic value, but also overlap through shared pathways such as inflammation, cerebrovascular disease, and frailty. Joint assessment of HGS and functional disability could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of an individual’s functional reserve and improve the sensitivity of screening strategies for high-risk populations. In line with this perspective, the WHO has proposed the concept of intrinsic capacity, defined as the composite of all the physical and mental capacities of an individual, emphasizing the integrated assessment of multiple functional domains—including cognition, locomotion, and vitality. Both HGS and functional disability are central components of this construct and are essential for the development of health promotion strategies and public health policies targeting aging populations [17, 18]. Nevertheless, most existing studies have examined HGS and functional disability in isolation in relation to cognitive impairment, and research addressing their combined impact is limited.

Therefore, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we investigated the association between the combined presence of low HGS and functional disability and the risk of cognitive impairment. Our findings aim to inform evidence-based strategies for early identification and functional assessment in cognitively vulnerable older adults.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative, prospective cohort targeting individuals aged ≥ 45 years in China. Initiated in 2011, CHARLS employed a multistage, stratified probability sampling strategy to recruit participants from 150 counties or districts and 450 urban or rural communities nationwide. The survey collects extensive data on demographics, socioeconomic status, health conditions, and psychosocial factors. Follow-up interviews are conducted approximately every 2–3 years, with five waves completed through 2020 (Wave 1 in 2011, Wave 2 in 2013, Wave 3 in 2015, Wave 4 in 2018, and Wave 5 in 2020). Detailed descriptions of the CHARLS methodology have been published previously [19], and the data are publicly accessible at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en.

For the present analysis, we included data from the 2011 baseline wave and all subsequent follow-up waves through 2020. The original cohort comprised 17,708 participants. Exclusion criteria were: (1) age < 60 years at baseline; (2) missing baseline data on HGS; (3) missing baseline data on functional disability as assessed by ADL and IADL; or (4) missing baseline data on cognitive function. For the longitudinal analysis, we additionally excluded participants with cognitive impairment at baseline or without follow-up cognitive data. After applying these criteria, the final analytic sample consisted of 2423 participants (Fig. 1).

The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Assessment of HGS and functional disability

HGS was measured by trained health workers using a mechanical dynamometer (Yuejian WL-1000, Nantong, China). Participants were instructed to stand upright, or remain seated if unable to stand, with the elbow flexed at 90° while holding the device. Each hand was tested twice, and the highest value from the four trials was recorded [20]. Consistent with the 2019 consensus of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), low HGS was defined as < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women [21].

Functional disability was assessed based on self-reported ability to perform six ADLs, including dressing, bathing, eating, getting out of bed, using the toilet, and maintaining continence [22]. In addition, five IADLs were evaluated, which comprised performing housework, cooking, shopping, taking medication, and managing finances [23]. Each activity was rated using four response categories: no difficulty, some difficulty but able to perform independently, difficulty requiring assistance, and complete inability to perform the task. For analysis, a score of 0 was assigned to responses indicating no difficulty, and a score of 1 was assigned when any level of difficulty or inability was reported. ADL and IADL scores were calculated separately, and functional disability was defined as a score of 1 in either domain [24].

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was evaluated using a validated test battery comprising episodic memory, the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS), and a figure-drawing task, consistent with the methodology employed in previous CHARLS studies [25]. Episodic memory was assessed through both immediate and delayed recall of a 10-word list. For immediate recall, participants repeated the words directly after hearing them; delayed recall was assessed approximately 4 min later. The average of the two recall scores was calculated, producing an episodic memory score ranging from 0 to 10. Mental status was measured using the TICS, which included five orientation items (year, month, day, day of the week, and season) and five serial subtractions of seven from 100, with one point awarded for each correct response. Visuospatial ability was assessed by asking participants to replicate a figure of two overlapping pentagons, with one point awarded for an accurate reproduction. The total cognitive function score was calculated as the sum of the episodic memory score (0–10), TICS score (0–10), and figure-drawing score (0–1), resulting in a composite range of 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. Cognitive impairment was defined as a composite score at least 1.0 standard deviation (SD) below the mean cognitive score, consistent with prior CHARLS-based research [26–28].

Data collection and definitions

Baseline characteristics included a comprehensive set of demographic, lifestyle, medical history, anthropometric, and biochemical variables. Demographic data comprised age, gender, marital status, residence (urban or rural), and educational attainment. Lifestyle behaviors were categorized according to smoking status (never or former vs. current smokers) and drinking status (never or former vs. current drinkers). Medical history encompassed self-reported health status and the presence of chronic conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, stroke, and other comorbidities. Anthropometric measurements were obtained using standardized protocols. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2) and categorized as underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese. Laboratory assessments included total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c).

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into four groups according to their baseline HGS and functional disability status: Group 1 (neither functional disability nor low HGS), Group 2 (functional disability only), Group 3 (low HGS only), and Group 4 (both functional disability and low HGS). Baseline characteristics were summarized using appropriate descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test for categorical variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables.

For the cross-sectional analysis, logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations between HGS/functional disability status and cognitive impairment, with results reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For the longitudinal analysis covering 2011–2020, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI for incident cognitive impairment across the four groups, with Group 1 serving as the reference. The underlying time scale was defined as the time elapsed since study baseline in 2011. Incident cognitive impairment was defined as the first occurrence of cognitive impairment identified during follow-up, and participants who did not develop the outcome were censored at the date of their last available follow-up or in 2020, whichever came first. Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and gender; and Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, marital status, residence, education level, self-reported health status, smoking status, drinking status, chronic diseases, hypertension, dyslipidemia, FBG, HbA1c, and baseline cognitive function in 2011. The selection of covariates for multivariable models was informed by previous empirical findings, observed baseline imbalances, and their theoretical and clinical relevance as potential confounding variables [29–31]. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals for key exposure variables, and no significant violations were detected. Differences in cognitive trajectories across groups were examined using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes. Standardized cognitive z-scores were treated as repeated measures over time and modeled as a continuous variable. Group, time, and their interaction term were included to test whether rates of cognitive decline differed among groups. Cognitive test scores were standardized using the baseline mean and SD across the full sample. Random intercepts and slopes were specified at the individual level to account for baseline differences and within-person variation over time.

Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted by including stroke as an additional covariate in Model 3 to evaluate the robustness of the associations in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification by age (60–69 vs. ≥ 70 years), gender (male vs. female), BMI (< 24 vs. ≥ 24 kg/m2), education (no formal education vs. primary school or above), residence (urban vs. rural), and number of chronic diseases (0 vs. ≥ 1). All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 2,423 participants were included in the final analysis, with a mean age of 65.4 ± 4.7 years; 44.1% (n = 1,069) were female. Based on baseline HGS and functional disability status, participants were classified into four groups: Group 1 (neither condition; n = 1,433), Group 2 (functional disability only; n = 477), Group 3 (low HGS only; n = 347), and Group 4 (both conditions; n = 166). The majority resided in rural areas (61.6%) and were married (86.2%), while 42.2% reported no formal education. Over the 9-year follow-up period, the mean cognitive function score was 8.92 ± 4.15. Significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed across the four groups, including variations in age, gender distribution, BMI, residence, education level, self-reported health status, smoking and drinking behaviors, history of stroke, number of chronic conditions, and baseline cognitive scores (all P < 0.05). Associations between the joint effect of HGS and functional disability and incident cognitive impairment

In the cross-sectional analysis of 2011 data, the prevalence of cognitive impairment was 9.9% in Group 1, 21.2% in Group 2, 19.9% in Group 3, and 34.0% in Group 4. After adjusting for covariates in Model 3, the odds of cognitive impairment were significantly higher in Group 2 (OR: 1.62; 95% CI 1.23–2.13; P < 0.001), Group 3 (OR: 1.81; 95% CI 1.35–2.41; P < 0.001), and Group 4 (OR: 2.81; 95% CI 2.07–3.83; P < 0.001) compared with Group 1 (Table 2). During the 9-year follow-up, mean cognitive function scores declined from 12.42 ± 2.57 in 2011 to 8.92 ± 4.15 in 2020. The cumulative incidence of cognitive impairment was 27.6% in Group 1, 40.9% in Group 2, 40.1% in Group 3, and 46.4% in Group 4. Multivariable-adjusted Cox regression analyses demonstrated that, relative to Group 1, the hazard of incident cognitive impairment was significantly higher in Group 2 (HR: 1.52; 95% CI 1.16–1.99; P < 0.001), Group 3 (HR: 1.63; 95% CI 1.21–2.20; P < 0.001), and Group 4 (HR: 1.73; 95% CI 1.16–2.59; P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Cognitive trajectories across combined categories of HGS and functional disability

Cognitive trajectories differed significantly across the four groups. Linear mixed-effects models identified a significant interaction between time and group (P < 0.001), indicating variation in the rate of cognitive decline among groups. Participants with both low HGS and functional disability maintained the lowest standardized cognitive scores throughout follow-up, underscoring a cumulative adverse effect of these combined deficits.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, BMI, educational level, residence, and presence of chronic diseases yielded results consistent with the primary analyses. No significant interaction was observed between group status and any stratification variable (all P for interaction > 0.05). Across nearly all subgroups, participants in Group 4 consistently exhibited the highest risk of incident cognitive impairment, reinforcing the additive and stable impact of combined low HGS and functional disability over time (Fig. 3). The sensitivity analyses showed similar results after additional adjustment for stroke, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

The joint presence of low HGS and functional disability was significantly associated with an elevated risk of cognitive impairment. Compared with individuals without either condition, those with both demonstrated the most pronounced cognitive decline over time. These findings indicate an additive adverse effect of reduced muscle strength and compromised functional capacity on cognitive trajectories. The associations remained robust after adjustment for a broad spectrum of demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates, and were consistent across multiple subgroups. Collectively, these results emphasize the clinical importance of jointly assessing HGS and functional disability to identify older adults at increased risk for cognitive impairment.

With the rapid aging of the Chinese population, the prevalence of age-related conditions such as cognitive decline and physical impairment is rising sharply, posing substantial challenges to public health systems. As traditional family-based caregiving structures weaken, the societal burden of long-term care for individuals with both cognitive and physical impairments continues to grow [32–34]. Although previous research has demonstrated strong individual associations of both HGS and functional disability with cognitive impairment, most studies have evaluated these factors independently, and little is known about their combined influence on cognitive outcomes. Regarding HGS, numerous studies conducted primarily in Western populations have shown that lower baseline HGS is associated with accelerated declines in cognitive test performance and a higher subsequent risk of dementia [6, 7]. These associations have been consistent across diverse demographic and comorbidity profiles, suggesting that reduced muscle strength may serve as an early, sensitive marker of neurodegenerative processes preceding overt cognitive deficits [35, 36]. However, most available evidence is derived from cross-sectional studies or non-Asian cohorts, and longitudinal data specific to older Chinese adults remain limited [37, 38]. Few studies have assessed whether reduced muscle strength predicts cognitive outcomes similarly across diverse cultural contexts, such as among older Chinese adults, where variations in body composition, physical labor history, and caregiving structures may influence this association. Functional disability, typically assessed through limitations in ADL and IADL, has likewise been recognized as a precursor to cognitive deterioration. Such impairments frequently manifest during the prodromal stages of cognitive decline, often before clinical diagnosis [12, 13]. Longitudinal studies have shown that functional disability not only predicts faster cognitive decline, but also substantially increases the risk of developing dementia [11]. These findings reinforce the value of incorporating physical function assessments into early risk stratification for cognitive impairment. Moreover, disability-related limitations in daily living may reduce opportunities for social participation, physical activity, and cognitive stimulation, thus accelerating neurocognitive aging via behavioral and psychosocial pathways.

Notably, low HGS and functional disability frequently coexist in older populations. Their co-occurrence is increasingly understood to reflect both independent contributions to functional decline and areas of biological overlap, including age-related neuromuscular change, chronic inflammation, and reduced physiological reserve [15, 16, 39]. Despite these links, the joint effect of low HGS and functional disability on cognitive outcomes has received limited investigation. This gap is especially important in public health practice, where screening tools must be both simple and sensitive. Understanding whether the combined use of HGS and disability indices enhances risk prediction can inform cost-effective and scalable screening strategies. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical for developing a comprehensive understanding of multidimensional functional decline and its impact on cognitive health, and for informing integrated, preventive strategies for early detection and intervention.

Leveraging a nationally representative cohort of older Chinese adults, we comprehensively examined both the independent and combined effects of low HGS and functional disability on the risk of cognitive impairment. Each condition was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment in both cross-sectional analyses and longitudinal follow-up over nine years, with the highest risk observed when the two coexisted. Importantly, the associations remained robust and directionally consistent across multiple subgroups stratified by age, gender, education, BMI, residence, and presence of chronic diseases, further supporting the generalizability and applicability of these findings in diverse older populations. Trajectory analyses further demonstrated that participants with both low HGS and functional disability consistently had the lowest cognitive scores throughout follow-up, indicating a persistent cumulative adverse effect on cognitive function. In contrast to prior investigations that primarily focused on a single functional parameter or shorter follow-up periods, the present study is, to our knowledge, the first to evaluate the joint influence of HGS and functional disability on cognitive outcomes in a large, nationally representative Chinese cohort. These findings underscore the value of multidimensional functional assessment in accurately identifying older adults at high risk and in guiding the development of integrated intervention strategies.

Several plausible physiological and neurological mechanisms may underlie the observed associations. First, oxidative stress and chronic systemic inflammation are recognized as shared pathological pathways contributing to both muscle strength decline and cognitive deterioration [40–42]. Sustained inflammation and oxidative damage can impair neuronal integrity and promote cerebrovascular degeneration, thereby disrupting brain regions critical for motor control and cognition [43]. Second, functional disability often restricts engagement in physical and social activities, reducing physical reserve and diminishing stimulation of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, thereby accelerating cognitive decline [44, 45]. Additionally, functional disability is frequently accompanied by suboptimal nutritional intake, heightened psychological stress, and reduced social interaction, all of which impair neuroplasticity and compromise brain health [46, 47]. Furthermore, low HGS and functional disability often coexist with unhealthy lifestyle patterns and multiple chronic conditions, including reduced physical activity, prolonged sedentary behavior, and mental health disturbances, which cumulatively exacerbate the adverse effects on cognitive function [48–51].

The findings of this study highlight the clinical relevance of incorporating multidimensional functional assessments into routine geriatric care. Over the 9-year follow-up period, we observed a mean cognitive score decline of 3.50 points. This decline exceeds the typical rate of age-related cognitive decline reported in older adults and suggests a sustained downward trajectory rather than random fluctuation, underscoring its potential clinical impact. Concurrent evaluation of HGS and daily functional abilities can facilitate earlier identification of older adults at heightened risk for cognitive decline, enabling timely, targeted preventive measures. Clinically, interventions that enhance muscle strength, preserve independence in daily activities, and provide comprehensive health management, including nutritional support and cognitive training, may help slow functional deterioration and support cognitive health. At the public health level, integrating these assessments into primary care and community health programs could provide a practical, scalable approach for early risk stratification and guide the development of evidence-based prevention strategies tailored to aging Asian populations.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was restricted to older adults in China, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic and regional populations. Moreover, regional heterogeneity within China could not be fully addressed due to limited geographic granularity in the available data. Future studies are needed to validate these results in different countries and more diverse cohorts. Second, certain variables were based on self-report, introducing the potential for recall bias. In addition, some participants were lost to follow-up during the study period, which may have introduced selection bias. Third, although analyses adjusted for a wide range of covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be entirely excluded. Moreover, we used baseline assessments of functional disability and HGS as the primary exposure, which may not fully capture their temporal changes over time. Finally, the observational nature of this study precludes establishing causal relationships, and further research is required to confirm the causal impact of the combined presence of low HGS and functional disability on cognitive outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, older adults with both low HGS and functional disability exhibited a markedly increased risk of cognitive impairment during follow-up, with the combination serving as a stronger predictor. These results support the integration of multidimensional functional assessments into routine practice for risk stratification and prevention. Large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to inform the development of targeted, multidomain strategies for early identification and prevention of cognitive impairment in aging populations.
