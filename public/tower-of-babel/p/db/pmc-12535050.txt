Association of BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight with psoriasis severity and treatment response: evidence from a real-world cohort
Authors: Yifei Xu, Chen Peng, Suwei Tang, Yizhen Zhang, Yingbao Zhen, Yue Yin, Xin Wang, Yangfeng Ding, Qian Yu, Rui Ma, Yuling Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough biologic and systemic therapies have advanced psoriasis management, real-world evidence guiding individualized treatment remains limited. In particular, the influence of body size and metabolic parameters on disease severity and treatment response is underexplored.ObjectiveTo investigate the associations of body mass index (BMI), basal metabolic rate (BMR), body surface area (BSA), and body weight with baseline psoriasis severity and therapeutic response across different treatment modalities.MethodsThis multicenter, prospective study included 1955 patients from the Shanghai Psoriasis Effectiveness Evaluation CoHort (SPEECH) and 1663 patients for longitudinal follow-up. Multivariable regression models were used to examine the associations between body size/metabolic parameters and the baseline psoriasis area and severity index (PASI) scores, as well as PASI-based treatment responses at Week 12 and Week 20. Stratified analyses by treatment type and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were conducted to assess predictive performance.ResultsAll four parameters were positively associated with baseline PASI scores (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05). Prospectively, elevated BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight were significantly associated with reduced likelihood of achieving PASI 75/90/100, and lower percentage reduction in PASI score at both time points. These associations were particularly pronounced in patients receiving biologic therapies. In the ustekinumab subgroup, body composition showed enhanced predictive accuracy for high-level PASI responses.ConclusionElevated BMI, BSA, body weight, and BMR are associated with more severe psoriasis and diminished treatment efficacy, especially those treated with biologics. These findings underscore the need for personalized dosing strategies in biologic therapy, especially for fixed-dose agents like ustekinumab.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07130-w.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease affecting approximately 125 million individuals worldwide, representing 2–3% of the global population [1, 2]. The condition imposes a significant physical and psychological burden and often necessitates long-term management [3–5]. Current therapeutic strategies include topical agents (e.g., corticosteroids, vitamin D analogs), systemic medications (e.g., methotrexate, acitretin, cyclosporine), biologic agents, and physical therapies such as narrow-band ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) phototherapy [4]. Biologic therapies, in particular, have revolutionized psoriasis management by selectively targeting pivotal cytokines in disease pathogenesis. The most widely used biologics are tumor necrosis factor- alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors, interleukin (IL)-12/23 inhibitors, and IL-17A inhibitors, which suppress pro-inflammatory signaling within the IL-23/Th17 axis to attenuate keratinocyte hyperproliferation and chronic inflammation [6]. Compared with conventional systemic drugs, biologics achieve higher rates of clinical response and durable remission in many patients. However, a considerable subset of patients fails to achieve optimal outcomes, disease relapse is common upon discontinuation, and the requirement for long-term injectable administration remains a challenge [7–9]. These limitations highlight the urgent need to optimize treatment selection through a more individualized approach.

Mounting evidence indicates that metabolic factors play an important role in psoriasis pathogenesis and therapeutic response [10–12]. Epidemiological and mechanistic studies have demonstrated that obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome are more prevalent among psoriasis patients [13–15]. Shared pathogenic pathways including chronic systemic inflammation, activation of the IL-17/23 axis, and dysregulated adipokine secretion are thought to underlie the close interplay between psoriasis and metabolic comorbidities [16, 17]. In addition, meta-analyses suggest that increased body mass index (BMI) is associated with reduced responsiveness to biologic therapies in psoriasis, suggesting that body composition may influence treatment efficacy [6, 18]. In oncology, metrics such as body weight and body surface area (BSA) are routinely used to guide chemotherapeutic dosing [19, 20], highlighting the potential relevance of these parameters in other therapeutic domains. However, their clinical utility in psoriasis management remains underexplored.

To date, there is a paucity of large-scale, prospective, multicenter, real-world studies that systematically examine the impact of body size and metabolic parameters, including BMI, basal metabolic rate (BMR), BSA, and body weight, on psoriasis severity and treatment response. To address this gap, we utilized data from a large-scale real-world cohort in China to assess the associations of these parameters with baseline disease severity and therapeutic outcomes across different treatment modalities. We also evaluated the predictive performance of these metrics in patients receiving biologics, particularly ustekinumab, to inform the development of personalized treatment strategies.

Methods

Study design

The Shanghai Psoriasis Effectiveness Evaluation CoHort (SPEECH) is a prospective, multicenter observational study launched in November 2022 [12, 21, 22]. The cohort included patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis recruited from seven sites in Shanghai, including the Shanghai Skin Disease Hospital, Huashan Hospital of Fudan University, Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University, Navy Military Medical University Affiliated Changhai Hospital, Ruijin Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and Tenth People’s Hospital of Tongji University [12, 21, 22]. By May 2025, 2592 patients had been enrolled. The study has been registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000036186).

Participants

This study analyzed data from the SPEECH cohort. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of chronic moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis [12, 21, 22]. In line with the study protocol, patients were excluded if they had received phototherapy or conventional systemic medications (e.g., acitretin, methotrexate, cyclosporine) within the preceding month, or biologic agents within the last three months prior to enrollment [12, 21, 22].

After enrollment, patients initiated systemic treatments according to standard clinical practice, including phototherapy, conventional systemic medications (acitretin or methotrexate), or biologic agents (adalimumab, guselkumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, or ustekinumab). Biologics were administered at approved standard doses, following established protocols: adalimumab: 40 mg subcutaneous injection at week 0, followed by 40 mg every other week starting one week after the initial dose; guselkumab: 100 mg subcutaneous injection at weeks 0 and 4, then every 8 weeks thereafter; secukinumab: 150 or 300 mg subcutaneous injection for the first 5 weeks (weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4), followed by 150 or 300 mg every 4 weeks; ixekizumab: 160 mg subcutaneous injection at week 0 (two 80 mg injections), followed by 80 mg at weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12, and then 80 mg every 4 weeks; ustekinumab: 45 mg subcutaneous injection at weeks 0 and 4, followed by 45 mg every 12 weeks.

Patients with missing baseline characteristics, the psoriasis area and severity index (PASI) scores, or treatment group information were excluded from the analysis. Treatment modalities and efficacy outcomes

Treatment response was assessed using the PASI [26]. The primary treatment efficacy was defined as achieving PASI 75 (≥ 75% reduction in PASI from baseline) at Week 12. Secondary outcomes included PASI 90, PASI 100, and percentage reduction in PASI score at Week 12 and 20.

Statistical analysis

The cross-sectional analysis aimed to examine the associations between baseline body size/metabolic parameters, including BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight, and psoriasis severity, as measured by the PASI score. Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Student’s t-test, Kruskal–Wallis test, Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To evaluate the independent associations between body size/metabolic parameters and PASI score at baseline, multivariable generalized linear regression models (GLMs) were constructed with PASI score as the dependent variable and each body size/metabolic parameter as the independent variable. All models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, disease duration, psoriasis treatment history and comorbidities (hypertension and type 2 diabetes (T2D)) [27, 28].

The longitudinal analysis was designed to assess the association between baseline body size/metabolic parameters and therapeutic response to systemic treatment at Week 12 and Week 20. Treatment efficacy was evaluated using both binary outcomes including achievement of PASI 75, PASI 90, and PASI 100, and continuous outcomes including percentage reduction in PASI score from baseline. The primary analysis was conducted in the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, defined as patients with available baseline data and at least one post-treatment efficacy assessment. Missing follow-up data were imputed using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method [29]. LOCF was applied method in accordance with the original temporal sequence of the data and is widely applied in real-world prospective clinical studies to supplement partially missing data in a manner that closely reflects real clinical practice, thereby reducing imputation bias [29]. Given the clinical context, LOCF was considered an appropriate approach for imputing missing PASI scores in this study [12]. We acknowledge, however, that LOCF assumes stability of disease trajectory beyond the last observation and may underestimate variability, which represents a potential limitation. A per-protocol (PP) analysis was also conducted in patients with complete outcome data at both Week 12 and Week 20 to assess the robustness of the findings. Sensitivity analyses were performed to further evaluate the imputed dataset, ensuring the reliability of the results.

Multivariable generalized logistical models and GLMs were applied to estimate the association between baseline body size/metabolic parameters and treatment outcomes. Logistic regression models were used for binary endpoints, and linear regression models were used for continuous endpoints. All models were adjusted for baseline PASI score, treatment type, age, sex, education level, smoking history, alcohol use, disease duration, and comorbid conditions [27].

Stratified analyses were further conducted by treatment modality (conventional systemic therapies, NB-UVB phototherapy, and biologic agents) to evaluate potential effect modification. To formally test whether the effects of body size and metabolic parameters on treatment outcomes differed significantly among treatment modalities, interaction analysis was included in the models. To assess the discriminatory capacity of body size/metabolic parameters in predicting therapeutic response, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each parameter at both Week 12 and Week 20.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To account for multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method where appropriate. The FDR correction approach is widely applied in analyses involving multiple correlated tests, as it controls the expected proportion of false positives while maintaining greater statistical power than more conservative procedures such as Bonferroni correction [30, 31].

When stratified into low, medium, and high groups according to each parameter, distinct demographic and clinical patterns emerged. Patients in the higher groups of BMR, BSA, and body weight were generally younger, more often male, and more likely to have higher educational attainment, as well as to report smoking and alcohol consumption (all P < 0.001). These patterns suggest that lifestyle and sociodemographic factors may contribute to both metabolic status and disease burden. Clinical characteristics also varied across strata: individuals in higher tertiles of body size showed longer disease duration and a greater prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, including hypertension and T2D. These observations are consistent with the established evidence linking obesity, metabolic dysfunction, and psoriasis [13–15], and they underscore the importance of incorporating metabolic assessment into the baseline management of patients with psoriasis.

Cross-sectional association with psoriasis severity

In multivariable linear regression analyses adjusted for demographic and clinical confounders, all four body size and metabolic parameters were positively associated with baseline PASI scores. Specifically, higher BMI (β = 0.17, 95% CI 0.08, 0.26), BMR (β = 0.01, 95% CI 0.00, 0.01), BSA (β = 2.77, 95% CI 0.65, 4.90), and body weight (β = 0.05, 95% CI 0.02, 0.07) were each significantly associated with greater psoriasis severity (all FDR-adjusted P < 0.05; Table 2). Longitudinal association with treatment response

We evaluated PASI75, PASI90, and PASI100 achievement rates at Week 4, 8, 12, and 20. Across all four stratification parameters (BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight), patients in the higher-level groups consistently exhibited lower probabilities of achieving PASI targets. These differences were apparent for PASI75, PASI90, and PASI100, and became more pronounced with longer follow-up (Figure S1-S3).

At both Week 12 and Week 20, higher values of BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight were consistently associated with reduced treatment efficacy. In multivariable-adjusted analyses, each one-unit increase in BMI was associated with significantly lower odds of achieving PASI 75 (Week 12: OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.93, 0.98; Week 20: OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.93, 0.98), PASI 90 (Week 12: OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.90, 0.96; Week 20: OR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.91, 0.97), and PASI 100 (Week 12: OR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.88, 0.95; Week 20: OR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.90, 0.97). Discussion

In this large, real-world cohort of patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis, we found that elevated body size and metabolic parameters, including BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight, were significantly associated with both greater baseline disease severity and reduced therapeutic response. These associations were especially pronounced in patients treated with biologic agents, and the predictive value of these parameters was strongest in those receiving ustekinumab. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating individual variations in body composition into treatment planning for psoriasis [32].

Our findings provide important insights into the interplay between body size, metabolic parameters, and psoriasis. The observation that higher values of BMI, BSA, BMR, and body weight were more common in individuals with higher educational attainment, males, and those reporting smoking or alcohol use may reflect lifestyle- and nutrition-related influences on metabolic status. In addition, the associations of hypertension and diabetes-two well-recognized metabolic comorbidities of psoriasis may be partly explained by obesity-related chronic inflammation [10, 11, 16, 17]. This underscores the potential clinical relevance of using the four body size and metabolic parameters investigated in our study as key indicators for metabolic assessment in patients with psoriasis.

Previous studies have suggested that obesity and metabolic dysregulation exacerbate psoriasis through systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation [33, 34]. Consistent with this hypothesis, our cross-sectional analysis showed that all four anthropometric and metabolic indicators were positively associated with baseline PASI scores, even after adjusting for potential confounders. This aligns with existing literature demonstrating that metabolic comorbidities such as insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and adiposity-related cytokine release (e.g. IL-6, TNF-α) can worsen psoriatic disease activity [15, 35–38].

Our prospective analysis extends these findings by demonstrating that elevated BMI, BMR, BSA, and body weight were independently associated with lower probabilities of achieving PASI 75/90/100 responses and with smaller improvements in PASI score at both Week 12 and Week 20. These relationships remained robust after multiple adjustments and sensitivity testing. Notably, results from non-imputed data were consistent with those of the main imputed dataset, reinforcing that the observed associations were not driven by the imputation procedure. This suggests that body composition plays a consistent and clinically meaningful role in modulating treatment efficacy. Importantly, the strength of these associations was most evident in patients receiving biologic therapies, especially ustekinumab. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms may explain the reduced efficacy of biologics in patients with larger body size. Most biologics for psoriasis are administered at fixed doses, meaning that patients with higher body mass receive proportionally lower drug exposure per kilogram of body weight [39, 40]. Additionally, in the context of skin-targeted inflammation, increased total body surface area may reduce the concentration of drug available per unit area of psoriatic lesion, thereby attenuating clinical response. These effects may be particularly relevant for biologics such as ustekinumab, whose mechanism involves systemic IL-12/23 inhibition and where tissue penetration may vary with metabolic distribution [41].

Obesity is closely linked to metabolic disorders such as hyperlipidemia, metabolic syndrome, and insulin resistance, all of which are known to compromise treatment responses in psoriasis. The chronic low-grade inflammation associated with these conditions may drive the overactivation of key pathways central to psoriasis pathogenesis [42, 43]. For example, activation of NF-κB has been associated with insulin resistance, elevated insulin and C-peptide levels, and altered androgen metabolism [44, 45]. Dysregulated mTOR signaling may also contribute to aberrant keratinocyte proliferation and immune cell activation, further linking metabolic dysfunction to psoriatic inflammation [46]. In addition, enhanced activation of Th17 and Th1 cells promotes secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-22, IL-17, IFN-γ, and IL-2/4/6/10 [47]. These cytokines exacerbate psoriasis by engaging the JAK-STAT and NF-κB pathways, thereby suppressing apoptosis and promoting angiogenesis [48, 49]. Thus, beyond the potential influence of drug concentration per kilogram of body weight, excess adiposity may alter the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of biologics, leading to lower circulating drug levels and reduced therapeutic efficacy. This highlights the importance of incorporating body size and metabolic parameters into treatment decision-making. Although the present study cannot fully clarify these mechanisms, our findings underscore a clinically relevant pattern. Routine screening of metabolic indicators in psoriasis patients may not only help predict treatment outcomes but also provide valuable insights into the mechanistic links between metabolic status and prognosis. Moreover, these insights may inform future treatment guidelines and promote the development of individualized therapeutic strategies, such as weight-adjusted dosing regimens or combined metabolic interventions.

Although ustekinumab is the only biologic in our study with an approved weight-based dosing adjustment (double dosing for patients > 100 kg) [50], our results suggest that this binary cutoff may not adequately capture the full spectrum of body size variation. In our cohort, even after excluding the few patients who received the higher dose, elevated body size and metabolic parameters continued to negatively influence treatment response in the standard-dose group. These findings support a more granular, continuous model of dose optimization, rather than simple categorical thresholds. The implications of these findings are twofold. First, clinicians should consider body size metrics in treatment selection and monitoring, especially when prescribing biologics. Second, these results provide a rationale for individualized dosing strategies, which may improve therapeutic outcomes, reduce drug wastage, and enhance cost-effectiveness, particularly for agents with narrow therapeutic windows or nonlinear pharmacokinetics.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size, multicenter real-world design, and comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a proportion of patients were lost to follow-up, although consistent results in per-protocol analyses mitigate this concern. Second, the sample size for individual biologic subgroups, such as ustekinumab, was relatively limited, warranting cautious interpretation and external validation in larger, biologic-specific cohorts. Third, the limited sample size within individual biologic treatment subgroups restricted the robustness of our analysis. Given the heterogeneity of psoriasis phenotypes and the influence of residual or unmeasured confounding factors, the ROC analyses yielded relatively modest AUC values, which should be interpreted as indicative of potential trends rather than definitive evidence. Future studies with larger sample sizes and validation in independent cohorts will be required to confirm the reliability and generalizability of these findings. Additionally, the modest discriminative power observed may also reflect the multifactorial nature of psoriasis, in which clinical response is shaped not only by body size and metabolic parameters but also by genetic, immunologic, and environmental determinants that were not captured in the present study. Fourth, our use of LOCF to handle missing data may underestimate variability, although sensitivity analyses suggested that this did not materially affect our findings. Lastly, pharmacokinetic data were not available, precluding direct assessment of drug exposure differences by body size.

In summary, this study provides robust real-world evidence that elevated BMI, BSA, body weight, and BMR are associated with both increased baseline psoriasis severity and diminished treatment response, particularly in patients receiving biologic therapies. Among various biologic agents, body size and metabolic parameters demonstrated the highest predictive value for clinical efficacy in ustekinumab-treated patients, suggesting a potential body size-dependent therapeutic effect. These findings highlight the limitations of fixed-dose regimens and support the integration of individualized body composition metrics into treatment planning. Future research incorporating pharmacokinetic modeling and dose–response optimization is warranted to inform precision dosing strategies and improve long-term outcomes in patients with psoriasis. Similar approaches are already underway in engineered T-cell therapies, where patient-specific variables and target expression guide product development and response prediction [51, 52].
