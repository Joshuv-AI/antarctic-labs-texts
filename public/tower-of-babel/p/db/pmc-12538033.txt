Late toxicities of head and neck radiotherapy
Authors: Edward Christopher Dee, Yingzhi Wu, Annu Singh, Irini Yacoub, Cherry L Estilo, Nancy Y Lee
Published: 2025

The head and neck service at Princess Margaret Cancer Center should be commended for their excellent study of radiation late toxicities (RLTs).1 In a cohort of 7622 patients, Mathew and colleagues identify modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors associated with RLTs. RLTs were found to be associated with decreased survival, but this association was mitigated in a cohort referred to a specialist RLT clinic.

First, the association between RLTs and decreased survival underscores the need to evaluate strategies to prevent RLTs while maintaining high rates of cancer control; in the early 2000s, the adoption of intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) represents one such advance.2 In the 2020s, recent prospective evidence in oropharynx cancer (OPC) suggests a survival benefit of proton therapy over IMRT,3 in addition to lower rates of toxicities including gastrostomy tube dependence. While RLTs are not fully reported in the published abstract at the time of writing, the mechanisms through which this survival association is mediated merits further exploration; the work of Mathew and colleagues suggests that differential rates of toxicities among modalities may play a role. Differences in salvage options may contribute as well.

Second, we note that in the PMH cohort, the most common RLT was osteoradionecrosis (ORN; 34% of observed RLTs), highlighting the need to improve ORN mitigation. Our own institutional series suggests that ORN rates may be similar between protons and photons in oral cavity cancer,4 and in some settings like oropharynx cancer, may be higher for patients treated with protons.5 Further work should explore the radiobiology of ORN; for instance, the possibility of heterogeneity in relative biological effectiveness (RBE) corrections for soft tissue and bone (particularly at the tissue-bone interface of the mandible) should be studied further. In addition, dose reduction strategies for radiosensitive tumors such as human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated OPC merit further examination.

Additionally, we advocate for close dental oncology follow-up before and after radiotherapy for patients with cancers of the head and neck, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration,6 in line with the findings of Mathew and colleagues. The prevention and management of ORN depend heavily on pre-radiation dental evaluation and necessary dental treatment, optimization of oral health, and dental follow-up after radiation therapy.6 Furthermore, global disparities in access to advanced modalities mean that many patients may continue to receive treatments with higher toxicity rates. Dental oncology provides a broadly applicable and cost-effective strategy to mitigate oral complications, preserve oral health and function, and support quality of life.6 In addition, dental oncologists play a vital role in the management of trismus, xerostomia, and radiation-associated dental caries, which affect quality of life for long-term survivors.

Lastly, the findings of Mathew and colleagues supporting multidisciplinary RLT clinics in the mitigation of the survival decrement associated with RLTs is a promising development. Protons and even IMRT may not be universally available, cognizant of global disparities in access to radiotherapy care. Specialist survivorship clinics for patients with head and neck cancers may represent a cost-effective, patient-centric approach to mitigating downstream effects of late toxicities of radiotherapy. As more patients live longer after treatment for head and neck cancer, we must pair curative gains with scalable, multidisciplinary strategies that anticipate, prevent, and treat late toxicities to preserve function and quality of life.
