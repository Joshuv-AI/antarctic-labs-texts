Factors affecting timeliness in vaccination of under-five children in India: A cross-sectional study using the NFHS 5 survey during COVID-19
Authors: Divya Bharathi Gattam, Mohammed Shoyaib Khazi, Nitu Kumari, Afraz Jahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmunization is an essential intervention that protects millions of children against vaccine-preventable diseases across the world. Although vaccination coverage has improved over the years, ensuring timely administration continues to be a major challenge affecting child health in India. The COVID-19 pandemic further aggravated this situation. A cross-sectional study using the Kids Recode dataset of the National Family Health Survey 5 was conducted, which inlcuded 232920 children under 5 y of age. Timeliness was considered as 4 d earlier and up to 28 d after the standard date in comparison with the National Immunization Schedule. Regression analysis was used on Survey Data to assess the impact of factors related to children, mothers, households, communities, and the COVID-19 pandemic on timeliness by deriving adjusted Prevalence Ratios. Timeliness at birth was highest (94.6%), however, it declined for subsequent doses at 6 weeks (74.2%), and 14 weeks (67.5%). Untimeliness at 14 weeks was mainly due to Polio 3 (60.2%) and Rotavirus 3 (58.0%) vaccines. Factors associated with untimeliness included – low birth weight, fewer antenatal visits, non-institutional delivery, no maternal education, rural residency and belonging to lower socioeconomic strata. Children residing in the Central and Northeast zones had the highest prevalence of untimeliness in receiving vaccines. Despite the COVID-19 pandemic disruptions, the timeliness of vaccination was comparable between the pre- and post-pandemic periods. This study shows that the untimeliness of vaccination is influenced by factors related to the child, mother, household and community. Comprehensive interventions involving various stakeholders are required to improve the timeliness of vaccination.

Introduction

Immunization is one of the most valuable health expenditures money can buy, essential to primary healthcare, and an unquestionable human right. Furthermore, they underpin the security of the world’s health and are an essential weapon in the fight against antibiotic resistance.1 Therefore, timely vaccination is essential to achieve the full potential of vaccination, especially in early childhood and to prevent vaccine- preventable diseases (VPD).

Worldwide immunization has resulted in significant declines and regional elimination of many serious childhood infectious diseases.2 The WHO estimates that globally, 2–3 million deaths could be prevented through immunization.3 Nonetheless, the WHO also estimates that vaccine-preventable deaths are still responsible for 1.5 million deaths annually.4 Pneumonia and diarrheal disease rank among the top three leading causes of under-five mortality and are the leading cause of malnutrition in children.5 Apart from vaccine coverage, timely vaccination is a crucial indicator for evaluating the efficiency of the immunization program. Although India has been successful in increasing the coverage of the largest cohort of children in the world, the timeliness of vaccination remains a challenge.6 Children receiving delayed vaccinations remain susceptible to vaccine preventable diseases, compromising their own health and herd immunity.7 Timely vaccination also lessens the burden on healthcare systems. Governments and families can minimize the financial cost of treating illnesses that vaccinations are intended to prevent by preventing these diseases through timely vaccination.8

The Expanded Programme on Immunization was launched in 1978. It was renamed the Universal Immunization Programme (UIP) in 1985 when its reach was expanded beyond urban areas. Under UIP, immunization is provided free of cost against 12 vaccine preventable diseases – nationally against 9 diseases viz. Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Polio, Measles, Rubella, severe form of Childhood Tuberculosis, Hepatitis B, and Meningitis and Pneumonia caused by Hemophilus Influenza type B; sub-nationally against three diseases viz. Rotavirus diarrhea, Pneumococcal Pneumonia and Japanese Encephalitis (JE); of which Rotavirus and Pneumococcal Conjugate vaccines are in the process of expansion while the JE vaccine is provided only in endemic districts.9,10 Innovations such as alternate vaccine delivery, name-based tracking, Open Vial Policy, Electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network and Mission Indradhanush (MI) have been introduced to further strengthen and augment immunization service delivery. The Government of India, as part of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, Target 3.2, aims to end preventable deaths of newborns and children under 5 y of age by 2030, a very achievable target through robust and timely vaccination.

During the COVID 19 pandemic, routine immunization coverage plateaued as the entire health system suffered severe setbacks in 2020 and 2021, and the main focus shifted from routine immunization program to COVID 19 preventive vaccination strategies.11 Furthermore, the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) 5 coverage of the BCG vaccine, three doses of polio and Pentavalent or Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus (DPT) vaccines among children aged 12–23 months who had received it was 95.2%, 80.5% and 86.7%, respectively.

In addition, 87.9% of children had received one dose of the Measles-Containing Vaccine (MCV) by the time they turned two, and only 31.9% had received a second dose by the age of three. Rotavirus Vaccine (RVV) coverage was estimated at 36.4%.10,12–14

Literature reports increased delay in vaccination with the next schedule and becomes maximum for Pentavalent 3 vaccine. Place of residence, religion, education of mother, age and gender of the child were the most influential factors affecting the delay in administering all vaccines. Apart from the aforementioned factors, delivery place, preferred system of treatment, preferred care center for immunization and follow-up of health care providers were important factors that determined the delay in getting BCG vaccine.15

However, despite achieving the above coverage levels, it is essential to ensure that the above coverage estimates on immunization had occurred on time for several crucial reasons, such as optimal protection during vulnerable years, preventing disease outbreaks, avoiding serious complications in VPDs, reducing healthcare costs for government and families, supporting public health goals, building strong herd immunity, ensuring accurate medical records for future medical care, school admissions, and travel requirements. Therefore, this study was undertaken to support the immunization program and to identify and address the variables related to the timeliness of immunization coverage among Indian children aged less than 5 y.

Methodology

Study design

Cross-sectional study based on a complex sample survey of a nationally representative population from 2019 to 2021.

Setting

The NFHS 5 was conducted from 2019 to 2021, including participants from all states and union territories. A uniform, stratified, two-stage sampling design for NFHS 5 was adopted. Each district was stratified into rural and urban areas. Furthermore, in rural areas, villages served as Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) and were further sub-stratified by population size and proportion of Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe (SC/ST) households, with selection done using probability proportional to size (PPS) after sorting by female literacy. However, in urban areas, Census Enumeration Blocks (CEBs) were used as PSUs, sorted by SC/ST proportion, and selected using PPS. In total, 30,456 PSUs were selected across India, and fieldwork was completed in 30,198 PSUs. Prior to the survey, household mapping and listing were undertaken in each PSU. If a PSU had ≥300 households, it was segmented, and two segments of about 100–150 households each were selected. In the second stage, 22 households were systematically chosen from each PSU or segment for the interviews. Census from 2011 served as the sampling frame, and PSUs with fewer than 40 households were linked to the nearest PSU.

Data sources

The dataset of the NFHS 5th round, conducted between 2019 and 2021, was used. After obtaining authorization to use the datasets from the DHS Program (https://dhsprogram.com/), the Kids Recode (KR) dataset, which contains all the details about women and their children who were interviewed, was used.

The dataset had 724,115 entries. The inclusion criteria for analysis were to include only those children below the age of 5 y, for whom the date of vaccination was marked clearly in the immunization card available with the mother. The age at vaccination was computed based on the accuracy of the data using the following equation:

Computed Age at Vaccination = Computed Date of Vaccination – Computed Date of Birth

On computing the three dates mentioned above for each vaccine and dose, those with invalid dates leading to negative age were excluded from each copy of the dataset.

Hence, the final number of records included for each dose of each type of vaccine is shown in the flowchart in Figure No. 1.

Dependent variable

Timeliness

The standard age in days for each dose of any vaccine was obtained from the National Immunization Schedule (NIS). For vaccine doses given at birth, immunization up to 28 d from birth was considered “Vaccine given on time.” Furthermore, for subsequent doses of vaccines, timeliness was considered if the child received the vaccine during the age range of 4 d early or up to 28 d of the standard age specified for the vaccine dose as per the NIS. Timeliness of Vaccination for each type and dose of vaccine was used as the reference category. Furthermore, unadjusted and adjusted prevalence ratios were calculated among those children who did not receive the vaccine on time.

Independent variables

Child factors

Childbirth order, health checkup of the baby within 2 months of delivery and size at birth.

Mother factors

Current age of mother with a class interval of 5 y and mother’s education, marital status of mother and number of antenatal visits for the most recently born child and place of delivery.

Household Factors

Socioeconomic status (Wealth Index), distance from the health facility, sex of the head of the household and religion. Wealth Index in its final form is given as a variable in the dataset. Calculation of Wealth Index is given elsewhere.16 Distance from the health facility had three categories – Not a problem, Not a big problem and A big problem.

Community factors

Zone: (geographical zone based on the administrative divisions of India), type of residency and time of survey (before the COVID-19 pandemic or during the period when the lockdown was lifted or removed).

Exclusion criteria

In this study, we included only the children of respondents who had vaccination cards with clearly marked dates. A total of 5.07% of records available in the dataset were not considered during the analysis due to a lack of complete information on the dates for computing the age at vaccination. Considering the large and representative nature of the sample, no further adjustments were made in statistical analysis, as adjustments would introduce bias. Furthermore, the records that were excluded were due to a lack of good-quality data, hence improving internal validity.

Ethical considerations

We used secondary data for analysis from the KR dataset of the NFHS 5 survey provided by the Demographic Health Survey (DHS) which was de-identified and recoded to protect the privacy of the respondents. To comply with DHS rules, all authors separately obtained authorization to use the dataset.

Statistical analysis

The dataset was imported to STATA MP 4 Core v18, and declaration for the survey design for the dataset was done using computed weights and variables as instructed by the DHS. The dependent variable was computed using the equation mentioned above, followed by the recoding and computation as per need in each copy of the dataset used for each vaccine dose. Assessment of association was done using design-adjusted chi-square test followed by Poissons regression with timeliness or on-time vaccination as the reference category to derive unadjusted and adjusted Prevalence Ratios. Initially, unadjusted prevalence ratio for each variable was calculated, and subsequently these variables were added to the final model which was categorized as factors. To account for the complex survey design and clustering, the analysis was carried out under the subset of the “svy” command in Stata v18.0 MP, along with, “vce robust” option to obtain robust (Huber – White sandwich) variance estimators. This provided standard errors and 95% confidence intervals that are valid under the complex survey design and guard against mild model misspecification or over-dispersion. Variance estimation was performed using Taylor linearization as recommended for survey weighted Poisson regression models. For a few vaccines, some of the variables were not significant; hence, to maintain the parsimony of the models, these variables were removed. The study uses secondary analysis of data from DHS survey. As per the guidelines, each author separately obtained permission from the DHS to use the dataset. Therefore, ethical approval was not required for this study.

Among factors related to the child, babies with very small size at birth had a 62% higher prevalence of untimeliness (aPR = 1.62, 95% CI = 1.36–1.95) compared with those with average birth size. Among the maternal factors, no maternal education and less than four ANC visits were associated with a 53% (aPR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.35–1.74) and 24% (aPR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.16–1.33) higher prevalence of untimeliness, respectively, compared to mother with higher education and more than four ANC visits. Furthermore, home deliveries carried nearly three times (aPR = 2.92, 95% CI = 2.65–3.22) higher prevalence of untimely vaccination compared to institutional deliveries in Government institutions. Among household and community factors, children belonging to households where distance to health facility was reported as a big problem were associated with 11% (aPR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.02–1.21) higher prevalence of untimeliness compared to those children for whom distance to health facility was not a problem. In addition, belonging to a non-Hindu religion was associated with an 8% (aPR = 1.08, 95% CI = 1.00–1.18) higher prevalence compared to Hindu households. Moreover, residing in the Central Zone as associated with an 82% (aPR = 1.82, 95% CI = 1.63–2.05) higher prevalence of untimeliness when compared with those residing in the South Zone.

Untimely vaccination at 6 weeks

Table 3 shows that twin babies carry a 35% (aPR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.04–1.75) higher prevalence of untimely vaccination compared to singletons. Very small babies were associated with a 23% (aPR = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.06–1.43) higher prevalence of not receiving vaccines on time in comparison with average sized babies. Further, children of uneducated mothers were 45% (aPR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.30–1.62) more likely to receive vaccines late when compared with those of highly educated mothers. The study also observed that household factors such as distance from health facility and non-Hindu religion persists to contribute 6% (aPR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.00–1.13) and 12% (aPR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.05–1.20) higher prevalence respectively of untimeliness in vaccination at 6 weeks. Children from the poorest wealth index were observed to have a 31% (aPR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.18–1.46) increased prevalence of untimeliness compared to children from the richest quartile. In terms of community characteristics, children in the Western Zone of India had a maximum delay of nearly three times (aPR = 2.83, 95% CI = 2.21–3.62) in vaccination at 6 weeks for all vaccines compared to the South Zone. Discussion

This study utilized data from the NFHS 5 survey, encompassing 232,920 children under 5-y old, to assess the timeliness of childhood vaccinations in India.

At Birth, 5.41% of children received delayed vaccination, with BCG being the highest (14.84%), followed by the birth dose of Hepatitis B vaccine (9.26%) and Polio Vaccine 0 dose (9.08%). This is an improvement from the previous round of survey NFHS- 4 where the proportion of children with delayed BCG vaccination was found to be 23.1%, as reported by Choudhary et al.17 The reasons for the significant delay in the BCG vaccine could be because BCG is not readily administered in some facilities as the convention is to get a minimum number of infants in order to reduce wastage of vaccine following its opening. A study conducted in Ghana by Laryea et al. in 2012 reported a delay in BCG vaccination in 6.6% of babies.18 This difference could be because the study in Ghana only included children who were delivered at a healthcare facility.

We observed progress in improving vaccination timeliness in our study compared to a similar study conducted in India, which showed a higher proportion of children who received untimely vaccines. Choudhary et al. (2019) found that 23.1%, 29.3%, and 34.8% of children aged 10–23 months had delayed vaccination for BCG, DPT-1, and Measles, respectively.17 Shrivastwa et al. (2016) reported that the timely administration of BCG, DPT3, and MCV was achieved only in 31%, 19%, and 34% of children, respectively.19

In a study by Dhalaria, Pritu et al., timely vaccination was poor for most antigens except measles. Only 52.6% of children received BCG vaccination on time, and very few received Pentavalent doses as scheduled (Penta-1: 3.4%, Penta-2: 1.4% and Penta-3: none). In contrast, measles showed better coverage (75%).15 The very high prevalence of untimely vaccines in this study could be due to the time frame used to define delayed vaccination, which is much narrower than that in the present study.

Although there has been an improvement from previous years, delayed vaccination remains a significant challenge in India’s UIP, with studies showing that the public health implications of systematic delays in the receipt of vaccines likely serve as a major contributor to the country’s high burden of VPD-related morbidity and mortality.19 South Africa demonstrated regional disparities, with rural areas performing worse than urban areas in terms of vaccination coverage and timeliness.20 The Philippines reported substantial delays in vaccine receipt, with only 74.4% for BCG, 70.3% for Penta 1, and 39.1% for pentavalent three vaccines.21

Factors associated with delayed vaccination

Regional disparities

Children in the Northeast zone of India had the maximum delay for the BCG vaccine, whereas the overall delay was greater in the Central Zone. These regional variations could be due to differences in healthcare infrastructure, awareness, and accessibility of healthcare services.

Socioeconomic groups

Timely vaccination was found to be poor among children from poorer households compared to wealthier households. This is an important finding because vaccinations in India are provided free of cost under the government’s Universal Immunisation Programme but children from poorer households often experience delays in vaccination due to a combination of financial, geographic, and informational barriers. Even when vaccines are free, indirect costs such as transportation and lost wages can limit health facility attendance, particularly in rural or underserved areas where services are irregular and stock-outs are common.22,23 Furthermore, lower maternal education in poorer households further reduces awareness of vaccine schedules, while competing livelihood priorities often take precedence over preventive healthcare.24

Urban vs. rural divide

Rural children were more likely to receive untimely BCG vaccination than urban children. Interestingly, the difference in urban and rural residency was insignificant for the other vaccines received at Birth or 6,10 and 14 weeks or later. This concurs with the findings of the study by Choudhary et al. which did not observe a rural–urban disparity, even for the BCG vaccine.17

Maternal factors

Vaccination delays were significantly higher among children of m others with lower education levels. This difference was more significant for vaccines received at 6, 10, 14 weeks and later. Mothers with lower education levels often need to be more informed about the importance of timely vaccination, contributing to delays. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in India.15,17

Healthcare utilization and accessibility

Less than half of the mothers in the survey did not receive the minimum recommended four ANC visits. Even with an inadequate number of ANC visits, the chances of delayed vaccine at birth would be lower in the case of institutional deliveries, however this poor attitude and behavior would reflect the delay in the subsequent doses of the vaccine. Babies delivered at home were around three times more likely to receive all vaccines untimely at birth. This result could be related to a delay in seeking healthcare at birth in case of home delivery and the occurrence of vaccination at birth of children before discharge in hospital or institutional deliveries.25 It was interesting to note that babies delivered at private institutions also did not receive timely vaccines at birth. This could be due to the fact that not all private institutions have the provision for providing vaccination and will be referred elsewhere for the same. While institutional deliveries and antenatal care are crucial to ensuring timely vaccinations, gaps in postnatal follow-ups are still evident, as 55.53% of children did not receive a health checkup within the first 2 months of delivery and were directly associated with a delay in receiving the timely vaccination which was more significant at 6 weeks. Concerning the accessibility to healthcare services, it was observed that children from poorer households and those living far from health facilities faced challenges in accessing timely vaccinations, particularly for the early vaccines, which could also be a factor contributing to children not receiving a health checkup within the first 2 months of delivery. Similar findings have been reported earlier by studies in India.17

Child-associated factors

“Child size at birth” is based on maternal perception of the baby’s size at birth rather than the measured birth weight used in surveys such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) or national immunization studies.26

It serves as a proxy indicator for birth weight when actual measurements are missing. Children born smaller than average or very small were less likely to receive timely vaccination, potentially due to concerns over health complications. This delay was observed across all vaccines at all ages. Similar findings have been reported earlier by studies in India.17 Children with birth weight <2 kg usually require Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission for many associated morbidities at birth. If the NICU stay of these children is prolonged, then vaccination is delayed.25

Other factors

Religion also played a role in receiving timely vaccination. Religious affiliation if often intertwined with community norms and beliefs, which shape parents’ attitudes and actions regarding childhood immunization. A broad multi-country analysis found that in many low- and middle-income countries, Muslim children – and those from traditional or unaffiliated religious groups – had higher rates of being zero-dose (unvaccinated) compared to Christian children, even after adjusting for socioeconomic factors.27 Also, beliefs interpreted within the religious context can lead to vaccine delays or refusal. In Thailand, a qualitative study found that some Muslim parents perceived vaccines as non-halal or part of harmful foreign plots, whereas another study reported a drop in intention to vaccinate with increasing belief in divine intervention relying more on religious authorities or community leaders than on medical guidance.28,29 Similarly, children belonging to households other than Hindu religion were observed not to have received vaccines on time.15,17

In addition, it was observed that children who received the initial vaccines untimely were more likely to receive subsequent doses of vaccine late.

Data analysis did not reveal a notable disparity between the pre-COVID and post-COVID timeliness of vaccination. This highlights the robust healthcare services provided in the country for routine childhood immunizations.

The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve vaccination timeliness such as –

Strengthening Healthcare Systems: Routine immunization services should focus on timely vaccination, particularly in rural and underserved areas, to reduce childhood morbidity and mortality. Parents may find it difficult to attend weekday sessions because of work, leading to delays in vaccination. Aligning vaccination days with the community could improve timeliness.

Educational campaigns

Maternal education and awareness programs are crucial for improving timely vaccination, especially among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.

Improved access to facilities in hard-to-reach areas: Efforts should be made to enhance healthcare delivery in hard-to-reach areas through mobile clinics, better transportation options, and increased healthcare staff. Future studies could explore the reasons behind regional disparities in vaccine coverage, the impact of outbreaks/pandemics on routine immunization and how to mitigate future disruptions and interventions aimed at increasing timely postnatal checkups, which can significantly influence vaccination adherence.

Limitations

This study had certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was based on secondary data from the NFHS-5, which restricted the variables to those available in the dataset, thereby limiting the exploration of other potential determinants of vaccination timeliness such as health system readiness, vaccine stock-outs, or parental attitudes. Second, information on vaccination dates was included only for children with immunization cards containing clearly marked dates; thus, recall-based data were excluded from the study. Third, child size at birth was based on the mother’s perception rather than measured birth weight, which could have resulted in misclassifications. Fourth, as this was a cross-sectional study, causal inferences cannot be established between the identified factors and vaccination timeliness. Finally, though the study attempted to compare pre- and post-COVID-19 periods, the categorization was based on survey timing rather than actual service availability, hence residual confounding related to pandemic disruptions may still exist. Despite these limitations, the large nationally representative sample strengthens the validity and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

The key factors for the untimeliness of routine vaccinations include low birth weight for vaccinations near birth. Among maternal and household factors, poor education, antenatal care, and rural residence were significantly associated with the untimely vaccination. The COVID-19 pandemic was a protective factor, and delay in the previous dose was a risk factor for the untimely vaccination.
