Development and validation of machine learning models for obstructive sleep apnea risk stratification in high-risk pregnant women
Authors: Yu Wang, Kaiwen Wang, Xiaying Niu, Xuexin Wang, Zhifang Hu, Nan Shen, TingTing Jiang, Tong Tong, Hong Gao, Jing Ma, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) affects approximately 15% of pregnancies and is associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. Although polysomnography (PSG) is the diagnostic gold standard, increasing clinical demand creates substantial bottlenecks in manual PSG scoring and specialist review. Therefore, optimized risk stratification models are needed to streamline clinical workflows, prioritize diagnostic resource allocation, and facilitate timely intervention for high-risk pregnant patients.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study recruited pregnant women with suspected OSA who underwent level 2 portable PSG. Six machine learning algorithms, including XGBoost, logistic regression, GBM, neural networks, KNN, and AdaBoost, were constructed based on integrated clinical and oximetry features. Feature importance screening and DeLong’s test-based pairwise comparison were performed to determine the optimal feature combination for model construction. Leveraging structured medical records incorporating sleep history and physical measurements, this tool serves as an auxiliary triage strategy to assist clinical decision-making. Background

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) affects approximately 15% of pregnant women globally, with a prevalence as high as 26.7% in the third trimester [1, 2]. This condition is strongly associated with numerous adverse maternal and fetal outcomes including gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, preterm birth, and fetal growth restriction [3, 4], underscoring the urgent need for early detection and intervention.

Polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing OSA. While highly accurate, the conventional diagnostic pathway is often limited by the extensive time and specialized personnel required for comprehensive manual scoring, especially in high-volume clinical environments [5, 6]. Conventional clinical instruments including various sleep questionnaires are available, yet their performance in the obstetric population is frequently suboptimal due to the physiological changes of pregnancy [7, 8]. Nocturnal oximetry provides valuable objective data regarding oxygen saturation parameters such as average and minimum levels [9]. However, the wide variability in reported sensitivity and specificity for isolated oximetry metrics often necessitates more sophisticated integration with clinical markers to achieve reliable stratification [10]. Therefore, refined machine learning models are urgently needed to streamline diagnostic workflows and allocate limited specialist resources to patients with the most pressing clinical demands.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) offer a promising strategy to develop more accessible and cost-effective tools for OSA diagnosis [11, 12]. For instance, Wang and colleagues constructed the MoMOSA model and confirmed that combining clinical data with selected questionnaire items markedly improves diagnostic accuracy among Chinese pregnant women [13]. On this basis, the current study aimed to establish a machine learning-based framework to improve the efficiency of the existing diagnostic pathway. By integrating readily available clinical indicators and nocturnal oximetry data, we constructed a practical triage tool to prioritize manual PSG scoring and specialist review for high-risk patients in obstetric departments.

Methods

Study population and clinical assessment

This retrospective observational cohort study enrolled pregnant women with suspected OSA who attended Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital between December 2018 and September 2024. Suspected OSA was defined by at least one of the following indicators: chronic nocturnal snoring, witnessed apnea episodes, or pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2. A definitive OSA diagnosis was confirmed via PSG after specialist referral, and participants were grouped according to diagnostic outcomes. Exclusion criteria included complicated pregnancies (e.g., fetal malformations), incomplete nocturnal PSG recordings, missing clinical data, total sleep time < 4 h, and clinically unstable conditions (e.g., decompensated heart failure, active respiratory tract infection). Patients taking medications that may alter sleep architecture or OSA severity (antipsychotics, anxiolytics, hypnotics, sedatives, and benzodiazepine-based muscle relaxants) were also excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval number: 2025-KY-083-02).

At enrollment, each subject completed a hospital-designed sleep questionnaire covering demographics, sleep history, personal medical history, lifestyle factors, family disease history, prior positive airway pressure therapy, physical examination results, and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) scores (see Additional file 1 for the full questionnaire). Initial candidate predictors contained subjective symptoms including snoring and excessive daytime sleepiness. Combined statistical screening and clinical judgment were used to retain predictors with optimal discriminative capacity for this high-risk referred cohort.

All participants underwent unattended portable PSG monitoring using the SOMNOscreen™ plus system (SOMNOmedics GmbH, Germany), a certified AASM Type 2 portable device. Continuous multichannel physiological signals were recorded, including electroencephalography, bilateral electrooculography, electrocardiography, nasal airflow, inductance plethysmography-based respiratory effort, pulse oximetry, and body position. Certified sleep technologists attached sensors strictly following the AASM scoring manual (Version 2.3), instructed participants on device wearing procedures and sleep diary completion requirements. Raw PSG data were analyzed offline with DOMINO® software v2.7.0 by accredited scorers blinded to all baseline clinical data. A unified institutional protocol was applied to overnight monitoring and manual scoring throughout the entire enrollment period. All PSG tracings were manually scored by the same fixed team of expert technicians in accordance with standardized criteria.

Obstructive apnea was defined as a ≥ 90% reduction in oronasal airflow for ≥ 10s, with persistent respiratory effort. Hypopnea was defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in airflow for ≥ 10s, accompanied by either an arousal or ≥ 3% oxygen desaturation. All oximetry variables were extracted from the exact same PSG recording used to calculate the reference apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) for each participant. SpO2 data obtained from these synchronized recordings were analyzed as diagnostic features, including the 3% oxygen desaturation index (ODI), lowest SpO2 (LSpO2), and mean SpO2. The AHI was defined as the average number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour of sleep. OSA severity was classified using standard AHI cut-offs as mild (AHI ≥ 5 events/h), moderate (AHI ≥ 15 events/h), or severe (AHI ≥ 30 events/h). For predictive modeling, participants were stratified into two groups: a non-OSA group (AHI < 5 events/h) and an OSA group (AHI ≥ 5 events/h). The primary objective was to construct and validate machine learning models to identify any-grade OSA. The secondary objective was to evaluate model performance for predicting moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI ≥ 15 events/h), which requires more intensive clinical intervention.

Model development and validation

We developed the predictive model using a multi-stage approach. We utilized a random forest-based approach to assess variable importance using two complementary metrics: the mean decrease in gini (impurity-based) and permutation importance (accuracy-based). Only features demonstrating high consistency across both importance measures were retained for the final predictive modeling [14]. During this process, certain clinically relevant variables, such as snoring and Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS) scores, were excluded because they exhibited low discriminative power within this referred symptomatic population. This decision was based on the observation that such symptoms were highly prevalent across the entire cohort, which limited their ability to effectively differentiate between patients with and without OSA.

Six machine learning (ML) algorithms were applied to assess gestational OSA risk: XGBoost [15], logistic regression [16], gradient boosting machine (GBM) [17], Neural Networks [18], K-nearest neighbors (KNN) [19], and AdaBoost [20]. The combined clinical and oximetry features served as the input data for the model. The total cohort was randomly partitioned into a training set and an independent test set at a ratio of 7:3. All subsequent procedures, including feature selection and hyperparameter tuning, were conducted exclusively within the training set. To ensure the robustness of the models, hyperparameter optimization and model training were conducted using a 10-fold cross-validation strategy within the training dataset. The test set was used for the final evaluation of all models.

Model performance was assessed by accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The AUCs of the integrated clinical and oximetry models were compared with those of the ODI-only and ODI plus BMI baseline models using DeLong’s test. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots, calibration slope and intercept, and Brier scores. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA). Train–test AUC gaps were calculated to assess potential overfitting. Alternative operating thresholds were also evaluated to determine whether the models could prioritize screening sensitivity or referral specificity according to clinical needs.

Temporal stability of the model performance was further evaluated in a post-hoc temporal subgroup stratified analysis by dividing the cohort into three distinct time intervals, including December 2018 to December 2020, January 2021 to December 2022, and January 2023 to September 2024, using the chi square test for heterogeneity.

Missing data and statistical analysis

Regarding the handling of missing data, the proportion of missingness for each candidate predictor was assessed and found to be less than 5% for all variables. Missing continuous variables were imputed using the median value of the observed data, while missing categorical variables were imputed using the mode. Following this preprocessing step, all continuous inputs were subjected to Z-score standardization to facilitate numerical stability during the machine learning training process. Detailed missingness proportions for all predictors are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are reported as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts (percentages), and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. ROC curves were generated to assess model performance via AUC. True positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN) were determined from the true labels and used to calculate sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and the F1 score. To evaluate the stability of the models across different gestational stages, subgroup analyses were performed stratified by trimester (early, mid, and late). To evaluate the robustness of our findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing the models trained on the primary imputed dataset with those using only complete cases. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.2).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 667 pregnant women were included in the final analysis, comprising 216 participants in the first trimester, 304 in the second trimester, and 147 in the third trimester. Overall, 305 (45.7%) participants were diagnosed with OSA (Fig. 1). Compared with the non-OSA group, the OSA group showed significantly higher BMI (31.47 ± 5.06 kg/m2 vs. 28.16 ± 4.38 kg/m2) and a greater snoring prevalence (75.4% vs. 61.6%). Feature selection

Feature importance was quantified using two Random Forest-derived metrics (permutation importance and Mean Decrease Gini), alongside clinical expert judgment. Specifically, LSpO₂, ODI, BMI, waist circumference, and abdominal circumference were consistently ranked highest across all evaluation strategies. These five variables were therefore retained for constructing the final predictive model (Fig. 2). To further optimize feature composition, three alternative feature sets were established: the integrated Clin + Oxi set containing all five selected predictors, the ODI-only single-feature set, and the ODI + BMI two-feature combination. Pairwise AUC comparisons via DeLong’s test were then performed to identify the optimal feature combination (Supplementary Table 2).

Performance of the primary OSA prediction models

In the independent testing set, all six machine learning models exhibited robust discriminative ability, with AUC values ranging from 0.847 to 0.874 (Fig. 3A). The AdaBoost model achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.874, 95% CI: 0.821–0.927), followed closely by logistic regression (AUC = 0.872, 95% CI: 0.820–0.925) and the neural network (AUC = 0.871, 95% CI: 0.818–0.923). While a slight decrease in AUC was observed when transitioning from the training set to the testing set, the models maintained stable performance in the independent testing set.

Predictive metrics derived from the testing set further validated the clinical applicability of these models (Fig. 3B). The GBM model yielded the highest overall accuracy (0.834) and F1-score (0.818), reflecting a well-balanced trade-off between precision and recall. Moreover, the GBM model had the highest sensitivity of 0.813, making it preferable for initial population screening aimed at minimizing missed diagnoses. XGBoost and AdaBoost attained favorable specificity values of 0.880 and 0.870, respectively, which helps reduce false-positive results and avoid unnecessary clinical examinations. The neural network delivered balanced performance across all evaluated metrics (accuracy = 0.819, F1-score = 0.795). In contrast, the KNN model showed relatively inferior performance across most indicators (accuracy = 0.794, F1-score = 0.778), rendering it the least optimal algorithm in this head-to-head comparison.

To further verify the robustness and stability of the models, we performed a 10-fold cross-validation. Consistent AUC curves across different folds for all six algorithms are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Final hyperparameter sets were determined by balancing model complexity and predictive performance (Supplementary Table 3). Subgroup analyses stratified by gestational trimester confirmed stable model performance throughout pregnancy. Prediction accuracy gradually increased with advancing gestation, while all models still maintained acceptable predictive performance within each trimester (Supplementary Table 4). A sensitivity analysis restricted to complete-case participants produced outcomes consistent with the primary analysis, verifying that missing data imputation did not introduce substantial bias. Key metrics including AUC and specificity remained stable for all machine learning algorithms in the complete-case subset (Supplementary Table 5).

Comparison with conventional and baseline screening approaches

We further evaluated the clinical utility of our framework through an internal head-to-head comparison with established screening tools (Supplementary Table 6). In this specialized cohort, conventional instruments such as the ESS and the standalone clinical model demonstrated relatively modest discriminative power, with AUC values ranging from 0.480 to 0.712. In contrast, the integrated machine learning frameworks exhibited significantly higher AUC values, consistently exceeding 0.84. Notably, both linear and non-linear architectures demonstrated comparable and robust performance (AUC ≥ 0.87). These integrated models provided a more sophisticated triage capability, achieving a superior balance between sensitivity (75.8%–81.3%) and specificity (79.6%–88.0%). By maintaining a lower false-positive rate compared with conventional scoring systems, these frameworks represent a more reliable approach for identifying high-risk individuals and optimizing the allocation of diagnostic resources.

To further enhance clinical readiness, we evaluated the operational flexibility of the framework by assessing performance at alternative thresholds beyond the optimized Youden Index. As shown in Supplementary Table 7, the models offer the flexibility to prioritize either screening sensitivity or referral specificity. Consequently, the tool can be customized to meet distinct institutional goals based on varying clinical capacities and resource availability.

Clinical utility, calibration, and temporal subgroup analysis

The clinical utility of the developed algorithms was evaluated via DCA on the independent testing set (Fig. 4A). All six machine learning models yielded positive net clinical benefits across a wide range of threshold probabilities relative to the “treat-all” and “treat-none” reference strategies. Specifically, the neural network, AdaBoost, and logistic regression models consistently delivered favorable net benefits, supporting clinical decision-making by accurately identifying high-risk individuals while reducing unnecessary diagnostic procedures.

By contrast, the ensemble models including XGBoost and GBM, as well as the KNN model, tended to overestimate OSA risk. This pattern was reflected in their lower calibration slopes and negative intercepts, which indicated an aggressive prediction tendency generating higher risk estimates than the actual observed outcomes. For triage and risk stratification in a high-risk pregnant cohort, this sensitive prediction strategy is clinically acceptable: it prioritizes potential OSA suspects and ensures all high-risk patients are captured for further diagnostic workup. Raw classification metrics including true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives for each model are summarized as confusion matrices in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Furthermore, we conducted a post-hoc temporally stratified subgroup analysis to compare model predictive performance across different enrollment time windows within the full cohort. As presented in Supplementary Table 8, most machine learning models exhibited comparable diagnostic performance across the six-year study period. Heterogeneity testing revealed significant inter-period AUC differences only for the Neural Network and K-Nearest Neighbors models (both P < 0.05). Nevertheless, their AUC 95% confidence intervals largely overlapped across all three subgroups.

Exploratory analysis of moderate-to-severe OSA

Regarding the secondary outcome, we evaluated the ability of the machine learning models to identify moderate-to-severe OSA. This analysis involved 61 positive cases in the training set and 26 positive cases in the independent testing set. Given the modest number of events, these findings are considered preliminary and exploratory. The evaluated machine learning models demonstrated strong discriminative potential in identifying moderate-to-severe OSA. In the independent testing set, AUC values ranged from 0.898 (95% CI: 0.814–0.982) for the KNN model to 0.956 (95% CI: 0.902–1.000) for the AdaBoost model (Fig. 5A). The GBM and neural network models also yielded robust predictive efficacy, with AUCs of 0.955 (95% CI: 0.898–1.000) and 0.946 (95% CI: 0.872–1.000), respectively.

Discussion

This study developed a ML framework integrating multidimensional clinical and oximetric parameters to support individualized risk stratification for gestational OSA in a specialized referral cohort. For the primary OSA screening task, parsimonious linear models achieved competitive discriminative performance comparable to sophisticated ensemble algorithms. Specifically, the logistic regression model yielded a robust testing-set AUC of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.820–0.925), demonstrating that linear aggregation of core predictors can deliver reliable predictive efficacy. From a translational perspective, such simplified, interpretable models offer unique clinical advantages. Unlike black-box ensemble architectures, linear models can be readily converted into bedside scoring systems or nomograms without intensive computational requirements, facilitating seamless real-world implementation.

Notably, all established models exhibited excellent predictive efficacy for detecting moderate-to-severe OSA. Nevertheless, these exploratory findings should be interpreted with caution, as the broader confidence intervals were largely attributable to the limited number of positive events in the independent testing cohort. Despite this statistical limitation, the framework presents promising clinical value owing to its reliable exclusionary performance. Equipped with consistently high NPVs, the proposed algorithms serve as efficient triage tools that accurately identify high-risk patients requiring urgent evaluation while safely excluding low-risk individuals from unnecessary PSG. Specifically, the framework can be operationalized as a triage mechanism to prioritize expedited manual scoring for flagged high-risk individuals. This model-based strategy enables prioritized specialist review for screen-positive cases, allows timely clinical referral and intervention after routine data acquisition, and optimizes diagnostic resource allocation within specialized obstetric care settings.

The prominent predictive contribution of oximetric variability in our models aligns with the well-established pathophysiological mechanisms of OSA, in which intermittent nocturnal hypoxemia drives systemic physiological disturbances [21]. In pregnant populations, repetitive apneic episodes induce cyclic oxygen desaturation, which has been closely associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, including neonatal respiratory distress syndrome [22]. Conventional oximetry-only screening strategies achieve high sensitivity for simple exclusion of OSA in general populations, yet they suffer from unsatisfactory specificity and abundant false-positive results, which prevents their independent clinical use [23]. Nevertheless, this advantage in sensitivity could not be replicated in our pregnant cohort. Predictions based exclusively on the ODI yielded low sensitivity of 62.9% to 69.4%, which further illustrates the limitations of relying on a single oximetric indicator for gestational OSA screening. By integrating objective oximetric metrics with multidimensional clinical variables, our ML framework more comprehensively captures pregnancy-specific physiological alterations. The proposed framework strikes a reasonable balance between sensitivity ranging from 75.8% to 81.3% and specificity ranging from 79.6% to 88.0%, accompanied by a reduced false-positive rate compared with conventional scoring tools. Pairwise AUC comparisons via DeLong’s test further confirmed statistically significant differences in predictive efficacy relative to classic screening scales and standalone clinical models. This synergistic combination effectively compensates for the inherent noise of isolated oximetry monitoring, achieving more robust and individualized OSA risk stratification and facilitating optimized allocation of diagnostic resources in high-risk pregnant cohorts.

Traditional subjective screening questionnaires, including the ESS and STOP-Bang scale, frequently exhibit compromised diagnostic accuracy in pregnant populations. Normal gestational physiological changes such as hormonal fatigue, nasal congestion, and excessive daytime somnolence overlap considerably with classic OSA manifestations. These tools rely entirely on patient self-reported symptoms and cannot reliably differentiate physiological pregnancy-related tiredness from pathological OSA symptoms, eventually leading to low specificity among obstetric cohorts [24]. Consistent with this reasoning, comparative assessment verified the unsatisfactory performance of conventional screening tools. The standalone ESS produced a modest AUC of 0.595 (95% CI: 0.516–0.675) in our study cohort. By contrast, our ML framework alleviates this limitation by adopting objective clinical and oximetric indicators to capture complex nonlinear risk associations, which may help raise screening specificity accordingly. This paradigm shift can potentially streamline clinical workflows and cut down redundant PSG referrals, enabling accurate prioritization of high-risk individuals. Nevertheless, these advantages have only been validated in a high-risk referral cohort, and additional external validation is still needed before widespread clinical adoption.

This study complements and extends existing ML-based OSA prediction models with a specific focus on pregnant populations. Previous predictive models have largely focused on pediatric or general adult populations or relied on inaccessible imaging modalities with limited clinical feasibility. For example, Ye et al. constructed an XGBoost model using heart rate and oximetry data for pediatric OSA stratification, highlighting the utility of oximetric parameters but lacking generalizability to pregnant individuals [25]. Kim et al. developed a high-accuracy deep learning model based on craniofacial computed tomography, yet imaging-dependent approaches increase clinical burden and hinder large-scale screening implementation [26]. Chen et al. established a morphology-based predictive model, but their male-dominated cohort precluded valid extrapolation to gestational populations [27]. Yang et al. previously adopted questionnaire-based logistic regression for screening sleep-disordered breathing in pregnancy but reported poor specificity and high false-positive rates, limiting its practical value [28]. Building on these prior studies, our framework integrates routinely accessible clinical and oximetric features and targets an understudied high-risk obstetric population, providing a feasible, low-cost, and severity-stratified screening strategy.

To evaluate the diagnostic stability of the framework across the biological continuum of gestation, a sequential subgroup analysis was performed, with results stratified by trimester. The corresponding findings are provided in Supplementary Table 4. Although the predictive performance was relatively modest in the early trimester, with an average area under the curve of approximately 0.75, a distinct upward trajectory was observed as pregnancy progressed. Performance values reached a range between 0.91 and 0.95 in the mid and late trimesters. This performance gradient aligns with the expected pathophysiological evolution of obstructive sleep apnea. As anatomical and physiological alterations, such as upper airway edema and abdominal adiposity, become more pronounced in the later stages of gestation, they provide more distinct predictive signals for the algorithms to capture. The capacity of these models, particularly the logistic regression and neural network architectures, to maintain high discriminative power across these sub-cohorts supports their potential utility for risk stratification across different stages of pregnancy, although further prospective validation is needed before longitudinal clinical implementation.

The incorporation of BMI, waist circumference, and abdominal circumference as core predictive markers reflects the multifactorial pathogenesis of gestational OSA [2, 29]. While BMI is a classic indicator of generalized obesity, central adiposity indices (waist and abdominal circumferences) exhibited superior predictive value in this cohort, likely due to their closer correlation with visceral fat accumulation and pregnancy-related respiratory restriction. Mechanistically, visceral adiposity exacerbates upper airway collapsibility via pharyngeal fat deposition, whereas increased abdominal girth elevates diaphragmatic position and reduces pulmonary functional residual capacity [30]. Visceral obesity also promotes leptin resistance and oxidative stress, further deteriorating upper airway neuromuscular function and aggravating nocturnal hypoxemia [31, 32]. In late gestation, uterine expansion synergizes with visceral fat accumulation to elevate intra-abdominal pressure, prolonging hypopneic events and worsening sleep-disordered breathing [33]. By integrating these readily available anthropometric and physiological markers, our parsimonious ML framework achieves comprehensive and refined risk profiling for gestational OSA.

The findings in this report are subject to several limitations. First, we acknowledge a major methodological concern regarding incorporation bias, as several key predictors, including the ODI, LSpO2, mean SpO2, and AHI, were derived from the same nocturnal recording used to define the reference standard. This structural dependency likely inflates model performance estimates, particularly for moderate-to-severe cases. In future studies, we seek to further validate these models using simplified oximetry devices in primary care settings to better understand their potential generalizability and real-world clinical utility. Second, the single-center design and reliance on a high-risk cohort may limit generalizability to lower-prevalence community settings, where the observed performance may not be fully replicated. While our models demonstrated stable performance throughout the six-year study duration, a prospective validation with an independent future cohort remains essential to ensure clinical readiness. Third, standardized sleep questionnaires, such as the STOP-Bang and Berlin instruments, were not systematically collected within our clinical database. While we utilized the Epworth Sleepiness Scale along with a departmentally developed questionnaire, it is important to note that the latter has not been formally tested for reliability or validity. Future prospective studies should incorporate these conventional screening instruments to better evaluate the incremental diagnostic gain provided by our proposed models in the obstetric population. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes an assessment of the longitudinal impact of obstructive sleep apnea on pregnancy outcomes. Future prospective and multicenter studies are essential to provide the external validation necessary to confirm the robustness of these predictive frameworks across diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study suggests that machine learning models integrating clinical and oximetric markers may offer high discriminative potential for identifying OSA within a specialized pregnant population. Our analysis indicates that logistic regression provides a stable baseline for detecting any OSA, non-linear models such as Neural Networks and XGBoost show favorable sensitivity for stratifying moderate-to-severe disease. This enables clinicians to tailor algorithm selection to specific triage needs, utilizing simpler models for broad screening while leveraging advanced architectures to pinpoint high-risk individuals requiring prioritized intervention. However, it is essential to acknowledge that these findings are based on a single-center retrospective dataset from a referred high-risk population with a high disease prevalence. Consequently, our models should currently be viewed as triage and risk-stratification tools specifically for high-risk cohorts. Future prospective and multi-center studies are required to provide the external validation necessary to confirm the generalizability and robustness of these predictive frameworks across diverse healthcare settings.
