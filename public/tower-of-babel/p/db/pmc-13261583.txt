Methylglyoxal and Glyoxal in High-Puff Disposable Electronic Cigarette Liquids: Unexpected Accumulation and Enhanced Cytotoxicity
Authors: Esther E. Omaiye, Wentai Luo, Kevin J. McWhirter, Man Wong, Prue Talbot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite concerns regarding the potential health risks associated with EC use, their design, puff count, and e-liquid formulations have continued to evolve. Aldehydes are a key class of thermal degradation byproducts generated during the vaporization of EC fluids. This study tested the hypothesis that aldehyde concentrations would be elevated in used ECs with high puff numbers. Devices from multiple brands were analyzed to assess the impact of extended use on aldehyde formation. E-fluids were extracted from field-collected and user-donated devices and analyzed using gas chromatography/mass spectrometry to quantify 178 flavor chemicals, two synthetic coolants, nicotine, solvents, and aldehydes (methylglyoxal (MGO), glyoxal (GO), formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, acrolein, glyceraldehyde, dihydroxyacetone, propanal, and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural). Pulmonary cells were exposed to MGO and acetaldehyde in submerged cultures and at the air–liquid interface, and their toxic effects were analyzed in multiple assays. E-fluid consumption averaged 77% in ElfBar, 58% in FLUM, and 42% in “Other Brands”, with chemical profiles varying significantly across brands and collection groups. Total flavor chemical concentrations averaged 34 mg/mL ± 21.6 for ElfBar and 9.7 mg/mL ± 7.9 for FLUM, and these concentrations were significantly reduced in vaped fluids. MGO, GO, and formaldehyde concentrations increased significantly in vaped fluids, while acetaldehyde and acrolein both decreased. Glyceraldehyde and dihydroxyacetone were detected only in vaped fluids and were generally elevated in ElfBar compared to FLUM products. MGO and acetaldehyde significantly disrupted the morphology of BEAS-2B cells and increased superoxide production in a concentration-dependent manner. These findings underscore the potential for increases in fluid aldehyde concentration with high-puff number ECs and raise concerns about the inhalation of cytotoxic byproducts generated during puffing. Regulatory policies should consider the influence of puff count on aldehyde generation.

Introduction

Electronic cigarettes (ECs) have been used in the United States since about 2007, with a surge in popularity around 2015 when JUUL Laboratories introduced a design that became immediately popular with young users. − EC aerosols have a complex chemistry that has been linked to adverse health effects reported by EC users in online forums, physicians’ offices, and case reports, − many of which have been summarized in a recent meta-analysis of existing data.

ECs have undergone several generations of design, incorporating changes in size, appearance, power, and fluid chemistry. − Currently, popular brands, such as Geek Bar Pulse 15000 and Kanger Subox 50000, are disposable and have been designed with large capacity fluid tanks that are advertised to deliver up to 50,000 puffs/unit (e.g., Kanger Subox 50000), in contrast to the earliest models, which delivered 150–200 puffs/unit. During storage and each heating cycle, there is a possibility of creating chemical changes in the e-fluids and producing new chemicals, such as aldehydes and their acetals, that could increase the toxicity of EC aerosols. − Aldehydes have been produced with as little as one puff on a KangerTech Protank II clearomizer coupled to an INNOKIN iTaste VV4 battery, and emissions increased significantly when devices were reused multiple times. The introduction of high puff number ECs raises concerns about users’ exposure to a broader range of chemicals and higher concentrations of toxicants as more puffs are taken. However, this possibility has not been previously investigated in ECs marketed to deliver “high puff numbers.” In this study, we use the term ‘high-puff’ to refer to disposable devices labeled for ≥1000–6000 puffs, which represent the majority of the samples we analyzed.

Our study evaluated e-fluid from high-puff ECs donated by users who had finished using them or collected them at field sites in Southern California, where they had been discarded. Unvaped ECs of the same type and flavor as those in our collection were purchased so that e-fluid from unvaped and vaped ECs could be compared chemically. The constituents that were analyzed included propylene glycol (PG), glycerol (G), nicotine, 178 flavor chemicals, two synthetic coolants, and nine aldehydes.

Materials and Methods

Sample Acquisition and Storage

Over 9 months, 77 vaped ECs from 20 brands were donated by users or collected at field sites (recreational parks, a university campus, or shopping centers) in Riverside, Santa Ana, and Los Angeles, California. Brands studied were ElfBar (n = 23), FLUM (n = 16), Hyppe (n = 7), Vuse (n = 6), and “Other Brands” (n = 25). Unvaped counterparts for most products were purchased locally, allowing comparison of chemical constituents in unvaped and vaped fluids. When available on the package labels, nicotine concentrations ranged from 5 to 5.5%. The labeled volume on each device ranged from 1.3 to 13 mL, and the labeled puff count/unit ranged from 300 to 6000. All devices were inventoried, stored in the dark at room temperature, and analyzed within 2–3 weeks of receipt. Individual products and their methods of acquisition (user-donated or field-collected) are listed in Table S1. Brands with ≤7 products were grouped into an “Other Brands” category to avoid unstable model-specific statistical considerations.

Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry (GC/MS) Analysis of Solvents, Flavor Chemicals, Synthetic Coolants, and Nicotine

Fluid-saturated wicks were removed from dissected ECs and centrifuged in 15 mL conical tubes or MinElute spin columns (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) at 3000 rpm for 3 min to separate the fluid from the wick. The extracted fluid from 70 devices was analyzed using previously described GC/MS methods. , Fifty microliters of each sample were dissolved in 0.95 mL of isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and shipped overnight on ice to Portland State University, where they were analyzed on the day of receipt. Fifty microliters of the above diluted samples were further dissolved in 0.95 mL of IPA and used to quantify solvents and high-concentration chemicals. An aliquot of 20 μL/mL of the internal standard (2000 ng/μL of 1,2,3-trichlorobenzene in IPA) was added to each diluted sample before analysis. Analyses were performed with an Agilent 5975C GC/MS system (Santa Clara, CA). A Restek Rxi-624Sil MS column (Bellefonte, PA) was used (30m long, 0.25 mm id, and 1.4 μm film thickness). A 1.0 μL aliquot of the diluted sample was injected into the GC with a 10:1 split. The injector temperature was 235 °C. The GC temperature program for analysis was as follows: 40 °C was held for 2 min, then increased at 10 °C/min to 100 °C, followed by a 12 °C/min increase to 280 °C and a hold for 8 min at 280 °C. The temperature was then decreased at 20 °C/min to 230 °C. The MS was operated in electron impact ionization mode at 70 eV in positive ion mode. The ion source temperature was 220 °C, and the quadrupole temperature was 150 °C. The scan range was 34–400 amu. The method analyzes 178 flavor chemicals, two synthetic coolants (WS-23 and WS-3), two solvents (propylene glycol and glycerol), plus nicotine. The quantification was performed using authentic standard reference material with internal-standard-based calibration procedures described elsewhere. , The chemicals detected and not detected are shown in Tables S2 and S3.

Quantification of Aldehydes in E-Fluids Using GC/MS

Nine (9) target analytes were investigated in e-fluids collected from each device (Table S4). 1 mL of HPLC grade water, 100 μL of e-fluid or calibration standards, 20 μL of 2 mg/mL internal standard solution (2′,4′,5′-trifluoroacetophenone in 50/50 methanol/water), and 1 mL of 10 mg/mL derivatization solution (PFBHA in pH = 4 citric acid buffer) were added to a Reacti Vial (ThermoFisher Scientific, #TS-13097). The vial was capped and vortexed for 30 s, then covered with aluminum foil and left at room temperature for 24 h. After 24 h, 3 drops of 18N H2SO4 and 1 mL of dichloromethane (DCM) were added to the solution, vortexed for 30 s, and then placed at room temperature for a 30 min extraction. The vial was then centrifuged for 3 min, and the bottom layer was collected into a test tube with 100 mg of Na2SO4. The DCM phase was then transferred into an autosampler vial for GC/MS analysis. The samples were analyzed using an Agilent 5975C GC/MS system (Santa Clara, CA) with a Restek Rxi-624Sil MS GC column (30 m, 0.25 mm id, and 1.4 μm film thickness) (Bellefonte, PA). The GC program condition was set as follows: 50 °C hold for 2 min; 10 °C/min to 100 °C; 5 °C/min to 250 °C; then 10 °C/min to 280 °C hold for 10 min at 280 °C; then 25 °C/min to 230 °C. The MS was operated in electron impact ionization mode at 70 eV in positive ion mode. The ion source temperature was 250 °C, and the scan range was 50–500 amu.

BEAS-2B Cell Culture for Submerged Exposures

Human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia; Catalog No. CRL-3588) were cultured in BEGM Bronchial Epithelial Cell Growth Medium BulletKit (LONZA, Catalog No. CC-3170), using a method described previously in detail. Nunc T-25 tissue culture flasks were coated for 2 h with Basal Medium (BEGM medium without BulletKit), collagen, bovine serum albumin, and fibronectin prior to subculturing. Upon reaching 90% confluency, the cells were rinsed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS−) without calcium and magnesium and then detached using 1.5 mL of 0.25% trypsin–EDTA with polyvinylpyrrolidone for 1.5 min at 37 °C. Cells were subcultured into T-25 flasks at a density of 75,000 cells per flask, and the medium was changed every other day.

Phase Contrast Imaging

BEAS-2B cells were plated in a 24-well plate at a density of ∼21,000 cells/cm2 and allowed to attach and expand for 24 h, then treated with acetaldehyde (1–1000 μg/mL) or MGO (1–100 μg/mL) for 12 h. Live-cell imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse TS100 inverted microscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY, USA) with a 10× objective.

Superoxide Analysis Using MitoSOX Red

BEAS-2B cells were plated in 8-well chamber slides (Ibidi, Grafelfing, Germany) at 10,000 cells/well and allowed to attach for 24 h, then treated with acetaldehyde (1–1000 μg/mL) or MGO (1–100 μg/mL) for 12 h. Afterward, the medium was removed, and 5 μM MitoSOX Red from ThermoFisher Scientific (Catalog No. M36008) prepared in fresh medium was added to the wells and incubated for 30 min. The wells were then rinsed with Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline with calcium and magnesium (PBS+) three times and replaced with fresh medium. Live-cell imaging was performed using a Nikon Ti-Eclipse inverted microscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY, USA) equipped with an Andor Zyla VSC-04941 camera and a 60× water-immersion objective.

Phalloidin Labeling

BEAS-2B cells were plated in Ibidi 8-well chamber slides (Grafelfing, Germany, No. 80826) at 10,000 cells/well. After 24 h, cells were treated with acetaldehyde or MGO for 12 h. Acetaldehyde concentrations ranged from 1 to 1000 μg/mL (0.0227 to 22.7 mM), while MGO concentrations ranged from 1 to 1000 μg/mL (0.0139 to 13.9 mM). After exposure, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, then stained with 1x Phalloidin-iFlour 594 reagent (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom, Catalog No. ab176757) for 1 h, following the manufacturer’s assay procedure. Cells were labeled with DAPI and imaged at 60× magnification.

Air Liquid Interface Exposure

BEAS-2B cells were plated on Corning transwell inserts (Corning, NY, USA, Product No. 3460) and expanded for 6 days to reach maximum confluence. Cells were then exposed in a VITROCELL cloud chamber (12/12 base module, Waldkirch, Germany) to 1–9 puffs of MGO. 200 μL of a 1 μg/mL MGO solution (diluted in PBS-) were loaded into the cloud chamber nebulizer; the total amount of MGO in each puff was 0.2 μg. The nebulization of the aerosol was for 90 s, followed by settling for 210 s. In the VITROCELL chamber, the aerosol deposition unto the inset was about 150x lower than the mass loaded in to the nebulizer. , An estimated 0.0013 μg of MGO was deposited onto each insert (growth surface area: 1.12 cm2) per puff. At the maximum exposure of 9 puffs, the total deposited dose was ∼0.0117 μg/insert. After exposure, cells were passaged and plated on Ibidi 8-well chamber slides and allowed to attach for 12 h, after which they were stained with MitoTracker Red (ThermoFisher Scientific, Catalog No. M7512) and Hoechst 33342 (Immunochemistry Technologies, Catalog No. 639). Cellular and nuclear abnormalities were assessed manually in images collected with a Nikon Eclipse inverted microscope.

Data Analysis and Statistics

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA). Normality was assessed using D’Agostino–Pearson, Shapiro–Wilk, Anderson–Darling, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and variance homogeneity using Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests. Data sets with unequal variances were analyzed using Welch’s ANOVA with heteroscedasticity-robust posthoc tests. Data sets meeting normality and equal-variance assumptions were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with appropriate multiple-comparison procedures. All tests were two-tailed with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

EC Sources and Brands

A total of 77 ECs from multiple manufacturers were either donated by users (n = 50, 65%) or collected at field sites (n = 27, 35%), which included recreational areas, university campus, streets, and parks (Figure A). ECs represented 20 brands, including four of the most popular (ElfBar, FLUM, Hyppe, and Vuse) (Figure B). For ElfBar, 61% of the samples were collected in the field, and 39% were donated (Figure C). For FLUM, 31% were collected, and 69% were donated (Figure D). All other products were placed in “Other Brands,” and for this category, 16% were collected, and 84% were donated (Figure E).

E-Fluid Extraction and Volume Estimation

The fluid volumes collected from unvaped ElfBar, FLUM, and “Other Brands” disposables were compared with the volumes listed on the packaging. Volumes collected from unvaped devices ranged from 1.3 mL (“Other Brands”) to 9.4 mL (ElfBar) (Figure F).

Labeled volumes on the product packages were 13 mL (ElfBar), 8 mL (FLUM), and 1.3–14 mL for “Other Brands.” Vaped devices contained significantly lower amounts of e-fluid than their unvaped counterparts (Figure F). In all brand groups, the average residual volumes were similar (2.0 mL = ElfBar; 2.3 mL = FLUM, and 2.0 mL = Others) (Figure F). Fluid collected from unvaped ElfBar ranged from 7.5 to 9.4 mL, while identical vaped devices had fluids ranging from 0.1 to 6.8 mL (Figure F). Volumes collected from the FLUM devices were lower, ranging from 5.2 to 5.8 mL for unvaped products and 0.3–3.5 mL for vaped products. For “Other Brands”, volumes ranged from 1.3–5.2 mL for unvaped products and from 0.1 to 4.3 mL for vaped products. For vaped products for which unvaped counterparts were unavailable, volumes ranged from 0.1 to 5.6 mL (Figure F).

E-fluid consumption was calculated by subtracting the volume recovered from vaped products from identical unvaped products (Figure G). Significantly more e-liquid was consumed from ElfBar products than from FLUM or “Other Brands” (Figure G). In contrast, FLUM users consumed more than the users of “Other Brands.” The percentage of e-fluid consumption averaged 77% ± 25 for ElfBar, 58% ± 19 for FLUM, and 46% ± 30 for “Other Brands” (Figure H).

Solvents, Total Flavor Chemicals, Nicotine, and Coolant Concentrations in Unvaped and Vaped E-Fluids

PG and G concentrations in unvaped products ranged from 250 to 632 mg/mL and 339 to 578 mg/mL, respectively (Figure A–F). Solvent ratios were highly variable within unvaped ElfBar (40:60, 50:50, and 60:40) and FLUM (30:70, 40:60, 50:50, and 70:30) brands. In vaped fluids, PG and G concentrations were 15–672 mg/mL and 292–1198 mg/mL, respectively (Figure A–F). The PG/G ratios were similar to the unvaped in all but two samples. In general, PG and G concentrations were not changed significantly in user-donated versus field-collected groups or in unvaped versus vaped samples (Figure A–F). When significance was found, differences between means were minor (e.g., Figure E, user-donated unvaped versus vaped) or were not relevant (e.g., Figure A, field-collected vaped versus user-donated vaped).

For all three brand groups, the mean concentrations of total flavor chemicals in unvaped e-fluids ranged from 5.5–37.9 mg/mL, with ElfBar having the highest mean concentration (Figures G–I, S1). ElfBar Blue Razz Ice contained an unusually high total flavor chemical concentration (∼83 mg/mL), primarily due to the inclusion of 40.9 mg/mL of triacetin (Figures G, S1). For vaped samples, the mean concentrations ranged from 2.4 to 19.9 mg/mL in all three brand groups (Figures G–I, S1–S3). After vaping, the concentration of total flavor chemicals decreased significantly when compared to the unvaped samples (Figure G–I).

In unvaped fluids, nicotine concentrations were similar (∼50 mg/mL) across brand groups (Figures J–L, S1–S3). In general, nicotine concentrations did not change after vaping, except for ElfBar, in which nicotine decreased significantly in the vaped versus the unvaped user-donated group (Figure J).

The synthetic coolant WS-23 was present in unvaped e-fluids at about 20 mg/mL in all three brand groups (Figures M–O, S1–S3). WS-23 concentration decreased after vaping and was significant in user-donated ElfBar, but not in FLUM or “Other Brands.” A second synthetic coolant, WS-3, was present in relatively few unvaped and vaped ElfBar, FLUM, and “Other Brands” at low mean concentrations ranging from 0.04–5.4 mg/mL (Figures P–R, S1–S3).

Two aldehydes were not present in unvaped fluids but formed during vaping of FLUM (glyceraldehyde, dihydroxyacetone) and “Other Brands” (glyceraldehyde) (Figure S4). Additionally, one ElfBar product showed an increase in 5-hydroxymethylfurfural after vaping (Figure S4). The total concentration of aldehydes increased significantly in vaped vs unvaped fluid for both ElfBar and FLUM (Figure M). However, the direction of change in concentrations varied among “Other Brands” (Figure O). Because vaped liquid volume decreased, we converted measured concentrations to absolute analyte masses using the experimentally determined liquid volumes (unvaped, vaped and consumed) to distinguish between concentration due to volume loss and true chemical formation (Figures , S5 and Table S7). While the mass of MGO, GO and formaldehyde increased in most vaped fluids (Figure A–C), acetaldehyde and acrolein decreased (Figure D–E). For total aldehydes, the trend was more net formation in vaped ElfBar and FLUM products (Figure F). The average net formation and net loss for aldehydes are shown in Table S7. The result indicates that elevated aldehyde levels in the liquid are reflective of net chemical formation during vaping rather than simple solvent loss.

Acetaldehyde and MGO Alter BEAS-2B Cell Morphologies

In submerged cultures, untreated BEAS-2B cells exhibited a characteristic epithelial morphology when observed with phase-contrast microscopy (Figure A–H). After 12 h of exposure, acetaldehyde produced a concentration-dependent change in BEAS-2B cell morphology (Figure A–D). Morphological changes were most pronounced at 1000 μg/mL and included cell rounding and shrinkage (Figure D). In contrast, exposure to a much lower concentration of MGO (10 μg/mL) (Figure E–H) resulted in cell shrinkage, rounding, and detachment, indicating significant cytotoxic effects (Figure G). At the highest MGO concentration (100 μg/mL), confluency was lower, and cells were elongated and appeared fixed to the substrate (Figure H).

MGO Aerosol Altered BEAS-2B Cell and Nuclear Morphology at ALI

To determine if similar effects occurred during aerosol exposure, BEAS-2B cells were exposed to MGO at 0, 1, 3, 6, and 9 puffs at the ALI, followed by labeling with MitoTracker Red, which measures mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure ). Compared to untreated controls (Figure I), cells exposed to MGO became shorter and eventually round as puff number increased (Figure J,K). Nuclear abnormalities, including fragmentation, were also observed (Figure J,K). Quantitative analyses showed a statistically significant increase in the percentages of abnormal cells and cells with fragmented nuclei as the MGO puff number increased (Figure L,M).

Acetaldehyde and MGO Induced Mitochondrial ROS Generation in Submerged Culture

The impact of acetaldehyde and MGO on mitochondrial superoxide production was assessed using BEAS-2B cells exposed in submerged culture, then labeled with MitoSOX Red, a fluorogenic dye specific for mitochondrial superoxide detection (Figure A–H). Both acetaldehyde and MGO induced a concentration-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity, indicating elevated mitochondrial superoxide production (Figure B–D,F–H). MGO (10 μg/mL) increased fluorescence at a concentration 100 times lower than acetaldehyde (1000 μg/mL), consistent with the phase-contrast data.

Acetaldehyde and MGO Disrupted Actin Filaments in BEAS-2B Cells in Submerged Cultures

Exposure of BEAS-2B cells to acetaldehyde or MGO resulted in a concentration-dependent fragmentation of f-actin, as visualized with phalloidin labeling (Figure I–P). Acetaldehyde (100–1000 μg/mL) and MGO (10–100 μg/mL) caused cytoskeletal collapse accompanied by cell rounding (Figure J–L,N–P). At the highest concentration of MGO (100 μg/mL), there was no phalloidin labeling, indicating complete depolymerization of f-actin.

WS-23 Margin of Exposure (MOE) Assessment

Because no chronic inhalation point of departure (POD) has been established for WS-23, a conservative route-to-route extrapolation from the oral NOAEL (5 mg/kg-bw/day) was used as an inhalation-equivalent POD based on systemic dose. A 60 kg adult body weight and 1 mL of fluid consumption per day was considered under three liquid-to-aerosol transfer assumptions (10%, 70%, and 100%). Transfer of WS-23 from e-liquid to aerosol has been reported to be approximately 70% under controlled vaping conditions. However, to bound uncertainty, MOEs were calculated assuming 100% (upper-bound), 70% (empirically supported), and 10% (lower-bound) transfer efficiencies and interpreted as screening-level indicators not as a quantitative estimate of risk (Figure ).

Screening-level margin of exposure (MOE) for WS-23. MOEs are shown assuming 10% (lower-bound), 70% (empirically supported), and 100% (upper-bound) transfer of WS-23 from e-liquid to aerosol. The 100 threshold represents the default uncertainty factor for inter- and intraspecies variability, and values below 100 indicate the need for further evaluation under conservative screening assumptions rather than a quantitative prediction of adverse health effects.

Discussion

Unvaped and vaped fluids from high-puff-number ECs (ElfBar, FLUM, and “Other Brands”) were analyzed to determine chemical differences before and after vaping and to assess whether extended use increases concentrations of toxic aldehydes. Concentrations of solvent, nicotine, synthetic coolant, and flavor chemical in field-collected and user-donated e-liquids were generally within ranges reported in previous studies of commercial products. ,− Comparisons between concentrations of these chemicals in the unvaped collected vs the unvaped donated groups and in the vaped collected vs the vaped donated were generally not significantly different, supporting the idea that environmental factors did not affect concentrations of chemicals in the collected groups. In contrast, MGO and GO increased significantly and reached mg/mL concentrations in some vaped fluids, which are substantially higher than concentrations previously reported in aerosols. , Conversely, some aldehydes, including acetaldehyde and acrolein, decreased following vaping. Functional assays using BEAS-2B cells demonstrated that MGO was approximately 100-fold more potent than acetaldehyde in disrupting cell morphology, inducing superoxide production, and impairing actin cytoskeleton integrity. These findings indicate that toxic aldehydes, particularly MGO and GO, accumulate during vaping of high-puff-number ECs, supporting our hypothesis that extended device puffing exacerbates chemical toxicity.

The residual fluid in vaped products (2.1 ± 1.7 mL) likely reflects unpleasant flavor, increased harshness, and poor wick saturation at lower fluid volumes, and variability in puffing topography. Four vaped ElfBar products (Lemon Mint #1 and #2, Peach Berry #1, and Sakura Grape#1) contained ∼ 6–7 mL of vaped fluid, suggesting premature disposal or device malfunction. Overall, ElfBar users consumed more e-liquid during vaping (∼6.8 mL/device) than users of “Other Brands” (<2 mL/device), underscoring brand-specific differences in consumption patterns and exposure levels.

PG, G, and nicotine levels were similar across brands and comparable to previous studies. ,− Typical solvent ratios used in high-puff-number devices likely ensure consistent aerosol and nicotine delivery. Nicotine concentrations were generally similar before and after vaping. Decreases in ElfBar are likely due to thermal degradation of nicotine (e.g., into cotinine), increased vaporization with increasing number of puffs, loss during storage, or batch variability.

Our data on flavor chemicals in high-puff number ECs align well with other studies on low-puff number devices. ,− Although many flavor chemicals are “generally regarded as safe” (GRAS) for ingestion, the GRAS designation does not extend to inhalation, and frequently used flavor chemicals, such as vanillin, ethyl vanillin, maltol, ethyl maltol, triacetin, benzyl alcohol, and menthol, contribute to EC toxicity. ,, Several GRAS chemicals in our samples, including linalool, acetoin, carvone, and ethyl vanillin, are respiratory irritants that have been implicated in pulmonary toxicity and disease. −

Unvaped and vaped fluids of “Strawberry Mango” from ElfBar and FLUM shared similar dominant flavor chemicals (>1 mg/mL), except for triacetin, which increased in vaped FLUM fluid but decreased in vaped ElfBar. These changes in triacetin were inversely related to the levels of MGO, GO, and formaldehyde, reflecting its thermal degradation to acetic acid and its impact on aldehyde chemistry. , Variations in puffing topographies and device characteristics , are likely contributors to the interproduct differences in triacetin levels.

Synthetic coolants are common as nonflavor additives and masking agents in modern vaping products. − All ElfBar, FLUM, and 77% of “Other Brands” contained WS-23 (mean = 18.4 mg/mL), with >10 mg/mL in all unvaped fluids and 90% of vaped fluids, indicating high abundance in disposables and relative stability during aerosolization. WS-3 appeared infrequently and at lower levels, consistent with prior studies. , Notably, WS-23 was present even in products not marketed as “cool” or “icy,” such as Lime Romance and Sukura Grape in this study and Jungle Juice, Passion Fruit Mango, and Lychee in prior studies. − WS-3 and WS-23 are permitted in food and consumer products at far lower concentrations, such as 3 mg/mL or 3000 ppm in gum, 0.15 mg/mL or 150 ppm in candy, and 0.08 mg/mL or 80 ppm in beverages and baked goods. , Nearly all ElfBar and FLUM products exceeded these limits.

There are a few inhalation toxicology studies on WS-3 and WS-23. However, when tested in vitro with airway models, WS-3 and WS-23 have demonstrated cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammation, actin filament depolymerization, and airway irritation. ,,,, MOE analyses have indicated that coolant levels in fourth-generation ECs fall below the MOE safety threshold of 100, warranting further evaluation for potential health concerns. , In the current study, almost all products yielded MOEs <100 when a 70% aerosol transfer efficiency was assumed, but yielded MOEs >100 at 10% aerosol transfer, indicating that screening-level conclusions for WS-23 are dominated by uncertainty in aerosolization rather than by toxicological potency. This sensitivity highlights the need for product-specific transfer data and inhalation-relevant toxicological benchmarks to refine risk characterization EC.

In many vaped fluids from high puff EC products in this study, MGO and GO increased, sometimes to exceptionally high levels (up to 13.1 mg/mL and 12.2 mg/mL, respectively), far exceeding average concentrations in vaped fluids from SURGE (45 μg/mL and 38 μg/mL) and JUUL (5.2 μg/mL and 3.1 μg/mL), which are low puff number ECs. Elevated levels of MGO and GO likely resulted from thermal degradation of PG and G, − with the resulting aldehydes remaining stable in the solvent once formed. In contrast, volatile aldehydes (formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, and acrolein) showed fluctuations or decreased in vaped fluids likely due to simultaneous formation, degradation, evaporation, and transformation during heating, and conversion into hemiacetals/adducts (e.g., formaldehyde). − The balance/imbalance of these processes are influenced by acids, bases, nicotine, flavor chemicals, and device components, which can affect aldehyde formation/degradation and contribute to the complex aldehyde chemistry observed in residual e-liquids. − Mass change calculations confirmed that increased concentrations of aldehydes in vaped fluids translated into true increases in absolute mass in general. Across products, MGO, GO, dihydroxyacetone, and formaldehyde showed positive net formation, consistent with their thermal generation from PG/G and low volatility, which favors retention in the vaped liquid. In contrast, acrolein, acetaldehyde, and propanal exhibited negative net formation, reflecting their high volatility and reactivity, which promote transfer to aerosol or loss through secondary reactions. These outputs validate the concentration-based findings and clarify which aldehydes can accumulate versus those that are depleted during use. MGO is a highly toxic aldehyde. − During ALI exposure of human airway epithelium followed by proteomics analysis, MGO (100 μg/mL) disrupted multiple cellular processes, including mitochondrial function, fatty acid metabolism, RNA binding, cytoskeletal organization, and DNA repair. Complementary functional assays with BEAS-2B cells confirmed that MGO elevated ROS, impaired mitochondrial activity in the MTT assay via activation of the TRPM8 channel, and depolymerized actin filaments. Cytoskeletal effects were partially mediated by TRPA1 and TRPM8 ion channels, which modulate aldehyde responses and cytoskeletal integrity. , In our study, MGO exposure significantly increased MitoSOX fluorescence in BEAS-2B cells, indicating elevated mitochondrial superoxide and oxidative stress. The increased superoxide production suggests MGO can impair mitochondrial function and overload endogenous antioxidant defenses. In contrast to MGO, acetaldehyde is efficiently detoxified by aldehyde dehydrogenases, , resulting in lower ROS levels and reduced cytotoxicity. Although acetaldehyde disrupted actin in the current study, its effects were weaker and required higher concentrations, underscoring the greater biological impact of MGO.

Human inhalation data for MGO and GO are limited with no permissible exposure limits available. Rodent studies showed acute respiratory tract irritation, − and amplified pulmonary inflammation via oxidative stress and RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end products) signaling. , Hubbs et al. (2019) demonstrated that MGO induced airway necrosis at a far lower level than diacetyl (200 ppm or 200 μg/mL) and 65,500 times lower than concentrations found in some vaped fluids (e.g., 13 mg/mL in Hyppe Bar Peach Ice). These results highlight the cytotoxic potential of MGO and its capacity to induce epithelial injury and amplify inflammatory responses in respiratory tissues.

Conclusion

Fluid volumes and chemical concentrations in fluids from high puff number ECs were analyzed before and after use. Residual volumes averaged ∼2 mL with PG, G, nicotine, flavor chemicals, WS-23, and aldehydes as major constituents. MGO and GO, well-known toxicants, − reached mg/mL levels in some vaped fluids. When tested in vitro, MGO reduced cell viability, induced mitochondrial dysfunction, increased superoxide production, depolymerized actin filaments, leading to cell rounding, and altered cellular and nuclear morphology. MGO was 10–100 times more potent than acetaldehyde. Existing data support the conclusion that vapers should exercise caution when using high puff number devices.

Limitations

For unvaped fluids, the time between manufacture and sample analysis may vary. Field-collected devices may have experienced unknown environmental exposures, leakage or evaporation, contamination, and variable storage durations before collection. Although donated devices provide a reference for controlled environmental conditions due to storage indoors, nonuse-related chemical changes in field-collected samples cannot be fully excluded. The measured aldehyde concentrations in vaped fluids may underestimate peak levels due to aerosol-phase partitioning, chemical loss once vaping stops, and the stability of individual aldehydes. Additionally, some EC brands now market devices capable of delivering up to 50,000 puffs/unit, which may generate higher aldehyde concentrations than those measured in this study. Fluids from the unvaped and vaped products were not paired to individual users, which may have increased variations in the vaped data; nevertheless, our sampling method provides good insight into the range of concentrations of aldehydes that can be found in vaped fluids from high puff-number ECs.
