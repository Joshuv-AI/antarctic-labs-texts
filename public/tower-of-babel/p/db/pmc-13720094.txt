FISIA, an information-based imaging tool, uncovers new spatial distributional patterns in neural circuits beyond fluorescence intensity
Authors: Zhiwei Xu, Wenbo Yang, Cui Zhang, Qiang Sun, Qi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluorescence imaging is a cornerstone of neural circuit mapping, yet conventional intensity-based analysis methods overlook critical spatial distributional information variation, limiting the ability to relate anatomy to function. We developed FISIA (Fluorescence Image Spatial Information Analyzer), an information-based framework that separates signal magnitude from spatial organization and reveals structure that conventional pipelines cannot access. FISIA automates subregional subdivision, Kullback-Leibler Divergence-based distributional variation quantification, Andrews plots for latent factors differentiation, and 2D local fluorescence comparisons. We quantitatively benchmarked FISIA against established tools and showed that only FISIA directly quantified spatial distributional variation and showed stronger anatomy-behavior associations than the comparison tools. Moreover, despite adeno-associated virus (AAV) tracer’s higher fluorescence intensity at the injection epicenter, AAV and biotinylated dextran amine (BDA) tracers encoded similar spatial distributional variation in primary motor cortex (M1). While M1 to the secondary motor cortex’s projections were dense and spatially concentrated, M1 to reticular formation’s projections were dispersed and highly variable, suggesting fundamental differences in mesoscale architectures. In the spinal cord dataset, only FISIA-derived metrics, but not comparison measures, were significantly associated with locomotor recovery. Thus, FISIA provides an information-based, spatially aware, quantitative framework advancing beyond what is bright toward what is biologically organized and functionally informative.Subject terms: Dynamical systems, Biophysical models

Introduction

Understanding neuronal connectivity is fundamental to neuroscience. Traditionally, fluorescence image analysis has relied on intensity-based comparisons, assuming that stronger fluorescence signals correspond to more effective neuronal labeling1–4. However, this assumption fails to account for the spatial distribution of fluorescence, which is critical for accurately interpreting how neural circuits are structured and how projections are organised across brain regions. Yet existing fluorescence imaging methods struggle to provide a complete and quantitative picture of how neurons are spatially organized and connected. Without a computational framework that distinguishes intensity from spatial variation, essential details about neural connectivity remain obscured, leading to potential misinterpretations of tracer behavior and circuit architecture3–12. The limitation of intensity-only analysis is not that fluorescence intensity lacks biological relevance, but that it is typically treated as a global, spatially agnostic scalar. This limitation is especially consequential in injured tissue, where fluorescence is spatially heterogeneous and functionally structured rather than uniformly distributed.

A critical but often overlooked distinction is that fluorescence intensity can be interpreted either as a continuous photometric quantity or as a spatial indicator of neural presence. Conventional software platforms typically preserve the full dynamic range of intensity values, emphasizing fine-grained brightness differences through continuous thresholding and pixel-wise weighting. While appropriate for photometric characterization, this approach can be suboptimal in contexts where biological function depends primarily on whether neural elements are present and spatially integrated, rather than on their absolute signal magnitude. As a result, intensity-only measures often show non-significant correlations with behavioral outcomes, not because intensity is irrelevant.

To address these fundamental limitations, we introduce the Fluorescence Image Spatial Information Analyzer (FISIA) tool, a novel computational platform that enables systematic quantification of fluorescence intensity, spatial distributional variation, and regional connectivity differences, to the best of our knowledge. Unlike conventional approaches that focus on intensity measurements13–15, FISIA integrates a set of comprehensive and diversified analyses covering multiple aspects, including Kullback-Leibler Divergence (KLD) to quantify spatial distributional variation, Andrews plots to identify latent differentiating factors, fluorescence intensity frequency analysis to assess intensity distribution, and two-dimensional fluorescence histograms to enable direct spatial comparisons of fluorescence intensity. Additionally, our implementation of irregular polygon region-of-interest (ROI) selection overcomes the limitations of fixed geometric ROIs, allowing for precise segmentation of neuronal structures.

In addition, FISIA addresses a longstanding methodological gap in neuroscience that studies of neural structure-behavior relationships, such as those after spinal cord injury, have traditionally relied on measures such as varicosity density, axonal length, or manual lesion reconstructions16–18, which are labor-intensive, require multiple layers of manual quantification, fragmented and indirect. FISIA overcomes these limitations by replacing these laborious processes with a streamlined analysis button on standard fluorescence images that were already routinely collected for tracer validation, and generates spatially resolved quantitative information with little knowledge of coding or information theory required. With minimal user burden, FISIA computes spatial entropy and KLD across entire tissue architectures, generating a reproducible and objective link between fluorescence-encoded structure and behavioral function.

Using FISIA, we uncovered patterns that were not readily revealed by conventional fluorescence analysis methods4,5,8,9,19–21. We started by asking whether two tracers could exhibit different fluorescence intensities yet encode similar spatial information, and whether such a result would challenge the long-held practice of quantifying fluorescence intensity without explicit consideration of connectivity mapping. In the primary motor cortex (M1), we demonstrate that adeno-associated virus (AAV) tracer showed higher fluorescence intensity at the injection epicenter. However, both AAV and biotinylated dextran amine (BDA) tracers exhibit similar spatial distributional variation, suggesting that intensity differences do not necessarily indicate distinct connectivity patterns. In the secondary motor cortex (M2), we reveal that projections from M1 are highly concentrated and spatially clustered, contrasting with the dispersed and more spatially variable projections to the reticular formation (RF). Extending this framework beyond cortex to the spinal cord, we establish a direct link between fluorescence distribution patterns and motor recovery, showing that a broader, more evenly distributed neural sprouting pattern is associated with better locomotor function. In our spinal cord dataset, this functional association emerges only when fluorescence intensity is evaluated within FISIA’s spatial framework, while the comparison intensity-only approach did not reach statistical significance despite capturing similar raw signals. In contrast to the continuous photometric quantity of comparison tools, FISIA deliberately suppresses low-level noise and binarizes fluorescence signals to distinguish neural contact from non-neural background, effectively quantifying the spatial occupancy and availability of neural connections. Under this representation, weak but spatially integrated connections contribute meaningfully, whereas isolated high-intensity clusters do not dominate the analysis. This distinction is particularly important in the injured spinal cord, where functional recovery may depend more on the re-establishment of distributed connectivity than on dense or intense labeling at any single location. These findings illustrate the potential importance of broader neural connection range, more diverse neural connections, and spatial distributional variation, rather than signal magnitude alone, in neural circuit analysis and rehabilitation research. This may explain why FISIA-derived intensity measures reach statistical significance in the motor recovery dataset, whereas the comparison continuous intensity metrics fail to do so.

By introducing a spatially aware, quantitative framework for fluorescence image analysis, this study challenges the conventional assumptions underlying neural tracer studies. The ability to distinguish fluorescence intensity from spatial distributional variation aids in supporting connectivity interpretations that are less likely to be driven by imaging artifacts. Our findings have broad implications for neuroscience research, tracer selection, and behavioral functional studies. By providing a standardized computational platform, FISIA enables researchers to compare neural tracers in a rigorous, unbiased manner, ensuring that fluorescence-based connectivity interpretations are based on quantifiable spatial properties rather than intensity alone. Our findings refine the understanding of motor circuits and suggest that different projection patterns may associate with distinct functional roles in movement execution and coordination. FISIA thereby transforms conventional fluorescence imaging from descriptive signal quantification to information-based spatial decoding, enabling neuroscience to move beyond what is visible toward what is structurally and functionally encoded in space.

Graphical User Interface (GUI)-Based Analysis System (GBAS) of FISIA

We implemented a GUI-based Analysis System (GBAS), specifically designed for neuronal distribution analysis and cell count calculations. FISIA offers an intuitive and efficient user interface, leveraging comprehensive analysis capabilities for data processing, information-based calculation, visualization, and statistical testing. FISIA automates dataset traversal, facilitating region-specific computations while allowing users to define regions of interest and customize analytical parameters. The system supports flexible subdivision and region selection, ensuring adaptability to various experimental designs. Users can rectify selection errors and manage datasets by retaining or discarding files as needed. Comprehensive results are presented through interactive figures and charts, with options for exporting graphical and numerical outputs. Additionally, GBAS accommodates custom analytical scripts, enhancing compatibility with lab-specific computational workflows. The workflow overview of FISIA is shown in Fig. 1a.

Develop custom ImageJ pre-processing script and plugin

To streamline image preprocessing, we developed a custom ImageJ script and plugin as part of the FISIA pipeline, designed for efficient segmentation and thresholding of fluorescently labeled neuronal images. This plugin enhances visualization, reduces manual intervention, and standardizes preprocessing across datasets. Users can apply the plugin to process images of injected fluorescent viruses, facilitating the analysis of neuronal transmission and potential damage. The script and plugin operate through a structured workflow (Fig. 1a) and execute the following ImageJ processes:

1. Image Channels Segmentation: Load the raw fluorescent image into the ImageJ plugin and separate its channels using the “Make Composite” option. The plugin automatically divides the layers into distinct components.

2. Channel Arrangement: The “Arrange Channels…” function enables users to organize fluorescent images, ensuring proper visualization of virus-injected regions (e.g., green or red color channels, Fig. 1b, c).

3. Threshold Adjustment: The plugin provides an interactive threshold adjustment dialog, allowing users to refine fluorescence intensity. It executes the “Brightness/Contrast…” function to reduce background noise, and then uses the “Apply LUT” function to apply the selected threshold for improved contrast.

4. Background Modification: The background is converted to black via the “Black Background” function, enhancing the clarity of fluorescent signals.

5. Binarization: The plugin performs automatic binarization via the “Convert to Mask” function, generating a black-and-white binary image.

6. LUT Inversion: The plugin then reverses the black-and-white contrast using “Invert LUTs”, producing a final preprocessed image optimized for subsequent analysis.

This plugin eliminates the need for extensive manual adjustments and ensures reproducibility in preprocessing workflows. Additionally, we provide the plugin’s customizable ImageJ script, allowing users to modify image selections and threshold parameters to suit specific experimental requirements (Fig. 1d, e).

Note that in step “3. Threshold Adjustment”, the threshold adjustment step is explicitly used to mitigate saturation effects, particularly at sites where fluorescence plateaus can obscure spatial heterogeneity. There are three artifact mitigation options: automatic adjustment in FISIA, manual thresholding in FISIA, and manual thresholding via a customized ImageJ/FIJI plugin (users’ instruction manual as Supplementary File 1), to adjust thresholds to suppress saturated intensity plateaus while preserving weak but spatially contiguous fluorescence signals. This preprocessing ensures that binarized representations reflected the presence or absence of labeled neural elements rather than raw brightness, thereby preventing saturation-driven inflation of pixel counts in subsequent spatial analyses.

ROI selection

The ROI selection module in FISIA enables users to define and analyze specific regions within fluorescence images. Within FISIA’s GUI-based interface, users can select multiple images and define a two-dimensional ROI in the reference image using intuitive selection tools. The ROI can be drawn manually as an irregular polygon, or as rectangular ROIs subdivided according to the user-defined numbers of rows and columns. By default, to ensure uniform analysis, the selected region is divided into equal fractions. Only the area within the selection is processed for analysis, while unselected areas are assigned a value of zero and displayed in gray. Segmentation lines and spatial markers are applied for improved visualization and quantification. Manual fine-tuning of image divisions is supported to improve accuracy, allowing users to optimize fluorescence-labeled neuron comparisons across regions. If two images are analyzed at the same time, ROI selection in one image is automatically propagated to the corresponding location in the other image.

The system supports interactive fine-tuning of denoising algorithms and parameter adjustments to optimize the balance between noise reduction and image clarity. Following ROI selection, the processed data and task-specific information are stored in a MATLAB (.mat) file, serving as the primary dataset for subsequent analysis. All graphical outputs and image-based statistical computations are derived from this file, providing direct access to pixel values for quantitative evaluation.

In the results presented in this study, to minimize subjective bias, cortical ROIs were selected with reference to Allen Brain Atlas registration (Supplementary Fig. 2) to obtain the same ROI across different tissue sections. During co-registration with the Allen Brain Atlas, we identified local anatomical landmarks, such as a small “dent”, “protrusion”, “bent”, etc., in the ROI area and used these landmarks to manually place ROIs across different tools and repeated trials. We illustrated the anatomical landmarks using the Cross software, behavior-level quantitative comparisons analysis as an example (Supplementary Fig. 3). To quantitatively assess robustness of the manual ROI placement, we repeated landmark-guided manual ROI selection used in the Cross software, behavior-level quantitative comparisons analysis six independent times on five different brain sections and compared the resulting outputs with the exact computer code cropped reference ROI. Measurements obtained from repeated manual ROI selection in FISIA, ImageJ/FIJI, and Ilastik remained close to those from the exact reference ROI across sections with no significant difference (p = 0.32, Supplementary Data 1), indicating that the main conclusions were not driven by minor variation in manual ROI placement.

ROI definitions used for cross-software, behavior-level quantitative comparison analysis were anatomically guided and fixed before quantification. The identical ROI was then applied across FISIA, ImageJ/FIJI, Ilastik, and all process-inspired perturbation variants of the same image, ensuring a matched-input comparison. We used customized code to crop the exact ROI area at the pixel-level precision to ensure that the identical ROI was selected and comparisons demonstrated differences in analytical response rather than differences in region selection.

Metadata labeling to compare fluorescent expression using FISIA

Utilizing FISIA, we investigated the effects of AAV injection and fluorescent protein expression in the cortex, reticular formation, and spinal cord, and compared them with BDA-derived tracing patterns. We quantified AAV labeling and compared it with BDA-derived fluorescence patterns across neural pathways. Users can customize data organization by adding detailed experimental metadata, including the experiment name, subject number, grouping labels, event timestamps, and brain region of virus injection (e.g., M1 area, reticular formation). Additionally, FISIA allows fine-grained partitioning and region segmentation for more precise selection of neural regions, facilitating detailed comparative analyses. This functionality supports neural pathway mapping and enhances the evaluation of neuronal tracing methodologies24.

Fluorescence intensity

Following ROI identification, neuronal fluorescence intensity is quantified based on pixel-based segmentation. The quantification process uses a binarized, pixel-occupancy-based representation rather than raw grayscale intensity. After thresholding and binarization, pixels are classified as fluorescence-positive or negative, enabling quantification of labeled neural presence while suppressing variability arising from imaging gain, tracer expression level, and saturation artifacts. This design intentionally prioritizes spatial occupancy over absolute brightness because our goal was to extract spatial information rather than photometric intensity. FISIA automatically calculates the number, size, and area of neurons using an adaptive segmentation tool, facilitating precise cell counting. The software then processes all selected images, generating output metrics, including the quantified area, cell count, total occupied area, and fluorescence area. Regions with no fluorescence, indicating the absence of labeled neurons, are displayed in gray to assist users in qualitative assessment of neuronal fluorescence distribution. Users can flexibly magnify selected regions using the mouse wheel to interact with GUI-based zoom functions for detailed image inspection. Fluorescence intensity (FI) heatmap

FISIA can analyze spatial information within a single fluorescence image, compare across different brain regions in one image, and between two images. To facilitate comparative fluorescence analysis, FISIA generates heatmaps that visualize the fluorescence intensity distribution across user-defined regions. In the heatmap analysis, we normalize maximum fluorescence values across different regions, enabling comparability between experimental conditions. Darker colors represent lower fluorescence intensities, while lighter colors indicate higher values, providing an intuitive representation of fluorescence distribution, anomalies, and potential correlations.

To improve quantitative interpretation, numerical values are overlaid on the heatmap. By comparing heatmaps, researchers can identify brain regions affected by different fluorescent protein virus injections, allowing for a detailed evaluation of their transmission efficiency, infection specificity, and potential non-specific labeling. Heatmap normalization is a required step for spatial comparison across images with different absolute fluorescence intensities. Normalization rescales fluorescence occupancy values so that spatial heterogeneity within each image can be directly compared without dominance from global brightness differences or saturation effects. Both normalized and unnormalized heatmaps are generated by FISIA to allow transparent inspection of raw signal magnitude alongside normalized spatial structure. Fluorescence intensity variations and spatial information distribution

Following region selection, FISIA automatically conducts statistical tests on fluorescence intensity variations and spatial information distribution. FISIA extracts maximum intensity values and computes the KLD29, a mathematical measure quantifying differences between two probability distributions, from user-defined areas of interest. The average of these values serves as the reference distribution, providing a baseline for comparison. Because KLD is computed from normalized probability distributions, it is mathematically invariant to global intensity scaling and therefore insensitive to saturation artifacts that uniformly affect large contiguous regions. FISIA displays both the normalized and unnormalized heatmaps of the second image.

To ensure data integrity, FISIA performs statistical tests on ROIs defined by the users without incorporating non-selected regions. It will also alert users about imbalanced datasets. Fluorescence intensity trends

To analyze multivariate fluorescence spatial information differences, FISIA employs Andrews curves, a visualization technique that transforms KLD numerical data into distinct continuous curves through a specific encoding process. The difference between two images can thus be visualized as the separation of their corresponding curves in the Andrews plot, allowing researchers to observe intricate latent patterns and relationships across two images. To enhance interpretability, FISIA assigns a consistent color scheme to each image being compared. Fluorescence intensity distribution

FISIA employs histograms to analyze fluorescence intensity distributions across different regions. By visualizing intensity variations within data samples, histograms provide insights into central tendencies and distribution ranges. Histograms serve two key purposes: data normality evaluation and comparative analysis. For the data normality evaluation, histograms help assess whether the fluorescence intensity data follows a normal distribution, enabling the detection of outliers or extreme values. For the comparative analysis, histograms compare distributions across different staining methods, brain regions, and experimental conditions. This approach enables the identification of small-scale variations, aiding in the assessment of fluorescence intensity differences between regions in the same image and between different images.

The fluorescence intensities of the selected ROIs are thus compared between two images. Within each ROI, the image area is partitioned into two or three equal segments, followed by paired t tests to evaluate statistical significance. The fluorescence intensity distribution is then visualized using a three-dimensional histogram, spanning the ROI from the top-left to the bottom-right30. Statistically significant differences are marked with asterisks as visual indicators of intensity variations between two images. This method facilitates the quantitative evaluation of staining method efficiency, regional fluorescence intensity differences, and potential biases in labeling.

Gait analysis using the irregular horizontal ladder rung walking test

Adult female SD rats underwent C4 spinal cord injury and were randomly assigned to a rehabilitation group and a control group, n = 4 animals per group, n = 8 in total. The rehabilitation group animals received paired cortex and spinal cord epidural stimulation, which we and other researchers have shown could significantly improve motor recovery17,18. The control group received no treatment. Animals’ gait performance was assessed using the irregular horizontal ladder rung walking test31. We quantified the number of total steps, long stride steps, and uncoordinated steps. A long stride step was defined as any step in which the hindlimb advancement was longer than the median of all steps’ length taken by the animal during baseline (before injury/intact) stage. An uncoordinated step was defined as any step in which the animal’s hind paws failed to grasp the rung properly, which corresponded to steps classified other than “good” in the irregular horizontal ladder rung walking test31, including “miss”, “digit” and “wrist” placements, etc. Step counts were quantified at baseline (before injury/intact), early stage (10 weeks post rehabilitation/13 weeks post injury), mid-stage (20 weeks post rehabilitation/23 weeks post injury), and late-stage periods (30 weeks post injury/33 weeks post injury) for both rehabilitation and control groups. These behavioral outcomes were subsequently correlated with FISIA-derived metrics extracted from corresponding spinal cord sections to establish anatomical-functional relationships.

Benchmarking against established fluorescence quantification tools

To evaluate the analytical performance and distinctiveness of FISIA relative to established fluorescence analysis platforms, we conducted systematic benchmarking against ImageJ/FIJI32,33, Ilastik34, NeuronStudio35,36, and Imaris. Each tool represents a prevailing computational strategy within the field of intensity-based quantification, supervised categorical segmentation, and single-neuron morphometric reconstruction. All comparisons were performed using the same images and ROIs as analyzed by FISIA, including cortical and spinal cord fluorescence-labeled tissues by AAV and BDA. FISIA’s analyses were performed in its unsupervised mode using identical ROI definitions, enabling direct comparison across methods.

To compare with ImageJ/FIJI, fluorescence quantification in ImageJ/FIJI was performed using standard workflows. Grayscale conversion and manual ROI selection were carried out using the polygon selection tool. Quantitative parameters including “mean gray value”, “min & max gray value”, “integrated density”, “area”, etc. were extracted. Histograms were generated to visualize fluorescence intensity distributions. This approach provided conventional pixel-intensity measurements and distribution profiles for each ROI. Unlike the proposed FISIA, FIJI does not incorporate subregional spatial analysis, entropy quantification, or multidimensional statistical modeling; thus, these analysis results were inaccessible in FIJI. These comparisons confirmed that while FIJI and Ilastik captured absolute intensity differences, FISIA selectively suppressed their dominance to reveal spatial organization, explaining why normalized spatial metrics, but not raw intensity, correlated with anatomical and behavioral outcomes.

To compare with Ilastik, we employed Ilastik’s “Cell Density Counting” workflow, which uses a supervised random forest classifier for pixel-wise segmentation. Training datasets were manually annotated to define foreground and background classes. Feature selection included color, edge, and texture filters across Gaussian σ levels between 0.7 and 1.6. Classifier training and application were performed according to the developer’s standard protocol; after manually defining the ROIs, it generated neuron number count estimates. While Ilastik efficiently distinguished labeled neurons with fluorescence from background, it required user-dependent training and yielded categorical, rather than continuous, representations of fluorescence distributions, and its accuracy was impacted when neurons’ size and appearance were highly variable, which was likely or expected in injured neural tissues.

To compare with NeuronStudio, we followed the software’s established 3D morphometric reconstruction protocol. Since NeuronStudio cannot directly process images in JPG format downloaded from Allen’s Institute, we extracted individual fluorescence channels and converted them to 8-bit grayscale before import. Neuronal structures were reconstructed manually by tracing individual arbors and assigning branch points for total length and branching complexity quantification. This approach enabled precise single-cell reconstruction, but was constrained by its reliance on sparsely labeled images and its inability to quantify dense or overlapping fluorescence signals typical of mesoscale tracer images.

We further benchmarked FISIA against a widely used commercial platform for neuronal reconstruction, Imaris (Bitplane, Oxford Instruments), focusing on its filament tracing workflow. The same cortical fluorescence images analyzed by FISIA were used for Imaris-based reconstruction to ensure direct comparability. Fluorescence images were first preprocessed in ImageJ/FIJI and converted to 32-bit TIFF format before import into Imaris. A filament object was initialized using the “Add New Filaments” tool, and reconstruction was performed using the AutoPath algorithm with the option “calculate diameter of filaments from image” enabled. The green fluorescence channel was selected as the source channel for tracing. Automatic soma and dendrite detection thresholds were initially applied but produced extensive spurious detections in densely labeled regions. To mitigate this, all auto-generated seed points were removed manually, and soma centers and terminal endpoints were also manually placed before initiating automatic tracing. We visualized reconstructed filaments for continuity, branching topology, and correspondence to the underlying fluorescence signal. We evaluated the tracing outcomes qualitatively with respect to the coverage of the injected region and quantitatively with respect to the proportion of fluorescence signal that could be represented by reconstructed filaments.

Quantitative benchmarking on images with perturbation

To obtain a quantitative benchmark, we performed controlled perturbation analyses using FISIA, ImageJ/FIJI, and Ilastik on identical green channel ROIs from five Allen Brain Atlas sections. NeuronStudio and Imaris were not included in the quantitative comparison because their filament reconstruction workflows do not directly quantify total fluorescence intensity within a matched ROI. Their performance was therefore evaluated with respect to reconstructable signal coverage, required manual intervention, etc. (Table 1). We applied four classes of perturbations to each original image (Supplementary Data 4). The perturbation of fluorescence signal attrition simulated fluorescence signal loss potentially caused by heterogeneous tracer uptake or transport, tissue disruption during injection or sectioning, incomplete paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixation, quenching during storage, local bleaching during imaging, compression or export-associated signal dropout, by removing the locally strongest signal pixels at levels from 10% to 80%. The perturbation of global fluorescence intensity linear gain simulated field-wide signal amplification potentially introduced by laser power, detector sensitivity, exposure time, section thickness, staining efficiency or post acquisition normalization, by applying multiplicative scaling factors from 2.0 to 3.0. The perturbation of nonlinear remapping simulated monotonic intensity distortions potentially introduced by detector response characteristics, gamma correction, LUT application, histogram remapping, bit depth conversion, or software-dependent export processing, using γ from 0.10 to 0.60. The perturbation of bright-range remapping simulated the compression or expansion of higher intensity points potentially caused by auto-contrast adjustment, highlight compression, saturation roll-off, denoising, or file format conversion, using γ ranging from 0.10 to 0.60. After perturbation, all original and perturbed images were analyzed with the same predefined ROI. We compared FISIA-derived KLD and total fluorescent-positive pixel count with total fluorescence intensity measured by ImageJ/FIJI and Ilastik.

Statistics and reproducibility

FISIA has built-in statistical testing functions. For subregion and voxel-level fluorescence comparisons, Bonferroni correction is applied automatically within the FISIA pipeline to control for multiple comparisons across spatial subdivisions. Both uncorrected and corrected p values are reported through the built-in function of FISIA. FISIA automatically exports a complete statistical summary file that includes the test statistic (t or F), degrees of freedom, p values, 95% confidence intervals, etc. All statistical tests are two-tailed, with significance set at p < 0.05. For each figure and Supplementary Fig., detailed statistics, including the tests used, mean, SD, n, 95% confidence interval, t, df, p value, Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons, effect size, statistical power, Pearson correlation values, and individual data points where applicable, are provided in Supplementary Data 1–15. Effect sizes were calculated following standard conventions37. Post-hoc power analyses were performed using G*Power 3.138. The unit of statistical inference was the tissue section or animal. The spatial subdivisions within an ROI were not treated as independent biological replicates to avoid pseudoreplication. Subregion-level statistical tests were used only to localize within image spatial differences and were corrected for multiple comparisons as described above.

Results

AAV tracer shows higher fluorescence intensity than BDA at injection epicenter

To validate the efficacy of FISIA, we used M1 neural tracing from the Allen Brain Atlas. We compared the diffusion patterns of two distinct tracers, AAV and BDA, both injected into the same mouse (Fig. 3). FISIA automated ImageJ-based script and plugin pipeline processed the tracers overlapping image into black and white binary images, one image for the AAV injected fluorescent channel (Fig. 3b), one for BDA (Fig. 3c). These images were binarized after threshold adjustment, encoding fluorescence occupancy rather than grayscale intensity, thereby reducing sensitivity to imaging gain and saturation artifacts. The FISIA automatically processed binary images, capturing the fluorescence down to the pixel resolution level. The selected ROI was magnified and divided into user-defined 6×6 sub-regions for AAV (Fig. 3d) and BDA (Fig. 3e) tracing, respectively, for detailed fluorescence intensity and spatial information distribution comparisons.

FISIA rapidly quantified and visualized fluorescence intensity data within the selected M1 region, providing a comprehensive analysis of tracer diffusion patterns and spatial relationships (Fig. 3f–m). AAV (Fig. 3d, f) produced stronger fluorescence at the injection epicenter than BDA (Fig. 3e, k) as indicated by the colors and values in the heatmaps. This difference reflected absolute tracer brightness and expression efficiency rather than spatial organization. For analyses such as KLD, the number of fluorescent-positive pixels in each sub-region of the second image was quantified, and the normalized numbers were displayed in heatmaps (Fig. 3g). These labeled values corresponded to normalized maxima, not raw fluorescence intensity. We therefore explicitly distinguished raw, unnormalized representations such as in Fig. 3k or Supplementary Fig. 4k from normalized spatial maps used for cross-image comparison such as in Fig. 3g or Supplementary Fig. 4g. Normalization preserved relative subregional proportions within each image while preventing saturation at the injection epicenter from dominating spatial metrics. AAV tracer yielded more fluorescent-positive epicenter areas than BDA (Fig. 3k, l, m, Supplementary Data 5). Consistently, low intensity bins were more frequent in the BDA tracer images, whereas AAV exhibited a larger contiguous epicenter of fluorescence occupancy (Fig. 3j). Our results demonstrated that AAV produced higher fluorescence intensity than BDA at the injection site and in certain surrounding regions. These differences reflected tracer-specific labeling efficiency and diffusion near the injection core, not differences in mesoscale spatial topology.

FISIA decouples spatial connectivity from tracer-specific intensity differences and reveals equivalent organization of AAV and BDA

Although AAV and BDA tracers exhibited clear differences in fluorescence intensity at the injection epicenter, with AAV producing stronger local fluorescence than BDA, conventional intensity-only analysis would interpret these differences as reflecting distinct connectivity strengths or labeling efficacy. FISIA was additionally explicitly designed to avoid this conflation by separating fluorescence magnitude from spatial organization. Thus, we sought to determine whether AAV and BDA tracers carried the same amount of neural spatial distribution information. To assess this, we analyzed their KLD values, which quantified differences in the probability distributions of fluorescence. A higher KLD value indicated greater variation between sub-regions, reflecting a more heterogeneous and uneven fluorescence distribution, while a lower KLD value suggested minimal differences among sub-regions, corresponding to a more uniform fluorescence distribution. Our results showed that AAV and BDA tracers had similar spatial distribution variation, as their KLD values were not significantly different from each other (p = 0.0518, Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Data 6). This was further supported by the Andrews plot, that there was no clear separation of curves of the two tracers (Fig. 3i). Thus, our results indicated that AAV and BDA tracers carried similar amounts of spatial distributional information and revealed comparable spatial organization of neural structures despite intensity differences. AAV might be advantageous for detecting subtle structures because of its higher local fluorescence intensity.

Here, to avoid the result being an artifact of preprocessing, careful thresholding and binarization were applied uniformly to mitigate saturation through automatic and manual threshold adjustment options (Methods and users’ instruction manual in Supplementary File 1). Moreover, KLD was computed from normalized distributions, ensuring that spatial metrics reflected how fluorescence was distributed across regions rather than how bright it was at any single location.

These findings suggested that tracer-specific brightness differences did not necessarily correspond to differences in anatomical connectivity. By suppressing intensity bias while preserving spatial structure, FISIA revealed shared underlying anatomical organization that would otherwise be overestimated or misclassified by intensity-only approaches. Thus, these results suggested that connectivity-relevant information was better encoded in spatial structure than by raw intensity alone. This decoupling of fluorescence magnitude from spatial topology enabled FISIA to reduce the likelihood of tracer-induced false positives that tracer-specific brightness differences would be interpreted as differences in spatial organization.

Trans-regional transmission patterns of AAV and BDA

M1 neurons are known to relay information to various brain regions, including M2 and RF. How M1-originating neural projections differ between M2 and RF, and do these targets show similar spatial distributional information variation? After revealing the fluorescence intensity and spatial distribution patterns of AAV and BDA tracers at M1, we utilized FISIA to further analyze the fluorescence intensity and distribution of neural sprouts at M2 (Fig. 4b, e, h) and RF (Fig. 4c, f, i) after AAV and BDA injection at the M1 region in mice (Fig. 4a, d, g). Our analysis revealed distinct differences in the intensity and distribution of AAV and BDA tracer diffusion from M1 to M2 and RF. While both tracers spread from M1, we observed that M2 exhibited a denser and more localized distribution (Figs. 4k, 5a, e–g), whereas RF displayed a broader and more scattered pattern (Fig. 4i, l). This suggested denser and more concentrated labeling from M1 to M2, and sparser, broader neural sprout distribution from M1 to RF. Such differential patterns of tracer propagation might reflect the intricate interplay between brain architecture and functionality, thereby providing additional quantitative insight into the organization and operational dynamics of neuronal networks.

Above, we showed that at the injection site in M1, AAV and BDA tracers showed similar neuronal spatial distributional information variation, but AAV tracer has higher intensity in the epicenter area than BDA. Would this pattern continue when assessing neural projections at M2 and RF? We compared the fluorescence intensity and spatial distributional variation between AAV and BDA tracers in M2 (Fig. 7) and RF (Fig. 8), respectively. Despite its higher fluorescence intensity (Fig. 7f, g, j–l, 9a, Supplementary Data 11) at M2, AAV exhibited slightly less diffusion variation compared to BDA (Figs. 7h, 9b, Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Data 12). For RF, where the neural projection was sparse and scattered itself, there was little difference between AAV and BDA tracers (Fig. 8, Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Data 13, 14). We further explored whether AAV and BDA tracers had tracer-specific brain area preferences, that is, whether one tracer preferentially labeled one projection target relative to another. Thus, we compared the fluorescence intensity ratio of M2:RF for AAV and BDA tracers, respectively. Our result showed that for both AAV and BDA tracers, the M2:RF fluorescence intensity ratio was 6.40±1.57 and 6.12±1.71 (Fig. 9d), despite small variation in different brain slices (Fig. 9c). These results suggested that while AAV produced higher local fluorescence intensity, BDA exhibited greater trans-regional spread, allowing it to cover a broader neuronal network following injection.

Hindlimb gait pattern alterations as compensation for spinal cord injury

We designed FISIA with the ultimate goal of associating function with anatomy. Having established its capacity to quantify spatial organization in cortical projections, we extended the same framework to another part of the central nervous system, the spinal cord. Particularly, we aimed to use the same spatial information metrics to capture injury-induced neural remodeling and associate these metrics with functional outcomes in motor behaviors. As a first step toward this goal, we analyzed gait patterns following cervical spinal cord injury, focusing on compensatory locomotor strategies rather than overt paralysis.

We examined rats subjected to a C4 spinal cord injury, which produced a central cavitation consistent with mild-to-moderate lesions (Fig. 10a–e). Following injury, a subset of animals received ten days of paired cortical and spinal epidural stimulation rehabilitation, while control animals received no intervention. Although C4 injury primarily affected forelimb function, hindlimb locomotion was also subtly altered. These changes were often difficult to directly associate with cervical spinal cord anatomy, making hindlimb gait an ideal test case for whether FISIA-derived spatial metrics can bridge anatomy and behavior.

FISIA reveals motor function’s relationship to the spatial organization of spinal cord remodeling.

Scale bar: 10 μm. Error bar: STD.

We quantified injured animals’ motor recovery level using gait performance in the horizontal ladder rung walking test across baseline, early stage, mid-stage, and late-stage time points (Fig. 10f–i). To anchor post-injury gait changes to each cohort’s pre-injury motor repertoire rather than imposing an arbitrary threshold, gait performance was evaluated using percentage of long stride steps and uncoordinated steps as detailed in the Methods. Long stride steps were steps longer than the median of all steps’ length before injury at baseline. Uncoordinated steps were improper placement of paws on the ladder rung, steps classified other than “good” as in the irregular horizontal ladder walking test31. A total of 4743 steps from eight animals were obtained: 689, 654, 522, and 586 steps from four animals in the rehabilitation group; 603, 584, 572, and 533 steps from the control group at baseline, early, middle, and late stages, respectively.

Our gait analysis revealed a significant reduction in elongated stride pattern in rehabilitation rats after injury, possibly the result of post-injury locomotor adaptations in response to effective treatment (Fig. 10f, rehabilitation group, baseline vs. late stage, p = 0.046), whereas the control group did not (p = 0.635). This alteration suggested that compensatory mechanisms might be engaged following spinal cord injury, reflecting the complex interactions between neural circuits and motor control. With rehabilitation, animals exhibited fewer instances of prolonged gait patterns compared to untreated counterparts (Fig. 10g). By late stage post-injury, the rehabilitation group exhibited a significantly lower proportion of long steps than controls (p = 0.045), while this did not differ between rehabilitation and control groups at baseline (p = 0.160). These results indicated that therapeutic interventions might alleviate hindlimb locomotor impairments, potentially promoting functional recovery through neural plasticity mechanisms.

Thus, rehabilitation likely did not simply restore pre-injury gait but selectively suppressed exaggerated stride-length strategies. This did not posit a direct causal relationship between cervical spinal cord histology and hindlimb gait kinematics. Given the anatomical distance and distributed circuitry underlying locomotion, such a claim would be neither mechanistically justified nor experimentally supported. Instead, we treated hindlimb gait alterations as a quantifiable behavioral signature of post-injury compensation, reflecting system-level adaptations to disrupted descending control rather than localized tissue damage. In this manner, we deliberately decoupled gross lesion anatomy from behavioral output, establishing a statistically robust functional phenotype against which FISIA-derived spatial metrics could be evaluated. This framing allowed us to test whether spatial organization of neural remodeling predicted recovery-relevant behavior, rather than merely correlating anatomy with impairment severity.

FISIA-derived intensity and spatial metrics, but not comparison measures, correlate significantly with functional recovery after spinal cord injury

Using the same spinal cord fluorescence images, the same animals, and identical ROIs, we directly compared the ability of FISIA-derived metrics and conventional fluorescence analysis pipelines to correlate with motor recovery after C4 spinal cord injury. For FISIA, both spatial and intensity-based metrics were extracted from binarized fluorescence images of spinal cord sections. Conventional analyses were performed using tools that quantified fluorescence intensity, ImageJ/FIJI and Ilastik, on the same images and ROIs.

We have shown in the previous study that the neural sprout distribution was broader and located differently after treatment compared to the non-treatment group, alongside better forelimb motor recovery18. Hindlimbs were also influenced by C4 spinal cord injury. However, the impact on hindlimb motor function was subtle and hard to find direct connections with C4 spinal cord tissue, posing a challenge to associate hindlimb gait with anatomy. Here we probed their relationship with FISIA quantitatively and showed that FISIA-derived spatial and intensity metrics retained stronger associations with behavior than the comparison tools tested here (Supplementary Data 15).

Applying FISIA to fluorescence images from corresponding spinal sections revealed that the rehabilitated animals exhibited higher fluorescence intensity and broader, spatially more homogeneous distributions of preserved fluorescence, indicating more extensive neural sprouting on the ventral side of the spinal cord (Fig. 10b, d) than in the control group (Fig. 10c, e). These spatial metrics were tightly coupled with behavioral performance.

Our results showed that animals exhibiting lower KLD values, greater spatial homogeneity (Fig. 10h), and higher neural fluorescence intensity (Fig. 10i) displayed fewer uncoordinated steps and better locomotor coordination, and hence better motor recovery. FISIA-derived spatial divergence, quantified by KLD, showed a strong and statistically significant correlation with motor recovery scores measured by gait performance (Pearson correlation r = 0.77, p = 0.013, Fig. 10h). FISIA-derived fluorescence intensity also correlated significantly with functional recovery (r = -0.71, p = 0.025, Fig. 10i). The same analytical framework applied in the cortex above was used here without modification, demonstrating that FISIA generalized seamlessly across the central nervous system. This cross-scale consistency suggested that motor recovery might depend not only on the number of neural sprouts, but also on how widely and evenly they were distributed. We also conducted additional validation that this also held for long stride steps vs. FISIA-derived KLD values (r = 0.78, p = 0.011, Supplementary Fig. 10a) and fluorescence intensity (r = -0.72, p = 0.021, Supplementary Fig. 10b).

In contrast, intensity only measurements obtained using ImageJ/FIJI exhibited a similar directional trend of uncoordinated steps vs. anatomy (Supplementary Fig. 11), but did not reach statistical significance (r = -0.53, p = 0.091, Supplementary Fig. 11c). Ilastik derived fluorescence intensity measures likewise (Supplementary Fig. 12) failed to show significant correlation with behavioral outcomes (r = -0.30, p = 0.234, Supplementary Fig. 12k). Spatial divergence metrics analogous to KLD were not available in ImageJ/FIJI or Ilastik. Across all analyses, the input data were identical, including fluorescence images, animal cohorts, and anatomical ROIs. The only difference lay in the analytical framework applied to the data. Under these controlled conditions, only FISIA-derived metrics reached statistical significance for association with functional recovery following spinal cord injury in the dataset and among the comparison tools tested here. One possible reason that only FISIA showed a significant correlation between fluorescence intensity and functional motor behavior was that it deliberately suppressed low-level noise and binarized fluorescence signals to distinguish neural contact from non-neural background, thus estimating connectivity presence and spatial availability, whereas comparison intensity pipelines estimated signal magnitude. In biological contexts such as sparse neural connections after spinal cord injury, the existence of a connection mattered more than how bright it was.

These findings emphasized the importance of integrating both anatomical and behavioral assessments to evaluate the efficacy of treatment strategies. Understanding the relationship between neural damage, compensation, and rehabilitation would provide valuable insights for developing targeted therapeutic approaches for spinal cord injury recovery. The FISIA-derived metrics thus provided continuous valued quantitative indices of neural remodeling that were significantly associated with functional outcomes in this dataset, a feature not captured by the comparison intensity-based methods used here.

Comparison with other tools

We further conducted benchmarking to demonstrate that FISIA established a new analytical dimension in fluorescence imaging to the best of our knowledge, by integrating spatial information into a unified quantitative framework. To assess its performance relative to existing tools, we conducted side-by-side comparisons with existing tools including ImageJ/FIJI, Ilastik, NeuronStudio, and Imaris (Table 1) using the same cortical AAV and BDA fluorescence images, as well as spinal cord images.

With FIJI’s workflow (Supplementary Fig. 13), the resulting measurements in the cortical tissues accurately reflected global signal intensity, with histograms representing average brightness across manually defined ROIs (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). However, ImageJ/FIJI did not capture subregional heterogeneity or spatial structure, whereas FISIA partitioned each ROI into spatial subregions, revealing non-uniform intensity gradients and spatial distribution patterns that encoded mesoscale anatomical organization. In spinal cord tissues, FIJI quantified overall neuronal fluorescence differences between rehabilitated and control groups and showed the rehabilitated animals had fewer uncoordinated steps and more fluorescence in spinal cord tissues than the controls (Supplementary Fig. 11c), consistent with our findings with FISIA. Yet with FIJI, the outlier variability remained high, and the distinction of fluorescence intensities between rehabilitated animals’ and controls’ tissues was weaker than that with FISIA. FISIA, in contrast, not only showed clearer separation in both behavioral and spinal cord tissue fluorescence between the two groups of animals, but also identified statistically significant subregional divergence in fluorescence organization that paralleled behavioral outcomes, demonstrating the capacity to detect features beyond mean intensity measures in this dataset.

We also benchmarked Ilastik to evaluate its supervised machine learning approach (Supplementary Fig. 12a–h) in comparison to FISIA’s unsupervised quantitative framework. Using identical fluorescence images from M1, M2 and RF regions, Ilastik successfully segmented neuronal and background signals, and produced categorical cell density maps after manual labeling and classifier training (Supplementary Fig. 12i). This workflow required manual adjustment of Gaussian filter parameters σ (Supplementary Fig. 12e) and user-defined annotation of neurons and background (Supplementary Fig. 12g). It yielded total cell count estimates that were higher in M1 and M2 than in RF (Supplementary Fig. 12j), consistent with FISIA’s findings on regional projection density. However, Ilastik’s classification logic inherently reduced fluorescence variation to discrete categories, thereby obscuring the continuous spatial topology and local intensity gradients that characterized dense projection fields. When applied to spinal cord sections, Ilastik’s categorical output failed to delineate fine-grained structural organization and produced weaker separation between rehabilitated and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 12k) than observed in FISIA analyses. This discrepancy likely reflected Ilastik’s Random Forest classifier’s dependence on training data, which struggled to accommodate the morphological irregularities and heterogeneous fluorescence patterns of injured tissue34. In contrast, FISIA directly quantified spatial entropy and KLD across ROIs, producing continuous-valued measures that captured graded spatial variability across cortical and spinal regions. These metrics revealed structured fluorescence organization and entropy-driven heterogeneity that reflected the anatomical and behavioral gradients. This capacity to quantify spatial information from dense labeling fields without classifier bias or manual intervention underscored FISIA’s strength as a data-driven and less biased framework for analyzing fluorescence images.

We also evaluated NeuronStudio for comparison with FISIA in analyzing mesoscale tracer distributions (Supplementary Fig. 14). NeuronStudio reconstructed individual neuronal arbors effectively in sparsely labeled fields but was not designed to process the densely labeled cortical and subcortical tracer images common in neural circuit functional manipulation studies and tracing studies such as the ones from Allen’s Institute. NeuronStudio’s reconstruction was confined to isolated axonal arbors in the periphery of injection sites, as dense labeling prevented accurate tracing of overlapping neuronal structures (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). The quantification results were therefore biased toward sparsely labeled peripheral regions, leaving the majority of fluorescence signal at the injection epicenter unmeasured (Supplementary Fig. 14c). FISIA, in contrast, directly quantified spatial information within these densely labeled territories, capturing continuous patterns of clustering, dispersion, and regional divergence that were inaccessible to reconstruction-based methods. This ability to extract mesoscale spatial structure from densely labeled images connected directly to FISIA’s central finding that the organization of fluorescence, rather than its total intensity, reflected connectivity and circuit topology.

We further evaluated Imaris to assess how a commercial filament reconstruction platform performed on the same densely labeled cortical fluorescence images analyzed by FISIA (Supplementary Fig. 15). Imaris successfully reconstructed individual axonal trajectories in sparsely labeled regions distal to the injection site, where neuronal processes were sufficiently isolated to permit unambiguous tracing. In these regions, reconstructed filaments closely followed the underlying fluorescence signal and preserved local branching topology. However, within denser labeled territories closer to the injection epicenter, Imaris’ automatic tracing generated numerous false-positive paths due to signal overlap and saturation, requiring extensive manual intervention. Even after manual seeding, the majority of fluorescence signal within these regions could not be reliably reconstructed, as overlapping axons and somata did not follow the separability assumptions underlying filament-based reconstruction. As a result, Imaris-derived results were potentially biased toward peripheral, sparsely labeled regions and systematically excluded higher-density fluorescence zones. In contrast, FISIA directly quantified fluorescence spatial organization across both dense and sparse regions without requiring reconstruction of individual neuronal elements. By treating fluorescence as a spatial probability distribution rather than a set of separable objects, FISIA captured mesoscale patterns of clustering, dispersion, and regional divergence. This was likely due to the key conceptual distinction that Imaris was optimized for single-neuron morphology, whereas FISIA was designed to decode population and circuit-level spatial organization. The two approaches therefore addressed fundamentally different biological questions.

Across these benchmarks, FISIA consistently revealed structured spatial information that was not directly available from the comparison intensity or reconstruction-based pipelines, while other tools remained informative for their intended purposes. We summarized these methodological distinctions qualitatively in Table 1, and quantitatively in Supplementary Data 1, 4, 15, demonstrating FISIA’s unique integration of fluorescence intensity, spatial entropy, and divergence within a unified unsupervised framework. The convergence of these comparisons supported FISIA as a unified framework for extracting mesoscale spatial organization from fluorescence images, particularly when the biological question concerned regional distribution patterns or anatomy-behavior association rather than signal intensity alone, bridging cellular scale and systems scale understanding of neural architecture in both brain and spinal cord tissues.

Quantitative benchmarking

To directly compare the tools quantitatively, we tested FISIA, ImageJ/FIJI and Ilastik on identical image ROIs with controlled perturbations and calculated percentage change relative to the original fluorescence image (Supplementary Fig. 16). These perturbations were designed to mimic fluorescence signal variation introduced during tissue preparation, image acquisition, and downstream image processing. Across all four kinds of perturbations, ImageJ/FIJI and Ilastik quantification of the total fluorescence intensity were highly concordant as expected. The total intensity changed substantially after the perturbation of fluorescence signal loss in all tools (-44.9997 ± 23.2050% for FISIA, -47.7836 ± 21.3140% for ImageJ/FIJI, -47.7840 ± 21.3138% for Ilastik). In contrast, FISIA’s KLD metrics remained highly consistent with minimal change of -0.0043 ± 0.045% across 10%-80% signal loss perturbation. For perturbations on global fluorescence signal amplification, nonlinear response, bright signal compression or expansion, the total intensity quantification from ImageJ/FIJI and Ilastik also changed substantially (percentage of change averaged over all three perturbations and brain sections, 54.2860 ± 23.3604% for ImageJ/FIJI, 54.2850 ± 23.3604% for Ilastik). In contrast, both KLD and total intensity metrics of FISIA remained highly stable under these perturbations and therefore more robust to some common photometric and image processing distortions (Supplementary Data 4). These results supported the intended role of FISIA as a spatial distribution metric that was more robust in the tests here to nuisance distortions that can confound fluorescence quantification.

Discussion

Fluorescence imaging has long been used to map neuronal connectivity, but traditional intensity-based comparisons often focus on fluorescence intensity and implicitly equate signal magnitude with biological meaning3–6,9. Our study introduces an information-based approach to fluorescence analysis, quantifying spatial distributional information variations, revealing that in addition to intensity differences between tracers or brain sections, spatial organizational patterns also vary and contain informative structure. Using two complementary validation regimes, tracer comparison in cortex and behavioral association after spinal cord injury, we show that FISIA reduces the likelihood that tracer-specific brightness differences will be interpreted as false positives, while increasing the likelihood of recovering biologically meaningful structure-function relationships that appeared as false negative results in the intensity-based tracer comparison analyses applied to the datasets tested here. This challenges the common assumption that intensity differences directly correlate with variations in neural connectivity and highlights the need for more nuanced methods of tracer comparison. The quantitative comparison on perturbed images further supports this by showing that conventional intensity-based outcomes are sensitive to certain non-biological variations, whereas FISIA’s metrics remain comparatively stable under the same perturbation tested here.

A more comprehensive approach to fluorescence imaging analysis

At its core, FISIA redefines fluorescence images as structured spatial systems rather than intensity maps. FISIA is not the addition of individual analytical techniques, but their unification under a single information-based framework that treats fluorescence as structured spatial data rather than a collection of independent pixels. By integrating KLD, Andrews plots, intensity frequency comparisons, and two-dimensional fluorescence histograms, we provide a more holistic view of neural tracer behavior, transforming fluorescence microscopy into a quantitative map of spatial information, analogous to how information theory reframes neural coding beyond firing rates alone41,42, and how network science reframes connectivity beyond pairwise links43.

The strength of this framework lies in how each analytical module extracts a distinct dimension of spatial organization. Each of these analytical methods provides unique insights. KLD helps quantify how spatial distributional variation differs across tracers and regions. Andrews plots identify latent differentiating factors and reveal whether different tracers capture distinct or overlapping neuronal features, offering a way to compare tracers beyond their absolute fluorescence values. Fluorescence intensity frequency plots show regional fluorescence trends, expose hidden patterns in how intensity levels are distributed, clarifying whether a tracer is biased toward labeling high- or low-intensity regions. Two-dimensional fluorescence histograms allow direct spatial comparisons of fluorescence intensity, allowing capture of detailed spatial difference patterns rather than just a fluorescence overview. By applying these methods, our study provides a framework ensuring that conclusions drawn from tracer studies are not confounded by intensity biases, diffusion artifacts, or regional labeling inconsistencies.

The benchmarking results showcase FISIA addressing a long-standing analytical gap in fluorescence imaging: the inability of conventional methods to quantify the organization of fluorescence rather than its mere intensity. Traditional platforms such as FIJI32, Ilastik34, NeuronStudio35,36, and Imaris each serve various purposes, such as measuring global brightness, segmenting categorical regions, reconstructing sparse single neurons, etc. However, none directly captures the spatial information embedded in densely labeled fluorescence fields, where structure arises from graded, continuous variations in signal distribution that mirror biological organization at the mesoscale. FISIA directly quantifies spatial entropy, divergence, and topology from fluorescence intensity patterns, transforming microscopy data into interpretable maps of structural information, representing a conceptual shift from descriptive imaging to quantitative spatial decoding.

By measuring how fluorescence is distributed rather than only how much is present, FISIA captures principles of neural organization that have been obscured by intensity-based paradigms. This capability resolves the scale discontinuity between cellular and systems-level imaging, bridging the microscopic detail of morphometric reconstruction with the population-level architecture revealed in mesoscale connectomics27,44, and linking anatomical organization with behavioral outcomes that were not apparent from comparison analyses tested here. Beyond neuroscience, FISIA’s unsupervised, information-theoretic approach may generalize to any biological imaging modality where spatial distribution encodes function, from tumor microenvironment mapping45 to developmental patterning.

The relationship between fluorescence intensity and spatial distributional variation

Tracer studies often prioritize intensity-based quantification, assuming that higher fluorescence signals equate to more effective neuronal labeling28,46–48. This has to be carefully done to avoid fluorescence saturation artifacts. Thus, we have implemented three different ways to threshold the fluorescence intensity and provided immediate visual feedback. However, our findings suggest that intensity alone is not a sufficient metric for evaluating tracer efficacy. Despite clear differences in fluorescence intensity at the injection epicenter, both AAV and BDA tracers exhibit comparable spatial distributional variation patterns, as demonstrated by their similar KLD values. This equivalence emerges after normalization, which rescales global intensity while preserving relative subregional proportions; thus, identical peak values in normalized heatmaps here do not imply identical raw fluorescence, but rather comparable spatial structure. The robustness of the main findings to repeated manual ROI placement further suggests that the observed differences were not an artifact of minor variation in anatomical landmark-guided ROI region selection. This raises a critical question: is fluorescence intensity merely a property of the tracer, or does it reflect a fundamental difference in neural connectivity? If two tracers carry similar spatial distributional information but differ in intensity, then intensity alone may not be an adequate indicator of how well a tracer captures connectivity patterns. Instead, our results suggest that tracers should be assessed with an extra metric based on how they distribute their fluorescence across neural structures, rather than only how brightly they fluoresce. This distinction is especially relevant when choosing between high-intensity, localized tracers like AAV and broader-diffusing tracers like BDA. Our tracer comparison therefore constitutes an explicit false positive test, showing that intensity-only analysis could incorrectly infer connectivity differences where none exist, whereas FISIA yields similar spatial organization estimates across tracers in this dataset. To ensure that these conclusions are not preprocessing artifacts, FISIA incorporates three explicit saturation mitigation options at the thresholding stage, but the spatial metrics themselves remain invariant to global intensity scaling by construction.

The choice of tracer is often framed as a trade-off between resolution and coverage28,47,49. AAV provides sharper, more localized labeling28,50–52, while BDA is more diffuse and extends further across neural networks28,53–55. However, our results suggest that this difference is not merely a technical limitation but may reflect biologically relevant properties of tracer diffusion within neural tissue.

This also has important implications for how fluorescence-based connectivity maps are interpreted. A denser signal does not necessarily indicate a stronger neural connection, just as a broader spread does not necessarily imply weaker connectivity56–58. Instead, the observed patterns may reflect differences in how neural circuits process and propagate signals, adding a layer of functional complexity to structural connectivity studies. Our observations suggest that the spatial arrangement of labeling may provide information that is vital in addition to signal magnitude alone. Whether spatial organization is more informative than signal density for specific functional outcomes will require future experimental testing.

The functional implications of neuronal projection density

Neural connectivity is often discussed in terms of structural pathways56–62, but our results reveal that the way fluorescence intensity is distributed across different regions reflects underlying functional differences in projection density and organizational complexity. The higher projection density from M1 to M2, compared to the broader and more dispersed projection pattern toward RF, suggests distinct roles in motor control. The significantly greater spatial distributional variation observed in M1-to-RF projections, as quantified by higher KLD values, aligns with the known functional heterogeneity of the reticular formation. The RF is not a monolithic structure but a complex, functionally diverse network spanning the brainstem, involved in regulating arousal, autonomic functions, and integrative motor control, including postural adjustments, locomotion, and coordination of multi-joint movements39,40,63. The diffuse projections to RF could indicate a more distributed processing network for subdivided function, or an evolutionarily conserved risk diversification strategy in case of injury18,40. The distributed, parallel-processing architecture would allow for flexible, context-dependent modulation of motor output across multiple spinal and cranial targets, potentially vital in neuromodulation and motor recovery40. This spatial heterogeneity could enable the RF to integrate inputs from diverse cortical and subcortical sources and broadcast modulatory signals in a graded, region-specific manner, supporting the idea that functional diversity is encoded in structural dispersion. In contrast, M2’s localized, high-density connectivity may support fine-tuned motor coordination and planning. Thus, the distinct spatial distribution patterns revealed by FISIA are consistent with a potential fundamental organizational principle of focused connectivity for dedicated computation (M2), and dispersed connectivity for integrative, modulatory control (RF). This distinction underscores the importance of moving beyond intensity-based metrics to capture the spatial logic of neural circuit organization.

The observed differences in fluorescence distributional variation between M2 and RF further suggest that not all neural pathways exhibit the same degree of organizational uniformity. The greater spatial variability in RF projections is consistent with findings that certain pathways are more plastic or subject to functional modulation, potentially supporting recovery from injuries40,63, while others maintain a more hierarchical structure. This raises important questions about how projection stability and plasticity contribute to motor function and adaptability in response to injury or learning. These organizational principles are not imposed by anatomical priors, but emerged directly from fluorescence spatial statistics, underscoring that FISIA recovers biologically interpretable circuit logic without manual annotation or model assumptions.

It is also one of the central challenges in spinal cord injury research to understand which neural changes contribute to functional recovery64–66, and to distinguish neural remodeling that merely increases signal magnitude from remodeling that meaningfully restores function. Thus, the spinal cord results constitute an explicit false negative test, demonstrating that comparison intensity-based metrics do not detect associations that are recovered by FISIA-derived spatial and binarized intensity measures in the dataset here. The broader and more even distribution of neural sprouting in the stimulation-treated group is associated with better motor recovery, whereas localized, irregular sprouting patterns may indicate less effective functional integration. This finding suggests that functional recovery is not just about regenerating neurons but may also be about how well those neurons integrate into the residual motor control system. If neural sprouting occurs without a corresponding spatial organization, the new connections may be functionally inefficient, leading to uncoordinated motor outputs or compensatory movement strategies. This aligns with behavioral observations of elongated step strides in less rehabbed rats, which may represent adaptive gait modifications rather than true restoration of normal movement patterns. Thus, neural plasticity alone may not be enough. Functional connectivity must be re-established in a way that makes physiologically meaningful reconnections. Histologically, this may be reflected by the natural distribution of motor circuits.

The spinal cord injury study is also a critical translational validation of FISIA’s conceptual framework. Using the same metrics that are used in cortical projection mapping, FISIA quantifies the spatial organization of neural remodeling after injury and links it to behavioral recovery. This one-click, information-based approach transforms fluorescence microscopy from a descriptive histological tool into an associative analytic framework capable of bridging anatomy and function. In contrast to traditional spinal cord analyses that rely on manual lesion reconstructions, axonal length measurements, etc., FISIA achieves such reproducible results from standard fluorescence images that are collected routinely without extra labor. Thus, it also provides an opportunity to uncover previously overlooked anatomy-function relationships from previously collected or published studies.

Our findings from the cortex and spinal cord suggest that spatial organization may represent a conserved and functionally meaningful principle across the central nervous system, and that lower spatial divergence and greater homogeneity are associated with preserved or recovered function in the datasets analyzed here. FISIA revealed significant associations between histological information and behavioral outcomes, whereas the comparison intensity-based methods did not in this dataset. These findings suggest that FISIA may help bridge mesoscale connectomics and functional neurorehabilitation67,68. The cortical analyses were intended to establish methodological behavior on anatomically matched fluorescence datasets, and the regional interpretations should be viewed as dataset-bounded and hypothesis-generating. Future studies should be conducted to provide population-level biological estimates across animals.

In conclusion, we proposed FISIA, a comprehensive approach to fluorescence imaging that improves both structural and functional interpretations of neural circuits. Rather than treating fluorescence as a proxy for quantity alone, FISIA decodes the spatial information embedded in fluorescence distributions to reveal functionally informative organization, a transformation that parallels the broader shift in biological sciences from measuring form to quantifying information69,70 and principled data-driven inference of structure-function relationships68. By suppressing tracer-specific false positives and recovering functionally relevant signals missed by the comparison pipelines, FISIA may provide a less biased framework for assessing connectivity across brain and spinal cord. As neuroscience moves toward more quantitative, data-driven approaches2,4 and fluorescence imaging continues to scale in resolution and volume, tools like FISIA may prove valuable in ensuring the accuracy and reliability of fluorescence-based connectivity studies.
