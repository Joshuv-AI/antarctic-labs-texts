Advancing preventive care under vision 2030: a retrospective study on integrating oral health into the health coach program in Jeddah’s second health cluster
Authors: Noha M Alhazmi, Nooralhuda A Felemban, Lobna A Alsulaimani, Nada S Alghamdi, Sarah I Al-Hawsah, Khulood A Hawsawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral health is a key component of overall well-being, yet it is often underrepresented in public health training programs. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of introducing dental health education sessions into the annual Health Coach Program at Jeddah Second Health Cluster. A cohort of 55 health coaches participated in an educational intervention that incorporated essential dental health topics into their training curriculum. Participants’ knowledge and understanding of oral health concepts were measured using a structured, validated questionnaire administered before and after the intervention to assess changes attributable to the program. The results show that the educational intervention awareness for the participants of fluoride benefits increased from 40 to 80% (p < 0.001), and knowledge of pediatric oral health improved from 55 to 85% (p = 0.002). Self-reported tooth brushing twice daily rose from 70 to 95% (p = 0.001*). Participants with bachelor’s degrees demonstrated greater knowledge gains (OR = 2.10, p = 0.016), while the intervention itself was the strongest predictor of improvement (OR = 4.25, p < 0.001). The study concluded that integrating oral health into the Health Coach Program may strengthen community prevention efforts and advance the national agenda for holistic, preventive healthcare.Keywords: Oral health education, Health Coach Program (HCP), Retrospective study, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Preventive healthcare is now a foundational pillar of contemporary health systems, prioritizing health promotion, disease prevention, and the reduction of long-term healthcare expenditures. The Health Coach Program (HCP) is a national preventive health initiative developed to equip healthcare professionals with the competencies required to promote healthy behaviors and deliver community-based preventive health education (Ryn 1997; Palmer et al. 2003). The program typically incorporates structured educational modules addressing nutrition, physical activity, chronic disease prevention, and other lifestyle-related health determinants (Mufti 2000). Substantial evidence demonstrates that lifestyle-oriented education programs contribute to improved chronic disease management, strengthened patient self-efficacy, and sustained long-term health outcomes (Egger et al. 2008). In parallel with this global movement, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 places strong emphasis on preventive care, community participation, and the integration of health promotion strategies across healthcare systems to enhance population health outcomes. Within this national agenda, initiatives that empower healthcare professionals to influence healthy behaviors represent a critical mechanism for achieving sustainable, holistic, and patient-centered healthcare delivery.

At the Jeddah Second Health Cluster, the Health Coach Program is implemented annually to strengthen the preventive health capacity of healthcare staff, particularly nurses and health educators. Through structured educational sessions, participants receive training across a range of health topics designed to support behavior change and promote healthy lifestyle practices among patients and community members (Jeddah Second Health Cluster Annual Report 2023). Prior evaluations of health coaching initiatives in Saudi Arabia have demonstrated significant improvements in glycemic control among patients with type 2 diabetes (Soliman et al. 2022), as well as positive outcomes in physical activity engagement and weight management behaviors (Elfakki et al. 2025). These findings highlight the program’s effectiveness as a platform for preventive health interventions. However, despite its broad preventive focus, oral health education has not been systematically incorporated into the Health Coach Program (Jeddah Second Health Cluster Annual Report 2023).

Oral health constitutes an integral component of overall health and is strongly associated with systemic conditions, including diabetes (Lamster et al. 2008), cardiovascular disease (Dietrich et al. 2017), and adverse pregnancy outcomes (Javaid et al. 2025). Globally, oral diseases affect billions of people and remain largely preventable through early education, routine preventive practices, and integration within primary healthcare services. Nonetheless, non-dental healthcare professionals frequently receive limited training in oral health assessment and promotion, leading to missed opportunities for early detection, prevention, and timely intervention (O’Brien et al. 2022).

Oral health assessments are rarely incorporated into routine patient evaluations by non-dental healthcare providers outside specific clinical contexts such as pediatric and geriatric care, and guidance on oral hygiene is seldom provided even when patients present with symptoms such as gingival bleeding (Delgado et al. 2021; Gustafsson et al. 2021). This gap has been attributed to insufficient training, competing clinical priorities, and the lack of institutional emphasis on oral health integration within preventive care frameworks. Thus, critical opportunities for prevention and early intervention—when disease progression remains reversible—are frequently overlooked, thereby perpetuating preventable oral diseases (Harnagea et al. 2017).

Within the Saudi Arabian context, this gap poses a significant barrier to achieving the preventive healthcare objectives outlined in Vision 2030. Integrating oral health education into established preventive programs offers a strategic opportunity to strengthen healthcare delivery, optimize resource utilization, and enhance interprofessional collaboration. Health coaches, due to their trusted role in patient education and behavioral change facilitation, are uniquely positioned to extend oral health promotion beyond traditional dental environments into routine healthcare interactions (Almajed et al. 2024).

Accordingly, this study seeks to advance preventive healthcare under Vision 2030 by integrating structured oral health education into the Health Coach Program at Jeddah’s Second Health Cluster and evaluating its impact on participants’ oral health knowledge and self-reported preventive behaviors. By embedding oral health within a national preventive framework, this research aims to contribute to the development of a more integrated, comprehensive, and patient-centered model of health promotion in Saudi Arabia.

Methodology

Study design

This retrospective study determined the impact of educational intervention on oral health knowledge, awareness, and practice among health catches at Jeddah Second Health Cluster. The study was conducted using a pre-test and post-test quasi-experimental design. A structured questionnaire was distributed to participants before and after attending targeted oral health educational sessions. The instrument was used at both time points to measure changes in participants’ knowledge and awareness, enabling within-subject comparison and evaluation of the intervention’s effectiveness.

Population size

A total of 55 participants who consented to take part and completed both assessments were included in the analysis. The population consisted of health educators and nurses nominated by the Jeddah Second Health Cluster to participate in the Health Coach Program and obtain a Health Coach Certificate. These participants were selected from primary health care centers and hospitals within the cluster. Due to their active roles in patient education and preventive health services, they were considered well-positioned to serve as oral health advocates in their communities.

Data collection and intervention

Data were collected using a structured Knowledge, Attitude, and Behavior (KAB) questionnaire, adapted from previously validated tools and relevant literature (Selvaraj et al. 2022). The KAB questionnaire instrument was adapted based on previously validated tools designed to assess oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors

The questionnaire comprised three main sections:

Knowledge about oral health practices (e.g., purpose of brushing, impact of breastfeeding on oral health),

Knowledge about oral health practices (e.g., purpose of brushing, impact of breastfeeding on oral health),

Attitudes toward the importance of maintaining oral health,

Attitudes toward the importance of maintaining oral health,

Self-reported behaviors (e.g., type of toothbrush used, use of fluoride toothpaste).

Self-reported behaviors (e.g., type of toothbrush used, use of fluoride toothpaste).

The KAB questionnaire was distributed electronically to participants prior to the educational intervention and then re-administered following the intervention. The intervention consisted of a structured dental health course delivered by dental professionals, which included four focused lectures. The content addressed key areas such as oral disease prevention, common misconceptions, and bad oral habits.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics, while chi-square test assessed pre-and post-intervention changes. Data were analyzed using SPSS [Version 27]. Logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of improvement, with statistical significance set at (p < 0.05). Although the convenience sampling approach allowed efficient data collection, it introduced potential selection bias, and the reliance on self-reported data may have led to social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies (Sedgwick 2013). These limitations were considered when interpreting the findings.

Ethical consideration

The study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Jeddah Second Health Cluster before data collection (Approval code: A01962). All participants received written information about the study’s purpose and procedures and provided informed consent before participation. Data confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout, following institutional and national research ethics guidelines.

A total of 55 health workers (educators and nurses) from primary healthcare centers within the Second Health Cluster in Jeddah participated in the study. The same participants completed both the pre-intervention and post-intervention surveys, ensuring consistency in demographic characteristics across both time points.

Oral health knowledge improvement

The educational intervention led to a statistically significant improvement in all measured domains of oral health knowledge among participants (Table 2). Knowledge regarding the benefits of fluoride toothpaste increased from 40% before the intervention to 80% after the intervention, representing the largest relative improvement among the assessed domains (p<0.001). Other knowledge such as, the primary purpose of tooth brushing, preventing cavities, and gingivitis, also improved markedly from 65% pre-intervention to 90% post-intervention (p<0.001).

Participants with bachelor’s degrees had 2.1 times greater odds of demonstrating improvement compared to those with diploma qualifications (95% CI: 1.15–3.85, p = 0.016). This suggests that higher educational attainment—and potentially greater baseline health literacy—was associated with more effective learning and knowledge retention.

Importantly, the intervention itself emerged as the strongest overall predictor of knowledge gain (OR = 4.25, 95% CI: 2.50–7.20, p < 0.001), confirming its effectiveness across all participant subgroups. No statistically significant associations were observed for age or gender, suggesting that the intervention content and delivery were equally effective across these demographic variables (Sanchez et al. 2019).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a structured educational intervention can significantly improve oral health knowledge, literacy, and self-reported practices among non-dental primary healthcare workers.

Participants were assessed before and after the educational intervention; demographic variables remained constant throughout the study period. The cohort included individuals with diverse educational backgrounds: 55% held a diploma (three-year certified degree), 37.5% held a bachelor’s (four-year certified degree), and 7.5% held a postgraduate degree (Master’s and PhD). Although the health trainer certification at the Health Cluster does not require advanced academic qualifications, the study aimed to include all available trainers working with the Second Jeddah Health Cluster. Consequently, the educational distribution within the sample reflects the realistic workforce structure of primary healthcare personnel, where diploma-level professionals are more prevalent than those with advanced degrees.

The finding provides strong evidence that a structured educational intervention can significantly enhance oral health awareness and practices among non-dental primary healthcare workers. Most notably, knowledge of fluoride efficacy increased from 40% pre-intervention to 80% post-intervention (p < 0.001). This represents a clinically meaningful gain given the central role of fluoride in caries prevention (Cheng et al. 2019). These findings highlight the importance of integrating oral health training within the continuing education of primary healthcare professionals, who often represent the first and only point of health contact for many community members (Tafere et al. 2018).

Similarly, understanding of the importance of the primary teeth and knowledge related to baby teeth care increased around 38.5% (p = 0.002), suggesting that the intervention effectively addressed common misconceptions about pediatric oral health (Han 2017). In addition, understanding of the purposes of tooth brushing also improved markedly, demonstrating successful knowledge transfer and enhanced awareness of fundamental preventive concepts. This indicates that participants initially had a limited understanding of this key evidence-based preventive measure, but their knowledge improved substantially following the educational session.

Overall, the pattern of improvement across multiple knowledge areas including prevention, fluoride science, and pediatric oral health indicates that the educational content was well structured and effectively delivered. Importantly, the post-intervention gains suggest that the intervention successfully corrected previously held misconceptions rather than merely filling knowledge gaps (Cepova et al. 2018; Kanupuru et al. 2015).

The intervention also resulted in a great improvement in behavioral changes. For example, dental floss use increased threefold (p < 0.001). This improvement is particularly noteworthy given that flossing is typically a difficult behavior to sustain consistently. Additionally, the proportion of participants reporting at least one annual dental visit rose 2.6-fold (p < 0.001), suggesting that the intervention helped to reduce barriers related to knowledge and perceived need for professional dental care (Llena et al. 2019). These behavioral changes are especially important because healthcare professionals often serve as role models for the communities they serve. When healthcare workers adopt healthier oral hygiene practices themselves, they may be more likely to encourage similar behaviors among their patients (Aldowah et al. 2023).

Educational background also remained a significant predictor of knowledge improvement, despite the fact that all participants received the same standardized training. This finding implies that differences in cognitive engagement and information processing may influence how participants benefited from the intervention. Notably, the magnitude of improvement observed in this study exceeds that reported in several previous oral health literacy interventions, which may be attributed to the targeted nature of the training and the participants’ professional background as healthcare workers who are generally more receptive to health education (Sandelowsky et al. 2019).

These findings carry important implications for healthcare systems aiming to integrate oral health into primary care. First, they demonstrate that targeted, time-efficient educational interventions can produce measurable improvements in oral health literacy among non-dental health professionals (Veerasamy et al. 2022). Second, they highlight existing gaps in fundamental preventive knowledge—particularly regarding fluoride and oral hygiene practices—that can be addressed through structured training. Finally, improving healthcare providers’ own oral health behaviors may enhance their confidence and credibility when promoting preventive oral care to patients, ultimately contributing to better level oral health outcomes.

Study limitation

Some limitations should be acknowledged; the study used a convenience sample of 55 participants, which limits generalizability (Philip et al. 2019). This is because most of the researchers involved in this study are employees of the Second Jeddah health cluster, which is separate from other clusters in terms of its hospitals and primary healthcare center. Therefore, it was easier for the researcher to access participants and complete the logistical procedures without difficulties. Furthermore, this is an initial study that will be expanded in the future to include other health clusters. Additionally, reliance on self-reported practices may have introduced social desirability bias. Future research should include objective clinical indicators and longitudinal follow-up to assess knowledge retention and sustained behavior change (Verhulst et al. 2019). The observed improvements support the inclusion of similar educational interventions within continuing professional development programs. Strengthening oral health awareness at the primary care level may ultimately enhance the promotion and delivery of preventive oral healthcare across the wider health system (Wong et al. 2021; Pollick 2018)

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a structured educational intervention within the Certified Health Coach program effectively improves oral health knowledge, literacy, and practices among primary healthcare workers. These improvements can contribute to better individual and community health outcomes. Based on the positive impact observed and participants’ enthusiastic response, we recommend incorporating oral health lectures into the core curriculum of the Certified Health Coach program at the Jeddah Second Health Cluster. This integration represents a strategic step toward bridging the gap between general preventive health and oral health promotion, enhancing the overall quality of public health education.
