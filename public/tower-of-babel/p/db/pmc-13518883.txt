Triple IFN pathway deficiency sensitizes mice to human respiratory virus infection independent of human viral receptor expression
Authors: Qinghong Fan, Meifang Pan, Mengling Jiang, Chengqian Feng, Jianping Cui, Jun Zhang, Shiquan Liang, Yaping Wang, Jingrong Shi, Rui Li, Wei Zhang, Liqiang Feng, Haisheng Yu, Yi-Ping Li, Fengyu Hu, Xiaoping Tang, Hongbo Guo, Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interferon (IFN) pathways form the innate barrier against viral invasion and partial deficiency in these pathways allows human virus infection. Whether a complete IFN pathway deficiency could confer the susceptibility to human viral infection independent of expressing the human viral receptor remains unknown. We develop an innate immunity severely deficient mouse model, designated AGL, which features one-step knock-out of the IFNAR, IFNGR and IFNLR. The AGL mice become susceptible to diverse representative human respiratory viruses, including adenovirus type 55 (HAdV-55; double-stranded DNA), human monkeypox virus (MPXV) clade IIb (double-stranded DNA), parainfluenza virus (PIV; negative-sense single-stranded RNA), and the clinically isolated SARS-CoV-2 delta variant (positive-sense single-stranded RNA). Our results suggest that the type III IFN pathway constitutes a backup layer of antiviral frontline beneath the type I and II IFN pathways. In addition, proof-of-concept studies testing MPXV and PIV antivirals highlight the translational value of AGL mice. The AGL mice, as a universal model, substantially enhance the ability to investigate both emerging and established viruses without the need for tailored mouse models.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, multiple respiratory viruses have caused outbreaks, epidemics or pandemics, with some subsequently becoming endemic in human populations. Examples include epidemic-associated viruses such as avian influenza A(H5N1) and A(H7N9) viruses, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus MERS-CoV, pandemic viruses such as influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus and SARS-CoV-2, and endemic respiratory viruses such as seasonal influenza viruses1,2. Emerging and re-emerging respiratory viruses are estimated to be the main threats to human health in the future, given their efficient and rapid aerosol transmission, even though it is impossible to predict when, where, or which viruses will appear3. Meanwhile, common respiratory viruses, such as human parainfluenza virus (hPIV), adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and human metapneumovirus (hMPV), contribute to the seasonal respiratory infections worldwide4,5. Despite recent advances in RSV prevention and treatment6, effective antivirals and vaccines remain limited for many respiratory viral infections.

Robust animal models that accurately replicate human respiratory viral infections and disease mechanisms are still rare7,8. Species-specific infection preferences prevent most human viruses from directly infecting commonly used wild-type inbred mice, with only a few exceptions. Notable examples include BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice for the influenza virus9,10, CAST/EiJ mice for monkeypox virus (MPXV)11,12, and neonatal mice for certain types of rotaviruses and noroviruses13,14. Engineering mice to express human viral receptors in mice, either stably through transgenic methods or transiently via viral vectors, has been successful for those well-characterized viruses, such as ACE2 for SARS-CoV-215,16, DSG2 for Human adenovirus type 55 (HAdV-55)17, and SCARB2 for enterovirus 71 (EV71)18. However, simply providing these human viral receptors in mice does not guarantee successful viral infection. For example, mice expressing the human NTCP receptor fail to support hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection19, indicating that additional intrinsic factors are crucial for viral replication after entry. Alternatively, to transplant human cells and tissues into mice (immune system- and liver-humanized mic20) has been used to support viral infection in vivo. Lung-humanized mouse models have also been developed and successfully applied to study SARS-CoV-2 infection21–23. However, these models are based on ectopic engraftment of human lung tissue and therefore differ from in situ organ humanization approaches achieved in liver-humanized mice. In addition, robust infection models for certain viruses have been established in other species. For example, hamster models have been widely used for SARS-CoV-2 infection and pathogenesis, including STAT2 knockout hamsters24, and have also been developed for human adenoviruses such as hAd525. Despite these advances, mouse models remain uniquely advantageous due to their genetic tractability, well-defined backgrounds, and extensive availability of immunological tools.

Another strategy to generate viral infection models is the continuous passaging of clinical viruses in wild-type mice, during which viruses gradually acquire new mutations to overcome host restrictions. This approach has been successfully applied to SARS-CoV-28,26,27, SARS-CoV28, dengue virus29, and Ebola virus30. Nevertheless, the viral mouse-adaptation strategy suffers from several limitations. First, the whole adaptation process, which usually takes multiple generations, is time-consuming. Second, not all viruses can have mouse-adapted strains. The viruses with a high mutation rate, such as RNA viruses, are more likely to evolve into mouse-adapted strains than genetically stable viruses less prone to adaptive genomic changes. Third, the acquired mutations might alter key viral properties that differ from those of its ancestral human counterpart. In short, this approach is virus-specific, time-consuming and cannot be readily applied to emerging or difficult-to-culture pathogens.

Interferon pathways, which include type I, II, and III interferons, are central to antiviral immunity, acting through JAK-STAT signaling to induce interferon-stimulated genes that restrict viral replication31. Type I and III IFNs mediate innate antiviral defense, whereas IFN-γ primarily shapes adaptive immune responses. In addition to direct antiviral effects, IFNs enhance antigen presentation and T and B cell responses, and defects in IFN signaling are associated with increased susceptibility to severe viral disease32–34. Genetic ablation of interferon pathways has been successful in expanding viral tropism in mice. For instance, IFNAR⁻/⁻ mice show markedly increased viral replication and disease severity for viruses such as West Nile virus (WNV) and Zika virus35,36. IFNGR⁻/⁻ mice enhance the replication of herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) and disease severity37, whereas IFNLR⁻/⁻ mice exhibit increased intestinal replication of murine norovirus and reovirus38. Although some of these viruses can infect wild-type mice under specific experimental conditions, such as WNV36 and HSV-139,40, interferon responses play a critical role in restricting viral replication and limiting disease progression. Notably, combined deficiencies in IFNAR and IFNGR (e.g., AG129 mice, Stat1⁻/⁻ mice) or IFNAR and IFNLR synergistically increase susceptibility to various viruses, including dengue and chikungunya41,42. However, the triple deficiency of all three interferon pathways-IFNAR, IFNGR, and IFNLR-has not yet been explored. Considering that type I and type III interferon pathways are known to share the same downstream signaling (JAK/STAT2) and elicit largely identical profiles of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), the deficiency due to IFNAR ablation will be compensated by the type III IFN pathway, particularly in the mucosal region where the IFNLR is enriched. Therefore, we hypothesize that disabling all three interferon pathways will lead to a comprehensive interferon signal loss in the respiratory tract and sensitize the mouse to be permissive and vulnerable to human virus infection through the nasal route.

In this study, we generated AGL mice with targeted deletions of the Ifnar2, Ifngr2, Ifnar1, and Ifnlr2 genes, effectively achieving comprehensive ablation of type I, II, and III interferon receptor signaling. We aimed to evaluate whether the AGL model can broadly support infection by diverse human respiratory viruses without the need for receptor humanization. Viruses were selected to represent diverse viral properties relevant to human respiratory infections, including differences in genome type, virion structure, receptor usage, and interferon sensitivity. Specifically, clade IIb MPXV represents an enveloped DNA virus of current public health relevance, with incompletely defined entry mechanisms. HAdV-55 represents a non-enveloped DNA respiratory virus with a defined receptor, but for which no robust susceptible mouse model has been established. hPIV3 is a negative-sense RNA virus known to be highly sensitive to interferon responses, with limited pathogenic mouse models. SARS-CoV-2 delta variant represents a positive-sense RNA virus with well-characterized ACE2-dependent entry. Independent of human viral receptor expression, AGL mice exhibited unprecedented permissiveness to representative human DNA and RNA viruses via the nasal route, highlighting the crossing-complementing role of type I, II and III pathways in gating virus infection in the respiratory tract. The AGL mouse is a transformative resource for virology research that bridges the gap between mechanistic studies and therapeutic development.

Results

Generation of IFNAR-IFNGR-IFNLR triple pathway-deficient mice

The Ifnar2, Ifnlr2, Ifnar1 and Ifngr2 genes are located tandemly within a 193 kb locus on chromosome 16 (Fig. 1A), which provides an opportunity to one-step knock out all four genes, effectively disabling all three interferon pathways. We named the newly created mouse model C57BL/6-IFNAR-/-IFNGR-/-IFNLR-/- (AGL). The knockout was confirmed through PCR, genomic sequencing (Fig. S1A–D and Fig. 1B), and Western blot analysis (Fig. 1C). The ablation of both Ifnar1 and Ifnar2 ensures a deficient IFNAR. Previously established methods in creating IFNGR- and IFNLR-deficient mice involved the deletion of Ifngr1 and Ifnlr143,44. We confirmed that the deletions of Ifngr2 and Ifnlr2 deactivated the responsiveness of IFNGR and IFNLR to stimulation by murine IFNγ and IFN-λ2, using PCR and RNA sequencing (Fig. 1D–G and Fig. S1E–G). Human adenovirus type 55 infection and pathogenesis in AGL mice

HAdV-55 is an emerging and highly pathogenic respiratory adenovirus associated with outbreaks of acute respiratory disease and severe pneumonia, particularly in children and young adults, posing a significant public health threat45. Previously, we identified DSG2 as the HAdV-55 receptor in a cell line17 and demonstrated that hDSG2 transgenic mice could only support HAdV-55 infection at high doses ( > 1 × 108 plaque-forming units (PFUs) per mouse) in vivo, without developing any obvious disease symptoms46.

To assess the vulnerability of AGL mice to HAdV-55, we intranasally inoculated them with 3.5 × 105 PFU HAdV-5517,46 (Fig. 2A). Remarkably, this low infection dose resulted in significant weight loss of over 10% within 4 days, and 67% mortality by 3 days post-infection (dpi) in AGL mice compared to the Ifnar1/Ifngr1-deficient (AGB6) and C57BL/6 (WT) control groups (Fig. 2A–C). High levels of viral DNA (5.7 × 105 vg copies/mg tissue) and infectious virions were detected in the lung but not in other tissues (Fig. 2D, E). AGL mice developed severe viral interstitial pneumonia characterized by extensive lymphocytic infiltration and marked thickening of alveolar septa, along with congestion, interstitial edema, and mild exudation (Fig. 2F). Viral infection kinetics indicated that lung viral loads peaked at 3 dpi (on average, 3.2 × 106 vg copies/mg tissue) and became undetectable by 10 dpi (Fig. 2G and Fig. S2A). Single-cell RNA-seq identified 27 major cell types based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 2H and Fig. S2B). The most affected targets included macrophages, including Chil3-expressing macrophages (Mac-Chil3) with an alveolar macrophage-like profile and C1qa-expressing macrophages (Mac-C1qa), as well as club cells, with peak infection observed at 3 dpi (Fig. 2I). Notably, Mac-C1qa, enriched for complement and antigen presentation genes (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, Ctss, Cd74, H2-DMa), are consistent with resident or immunoregulatory-like macrophage populations, suggesting a functional bias toward immune surveillance and homeostatic regulation47,48. In contrast, Mac-Chil3 are characterized by elevated expression of Chil3 and associated genes (Ear1/2, Ccl6, Lpl, Cd68, Ctsd), which are commonly linked to tissue remodeling and immunomodulatory macrophage states49,50. RNA in situ hybridization of HAdV-55 viral Hexon transcripts confirmed viral co-localization with macrophages (marker: F4/80+) and club cells (CC10+) (Fig. 2J and Fig. S2C, D). We observed scattered inflammatory cell infiltration at 1 dpi, serous inflammatory cell infiltration with numerous inflammatory foci at 3 dpi, and clear absorption with residual inflammatory foci at 10 dpi (Fig. 3A). These features closely resembled acute lower respiratory tract injury observed in severe human HAdV-55 infection51. Virus-positive Mac-Chil3 upregulated 415 genes enriched in cytokine/chemokine signaling, inflammatory response, and intrinsic apoptotic pathways (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B, C). Virus-positive Mac-C1qa upregulated 378 genes enriched in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and leukocyte migration (P < 0.05) (Fig. S3A, B). Time-resolved pathway analysis highlighted early activation of acute inflammatory, chemokine-mediated, apoptotic, and metabolic programs across myeloid subsets at 1 dpi, including Mac-Chil3, Mac-C1qa, Il1b-expressing macrophages (Mac-Il1b), DCs, monocytes, and Il1b-expressing neutrophils (Neu-Il1b). Inflammatory activation was sustained at 3 dpi, while Mac-Il1b- and neutrophil-driven chemokine signaling and macrophage antigen presentation persisted through 10 dpi (Fig. 3D). Epithelial cells similarly exhibited early enrichment for inflammatory, oxidative stress, and apoptotic pathways, with persistent innate immune signatures at 3 dpi and gradual resolution by 10 dpi (Fig. S3C). Spatial transcriptomics at 10 dpi revealed mechanisms of sustained immunopathology. Inflammatory, oxidative stress, immune recruitment, and fibrosis signatures co-localized within lung lesions, whereas immune clearance signatures localized to adjacent bronchioles (Fig. 3E). Spatial maps illustrated a co-localization axis of inflammation-immune recruitment-ECM remodeling-fibrosis (Fig. 3F). Robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) analysis confirmed the infiltration of Mac-Il1b and neutrophils into lesion areas (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these data support a model in which persistent inflammatory microenvironments, ongoing myeloid cell infiltration, and fibrotic remodeling contribute to sustained lung pathology following HAdV-55 infection. AGL mouse becomes highly susceptible to mild clade IIb monkeypox virus infection and pathogenesis

MPXV, a representative enveloped DNA virus, causes a zoonotic disease characterized by febrile illness, rash, and, in some cases, severe systemic complications. Since its global spread beyond endemic regions, MPXV has caused multi-country outbreaks and prompted repeated declarations of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) by the World Health Organization (WHO), highlighting its growing global health threat52,53. CAST/EiJ mice, previously established as the standard model for studying MPXV, failed to support the clade IIb MPXV infection12. Our previous study demonstrated that deficiencies in the type I and II interferon pathways render the mouse vulnerable to intranasal infection with a clinically isolated MPXV IIb virus54. To explore whether additional depletion of the IFN-λ pathway increases the vulnerability to MPXV clade IIb virus, we intranasally infected AGL mice with 4.0 × 105 PFU MPXV12 (Fig. 4A). AGL mice showed a significantly faster weight loss than the AGB6 (Fig. 4B) and severe morbidity and mortality (Fig. S4A, B). Viral titers in multiple tissues from AGL mice were considerably higher than those in AGB6 mice (Fig. 4C). Specifically, nearly 100-fold higher titers of live MPXV viruses were detected in the lungs of AGL mice (on average, 1.8 × 105 TCID50 /mL) compared to AGB6 mice (2.3 × 103 TCID50/mL) (Fig. 4D). Viral antigen A29 was localized in immune cell–rich regions of the AGL lung, trachea and turbinate contrasting with AGB6 mice (Fig. 4E and Fig. S4C). Both AGL and AGB6 mice developed typical necrotizing bronchopneumonia and interstitial pneumonia; however, marked lymphocyte infiltration was observed in the liver of AGL mice (Fig. 4F). In this section, we examine the susceptibility of AGL mice to RNA viruses. hPIVs are enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA viruses that belong to the Paramyxoviridae family. They are responsible for common acute respiratory illness and severe pneumonia, primarily affecting young children, immunocompromised individuals, and the elderly55,56. While AG129 mice were shown to support hPIV3 infection and develop mild lung pathology without exhibiting weight loss57, intranasal infection of AGL mice with 7.0 × 104 PFU of hPIV357 resulted in progressive weight loss from 1 to 7 dpi (Fig. 6A, B) and a mortality rate of 60% by day 7, in contrast to AGB6 and WT mice, both of which showed no mortality (0%; Fig. 6C). Viral RNA was detected at higher levels in AGL mice compared to AGB6 and WT mice in the turbinate (on average, 3.2 × 108 copies/μg RNA) and lung (4.0 × 107 copies/μg RNA), whereas no significant differences were observed in the trachea (1.1 × 106 copies/μg RNA) (Fig. 6D). Extremely high titers of live virus isolated from turbinate tissue (1.1 × 107 TCID₅₀/mg) (Fig. 6E). In comparison, WT and AGB6 mice had barely detected viral loads and had no infectious virus. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed robust viral replication in the lower airway of AGL but not in WT or AGB6 mice (Fig. 6F). AGL mice are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 delta variant infection without the requirement of human ACE2

SARS-CoV-2 is an enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus that causes COVID-19, a respiratory illness ranging from asymptomatic infection to severe pneumonia and systemic complications59,60. Since its emergence in late 2019, SARS-CoV-2 has caused a global pandemic with substantial morbidity and mortality, posing the greatest public health threat recorded. The expression of human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) is essential for modeling viral infection in mice16,61. However, unknown members of Coronaviredae are still predicted to cause human infections in the future62,63. Therefore, we investigated whether AGL mice are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Inoculation of 6.0 × 10⁵ PFU of the SARS-CoV-2 delta variant64 did not cause body weight loss in WT, AGB6, and AGL mice (Fig. 8A and Fig. S8A). However, viral RNA was readily detected in both turbinate and lung tissues (Fig. S8B). Although an infectious virus was isolated at low levels in the lungs of AGL mice, the titers were insufficient for reliable quantification. To further assess viral activity, levels of subgenomic RNA (sgmRNA), an indicator of active SARS-CoV-2 viral replication65–67, were substantially higher in the lungs of AGL mice (Fig. 8B). These findings suggest limited viral activity in the lungs. AGL mice also developed increased levels of receptor-binding domain (RBD)-specific IgG and IgA antibodies in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and in the nasal lavage fluid (NLF) (Fig. 8C, D). Furthermore, the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein was detected exclusively in the lungs of AGL mice, but not in WT or AGB6 mice (Fig. 8E). Infected AGL mice displayed pronounced interstitial pneumonia with diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration and reactive thickening of the alveolar interstitium in the lung, while AGB6 lungs exhibited only mild perivascular inflammation (Fig. 8F). We then examined which cells were infected by SARS-CoV-2. The N protein expression overlapped extensively with macrophages (F4/80⁺), which also co-expressed CD169. This indicates that CD169⁺ interstitial macrophages are the principal target cells of SARS-CoV-2 in AGL mice (Fig. 8G and Fig. S8C, D). In addition, AGL mice showed significant upregulation of upstream immune-activation and inflammatory genes, such as Ifng, and suppression of ISGs, along with markedly reduced expression of genes related to mucosal repair and epithelial integrity (Fig. 8H, I and Fig. S8E–H). GO enrichment analysis revealed that AGL mice can still sense virus infection by upregulating inflammatory pathways, but they did not initiate downstream interferon-responsive pathways (Fig. 8J). Overall, our study reveals that the SARS-CoV-2 delta variant can directly infect and replicate in the CD169⁺ interstitial macrophages in AGL mice, independent of human ACE2.

Discussion

We demonstrated that triple IFN pathway deficiency in the AGL mouse makes the mice highly susceptible to human viruses through intranasal infection. First, AGL supports multiple representative human respiratory virus infections. For example, HAdV-55, a non-enveloped double-stranded DNA virus, could infect DSG2-transgenic mice at approximately 10^8 PFU but caused no disease phenotype46. In this study, we demonstrated that a lower dose of 10^5 PFU, nearly 1000-fold less than used previously, can lead to robust viral replication in multiple organs and result in notable disease symptoms, including weight loss, death, and organ injury (Fig. 2B–D). Next, we examined MPXV, an enveloped double-stranded DNA virus. The clade IIb branch of MPXV is known for its low virulence and does not cause disease symptoms in the CAST/EiJ mouse12. We recently reported that AGB6 mice are vulnerable to MPXV IIb infection54. In this study, we demonstrated that AGL mice are more sensitive than AGB6 in supporting MPXV IIb infection and replication, leading to the development of severe symptoms (Fig. 4B–F). We also investigated hPIV3, an enveloped, minus-stranded RNA virus. It was not until September 2024 that AG129 mice were reported to support hPIV3 infection, but without obvious disease symptoms57. We found that hPIV3-infected AGL mice exhibited significantly higher viral replication, increased weight loss, and death, along with massive lung injury (Fig. 6B–G). In addition, SARS-CoV-2, an enveloped positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus that utilizes the ACE2 receptor for infection (the same receptor that SARS-CoV used during its outbreak in 2003, was also evaluated15,68. We found that AGL mice were permissive to SARS-CoV-2 delta variant infection and displayed a non-lethal disease course, showing no weight loss but clear viral replication in the lungs, together with interstitial pneumonia and alveolar damage (Fig. 8B–F). In contrast, the beta (B.1.351) variant strain had been reported to infect WT mice via enhanced affinity for murine ACE2, highlighting variant-dependent differences in host range and infection mechanisms69,70. Among all ACE2-expressing mouse models, only the K18-ACE2 transgenic mouse, which expresses human ACE2 in all organs via a strong promoter, developed a lethal infection, primarily due to its neurological pathogenicity71,72. Our results are consistent with observations made in models with in situ ACE2 expression driven by the native mouse ACE2 promoter64 or adenoviral vector-mediated ACE2 expression in mice73, in which only mild pneumonia and inflammation developed. HAdV-55, MPXV, hPIV3 and SARS-CoV-2 were inoculated intranasally in the experiments. In summary, the triple IFN pathway deficiency makes the AGL mouse a novel model that is capable of supporting the broadest range of human respiratory viral infections independent of engineering human viral receptors.

While both AGL and STAT1-deficient models disrupt signaling across multiple IFN pathways, they differ fundamentally in the level at which signaling is impaired. STAT1 functions as a central transcriptional regulator, and in its absence, alternative pathways such as STAT2-IRF9 can partially compensate for canonical IFN signaling, sustaining residual antiviral responses74,75. In contrast, the AGL model ablates IFN receptor-mediated signaling upstream, thereby preventing activation of both canonical and alternative IFN pathways. In addition, IFN-λ plays a non-redundant role in mucosal antiviral defense, particularly at epithelial barriers in the respiratory tract. Loss of IFNLR signaling has been associated with increased viral replication in the upper airway, and IFN-λ treatment can accelerate SARS-CoV-2 clearance76–78. Beyond antiviral effects, IFN-λ also contributes to epithelial barrier integrity and repair79. The combined disruption of type I, II, and III IFN pathways in AGL mice is therefore expected to further compromise mucosal antiviral defense. Consistent with these conceptual differences, our previous data show that STAT1−/− mice exhibit intermediate susceptibility to MPXV infection compared to AGL and AGB6 mice, with less severe weight loss and lower lung viral titers than AGL mice, but higher than AGB6 mice. Altogether, these findings suggest that AGL mice provide a more stringent and consistent system for interrogating IFN-dependent restriction of respiratory viral infection.

We have uncovered a previously hidden function of type III interferon in controlling viral replication. The Type III IFN pathway has often been viewed as secondary to the type I IFN pathway for several reasons: (1) the intracellular downstream pathways regulated by type I and III interferons are highly overlapping80, (2) type I IFN pathway generate a stronger signal compared to the type III IFN pathway81, and (3) the function of type III IFN pathway is primarily limited to the mucosal regions and certain unique cell types82,83. So far, only double-deficient mice lacking both type I and type II IFN pathways or type I and III pathways have been reported84–86. Mice that are triple-deficient in type I, II and III IFN pathways are considered nonessential. Our sequencing results indicate that the Ifnlr1 and Ifnlr2 genes are expressed in multiple organs (Fig. S1D). Furthermore, the additional deletion of Ifnl2 on top of type I and type II pathway deficiency renders them more vulnerable to multiple virus infections. These findings collectively suggest that the type III pathway constitutes another layer of systematic antiviral frontline, complementing the type I pathway. The AGL mice might become sensitive to virus infection through other routes.

Across the viral infection models examined, macrophages consistently emerged as a major cell population associated with viral signals. Viral signals detected in macrophages may reflect both phagocytic uptake and productive infection. Consistent with this, our previous data showed that viral RNA was detectable in macrophages from WT mice following hPIV3 exposure, likely due to phagocytosis. However, macrophages from AGL mice exhibited markedly higher intracellular viral loads, supporting enhanced viral replication under IFN-deficient conditions. These observations suggest that while limited viral replication may occur in immunocompetent macrophages, it is likely rapidly controlled, whereas the absence of IFN signaling permits sustained viral replication. Accordingly, macrophage-associated viral signals in AGL mice likely represent a combination of phagocytosis and amplified replication, and should be interpreted with caution when inferring cell tropism and extrapolating to immunocompetent hosts.

Traditional strategies for creating a human viral infection mouse model involve first identifying the viral entry receptor and then expressing it in the mouse68. Moreover, this approach is only effective when a virus is well-studied and understood. In contrast, the increased susceptibility of AGL mice to multiple viral infections is independent of viral receptor expression; it is due to a compromised host antiviral barrier. We speculate that the inbred mouse possesses receptors that support viral entry, or it has alternative mechanisms that facilitate this entry. Consistent with this, SARS-CoV-2 entry can be enhanced by alternative attachment factors such as Neuropilin-1 and heparan sulfate87,88, while receptor tyrosine kinases, including AXL have been implicated in ACE2-independent entry in certain cell types89. In addition, intracellular factors such as TMEM106B may facilitate endosomal trafficking and membrane fusion90. Together, these pathways may enable limited viral entry and replication even in the absence of canonical ACE2-mediated infection. In wildtype mice, a robust host defense system efficiently suppresses or eliminates invading viruses, preventing damage to the host. In comparison, the AGL mice exhibit a significantly weakened antiviral defense system, allowing multiple viral infections and replication without prior knowledge of their receptors of the corresponding receptors. Additionally, AGL mice display noticeable disease symptoms, including weight loss, organ injury, and even mortality, supporting their utility for evaluating antiviral treatments. Therefore, AGL mice can be used to study viruses that lack suitable mouse models. Such models may also be valuable in the context of epidemic and pandemic preparedness.

Our study has several limitations. First, because the functions of interferon pathways are not restricted to innate immunity against pathogens, other physiological functions will also be affected in AGL mice, despite their non-lethality. For example, the AGL mice grow more slowly than wild-type C57BL/6 mice. We also noticed that homozygous female AGL mice experience pregnancy complications, delivering small litters when fed normal chow. The addition of high-vitamin E-containing ingredients rescues homozygous female AGL mice and allows them to have normal pups. Second, we tested only a limited panel of four viruses that were readily available in our laboratory, leaving uncertainty regarding the model’s performance against other emerging or re-emerging viral pathogens with epidemic or pandemic potential, as well as zoonotic viruses with uncertain host adaptability. We will continue outsourcing other viruses and test their performance. Third, it remains unclear to what extent the AGL mice recapitulate the pathogenesis of viral infections in humans. While AGL mice permit viral replication and display disease manifestations, their profoundly altered interferon signaling may not fully reflect the immune responses and disease progression observed in immunocompetent human hosts. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating findings from this model to human disease. Finally, IFN pathways also participate and influence the adaptive immune response by shaping the activation and differentiation of T and B cells. The ablation of all type I, II, and III IFN pathways in the AGL mice shuts down the downstream cascade, thereby potentially compromising the sensitization of antigen-presenting cells (APC) and signal cross-talk between APC and T and B cells. Unfortunately, we have not addressed this issue in the current study.

More effort is required to translate these findings into clinical practice. First, the model should be further validated with additional clinically relevant viral strains and expanded to include co-infection scenarios common in human populations. Second, standardized protocols for viral inoculation, sample collection, and efficacy assessment should be established to ensure reproducibility across research teams. Finally, collaborative multidisciplinary evaluations are needed to generate sufficient data to support regulatory acceptance, enabling the model to be incorporated into routine preclinical evaluation pipelines for antiviral drugs.

Methods

Ethical statement

All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Eighth People’s Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University (Approval number: IACUC-LF-2024-05). For SARS-CoV-2 and MPXV infection, all procedures were performed in an ABSL-3 facility with approval from the Biosafety Committee of the Guangzhou Eighth People’s Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University.

Study design

This study aimed to develop a triple interferon pathway-deficient mouse model (AGL) with targeted deletions of type I (IFNAR), II (IFNGR), and III (IFNLR) interferon receptor signaling components, and to systematically evaluate its susceptibility to a broad spectrum of human viruses and utility for preclinical antiviral therapeutic testing. First, the AGL mouse model was generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing on a C57BL/6 background, targeting the tandemly located Ifnar2, Ifnlr2, Ifnar1, and Ifngr2 genes on chromosome 16 to ablate all three interferon pathways. Subsequently, the susceptibility of AGL mice to five representative human viruses—including double-stranded DNA viruses (HAdV-55, MPXV clade IIb), negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus (hPIV3), and positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (SARS-CoV-2 delta variant) was assessed. Main analysis included (1) body weight changes, survival rate, clinical scoring; (2) viral tropism and titers (qPCR/RT-qPCR for viral nucleic acids, TCID₅₀ for infectious virions), pathogenicity, and host responses and histopathological analysis; (3) transcriptomic profiling (bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics [Stereo-seq]). For translational application of the AGL mice, proof-of-concept studies were conducted using AGL mice infected with MPXV clade IIb (treated with cidofovir) and hPIV3 (treated with GS-441524).

Mice

Three genetically distinct mouse strains on a C57BL/6 background were used in this study: (1) Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6; (2) AGB6 mice deficient in both type I and II interferon receptors (Ifnar1-/-Ifngr1-/-); and (3) AGL mice deficient in type I, II, and III interferon receptors (Ifnar1-/-Ifnar2-/-Ifngr2-/-Ifnlr2-/-). ABG6 mice were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center and subsequently bred in-house. The Wild-type and AGL mice were bred within our laboratory. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in our animal facilities (Biosafety Level (BSL) 2 or 3) in accordance with our Animal Studies Committee-approved protocol. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages at 23 °C with 40–60% humidity and a 12:12-h light-dark cycle. They were provided with nutrient-rich, sterile food and water. For breeding, AGL mice were maintained at a male-to-female ratio of 1:3. Under standard conditions, litter sizes were typically 2-3 pups per litter. To improve reproductive performance, sunflower seeds (5 seeds per mouse per day), which are rich in vitamin E, were provided as dietary supplementation, resulting in an increased average litter size of approximately 6 pups per litter.

AGL mice generation

AGL mice were generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing in fertilized C57BL/6 J embryos. Female C57BL/6 J mice (4-6 weeks old) were superovulated and mated with proven C57BL/6J males. Zygotes were harvested from the ampullae of the oviducts 14-16 h post-mating, and only pronuclear-stage embryos were selected for further manipulation.

The embryos were microinjected with in vitro-transcribed Cas9 mRNA and guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting Ifnar2, Ifnlr2, Ifnar1, and Ifngr2 (Chromosome 16: 193 kb fragments), then cultured in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37 °C until reaching the 2-cell stage. Viable embryos were surgically transferred into the oviducts of pseudopregnant recipient females. Post-surgical recovery was supported by heat sources such as a warming lamp or electric blanket. Offspring were delivered ~21 days after embryo transfer and monitored daily after birth.

Toe clipping for genotyping was performed on postnatal day 7. Genomic DNA was extracted for PCR-based screening using the following primers:

Fwd 1: 5′ - GGGTGCCTGGCCTAGTGATG - 3′,

Fwd 2: 5′ - TCCAGGAAGGCCTTGGCAGG - 3′;

Rev 1: 5′ -CGCAGACCTCGAGTGAACAA - 3′,

Rev 2: 5′ - GCGGCCACCTTTGCACCACC - 3′.

Targeted deletions were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of PCR amplicons.

Genotyping of AGL mice

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse toe samples using PCR sample processing solution (GDSBio, P9051, P9052), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific primers were designed for both ends of the targeted gene and within the gene to differentiate knockout and WT mice.

WT primers:

Fwd: 5′- CTGACAGCATTCATAGCCCTTCC -3′,

Rev: 5′- TACGACGTTTTCCTGGCTCTGA -3′.

Knockout primers:

Fwd: 5′- CTGGGGAATAGTAAGCAAAGACCC -3′,

Rev: 5′- AACCTTGCACTCTCCTAAACTGTGC -3′.

PCR reactions were assembled in a total volume of 10 μL, containing 5 µL of 2× phanta max master Mix (Vazyme, P515-01), 1 µL of nuclease-free water, 0.4 µL of each primer (10 pmol/µL), and 4 µL of template DNA (100 ng/µL). The PCR program included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 68 °C (decreasing by 0.5 °C per cycle) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min and a hold at 10 °C.

Western blot analysis

Small intestine tissues were rinsed three times with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and homogenized in 200 μL of RIPA lysis buffer using a tissue grinder at 60 Hz for 3 min. The homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, and either boiled for 10 min for immediate use or stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to 0.2 µm nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After five washes in TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, protein bands were visualized using the Bio-Rad imaging system. The following antibodies were used: anti-ACTIN (Ray Antibody, RM2001, 1:10,000), anti-IFNAR2 (ABclonal, A1769, 1:1000), anti-IFNGR2 (ABclonal, A14221, 1:1000), and anti-IL10RB (ABclonal, A15680, 1:1000).

Isolation, culture, and cytokine stimulation of Mouse Embryonic Fibroblasts

Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were isolated from embryos at embryonic day E12.5 to E13.5. Embryonic tissue was dissected and enzymatically dissociated into single cells using trypsin. The isolated cells were cultured in MEF growth medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco), and 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA; Gibco).

To verify the function of the interferon pathways, MEF cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ atmosphere, cells were treated with recombinant mouse cytokines: IFN-α4, IFN-γ, or IFN-λ2 (each at 500 ng/mL; PeproTech). Stimulation was performed for either 8 h or 16 h. Following treatment, cells were harvested using trypsin, collected by centrifugation, resuspended in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction and downstream gene expression analysis.

Viral infection

Human adenovirus type 55 (HAdV-55) and human parainfluenza virus type 3 (hPIV3) were kept in Dr. Liqiang Feng’s lab (Guangzhou Institutes of Biomedicine and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences). Monkeypox virus (MPXV) clade IIb isolate MPXV-IIb-gz8h-01 (GISAID accession ID: EPI_ISL_18040906) was isolated from a skin lesion swab of a confirmed case. The SARS-CoV-2 delta variant was isolated from a nasopharyngeal swab of a confirmed COVID-19 patient. Mice were anesthetized with 1.25% (v/v) tribromoethanol prior to inoculation. Intranasal infection was performed with 70 µL of viral suspension (HAdV-55, 3.5 × 10⁵ PFU; hPIV3, 7.0 × 10⁴ PFU; MPXV, 4.0 × 10⁵ PFU; SARS-CoV-2 delta strain, 6.0 × 10⁵ PFU).

Therapeutics

For the MPXV infection model, AGL mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of cidofovir (10 mg/kg; Sigma) from day post-infection (dpi) 0 to dpi 3, followed by 5 mg/kg daily from dpi 4 to dpi 7. Treatment control animals were administered an equal volume of PBS. At dpi 7, the experimental endpoint, turbinate, trachea, lung, heart, liver, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, spleen, kidney, testis, muscle, skin, and blood were collected for MPXV DNA quantification or histopathological analysis. For the hPIV3 infection model, AGL mice were administered either vehicle or GS-441524 (50 mg/kg) by intragastric administration twice daily from dpi-1 to dpi 6. At dpi 7, the turbinate, trachea, and lung were harvested for hPIV3 RNA quantification, infectious virus titration, or histopathological analysis.

Sample collection

For viral load analysis, tissues and blood were collected as follows: turbinate, trachea, lung, liver, and kidney (HAdV-55 model); turbinate, trachea, lung, heart, liver, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, spleen, kidney, testis, muscle, and skin (MPXV model); turbinate, trachea, and lung (hPIV3 model); turbinate and lung (SARS-CoV-2 model). Each tissue sample was placed in a bead homogenization tube containing 0.8 mL Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 1% penicillin (100 U mL⁻¹)-streptomycin (100 µg mL⁻¹) and homogenized. For nucleic acid extraction, 200 µL of the homogenate was mixed with 180 µL buffer ATL plus 20 µL proteinase K (Qiagen) for DNA isolation, or with 800 µL TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for RNA isolation. Viral DNA or RNA was subsequently quantified by qPCR. The remaining homogenates were clarified by centrifugation (6000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and the supernatants were used for infectious virus titration.

Viral titers

Infectious titers of HAdV-55, MPXV, SARS-CoV-2, and hPIV3 were determined by endpoint dilution assays in 96-well plates using permissive cell lines (HEK293 for HAdV-55, Vero E6 for MPXV and SARS-CoV-2, and LLC-MK2 for hPIV3). Cytopathic effects were monitored microscopically and scored, and viral titers were calculated by the Reed–Muench method, expressed as the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID₅₀) per milliliter of lung or turbinate homogenate.

Viral loads

Genomic DNA or RNA was extracted from blood and tissues using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen) or TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), depending on the virus, and quantified by qPCR or RT–qPCR on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Viral copy numbers were calculated from standard curves generated with serially diluted viral DNA, RNA, or plasmid standards of known copy number. For HAdV-55, DNA was extracted using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit and quantified by SYBR Green-based qPCR (FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master, Roche). The primers were as follows: Ad-F, 5′-CATGGTGTGGAAGATGAACTTCC-3′, and Ad-R, 5′-GGAAACTTCGCCA TAGATTGGC-3′46. Thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 5 min; 45 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; and a melting curve was produced at 65 °C to 95 °C with an increment of 0.5 °C per cycle for 5 s. For MPXV, DNA was extracted using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit and quantified by probe-based qPCR with primers and probe targeting the F3L gene (MPXV Detection Kit, Da Nong Co. Ltd, Guangdong, China). Thermal cycling conditions were 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 5 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 35 s. For hPIV3, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and quantified by one-step SYBR Green RT-qPCR (BeyoFast SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR Kit, Beyotime) with primers targeting the HN gene (HN-F: 5′-TGATGAAAGATCAGATTATGCAT-3′, HN-R: 5′-CCAGGACACCCAGTTGTG-3′)57. Thermal cycling conditions were 50 °C for 15 min, 95 °C for 2 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s; and a melting curve was produced at 65 °C to 95 °C with an increment of 0.5 °C per cycle for 5 s. For SARS-CoV-2, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and quantified by probe-based RT-qPCR (2019-nCoV Detection Kit, Daan Co. Ltd, Guangdong, China) targeting the N and ORF1a/b genes. Thermal cycling conditions were 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 35 s.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific IgG and IgA were measured in nasal lavage fluid (NLF) and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) using commercial ELISA kits (Wuhan Fine Biotech), following the manufacturer’s instructions. NLF and BALF samples were heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min before use to reduce potential viral risk. A total of 100 µL of each sample was used per well for antibody quantification.

Histology

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for over 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general morphology and inflammatory cell infiltration, or with Masson’s trichrome for assessment of fibrosis and collagen deposition, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Histopathological changes were evaluated by light microscopy in a blinded manner, including epithelial integrity, alveolar structure, inflammatory infiltrates, hemorrhage, edema, necrosis, and tissue remodeling, depending on the infection model.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for over 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). For MPXV detection, sections were incubated with a rabbit polyclonal anti-vaccinia virus antibody (Abcam, ab35219, 1:500), followed by a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB23303, 1:200) and an alkaline phosphatase–based chromogenic detection system with Fast Red as substrate. For SARS-CoV-2 detection, sections were incubated with a rabbit polyclonal anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid antibody (GeneTex, GTX135357, 1:500), followed by the same secondary antibody and detection system. In both cases, nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and images were acquired using a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH).

RNAscope in situ hybridization

In situ hybridization was performed on 4% paraformaldehyde - fixed, paraffin -embedded lung sections using the PolyBranch HCR™ RNA FISH Kit (RiboBio, HKR12D) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 4 μm sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to Proteinase K digestion to expose target RNA. After blocking, sections were incubated with digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled probes specific to the target HAdV-55 hexon transcripts (100 μL per section), followed by hybridization at 37 °C for 3 h in a humidified chamber. Hybridized probes were amplified using a branched-DNA signal amplification system and detected with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-DIG antibody (Biogradetech, BGT-ANT-01017, 1:1,000). Fluorescent signals were developed with a tyramide signal amplification (TSA) system using TSA-Cyanine 570 at room temperature for 10 min. Sections were counterstained with DAPI (Proteintech, CM07245), mounted with antifade reagent, and imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

For the HAdV-55 infection model, immunofluorescence was performed to co-detect HAdV-55 Hexon transcripts and macrophages following in situ hybridization. After antibody stripping, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-F4/80 antibody (CST, 70076, 1:300), followed by incubation with a ready-to-use HRP goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. Fluorescent signals were developed with a TSA 520 at room temperature for 10 min.

For the SARS-CoV-2 infection model, Multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF) was performed on 4-μm paraffin-embedded lung sections. Sections were processed sequentially using primary antibodies paired with a TSA 7-color kit (Absinbio, abs50015-100T), followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. Briefly, deparaffinized sections were incubated with anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody (GeneTex, GTX135357, 1:500) for 30 min, followed by HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2) for 10 min. Signal was developed with TSA 690 for 10 min at room temperature, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were washed in TBST, subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (90 °C, 15 min, microwave at 20% power), and cooled to room temperature in the same buffer. The same process was repeated for the following antibodies/fluorescent dyes, in order: anti-F4/80 (CST, 70076, 1:300)/TSA 620, anti-CD169 (CST, 82470, 1:1000)/TSA 570, anti-CD11b (Abcam, 133357, 1:2000)/TSA 780, anti-CD11c (CST, 97585, 1:200)/TSA 480, and anti-AGER (Abcam, 216329, 1:2000)/TSA 520. After completion of all staining rounds, sections were counterstained with DAPI (Proteintech, CM07245), rinsed in distilled water, and coverslipped. Sections were air-dried and imaged using a Pannoramic MIDI II scanner (3DHISTECH). Image analysis was performed with HALO software (Indica Labs).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol-chloroform method. Library construction was performed using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit V3.1 (MGI) with mRNA enriched by Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The purified mRNA was sequentially fragmented, reverse-transcribed, end-repaired, adapter-ligated, and PCR-amplified to generate sequencing libraries. The resulting libraries were circularized into DNA nanoballs (DNBs) and sequenced on the DNBSEQ platform to generate 100 bp paired-end reads, yielding approximately 40 million reads per sample. For bioinformatic processing, the raw sequencing reads were first assessed for quality using FastQC. Adapters, low-quality reads, and reads containing ambiguous bases (N) were then filtered out using fastp. The high-quality cleaned reads were subsequently aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using STAR. Gene expression quantification was performed using featureCounts to generate the count matrix. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, and functional enrichment analysis of gene sets was carried out with the clusterProfiler package.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Viable single-cell suspensions were freshly prepared and accurately quantified using a Countess cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing libraries were constructed with the MGI DNBelab C Series High-Throughput Single-Cell RNA Library Prep Kit V3.0, following the manufacturer’s protocol for droplet-based single-cell barcoding, reverse transcription, and PCR amplification. The resulting libraries were sequenced on the BGI DNBSEQ platform to generate paired-end reads, yielding approximately 100 GB of data per sample. A custom reference genome was first created by combining the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) with the viral genome. Raw sequencing reads were then processed using the BGI DNBelab_C_Series_HT Single-Cell Analysis Software for quality control and alignment to the integrated reference genome, generating a count matrix. Subsequent analysis was performed in Seurat, which included cell filtering, normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Cell types were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes. Viral target cell types were identified by analyzing the expression levels of viral genes across the clustered cell populations. Differential gene expression analysis on identified clusters was conducted, and functional enrichment of the resultant gene sets was performed using clusterProfiler.

Spatial transcriptomics and bioinformatic analysis

Mouse tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for over 48 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and mounted onto BGI Stereo-seq chips. The sections were then subjected to ssDNA fluorescence staining and microscopic imaging. After permeabilization, mRNA was captured in situ by probes containing spatial barcodes and poly(T) tails, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA and amplification to construct sequencing libraries. The libraries were sequenced on the BGI DNBSEQ platform, generating approximately 3 billion reads per sample. A combined reference genome was created by integrating the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) with the viral genome. Raw reads were aligned to this reference using the Stereo-seq Analysis Workflow (SAW). By integrating microscopic images, sequencing data were mapped to their spatial coordinates on tissue sections to quantify gene expression and generate a spatially resolved expression matrix. Image quality assessment and processing were performed using StereoMap to visualize spatial localization and expression levels of genes. Based on the spatial expression matrix at bin50 resolution (25 μm), viral spatial distribution and expression levels were visualized using Seurat. Cell type composition in the spatial transcriptomic data was deconvoluted using RCTD, with a single-cell RNA-seq expression matrix as the reference.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). For multiple-group comparisons, the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was applied. For pairwise comparisons, unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests were used as appropriate. For one-sample analyses, two-tailed one-sample t-test or a two-tailed one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as appropriate. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Exact P-values are reported unless smaller than 0.0001, in which case they are indicated as P < 0.0001. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. Each viral infection model was independently established and validated in at least three experiments with consistent results.
