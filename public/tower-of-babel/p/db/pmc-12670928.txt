Response characteristics of near-surface air temperature to soil moisture across different climate zones in china
Authors: Lihua Lan, Zhenbo Wang, Fei He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySoil moisture (SM) not only directly influences surface energy balance but also significantly alters near-surface air temperature (T) by modulating latent and sensible heat fluxes, thereby affecting regional climate and ecosystem stability. This study decodes how SM shapes T across China from 1980 to 2023, using the evaporative fraction-soil moisture (EF-SM) coupling method. Key findings highlight strong spatial heterogeneity: while most regions cool with wetter soils—especially inner Mongolia (∂T/∂SM, −22.38) and closed shrublands, the Qinghai-Tibet plateau shows unusual warming feedback (+1.07). SM-driven temperature changes are most extreme in arid inner Mongolia (1.76°C), far surpassing humid south China (0.28°C). The regulatory role also shifts by climate: EF directly controls temperature in dry zones, while SM indirectly influences it in humid regions. These insights refine climate predictions and ecological strategies under global change.Subject areas: Earth sciences, Atmospheric science, Earth-surface processes

Introduction

The evolution of near-surface air temperature (T) is strongly influenced by soil moisture (SM),1,2,3,4,5,6 which in turn modifies local climatic conditions and land-atmosphere interactions.7 By modulating net radiative energy and the partitioning of sensible and latent heat fluxes between the land and atmosphere, SM plays a critical role in determining key meteorological variables, including T, humidity, boundary layer stability, atmospheric circulation, and precipitation.3 Under SM-limited conditions, evapotranspiration is directly constrained, leading to elevated T for a given net radiation level and driving positive land-atmosphere climate feedback.8,9 Conversely, when SM is abundant, cooling occurs through enhanced evapotranspiration. However, this relationship is nonlinear, as evapotranspiration does not increase indefinitely with SM; beyond a certain threshold, further increases in SM no longer induce additional cooling, thereby weakening the coupling strength among water, carbon, and energy cycles.10,11

In recent years, the relationship between SM and temperature has garnered increasing attention in climate modeling.1,12,13,14 Research indicates that SM variability significantly impacts near-surface climate and is closely linked to extreme heat events and heatwaves.1,15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22 Studies have established that droughts amplify the likelihood of extreme warm events, as reduced SM limits evaporative cooling, enhancing surface warming.21 In regions such as Europe and North America, where climate models project increased heatwave frequency and intensity,23,24 SM-T coupling has been identified as a key driver of summer climate variability.1,25 Global modeling studies, such as the GLACE experiments,12 have identified “hotspots” of strong SM-T coupling, particularly in transitional climate zones where SM deficits amplify heatwaves.1 At regional scales, studies highlighted the importance of SM in modifying near-surface temperature variability.16,26,27 Statistical analyses of observational data21,28 further confirmed that dry soils exacerbate high temperatures, reinforcing positive feedback loops. The pivotal role of SM deficits was particularly evident during the 2018 European heatwave.29,30 Further analysis revealed that persistent anticyclonic conditions, when coupled with prolonged SM reduction, significantly amplified both the 2003 European and 2010 Russian heatwave events.31 This finding aligns with earlier work, which identified severe SM depletion as a key contributor to the record-breaking 2003 heatwave.32 In China, the influence of SM on temperature variability is particularly pronounced in the east.12 Observations have identified strong SM-temperature coupling in northern China, where increased SM corresponds to reduced temperatures, whereas no such relationship was observed in southern China.33 Similarly, the WRF model was used to simulate SM-temperature interactions, demonstrating that the region spanning from northeastern to central China exhibits high SM-temperature coupling intensity.34

Vegetation dynamics are closely linked to SM-climate feedback mechanisms. The coupling between vegetation and the climate system is influenced by SM and temperature35 and may trigger shifts in ecosystem-climate interactions over extended temporal scales.36,37,38,39 The impact of SM on temperature is primarily mediated through plant transpiration,22 with vegetation characteristics (e.g., land-cover type) and their dynamic variations playing a critical role in modulating these interactions across different regions. For instance, it was demonstrated that geographic variations in land-cover types in drought-prone regions of France are distinctly reflected in local temperature anomalies due to differential adaptations to drought and thermal stress.40 Similarly, ecosystem-specific sensitivities to drought during the heatwave event were observed in forest stands.41 These findings are underscored as significant in the role of vegetation type and dynamics in regulating SM and local climatic conditions. Furthermore, model-based projections suggest that the climatic effects of land cover and land use changes may be indirectly realized through alterations in SM and the Bowen ratio, subsequently influencing the climate system.42,43,44,45,46 Such changes may exhibit strong correlations with vegetation dynamics.46,47 Concurrently, vegetation dynamics may also interact with SM-climate feedbacks and be modulated by them.48,49 Over longer timescales, these interactions may induce multiple equilibria and regime shifts within the climate system.36,39,50,51

Although previous studies have investigated the influence of SM on land surface temperature, most have focused on specific geographic regions or vegetation types, lacking systematic comparisons across climatic zones and vegetation types.2,9 Furthermore, how SM-T interactions vary among different climatic zones and vegetation types,9 and how these interactions feedback to influence near-surface thermal regimes remain poorly understood.31,52 To address these gaps, this study provides a systematic assessment of SM-T coupling mechanisms across diverse ecosystems in China from 1980 to 2023. By integrating observational data and model simulations, we aim to (1) quantify the spatial heterogeneity of SM-T interactions under different vegetation covers and climate zones from 1980 to 2023, and (2) reveal how SM and EF variations modulate T dynamics in different regions. Our findings advance the understanding of land-atmosphere thermal feedbacks in China’s terrestrial environments, offering insights for regional climate prediction and heat stress mitigation.

Study areas

China, characterized by its vast territory and diverse natural conditions, exhibits a wide range of climate types and complex land cover patterns, making it an ideal region for conducting large-scale environmental studies. To systematically analyze spatial and temporal variations across different eco-climatic contexts, the country was divided into seven major climatic zones: the temperate and warm-temperate deserts in the northwest (NW), the temperate grasslands of inner Mongolia (IM), the temperate humid and sub-humid regions of northeast China (NE), the warm-temperate humid and sub-humid regions of north China (NH), the subtropical humid regions of central and south China (CSC), the Qinghai-Tibet plateau (QTP), and the tropical humid regions of the south (TC). Land-use and land-cover data were obtained from the MCD12Q1 product at a spatial resolution of 500 m, which includes 17 distinct land cover types based on the MODIS/MOD12Q1 classification scheme. These categories comprise: evergreen needleleaf forests (ENF), evergreen broadleaf forests (EBF), deciduous needleleaf forests (DNF), deciduous broadleaf forests (DBF), closed shrublands (CSH), open shrublands (OSH), woody savannas (WSA), savannas (SAV), wetlands (WET), croplands (CRO), grasslands (GRA), mixed forests (MF), cropland/natural vegetation mosaics (CNV), water bodies (WB), permanent snow and ice (PSI), urban and built-up lands (UBL), and barren lands (BAN) (Figure 1).

Results

Spatiotemporal variation of near-surface air temperature, evaporative fraction, and soil moisture

The reliability of a reanalysis dataset in capturing latent heat flux, sensible heat flux, and SM was assessed against observational measurements. A high level of consistency was observed between the reanalysis products and in situ data across both spatial distributions and temporal dynamics, affirming the robustness of the reanalysis-derived estimates (Figure 2). This study revealed significant spatial heterogeneity in T, evaporative fraction (EF), and SM across different climatic zones and land-use types (Figure 3). The Qinghai-Tibet plateau exhibits the lowest T (10°C). In contrast, inner Mongolia experiences relatively moderate T (around 25°C). The northeastern and northern regions both record T exceeding 25°C, with north China approaching 30°C. Evergreen broadleaf forests and deciduous broadleaf forests both exhibit T above 25°C. Evergreen needleleaf forests are slightly cooler (approximately 23°C), whereas croplands and mixed forests also surpass 25°C. Grasslands are relatively cooler (around 22°C), while savannas and woody savannas exceed 25°C. Overall, pronounced T disparities are observed among regions, with Qinghai-Tibet plateau registering the lowest and the tropical humid regions with the highest T (Figures 3A and 3B).

The northwest region generally exhibited lower EF values, whereas the subtropical humid region, northeast China, north China, and the tropical humid region display higher EF values, with the tropical humid region showing the strongest evaporative capacity, consistent with its hot and humid climatic conditions (Figure 3A). Notably, the northwest region exhibits the lowest EF values among all climatic zones, reflecting its arid and low-precipitation environment, which limits evaporation. Among vegetation types, forests exhibit higher EF values, indicating that forest ecosystems possess a stronger capacity for evapotranspiration. In contrast, grasslands show significantly lower EF values compared to forested areas, while unused land (e.g., bare soil and deserts) exhibits the lowest EF values (Figures 3C and 3D), further underscoring the critical regulatory role of vegetation cover in surface water cycling.

In different climate zones, tropical climates maintain the highest mean SM (0.35 m3/m3), followed by humid subtropical regions (0.3 m3/m3). In contrast, the inner Mongolian and the northwest region exhibit substantially lower SM values (0.2 m3/m3 and 0.25 m3/m3, respectively) (Figures 3E and 3F). Evergreen needleleaf forests and evergreen broadleaf forests demonstrate the highest SM values (0.35 m3/m3), coupled with relatively low coefficients of variation. Croplands/natural vegetation mosaics exhibit intermediate SM levels (0.32 m3/m3), whereas open shrublands and barren lands display the lowest SM values (0.15 m3/m3). Collectively, these findings reveal that humid climatic zones (e.g., tropical and subtropical regions) exhibit significantly higher SM levels compared to arid and semi-arid regions (e.g., temperate grasslands and deserts). Moreover, forest ecosystems—particularly evergreen forests—retain the most abundant SM, while sparsely vegetated areas (e.g., open shrublands) experience the most limited water availability.

Spatiotemporal trends of T, EF, and SM

Between 1980 and 2023, China exhibited a pronounced upward trend in T, with a warming rate of 0.0273°C/yr (p < 0.001) (Figure 4A). The most pronounced warming type occurred in deciduous needleleaf forests (0.040 ± 0.005°C/yr), followed by mixed forests (0.035 ± 0.003°C/yr). Croplands warmed at a 0.025 ± 0.002°C/yr rate. Evergreen needleleaf forests displayed the weakest warming trend (0.013 ± 0.002°C/yr), indicating their comparatively delayed response to climate change. Other vegetation types (evergreen broadleaf forests, deciduous broadleaf forests, closed shrublands, open shrublands, woody savannas, and savannas) exhibited intermediate warming rates ranging from 0.020 to 0.025 (±0.002–0.003) °C/yr. The northeast China and north China exhibited similar warming rates (0.030 ± 0.002°C/yr), while the subtropical humid region showed a slightly lower rate (0.028 ± 0.004°C/yr). The Qinghai-Tibet plateau also experienced relatively moderate warming (0.022 ± 0.006°C/yr) (Figures 4B and 4C).

From 1980 to 2023, the EF in China exhibited an overall declining trend (Figure 5A), with an average rate of −0.0006/yr (p = 0.13). However, significant variations in EF trends were observed across different climate zones and land-use types (Figure 5B). Notably, the inner Mongolia region showed the most pronounced decline in EF (−0.0049 ± 0.0004/yr), followed by the northwest region (−0.0026 ± 0.0002/yr). The northeastern and northern regions also displayed declining EF trends, with rates of −0.0024 ± 0.0002/yr and −0.0027 ± 0.0003/yr, respectively. In different land-use types, open shrublands experienced the most significant EF reduction (−0.0068 ± 0.0006/yr), whereas croplands exhibited slight increasing trends (0.0002 ± 0.00008/yr and 0.0001 ± 0.00006/yr, respectively) (Figures 5B and 5C). It is noteworthy that EF in a few regions, including the tropical humid region, barren lands, and grasslands, showed an upward trend.

SM exhibited a significant increasing trend from 1980 to 2023 (p < 0.01), with an average rate of 0.0002 m3/m3/yr (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A). Among the climate zones, the Qinghai-Tibet plateau showed the most pronounced SM increase, with an annual growth rate of 0.0006 ± 0.00008/yr. The northwest and the subtropical humid regions exhibited similar annual increases of 0.0002 ± 0.00004/yr. In contrast, inner Mongolia and northeast China displayed slight declining trends, with annual rates of −0.0001 ± 0.00007/yr and −7.00 × 10−6 ± 8.0 × 10−7/yr, respectively. Regarding land use types, open shrublands experienced the highest annual increase (0.0007/yr), while most vegetation types showed modest SM gains (Figures 6B and 6C). Collectively, although the majority of regions and land-use types exhibited increasing SM trends, the inner Mongolia and northwest climate zones demonstrated unique decreasing characteristics.

Impact of soil moisture variations on near-surface air temperature

The spatial distribution of the sensitivity of T to EF (∂T/∂EF) at the national scale exhibits pronounced heterogeneity, quantifying the magnitude of T response per unit change in EF (Figures 7A and 7C). The Qinghai-Tibet plateau demonstrates the highest positive sensitivity (8.09 ± 0.778), indicating that an increase in EF significantly elevates T in this region. In contrast, the tropical humid zone exhibits the strongest negative sensitivity (−9.15 ± 0.898), reflecting the effective suppression of surface warming through enhanced latent heat flux under abundant SM conditions. Other regions, including north China, inner Mongolia, northeast China, and the central subtropical zone, also display negative sensitivities (ranging from −4.88 ± 0.587 to −6.85 ± 0.694), though their magnitudes are weaker than those in TC. From the perspective of land-use types, grasslands exhibit positive sensitivities (3.34 ± 0.521), whereas evergreen broadleaf forests and closed shrublands show the most pronounced negative sensitivities (−9.84 ± 0.679 and −10.17 ± 1.21, respectively). Overall, while the sensitivity of T to EF is the lowest in the northwest and predominantly negative across most of the country, this trend reverses in the Tibetan plateau, where EF has a positive effect on T. The strong negative sensitivities observed in areas with high vegetation cover (e.g., evergreen broadleaf forests and closed shrublands) further underscore the critical role of biophysical processes in local climate regulation.

The sensitivity of the EF to SM (∂EF/∂SM) exhibits significant differences across climate zones and land-use types (Figures 7B and 7D). Among the climate zones, the highest positive partial derivative value is observed in the northwest region (5.72 ± 0.579), indicating that the EF in this area is most sensitive to SM, with SM rise significantly enhancing EF. Similarly, the inner Mongolia and Qinghai-Tibet plateau regions also demonstrate strong positive sensitivities, with partial derivative values of 3.67 ± 0.543 and 3.22 ± 0.248, respectively. In contrast, the tropical humid region exhibits the lowest partial derivative value (0.42 ± 0.05), reflecting the weakest response of EF to SM changes. Regarding land-use types, barren lands show the most pronounced positive responses to SM (5.60 ± 0.679) (Figure 7D). Overall, the sensitivity to SM changes varies distinctly across regions and land use types, with the highest sensitivity observed in the northwest, while the TC exhibits the lowest response.

The spatial distributions of T sensitivity to SM calculated using the alternative datasets (Figures 8A and 8B) are consistent with those derived from ERA5 (Figure 8C). Furthermore, Figure 8D indicates that during the summer months, most regions of China exhibited transitional SM conditions—a state in which SM-T coupling is the strongest. This further confirms that the influence of SM on T can be effectively studied through variations in SM and EF. Additionally, Figure 8F validates the robustness of the EF-SM coupling method using observational data from Chinese flux stations. The results demonstrate that this method accurately estimates the critical SM threshold, thereby providing a reliable basis for quantifying the impact of SM on T.

The sensitivity of T to SM exhibits significant differences across climate zones and land-use types (Figure 8E). Inner Mongolia demonstrates the highest sensitivity, with a partial derivative of −22.38 ± 2.14, likely attributable to enhanced evaporative cooling effects associated with increased SM. Similarly, north China and northeast China also exhibit relatively high sensitivities, with partial derivatives of −12.25 ± 1.32 and −7.15 ± 0.89, respectively. In contrast, the Qinghai-Tibet plateau shows an opposite trend, with a partial derivative of 1.07 ± 0.14, suggesting that increased SM in this region may coincide with rising T. Evergreen needleleaf forests, evergreen broadleaf forests, and deciduous needleleaf forests display negative responses, with croplands showing the most pronounced negative effect (−9.06 ± 1.05). Closed shrublands and deciduous needleleaf forests also exhibit strong sensitivities to SM (Figure 8E). Overall, increased SM in most regions and land use types tends to lower T through enhanced evaporative cooling. However, the Qinghai-Tibet plateau exhibits unique positive feedback mechanisms.

The extent influence of SM on T was quantified using the ΔTmax formula, reflecting its regulatory magnitude on T from 1980 to 2023 (Figure 9A). Analysis of the influence of SM on T across different climate zones and land-use types (Figure 9C) revealed that the inner Mongolia region exhibited the strongest influence (1.76 ± 0.2°C), followed by north China and northeast China (1.15 ± 0.09°C and 0.74 ± 0.06°C, respectively), while the tropical humid regions and Qinghai-Tibet plateau showed weaker effects (0.28 ± 0.03°C and 0.25 ± 0.03°C, respectively). Among land-use types, closed shrublands (0.91 ± 0.10°C), croplands (0.88 ± 0.09°C), and grasslands (0.80 ± 0.08°C) demonstrated significant influences, indicating that changes in SM exert a stronger regulatory effect on T in these land-cover types. So, inner Mongolia exhibited the most pronounced impact, while the tropical humid regions in the south showed the weakest influence. Further analysis using δ (∂T∂EF -∂T∂SM) to delineate the dominant regions where SM or EF controls T (Figure 8D) demonstrated that T in the inner Mongolia region and the eastern Qinghai-Tibet plateau is predominantly governed by EF, whereas in other regions, SM plays a more dominant role in regulating T.

Discussion

Prior research supporting the role of soil moisture in air temperature variability

Previous studies have demonstrated that temperature variability and extremes on seasonal timescales across specific regions of North America exhibit significant sensitivity to land-surface conditions, particularly SM, under both current12,53,54,55,56,57 and future climate scenarios,58,59,60 which are consistent with our research focus (Figure 9). Moreover, multi-model assessments focused on the impact of accurate initialization of SM field on forecast skill highlighted the key role of realistic initialization of SM on sub-seasonal forecast skill of temperature over several regions of the world.61,62 Extensive research has confirmed the profound influence of SM on near-surface temperatures. In north China, SM feedback accounted for 36% of temperature variations from 1994 to 2012,63 while in subtropical southwestern China, SM contributed to 10%–50% of temperature variability.64 In monsoon-dominated India, declining SM has increased the frequency of extreme temperatures.65 Multiple studies have reported a temperature rise of 0.32°C–0.35°C per decade over the past 60 years in Xinjiang,66 exceeding both Chinese and global warming rates—a finding broadly consistent with our research (Figure 8). Hao et al.67 further identified peak temperature sensitivity to SM during summer, with plains exhibiting the highest sensitivity, followed by deserts, while summer SM generally exerted a cooling effect, aligning with our results (Figure 8E). Previous studies have documented stronger SM-temperature coupling in northeast Asia compared to southeast Asia,2,3,12,33,68,69,70 a pattern corroborated by our findings showing more intense coupling in northeastern China than in the southwest (Figure 9D).

Furthermore, this study demonstrated that the influence of SM on T predominantly exhibits a negative correlation across most regions (Figure 8E), where increased SM typically leads to cooling.71,72,73 However, unlike other studies, this study found that in certain areas, such as the Qinghai-Tibet plateau, displaying a warming effect, while the inner Mongolia region exhibits a pronounced cooling response (Figure 8), which aligned with other studies.74,75,76 This spatial heterogeneity primarily stems from variations in the coupled climate-hydrology-vegetation mechanisms across different regions (Figure 3). On the Qinghai-Tibet plateau, increased SM (Figure 6B) under high-altitude conditions significantly reduces surface albedo,77,78 enhancing the absorption of solar radiation.79,80 Concurrently, the thin atmosphere amplifies the trapping of longwave radiation by water vapor,79,81,82 while the weak convective environment on the plateau limits the dissipation of latent heat through evaporation,83 resulting in more energy being retained as sensible heat at the surface.84 Additionally, the widespread permafrost, with its increased heat capacity and reduced thermal conductivity upon moisture addition, further suppresses nocturnal cooling.85,86,87 In contrast, arid and semi-arid regions at lower elevations (e.g., inner Mongolia) are dominated by robust vegetation transpiration and vigorous turbulent exchange, rendering evaporative cooling the primary mechanism for T reduction (Figure 8).

The mechanisms underlying differential cooling variability across vegetation types

This study reveals that grasslands, closed shrublands, deciduous needleleaf forests, and cropland generally exhibited higher temporal variability in cooling capacity across China (Figure 8E), with prior research confirming that forest soil water exerts more pronounced cooling.88,89,90 Those studies demonstrated that trees, with their deeper root systems, access more subsurface water and transpire efficiently through larger leaf areas,91 releasing substantial latent heat to enhance cooling.92,93 Moreover, dense forest canopies effectively shade the ground, reducing direct solar heating and slowing SM evaporation,94,95 while their rough canopy structures enhance air turbulence, promoting the diffusion of water vapor and heat, thereby further improving cooling efficiency.96 However, our findings also indicate that grasslands exhibit a higher temporal variability in cooling capacity, a conclusion supported by Li et al.,97 who suggested that greening in low leaf-area index (LAI) regions may induce stronger cooling effects, which diminish and eventually plateau as LAI increases. This phenomenon arises because biophysical parameters such as albedo and roughness length saturate with rising LAI, weakening biophysical feedback in highly vegetated areas.98,99 An alternative explanation posits that high-LAI canopies may enhance near-infrared reflectance, reducing energy absorption,100,101,102 a mechanism that aligns with observations that afforestation may yield limited cooling or even warming effects.103,104 Furthermore, this study highlights that grassland T was more sensitive to short-term moisture fluctuations (Figure 9C), attributable to their lower soil water retention capacity (Figures 3F and 6B), which leads to rapid moisture variations due to evaporation or precipitation. Consequently, grasslands exhibit stronger instantaneous T responses,105,106 whereas forest cooling may rely more on sustained transpiration processes over longer timescales.

Spatial heterogeneity in the drivers of air temperature variations across china

Analyzing the spatial distribution of factors influencing T variations, namely EF and SM (Figure 9D), revealed distinct regional patterns. In inner Mongolia and the eastern Tibetan plateau, EF directly modulates T variations, whereas the arid northwest region is primarily governed by SM (Figure 9C). In the humid southern regions, seasonal or extreme climatic events (e.g., short-term droughts or heavy precipitation) may cause significant SM fluctuations (Figure 9D), which in turn affect EF and indirectly regulate T.107,108,109 This phenomenon can be attributed to the combined effects of climatic background, underlying surface characteristics, and energy partitioning mechanisms.110,111,112,113 In the semi-arid grasslands of inner Mongolia, EF may be predominantly driven by energy inputs (e.g., net radiation) rather than being strictly limited by SM.9,114,115 Consequently, EF directly regulates T through latent heat cooling, manifesting as a strong negative correlation between EF and T.116,117 In contrast, the arid western region exhibits extremely low SM (Figure 3F), which strongly suppresses EF, leading to energy release as sensible heat and resulting in rapid T increases (strong SM-T negative feedback).66,67 In the humid southern regions, high SM fluctuations directly influence the partitioning of sensible and latent heat (Figure 3F). However, EF responds more sluggishly to SM variations due to near-saturated atmospheric humidity and smaller vapor pressure deficits.118,119,120 In densely vegetated regions (e.g., forests), transpiration is regulated by canopy resistance, meaning short-term (e.g., daily scale) SM variations typically impact soil evaporation more than vegetation transpiration.1,121 As a result, the relationship between SM and T is more direct than that between SM and EF. However, under prolonged (e.g., multi-week) water stress, stomatal closure in vegetation leads to a pronounced decline in T, thereby strengthening the linkage between EF and SM.122,123 Thus, in the humid southern regions, T responses are more dependent on SM dynamics than on the stable regulatory role of EF.119,124 This spatial heterogeneity reflects the diversity of surface energy balance: EF-dominated regions are more sensitive to climate change (e.g., increased radiation), whereas SM-dominated regions exhibit more pronounced time-lagged effects. These findings hold significant implications for regional climate adaptation strategies, such as irrigation in arid zones or water resource management in grasslands.5,125

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, it does not fully account for the influence of anthropogenic activities—such as industrial emissions, urbanization, and energy consumption—on surface temperature, thereby constraining a mechanistic understanding of human-driven thermal changes. Second, the systematic effects of land-use and land-cover changes on the spatial heterogeneity and long-term trends of surface temperature were not quantitatively assessed, limiting the comprehensiveness of our attribution analysis. Furthermore, due to constraints in the spatial resolution of available datasets and the sparse distribution of ground-based monitoring stations, certain regional-scale temperature characteristics, and dynamic processes could not be accurately resolved. Future studies should incorporate remote sensing data with higher spatial and temporal resolution, multi-source observational products, and more detailed anthropogenic and land-use data to advance multi-scale and multi-factor coupled analysis of surface temperature dynamics.

Method details

Acquisition of the ERA5-Land dataset for EF-SM modeling

The data for investigating the soil moisture-temperature coupling mechanism were obtained, comprising hourly records from 1980 to 2023. Given that the relationship between soil moisture content and near-surface air temperature is most pronounced during the summer months (June-August, JJA), ERA5 data for this period were selected, including hourly soil moisture data, latent heat flux (LE), and sensible heat flux (SH), and 2-meter air temperature data. The soil moisture data used in this study represent the weighted average of four different soil depth layers provided by ERA5, the spatial distribution of each variable is shown in Figure 3.

Processing of validation data

For validation analyses, we employed the TerraClimate dataset, which provides global soil moisture data at 0.5° spatial resolution based on a modified Thornthwaite–Mather climatic water balance model incorporating extractable soil water storage capacity. The second soil moisture product was obtained from the GLEAM, which combines satellite observations with a multi-layer water balance model at 0.1°resolution. For this study, we used the arithmetic mean of GLEAM’s shallow-layer and root-zone soil moisture estimates. We also utilized latent and sensible heat flux data from GLDAS-2.0 with a focus on the June–August period. All datasets were resampled to a consistent 0.1°spatial resolution. Soil moisture data from two alternative sources, along with sensible and latent heat flux data derived from GLDAS-2.0 and supplemented with air temperature data, were independently utilized to recalculate the corresponding soil moisture and heat fluxes. This approach was employed to validate the results obtained from ERA5.

To validate the temporal and spatial accuracy of different reanalysis data, this study employs half-hourly sensible and latent heat flux observations obtained from eddy covariance systems between 2003 and 2010 as a ground-truth benchmark to assess the performance of several reanalysis datasets—including GLDAS and ERA5—in representing surface energy fluxes and soil moisture. We compiled measurements from a network of flux towers and meteorological stations distributed across China over the same period. The raw half-hourly data were first aggregated to hourly resolution, and then further averaged to generate monthly values, resulting in a gridded monthly observational dataset. Corresponding variables from the reanalysis products—sensible and latent heat fluxes from GLDAS and ERA5, along with depth-integrated soil moisture from ERA5, TerraClimate, and GLEAM—were similarly processed to monthly means to ensure temporal consistency for comparison. Using the in-situ measurements from 2003 to 2010 as reference, we systematically evaluated the ability of the reanalysis products to capture both the spatial structure and temporal evolution of the examined variables. Figure 2 presents a comparison of the reanalysis data for each variable against the measurements from the flux tower stations.

Trend analysis methods

In addition to spatial variations across different locations, T, EF, and SM may also exhibit temporal changes. To analyze the spatial distribution characteristics and temporal evolution patterns of T, EF, and SM from 1980 to 2023, trend analysis methods were employed. Specifically, the Mann-Kendall test and the Theil-Sen Median were applied to assess these variables at the pixel scale. The magnitude and significance of the trends were determined using the slope and the significance test (p). A positive value of p indicates an increasing trend over time, while a negative value suggests a decreasing trend. Additionally, the significance of the trend was evaluated based on the Z value; a trend was considered statistically significant at the 5% level if the absolute value of Z exceeded 1.96. The results were demonstrated in Figures 4, 5, and 6.

Influence of soil moisture on near-surface air temperature

The SM-T coupling process can be decomposed into two interrelated mechanisms: first, the interaction between soil moisture and the evaporative fraction constitutes the foundational component of the coupling process; second, the regulation of near-surface air temperature by the evaporative fraction forms the feedback component of this coupling. The synergistic effect of these two mechanisms collectively establishes the complete soil moisture-temperature coupling system.117 Based on this theoretical framework, this study integrates two key elements: (1) the coupling intensity characteristics between near-surface air temperature and soil moisture, and (2) typical soil moisture variation patterns—to develop a quantitative assessment model for the impact of soil moisture fluctuations on temperature.

Specifically, variations in soil moisture alter the distribution of surface energy balance by modulating the evaporative fraction. For instance, a reduction in the evaporative fraction simultaneously enhances both soil heat flux and sensible heat flux. Given that the temporal scale adopted in this study is hourly, sensible heat flux typically dominates, meaning that changes in the evaporative fraction are primarily reflected through alterations in sensible heat flux. This energy distribution characteristic indicates that the influence of evaporative fraction changes on near-surface air temperature is most pronounced when soil moisture is in an intermediate state (i.e., the transitional region between dry and wet conditions).
