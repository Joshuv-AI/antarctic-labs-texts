FET Fusion Oncoproteins Disrupt Physiologic DNA Repair and Create a Targetable Opportunity for ATR Inhibitor Therapy
Authors: Daniel E. Gracilla, Shruti Menon, Marcus R. Breese, Yone Phar Lin, Filemon S. Dela Cruz, Tamar Y. Feinberg, Elisa de Stanchina, Ana-Florina Galic, Hannah Allegakoen, Shruthi R. Perati, Nicholas Everin, Teresa Vizconde, Nicholas J. Wen, Ann Heslin, Romel Somwar, Marc Ladanyi, Max Horlbeck, Jonathan S. Weissman, E. Alejandro Sweet-Cordero, Trever G. Bivona, Asmin Tulpule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
FET fusion oncoproteins subvert genome maintenance by impairing ATM activity, bridging two key fields—oncogene biology and DNA repair—and proposing ATR inhibition as a targeted therapeutic approach for FET rearranged cancers.

Introduction

The DNA damage response (DDR) is a tightly regulated and redundant network that tailors specific repair complexes to address a diverse set of genotoxic insults. In cancer, oncogene-induced hyperproliferation and replication stress (RS) activate the DDR which can trigger senescence or cell death programs (1). Thus, the physiologic DDR represents an important barrier to oncogenic transformation, and genetic loss of tumor-suppressive DDR pathways or downstream effectors are frequent cooperating events in cancer (e.g., ATM or p53 mutations; ref. 2). Loss of these DDR genes can also create collateral dependencies and therapeutic opportunities in specific cancers as exemplified by the use of poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors to target defective homologous recombination (HR) repair in BRCA1/2-mutant (mut) breast and ovarian cancers (3, 4). Outside of HR-deficient cancers, although multiple synthetic lethal DDR dependencies have been described (e.g., oncogene-induced RS and ATR; ref. 1), the clinical benefit of DDR-targeting therapies is less established, and the mechanisms underlying many of these aberrant DDR dependencies remain to be defined (5). More generally, whether and how oncogenes themselves regulate physiologic DNA repair to create functional defects or dependencies within DDR networks is poorly understood.

The FET family of intrinsically disordered proteins (FUS, EWSR1, and TAF15) is frequent 5′ oncogenic transcription factor (TF) fusion partners in a diversity of sarcomas and leukemias (6). These TF fusion oncoproteins are often the sole oncogenic driver alteration in these cancers, and due to the difficulty in pharmacologic targeting of TFs, precision medicine approaches have been lacking. The most studied cancer in this class is Ewing sarcoma, a pediatric bone tumor driven by the EWSR1::FLI1 TF fusion oncoprotein. Patients with relapsed or metastatic Ewing sarcoma continue to have dismal outcomes despite maximally intense combination cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens (7). Paradoxically, decades of clinical experience and laboratory testing of Ewing sarcoma cancer cell lines have shown Ewing sarcoma to be among the most chemosensitive and radiosensitive cancers (7–10), at least initially. It has long been hypothesized that Ewing sarcoma tumors harbor an intrinsic DNA repair defect that explains their underlying sensitivity to DNA-damaging therapies (10). The prevailing model is that Ewing sarcoma belongs to a family of “BRCA-like” tumors that are functionally deficient in BRCA1 (due to sequestration of BRCA1 protein by RNA–DNA hybrid structures known as R-loops) and therefore defective in HR double-strand break (DSB) repair (11). However, both xenograft studies (9) and clinical trials in patients with Ewing sarcoma (12) failed to demonstrate any benefit for PARP inhibitor monotherapy, in stark contrast to the impressive clinical responses seen across HR-deficient BRCA-mut and BRCA-like cancers (13, 14). Thus, the precise nature of the DNA repair defect in Ewing sarcoma remains uncertain.

Native FET family proteins contain an N-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR) required for interactions among FET family members and a C-terminal domain with positively charged arginine–glycine–glycine (RGG) repeats, which mediate recruitment to DSBs via high-affinity interactions with negatively charged poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) molecules (6, 15). All three FET family members are rapidly recruited to DSBs in a PARP-dependent manner (15, 16), where they undergo liquid–liquid phase separation that is thought to enable compartmentalization of DSB repair proteins (17, 18), though the specific role of FET proteins in DSB repair is not well defined. Interestingly, all oncogenic FET fusion proteins, including EWSR1::FLI1, share a similar structure: the N-terminal IDR of the FET protein fused to the DNA binding domain of a TF (e.g., FLI1), with loss of the C-terminal RGG repeats (6).

In this study, we address the role of the oncogenic fusion protein EWSR1::FLI1 in regulating the DDR in Ewing sarcoma. Contrary to the current classification of Ewing sarcoma as a BRCA-like tumor with defective HR (11), we nominate the suppression of ATM activation and signaling as a new DDR lesion in Ewing sarcoma cells and identify the compensatory ATR signaling axis as a synthetic lethal therapeutic strategy for the class of FET-rearranged cancers.

Materials and Methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

CRISPR interference screen

Ewing sarcoma cell line A673 was transduced with dCas9-KRAB-BFP and then doubly sorted for BFP-positive cells. Lentiviral particles were generated from 293T cells transduced with pooled single-guide RNA (sgRNA) libraries (7,000 genes, 10 guides per gene) as described previously (23) and then used to infect A673 dCas9-KRAB cells at a multiplicity of infection of 0.3. Cells were selected in puromycin for 48 hours, and an aliquot was frozen for t0 analysis. The remainder of cells were seeded in 500 cm2 tissue culture plates at equal density (1 × 107 cells/plate) and then treated with vehicle (DMSO) or 12.5/40 nmol/L doxorubicin for 72 hours. After 72 hours, cells were trypsinized, counted, and pooled every 3 days and then replated in media without doxorubicin at the same density to maintain minimum 500× coverage of each sgRNA construct. Cells were viably frozen after 10 days, and cell counts were used to determine actual lethal dose values. Two biologic replicates were performed, and data were combined.

Deep sequencing and data analysis were performed as described (23). Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from t0 and tend cells using a DNEasy Blood & Tissue kit (QIAGEN), digested to enrich for lentiviral integration sites, and sgRNA sequences were amplified by PCR for subsequent sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq. Reads were aligned to the sgRNA library, and fold change from t0 to tend in the DMSO and low and high doxorubicin conditions were calculated. A gene-level score was then calculated as the mean of the top 3 scoring sgRNAs targeting a given transcript.

Computational analysis of genomic data

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from known BRCA-mut/wild-type (WT) breast cancer patient samples and Ewing sarcoma samples were obtained from the European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA). The WGS data for the Ewing sarcoma cohort were published in Tirode and colleagues (24) and deposited with study accession numbers EGAS00001000855 (Institute Curie cohort) and EGAS00001000839 (St. Jude’s cohort). The breast cancer WGS was reported in 2016 (25) and deposited under EGAD00001001322, as part of a larger WGS of 560 breast cancer samples (overall project accession number EGAS00001001178). The pediatric cohort comprised of 145 pan-cancer patient tumors collected at the University of California as part of the genomics efforts led by E.A. Sweet-Cordero. “Written informed consent” was obtained from patients, and studies were conducted in accordance with International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects and approved by the respective institutional review boards.

FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCh38 (patch 5) human genome reference using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner minimum essential medium, with pre- and post-alignment filtering and processing using NGSUtils and GATK. Insertions and deletions (indel) were called using Mutect 2 for short indels and Delly for larger indels. For each indel, the amount of microhomology (MH) at the flanking ends was calculated by determining the number of bases of homology between the insert or deletion and the flanking sequence. All indels with between 2 and 6 bp of homology were classified as MH-positive. Mutational signatures were calculated using the somatic single-nucleotide variants for each sample. COSMIC v2 signatures were calculated using deconstructSigs.

Determining deletion length bins

The binning ranges were determined by calculating the ability for the number of deletions of each length (1–100 bp) to accurately separate the BRCA-WT/mut samples into two groups. The deletions were classified as either MH-positive or MH-negative, and the total number of deletions for each length was calculated for BRCA-WT and BRCA-mut samples (data were aggregated for all samples in the group). For each deletion length, a Fisher exact test was used to calculate the statistical significance of whether the MH-positive to MH-negative ratio separated the BRCA-WT/mut groups. The distribution of P values was used to determine appropriate bin sizes for downstream analysis. Deletions were binned into ranges of 1 to 6 bp, 7 to 28 bp, 29 to 45 bp, 46 to 100 bp, and 101 to 1,000 bp.

DSB repair reporter assays

HR, alternative end-joining (Alt-EJ), single-strand annealing (SSA), and canonical nonhomologous end-joining (c-NHEJ) efficiencies were measured using previously established DR-GFP, EJ2-GFP, SA-GFP, and EJ5-GFP reporter systems in U2OS cells, respectively (26). Briefly, dual promoter plasmids coexpressing mTagBFP and EWSR1::FLI1 (or empty vector, EV) were generated by replacing the puromycin cassette with mTagBFP in the plasmid pCDH-CMV-EWSR1::FLI1 (Addgene #102813). Coexpression plasmids were delivered by nucleofection into U2OS DSB reporter cell lines using the SE Cell Line 4D-Nucleofector X Kit (Lonza Biosciences). To induce a DSB, the I-SceI-P2A-mCherry plasmid which we generated by replacing AmCyan with I-SceI in the bicistronic plasmid Amcyan-P2A-mCherry (Addgene #45350) was delivered by nucleofection after 24 hours. Flow cytometry was performed 72 hours after I-SceI introduction. DSB repair efficiency was calculated by determining the percentage of GFP-positive cells within the BFP and mCherry double–positive population. For siRNA experiments, siRNA (5 nmol/L) was delivered by transfection using DharmaFECT transfection reagent (Dharmacon) 72 hours prior to I-SceI introduction.

For the transient DSB repair reporter experiments in A673 Ewing sarcoma cells with a dox-inducible shRNA against EWSR1::FLI1, 10 μg of the DSB reporter plasmids (DR-GFP, EJ2, SSA, and EJ5) was transfected into 1 × 106 cells, followed by nucleofection of mCherry-I-SceI 24 hours later to induce DSBs. Forty-eight hours later, DSB repair efficiency was calculated as described above. Depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 (with 1 μg/mL dox) or 53BP1 occurred 48 hours prior to induction of DSBs with I-SceI. To directly examine junctional sequences after DSB induction for evidence of c-NHEJ or alt-EJ, we utilized a distinct I-SceI–based DSB repair reporter described here (27). Briefly, 5 μg of the NHEJ reporter was transfected into EWSR1::FLI1 (or EV) expressing U2OS cells, GFP+ cells were sorted at 72 hours for genomic DNA isolation, and then DSB junctional sequences were analyzed by recircularization of the repaired NHEJ reporter and plasmid sequencing.

DDR signaling experiments

For experiments in A673 and TC-32 cells with dox-inducible shRNA against EWSR1::FLI1, knockdown was induced by either addition of 1 μg/mL dox (A673) or 200 ng/mL (TC-32) for 72 hours, followed by ionizing radiation (IR) treatment. For A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” cells, 200 μmol/L IAA (auxin, Sigma-Aldrich) was added 3 hours prior to IR treatment for EWSR1::FLI1 depletion. For the ectopic expression experiments in U2OS cells, the respective fusion proteins (EWSR1::FLI1-GFP, EWSR1::ATF1-GFP, or EV-GFP) were introduced via nucleofection with homemade buffers (solution 1: 2 g ATP-disodium salt, 1.2 g MgCl2.6H20 in 10 mL water; solution 2: 6 g KH2PO4, 0.6 g NaHCO3, and 0.2 g glucose in 500 mL water, pH 7.4. Solutions were stored at −20°C, and 80 μL of solution 1 was mixed with 4 mL of solution 2 at the time of nucleofection). GFP-positive cells were selected by FACS, and 24 hours after sorting, they were subjected to IR. MEF cells were infected with pCDH-EWSR1::FLI1 (Addgene plasmid #102813) lentivirus or EV, selected with puromycin within 72 hours, and then subjected to IR.

For the shRNA experiments testing knockdown of ATM regulators in A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” or MEF cells, Mission pLKO.1 shRNA viruses (sequences below) were used to infect target cells, and selection with puromycin was completed within 72 hours to minimize effects on cell viability, followed by IR treatment. For experiments in SU-CCS-1 cells, siRNA against EWSR1::ATF1 was delivered by nucleofection 72 hours prior to IR. For improved induction of ATR/CHK1 signaling, cells were treated with neocarzinostatin (NCS) at 200 ng/mL for 30 minutes. Lysates were collected at defined time points after IR (or after NCS) and subjected to Western blotting.

DNA end-resection assays and immunofluorescence

For end-resection assays, A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” cells were seeded on glass coverslips, and 200 μmol/L IAA (auxin, Sigma-Aldrich) was added 3 hours prior to 5 Gy IR treatment for EWSR1::FLI1 depletion. Cells were preextracted (25 mmol/L HEPES pH 7.9, 300 mmol/L sucrose, 50 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 3 mmol/L MgCl2, and 0.5% Triton X-100), washed 3 times in PBS, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton-X-100 in PBS. For immunofluorescence (IF), cells were grown on 8-well Lab-Tek II Chamber Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific), irradiated with 0.5 Gy the day after plating, and then fixed at specified time points with 4% paraformaldehyde. Immunostaining was performed using anti-RPA32/RPA2 (Abcam, ab252861, RRID: AB_2927646, 1:250), BrdU (Abcam, ab6326, RRID: AB_305426, 1:1,000), pATM (Invitrogen, MA5-32751, RRID: AB_2810028, 1:500), γH2AX (MilliporeSigma, JBW301, RRID: AB_309864, 1:500), 53BP1 (Abcam, ab175933, RRID: AB_2890610, 1:250), and BRCA1 (Abcam, ab16781, RRID: AB_443466, 1:250). Confocal microscopy was performed on a Leica Stellaris at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC), and nuclear foci scoring was done using Image J. As a positive control for end-resection, A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” cells were infected using a mixture of three lentiviral Mission shRNAs targeting PARP1 (target sequences: CTTCGTTAGAATGTCTGCCTT, GCAGCTTCATAACCGAAGATT, and CCGAGAAATCTCTTACCTCAA).

Western blotting and quantification

For Western blotting, cells were washed with 1x PBS and scraped in RIPA buffer [25 mmol/L Tris⋅HCl (pH 7.6), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS], supplemented with 1x HALT protease inhibitor cocktail and 1x HALT phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Cells were lysed using a syringe, and cellular debris was separated by centrifugation. Lysates were quantified using Bradford’s reagent, and 15 μg of lysate was loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, followed by blotting of separated proteins. Blots were blocked with 5% BSA in TBS-T for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. After 1-hour incubation with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibody, signal was detected using ECL Prime reagent (Amersham, Cytiva) on an ImageQuant LAS 4000. Quantifications were performed using Fiji, and all protein levels were normalized to loading control actin. To measure CHK2 phosphorylation in MEFs as no reliable mouse pCHK2 antibody is commercially available, we quantified the intensity of the upper band (after IR) on total CHK2 immunoblots which corresponds to the phosphorylated species, as utilized previously by multiple groups (28, 29).

Cell survival assays

A total of 350,000 cells were seeded in six-well plates, 24 hours prior to drug treatment. Cells were treated with varying doses of ATR kinase inhibitor elimusertib (Selleck Chemicals) ± doxorubicin (Sigma-Aldrich) or CHK1 inhibitor LY2603618 (Selleck Chemicals) for 3 days, at the end of which cells were counted to determine fractional cell survival. For dox-inducible EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown in A673 and TC-32 cells, shRNA was induced by treatment with dox for 72 hours prior to ATR or CHK1 inhibitor treatment. For RNaseH1 experiments, A673 cells were infected with pLV-EF1a-RNAseH1-IRES-Blast lentivirus (derived from Addgene plasmid #85133) or EV and selected for stably infected cells. For ATM rescue experiments, GFP-tagged ATM (or EV) was transfected into A673 and TC-32 Ewing sarcoma cells, and cells were sorted for GFP positivity and then plated for elimusertib dose–response curves.

Laser microirradiation

Laser microirradiation was performed as previously described (16). Briefly, U2OS cells expressing EWSR1-GFP, EWSR1::FLI1-GFP, EWSR1::ATF1-GFP, and EWSR1::WT1-GFP or the various mut forms of the fusion oncoproteins were seeded in 8-well Lab-Tek II Chamber Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 24 hours. Cells were treated with 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes prior to microirradiation. Live-cell microscopy was performed using the Nikon Ti microscope with a CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal using a 100×/1.4 Plan Apo VC objective at the UCSF Center for Light Microscopy. To induce DNA damage, 5-pixel wide stripes were drawn in every cell nucleus to label the region of interest (ROI) and irradiated using a 405-nm diode laser (40 mW). Images were acquired before irradiation and at 1-minute intervals after laser damage for 15 minutes. To plot recruitment kinetics, the preirradiation fluorescence intensity was subtracted from the intensity of the ROI for every nucleus.

Coimmunoprecipitation experiments

For EWSR1 coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments, 293T cells were transfected with Flag-EWSR1 and EWSR1::FLI1 (or EV) for 48 hours. Cells were then treated with NCS (200 ng/mL) for 30 minutes, and cells were collected at 15 minutes, 30 minutes, and 1 hour after NCS treatment. Nuclear co-IP was performed using the Nuclear Co-IP Kit (Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by IP using M2 Flag beads (Sigma-Aldrich). For GFP co-IP experiments, 293T cells were transfected with EWSR1::FLI1-GFP, FLI1-GFP, EWSR1-GFP, or GFP EV for 24 hours, followed by IR treatment (5 Gy), cell collection at specified time points, the same nuclear co-IP protocol, and IP using ChromoTek GFP-Trap Agarose beads (Proteintech).

Cell-cycle analysis

U2OS cells were nucleofected with the dual-promoter pCDH-EWSR1::FLI1-EF1a-mtagBFP (or EV) and analyzed at 48 hours. Cells were fixed using 4% formaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature. After washing with 1% BSA in PBS, cells were resuspended in 500 μL of FxCycle PI/RNAse Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes at room temperature. Cell-cycle profiles were also analyzed using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Invitrogen). Cells were pulse-labeled with 10 μmol/L EdU for 30 minutes and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the cell-cycle profile for BFP-positive, EWSR1::FLI1 (or EV)-expressing cells.

Patient-derived xenograft experiments

All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at MSKCC. The establishment of patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models was performed by the laboratory of F.S. Dela Cruz and Dr. Andrew Kung from patients at MSKCC according to the IACUC and Institutional Review Board protocols. Tumor fragments from patients were serially transplanted into athymic nude (Foxn1nu) mice from Charles River Laboratories to establish the PDX for 3 to 5 passages, after which time seeds were implanted to begin the PDX experiments. When average tumor volumes exceeded 150 mm3, mice were randomized into either the elimusertib or vehicle group. Elimusertib was dosed at 40 mg/kg twice daily, 3 days on/4 days off as previously reported (30), and dissolved in 60% polyethylene glycol 400, 10% ethanol, and 30% water to a 4 mg/mL solution. The same solution without the compound was used as a vehicle control. Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation once the tumor volume exceeded 2,000 mm3 or body weight loss was higher than 20% (which did not occur), and tumors were collected for immunohistochemistry (IHC).

For IHC studies, paraffin cross-sections (thickness = 5 μm) of PDX tumor tissues were applied to Superfrost Plus microscope slides (Fisher Scientific, #1255015) for IHC analyses. After heat-mediated antigen retrieval (48 minutes, cell conditioning 1; Ventana, #950-500), PDX tumor cross-sections were incubated for 4 hours at room temperature with the following primary antibodies: rabbit polyclonal cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9661, RRID: AB_2341188, 0.05 mg/mL), rabbit -monoclonal Ki-67 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9027, RRID: AB_2636984, 0.25 mg/mL), gamma-H2AX (gH2AX) Ser139 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9718, RRID: AB_2118009, 1:1,000 dilution), pCHK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #2348, RRID: AB_331212, 1:100 dilution), or normal rabbit polyclonal control IgG (Invitrogen, #10500C). After sequential washes with PBS, sections were incubated for 20 minutes with Omni Map anti-Rb HRP (Roche, #760-4311, RRID: AB_2811043), followed by DAB ChromoMap Kit amplification (Ventana Medical Systems, #760-159). The slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with Permount (Fisher Scientific). Analysis was performed in a blinded fashion using QuPath software.

Statistics

Data from at least three independent experiments were used to calculate P values, which were determined with Student t tests and ANOVA using GraphPad software.

Results

Ewing sarcoma cells are dependent on specific HR factors for survival

To address the uncertainty surrounding putative DNA repair defect(s) in Ewing sarcoma, we set out to identify genes that modulate Ewing sarcoma cell survival in response to doxorubicin, a DNA DSB-inducing agent and major component of current chemotherapy regimens (Fig. 1A; ref. 7). We selected a CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)-based screening approach given the concern of studying DSB repair phenotypes using an active Cas9 that generates DSBs (23, 31). Surprisingly, we found that key HR factors BRCA1 and PALB2 were essential for the growth of Ewing sarcoma cells even in the absence of doxorubicin (Fig. 1B). Given the prevailing model that Ewing sarcoma is a functionally HR-deficient, BRCA-like tumor (11), the screen results were unexpected. We validated the finding of specific HR factor dependency using two independent gRNAs against BRCA1 and PALB2 in the screening cell line (A673) and two additional Ewing sarcoma cell lines TC-252 and ES-8 (Fig. 1C–E; Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B). The same BRCA1 and PALB2 gRNAs had limited effects on cell growth in two non–Ewing sarcoma cancer cell lines, which was consistent with the set of published CRISPRi screens (23, 32, 33) and suggests that the observed dependency on HR factors may be specific to Ewing sarcoma cells (Supplementary Fig. S1C and S1D). To test the role of EWSR1::FLI1 in inducing this dependency, we utilized an Ewing sarcoma cell line with a dox-inducible shRNA against EWSR1::FLI1 (Supplementary Fig. S1E; ref. 19). EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown rescued the growth defects caused by BRCA1 or PALB2 loss, confirming that the oncogenic fusion protein is necessary for the observed dependency on these specific HR factors in Ewing sarcoma cells (Fig. 1F).

CRISPRi screening also identified genes whose loss sensitized Ewing sarcoma cells to doxorubicin, including LIG4 and NHEJ1/XLF (Supplementary Fig. S1F). The presence of key c-NHEJ genes as top chemosensitizer hits validated our experimental approach, as this pathway is critical for repairing both drug and IR-induced DSBs and provides evidence for a functional c-NHEJ pathway in Ewing sarcoma cells. The final category of screen hits were genes whose loss promoted survival under high doses of doxorubicin (LD97), mirroring the residual disease state in patients with Ewing sarcoma (Supplementary Fig. S1G). SLFN11 is a notable hit, as loss of SLFN11 has been shown to promote chemotherapy resistance in multiple cancer subtypes, including Ewing sarcoma (34). The complete screen results are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (see Supplementary Note S1 for discussion of DepMap results). Given the paradoxical finding of HR factor dependence in Ewing sarcoma, we chose to focus on the regulation of DSB repair in Ewing sarcoma and whether Ewing sarcoma tumors are properly classified as BRCA-like cancers.

Ewing sarcoma patient tumors do not display the genomic scars of HR deficiency

We next directly examined genomic DNA from 100 Ewing sarcoma patient tumor samples (24) for evidence of functional HR deficiency. This is a validated strategy in both BRCA1/2-mut and BRCA-like tumors wherein defective HR repair produces specific genomic scars that result from increased utilization of compensatory pathways such as alt-EJ and SSA (25, 35). We developed a custom bioinformatics pipeline to analyze the genomic landscape of Ewing sarcoma tumor genomes, using BRCA1/2mut and WT breast cancer genomes (from EGAD00001001322; ref. 25) to validate our algorithms. The Ewing sarcoma cohort was reported in Tirode and colleagues (24) to represent diagnostic, pretreatment biopsies of the primary tumor site, with 63% of patients having localized disease. The Ewing sarcoma WGS data were previously deposited in the EGA (EGAS00001000855 and EGAS00001000839).

We focused on two major genomic characteristics of tumors with functional HR deficiency: a mutational single-base substitution signature (SBS3) and short stretches of overlapping MH at sequences flanking naturally occurring deletion breakpoints. At least 99 SBS signatures have been identified in human cancers, with signature 3 being the most highly associated with BRCA1/2-mut cancers (25). Our analysis confirmed high levels of signature 3 in BRCA-mut compared with BRCA-WT tumors but did not show increased levels of signature 3 in Ewing sarcoma tumors (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S2A). In addition to increased signature 3, BRCA1/2-mut tumors displayed an increase in the number and size of deletions compared with BRCA-WT tumors (Supplementary Fig. S2B and S2C), consistent with previous reports (25). Ewing sarcoma tumors are also distinct from BRCA1/2-mut tumors, displaying few deletions per genome and a size distribution skewed further toward small deletions than even BRCA-WT tumor samples (Supplementary Fig. S2B–S2D).

We further examined the sequences flanking deletion sites for evidence of breakpoint MH. Defective HR repair in BRCA-mut or BRCA-like tumors results in increased usage of alt-EJ, an error prone DSB repair pathway that uses MH for initial annealing of resected DSB ends (36). We utilized BRCA-mut/WT samples to computationally define deletion size bins and identified an increase in the proportion of intermediate size deletions [7–28 base pairs (bp), 29–45 bp] in BRCA-mut samples as reported previously (Supplementary Fig. S2E and S2F; refs. 25, 36). We then verified a significant increase in breakpoint MH at intermediate size (7–28 bp and 29–45 bp) deletions in BRCA-mut tumors compared with WT and no increase in MH at small deletions (1–6 bp) where c-NHEJ predominates (Fig. 2B and C; Supplementary Fig. S2E–S2G), both consistent with published findings (25). Ewing sarcoma samples clustered with BRCA-WT tumors and display a trend toward even less MH-mediated DSB repair than BRCA-WT samples (Fig. 2B and C; Supplementary Fig. S2G). Our findings in Ewing sarcoma were independent of the recurrent STAG2 and TP53 mutations that occur in a subset of these cancers (Supplementary Fig. S2H and S2I).

Finally, to address potential age-related confounding in our Ewing sarcoma genomic scar analyses, we analyzed an independent pediatric pan-cancer cohort of 145 tumors collected at the UCSF. Though the overall levels of mutational signature 3 and breakpoint MH at intermediate size deletions (7–28 and 29–45 bp) were low within the pediatric cohort, the Ewing sarcoma samples in this cohort displayed significantly lower percentages of signature 3 and breakpoint MH compared with other pediatric cancers (Fig. 2D and E; Supplementary Fig. S2J–S2M). We note that none of the pediatric tumors showed the high levels of both signature 3 and breakpoint MH that would classify them within the range of HR-deficient, BRCA1/2-mut (with biallelic loss) breast cancer samples (Supplementary Fig. S2J and S2L). These findings are consistent with multiple prior studies (37, 38) reporting that bona fide HR deficiency is extremely rare across pediatric cancers (e.g., in our cohort of 145 pediatric tumors none showed biallelic loss of BRCA1/2, PALB2, or RAD51C). Therefore, to directly test whether the absence of HR-deficient genomic signatures in ES tumors was the result of age-related confounding, we created an age-matched cohort by selecting the oldest patients with Ewing sarcoma (average age: 34.4) and youngest patients with BRCA-mut breast cancer (average age: 34.6) from the larger Ewing sarcoma and breast cancer datasets. By comparing these age-matched Ewing sarcoma and BRCA-mut cohorts using the same analytic pipeline, we confirm our finding that BRCA-mut tumors have high levels of signature 3 and breakpoint MH not present in age-matched Ewing sarcoma samples (Fig. 2F and G). In summary, these results demonstrate that tumors of patient with Ewing sarcoma do not display the genomic signatures of BRCA1/2-mut cancers and lack the footprint of isolated HR deficiency.

EWSR1::FLI1 reduces resection-dependent DSB repair

The absence of HR-deficient genomic scars in Ewing sarcoma tumors and paradoxical requirement of specific HR factors (e.g., BRCA1) for Ewing sarcoma cell survival prompted us to systematically reexamine how EWSR1::FLI1 affects DSB repair pathway utilization (Fig. 3A). We posited that previous reports of defective HR in Ewing sarcoma might alternatively be explained by a more general upstream defect in DSB repair. We utilized a set of well-established DSB repair reporter cell lines wherein expression of the I-SceI endonuclease induces a DSB within an interrupted GFP reporter cassette, such that utilization of a particular DSB repair pathway restores the GFP-coding sequence enabling a quantitative readout of individual repair pathway efficiency (26). The use of fluorescently tagged plasmids enabled the detection of DSB repair events specifically in cells that expressed both EWSR1::FLI1 (or EV) and I-SceI (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

DSBs either undergo direct repair via c-NHEJ or bidirectional end-resection to create an intermediate for HR, alt-EJ, or SSA. B–E, Relative repair efficiency as measured by GFP positivity in pathway-specific DSB repair reporters for HR (DR-GFP), alt-EJ (EJ2), SSA, and c-NHEJ (EJ5) upon expression of EV, EWSR1::FLI1, or siRNA gene knockdown for 72 hours. To examine HR repair, we utilized the DR-GFP reporter and observed a small reduction in HR upon EWSR1::FLI1 expression, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 3B; ref. 11). However, EWSR1::FLI1 expression also reduced the efficiency of MH-mediated alt-EJ repair (EJ2) and long-stretch MH-mediated SSA repair (Fig. 3C and D). EWSR1::FLI1–mediated reductions in HR, alt-EJ, and SSA repair efficiency were modest compared with knockdown of the key end-resection factor CtIP that is required for all resection-dependent DSB repair (39) or the relevant pathway specific controls (Fig. 3A–C). These data indicate that EWSR1::FLI1 expression does not result in isolated HR defects but instead an intermediate reduction in the efficiency of all three resection-dependent DSB repair pathways.

We also evaluated c-NHEJ, a fast-acting DSB repair pathway which does not require end-resection, and observed an increase in c-NHEJ utilization upon EWSR1::FLI1 expression using the EJ5 reporter system (Fig. 3E). Using a distinct I-SceI–based DSB repair reporter (27), we directly examined the junctional sequences after DSB induction for evidence of c-NHEJ or MH-mediated alt-EJ repair (HR and SSA events are not evaluable in this system) and confirmed an increase in c-NHEJ and decrease in alt-EJ upon the expression of EWSR1::FLI1 (Supplementary Fig. S3B). We verified that the repair phenotypes were not the result of EWSR1::FLI altering cell-cycle profiles or causing a cell-cycle arrest in these short-term assays (Supplementary Fig. S3C and S3D).

Lastly, we performed analogous experiments of DSB repair pathway efficiency in Ewing sarcoma cells to address potential adaptations to endogenous EWSR1::FLI1 that would not be captured by short-term, exogenous expression of the fusion oncoprotein. DSB repair reporters (DR-GFP, EJ2, SSA, and EJ5) were transiently expressed along with I-SceI-mCherry in A673 Ewing sarcoma cells with a dox-inducible shRNA against EWSR1::FLI1 (19). Consistent with the results upon the exogenous expression of EWSR1::FLI1, knockdown of EWSR1::FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma cells increased the efficiency of all resection-dependent DSB repair pathways (HR, alt-EJ, and SSA) and decreased the efficiency of c-NHEJ (Supplementary Fig. S3E). The effect size of EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown was either smaller (HR and c-NHEJ) or comparable (SSA and alt-EJ) to the positive control, knockdown of 53BP1 which promotes c-NHEJ and limits end-resection (40, 41). In summary, we demonstrate that EWSR1::FLI1 does not induce isolated HR deficiency but instead moderately reduces the efficiency of all three resection-dependent DSB repair pathways.

ATM activation and signaling is suppressed by the Ewing sarcoma fusion oncoprotein

To explain our finding that EWSR1::FLI1 expression leads to intermediate reductions in resection-dependent DSB repair efficiency, we focused on the upstream regulation of the DDR in Ewing sarcoma cells (Fig. 3A). An important component of the initial DDR is a partially redundant signaling network with three principal kinases, DNA-PK, ATM, and ATR, each of which control distinct aspects of DDR signal amplification and DSB repair pathway choice (42). We tested whether EWSR1::FLI1 affects the activation and function of these three apical DDR kinases; in particular, ATM was a logical candidate as it promotes DNA end-resection, and ATM loss creates a synthetic lethal dependence on specific HR proteins such as BRCA1 (43, 44).

We utilized three distinct Ewing sarcoma cell line models with inducible EWSR1::FLI1 depletion: A673 and TC-32 Ewing sarcoma cells with dox-inducible shRNAs targeting EWSR1::FLI1 (either the junctional sequence or the 3′ FLI1 portion of the fusion, see “Materials and Methods”) and A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” cells with an AID (20) tag inserted at the endogenous EWSR1::FLI1 locus (bioRxiv 2022.06.24.497515). Short-term EWSR1::FLI1 depletion did not affect the baseline low-level activation of the three DDR kinases or cell viability in the absence of exogenous DNA damage (auxin-mediated depletion is within 3 hours and maximal shRNA depletion is ∼70% at 72 hours; Fig. 4A–D; Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4F). However, depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 prior to IR treatment increased both ATM activation (autophosphorylation) and downstream ATM signaling (phosphorylation of key downstream targets CHK2 and KAP1) consistently across all three Ewing sarcoma cell systems (Fig. 4A–D; Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B). In contrast, IR or radiomimetic NCS-dependent activation of the other apical DDR kinases (DNA-PK and ATR) was unaffected by EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown in Ewing sarcoma cells (Supplementary Fig. S4C–S4F).

We measured pATM foci formation in A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” Ewing sarcoma cells as a complementary test for ATM activation. In the absence of exogenous DNA damage, depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 did not alter pATM foci number (Fig. 4E and F). Upon IR treatment, Ewing sarcoma cells with intact endogenous EWSR1::FLI1 display a minimal increase in pATM foci, whereas rapid depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 prior to IR significantly increased pATM foci number, consistent with our Western blotting results (Fig. 4E and F). In addition, we observed that depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 prior to IR significantly increased gH2AX foci number (Supplementary Fig. S4G and S4H), consistent with ATM’s role as the principal DDR kinase responsible for the phosphorylation and amplifying spread of gH2AX surrounding DSBs (42). The rapid (3 hours) depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 and unchanged basal levels of DDR kinase activation suggest that the observed ATM effects are not secondary downstream consequences from loss of the oncogenic driver in Ewing sarcoma cells but instead may result from EWSR1::FLI’s partial suppression of IR-dependent ATM activation.

To test this directly, we performed the reciprocal experiment by expressing EWSR1::FLI1 in two non–Ewing sarcoma cell lines (U2OS cancer cells and MEFs) to determine whether ectopic EWSR1::FLI1 expression could impair IR-dependent ATM activation. In both cell lines, EWSR1::FLI1 reduced ATM activation (pATM) and downstream ATM signaling (pKAP1/pCHK2) only in response to IR (and not at baseline; Fig. 4G–J, see Materials and Methods on measuring CHK2 phosphorylation by mobility shift in MEFs; refs. 21, 28). Consistent with our finding in Ewing sarcoma cells, ectopic EWSR1::FLI1 expression did not affect DNA-PK or ATR activation and signaling (Supplementary Fig. S4I and S4J). These data indicate that EWSR1::FLI1 (either directly or indirectly) regulates the activation and downstream signaling of ATM, without affecting the other two apical DDR kinases.

We also directly assessed DNA end-resection given that EWSR1::FLI1 moderately reduced the efficiency of all three resection-dependent DSB repair pathways (Fig. 3). Depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 in A673 “EWSR1::FLI1 degron” Ewing sarcoma cells significantly increased IR-dependent DNA end-resection as assayed by BrdU and RPA2 foci, though the effect size was intermediate compared with knockdown of the end-resection antagonist PARP1 which we used as a positive control (Supplementary Fig. S4K–S4N; ref. 45). Also consistent with our DSB repair reporter results (Fig. 3), depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 resulted in a small increase in IR-dependent BRCA1 foci (HR) and small decrease in 53BP1 foci (c-NHEJ; Supplementary Fig. S4O–S4R). The combined data suggest that the primary defect in Ewing sarcoma cells is not DNA end-resection per se but rather suppression of ATM activation/signaling which is known to impair but not abolish end-resection (44, 46).

It is important to note that EWSR1::FLI1 does not completely suppress ATM functionality, as all three modifications (pATM/pCHK2/pKAP1) are induced to some extent upon irradiation of Ewing sarcoma cell lines. To determine the biologic and therapeutic significance of partial ATM suppression in Ewing sarcoma cells, we performed the following studies:We benchmarked our effect size to loss of well-characterized activators of ATM, including the MRN (MRE11/RAD50/NBS1) complex, MDC1, RNF8, and RNF168, in order to contextualize the extent of ATM suppression in Ewing sarcoma and establish the biological significance. There is a hierarchy among genes regulating ATM activity in response to DSBs: the MRN complex is required for ATM activation (47) and serves as an upper bound for effect size, although the scaffolding protein MDC1 (29) and E3 ubiquitin ligases RNF8 (48) and RNF168 (49) have an intermediate phenotype given their important but not essential role in amplifying ATM signaling. Importantly, even loss of intermediate phenotype genes (i.e., MDC1; ref. 29; RNF8; ref. 48; or RNF168; ref. 49) still produces a significant functional impact in terms of increased sensitivity to DSB-inducing drugs and IR. Using both ectopic expression in MEFs and endogenous depletion of EWSR1::FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma cells, we show that the suppressive effects of EWSR1::FLI1 on ATM activation/signaling are indeed not equivalent to the near-complete functional ATM deficiency seen with loss of the MRN complex (∼30 to 60%) but overall comparable with loss of MDC1, RNF8, or RNF168. We provide a detailed description of these experimental results in Supplementary Note S2. Next, we tested the functional relevance and therapeutic consequence of the partial ATM defects in Ewing sarcoma cells. Both ATM and ATR coordinate aspects of resection-dependent DSB repair (Fig. 3A) and ATM-mut tumors display synthetic lethality with ATR inhibitors, reflecting the vital compensatory role of the ATR signaling axis in the absence of ATM (note ATR signaling itself is not increased in the setting of ATM loss, consistent with our findings in Ewing sarcoma cells; refs. 50, 51). We therefore asked whether the extent of ATM suppression in Ewing sarcoma was sufficient to induce a collateral dependence on the ATR signaling axis. Indeed, A673 and TC-32 Ewing sarcoma cells displayed nanomolar range sensitivity to the ATR inhibitor elimusertib (IC50 values of 28 and 34 nmol/L, respectively, comparable with cancer cell lines with known pathogenic ATM loss-of-function mutations; ref. 52), and ATR inhibitor sensitivity was significantly reversed by EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown in both cell lines (Fig. 4K and L). EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown also reversed Ewing sarcoma cell sensitivity to an inhibitor of ATR’s key downstream target, CHK1, and to treatment with the DSB-inducing chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin (Supplementary Fig. S4S–S4U), further establishing the functional relevance of the partial ATM defects in Ewing sarcoma cells.

Previously described mechanisms of ATR sensitivity, most notably RS (1), could also contribute to the elimusertib effects that we observed. To study this directly, we expressed RNAseH1, which degrades R-loops, the major source of oncogene-induced RS in Ewing sarcoma (11), in A673 Ewing sarcoma cells. RNAseH1 expression did not alter the ATR inhibitor sensitivity of Ewing sarcoma cells, (Supplementary Fig. S4V and S4W), suggesting that the molecular basis of ATR inhibitor response in Ewing sarcoma cells may largely be a consequence of ATM suppression. To test causality, we attempted to rescue the partial ATM defects in Ewing sarcoma cells through overexpression of ATM and determine the impact on ATR inhibitor response. In both TC-32 and A673 Ewing sarcoma cells, ATM overexpression significantly reduced sensitivity to the ATR inhibitor elimusertib (Fig. 4M and N; Supplementary Fig. S4X and S4Y). In total, these data demonstrate that the partial ATM defects in Ewing sarcoma have a direct functional consequence in terms of ATR inhibitor sensitivity.

We note here that the above data do not exclude additional roles for EWSR1::FLI1 in regulating the DDR that may contribute to the chemosensitivity of these tumors, and we address the genomic instability of ATM-mut tumors and the need to define mutational/deletion signatures as a comparator for Ewing sarcoma tumors in our discussion. In summary, our collective findings nominate partial suppression of ATM activation/signaling and resultant synthetic lethality with the compensatory ATR signaling axis as a new and therapeutically relevant DNA repair lesion in Ewing sarcoma.

EWSR1::FLI1 and other FET fusion oncoproteins are recruited to DNA DSBs

How does EWSR1::FLI1, a TF fusion oncoprotein, regulate DNA repair to suppress IR-dependent ATM activation? To assess how EWSR1::FLI1 regulates the transcription of DDR and DSB repair genes, we analyzed published RNA sequencing data in Ewing sarcoma cells before and after EWSR1::FLI1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S5A; ref. 53). Interestingly, we found that EWSR1::FLI1 either increased or had minimal effect on the expression of key genes involved in ATM activation (e.g., MRN complex, MDC1, RNF8, RNF168, ATM, and CHK2). We also readily detected these same ATM regulators by Western blotting in A673 and multiple other Ewing sarcoma cell lines. These data suggest EWSR1::FLI1’s transcriptional effects do not create a state of low/absent DNA repair factors (e.g., MRN complex deficiency) that would explain the suppression of IR-dependent ATM activation/signaling that we observe here.

As an alternate hypothesis, we tested whether previously reported interactions between IDRs of EWSR1::FLI1 and native FET proteins (e.g., EWSR1; Fig. 5A; ref. 54) might contribute to the observed ATM effects, given that native FET proteins localize to DSBs and are thought to nucleate initial compartmentalization of DSB repair factors. Consistent with these prior studies, EWSR1::FLI1 and another native FET family member (FUS) coprecipitated with FLAG-tagged EWSR1 (Supplementary Fig. S5B). The strength of these interactions was unchanged in the setting of the DNA DSB–inducing agent NCS indicating a high-affinity interaction not disrupted by the DDR.

We then directly tested whether the interaction between native EWSR1 and EWSR1::FLI1 could promote localization of the fusion oncoprotein to DNA DSBs. Indeed, we discovered that EWSR1::FLI1, like native EWSR1, is recruited to laser-induced DSBs (Fig. 5B–E). The kinetics of EWSR1::FLI1’s DSB recruitment were delayed compared with native EWSR1, whose rapid recruitment is attributed to C-terminal, positively charged RGG-rich domains not present in EWSR1::FLI1 (Fig. 5A–E). To test how loss of these RGG domains affects DSB recruitment of EWSR1::FLI1, we reintroduced either one or all three RGG-rich domains (the entire EWSR1 C-terminus) into the fusion oncoprotein. In an RGG dose–dependent manner, the RGG containing versions of EWSR1::FLI1 displayed earlier DSB recruitment kinetics and higher levels of overall recruitment when compared with EWSR1::FLI1 (Supplementary Fig. S5C and S5D). Moreover, the full (3) RGG domain containing version of EWSR1::FLI1 further suppressed ATM signaling beyond the effects seen with EWSR1::FLI1 (Supplementary Fig. S5E and S5F), suggesting a potential correlation between the extent of EWSR1::FLI1 DSB recruitment and ATM suppression.

Importantly, EWSR1::FLI1 recruitment to DSBs represents an unanticipated neomorphic property of the fusion protein contributed by the N-terminal IDR of EWSR1. Control experiments with the full-length FLI1 protein showed no accumulation at laser-induced DSBs (Supplementary Fig. S5G). Consistent with the N-terminus of EWSR1 mediating DSB recruitment, mutation of the DNA-binding domain of FLI1 (R2L2 mut of EWSR1::FLI1; ref. 55) had no effect on the DSB localization of EWSR1::FLI1 (Supplementary Fig. S5H). Lastly, knockdown of native EWSR1 (using siRNAs targeting C-terminal sequences not present in the fusion) reduced accumulation of EWSR1::FLI1 at laser-induced DSB stripes, indicating that the localization of EWSR1::FLI1 to DSBs depends, at least in part, on native EWSR1 (Supplementary Fig. S5I).

Given the aberrant localization of EWSR1::FLI1 to DNA DSBs, we asked whether EWSR1::FLI1’s effects on ATM might result from direct protein–protein interactions with critical DDR factors that promote ATM activation. We utilized a candidate-based approach and performed co-IP experiments using EWSR1::FLI1 as the bait, both at baseline and after IR treatment. Interestingly, we found that EWSR1::FLI1 demonstrates an IR-independent interaction with all three members of the critical ATM activating MRN complex (MRE11, RAD50, and NBS1) using both GFP- and FLAG-tagged versions of EWSR1::FLI1 (Fig. 5F and G). We also compared EWSR1::FLI1 with full-length native EWSR1 and FLI1 to assess domain contributions to MRN complex binding. EWSR1::FLI1 demonstrated greater binding to MRN complex members than either native full-length EWSR1 or full-length FLI1 (note the higher relative expression/IP levels for EWSR1 and FLI1 and that differences are most prominent for MRE11, Fig. 5G). These data suggest that suppression of ATM signaling in Ewing sarcoma may result from re-localization of EWSR1::FLI1 to DNA DSBs and interaction/interference with the MRN complex, though further studies are needed to define the exact nature (i.e., direct or indirect) and functional impact of EWSR1::FLI1’s binding to the MRN complex, as well as contributions from other EWSR1::FLI1 interactions (e.g., with native FET proteins) not assayed here.

Finally, we tested the generalizability of the concept that N-terminal IDRs, as a shared structural feature of FET fusion oncoproteins (Fig. 5A), could promote aberrant DSB recruitment in other tumors within this class. EWSR1::ATF1 is the sole oncogenic driver of clear cell sarcoma (CCS) and contains the identical N-terminal IDR sequence as EWSR1::FLI1. The EWSR1::ATF1 fusion oncoprotein localized to laser-induced DSBs with delayed recruitment kinetics similar to EWSR1::FLI1, though with differences in departure timing (Fig. 5H and I). Like EWSR1::FLI1, reintroduction of either 1 or all 3 RGG-rich domains into EWSR1::ATF1 resulted in earlier DSB recruitment kinetics and higher levels of overall recruitment in an RGG dose–dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. S5J).

Analogous to our findings in Ewing sarcoma, CCS cells are dependent on HR factors PALB2 and BRCA1 for survival (Supplementary Fig. S5K and S5L). Moreover, transient knockdown of EWSR1::ATF1 in a patient-derived CCS cell line increased IR-dependent downstream ATM signaling (phosphorylation of ATM targets CHK2 and KAP1), with either no effect (DNA-PK) or slight decrease (ATR) in activation of the other upstream DDR kinases (Supplementary Fig. S5M–S5P). These data also reveal differences between FET fusion oncoproteins which may relate to the efficiency of EWSR1::ATF1 knockdown in these experiments or to fusion-specific biology; only EWSR1::FLI1 affected the initial activation of ATM (autophosphorylation), whereas both EWSR1::ATF1 and EWSR1::FLI1 caused suppression of downstream ATM signaling. Despite these differences, the consequence of partial suppression of ATM signaling was a similarly increased reliance on the compensatory ATR signaling axis, as CCS cells displayed EWSR1::ATF1-dependent, nanomolar range sensitivity (IC50: 27 nmol/L) to ATR inhibitor treatment with elimusertib (Supplementary Fig. S5Q). Taken together, our results support a model in which EWSR1 fusion oncoproteins are aberrantly recruited to DSB repair sites and impair ATM function.

Antitumor activity of ATR inhibitor elimusertib across FET rearranged PDX models

Lastly, we tested ATR inhibition as a therapeutic strategy in FET fusion oncoprotein–driven cancers. We hypothesized that the ATM/ATR synthetic lethality seen in ATM-mut (loss of function) cancers (50–52) might extend to FET-rearranged tumors with partial ATM defects caused by the FET fusion oncoproteins themselves. First, we determined whether FET fusion oncoprotein–driven cancer cell lines display increased sensitivity to the ATR inhibitor elimusertib that is currently being tested in multiple early-phase clinical trials (56). Our panel included five Ewing sarcoma cell lines with varying TP53 and STAG2 status (see Supplementary Note S3), two CCS (EWSR1::ATF1), three desmoplastic small round cell tumor (EWSR1::WT1), one myxoid liposarcoma cell line (FUS::CHOP), and multiple non–FET-rearranged cancer cell lines as controls. FET fusion–driven cancer cell lines were significantly more sensitive to ATR inhibitor treatment than the control cell lines tested, with IC50 values between 15 and 70 nmol/L (Fig. 6A) comparable with previously reported elimusertib IC50 values for ATM-mutant cancer cell lines (52). To test our hypothesis in a larger unselected panel of cancer cell lines, we utilized DepMap screening data for elimusertib in 880 cancer cell lines which included 17 Ewing sarcoma samples. Ewing sarcoma cell lines were again significantly more sensitive to ATR inhibition than the larger cohort of cancer cell lines (Fig. 6B), though our data do not exclude additional mechanisms of sensitivity to ATR inhibition in non–Ewing sarcoma cell lines such as ATM and other DDR gene mutations as previously reported (see Supplementary Note S4 regarding heterogeneity within FET-rearranged cell lines in terms of ATR inhibitor response; ref. 52).

We then tested whether the observed in vitro sensitivity to ATR inhibition would translate to in vivo PDX models of FET-rearranged cancers. Elimusertib was tested in five PDX models: two Ewing sarcoma models (ES-1 from initial diagnosis and ES-2 from a multiply relapsed patient), two CCS models (CCS-1 from initial diagnosis and CCS-2 from relapsed disease), and one DSRCT PDX derived from a posttreatment surgical resection, for which we also verified that the DSRCT fusion EWSR1::WT1 is recruited to laser-induced DSBs with similar kinetics to EWSR1::FLI and EWSR1::ATF1 (Supplementary Fig. S6A and S6B). Clinical details on the tumor models are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Elimusertib was dosed at 40 mg/kg twice daily per oral gavage, on a 3-day on/4-day off schedule until tumors exceeded prespecified size endpoints as previously described (52). There was minimal toxicity associated with this treatment regimen as assessed by weights and general activity scores, suggesting good tolerability of this dosing schedule.

Single-agent ATR inhibitor treatment significantly decreased tumor growth and increased progression-free survival in all five PDX models, including those derived from patients with relapsed Ewing sarcoma and CCS (Fig. 6C–G). The DSRCT xenograft showed the best response (partial by RECIST criteria, >50% reduction in tumor volume, Fig. 6E). Interestingly, the ES-1 and CCS-1 PDX models from initial diagnosis showed prolonged stable disease (18 days of stable disease compared with immediate progression in vehicle treated tumors at day 4) and better overall response to elimusertib than the ES-2 and CCS-2 PDXs derived from relapsed disease, though testing of additional Ewing sarcoma and CCS PDX models will be important to clarify whether PDXs derived from diagnostic and relapsed disease have differential responses to ATR inhibition.

To identify pharmacodynamic markers of elimusertib activity, we performed IHC for Ki-67 (cell proliferation), cleaved caspase-3 (apoptosis), and DNA damage (gH2AX). We observed decreased Ki-67 in elimusertib-treated tumors compared with vehicle controls reflecting decreased proliferation, whereas increased cleaved caspase-3 was only observed in the two Ewing sarcoma PDXs (Supplementary Fig. S6C–S6F). Consistent with prior reports (56, 57), we were unable to validate a direct biomarker of ATR inhibition, as pATR could not reliably be assessed by IHC and its downstream target pCHK1 did not show a significant difference between vehicle and elimusertib treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6C–S6F), which may reflect the low level of ATR pathway activation at baseline. However, gH2AX proved to be a reliable biomarker for elimusertib activity. The fraction of gH2AX-positive cells was significantly increased in all elimusertib-treated tumors compared with vehicle controls, consistent with unrepaired and ongoing DNA damage as the mechanism of cell death and antitumor activity. Taken together, our data demonstrate that FET rearranged cancers are preferentially sensitive to ATR inhibition and that single-agent treatment with the ATR inhibitor elimusertib has in vivo antitumor activity across multiple FET fusion oncoprotein–driven PDX models.

Discussion

Here, we describe how an oncogene can disrupt physiologic DNA damage repair by suppressing ATM activation and downstream signaling. We establish the compensatory DDR kinase, ATR, as a collateral dependency and therapeutic opportunity to target the (partial) ATM defects induced by the oncoproteins themselves using multiple FET fusion–driven cell line and PDX models. These data provide a template for the broader utilization of DDR-directed therapies in cancer through improved understanding of oncogene–DDR network interactions.

The specific nature of the DNA repair defect in Ewing sarcoma has been the subject of much debate given the strong clinical and laboratory-based data demonstrating chemosensitivity and radiosensitivity (8–11) and multiple synthetic lethal strategies to target presumptive HR defects (11, 58, 59). We find that Ewing sarcoma tumors are not “BRCA-like” (i.e., isolated loss of HR) but rather harbor partial ATM defects. Ewing sarcoma cells are dependent on key HR genes for survival and direct analysis of tumors of patients with Ewing sarcoma revealed none of the genomic hallmarks of HR pathway loss. These data may explain the lack of clinical responses to PARP inhibitor monotherapy in patients with Ewing sarcoma (unlike HR-deficient BRCA-mut and BRCA-like cancers; refs. 12–14). Instead, we show that EWSR1::FLI1 suppresses ATM activation and downstream signaling in response to DNA damage (IR) without affecting the other apical DDR kinases (DNA-PK and ATR). Although we do not exclude the possibility that additional DNA repair defects contribute to the DSB sensitivity of Ewing sarcoma cells, our finding of ATM (and not HR) impairment has important clinical implications for ongoing attempts to target the DDR in this disease, especially relating to ongoing PARP inhibitor combination trials.

To contextualize the magnitude and biological relevance of the partial ATM defects reported here, we benchmarked EWSR1::FLI1 against loss of canonical ATM activators (see Supplementary Note S2; Supplementary Fig. S7). The effect size of EWSR1::FLI1–mediated suppression of ATM activation/signaling is comparable with loss of established ATM activators, including MDC1 (29), RNF8 (48), or RNF168 (whose loss confer significant radiosensitivity to IR; ref. 49), but only ∼30% to 60% of the near-complete functional ATM deficiency seen with loss of the MRN complex. Thus, the magnitude of ATM impairment in Ewing sarcoma is biologically significant but not absolute. We further demonstrate the functional importance and therapeutic relevance of partial ATM suppression through increased reliance on a compensatory DDR kinase, ATR, and EWSR1::FLI1–dependent synthetic lethality with inhibitors of ATR and its major downstream target CHK1. Lastly, we establish a direct causal connection between the partial ATM defects in Ewing sarcoma and ATR inhibitor sensitivity by showing that rescue of the partial ATM defects through ATM overexpression diminishes the ATR inhibitor response. The observed ATM effects do not seem to depend on TP53 or STAG2 status, as the phenotypes were observed in both mut and WT Ewing sarcoma cell lines (see Supplementary Note S3 for additional discussion). We note that the genomic instability of ATM-mut (loss of function) tumors has not been associated with a specific mutational or copy-number alteration (CNA) signature (60), thus precluding comparison here, though Ewing sarcoma tumors do display recurrent CNAs (trisomy 8 in ∼50% of cases, ∼20% with trisomy 12 or 1q gain; ref. 24) which warrant further study. In total, our data nominate partial suppression of ATM as a therapeutically targetable DNA repair lesion in Ewing sarcoma.

How does EWSR1::FLI1, a TF fusion oncoprotein, create these DNA repair defects in Ewing sarcoma? We did not identify a set of EWSR1::FLI1 transcriptional targets that explain these ATM effects (e.g., MRN complex genes were not transcriptionally suppressed nor absent at the protein level by Western blotting). Instead, we find that EWSR1::FLI1 is recruited to DNA DSBs through homotypic IDR domain interactions with native EWSR1. Moreover, we show that EWSR1::FLI1 directly interacts with all three members of the MRN complex that is essential for ATM activation. Both DSB recruitment and MRN complex binding represent neomorphic functions of the fusion protein contributed by the EWSR1 N-terminal IDR, thus distinguishing EWSR1::FLI1 from WT, full-length FLI1. The specific nature of EWSR1::FLI1’s interaction with the MRN complex (e.g., direct or indirect, effect on MRN complex function) and whether other DNA repair proteins interact with EWSR1::FLI1 and contribute to the observed ATM effects (e.g., native EWSR1) are important unanswered questions for future studies to address. In summary, we show that EWSR1::FLI1 is unexpectedly recruited to DNA DSBs and interacts with the MRN complex, which may provide a molecular basis for the partial ATM defects in Ewing sarcoma that we describe here.

More generally, the shared structural organization across the class of FET fusion oncoproteins (i.e., retention of N-terminal IDRs that mediate homotypic and heterotypic FET protein interactions and loss of C-terminal RGG domains that promote rapid DSB recruitment, Fig. 5A) raised the intriguing hypothesis that FET fusion–driven cancers harbor a common set of DNA repair defects. Indeed, we show that the CCS fusion oncoprotein EWSR1::ATF1 localizes to DNA DSBs and induces similar suppression of IR-dependent ATM signaling (though with differences in ATM autophosphorylation that may relate to efficiency of EWSR1::ATF1 knockdown, Supplementary Fig. S5M). Analogous to our findings in Ewing sarcoma, CCS cells also display FET fusion oncogene-dependent nanomolar range sensitivity to the ATR inhibitor elimusertib. These data prompted us to test ATR inhibition across the class of FET rearranged cancers as a synthetic lethal therapeutic strategy to target partial ATM defects induced by FET fusion oncoproteins.

We selected a potent ATR inhibitor in clinical development (elimusertib) and observed nanomolar range IC50 values and preferential sensitivity of FET fusion–driven cancer cell lines in vitro (IC50 range: 15–70 nmol/L, comparable with reported elimusertib IC50 values in cell lines with pathogenic loss of function ATM mutations (52), see Supplementary Note S4 for discussion of the heterogeneity within FET-rearranged cell lines in terms of ATR inhibitor response). Moreover, we tested single-agent elimusertib treatment in five PDX models which included two Ewing sarcoma, two CCS, and one DSRCT model. All five models showed significant decreases in tumor growth and prolongation of progression-free survival, including Ewing sarcoma and CCS PDXs from multiply relapsed patients. Given that PDXs are a stringent bar in terms of disease control due to their inherent heterogeneity and single agents are highly unlikely to be curative for these set of diseases, the ability of ATR inhibitor monotherapy to control tumor growth in all five models was a striking finding. Our findings are consistent with published reports testing elimusertib in pediatric cancer PDXs (Ewing sarcoma was the only FET-rearranged tumor tested; ref. 30) and the ATR inhibitor AZ20 in Ewing sarcoma cell-line xenografts (61) but differ from a recent report with another ATR inhibitor in clinical trials (berzosertib) that showed no antitumor activity as monotherapy in Ewing sarcoma cell line xenografts (62), suggesting important potential differences between ATR inhibitors that warrant future study. We note that previously described mechanisms of ATR inhibitor sensitivity such as PGBD5 transposase expression (63) or RS (11) could also contribute to the elimusertib effects that we observed. In total, these data provide strong preclinical rationale for specifically testing elimusertib (and combinations thereof) in FET-rearranged cancers as part of ongoing early-phase clinical trials.

Finally, what might be the selective advantage for an oncogene to also suppress physiologic DNA repair? We speculate here that by partially disabling DDR signaling, cells with FET fusion oncoproteins can tolerate high levels of DDR activation caused by transcription and RS induced by the oncoprotein itself and thus overcome an important barrier to cellular transformation. Whether other TF fusion oncoproteins (or oncogenes more generally) interact with or disrupt tumor-suppressive DDR signaling networks as part of oncogenesis will be an intriguing topic for future work. In summary, our study describes how growth-promoting oncogenes can also interfere with physiologic DNA damage repair and provides rationale for a new targeted therapeutic strategy in Ewing sarcoma and the broader class of undruggable FET fusion–driven cancers.

Limitations of study

The limitations of this study relate to the clinical translation of our finding that FET fusion oncoproteins create partial ATM defects that enable synthetic lethal targeting of the compensatory ATR signaling axis. The clinical development of ATR inhibitors in FET-rearranged tumors would benefit from (i) biomarkers for patient selection and response and (ii) understanding of resistance mechanisms that might limit the long-term efficacy of ATR inhibition. These represent major challenges not only in the context of FET fusion–driven cancers but more generally for the early-phase clinical development of ATR inhibitors across many cancer subtypes and indications (e.g., ATM-mut tumors; refs. 56, 57). Regarding biomarker identification, we observed heterogeneity in terms of the ATR inhibitor response across both FET fusion cell lines (IC50 range: 15–70 nmol/L) and PDX models as discussed in Supplementary Note S4. Our data suggest, at least in Ewing sarcoma, co-occurring mutations (TP53 and STAG2) and the extent of ATM impairment are not correlated with ATR inhibitor response, but levels of total ATM may be predictive. Future studies in xenograft models will be critical to test this hypothesis and determine additional factors that contribute to the variability of ATR inhibitor response (e.g., similarities/differences between FET fusion oncoproteins, additional contributions to ATR inhibitor sensitivity such as RS or PGBD5 expression; refs. 56, 63, 64), in order to identify biomarkers for patient selection and clinical testing. Regarding ATR inhibitor resistance, we did not evaluate which, if any, of the many nongenetic mechanisms of ATR inhibitor resistance described previously apply to FET-rearranged cancers (note “on-target” ATR kinase domain mutations are extremely rare; refs. 56, 65, 66). We speculate that clonal selection for the low expression of the FET fusion oncoprotein may represent an additional mechanism of ATR inhibitor resistance in this class of tumors, as reduction in the levels of either EWSR1::FLI1 (Ewing sarcoma) or EWSR1::ATF1 (CCS) reduced ATR inhibitor sensitivity by restoring ATM signaling and reverting the synthetic lethal interaction (Fig. 4K and L; Supplementary Fig. S5Q). These data support a growing literature describing functional differences between EWSR1::FLI1–high and -low expressing cells in terms of proliferation, metabolic regulation, and metastatic potential that may affect therapeutic response (67, 68). Detailed studies in xenograft models and ultimately patients are needed to test this hypothesis and define the full set of mechanisms driving ATR inhibitor resistance.
