Cross Disease Similarity Awareness Learning (CDSAL) with DenseNet–EfficientNet embedding fusion for high-precision tomato leaf pathology classification with Grad-CAM explainability
Authors: C. Vanmathi, R. Mangayarkarasi, Rajat Agrawal, L. Sumalatha, Vijayalakshmi Shankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The research proposes Cross Disease Similarity Awareness Learning (CDSAL), a robust multiclass tomato leaf disease detection framework based on high-quality and explainable deep learning. The approach solves the problem of superimposed patterns of disease especially Leaf Miner, Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV), and nutrient deficiencies through the combination of multi-domain feature learning and inter-disease similarity modeling. In contrast to conventional metric learning or contrastive learning methods that function on pairwise or triplet sample associations, CDSAL develops a class-level Cross Disease Similarity Matrix that represents structured inter-disease proximity within the embedding space. Moreover, rather than employing episodic prototype construction typical of few-shot learning, the proposed system persistently updates centroid representations throughout supervised training and incorporates similarity-aware regularization directly into the loss function. This facilitates structural embedding reshaping specifically designed for visually overlapping illness categories, beyond traditional prototype-based learning methodologies. The input images are processed through HSV based green masking, morphological cleaning, extraction of leaf contours and resizing, and using a large amount of geometric and color-space augmentation to reduce the imbalance among the classes. DenseNet121 and EfficientNet-B0 are used to obtain feature representations and class-separated centroid of latent embedding’s to form a Cross Disease Similarity Matrix, where similarity-aware optimization is possible during training. Grad-CAM on the target layers offers decipherable disease-specific activation signatures. The findings of the experiments show that classification accuracy at unseen samples is 99.77% with high resilience to visual confounding. The predictions, proximity of diseases that are similar and explainable features are provided by CDSAL, thereby facilitating reliable decision-making in agricultural diagnostics.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is one of the crops of horticulture that is widely grown and is economically important globally as an important ingredient of the food security and sustainability of agriculture. Nevertheless, tomato growth is often limited by an extensive range of biotic and abiotic stress factors, such as fungal diseases, viral infections, insects and nutrient deficiencies. The symptoms usually present as visual effects on leaves in the form of mottling, discoloration, lesions and vein malformation which is why early detection is crucial in crop management and protection of yield. Conventional disease diagnosis is greatly dependent on visual observation by agricultural specialists, which is subjective, time consuming and dependent on human expertise especially on farming ecosystems in rural areas. Deep learning-based machine learning of plant diseases has become a revolution in the last decade, offering automated, scaling, and high-accuracy diagnostic systems1–3. Two types of transfer learning (Convolutional Neural Networks, CNNs) and attention-based architecture have shown impressive success in the plant pathological tasks, surpassing the traditional image processing method.

Despite these developments, one issue still exists: most plant diseases have visual or extreme similar symptoms and thus the advanced models cannot tell the difference between them. The signs of Leaf Miner, Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV), and nutrient deficiencies manifest similarly in tomato plants regarding leaf chlorosis, white spots, necrotic spots, and structural deformities4. This brings about misclassifications and lack of reliability of deep models in the real world by the similarities resulting in a false classification of similar-looking diseases.

Recent investigations have demonstrated that class discriminative learning without modeling inter-class relationships may result in biased representations especially on multi-class disease problems where the diseases have similar morphological characteristics5,6. One requires a new paradigm one that learns explicitly the similarity structure among diseases, instead of studying each category of disease as a single entity.

Deep learning is currently remarkably useful in automated detection of plant diseases, but its performance suffers greatly when the diseases have very similar or overlapping visual symptoms. Leaf Miner, Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV) and nutrient deficiencies in tomato plants tend to have similar morphological patterns, which are chlorotic spots, necrotic spots, or distorted veins. Traditional classification models assume that the categories of diseases are independent classes and do not learn similar relationships between visually related diseases which should exist. It results in biased learning of features and misclassification especially in field-like situations where there is high visual variability. Hence, the key issue of the proposed study is to create a deep learning model that can differentiate between various tomato leaf diseases with high accuracy and explicitly uses knowledge of cross-disease similarity to confuse non-similar classes and deliver interpretable decision support by means of explainable artificial intelligence.

This research suggests a new model called Cross Disease Similarity Awareness Learning (CDSAL) to face the challenges mentioned.

The purpose of this study is that it proposes a novel way of classifying plant diseases by incorporating the inter-disease similarity learning in the model training process. The proposed framework improves by modeling proximity of diseases by centroid feature and similarity matrices, thus increasing robustness against visually confounding symptoms and providing more reliable classification results. This is especially crucial in an agricultural environment where misdiagnosis can result to wrong application of chemicals, infection of transmittable diseases or huge economic hazards. The fact that explainability via Grad-CAM is also integrated helps to increase the practicality of the system as the farmers, agronomists, and domain experts can learn about the reasoning behind each prediction and make knowledgeable decisions. The study offers a contribution to the literature on scientific research by filling the major gap of existing methodologies, that is, the lack of similarity awareness learning in plant pathology, and offers a scalable solution that can be applied in mobile advisor systems and precision agriculture applications.

This research paper is limited to the deep learning image-based technique of detecting foliar disease in tomato plants. The work concentrates on visual overlapping category models of diseases, multi-domain feature extraction with pretrained CNN models, calculation of disease centrosomes, similarity matrices construction, as well as the generation of explainable activation maps. It excludes other parts of the plants like the stems, fruits or roots and it does not spread to other crops. Although the proposed model has high accuracy and good generalization, its performance is determined by the diversity and the quality as well as the representativeness of the available training images. Further, the similarity awaiting methods approach presumes that the intra class variability is adequate and inter class overlap is present in the data set to provide useful centroid relationships. The region-level explainability is offered by Grad-CAM, which, although successful, cannot provide pixel-level segmentation accuracy. These constraints identify the limit of the research and at the same time make the research results clear to interpret and implement.

Even though metric learning, contrastive learning, and prototype-based networks have been adopted extensively to complement feature discriminability, their goals are incompatible with that of the presented CDSAL framework. Triplet loss or contrastive learning methods are metric-based methods of learning that place sample-level distance constraints to minimize intra-class distances and maximize inter-class distances; these approaches do not explicitly model the structure of classes. In few-shot learning, prototype networks, which can be understood as episodically computing class centroids, can be used to facilitate classification in data-scarcity situations, but do not fit the centrally planned supervised optimization procedure with a global inter-class similarity model.

Contrary to it, CDSAL presents a Cross Disease Similarity Matrix at the level of the centroid of classes, thus providing architecture disease-to-disease proximity. This matrix is actively factored into the training goal through a similarity conscious regularization term, whereby the model is allowed to refactor the embedding topology in a disease category specific fashion pertaining to visually overlapping disease categories. As such, CDSAL is an approach that goes beyond metric-based learning paradigms by introducing structured semantic relationships between classes as part of the optimization process.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: “Literature survey”, details the related work carried out in the candidate field upon various aspects. “Proposed method”, demonstrates the various phases, including data preprocessing feature transformation, centroid computation, explainability component adopted to develop the CDSAL framework. “Experimental setup” furnishes the experimental setup used to build the presented CDSAL framework. “Results and discussion” summarizes the tested results with the suitable discussion. Finally in “Conclusion”, conclusion and future work is drawn.

Literature survey

In the past decade, the image-based plant diseases detection upon the CNN based architectures greatly assist the farmer to diagnosis and further treatment. The various concern regarding the candidate study including end-to end learning of multiple disease, multi-crop issues, choosing suitable methodology selection can be found in the venue7. Upon this foundation, later research summarizes the successful preprocessing, backbone choice, augmentation schemes, and the need for the inclusion of model’s interpretability, data imbalance, inter-class visual similarity were analyzed in8,9,18.

Deep learning architecture

In recent days, inclusion of pretrained models such as DenseNet, EfficientNet, MobileNet) and hybrid models that integrate more than one model shows promising results in perception of feature representations. Reviews and comparative analyses emphasize that no single backbone consistently outperforms the others across different datasets; rather, model performance is contingent upon the lesion size, background complexity, and implementation considerations (edge vs. server)9,18. Applied studies illustrate practical trade-offs where lightweight detectors (MobileNet-YOLO variants) allow quick use in the field in real time, but often miss details of fine lesions. On the other hand, DenseNet-based extractors yield richer embeddings that support downstream analysis10,11,14,15. More recent works indicate the use of hybrid CNN-transformers that capture both local texture and long-range dependencies, delivering consistent improvements on difficult leaf datasets22,29. These recent developments support CDSAL using multiple backbones (DenseNet121 and EfficientNet-B0), but the literature suggests some caution in engaging particularly with basic ensemble techniques rather than with principled fusion approaches that exploit complementarity across multiple backbones22.

Preprocessing, segmentation and data quality

There are multiple pieces of literature suggesting that effective preprocessing, particularly the green masking, morphological cleaning, and contour-based cropping, is instrumental in improving the localization of the lesions, eliminating background noise, and increasing the classifier accuracy in operational settings17,19,30. In the published works, segmentation-driven classifier pipelines work better than the unsegmented blind classifiers in instances where the lesions are small and occlusions exist17,30. That said, in literature, there exists segmentation algorithms that require curated datasets and gold standard imaging conditions, and in the field, domain shift (illumination, occlusion, and camera type) is well documented to lower segmentation accuracy19,20. These realities are the reason that CDSAL is focusing on an HSV-based segmentation model and heavy augmentation to capture and represent the variability in the field better.

Handling class imbalance and dataset bias

Class imbalance is still a serious issue since real datasets frequently have long-tailed distributions and dominant classes, which skew learning in favor of majority categories. Adaptive loss weighting, synthetic oversampling (ADASYN/SMOTE), class-aware augmentation, and ensemble techniques are among the methods described in the literature that result in significant gains in held-out accuracy but do not specifically address confusion between visually similar classes12,15,27. A shortcoming of approaches that optimize only for per-class accuracy is revealed by benchmarking efforts, which show that balancing strategies boost aggregate metrics but can conceal persistent misclassifications at the disease-pair level (e.g., nutritional deficiencies vs. viral spots)12,20. In order to directly handle pairwise confusions, CDSAL combines centroid-based similarity modeling with class weights during optimization.

Modeling inter-class similarity and centroid approaches

Only a small but increasing number of studies directly address inter-class similarity by combining spatial and frequency-domain data or by creating structures that lessen shared-feature confusion11,21. For instance, by capturing textural cues lacking in RGB alone, frequency-spatial fusion models reduce some forms of visual overlap. However, few studies compute centroids, or class-level representations, and use them as a training signal to regularize embeddings toward both structural proximity and discriminability. As a result, there is an analytical gap in the literature: whereas several methods increase overall accuracy, there is little precedent for using a Cross Disease Similarity Matrix that measures the distance across class centroids and guides learning iteratively. CDSAL seeks to close this methodological gap, which is crucial for issues like differentiating between Leaf Miner, TSWV, and nutrient deficiencies where visual characteristics are similar and confusing.

Feature fusion, multi-domain representations and hybrid solutions

To improve robustness to intra-class variability and inter-class similarity, recent studies support multi-domain or hybrid representations that combine spatial CNN features, frequency transforms, and attention-based contextualization [17, 22, 25]. By using CNNs for local texturing and transformers for global context, hybrid CNN-ViT frameworks show improved handling of complex lesion patterns, and frequency-domain components capture periodic or subpixel artifacts that are useful for specific diseases17,22. However, the existing studies recorded significant results, not covered all the aspects intend to build comprehensive model. Hence, the presented attempted to create reliable embeddings and precise diagnostics, integration of multi-backbone feature extraction with centroid similarity metrics and interpretability using Grad-CAM tool.

Significance of explainability

The inclusion of model interpretability is essential in diseases diagnosis system. In the field of agriculture, it is surely appreciable as the Grad-CAM visualization precisely highlights the lesion spots and enables further forecast8,25,28,31. Visualization boosts end-user trust and facilitates mistake investigation, as several platform and dataset publications show28. However, saliency maps are not linked to learnt inter-class correlations in current explainability work, which usually approaches visualization as a post-hoc add-on. There is a chance in the literature to combine explainability and similarity modeling such that heatmaps might provide deeper diagnostic cues by not only highlighting discriminative regions but also explaining why a sample is near several illness centroids. This integration is precisely what CDSAL’s combined centroid similarity analysis and Grad-CAM signatures aim to achieve.

Similarity and metric learning

In the field of plant disease detection, scrutiny of metric learning has been forsakenly thin in nature and the research that exists has primarily been aimed at either enriching generalization in small sample settings or refining discriminative feature learning. Few shot enabled models, perform classification upon the class centroids at isolated instances. Such methods are designed for the models that demand minimal samples and don’t include the global inter class similarity into the supervised optimization. In addition, the existing deep learning enabled plant diseases classification models focusing on attention mechanisms or data augmentation procedures, but do not address the embeddings regulation in an optimized way.

Although this has been achieved, the current state of metric-learning methodologies largely imposes inter-class segregation and does not consider the visual similarity between classes of diseases. Stringent distance maximization can be ineffective in capturing the underlying disease topology in cases of agricultural pathology where diseases often have a similar symptomology. To solve this shortcoming, the given Cross Disease Similarity-Aware Learning (CDSAL) model includes a Cross Disease Similarity Matrix that reflects semantic proximity on the level of classes and puts this structure into the objective function. Deviating conventional metric-learning paradigm, CDSAL reconfigures the embedding topology on a disease level enabling a gradual division of visual overlapping categories whilst maintaining biologically relevant similarity.

Summary of gaps and justification for CDSAL

A review of the literature (Table 1) shows steady advancements in explainability, segmentation, augmentation, and backbones, but there are still several unsolved problems. First, pairwise confusions caused by visual resemblance are rarely lessened by techniques that address class imbalance. Second, although multi-domain and hybrid characteristics increase resilience, illness proximity is rarely explicitly modeled using them. Third, explainability’s diagnostic relevance is limited since it is frequently post-hoc and unrelated to the acquired inter-class structure. When taken as a whole, these gaps support a framework that (a) standardizes leaf segmentation and augmentation for field robustness, (b) extracts complementary multi-backbone embeddings, (c) models cross-disease similarity during training by computing and utilizing class centroids, and (d) links similarity information to explainable visual signatures exactly the contributions suggested in CDSAL. By filling in these gaps, CDSAL hopes to provide interpretable, deployable diagnostics for precision agriculture and lessen misclassification among tomato diseases that visually overlap.

Proposed method

The classification of tomato leaf diseases is particularly difficult because of the high degree of visual similarities across several ailments, including Leaf Miner damage, Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV), and other nutrient shortages. It is challenging for traditional deep learning models to distinguish between these illnesses since they frequently cause overlapping symptoms like chlorosis, necrotic areas, stippling, and venous abnormalities. Most of the models considers discriminative feature to influence the classification. Incorporating features, sharing the visual similarity with suitable explanation, surely alleviate the issues incurred through the confused pattern. So, the frequently used embeddings unable to interpret the hierarchical structure in feature space, which often results in misclassifications when confronted with confusing visual data.

This study presents a comprehensive model known as Cross Disease Similarity Awareness Learning (CDSAL) to alleviate these drawbacks. Cross-disease similarity, feature representation using pre-trained models, made the CDSAL, regularize the feature space upon the visually significant similarity patterns, in contrast to the conventional classifiers relying strict class separations rules. Hence, this enables the CDSYL to acquire structured, discriminative representations that accurately depict the visual proximities between diseases. Additionally, CDSAL provides agricultural stakeholders with visible diagnostic help by integrating Grad-CAM-based explainability to show the particular regions that contributed to the forecast.

Leaf Preprocessing and Segmentation, Feature Extraction and Augmentation, and Similarity-Aware Learning and Explainability are the three main subsystems of the design, which is shown in Fig. 1. To guarantee that the final model makes use of both clear spatial representations and high-level similarity links between illness categories, each subsystem is connected to the others.

Preprocessing and leaf segmentation

The correct segmentation of the leaf areas is important in preventing noise caused by the background as well as a reliable quality of input. Raw images can be extremely different in terms of lighting, orientation, clutter in the background and so, the initial step of CDSAL is the color-space conversion, green masking, morphological processes and contour-based cropping to select the leaf region with exact precision.

Data augmentation and class weighting

To increase the resilience of the model, CDSAL uses both geometric and photometric augmentations as mentioned in Fig. 2. Geometric augmentation adds spatial variability by using random rotations, horizontal and vertical flips, zoom-in zoom-out, and random spatial shifts to allow the model to be more general to other orientations and scales of input images. In addition to this, photometric augmentation alters the appearance of the images by varying the brightness, applying contrast stretching, and introducing saturation jitter, which is useful to ensure the model is robust to changes in the lighting and color conditions naturally observed in the real world. The combination of these augmentation methods increases the variety of the training dataset and increases the capacity of the model to learn the discriminative features on heterogeneous samples. Table 2 provides the sample statistics and the strategy of the augmentation. This data augmentation is applied during the training dynamically. Aggressive augmentation is applied for Minority samples, Majority samples Mild augmentation and moderate for remaining classes. However, the total number of stored images is unchanged. Each class has the calculation of weights to maximize imbalance. Multi backbone feature extraction

Deep learning models are highly sensitive to extracted features and their quality and expressiveness. Due to the fact that tomato leaf diseases have very slight differences in their texture, tone, structural damages, and the distribution of lesions, the use of a single backbone usually leads to insufficient representational ability. The CDSAL model thus uses a multi-backbone approach with the complementary capabilities of DenseNet121 and EfficientNet-B0. DenseNet121 is one whereby the layers are highly connected with each layer being fed with feature maps by all the previous layers. This architecture promotes feature reuse and gradient flow that is useful in capturing fine-grained textures and micro-lesions that are good pointer of certain diseases. The output of DenseNet121’s final convolutional block is a high-dimensional tensor7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:F}_{DenseNet\:}\in\:\:{R}^{B\times\:{C}_{d}\times\:{H}_{d}\times\:{W}_{d}}$$\end{document}

where B is the batch size, Cd the channel dimension, and Hd​, Wd ​ the spatial dimensions. To obtain a compact representation, adaptive average pooling compresses this tensor into a class-activating vector:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{d}=AvgPool\left({F}_{DenseNet\:}\right)\in\:\:{R}^{{C}_{d}}\:$$\end{document}

The vector has been found especially useful in coding the textures of subtle lesions, streaking, and necrotic edges characterizing a disease, like Leaf Miner or Septoria Leaf Spot. Then the Efficientnet-B0 parameters are defined to make a expressive representations through compound scaling. It is excellent in recording world color variations, continuity of the patterns and distortion of structure in leaves. Likewise, the feature vector of its last block is pooled together.9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{e}=AvgPool\left({F}_{EffNet\:}\right)\in\:\:{R}^{{C}_{e}}$$\end{document}

Whereas DenseNet121 identifies micro-textures, EfficientNetB0 identifies macro-structural features including vein deformities, global chlorosis, color shifts, and others that play a vital role in distinguishing diseases with similar symptoms. The unification representation is achieved by way of feature concatenation. It yields an embedding that is a hybrid of local and global disease cues. This combination of the two models is very useful in making the model more sensitive to the minor inter-class variations as well as maintaining stability in the presence of noise in the environment. The feature space, which is enhanced by this feature, is also needed as illustrated in subsequent stages in order to build workable disease centroids.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F=\left[{F}_{d}\:\right|\left|{F}_{e}\right]$$\end{document}

The presented approach uses the complete features set derived through the two pre-training models without including any weighting constraints. Unlike the other weighted summation and attention-based fusion methods, the implemented concatenation doesn’t incur additional trainable variables, thereby ensure architecture simplicity and decreases the risk of overfitting. In addition, concatenation facilitates the subsequent layers of the network to learn the relevant relationships between the combined features, enabling the model to automatically detect their relative significance. Hence, this straightforward method ensures that the complementary features captured by Fd and Fe are retained without increasing computational overhead and ensures model convergence.

Centroid-based cross-disease similarity learning

One of the new aspects of the CDSAL framework is that it enables the use of disease-disease similarity into the learning process and in doing so allows the model to classify visually similar disorders more effectively than traditional deep classifiers. Having created fused feature embeddings based on the DenseNet121 and EfficientNet-B0 backbones, it is then followed by the construction of a structured representation of the characteristics of classes in the embedding space. To accomplish this, all the feature vectors of a given disease class are summed to calculate a centroid of that class that is used to represent a semantic signature of that disease. Mathematically, given a class c of nc samples with feature embeddings Fi(c), the centroid is given by11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{c}=\frac{1}{{n}_{c}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{{n}_{c}}{{F}_{i}}^{\left(c\right)}$$\end{document}

The centroid is a summary of the typical location of the disease cluster in high-dimensional space and is a scale of the underlying visual characteristics that define the disease such as texture continuity, lesion shape or color progression. When the computation of centroids is done on all classes, they all create a structured mapping that shows the relationship in which diseases are placed with each other. In continued analysis of the relationships among diseases, CDSAL generates a Cross-Disease Similarity Matrix, which measures similarity between all class centros similarity by using the cosine similarity. Two diseases i and j are similar and the similarity is calculated to be.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{ij}=\frac{{C}_{i}.{C}_{j}}{||{C}_{i}||\:||{C}_{j}||}$$\end{document}

where Sij∈[0,1]. A large similarity score means that there is a large visual overlap of diseases where higher values near to 1 mean high degree of visual similarity. The resulting similarity matrix is a strong diagnostic measure, showing what diseases lie in visually adjacent areas of the feature space. To illustrate, nutrient deficiencies and some types of viral infections as well as some types of fungal diseases tend to have similar chlorosis and patchy discolorations and therefore their similarities are naturally high. These relationships are important to comprehend in fields like agriculture where slight visual clues cannot be known to be treated wrongly. The outcome of this is a Cross-Disease Similarity Matrix:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S=\:\left[\begin{array}{ccc}S11&\:\cdots\:&\:S1K\\\:\vdots&\:\ddots\:&\:\vdots\\\:Sk1&\:\cdots\:&\:SKK\end{array}\right]$$\end{document}

To incorporate this similarity knowledge into the training of the model, CDSAL proposes a similarity-conscious loss function, which is an extension of normal cross-entropy loss. This extra loss term promotes the model to keep a proper degree of separation among diseases that may be confused visually and minimize the tendency of misclassification. To control the inter class structure, the proposed method introduced the margin based centroid level similarity. Let Ci and Cj denotes the centroid class in the fused embedding space. The cosine distance is computed using d(Ci,Cj) = 1-Cosine(Ci,Cj).

The similarity-aware loss is defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{sim}=\sum\limits_{i\ne\:j}{S}_{ij}\:max\left(0,m-d({C}_{i},{C}_{j})\right)$$\end{document}

Where m is the predefined margin to control the minimal separation. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{ij}$$\end{document} is the confusion aware weight factor and it is defined as15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{ij}=\frac{{C}_{ij}+{C}_{ji}}{{\sum\:}_{k}{C}_{ik}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{ij}$$\end{document} is the confusion frequencies between class i and class j, k is the number of disease classes.

This approach applies more distance for disease pairs that are often combined while applying smaller limitations to inherently distinct classes. This centroid-level formulation modifies the global embedding topology at the disease-class level, in contrast to standard triplet or contrastive losses that function on individual samples. This hierarchical regularization is especially appropriate for visibly overlapping plant diseases, where maintaining biologically significant similarity links is as crucial as enhancing separability.

The formulation moves the disease pairs that are likely to coincide in the embedding space but still manages to retain the natural form of the classes. The entire training goal, hence, becomes16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{L}={\mathcal{L}}_{CE}+\lambda\:{\mathcal{L}}_{sim},$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{CE}$$\end{document} is the weighted multi class cross-entropy loss which ensures the correct classification. In which λ defines the impact of similar regularization in relation to the overall classification goal. The other significant deliverable of the study is the incorporation of explainability that facilitates transparent and accountable forecasts. The visualizations of the grad CAM can be used as a part of the diagnostic data, and such a model can guarantee that its decision would be understandable. It is especially useful in the case of agricultural advisory systems, as well as real-life deployments, where actionable information must be accurate and explainable. Through system performance, the presence of care preprocessing, multi backbone fusion and similar regularization will provide high overall accuracy, as reflected by prototype results of up to 99.77% accuracy on unknown test samples. This accuracy enables the applicability of large scales to the fields, greenhouses and digital agriculture platform. The idea of CDSAL is made to be applicable in generalization beyond the exclusive field of tomato leaves. Centroid guided learning combined with explainability are applicable in other crops and types of diseases. In this way, this dataset is not the only expected result: CDSAL provides a methodological improvement to visually overlapping classes in computer vision tasks in agriculture, healthcare, biology and industrial inspection. In contrast to center loss or prototype learning, which focus on minimizing intra-class variance, the proposed similarity-aware loss explicitly addresses inter-class closeness and confusion patterns, facilitating structured control of disease-level embeddings rather than relying solely on distance-based separation.

Experimental setup

The developed Cross Disease Similarity Awareness Learning (CDSAL) system was developed completely using Python 3.10, with popular deep learning packages of PyTorch, Torchvision, NumPy, OpenCV, and Scikit-Learn. Each of the experiments was performed on a workstation with an NVIDIA RTX-series graphics card and 32 GB RAM, which provides an effective parallel calculation of feature extraction, augmentation, and similarity matrix creation. The execution has been done in the form of modules, whereby the preprocessing, data augmentation, model training, centroid computation, and Grad CAM explainability modules were run as independent modules but interconnected modules where reproducibility and controlled evaluation became possible.

To test the efficiency of the proposed strategy, we utilized the dataset available at PlantVillage32, which is among the most popular and the most tested research dataset in the field of precision agriculture. The data set is comprised of good-quality RGB pictures that are various tomato illnesses, such as Leaf Miner, Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV), mosaic viruses, late blight, early blight, and nutrient deficiencies presented in Fig. 3. The dataset that is used in this research is the PlantVillage data of tomato and potato leaf images, which consists of 20,639 images, with 15 classes, 13 of which are disease classes and 2 healthy classes. This dataset is a well-distributed and richly annotated set of leaf images, taken in a variety of conditions, and has become one of the established standards in the research on plant disease detection. The categories of disease include several pathogens that have an impact on both crops, such as Pepper bell Bacterial spot and Pepper bell healthy; Potato Early blight, Potato Late blight and Potato healthy; and a general category of tomato disease, such as Tomato Bacterial spot, Tomato Early blight, Tomato Late blight, Tomato Leaf Mold, Tomato Septoria leaf spot, Tomato Spider mites (Two-spotted spider mite), Tomato Target Spot, Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus and Tomato mosaic Before training, the images were all preprocessed with the above-described HSV based green masking, morphological cleaning, contour detection, cropping, and resizing to provide similar and background-free inputs to the feature extractors. To fairly consider the generalization performance, the data was randomly split into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing parts. The training was performed with the weighted cross-entropy loss and similarity-aware regularization, and the model that demonstrated the best performance according to the validation accuracy was saved at the end of each epoch.

The presented experiment uses the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 32 and trained with 100 epochs. Model learning process was monitored upon the validation set to retain the highest accuracy. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document} as mentioned in the Eq. 14, regulates the contribution of the similarity aware regularization term was computed using the empirical tuning upon the validation set and finally the value that result the best validation performance was selected. The class centroids C were calculated upon the feature embeddings of the samples belonging to each class and the similarity matrix S was recalculated at the end every epoch, ensuring that the similarity aware constrain reveals the evolving feature representation learned by the network.

Limitations and generalization

The proposed framework shows good results in plant disease classification; it is important to highlight the limitations. First the model uses pre-trained deep convolutional backbones to obtain discriminative features. Even though the proposed method enhances classification and training performance, the efficiency of the model might be determined by the representational ability of these backbone networks. Moreover, the model is trained using a set of defined disease classes, and its behavior in new disease or previously unknown disease categories could need additional assessment or adaptation using incremental learning schemes.

Second, the proposed method uses two backbone feature extraction architectures, which augments the computational cost of the inference. Although the method has benefit of considering feature diversity with better classification performance, it increases the computation costs to single-backbone models. This can be a problem with real-time implementation on devices with limited resources. Future directions will be in model optimization strategies like model pruning, lightweight architecture and knowledge extraction to enhance more efficient inference in agricultural real-time applications.

Third, the major dataset of this research, PlantVillage, consists of images that were taken in controlled laboratory settings, with backgrounds and light being the same, and abundant. Such controlled environments may not be sufficient to represent the real agricultural settings. To overcome this weakness further experiments were for ablation study with TomatoVillage dataset which covers more varied visual conditions.

Conclusion and future work

The research work attempted in this paper presents a interpretable solution for the automated plant disease classification, CDSAL especially by alleviating the existing challenges incurred due to the visual overlapping features. In contrast to the traditional deep learning methods that assume diseases to be independent categories, CDSAL directly learns inter-disease relationships based on centroid-based similarity learning with multi-backbone feature extraction and Grad-CAM explainability. The presented framework is very comprehensive to represent the fine-grained lesion texture and overall structural features, and this allows better differentiation between disease with similar visual appearances like Early and Late Blight and viral infections. The experimental results indicate that CDSAL has better performance with an accuracy of 99.86% on controlled data (plant village dataset), and a good generalization of 91.83% in real-field conditions (tomato village dataset). Also, similarity matrix and Grad-CAM images offer biologically meaningful information, which proves that the model is making decisions based on the relevant disease areas, as opposed to random image features. In a broader study, CDSAL improves the state of the art in the classification of plant diseases by transitioning beyond the entirely discriminative learning to structured similarity-conscious representation learning. This centroid-level topology control heavily implements the misclassification between visually similar classes and the maintenance of semantic relationships, overcoming a major drawback of current models.

Future work should consider adding multimodal data (hyperspectral imaging) to it, expanding the framework to transformer or graph-based architectures and allowing it to be used in edge devices to execute real-time applications, despite its high performance. Moreover, it is possible to increase its applicability by modeling the disease progression, as well as by applying it to other crops. Overall, this article adds to a solid, interpretable, and scalable solution, which provides a valuable contribution to the intelligent and reliable systems of plant health monitoring.
