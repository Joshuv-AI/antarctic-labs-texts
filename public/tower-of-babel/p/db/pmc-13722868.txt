Auxin-mediated RcARF8-RcWRKY29-RcERF98 module protects the rose petals against the Botrytis cinerea by modulating ascorbic acid biosynthesis and reactive oxygen species scavenging
Authors: Jie Wu, Mengyue Jing, Shiqiu Yang, Qiushi Li, Daliang Li, Qi Fu, Xujun Ma, Dan Wang, Jiaxing Li, Hongying Jian, Huijun Yan, Kaixue Tang, Hao Zhang, Xianqin Qiu, Weikun Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs one of the world’s most important ornamental crops, rose (Rosa sp.) is highly susceptible to grey mold disease caused by Botrytis cinerea. Outbreaks of this disease during long-distance transportation often lead to substantial economic losses. This study identifies the WRKY transcription factor RcWRKY29 as a key regulator in the defense response against B. cinerea. Integrated RNA sequencing and DNA affinity purification sequencing analyses revealed RcERF98 as a direct target of RcWRKY29. Knockdown or overexpression RcWRKY29 correspondingly altered RcERF98 transcript levels, which in turn modulated ascorbic acid (AsA) accumulation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging in petals. Furthermore, exogenous application of naphthalene acetic acid significantly enhanced disease resistance and concurrently upregulated RcWRKY29 expression. We demonstrated that the auxin response factor RcARF8, but not RcARF1, binds to the RcWRKY29 promoter and activates its transcription. Moreover, knocking down or overexpression of RcARF8 affected the expression of both RcWRKY29 and RcERF98, thereby regulating AsA biosynthesis and ROS homeostasis, and ultimately determining disease susceptibility. Collectively, our findings reveal a signaling module in which the auxin-mediated transcription cascade involving RcARF8-RcWRKY29-RcERF98 confers resistance to B. cinerea by coordinating AsA-dependent ROS scavenging in rose petals.

Introduction

Cut rose (Rosa spp.), the most economically valuable floricultural crop worldwide, contributes to over 30% of the global yield and market value of cut flowers. However, its postharvest quality and commercial value are seriously threatened by Botrytis cinerea, a major fungal pathogen responsible for economic losses exceeding 35% during long-distance transport. Accumulating evidence indicates that plant immune responses to various pathogens are critically modulated by complex hormonal networks, including auxins, cytokinin (CK), gibberellins, and ethylene, salicylic acid (SA), brassinosteroids, and jasmonic acid (JA) [1–4].

Among these phytohormones, auxin predominantly features as a regulatory hub that interconnects plant growth pathways with immune signaling [5–9]. Auxin enhances plant disease resistance by modulating defense-related pathways and physiological processes. For example, moderate auxin levels confer resistance to B. cinerea in kiwifruit [10]. In tobacco, calmodulin-mediated binding to YUC proteins (YUC1, YUC3, YUC5, and YUC6) promotes IAA biosynthesis, which subsequently activates phytosulfokine-induced immunity against the same pathogen [11]. Auxin response factors (ARFs), the key transcriptional regulators of auxin signaling, display remarkable functional diversity in plant immunity. For example, in rice (Oryza sativa), viral proteins may interact with some ARFs to suppress immunity and enhance susceptibility [12], whereas other ARFs play active roles in promoting resistance; OsARF17 enhances antiviral defense through specific protein interactions [13], and in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), StARF16 attenuates immunity to necrotrophic pathogens by directly binding to the StNPR1 promoter [14]. Furthermore, knockdown OsARF12 in rice results in heightened susceptibility to the fungal pathogen Magnaporthe oryzae [15]. Despite accumulating insights into ARF-mediated immunity across various plant systems, the specific roles and regulatory mechanisms of ARFs in modulating defense responses against B. cinerea in rose flowers remain largely unknown.

Integrating downstream of primary hormone signaling, WRKY transcription factors serve as pivotal executors of innate plant immunity. In B. cinerea-infected roses, multiple WRKY genes are differentially induced and exert a range of regulatory functions that can be diverse or even antagonistic. For example, loss of RcWRKY13 function enhances resistance, whereas knocking out RcWRKY41 compromises it [16, 17]. Notably, WRKY proteins frequently converge with auxin signals by physically or transcriptionally coupling with ARFs form hierarchical ARF-WRKY regulatory modules [18–21]. These modules act as universal intersecting hubs coordinating diverse environmental stimuli. For instance, operating strictly cooperatively, ARF5 and WRKY40 coactivate the IPT promoter to drive Agrobacterium-induced tumor formation [22], while in rice, OsARF12 directly targets the OsWRKY13 promoter to boost antiviral immunity [23]. Analogous ARF-WRKY complexes also profoundly govern abiotic tolerances, such as frost and drought adaptation in apples [24, 25]. Although such complexes undeniably orchestrate the critical balance between plant growth and systemic stress responses globally, whether an ARF-WRKY molecular cascade explicitly mediates innate resistance to B. cinerea in roses remain completely unexplored.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are central signaling molecules in plant immunity, and their dynamic homeostasis is crucial for effective defense against pathogen invasion [26–28]. Pathogen infection commonly triggers rapid ROS accumulation, particularly H₂O₂, which activates early immune responses but may also cause oxidative damage if not properly controlled [29, 30]. To maintain redox balance, plants employ both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems, including superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, ascorbate peroxidase, catalase, and ascorbic acid (AsA) [31]. Emerging evidence indicates that ERF transcription factors are important regulators of this process. For example, SlERF.F4 enhances disease resistance in tomato by promoting antioxidant capacity and phenylpropanoid metabolism [32], whereas AtERF98 and RcERF98 stimulate AsA biosynthesis through activation of VTC1, thereby enhancing ROS scavenging ability in Arabidopsis and rose [33, 34]. These findings suggest that ERF-mediated redox regulation is an important component of plant stress adaptation. However, whether specific ERF members contribute to resistance against B. cinerea through modulation of AsA accumulation and ROS homeostasis in rose petals remains unclear.

In this study, we identified RcWRKY29 as a positive regulator of rose resistance to B. cinerea that directly activates RcERF98 expression. Exogenous application of naphthalene acetic acid (NAA) enhanced this resistance, and further analyses showed that the auxin-responsive factor RcARF8 is induced by B. cinerea infection and positively regulates RcWRKY29 expression. Together, these findings establish an RcARF8-RcWRKY29-RcERF98 transcriptional module that promotes rose petal resistance to B. cinerea by modulating downstream defense responses and ROS homeostasis.

Results

RcWRKY29 contributes to resistance against B. cinerea in rose petals

To identify key regulators involved in defense responses, we analyzed our transcriptomic data and focused on differentially expressed WRKY transcription factor genes. Among these, RchiOBHmChr7g0196571 exhibited the significantly upregulation upon B. cinerea infection, suggesting a potential role in modulating resistance (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). Subcellular localization analysis revealed the presence of this molecule in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Phylogenetic reconstruction placed the Rosa chinensis gene RchiOBHmChr7g0196571 in the same clade as Arabidopsis WRKY29 (AT4G23550.1); consequently, the corresponding protein was designated RcWRKY29 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Despite the established roles of many WRKY genes in defense against B. cinerea, RcWRKY29 is still largely unknown. To verify the role of RcWRKY29 in response to B. cinerea, we conducted a virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) assay in rose petals to reduce the RcWRKY29 level, as described previously [17]. The silencing of RcWRKY29 resulted in a significantly larger lesion area and higher ion leakage rate than those of the TRV control petal discs (Fig. 1B–E), indicating that RcWRKY29 silencing promoted rose flower susceptibility to B. cinerea. Moreover, we overexpressed RcWRKY29 in rose petals as described previously [35]. In contrast, RcWRKY29 overexpression led to a smaller lesion area and lower ion leakage rate than did the pSuper1300 control (Fig. 1F–I), demonstrating that RcWRKY29 increased tolerance to B. cinerea in rose petals.

RcWRKY29 binds to the RcERF98 promoter and activates its transcriptional activity, thereby modulating AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging

To verify the downstream regulatory mechanism of RcWRKY29, we conducted DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq) to screen the targets involved in the response to B. cinerea. By performing a joint analysis between the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and the DAP-seq database, we identified 2824 DEGs and 9671 candidate RcWRKY29 target genes (Fig. 2A and B). Subsequent Venn diagram analysis revealed 769 overlapping genes, suggesting their potential role as direct targets of RcWRKY29 (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment results revealed that these genes were enriched mainly in ‘response to chitin’, ‘response to nitrogen compound’, ‘response to stimulus’, and ‘response to hormone’ (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S1). Chitin has been widely reported to play a key role in defense against fungal pathogens. However, whether RcWRKY29 can directly regulate these genes is still largely unknown. Hence, we performed a heatmap analysis of the 23 genes enriched in the ‘response to chitin’ category. Among them, the RchiOBHmChr6g0288241, RchiOBHmChr2g0093571, RchiOBHmChr3g0476021, and RchiOBHmChr4g0442301 levels were significantly increased at 36 h after B. cinerea infection (Fig. 2E). A yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay was subsequently used to identify direct interactions. After verification, we found that RcWRKY29 interacted with the RchiOBHmChr6g0288241 promoter but not with the other promoters (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S2). Phylogenetic analysis and conserved domain alignment revealed that RchiOBHmChr6g0288241 is an AP2-ERF transcription factor, a homolog of Arabidopsis ERF98 (AT3G23230.1); hence, we named it RcERF98 (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Subcellular localization analysis suggested that this molecule localizes to the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S3B), and its expression was significantly increased after B. cinerea infection (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Moreover, chromatin immunoprecipitation PCR (ChIP-qPCR) and dual luciferase assays further demonstrated that RcWRKY29 could bind to the P5 region of the RcERF98 promoter and increase its promoter activity (Fig. 3B–E). RT-qPCR then showed that the silencing or overexpression of RcWRKY29 caused decreased or increased RcERF98 expression, respectively (Fig. 3F and G). Moreover, silencing RcERF98 consequently enhanced disease susceptibility in petal discs, as evidenced by significantly increased lesion area and ion leakage compared to the control (Fig. 4A–D), a phenotype consistent with RcWRKY29 knockdown. In addition, RcERF98 overexpression resulted in a smaller lesion area and a lower ion leakage rate than the pSuper1300 control (Fig. 4E–H), thereby mimicking the effects of RcERF98 overexpression on B. cinerea. These results showed that compared to the pSuper1300 control, overexpression of RcERF98 in rose petals demonstrated increased tolerance to B. cinerea. Based on our previous study, we have demonstrated that RcERF98 promotes salt stress resilience in roses by inducing the biosynthesis of AsA and the scavenging of ROS [33]. Moreover, ChIP-qPCR and dual luciferase assays confirmed that RcERF98 binds directly to the P2 region of the RcVTC1 promoter and activates its promoter activity, which was the AsA synthesis gene (Fig. 5A and B) [32]. Overexpression or silencing of RcERF98 and RcWRKY29 led to corresponding changes in RcVTC1 expression (Fig. 5C and E). Hence, we investigated the AsA content and ROS accumulation after B. cinerea infection. We found that overexpression of RcWRKY29 and RcERF98 significantly enhanced AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging compared to the pSuper1300 control (Fig. 5H and I). DAB staining revealed decreased staining intensity (Fig. 5G), and the expression of genes involved in these pathways exhibited similar upregulation (Supplementary Fig. S4E and F). Conversely, silencing of RcWRKY29 and RcERF98 resulted in decreasing AsA levels and H2O2 accumulation compared to the TRV control (Fig. 5K and L). DAB staining revealed significantly increased staining intensity (Fig. 5J), accompanied by downregulation of the corresponding biosynthetic and scavenging genes (Supplementary Fig. S4B and C). Together, our findings suggest that the RcWRKY29-RcERF98 module functions vitally in plant defense against B. cinerea by modulating the AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging.

Auxin-mediated RcARF8 positively regulates the RcWRKY29-RcERF98 module in response to B. cinerea

As widely reported, CK, ABA, JA and auxin play critical roles in defense signaling against infection by B. cinerea [17, 36]. Whether hormones influence RcWRKY29 expression is still largely unknown. With respect to hormone treatment, NAA (50 μM) treatment significantly induced RcWRKY29 expression at 36 hours postinoculation (hpi), but we found no significant differences under the 6-BA, ABA, or MeJA treatments (Fig. 6A). To explore the roles of auxin in response to B. cinerea, we quantified the relative IAA content in rose petals after B. cinerea infection and found that the IAA content significantly increased at 36 and 48 hpi (Fig. 6B). We further found that NAA treatment significantly enhanced disease resistance against B. cinerea by enhancing AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging (Fig. 6C–G). In contrast, NPA treatment impairs AsA synthesis and ROS scavenging capacity, thereby making petals more susceptible to disease (Fig. 6H–L). DAB staining showed that NAA treatment resulted in lighter staining in petals, whereas NPA treatment led to darker staining (Fig. 6E and J). Therefore, we hypothesized that auxin might modulate resistance against B. cinerea via RcWRKY29. The functions of auxin usually depend on ARFs [37–39]. Using the JASPAR CORE website (https://jaspar.genereg.net/), we predicted cis-elements in the RcWRKY29 promoter and identified 8 auxin-responsive cis-elements (Fig. 7B). Among the 12 previously identified ARF genes, 6 presented high expression levels in petals [40]. Thus, we cloned the 6 ARF coding sequences (CDS) and conducted Y1H to identify the interaction between the 6 ARF proteins and RcWRKY29. Notably, RcARF1 and RcARF8 could interact with the RcWRKY29 promoter in yeast, but the other genes could not (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Fig. S5A). Moreover, the expression of RcARF1 and RcARF8 was induced by NAA (Supplementary Fig. S5B and C). The dual luciferase assay results suggested that RcARF8, but not RcARF1, could bind to and activate the RcWRKY29 promoter (Fig. 7C–E, Supplementary Fig. S5D–F). No significant change in resistance to B. cinerea was observed following the silencing of RcARF1, compared to the TRV control plants. ChIP-qPCR defined the P3 and P5 regions of the RcWRKY29 promoter as direct targets of RcARF8 binding (Fig. 7B). Moreover, compared with the control, RcARF8 knockdown resulted in a significantly larger lesion area and higher ion leakage rate (Fig. 8A–D), mimicking the results of RcWRKY29 silencing, resulting in increased flower susceptibility to B. cinerea. Moreover, RT-qPCR suggested that the silencing of RcARF8 caused decreased RcWRKY29 and RcERF98 expression (Fig. 8E and F). Silencing of RcARF8 resulted in decreased AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging and resulted in deeper DAB staining of the petals (Fig. 8G–I and Supplementary Fig. S4A). Furthermore, compared with the pSuper1300 control, the overexpression of RcARF8 in rose petals significantly promoted B. cinerea tolerance by regulating RcWRKY29 and RcERF98 expression (Fig. 8J–O). Besides, overexpression of RcARF8 resulted in enhanced AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging, as well as a reduction in DAB staining intensity.(Fig. 8P–R and Supplementary Fig. S4D). In addition, RcARF1 silencing did not affect petal resistance against B. cinerea (Supplementary Fig. S6A–D). We treated the samples with NPA, an auxin transport inhibitor, compared to the control (0 μM NPA), NPA(30 μM) treatment significantly reduced the expression levels of RcWRKY29, RcERF98, and RcARF8 (Supplementary Fig. S7). These results suggest that endogenous auxin signaling may be required for activating these TFs and the downstream regulatory module. To further investigate the potential interactions among these transcription factors, we performed BiFC and yeast two-hybrid assays. The results revealed no direct protein–protein interactions between any two of RcWRKY29, RcERF98, and RcARF8 (Supplementary Fig. S8). In summary, we hypothesized that auxin may function through RcARF8-RcWRKY29-RcERF98 module to regulate resistance to B. cinerea infection in rose petals by modulating the AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging (Fig. 9).

The proposed model: RcARF8- RcWRKY29 -RcERF98 module regulates the resistance to B. cinerea in rose petals. After B. cinerea infection in rose petals, endogenous auxin levels are elevated, which appears to induce RcARF8 expression. The increased RcARF8 then binds to and activates the promoter of RcWRKY29. In turn, RcWRKY29 upregulates transcript levels of RcERF98, leading to enhanced AsA levels and ROS scavenging, and thereby potentially increasing defense against B. cinerea in rose petals.

Discussion

In the flower industry, cut roses are highly susceptible to grey mold disease caused by B. cinerea during cultivation, leading to substantial economic losses. WRKY transcription factors play key roles in regulating plant growth and development, response to abiotic stresses, and defense against diseases. Previously, following the identification of 56 WRKY genes across the rose genome, transcriptomic data revealed a significant increase in the expression of this gene family in R. hybrida following infection by B. cinerea [16]. Studies have shown that the silencing of RcWRKY13 confers enhanced resistance against B. cinerea in rose petals, whereas the silencing of RcWRKY41 and RcWRKY30 increases their disease susceptibility [16, 17, 20]. Here, we found that RcWRKY29 expression was significantly induced by B. cinerea infection. Additionally, we found that there were no significant differences in RcWRKY13/RcWRKY30/RcWRKY41 expression in RcWRKY29-silenced or RcWRKY29-overexpression petals (Supplementary Fig. S9).

WRKY transcription factors regulate plant defense gene expression during pathogen invasion, either by directly activating or repressing downstream target genes or through indirect feedback mechanisms. The enhanced resistance provided by RcWRKY13 is regulated by its influence on the expression of RcCKX3 and RcABI4 [17]. RcWRKY30 strengthened defense against B. cinerea by promoting the deposition of lignin in rose petals [20]. In our study, RcWRKY29 was found to enhance resistance to B. cinerea infection in rose petals.

APETALA2-ETHYLENE RESPONSIVE FACTOR (AP2-ERF) family transcription factors are central regulators of immune responses across diverse crop species [41–43]. For example, AtERF9 confers resistance to B. cinerea [44]. In Arabidopsis, AtERF98 acts as a transcription regulator that enhances salt stress tolerance by activating AsA biosynthesis and improving the oxidative stress management [33]. Our previous study has revealed that RcERF98 regulates reactive ROS scavenging under salt stress conditions [34]. In this study, RcERF98 enhances resistance against B. cinerea in rose petals by regulating reactive AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging (Figs 4 and 5). WRKY and ERF transcription factors often form cascade regulatory relationships. For instance, in cotton, phosphorylated GhWRKY40a can directly activate GhERF1b, which subsequently induces the expression of defense genes and significantly enhances resistance to Verticillium wilt [45]. In our study, RcWRKY29 directly binds to and activates the promoter activity of RcERF98, thereby promoting the AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging, finally enhancing the resistance to B. cinerea infection in rose petals. Silencing or overexpressing RcERF98 did not significantly change the expression level of RcWRKY29 (Supplementary Fig. S10). These results suggested that ERF family members are involved in plant immune responses.

In this study, we observed that in rose petals, RcERF98 could regulate RcVTC1 expression (Fig. 5E and F) and enhance resistance to B. cinerea, yet the accumulation level of AsA showed relatively limited changes. This limited increase in AsA suggests that AsA-dependent ROS scavenging alone is unlikely to fully account for the observed resistance, and that other downstream mechanisms may also contribute. The relatively small AsA change may be attributed to the fact that AsA, as a metabolic end product, maintains homeostasis through a dynamic balance between multi-step enzymatic reactions in its biosynthetic pathways (e.g. the D-mannose/L-galactose pathway) and catabolic processes [32]. Because VTC1 acts upstream in the AsA biosynthetic pathway, transcriptional alterations in VTC1 are likely buffered by downstream metabolic flux, enzymatic feedback regulation, or cellular transport mechanisms, resulting in relatively subtle fluctuations in AsA levels. Furthermore, the disease resistance mechanism mediated by RcERF98 likely operates through multiple coordinated pathways: beyond its potential role in modulating AsA biosynthesis, RcERF98 may also enhance immunity by directly regulating other defense-related genes, including pathogenesis-related genes and key antioxidant enzyme genes [46], thereby establishing a multi-layered immune response network that collectively strengthens rose resistance to B cinerea.

Although plant defense hormones such as SA, JA, ABA, and ET play crucial roles in plant immune responses [1], auxin also plays an important role in the response to pathogen invasions [10, 11]. A previous study reported significant increases in IAA levels in tomato leaves during B. cinerea pathogenesis [11]. In our study, we also found that the IAA level increased in response to B. cinerea (Fig. 6). Like in kiwifruit, exogenous NAA significantly increased the resistance of rose petals to B. cinerea [10]. However, the specific role of auxin signaling in plant–pathogen interactions is incomplete. Researchers have revealed that the activation of auxin signaling can increase plant resistance to certain necrotrophic pathogens and plant viruses [47, 48]. Conversely, during interactions with biotrophic or hemibiotrophic pathogens, auxin signaling appears to predominantly facilitate disease progression [8, 9]. For example, OsARF17 increases disease resistance by interacting with different types of rice virus proteins [13]. In tomato, StARF16 functions as a negative regulator of defense against necrotrophic pathogens by directly associating with the StNPR1 promoter and suppressing its transcriptional activity [14]. In rose, RcARF8 regulates the RcWRKY29-RcERF98 module, thereby enhancing the AsA biosynthesis and ROS scavenging after B. cinerea infestation (Fig. 9).

We discovered that RcARF8 increases resistance to B. cinerea by controlling the RcWRKY29-RcERF98 pathway. This provides a new theoretical basis for hormone-mediated defence in roses and identifies a practical target for breeding. The module can be used as a molecular marker to identify favorable alleles (e.g. high-expression RcARF8 haplotypes) in germplasm, thereby accelerating the development of resistant varieties. As an alternative approach, CRISPR/Cas9 editing could be used to enhance RcARF8 expression, or transgenic overexpression of RcWRKY29 could be employed to directly improve resistance in susceptible cultivars. These findings offer clear strategies for translating our research into practical disease control applications.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and hormone treatments

The cut roses (R. hybrida) employed in the experiments were collected at stage 2 of development from the greenhouse facilities of the Flower Research Institute, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences [49]. We performed hormone treatments according to established methods [17]. Specifically, the rose petals of the outermost two whorls were punched into 10 mm discs, rinsed with ultrapure water, and transferred to 1% (w/v) agar plates containing 50 μM NAA, 30 μM NPA, 100 mM 6-BA, 100 mM ABA, and 50 μM MeJA, or in deionized water as a control for 24 h. The temperature was set at 25°C, with a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle and 60%–70% humidity. 16 discs were distributed per petri dish, which were subsequently used for B. cinerea inoculation.

B. cinerea inoculation

B. cinerea was inoculated onto rose petal discs according to previously described methods [50]. The CAU8324 isolate of B. cinerea was maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25°C for 3 weeks or more; conidia were harvested in sterile H2O, diluted to 1 × 105 conidia/ml in half-strength PDB, and used immediately for inoculation. Two microlitres of B. cinerea suspension or PDB (control) were applied to the center of each petal disc for fungal inoculation, and Petri dishes were sealed to maintain humidity. Phenotypes were evaluated by measuring the lesion area at 72 hpi using a minimum of fifty petal discs per treatment with ImageJ software; significance was evaluated by two-tailed Student’s t test.

VIGS and overexpression analyses

The VIGS experiment was performed according to previously described methods [11]. The specific fragments (250–350 bp) of RcARF1, RcARF8, RcWRKY29, and RcERF98 were amplified via PCR and subsequently cloned and inserted into the pTRV2 vector. The recombinant pTRV2 and pTRV1 vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Transformants were grown for 18 h at 28°C with shaking (200 rpm) in LB containing kanamycin (25 μg/ml) and rifampicin (50 μg/ml). After harvesting, the cells were resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 200 mM acetosyringone, and 10 mM MES, pH 5.6) with a final OD600 of approximately 1.0. An appropriate ratio of TRV1 to TRV2 agrobacterial suspensions was prepared, and rose petal discs were infiltrated under vacuum following the published procedure [17].

The CDS of RcARF8, RcWRKY29, and RcERF98 were PCR-amplified and ligated into pSuper1300, yielding C-terminal fusion constructs. Both the resulting plasmids and the empty backbone were electroporated into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, which was subsequently cultured in LB media supplemented with kanamycin (25 μg/ml) and rifampicin (50 μg/ml) at 28°C on a rotary shaker (200 rpm) for 18 hours. After harvesting, cells were pelleted and resuspended in infiltration medium to an optical density (OD600) of ~1.0. The bacterial suspensions were resuspended on a shaker for 1 hour (200 rpm, 28°C), and rose petal discs were vacuum infiltrated following the VIGS protocol. Six days after TRV and overexpression infection, rose petal discs were challenge-inoculated with B. cinerea; lesion diameters were measured at the designated time and analyzed by Student’s t -test.

Subcellular localization

The full-length CDSs of RcWRKY29, RcARF8, and RcERF98 were fused to GFP and inserted into the pSuper vector (pSuper::RcWRKY29, pSuper::RcARF8, and pSuper::RcERF98). Nuclei were visualized with DAPI counterstaining. Each recombinant construct was transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101, and the resulting suspension was syringe-infiltrated into leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. After three days, leaf samples were visualized with an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser-scanning microscope: GFP fluorescence was excited at 488 nm and DAPI was excited at 405 nm.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from rose petals following the protocol [51]. Complementary DNA was reverse-transcribed from 1 μg of RNA with TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme) according to the supplier’s manual. RT-qPCR was performed on a StepOnePlus instrument (Applied Biosystems) using 2 × M5 Hiper SYBR Premix EsTaq (with Tli RNase) (Mei5bio) under conditions specified by the manufacturer. Transcript levels were normalized against the reference gene RcUBI2. The primers are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

RNA sequencing

To investigate the transcriptional dynamics during fungal infection, rose petals were harvested at 0, 24, 36, and 48 hpi with B. cinerea. Three biological replicates were collected at each time point. All samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for RNA sequencing. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cDNA libraries were constructed and then subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were filtered with Trimmomatic, aligned to the R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’ reference genome (NCBI BioProject 8 255 808), and assembled with StringTie. Transcript abundance was estimated as FPKM and differential expression was declared for genes exhibiting |log2fold-change| ≥ 1 and FDR-adjusted q ≤ 0.05. Significant genes were functionally annotated and mapped to KEGG pathways. All treatments were represented by three independent biological replicates, and the cleaned reads have been deposited in NCBI BioProject PRJNA1202213.

Quantification of endogenous IAA in rose petals

Endogenous IAA was quantified in 100 mg fresh-weight petal tissue following the protocol [35]. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −70°C until analysis. IAA levels were measured by MetWare (Wuhan, China) via the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC–MS/MS platform (UPLC, ExionLC AD, MS, Applied Biosystems 6500; Triple Quadrupole, https://sciex.com.cn/). Each sample included three biological replicates.

DAP-seq of RcWRKY29

DAP-seq experiments were performed according to published methods [52]. Genomic DNA was extracted from rose petals and used to construct an affinity purification library with the MICH TLX DNA-Seq Kit (MICH, NGS0602). A Halo-tagged RcWRKY29 protein expression vector was generated and expressed via a wheat germ in vitro translation system. The Halo-tagged RcWRKY29 protein was incubated with the DNA library, and DNA fragments bound to RcWRKY29 were enriched via magnetic beads. Nonspecific chromatin was removed through multiple washing steps. The enriched DNA-protein complexes were purified, and the associated DNA fragments were sequenced via paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequencing reads were mapped to the reference genome (R. chinensis ‘Old Blush,’ GenBank ID 8255808) via BWA-MEM [53]. Peak calling and genome-wide binding site analysis were performed via MACS [54]. The DAP-seq reads were deposited in the NCBI BioProject database under accession number PRJNA1464212.

Y1H assay

Promoter fragments of RcWRKY29 and RcERF98 (2000 bp) were subsequently cloned and inserted into the pLacZ vector to generate pRcWRKY29-LacZ and pRcERF98-LacZ. The CDSs of RcARF1, RcWRKY29, and RcARF8 were subsequently cloned and inserted into the pJG4–5 vector to create pJG-RcARF1, pJG-RcWRKY29, and pJG-RcARF8. Each pJG effector construct was co-introduced with the corresponding pLacZ reporter into Saccharomyces cerevisiae EGY48 by the lithium-acetate method. Transformants were first selected on synthetic dextrose (SD) medium -Trp and -Ura, then replica-plated onto SD plates containing 80 μg/ml X-gal for β-galactosidase colorimetric detection [17].

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

For the evaluation of the transcriptional activity of RcARF1, RcARF8 and RcWRKY29, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. A reporter cassette carrying five tandem GAL4 sites upstream of the minimal CaMV 35S promoter was fused to firefly luciferase (LUC), while CaMV 35S-driven Renilla luciferase (REN) provided the internal reference. The open reading frames of RcARF1, RcARF8, and RcWRKY29 were cloned and inserted into the pGreenII62-SK vector, while the promoters of RcWRKY29 and RcERF98 were cloned and inserted into the pGreenII0800-LUC dual-reporter vector [55]. Empty vectors were used as controls. The effector and reporter constructs were mobilized into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 that additionally harbored the helper pSoup plasmid. Equal volumes of Agrobacterium cultures containing the constructs were mixed and pressure-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Three days after infiltration, leaf discs were lysed and LUC/REN ratios were determined with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a Microplate Reader; spatial luminescence was captured with a CCD camera. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

ChIP quantitative PCR

ChIP quantitative PCR (ChIP–qPCR) was executed according to the reported methods [35]. Petal tissues that had transiently expressed GFP-fusion constructs were fixed with 1% (w/v) formaldehyde, after which the cross-linking reaction was terminated by supplementing the medium with glycine to 0.125 M. The crosslinked chromatin was fragmented via sonication to produce DNA fragments ranging from 300 to 750 bp. GFP-Trap® A (1:1000 dilution; Chromotek, gtma-20) was used to immunoprecipitate the GFP-tagged protein–DNA complexes. After overnight incubation at 4°C with the antibody, magnetic beads were used to isolate the bound complexes. The beads were thoroughly washed, and the DNA was eluted. The crosslinks were then reversed by incubation at 65°C for 6 h, and the DNA was purified via a PCR purification kit. The resulting DNA was analyzed by qPCR with specific primers designed to amplify different regions. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

DAB staining

Using the 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining method for in situ H₂O₂ localization. Petals were immersed in DAB staining solution (pH 3.8), vacuum-infiltrated, and incubated in the dark for 10 hours. The stained petals were then destained in destaining solution (glycerol/acetic acid/ ethanol [1:1:3, v/v/v]) for 72 hours before photographic documentation of the staining patterns.

Ion leakage measurements

Uniform rose leaves were selected and thoroughly rinsed with distilled water. The leaves were placed into a 50 ml centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 40 ml of distilled water. A vacuum pump was employed to apply suction at 0.1 MPa for 5 minutes, after which the tube was shaken at 220 rpm for 1 hour and allowed to stand for an additional minute. The conductivity of the solution in the tube at this stage was measured using a conductivity meter and recorded as R1. Subsequently, the tube was boiled in water for 10 minutes, cooled to room temperature, and the conductivity of the solution was measured again, recorded as R2. The electrolyte leakage rate was calculated using the formula (R1/R2) × 100%.

H2O2 and AsA content measurements

Hydrogen peroxide was quantified with the Solarbio Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit. Petal tissue (0.1 g) was ground in 1 ml ice-cold extraction buffer, centrifuged at 8000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was retained. After addition of the kit reagents, the reaction mixture was incubated for 5 min and the absorbance was read at 415 nm.

For ascorbate (AsA), 0.1 g tissue was processed identically, and the supernatant was assayed with the Solarbio AsA detection kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol; absorbance was recorded at 420 nm.

Phylogenetic analysis

Protein sequences were first aligned with DNAMAN, after which MEGA-X was employed to infer evolutionary relationships; trees were generated under the maximum-likelihood criterion. This analysis incorporated 1000 bootstrap replicates and utilized the Jones–Taylor–Thornton substitution model.

Statistical analysis

Every assay was performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Pairwise comparisons were evaluated by two-tailed Student’s t-test, whereas multiple-group comparisons were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple-range test; differences were deemed significant at P < 0.05.
