Advances in Nutritional and Therapeutic Strategies for Type 2 Intestinal Failure: A Narrative Review
Authors: Yizhou A Xia, David S Rowlands, Gil Hardy, Rozanne Kruger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis narrative review aims to examine the nutritional consequences of type 2 intestinal failure (T2IF), assess current nutritional therapies, and explore advanced methodologies to address feeding challenges. Type 2 intestinal failure is characterized by severe nutrient malabsorption, micronutrient deficiencies, and disease-related malnutrition, compounded by complications such as microbiota disruption. Effective nutritional management is essential to improve clinical outcomes, yet an evidence gap hinders the establishment of standardized care guidelines. A literature search was conducted using major databases to identify clinical trials, cohort studies, and reviews on clinical nutrition and intestinal failure. Findings were analyzed narratively due to study design heterogeneity. Type 2 intestinal failure presents significant risks of disease-related malnutrition, inflammation, and muscle wasting due to impaired nutrient absorption and prolonged immobility. Optimizing protein and dietary fiber intakes is essential to preserve muscle mass and gut health. Parenteral nutrition (PN) remains essential but prolonged reliance may contribute to delayed intestinal adaptation and mucosal atrophy, highlighting the need for gut-focused strategies. Adjunct therapies, such as glucagon-like peptide 2 and somatostatin analogs, support intestinal adaptation but inadequately address protein, dietary fiber, and micronutrient requirements. Chyme reinfusion therapy, which returns nutrient-rich chyme to the gut, shows promise in reducing PN dependency, improving weight, and facilitating earlier oral intake, but it requires validation through robust comparative studies and clinical trials. Advanced methodologies, such as stable-isotope tracer techniques and positron emission topographic tracing, offer precise insights into tissue nutrient metabolism; however, cost and technical challenges are barriers. Tailored nutritional interventions are critical for mitigating disease-related malnutrition and optimizing outcomes in T2IF. Bridging evidence gaps through advanced techniques and standardized protocols will aid in refining therapeutic strategies, enhance patient quality of life, and inform comprehensive care guidelines.

INTRODUCTION

Intestinal failure (IF) is characterized by the gut’s inability to adequately absorb nutrients, necessitating the use of artificial nutrition administered intravenously, known as parenteral nutrition (PN), to sustain health.1 The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) defines IF as temporary or permanent, further classified into 3 types based on duration and severity.1 Type 1 IF is a short-term and self-limiting condition typically observed in the perioperative or critical care setting, requiring PN from a few days to a few weeks.1 Type 2 IF (T2IF) is a prolonged acute condition that frequently arises in septic, stressed, or metabolically unstable patients, such as those with an abdominal catastrophe resulting in an enterocutaneous fistula, complicated intra-abdominal infection, or acute mesenteric ischemia, which may last for several months.1 Type 3 IF is considered as a chronic, yet more stable condition where patients can be managed by home PN, and includes short bowel syndrome, chronic intestinal dysmotility, or other conditions that lead to permanent intestinal damage and malabsorption.1

Among these, T2IF presents unique challenges due to its association with the formation of enterocutaneous fistulae (ECF). A fistula is an abnormal connection or passageway that forms between 2 organs or structures that are not typically connected.2 The formation of a fistula begins with a disruption of the normal tissue layers that separate the organs involved3; this damage can be caused by injury, inflammation, or disease processes affecting the gastrointestinal tract (GIT).2,4 As a result, an abnormal opening or passage develops, allowing the contents of 1 organ to flow into another or to an external surface.3 Specifically, intestinal fistulae are abnormal links that form between the GIT and either the skin, known as an ECF, or the surrounding environment, known as an enteroatmospheric fistula (EAF).2–4

If an ECF or EAF develops, the primary pathology for T2IF is malabsorption due to bypassing or reduction in the absorptive mucosal surface area.5 Key symptoms include diarrhea or high fistula output, which may lead to dehydration, weight loss, and disease-related malnutrition.6,7 These symptoms are compounded by the significant losses of water and electrolytes, predisposing patients to dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and with complications such as acute kidney injury and impaired wound healing.6,7 Fistulae originating from the small intestine that have been present for several weeks often coincide with significant deficiencies in losses of zinc, copper, folate, and vitamin B12.4,8 These deficiencies not only contribute to malnutrition but also exacerbate inflammation, impair immunity, and delay recovery.4,8 Additionally, imbalances in both the microbial composition and diversity can lead to complications such as bacterial overgrowth, infections, and possible chronic inflammation.9–13 However, the body is not entirely defenseless against the disruptions caused by fistulae; in response, it initiates a process of intestinal adaption to optimize nutrient absorption and minimize complications.14,15 Adaptation comprises 3 phases: an initial hypersecretion, progressing to improved nutrient absorption, and culminating in the maintenance phase.14–17 During this process, structural changes occur, such as alterations in the intestinal mucosal architecture, including the increased expression of nutrient transporter proteins within the epithelial cells, and hormonal shifts that include variations in gastrointestinal hormone concentrations.14,16,17 These changes enhance the absorptive capacity of the intestine for both macronutrients and micronutrients.14,16,17 This complex and dynamic process varies depending on factors such as fistula location, size, duration, and patient health. Meeting daily minimal nutritional requirements, including adequate dietary fiber (DF) to support a healthy gut microbiome, plays a crucial role in determining the clinical outcomes during this adaptation period.16

Parenteral nutrition remains the cornerstone of nutritional management of T2IF, stabilizing nutritional status and supporting wound healing.4,16 However, PN is associated with risks, including infection and metabolic complications, and does not leverage the absorptive potential of the GIT.4,18–21 Addressing the nutritional consequences of T2IF requires a combination of emerging therapies that aim to optimize nutrient absorption, promote intestinal adaptation, and reduce dependence on PN.4,8,22 Adjunct therapies, such as glucagon-like peptide 2 (GLP-2) analogs and somatostatin analogs, enhance intestinal adaptation by promoting mucosal growth and reducing gastrointestinal secretions.23,24 Chyme reinfusion therapy (CRT) involves reintroducing nutrient-rich chyme from the proximal stoma or fistula into the distal bowel, utilizing the gut’s natural absorptive potential.25 However, the impacts of these therapies on resuming oral intake, enhancing nutrient absorption, and improving nutritional status remain unclear. Evaluating these therapies requires advanced methodologies. Stable-isotope tracer techniques, for instance, provide precise insights into nutrient metabolism and absorption, offering an objective basis for assessing the efficacy of these interventions.26,27 Further research is necessary to determine how these therapies can address the complex nutritional challenges in T2IF and improve patient outcomes.

This narrative review aims to examine the nutritional consequences of T2IF, assess current nutritional therapies, and explore advanced methodologies to address feeding challenges. By addressing these critical areas, we identify opportunities to optimize therapeutic approaches and improve patient outcomes.

METHODS

A literature search was conducted in May 2023 using PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane, and the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) to establish the framework for the literature review. The search was repeated in June 2024 to capture recent literature. The primary key words used were “Intestinal Failure”, “Enterocutaneous Fistula”, “Chyme Reinfusion”, “Dietary Practice”, and “Nutritional Support”. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the results. Inclusion criteria encompassed clinical trials, cohort studies, and reviews published in English that were directly relevant to the primary key words. The search period was limited to articles published from 1990 to June 2024 to capture both foundational and recent research developments. Additionally, key studies published before 1990 were identified through citation tracking of relevant articles to incorporate seminal research that remains pertinent to the field. Exclusion criteria included publications not written in English, case reports and case series, and those not directly related to the primary key words. A narrative synthesis approach was adopted due to the heterogeneity of the included studies. This approach allowed for a detailed exploration of the diverse findings across the varied designs and settings.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Nutritional Consequences of Type 2 Intestinal Failure

Protein Balance and Sarcopenia

Sarcopenia is a condition characterized by the progressive loss of muscle mass, strength, and function.28 While it is often associated with aging, it can also occur due to medical conditions such as malignancies, inflammatory bowel disease, and cirrhosis.28 Disease-related sarcopenia progresses more rapidly than age-related sarcopenia due to factors such as inflammation, disease-related malnutrition, or the catabolic effects of the underlying disease, often leading to increased healthcare costs, long-term care, and reduced longevity.28,29

Sarcopenia is a recognized phenomenon in patients with IF, with a recent study demonstrating a prevalence of 83%.30 The development of IF-related sarcopenia is often multifactorial, involving complex interactions among genetic factors, such as variations in alpha-actinin 3 and angiotensin-converting enzyme, which influence muscle fiber composition and metabolism31; hormonal imbalances, including reductions in growth hormone, insulin-like growth factor 1, testosterone, and estrogen concentrations32; and environmental and lifestyle factors, such as prolonged immobility during hospitalization, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and disrupted sleep patterns​.31,33 The catabolic stress associated with IF, compounded by endocrine responses that accelerate protein breakdown, underscores the importance of adequate protein intake.25 A critical factor is the negative net protein balance, where protein breakdown exceeds synthesis, leading to a net loss of protein that hinders the body's ability to efficiently maintain and repair skeletal and visceral muscle tissue and vital organs.34–36 To combat this, adequate stimulation of anabolic signaling pathways, such as the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 pathway,37 and the availability of substrate including amino acids from dietary protein intake and energy from exercise are vital.38 Net muscle protein gain is best stimulated by resistance exercise combined with the consumption of high-quality dietary protein.34 Intestinal failure exacerbates muscle loss through malabsorption and inflammation, with studies noting the interplay between inflammatory activity and sarcopenia.29,39,40 Skallerup et al41 observed that approximately one-third of patients with IF with sarcopenia had concurrent inflammatory activity, whereas only one-tenth of patients without sarcopenia had inflammation. While this finding highlights the interplay between inflammation and sarcopenia, it is limited by the study’s observational design and lack of longitudinal follow-up to establish causality. Additionally, inadequate intestinal nutrient absorption independently contributes to sarcopenia and subsequent disease-related malnutrition. Kopczynska et al7 found that low muscle mass is common in patients with T2IF, regardless of body composition or assessment modality. Half of their patients were assessed as having low muscle quality by computed tomography.7

Prolonged hospitalization and immobility further exacerbate muscle loss in patients with IF.18 A multicenter study across 17 European countries reported that the median hospital stay was 38 (21–61) days among 200 patients with acute IF.18 Immobility during hospitalization can accelerate muscle loss and weaken muscle strength.42,43 Wall and colleagues44 investigated the rate of muscle loss during 5 days of leg immobilization in healthy young individuals. They found that the cross-sectional area of the quadriceps decreased by 3.5% after 5 days and by 8.4% after 14 days of 1-legged knee immobilization in healthy young individuals.44 Leg strength also declined by 9% following 5 days of immobilization and further decreased by 22.9% after 14 days.44 While these findings highlight the rapid onset of sarcopenia among healthy individuals, it is evident that patients with IF with compromised health, higher risk of inflammation, and immobility might be at even greater risk.

Patients with ECF presenting with significant weight loss, reduction in strength, or other clinical indicators may experience acute muscle atrophy, which may lead to sarcopenia.7,8,45 Managing protein balance becomes an important strategy to attenuate the negative impacts on skeletal muscle mass, immune function, and metabolic health associated with T2IF. To manage protein balance effectively, it is critical to adapt the nutritional plan to account for both the total protein intake and quality, as well as the protein lost through the fistula output.24 Achieving stability or a positive protein balance is essential for promoting muscle protein synthesis and combating muscle wasting.46 Nitrogen balance, serving as a reliable barometer of anabolic status, helps to fine-tune protein intake and quality.26,27 However, since routine measurement of nitrogen balance is impractical in most clinical settings, alternative clinical assessments, including changes in muscle mass and fat distribution, functional strength testing, and clinical judgment based on overall nutrition-related physical findings, must be used to determine if a patient is experiencing a negative protein balance.47

The American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) and the Federación Latino Americana de Terapia Nutricional, Nutrición Clínica y Metabolismo (FELANPE) clinical guidelines recommend providing adult patients with ECF with a protein intake ranging from 1.5 to 2.0 g/kg per day, along with an energy intake that is tailored to the individual patient's energy requirements.25 Patients with EAF and high-output ECF may require even higher protein intakes, up to 2.5 g/kg per day.48 Although patients received 1500–2000 kcal per day had lower mortality and higher fistula closure rates compared with those receiving less than 1000 kcal per day,23,24 direct evidence linking increased protein intake to improved outcomes in T2IF is limited. Current recommendations are largely extrapolated from other clinical populations, where increased protein intake has been shown to support nitrogen balance, lean mass retention, and recovery.25,48 Preferred protein sources are those containing all essential amino acids in adequate quantities, also referred to as indispensable amino acids. The term “indispensable” is specifically used in the Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score, established by the Food and Agriculture Organization.49,50 While animal proteins generally meet these criteria, certain plant proteins like soy and pea—although lower in methionine—can also stimulate a high rate of tissue protein synthesis when consumed as part of a varied diet, or among those that favor plant proteins.49,50 Protein sources with a high Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score, or a blend of such sources, may provide optimal nutritional support to maintain lean body mass.49,50

Combining resistance exercise with high-quality dietary protein optimally stimulates muscle protein synthesis.35,36 Evidence supporting the benefits of resistance exercise in clinical populations is growing.51 A randomized controlled trial by Xiao et al52 reported that 4 weeks of resistance training was associated with improved appendicular skeletal muscle index, physical performance, and body composition in patients with IF-related sarcopenia. It is advisable to incorporate clinical exercise interventions alongside nutritional strategies for patients with IF. Such an integrated approach is more likely to mitigate the risks and impacts of sarcopenia under the physiological stresses of T2IF. Older adults with sarcopenia face additional challenges, as aging impairs muscle protein synthesis by up to approximately 30%.53 Houston et al54 demonstrated that older adults with the lowest quartile of protein intake (in negative nitrogen balance) had a higher risk of muscle wasting and functional decline. Thus, maintaining an adequate, and even above-average, dietary protein intake and quality — especially when combined with exercise — constitutes the ideal strategy to preserve muscle function and prevent sarcopenia in older adults.53,55

The consequences of sarcopenia are far-reaching, impacting functional capacity, decreased quality of life, and clinical outcomes.28,56–58 Current studies focus on descriptive data rather than on interventional outcomes, highlighting the need for future research on combined nutritional and exercise-based interventions to mitigate sarcopenia.

Dietary Fiber and Colonic Function

Dietary fiber, as defined by Food Standards Australia New Zealand, is the indigestible portion of plant foods, such as fruit, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes.59 It remains largely intact through the small intestine, reaching the colon where it promotes digestive health by increasing stool bulk and enhancing satiety, and is categorized based on its solubility, water-holding capacity, viscosity, and fermentability.60 Soluble fibers, such as pectin (in fruit, vegetables), beta-glucans (oats), and psyllium husk (seeds of Plantago ovata and Plantago psyllium) dissolve in water and form gels.60,61 These gels assist in regulating blood lipid and glucose concentrations, and support a healthy gastrointestinal environment by promoting beneficial microbial activity and reducing inflammation.62 In contrast, insoluble fibers such as cellulose (in whole grains, vegetables) and lignin (in flaxseeds, bran) add bulk to the diet and subsequently the stools, thereby improving bowel movements and health, preventing constipation.63,64

Some DFs exhibit fermentability, such as fructo-oligosaccharides found in onions and garlic and resistant starch found in undercooked potatoes and legumes, which serve as prebiotics, promoting beneficial gut microbiota in the colon fermenting the fiber, to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and gases. The SCFAs (eg, butyrate) are an energy source for colonic cells and they regulate bowel movements, promote beneficial gut bacteria colonization, and exhibit anti-inflammatory effects.65,66 These effects, however, vary by the type and amount of fiber consumed, as well as individual gut microbiota profiles.67 Despite extensive studies, definitive conclusions about the specific relationships between DF intake, SCFA production, and health remain elusive.67 This reflects the diversity in fiber types, doses, and intervention durations studied, as well as variations in analytical methods and individual differences in gut microbiota composition, which together makes it challenging to draw consistent findings.

In the case of IF, the ability to metabolize DF may become compromised due to the underlying condition or the altered anatomy of the digestive system.68 The impact of this compromised state extends beyond nutrient absorption. Significant small-bowel resection alters the intestinal environment in several ways: luminal pH level may increase, creating a less acidic environment; oxygen concentration may increase, leading to a less anaerobic gut; and enterohepatic circulation of bile acids may become disrupted, potentially resulting in a decrease in bile acid reabsorption and alterations in the composition of the bile acid pool.69–72 Such alterations can accelerate a decrease in bacterial diversity.69–72 Several studies have observed that patients with IF had low levels of butyrate-producing bacteria such as Clostridium leptum and Clostridium coccoides,10,69 or low levels of Firmicutes, known as major fiber fermenters.71,73 These bacteria play a critical role in colonic health by serving as the primary energy source for colonocytes, maintaining mucosal integrity, and regulating inflammation.71,73 An increased presence of Firmicutes is considered beneficial for gut health.71,73 Bacteroidetes, which prefers fiber-rich, whole foods, and Firmicutes, which thrive on refined carbohydrates and added sugar, both contribute to the diversity of the gut microbiome and its ability to metabolize a broad range of dietary substances.71,73,74 The ratio between Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (F/B ratio) is widely accepted to play an important role in maintaining normal intestinal homeostasis.75 It is important to maintain a healthy, balanced F/B ratio as it reflects a diverse gut microbiome capable of efficient DF fermentation and SCFA production, essential for maintaining gut barrier function.71,73 For infants, adults, and elderly individuals, typical F/B ratios are approximately 0.4, 10.9, and 0.6, respectively.76 However, patients with IF often exhibit altered microbial compositions, including reduced levels of butyrate-producing Firmicutes and an overall decrease in bacterial diversity, which can lead to dysbiosis.71,73,75 An increased F/B ratio is often associated with obesity, whereas a reduced F/B ratio is common in inflammatory bowel disease.75 While restoring microbial balance through DF supplementation appears promising, evidence specific to IF populations is limited.67,71

Current clinical practice often restricts DF in patients with IF receiving PN.1,5 This is done to minimize the risk of gastrointestinal complications, including bowel obstruction, diarrhea, and bloating, which result from impaired gut motility and absorption. However, fiber restriction may inadvertently exacerbate dysbiosis by promoting overgrowth of gram-negative Proteobacteria and their family Enterobacteriaceae (Firmicutes), particularly during periods of “gut starvation” when DF is withheld.69,71–73,77 This microbial imbalance underscores the potential importance of selectively reintroducing fibers to support gut health in patients with IF.77

Soluble fibers, such as psyllium husk or pectin, may provide therapeutic benefits for patients with a remaining colon by forming gels that bind water, reduce diarrhea, and enhance stool consistency.6,68 In contrast, insoluble fibers, which increase stool bulk by absorbing water, can expand the stool mass to accelerate intestinal transit time, and exacerbate both dehydration and electrolyte imbalances.6,68 Thus, insoluble fibers are generally contraindicated due to their rapid movement through the gut, which does not allow sufficient time for the absorption of water and essential minerals, potentially worsening these imbalances in patients with IF.6,68

The relationship between DF and the gut microbiota should be considered in IF management. While fiber supplementation has been shown to increase SCFA production and microbial diversity in healthy populations, its applicability in IF remains uncertain.67,71 Dysbiosis in IF, characterized by reduced microbial diversity and a lower abundance of Firmicutes, impacts the F/B ratio.75 The reduced concentration of butyrate-producing Firmicutes specifically complicates fiber metabolism by disrupting fermentation and SCFA production, further contributing to microbial imbalance.69–71 Restoring microbial diversity and SCFA production through DF supplementation is theoretically beneficial but requires further investigation to determine the optimal fiber types, doses, and timing of reintroduction in the diet of patients with IF.71,77

Despite its theoretical benefits, the role of DF in IF management remains constrained by inconsistent evidence, with most research focusing on healthy populations or mild gut dysfunction, limiting its relevance to IF-specific challenges.67,71 Future research should evaluate the safety and efficacy of soluble fibers like psyllium to manage diarrhea and support nutrient absorption, and assess how DF influences microbial diversity, SCFA production, and gut barrier function. Results should aid in the development of tailored fiber recommendations based on patient-specific factors such as residual gut anatomy, microbial profiles, and tolerance. Any resultant dietary guidelines should help prevent complications and support intestinal recovery during the rehabilitation of these patients.

Fluid, Electrolyte Imbalances, and Micronutrient Deficiencies

Patients with T2IF face significant risk of dehydration and developing electrolyte imbalances due to excessive losses through high-output stomas or fistulae.6,78 Impaired absorptive capacity exacerbates these deficits, making it difficult for patients to maintain hydration and electrolyte balance through oral intake alone.18,79 Losses of sodium, potassium, magnesium, and bicarbonate can lead to metabolic acidosis, neuromuscular disturbances, and cardiovascular instability.6,78 Renal impairment is frequently observed in T2IF, with nearly 20% of ileostomy patients developing acute kidney injury due to high stoma output and dehydration.80–82 Chronic dehydration, kidney stones, and reduced intestinal length are additional independent risk factors, particularly among patients with short bowel syndrome.6,83

The damage to the intestinal lining inherent in acute IF reduces the gut’s ability to absorb nutrients effectively, especially those that are water-soluble, particularly impacting the uptake of vitamins and minerals.1,4,8,84–86 The initial phase of nutrient malabsorption in acute IF can be directly attributed to gut inflammation that damages the intestinal gut lining, thereby disrupting the normal absorption processes of essential micronutrients such as vitamin B12, iron, zinc, magnesium, and selenium, leading to deficiencies.41,79,85 Symptoms like nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and anorexia further limit oral intake, exacerbating the risk of micronutrient deficiencies.18,79 In the United Kingdom, for instance, 80% of 25 hospitalized patients with cancer who developed IF and required PN were identified as being at risk of malnutrition, highlighting the significant impact on micronutrient status due to a reduced appetite and poor oral intake.79 Beyond these factors, acute IF triggers a systemic inflammatory response that complicates micro-nutritional management further.87 This systemic response involves the release of proinflammatory mediators and leukocyte activation, which not only exacerbates intestinal barrier function but also alters the body’s overall micronutrient metabolism and demand.87–89 Micronutrients are diverted from their usual roles, such as growth, repair, and maintenance, to support the immune response and manage inflammation.88,89 For example, iron may be sequestered in the liver and spleen, away from pathogens and the general circulation, as part of the body’s defense mechanism.90 Similarly, zinc might be redirected to support the activity of immune cells, wound healing, and protein synthesis.91 This redistribution and increased nutrient requirements during systemic inflammation might lead to altered serum concentrations and decreased total body reserves of micronutrients, particularly affecting water-soluble vitamins due to their lack of significant storage capacity in the body.92,93 Research, such as the findings by Du et al94 on vitamin B6, highlights the potential immunoregulatory effects of vitamins. However, gaps remain in our understanding of the full spectrum of micronutrient needs during acute IF due to limited human clinical trials.94–97

Water-soluble vitamins such as vitamins C and B-complex are usually absorbed in the proximal small bowel. Under certain conditions like extensive surgical resections, diseases affecting the small intestine, or altered enterohepatic circulation, absorption can still occur.98 Neurological disorders or dermatitis/sores from lack of B vitamins and impaired wound healing and immune function from insufficient vitamin C are potential issues.93 In cases where more than 50–60 cm of the distal ileum— the primary site for vitamin B12 absorption — has been resected, a lifelong vitamin B12 supplementation administered intravenously is usually necessary.1,98 Effective absorption of vitamin B12 also requires the presence of intrinsic factor, produced by the stomach, and is influenced by adequate concentrations of folate, protein transporters, and hydrochloric acid.99 The interdependency between vitamin B12 and folate particularly highlights the importance of ensuring balanced concentrations of both vitamins to support effective absorption and utilization.99

Fat-soluble vitamins (eg, vitamins A, D, E, and K) and essential fatty acids are also commonly deficient in patients with IF due to impaired absorption of fat.1,98 Fat malabsorption further affects the absorption of bile salts, which are essential for fat digestion.100 Moreover, vitamins A, D, and E are stored in significant amounts, primarily in the liver and adipose tissues, allowing the body to draw on these reserves during periods of inadequate dietary intake.100,101 Therefore, deficiencies of these vitamins may develop over a longer period compared with water-soluble vitamins, which are not stored and thus excreted more rapidly if consumed in excess.93,101 Even with inherent storage capabilities, prolonged malabsorption due to IF can deplete these reserves, leading to deficiencies that may impact various bodily functions.101 For instance, vitamin A deficiency can affect vision and immune function, vitamin D deficiency can impair bone health, vitamin E deficiency can lead to neurological problems, and vitamin K deficiency can disrupt normal blood-clotting processes.101

Trace element deficiencies, especially zinc, are notable in patients with IF, with increased stool or fistula output.1,102 Intestinal dysmotility or surgical interventions in acute IF can result in rapid transit of food through the intestines, resulting in shortened transit time that limits the exposure of the intestinal lining to micronutrients, reducing absorption, and increasing the risk of deficiencies.16,98 Excessive losses of zinc and selenium are particularly prevalent in patients who develop enterocutaneous fistulae.92,103 Zinc, copper, and selenium supplementation is necessary to correct deficiencies and support normal physiological functions.102 Iron, however, is absorbed in the duodenum, and supplementation is typically unnecessary unless specific circumstances, such as reduced oral intake (requiring PN) or chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, are present.1,102

Addressing these deficiencies requires a tailored approach to supplementation. Serum concentrations alone may not accurately reflect true body stores, particularly under the altered metabolic demands imposed by the systemic inflammation of acute IF.104,105 Research into biomarkers that can more accurately reflect micronutrient status and metabolic demands, as well as the development of individualized supplementation protocols, is essential to optimize clinical outcomes. Controlled trials are necessary to standardize approaches for micronutrient management in T2IF, as noted in the latest ESPEN guidelines by Berger et al.106

Nutritional Strategies in Managing Type 2 Intestinal Failure

In managing IF, particularly for patients with T2IF and ECF, it is critical to address the unique nutritional challenges of malabsorption, sarcopenia, microbiome disruption, and micronutrient deficiencies to enhance patient outcomes and support recovery.5 Ensuring optimal nutrition is paramount, as adequate nutritional therapy has been closely linked to reduced mortality and improved spontaneous closure of fistulae.4,8,22 Traditionally, early oral nutrition or enteral nutrition (EN) is preferred for its numerous benefits, including the maintenance of gut integrity and immune function.19,107 However, in cases of T2IF with ECF, early oral nutrition or EN may not be viable due to the risk of exacerbating fistula output or due to the inability to absorb nutrients effectively through the compromised GIT.4,107,108

Traditionally PN has been used as the primary feeding method beneficial for patients with ECF with T2IF, when enteral feeding is not feasible.1 In recent years, adjunct therapies, such as GLP-2 analogs and somatostatin analogs, have also shown potential to complement PN by promoting intestinal adaptation and managing gastrointestinal secretions, respectively.109–111 Additionally, advancements in CRT technology have made it a practical and effective alternative to support these patients to achieve PN independence and earlier return to oral eating.6,84,112–115 The potential of these alternative feeding strategies to meet the patient's nutritional demands and to support recovery is reviewed in depth below.4,107,108

Parenteral Nutrition

Benefits of parenteral nutrition in type 2 intestinal failure

Parenteral nutrition is a fundamental therapy in the management T2IF, delivering essential nutrients and fluid intravenously when the GIT is unable to meet nutritional demands and/or the oral route is not available.84 Its historical significance dates back to 1665 with Sir Christopher Wren’s groundbreaking experiments116,117 and was later refined by Dr Stanley J. Dudrick and colleagues in the United States, demonstrating that intravenous administration of nutrients could sustain growth comparable to oral nutrition.118

In acute settings, PN stabilizes nutritional status, addresses fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and provides the GIT with time to heal.4,119 Recent evidence underscores the importance of timing in PN initiation. A study by Gao et al120 highlighted that early supplemental PN, initiated on day 3 after major abdominal surgery, significantly reduced nosocomial infections (8.7% vs 18.4%) compared with late supplemental PN, initiated on day 8. Further, early supplemental PN was also associated with improved nutritional markers, such as serum albumin and prealbumin concentrations at discharge, and reduced therapeutic antibiotic requirements.120 These findings emphasize that timely PN can bridge nutritional gaps, mitigate infectious risks, and improve clinical outcomes when EN alone is insufficient.120

Parenteral nutrition plays a critical role in preventing further nutritional decline in patients with ECF by stabilizing their nutritional status and allowing the fistulae time to heal.4,119 It not only helps to reverse the catabolic state often seen in these patients but also significantly reduces gastrointestinal secretions, which is essential in managing high-output fistulae.4,119

Challenges of parenteral nutrition in type 2 intestinal failure

Prolonged PN, while necessary, is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, with rates exceeding 20%, primarily due to sepsis, catheter-related infections, and intra-abdominal abscesses.18,20,21 A multicenter study by Reintam Blaser and colleagues,18 on behalf of the ESPEN Acute IF Study Group, found that 253 out of 338 (74.9%) patients with acute IF relied entirely on PN, while the remaining 25.1% received supplemental PN, which accounted for a median energy intake of 60% (10%–90%). Although PN effectively replaces fluid and nutrient losses, its use is also associated with metabolic complications.18,20,21

The ESPEN Acute IF Study Group discovered that receiving PN for more than 42 days due to acute IF (type 1 and 2) was the optimal threshold to predict hospital mortality within 90 days. Despite the benefits of PN, there is a substantial gap in evidence-based knowledge, particularly regarding the optimal supplementation dosages and the interplay between micronutrients, protein metabolism, and overall metabolic health during IF. Long-term reliance can delay intestinal adaptation, promote mucosal atrophy, and impair gut immunity.19,107

Growth Hormone

Growth hormone has been investigated for its anabolic effects on intestinal adaptation by stimulating epithelial proliferation and enhancing nutrient absorption. However, clinical trials have demonstrated only transient improvements in energy absorption when growth hormone is combined with glutamine and a high-carbohydrate diet​.109,110 These benefits were not sustained, and adverse effects such as fluid retention and hyperglycemia were frequently reported.109,121,122 Moreover, high costs and inconsistent replication of results raise questions about its practical utility.109,122 While growth hormone therapy is not recommended in current guidelines due to insufficient long-term efficacy data, its exclusion is based on small-scale studies with variable quality​.122

Growth Factors

Preclinical studies on growth factors, such as epidermal growth factor and insulin-like growth factor 1, highlight their potential in promoting mucosal healing and intestinal adaptation​.4,111,122 However, translation to clinical practice has been hindered by limited research — much of which is confined to pilot trials that lack standardization in dosing and rely on surrogate endpoints, such as histological changes, rather than clinically meaningful outcomes.4,109,122 Larger, more robust clinical trials are needed to establish their role in T2IF​.110,111,122,123

Somatostatin Analogs

Somatostatin analogs, including octreotide, are primarily used to manage high-output stomas or enterocutaneous fistulae by reducing gastrointestinal secretions and mitigating fluid losses.​109,124,125 Although effective in reducing output and dehydration, their impact on overall nutritional status and PN dependency is limited.4,122 Adverse effects, such as gallstone formation and glucose intolerance, necessitate cautious long-term use.109,122,123 Most studies supporting their use are observational, with limited data from randomized controlled trials.109,122

Glucagon-Like Peptide-2 Analogs

Glucagon-like peptide-2 analogs, particularly teduglutide, represent a significant advancement in managing short bowel syndrome–associated IF by promoting intestinal adaptation and reducing PN requirements.109,110 Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews have demonstrated reductions in PN volume by approximately 5 L weekly and increases in plasma citrulline concentrations.123,125,126 These benefits are achieved primarily through the stimulation of upper gut mucosal growth, making GLP-2 analogs particularly suitable for patients with residual small bowel length.78,127 While GLP-2 analogs have shown efficacy in short bowel syndrome–associated IF, their benefits in T2IF cases involving ECF remain less clear and require further investigation.78,127 This limitation is likely due to the distinct pathophysiological challenges posed by ECF, including high-output nutrient losses and variability in residual bowel anatomy, which may affect the therapeutic impact of GLP-2 analogs.78,127 Furthermore, patient responses can vary widely, influenced by factors such as residual intestinal anatomy, underlying etiology, and baseline PN dependency.123 Emerging analogs such as glepaglutide and apraglutide offer potential advantages, including less-frequent dosing and comparable efficacy, but their clinical utility remains under investigation​.123 While GLP-2 analogs are generally well tolerated, adverse events such as catheter-related bloodstream infections, gastrointestinal polyps, and leg edema warrant vigilance​.123 High costs further limit accessibility and necessitate the identification of predictive markers to optimize patient selection​.109,123,126 Despite these limitations, the stronger evidence base and long-term potential benefits position GLP-2 analogs as a pivotal therapy for T2IF.

Chyme Reinfusion Therapy

Chyme reinfusion therapy offers a direct approach to enhance intestinal function by leveraging the GIT’s absorptive capacity.112,113,115,128 As an innovative form of EN, CRT involves reintroducing nutrient-rich chyme collected from a stoma or fistula bag into the distal limb of the intestine.112,113,115,128 This technique potentially addresses some of the limitations of adjunct therapies, reducing dependency on PN while maximizing the use of the GIT's natural functions through a mechanically assisted process (Figure 1).107,112,113,115,128,129 The history of CRT dates back to the mid-20th century, when clinicians in France began exploring alternative methods to support patients with severe IF.130 Although chyme has traditionally been discarded as waste, it contains valuable digestive secretions, enzymes, nutrients, and electrolytes that are vital to the body and gastrointestinal health.114 Recognizing the importance of preserving the GIT's functional integrity, clinicians understood that the tract plays a crucial role in digestion, absorption, and overall gut health.131 This realization led to research exploring ways to reintroduce lost chyme back into the intestine, not only to facilitate nutrient absorption but also to maintain the structural and functional integrity of the gut.130

Schematic Representation of Chyme Reinfusion Therapy in Patients With Type 2 Intestinal Failure. (A) In the conventional approach, chyme is collected in the stoma bag and discarded, resulting in the loss of fluids, electrolytes, and nutrients. (B) Chyme reinfusion therapy redirects nutrient-rich chyme from the stoma bag into the distal small intestine using a mechanically assisted pump, supporting fluid and nutrient reabsorption and promoting intestinal adaptation

The evolution of chyme reinfusion devices has advanced from the dual-pump system developed by Dr Étienne Lévy and colleagues,132,133 in France in 1972, which required separate pumps for aspiration and infusion, to a modern, user-friendly single pump, pioneered by Professors Ian Bissett and Greg O’Grady in New Zealand.115 This new system, featuring a small-bore feeding tube and a portable automated single pump, combines both functions into 1 device, offering a practical, nonintrusive solution for long-term nutritional support and permitting at-home use.115,128 The principles outlined by ESPEN and ASPEN highlight the importance of physiological approaches, such as maximizing the use of the GIT for nutrient absorption, which aligns with the potential benefits of CRT in select patients with T2IF.25,84

Benefits of chyme reinfusion therapy in type 2 intestinal failure

Improved nutritional and hydration status. CRT enhances nutritional outcomes by promoting nutrient and fluid absorption. A systematic review by Bhat et al134 reported an increase in mean weight, body mass index, and the Nutritional Risk Index among patients receiving CRT, reflecting improved macronutrient and fluid absorption. Two studies included in the review demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in malnutrition risk, with the largest study involving 212 patients reporting a mean ± SD increase in Nutrition Risk Index of 10.9 ± 9.5 (P < .001).112,135 Picot et al113 subsequently found a mean ± SD percentage weight gain of 3.5% ± 8.4% (P < .0001) among 306 patients with temporary double enterostomies or ECF who underwent CRT treatment between 2000 and 2018. It is believed that CRT allows for the reabsorption of nutrients and fluids that would otherwise be lost through ostomy effluent or drainage.113 By reintroducing chyme into the digestive tract, it promotes enhanced nutrient absorption, leading to improved nutritional status and decreased reliance on PN.134 Picot et al113 found that CRT reduced dependence on PN by 90% within 1 week of initiation, emphasizing its role in mitigating malnutrition and hydration challenges in patients with double enterostomies or ECF​. When CRT is applied through a proximal jejunostomy, it has also shown effectiveness in reducing PN requirements upon discharge, despite small sample sizes in the studies.136,137 Studies have also highlighted CRT’s capacity to stabilize electrolyte concentrations by reinfusing sodium, potassium, and bicarbonate from chyme, reducing the risks of dehydration, metabolic acidosis, and renal complications​.113,138

Restored intestinal function. CRT facilitates bowel adaptation through continuous enteral stimulation, promoting natural peristaltic movement and the secretion of digestive enzymes, thereby maintaining intestinal motility and absorptive capacity.133 Lévy et al133 documented a 30% reduction in proximal fistulae output compared with an electrolyte solution, while Picot et al113 reported a significant decrease in the mean ± SD daily losses of intestinal contents, 2100 ± 960 mL per day. Moreover, chyme reinfusion aided in the adaptive response of the bowel, resulting in improved nutrient absorption.112,135 Two other studies conducted by Picot et al112,135 provided additional evidence for this enhancement of small bowel absorptive capacity, as indicated by a normalized plasma citrulline concentration (mean ± SD: 17.6 ± 8.4 vs 30.3 ± 11.8 µmol/L; P < .001) following CRT. Plasma citrulline produced by the enterocytes is an amino acid not incorporated into proteins and has been suggested as a marker of remnant enterocyte mass in patients with a short bowel.139 Hence, plasma citrulline has been proposed as a biomarker of intestinal absorptive function.140 A mean ± SD increase in nitrogen absorption by 32% ± 20% and lipid absorption by 43% ± 30% was also observed.113 Intestinal nitrogen and lipid absorption coefficients indicate the efficiency to which the body digests and absorbs protein and fat from the intestine.113 These coefficients represented the proportion of ingested protein and fat that is absorbed and utilized by the body, as opposed to being lost through intestinal processes.113 Interestingly, the increase in citrulline concentrations showed a correlation with the distal length of the bowel.113 However, variability in response necessitates tailored monitoring, as improvements in citrulline concentrations do not consistently correlate with reduced intestinal losses or enhanced absorption.113

Challenges of chyme reinfusion therapy in type 2 intestinal failure

Chyme reinfusion therapy holds significant potential in managing T2IF but presents challenges that can limit its broader application. Patient eligibility for CRT is influenced by a range of anatomical and medical factors.113,115 Contraindications, such as distal bowel obstructions, significant inflammation, or infections, may exclude some patients from benefiting​.113 The therapy’s efficacy is also highly dependent on the functional capacity of the downstream bowel and the length of the proximal small intestine.135​ For patients with particularly short residual bowel lengths, nutrient absorption may be insufficient, limiting the potential benefits of the therapy.135,136​ Despite promising improvements in nutritional markers, evidence regarding its broader outcomes, including micronutrient adequacy and overall quality of life, requires more data.113,115,135,136,138​ Ongoing monitoring is vital to mitigate risks, such as electrolyte imbalances, bacterial overgrowth, and device malfunctions.​113,115 Long-term data on CRT’s impact on liver health, intestinal adaptation, and metabolic stability remain scarce.113,138

Comparative Evaluation of Therapeutic Strategies

A summary of evidence-based recommendations, including therapeutic strategies and identified benefits and risks, is presented in Table 1.1,4,6,18,20,21,24–26,47,48,67,68,71,78,84,87,102,106,109–113,115,119,121–127,135,137,138,141 Parenteral nutrition is the fundamental nutritional management of T2IF, particularly for patients with high-output fistulae, due to its ability to bypass the compromised gut entirely. However, its risks highlight the need for alternatives. Adjunct therapies provide additional support by enhancing intestinal adaptation but are limited by high costs and variable efficacy across patient populations. Chyme reinfusion therapy offers a compelling approach by promoting the physiological use of the GIT. In clinical studies, CRT has been associated with reduced PN reliance and restoration of intestinal function. However, its adoption is limited by access to resources and a lack of long-term evidence on its impact compared with PN. Both adjunct therapies and CRT rely on surrogate endpoints rather than patient-centered outcomes, such as survival or sustained PN independence. Current evidence is limited by single-center studies and a lack of standardization, making generalization difficult. Future research should prioritize multicenter trials and direct comparisons to establish evidence-based guidelines for optimal use in T2IF.

Dietary Practices in Type 2 Intestinal Failure

The dietary management of patients with T2IF during CRT is especially noteworthy, as it allows for the integration of oral nutrition or EN alongside chyme reinfusion.112,113,115 This marks a departure from the traditional reliance on PN, where oral intake is typically restricted to mitigate gastrointestinal complications.4,119 Chyme reinfusion therapy utilizes the residual absorptive capacity of the GIT,112,113,115 which presents a unique opportunity to establish dietary strategies that optimize nutrient absorption and support intestinal adaptation.

Adjunct therapies, particularly GPL-2 analogs, could also play an indirect role in supporting dietary strategies by promoting intestinal adaptation.109,110 However direct evidence linking adjunct therapies and enhanced nutrient absorption, fiber tolerance, or metabolism remains limited, and their integration into CRT protocols is underexplored. Moreover, the broader impact of CRT on overall nutritional status beyond weight gain remains insufficiently investigated. Although Bhat et al134 highlighted improvements in weight gain as a surrogate marker of nutritional status, these findings provide limited insight into broader outcomes, such as protein balance, body-composition changes, micronutrient status, and colonic health.

The dietary practices during CRT, as outlined in Table 2,113,115,133,135–138,141,142 reveal considerable variation across studies and geographic regions, reflecting the lack of consensus in clinical protocols. Early studies by Lévy et al133 focused mainly on fluid management, as they prioritized isotonic dialysate solutions paired with exclusive PN without specifying nutrition goals. In contrast, more recent studies, such as those by Liu et al136 in Taiwan and Sharma et al115 in New Zealand adopted structured nutritional protocols integrating oral nutrition, EN, and PN. Reported nutrition targets ranged from 25–35 kcal/kg per day and 1.5–2.0 g/kg per day of protein,136,138,141,143 with the protein target aligning with the ASPEN-FELANPE clinical guidelines.25 However, the effectiveness of these nutrition targets remains uncertain, as actual oral and EN intakes during CRT were either not reported or not adequately evaluated in relation to comprehensive nutritional outcomes.

The choice of diet varied widely, further highlighting inconsistencies in practice. While some studies recommended puréed food to prevent tube clogging,113,135 others preferred low-residue diets to enhance patient comfort and compliance.115,136 The use of exclusive EN was more prominent in later studies, such as those by Wu et al141 in China, where succus entericus (CRT) and polymeric enteral feeds were used, highlighting a trend towards utilizing the GIT more effectively. However, despite these advancements, some studies reported unmeasured or unreported oral and enteral intake,115,136–138,141 suggesting a need for further standardization and documentation of dietary practices during CRT.

A critical limitation across many studies is the frequent lack of detailed documentation of oral and enteral intake. Studies by Liu et al136 and Sharma et al115 reported unmeasured or unmonitored dietary intake, which raises concerns about the reliability of conclusions regarding CRT’s nutritional impact​. This gap not only affects the comparability of findings but also hampers the development of evidence-based dietary practices for CRT patients. Additionally, the potential benefits of incorporating DF during CRT remain underexplored. Soluble fiber may improve stool consistency and SCFA production, potentially benefiting colonic health and microbial diversity. However, these factors have been insufficiently addressed in existing studies, leaving a significant gap in understanding the broader metabolic and functional outcomes of CRT.115,136–138,141

While progress has been made in defining dietary strategies for CRT, substantial variability in practices and limited published data on detailed dietary approaches leave questions unanswered. Standardized dietary protocols are urgently needed to optimize CRT’s potential in managing the complex nutritional challenges associated with T2IF.

Advanced Techniques for Investigating Nutrient Dynamics in Intestinal Failure

To explore the impact of CRT and adjunct therapies on nutrient metabolism, advanced methodologies that enable precise tracking and analysis of nutrient absorption and metabolism within the body following food intake are essential. Stable-isotope tracer techniques, for example, are particularly valuable for tracking nutrient pathways and metabolism.144–146 Additionally, advanced imaging techniques like positron emission tomography scanning and magnetic resonance imaging can provide in-depth insights into nutrient flux and gut health, although their use is often limited by the expertise and highly specialized equipment required.45,147

The advancement in stable-isotope methodology has introduced novel approaches to quantitatively and qualitatively follow nutrient absorption and metabolism in patients with malabsorptive disorders, aiding in the development of optimal nutrition interventions.146,148,149 An element, such as carbon, nitrogen, or hydrogen, within the biomolecule of interest (eg, phenylalanine) is labeled with 1 or more stable isotopes. The rate of flux through the systems is determined from the analysis of the tracer-to-tracee ratio within body fluids and tissues by way of mass spectrometry (MS).150 These tracer techniques involve administering a trace amount of a labeled compound, either orally or intravenously, to track its metabolism or the subsequent downstream pathways it influences.151 For instance, Engelen and colleagues146 used stable-isotope–labeled spirulina protein enriched with 15N to trace protein digestion, absorption, and utilization in patients with cystic fibrosis, who are prone to protein malabsorption and muscle wasting, demonstrating the efficacy of pancreatic enzymes in improving protein digestibility. Similarly, Rosado et al152 utilized stable isotopes to evaluate zinc absorption in populations consuming biofortified wheat, specifically in groups with high grain and phytate consumption, providing insights into how biofortification can address zinc deficiency. Despite these advances, challenges remain, including the invasiveness of repeated blood sampling, the need for sophisticated instruments like liquid or gas chromatography–MS, and high costs, which limits their widespread clinical adoption.146,153

A selective overview of stable-isotope tracer methodologies applied in various clinical and community settings is provided in Table 3.146,148,149,152–156 These studies used a range of isotopes, including 13C-labeled compounds and 15N-labeled spirulina to trace protein absorption146,148 and protein synthesis, breakdown, net balance148,154 and micronutrient bioavailability152,153,157 in different populations. For instance, stable isotopes have been used to study riboflavin absorption from milk and spinach, revealing no significant differences in bioavailability but highlighting variations in first-pass metabolism and the influence of food matrices.153 Additionally, studies like those by van der Beek et al155 and Verbeke et al,156 have shown the utility of isotope techniques in assessing SCFA production and gut microbial activity, further emphasizing their potential in clinical nutrition. These methodologies also vary in their level of invasiveness. Oral administration of isotopes, as reported by the aforementioned investigators,155,156 offers a noninvasive approach to measure nutrient absorption and fermentation products such as SCFAs using both breath and urine samples. In contrast, studies involving intravenous administration and tissue biopsies, although minimally invasive, required more complex and resource-intensive protocols to track protein absorption, amino acid metabolism, and whole-body and tissue protein synthesis and breakdown.36,158

While these methodologies provide insights, their implementation among patients with nutrient absorption and metabolism impairments is predominantly constrained by logistical and cost barriers.144,159 For example, isotope production requires specialized facilities, although commercial supply is readily available, and infusion preparation must follow aseptic protocols to ensure sterility and dosing accuracy.144,146,149,152,159 Data interpretation also demands a deep understanding of the isotope kinetics and the metabolic biochemistry and physiology, with the additional shifts and variation experienced within the clinical setting.144,148,149,159 A substantial financial investment is needed for consumables and staffing, isotope production, safe sample preparation, and specialized equipment for trials, sample analysis, and data interpretation.144,159 Furthermore, MS-based sample analysis demands skilled technicians and advanced equipment for detecting and quantifying isotopic enrichment.144,159

Stable-isotope methodologies may prove to be highly valuable tools for research in CRT or adjunct therapies, as they enable researchers and clinicians to measure nutrient absorption rates and evaluate the efficacy of interventions aimed at optimizing gut function.146,152 In T2IF, these methods allow tracking the movement of specific nutrients through the GIT, identifying potential malabsorption, quantifying therapy impacts, and adapting interventions to meet patients’ metabolic needs, therefore improving nutritional status and outcomes.

Moreover, exploring additional innovative techniques could further enhance our understanding of nutrient absorption.160,161 Although real-time positron emission tomography scanning in combination with oral tracers has been used in studying pharmacokinetics, it offers potential for precisely tracking how nutrients are absorbed in specific regions of the GIT and other tissues, helping to identify potential areas of dysfunction or malabsorption.147 This could be especially valuable in understanding conditions where absorption varies significantly throughout the gut, such as in patients with segmental intestinal resections or fistulae. Magnetic resonance imaging could provide detailed visualizations of gut motility and structural integrity at the organ level, offering insights into how these factors influence the absorption of nutrients.45,161,162 By combining advanced imaging techniques with stable-isotope tracing, clinicians could gain a more precise view of nutrient absorption and metabolism in patients with T2IF. This integrated approach would not only address the limitations of current practices but also provide a deeper understanding of the complexities involved in managing these patients, potentially leading to more tailored and effective nutritional interventions.

CONCLUSION

The current narrative review has revealed the nutritional consequences of stoma- and fistula-related T2IF and the need for targeted nutritional therapy to optimize patient outcomes. Nutritional therapy should account for adaptation within the GIT to T2IF, consider the likelihood or quantify macro- and micronutrient deficiencies, and include measurements of outcomes to determine the efficacy of nutrition support strategies. However, a significant gap remains in our understanding of how effectively adjunct approaches like GLP-2 and/or its analogs and CRT enhance nutrient absorption and colonic function in the unused distal intestine after cessation of PN. Filling this information gap may assist in improving clinical nutrition strategies for patients who fail to meet their nutritional needs when discontinuing PN, thereby potentially attenuating the occurrences of diseased-related malnutrition and sarcopenia.

Due to the rarity of T2IF, much of the current research is limited to single-center studies with small patient cohorts, presenting challenges to generalize findings to broader patient populations​. Advanced methodologies, such as stable-isotope tracer techniques, offer a feasible alternative, enabling precise quantification of nutrient absorption and metabolism even with small sample sizes. Moreover, there are no specific guidelines for assessing nutritional status of patients managed by CRT, beyond traditional measures of weight or nutrient intake. Utilizing stable-isotope tracer techniques could provide insights to develop specific dietary interventions that address the unique nutritional challenges faced by CRT patients with T2IF. The primary focus of future research should be on using the previously described range of advanced techniques to gain a deeper understanding of nutrient metabolism in patients with T2IF. This approach will not only refine our comprehension of nutritional adequacy but also facilitate the development of robust nutritional guidelines for these patients with the objective of improved patient outcomes, quality of life, and mortality rates.
