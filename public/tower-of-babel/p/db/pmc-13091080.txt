Clinicopathological significance of microRNA-9 and microRNA-34 methylation in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Nabiha Missaoui, Hajer Hamchi, Oussama Belkacem, Haifa Chamekh, Syrine Moussa, Khaoula Khlifi, Ahlem Bdioui, Sarra Mestiri, Salima Ferchichi, Sihem Hmissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA methylation is a key epigenetic mechanism involved in lung carcinogenesis. MicroRNAs (miRs) regulate gene expression and cellular processes, and their aberrant methylation may contribute to dysregulation in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). This retrospective study aimed to characterize the methylation profiles of the miR-9 (miR-9-2, miR-9-3) and miR-34 (miR-34a, miR-34b/c) families in NSCLC and to evaluate their clinicopathological significance. Promoter methylation status was assessed by methylation-specific PCR following bisulfite conversion of DNA isolated from 68 primary tumor specimens and 11 adjacent normal lung tissues. No promoter methylation of miR-9 or miR-34 family members was detected in normal tissues. Methylation of at least one miR-9 locus was observed in 50% of tumors, with miR-9-2 and miR-9-3 methylated in 41.2% and 38.2% of cases, respectively. miR-34 family methylation was detected in 69.1% of tumors, including miR-34a in 60.3% and miR-34b/c in 47.1%, with concurrent methylation of both miR-34 loci in 38.2%. miR-9-3 methylation was significantly associated with male sex (p = 0.046) and smaller tumor size (p = 0.035). miR-34a methylation was significantly associated with histological subtype (p = 0.041). Overall, the frequent of miR-9 and miR-34 family members supports their potential involvement in NSCLC epigenetic dysregulation and warrants further validation in larger, independent cohorts exploring their clinical relevance in personalized cancer management.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. It is broadly classified into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for approximately 85% of cases, and small cell lung cancer, which represents the remaining 15% [2]. Despite significant therapeutic advances, the prognosis of NSCLC remains poor, primarily due to late-stage diagnosis and the emergence of treatment resistance [3,4].

Lung carcinogenesis results from a complex interplay between genetic alterations, epigenetic dysregulation, and environmental exposures [5,6]. Epigenetic mechanisms, particularly aberrant DNA methylation and dysregulated non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), play a pivotal role in modulating oncogene and tumor suppressor gene expression [7]. MicroRNAs (miRs) are small ncRNAs of approximately 22 nucleotides in length, have emerged as key regulators of post-transcriptional gene expression [8].

The miR-34 family is frequently downregulated in several malignancies, including colorectal, prostate, breast, and lung cancers [9]. Through its well-described synergistic interaction with TP53, the miR-34 family participates in a positive feedback loop that reinforces tumor-suppressive signaling, inducing cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and senescence by targeting multiple oncogenic pathways [9,10]. In NSCLC, miR-34 has been shown to regulate tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis through modulation of several signaling cascades [11]. The family comprises three members, miR-34a, miR-34b, and miR-34c, encoded at two chromosomal loci: miR-34a on chromosome 1p36.22 and miR-34b/c at chromosome 11q23.1 [9]. Epigenetic silencing through promoter hypermethylation has been proposed as a major mechanism underlying miR-34 downregulation in NSCLC [10,11].

In contrast, the miR-9 family, encoded by three distinct genomic loci: miR-9-1 (1q22), miR-9-2 (5q14.3), and miR-9-3 (15q26.1), exhibits markedly context-dependent functions [12]. Downregulation of miR-9 has been reported in several cancers, including gastric, colorectal, ovarian, and esophageal carcinomas, where reduced expression is associated with inhibition of tumor growth and metastatic potential [[13], [14], [15]]. Conversely, miR-9 overexpression has been observed in breast and brain carcinomas, where it promotes cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and overall tumor progression [16,17]. However, in NSCLC, the biological and clinicopathological significance of miR-9 remains controversial and incompletely understood [[18], [19], [20]]. These discrepant findings suggest that regulatory mechanisms beyond transcriptional control, particularly epigenetic modifications, may contribute to the heterogeneous expression patterns of miR-9 in NSCLC [21].

Aberrant promoter methylation of miRNA genes has emerged as a key epigenetic mechanism contributing to their transcriptional silencing in cancer. In NSCLC, hypermethylation of the miR-34 and miR-9 family loci has been reported in several populations and implicated in tumor initiation and progression [9,10]. However, most available data originate from Asian and Western cohorts, with very limited evidence from North African and Arab populations. Moreover, few studies have examined the combined methylation status of multiple members of both families in parallel while integrating detailed clinicopathological correlations. To address this geographical and integrative gap, the present study investigates the promoter methylation patterns of miR-9 (miR-9-2 and miR-9-3) and miR-34 (miR-34a and miR-34b/c) family members in a North African NSCLC cohort using FFPE specimens and evaluates their associations with clinicopathological characteristics.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective analytical study included 71 patients with histologically confirmed NSCLC diagnosed in the Pathology Department, Sahloul University Hospital, Sousse (Tunisia) between January 2020 and December 2024. Cases were selected consecutively from archival formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks based on tissue availability and completeness of clinicopathological data. Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine in Sousse (CEFMSo_0089/2024).

Eleven samples of histologically normal adjacent lung tissue were included as non-neoplastic controls. These unpaired samples were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection for lung cancer and were collected at a distance from the tumor margin. All control tissues were confirmed to be free of malignant or dysplastic changes by histological examination. The limited number of normal samples reflects the restricted availability of suitable adjacent non-tumoral lung tissue in routine practice.

Histological diagnoses were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists using hematoxylin- and eosin- (H&E) stained slides. Tumors were classified and graded according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [22], and pathological staging was assigned based on the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system [23].

Only cases with >50% tumor cellularity, as estimated by pathological review, and sufficient biological material for molecular analyses were included. For each case, the most cellular and well-preserved tumor areas were identified under low magnification ( × 40). Necrotic regions, poorly fixed areas, and sections with significant artifacts were systematically excluded to reduce stromal contamination. Patients who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy before tissue sampling were excluded.

Clinicopathological data were retrieved from medical records and included patient age, sex, tumor location, tumor size, histological subtype, tumor grade, pathological TNM stage, and relevant medical history.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from 6-μm-thick FFPE tissue sections using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Before DNA extraction, a manual microdissection procedure was performed to enrich the sample for tumor cells. Briefly, H&E-stained slides were independently reviewed by two pathologists to delineate tumor-rich regions and confirm tumor cellularity. Corresponding areas were manually scraped from unstained serial sections to minimize contamination by adjacent stromal or inflammatory components.

DNA integrity was assessed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of a 268-bp fragment of the human β-globin gene as previously described [24,25]. Only samples yielding amplifiable DNA were included in downstream analyses.

Bisulfite conversion and β-actin amplification

Bisulfite conversion of genomic DNA was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. To assess DNA integrity and the presence of bisulfite-modified DNA, PCR amplification of a 133-bp fragment of the human β-actin gene was performed using primers specific for bisulfite-converted DNA (Supp. Table 1), as previously described [26]. Primers were designed to selectively recognize bisulfite-converted DNA irrespective of methylation status. PCR reactions and thermal cycling conditions were carried out as previously described [27,28].

Only samples yielding a single, specific 133-bp amplicon were considered suitable for downstream methylation analyses. Samples failing amplification after two independent attempts were excluded. This quality control approach ensures adequate DNA integrity and successful bisulfite modification; however, it does not provide a quantitative assessment of cytosine conversion efficiency at individual non-CpG sites.

Methylation-specific PCR

The methylation status of target loci, including members of the miR-9 (miR-9-2, miR-9-3) and miR-34 (miR-34a, miR-34b/c) families, was assessed by methylation-specific PCR (MS-PCR) as originally described by Herman et al. [29]. Methylation-specific primer sets were used to selectively amplify methylated alleles, with primer sequences, annealing temperatures, and expected fragment sizes provided in Supp. Table 1. PCR reactions and cycling conditions were carried out as detailed previously [27,28]. MSP was performed twice on all specimens, with a third repeat assay performed if discrepant results were obtained from the first two assays. Negative controls without a DNA template were included in each run to monitor for cross-contamination. Human HeLa genomic DNA was enzymatically methylated with CpG methylase (M.SssI, New England Biolabs) to generate a positive control for methylated alleles [29].

In our research laboratory, all PCR assays were performed with rigorous contamination-prevention measures. DNA extraction, PCR setup, and post-amplification analyses were conducted in physically separated laboratory areas. Aerosol-resistant pipette tips were used for all steps, and dedicated equipment and consumables were maintained for pre- and post-amplification work.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test when expected cell counts were <5. A p-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and tumor features

Patient age ranged from 21 to 83 years (median 63.5 years). The cohort was predominantly male (sex ratio: 13.2). Smoking status was available for only 21 patients, with pack-years quantified in 10 cases. Approximately 49.3% of the cohort presented with medical comorbidities, including 11 patients with prior malignancies. Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in Supp. Table 2. Tumors were most frequently located in the upper lobes. Macroscopically, 66.2% of tumors were infiltrative. Tumor size ranged from 0.1 to 22.5 cm. Histologically, adenocarcinoma was the predominant subtype (53.5%). Most tumors were well-differentiated (54.9%). Extensive tumor necrosis was observed in 29.6% of cases. Regarding pathological staging, T3 tumors were most frequent (26.3%). Lymph node involvement was absent in 63.2% of cases (N0), whereas 18.4% and 15.8% were classified as N1 and N2, respectively. Only one patient presented with distant metastasis (M1a).

DNA quality control

Genomic DNA was successfully extracted from FFPE tissue samples. Of the 71 initial samples, 68 yielded adequate DNA quality and were retained for bisulfite conversion and subsequent methylation analysis.

Methylation profiles of miRNAs

The analysis of histologically non-neoplastic adjacent lung tissues revealed no detectable promoter methylation of any miR-9 or miR-34 family members. However, within the miR-9 family, aberrant methylation of at least one locus was detected in 34 tumors. Specifically, miR-9-2 promoter methylation occurred in 28 cases, while miR-9-3 methylation was detected in 26 cases. Discussion

This study investigated the promoter methylation status of key tumor-related miRNA families, miR-9 and miR-34, in NSCLC and evaluated their associations with clinicopathological characteristics. Our findings demonstrate frequent promoter methylation affecting both miRNA families in tumor samples, supporting a relevant role for epigenetic deregulation of miRNAs in NSCLC pathogenesis.

Promoter methylation of at least one miR-9 locus was detected in 50% of tumors, whereas no methylation was observed in adjacent normal tissues analyzed in this cohort, suggesting a tumor-restricted distribution within the limits of the applied methodology. Within the miR-9 family, miR-9-2 and miR-9-3 were methylated in 41.2% and 38.2% of cases, respectively, with concurrent methylation in nearly one-third of tumors, suggesting coordinated epigenetic silencing of family members. Notably, miR-9-3 methylation was significantly associated with male sex and smaller tumor size, which may indicate involvement earlier in tumor development, however, given the cross-sectional design, temporal inferences cannot be made.

Previous studies have reported heterogeneous frequencies of miR-9 methylation in NSCLC, likely reflecting differences in ethnicity, sample size, tumor composition, and analytical methodology. For example, in a Japanese study, Kitano et al. [30] reported miR-9-3 methylation in 64.6% of cases and identified it as an independent predictor of advanced T status. However, Muraoka et al. [21] observed substantially lower frequencies of promoter methylation, with miR-9-3 hypermethylation in 12% and miR-9-2 hypermethylation in 11%. Interestingly, miR-9 methylation was significantly associated with improved overall survival, underscoring the context-dependent biological functions of miR-9 in lung cancer. A more recent study by Tan et al. [31] reported marked heterogeneity in methylation levels: miR-9-1 methylation ranged from 2% to 65% versus 1.5%–2% in adjacent normal tissues; miR-9-2 ranged from 8% to 37% versus 6%–8% in normal tissues; and miR-9-3 showed the greatest variability, ranging from 16% to 70% versus 3%–12% in normal tissues. These results collectively support the potential of miR-9 methylation as a diagnostic biomarker in NSCLC and highlight its possible role in patient stratification and targeted therapeutic approaches.

Our observation that miR-9 methylation is associated with smaller tumor size contrasts with reports linking miR-9 overexpression to aggressive disease, suggesting that epigenetic silencing and transcriptional activation of miR-9 may play distinct, stage-specific roles during NSCLC progression. This hypothesis is supported by functional studies demonstrating that demethylation-induced re-expression of miR-9-3 using the demethylating agent 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine reduced CpG island methylation by 45%, leading to a significant upregulation of miR-9-3 expression [32]. This re-expression suppressed proliferation and promoted apoptosis in NSCLC cell lines, confirming its tumor-suppressive potential under specific biological contexts. Collectively, these data support a dual and context-dependent role for miR-9, modulated by epigenetic regulation, tumor stage, and cellular environment.

In addition to methylation profiling, expression analyses have provided further insight into the role of the miR-9 family in NSCLC. Xu et al. [33] reported significantly elevated miR-9 expression in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues. High miR-9 expression was strongly associated with adverse clinicopathological features, including advanced TNM stage, larger tumor size, and lymph node metastasis. Prognostic analysis demonstrated markedly reduced progression-free survival and overall survival in patients with high miR-9 expression compared with those with low expression.

Beyond the miR-9 family, miR-34 family methylation was highly prevalent in our cohort (69.1%), with miR-34a and miR-34b/c methylated in 60.3% and 47.1% of tumors, respectively. As observed for miR-9, no methylation was detected in the adjacent non-neoplastic tissues analyzed, further reinforcing the tumor specificity of miR-34 epigenetic silencing, although validation in larger normal cohorts with quantitative methods is warranted. Importantly, miR-34a methylation was significantly associated with histological subtype, with a higher frequency in adenocarcinoma compared with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

The miR-34 family is well-established as a downstream effector of the p53 tumor-suppressor pathway, mediating cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and senescence. Epigenetic silencing of miR-34 has been repeatedly linked to poor prognosis in NSCLC. Gallardo et al. [34] reported that 67% of patients with low miR-34a expression experienced relapse compared with 37% of those with high expression, whereas no significant association was observed for miR-34b/c expression. Similarly, Wang et al. [35] identified miR-34b/c hypermethylation as an independent predictor of recurrence, especially in early-stage SCCs, highlighting its potential utility in risk stratification and clinical management. Together, these observations align with our findings of frequent miR-34 methylation and subtype associations, and further suggest clinical relevance for the miR-34 axis.

In addition to methylation, several studies have examined miR-34a and miR-34b/c expression in NSCLC. In the study by Zhao et al. [36], circulating and tissue levels of miR-34a and miR-34c were significantly correlated, whereas miR-34b showed no such correlation. Circulating miR-34a levels were inversely correlated with lymph node metastasis and TNM stage, while miR-34b was correlated with lymph node metastasis, and miR-34c with histological subtype. In tumor tissues, miR-34a expression was inversely correlated with lymph node metastasis and TNM stage, and miR-34c expression was associated with patient age and sex, and was inversely correlated with lymph node metastasis. High expression of miR-34a and miR-34c in both serum and tissue was linked to longer disease-free and overall survival, whereas miR-34b expression did not significantly affect outcomes [36]. These findings suggest that tumor tissue expression of miR-34a and miR-34c provides greater prognostic value than circulating levels, highlighting the potential of the miR-34 family as biomarkers for identifying high-risk NSCLC patients who may benefit from personalized management.

Several limitations should be considered. First, methylation was assessed using conventional MS-PCR, a qualitative method that does not provide absolute methylation levels or allele-specific quantification, unlike pyrosequencing or other quantitative approaches. Consequently, partial or low-level methylation events may have been underdetected. Moreover, because unmethylated-specific primers were not systematically applied, negative results indicate the absence of detectable methylation rather than a confirmed unmethylated status. Second, the number of adjacent normal tissues was limited (n = 11), reducing statistical power for tumor-normal comparisons and precluding definitive conclusions regarding tumor specificity. Third, multiple statistical comparisons were performed without formal correction, increasing the possibility of a type I error, particularly for associations with borderline significance. Fourth, the absence of survival, expression, and molecular subtype data, including TP53 status, limits the biological interpretation and comprehensive prognostic inference. Accordingly, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Despite these constraints, this study provides region-specific data from a North African and Arab NSCLC cohort using routinely available FFPE specimens, supporting the involvement of miRNA promoter methylation in NSCLC pathobiology. Given the limited representation of North African populations in molecular epigenetic studies of lung cancer, these findings contribute to the global characterization of miRNA promoter methylation patterns and may serve as a foundation for larger integrative analyses incorporating quantitative methylation, transcriptomic, and genomic profiling.

Conclusion

Our findings support that promoter methylation of miR-9 and miR-34 is a frequent event in NSCLC, while it was not detected in the analyzed adjacent non-neoplastic tissues, suggesting its tumor-specific nature. Distinct clinicopathological associations were observed: miR-9-3 methylation was significantly associated with male sex and smaller tumor size, whereas miR-34a methylation varied significantly by histological subtype, highlighting the potential biological relevance of miRNA promoter methylation within the epigenetic landscape of NSCLC.

While these results suggest a contributory role for miRNA methylation in NSCLC pathogenesis, the biological and clinical implications remain preliminary due to the qualitative assessment method and the lack of integrated miRNA expression, molecular subclassification, and survival analyses.

To refine the mechanistic and translational significance of these epigenetic alterations, future studies should incorporate quantitative methylation assays, paired miRNA expression profiling, TP53 status assessment, and comprehensive molecular characterization in large, independent, well-annotated NSCLC cohorts. Such integrative approaches will be essential to determine the robustness, reproducibility, and potential diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic value of miR-9 and miR-34 methylation within precision oncology frameworks for NSCLC.
