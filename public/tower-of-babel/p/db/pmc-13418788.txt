Changing the landscape of medical anatomy education: impact of flipped classroom pedagogy on learning outcomes and perceptions of medical students in practical gastrointestinal anatomy
Authors: Shabnam Mohammadi, Haniye Mastour, Maliheh Dadgar Moghadam, Ali Emadzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnatomy education faces challenges such as reduced teaching hours and limited cadaver access, emphasizing the need for innovative instructional strategies. The flipped classroom model, which shifts knowledge acquisition to pre-class activities and fosters active learning during class, has shown promise but remains underexplored in practical gastrointestinal anatomy teaching.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study involved 63 medical students at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Iran, assigned to either a flipped classroom (n = 28) or traditional lecture-based group (n = 35). By integrating pre-class preparation with in-class collaborative learning, it fosters deeper understanding and engagement. These findings support integrating flipped pedagogy into anatomy curricula to improve student performance and satisfaction.Keywords: Anatomy Education, Active Learning, Flipped Classroom, Gastrointestinal Anatomy, Learning Environment, Medical Education

Background

Medical anatomy education stands at a critical juncture, facing unprecedented challenges that demand innovative pedagogical solutions. The traditional lecture-based approach to teaching anatomy, long considered the cornerstone of medical education, is increasingly recognized as insufficient to meet the evolving needs of 21st-century healthcare professionals [1]. With diminishing contact hours, reduced cadaver availability, and mounting pressure to integrate clinical relevance into foundational sciences, anatomy educators worldwide are seeking transformative approaches to enhance student learning outcomes and engagement [2–4].

Traditional anatomy education has long relied on lecture-oriented instructional models that predominantly promote passive learning through didactic lectures and instructor-centered demonstrations [5, 6]. Although these approaches allow efficient content delivery, they often limit opportunities for active cognitive processing and meaningful knowledge construction. In particular, passive instructional formats tend to emphasize memorization of isolated anatomical facts rather than facilitating the integration of structure, function, and clinical relevance. As a result, students may acquire fragmented knowledge that is difficult to transfer to clinical contexts, where anatomical understanding must be applied dynamically to problem-solving and decision-making [7, 8].

A growing body of evidence suggests that passive, lecture-based approaches inadequately support higher-order cognitive processes essential for effective anatomical learning, including spatial reasoning, clinical correlation, and integrative thinking [9, 10]. Students frequently report that traditional teaching methods fail to prepare them sufficiently for the complex, integrated knowledge required in clinical practice [2, 10]. These limitations are further exacerbated by the ongoing reduction in anatomy teaching hours compared with historical standards, intensifying the need for more efficient and pedagogically effective instructional strategies [1, 3].

Despite their widespread use, passive approaches to anatomy education have been increasingly criticized for their limited capacity to foster deep learning, sustained engagement, and higher-order cognitive processing [6, 11, 12]. By positioning students as passive recipients of information, traditional didactic models provide few opportunities for active engagement, application of knowledge, or formative feedback. Experimental evidence indicates that although students may perceive passive lectures as effective, their actual performance in conceptual understanding and knowledge transfer is significantly lower than that of students engaged in active learning environments [13]. Empirical data further demonstrate that the sheer volume and spatial complexity of anatomical structures demand levels of cognitive engagement that passive observation alone cannot adequately provide [14].

In response to these limitations, multiple studies have demonstrated the educational advantages of active, student-centered instructional approaches. Baro et al. [15], for instance, showed that hands-on instruction using temporal bone sections, ear ossicles, handmade three-dimensional models, and otoscope examinations significantly improved students’ understanding, retention, and spatial comprehension of ear anatomy. This approach also enhanced practical skills, learner satisfaction, and self-confidence when compared with traditional lecture-based teaching. Consequently, the inherent constraints of the “sage on the stage” model have prompted increasing calls for pedagogical frameworks that prioritize student-led inquiry, active participation, and practical application [7, 13].

The limitations of passive learning become particularly pronounced in complex anatomical domains such as the gastrointestinal system, which is characterized by structural intricacy and requires the integration of anatomical, functional, and embryological concepts [16, 17]. Mastery of this system demands an understanding of dynamic structure–function relationships and imposes substantial cognitive demands on undergraduate medical students [3, 18, 19]. In such contexts, reliance on passive instructional methods may further impede deep learning and the transfer of anatomical knowledge to clinical settings. Collectively, these challenges underscore the necessity of pedagogical models that actively engage learners in applying, analyzing, and integrating anatomical knowledge rather than memorizing isolated facts.

Consistent with this perspective, comparative outcome-based research has highlighted the superiority of active learning strategies over traditional lecture-based instruction in anatomy education. El Sadik and Al Abdulmonem [20] reported significantly higher student performance across multiple cognitive levels following the implementation of a flipped classroom model, with particularly large effect sizes observed at the application and analysis levels of Bloom’s taxonomy. These findings suggest that shifting learners from passive information recipients to active participants substantially enhances cognitive engagement and academic achievement. Similarly, Plendl et al. [21] demonstrated that peer-assisted learning, digital microscopy, and student expert teams improved student engagement, self-confidence, and learning effectiveness, particularly in settings with reduced contact hours.

Recent experimental and interventional studies further support the effectiveness of active learning modalities in addressing the shortcomings of passive instruction. HadaviBavili and İlçioğlu [22] showed that artwork-based anatomy education significantly enhanced students’ self-efficacy and attitudes toward anatomy learning while promoting deeper conceptual understanding and critical thinking through creative, hands-on engagement. Likewise, student-centered technological interventions, such as self-prepared three-dimensional anatomical modeling, have been shown to increase motivation, reduce anxiety, and foster meaningful learning experiences that passive instructional methods fail to achieve [23]. Collectively, this growing body of empirical evidence demonstrates that passive, lecture-based anatomy education has inherent limitations in promoting deep learning, learner engagement, and cognitive development, thereby highlighting the need for the adoption and rigorous evaluation of active and blended learning approaches to meet contemporary educational demands.

In response to these challenges, medical educators have increasingly turned to active learning pedagogies, with the flipped classroom model emerging as a particularly promising approach [24, 25]. The flipped classroom represents a fundamental paradigm shift that inverts traditional instructional methods by moving initial content delivery outside the classroom and dedicating face-to-face time to active, collaborative learning activities [26–28]. This pedagogical transformation aligns with contemporary educational theories that emphasize student-centered learning, constructivist principles, and the development of higher-order cognitive skills [29, 30].

The theoretical foundation for flipped classroom pedagogy rests on several key educational principles. The approach facilitates active knowledge construction by requiring students to engage with material independently before class, while classroom time is optimized for collaborative problem-solving, critical thinking, and application of knowledge [26, 31, 32]. Research has consistently demonstrated that flipped classrooms can enhance student engagement, self-directed learning capabilities, and metacognitive awareness [28, 33, 34].

Moreover, the flexibility inherent in the flipped model allows for personalized learning experiences that can accommodate diverse learning styles and paces [35]. Within the context of medical anatomy education, the flipped classroom approach offers unique advantages for addressing the discipline’s specific challenges. The model’s emphasis on pre-class preparation enables students to familiarize themselves with basic anatomical concepts and terminology before engaging in more complex, interactive activities during class time [36, 37]. This preparation phase is particularly valuable for digestive system anatomy, where students must master extensive anatomical terminology and spatial relationships before progressing to functional integration and clinical applications [18, 19].

Empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of flipped classroom pedagogy in anatomy education has been steadily accumulating. Multiple studies have demonstrated significant improvements in academic performance, with students in flipped classroom environments consistently outperforming their peers in traditional lecture-based courses [36–38]. Perhaps more importantly, research has revealed enhanced student satisfaction and positive perceptions of the learning experience, with the majority of students expressing preference for flipped classroom approaches over traditional methods [37–39].

The implementation of flipped classroom pedagogy in digestive system anatomy education represents a strategic response to the evolving demands of medical education. As healthcare systems worldwide grapple with increasing complexity and the need for evidence-based practice, medical educators must prepare students with not only foundational knowledge but also the critical thinking skills and adaptive learning capabilities necessary for lifelong professional development [25, 40, 41]. The flipped classroom model’s emphasis on active participation, collaborative learning, and self-directed inquiry aligns perfectly with these contemporary educational objectives [30, 31, 42].

Although the effectiveness of the flipped classroom has been well documented in medical education, important gaps remain regarding its application in specific anatomical domains and its simultaneous impact on both objective learning outcomes and students’ perceptions of the anatomy learning environment. In particular, limited empirical evidence exists focusing on practical gastrointestinal anatomy, a domain characterized by high cognitive load, complex spatial relationships, and the need for integration of structure, function, and clinical relevance [4, 16, 18, 43, 44]. Moreover, few studies have examined the flipped classroom through the combined use of blueprint-based assessments aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy and a validated, anatomy-specific learning environment instrument. By addressing these gaps, the present study extends existing literature by providing domain-specific and methodologically rigorous evidence on how flipped classroom pedagogy influences both learning outcomes and educational environment perceptions in practical gastrointestinal anatomy.

Methods

Study design and objectives

The primary objective of this quasi-experimental study was to examine the impact of the flipped classroom instructional model on medical students’ learning outcomes and their perception of the learning environment in a practical gastrointestinal anatomy course.

Setting and participants

The intervention was conducted at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Iran, during the 2023–2024 academic year. All third-semester undergraduate medical students (n = 70) were eligible. Students were allocated to the intervention (flipped classroom, n = 35) and control (traditional lecture-based instruction, n = 35) groups using a quasi-random administrative grouping process. Inclusion criteria were willingness to participate and regular attendance. Exclusion criteria included missing more than two sessions, not completing the pretest/posttest or questionnaires, and failure to study pre-class materials in the flipped classroom group. Seven students who did not complete the pre-test were excluded, leaving 63 participants for analysis.

Intervention

The course consisted of eight 90-minute practical sessions, evenly divided between histology and gross anatomy, with the schedule identical for both groups. Both the intervention and control groups engaged in comparable levels of student interaction and active learning activities, including small-group discussions, problem-solving exercises, and peer-to-peer teaching.

The intervention group received instruction through a flipped classroom model. Pre-class learning resources—including short, instructor-developed videos and annotated images—were aligned with lower-order objectives of Bloom’s taxonomy (knowledge and comprehension) and made accessible via the university’s Learning Management System (LMS). During class, students participated in structured, clinically oriented problem-solving exercises conducted in small groups, requiring them to apply anatomical knowledge to realistic scenarios. These activities involved identifying anatomical structures and spatial relationships using labeled moulages, analyzing brief clinical vignettes of gastrointestinal disorders, and responding to guided questions designed to stimulate discussion of structure–function relationships and clinical implications. Students collaboratively proposed solutions, justified their reasoning, and received immediate formative feedback from the instructor. Each session concluded with an instructor-led synthesis of key concepts and reinforcement of clinically relevant points. These in-class activities targeted higher-order cognitive skills, including application and analysis, according to Bloom’s taxonomy. Labeled moulages were deliberately incorporated to support collaborative learning, enhance spatial understanding, and facilitate anatomical reasoning (Fig. 1).

The control group received the same set of in-class activities, exercises, and collaborative tasks as the intervention group, but the instruction was delivered through a traditional lecture-based format. Specifically, concepts were introduced through short lectures before students engaged in the same problem-solving exercises, group discussions, and peer-teaching tasks, followed by instructor-led synthesis and formative feedback. By keeping the level of student engagement and the types of activities consistent between groups, any differences in learning outcomes can be attributed primarily to the flipped classroom model itself rather than to variations in activity type or interaction level.

Outcome measures

Two primary outcomes were assessed to comprehensively evaluate the impact of the instructional approaches on both student learning and their perceptions in the practical gastrointestinal anatomy course. Knowledge acquisition was measured using standardized pre- and post-tests specifically developed for the gastrointestinal anatomy and histology course. Each assessment comprised 30 multiple-choice items, including 20 items on gross anatomy and 10 on histology. All items were developed using a content blueprint and aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy to ensure balanced representation of lower-order cognitive skills (knowledge and comprehension) and higher-order skills (application and analysis). To demonstrate alignment with Bloom’s taxonomy, lower-order items assessed factual recall and identification of anatomical structures (e.g., “Which artery directly supplies the greater curvature of the stomach?”), whereas higher-order items required application and analytical reasoning in clinical contexts (e.g., “A patient presents with a perforated posterior gastric ulcer. Injury to which anatomical structure is most likely responsible for the observed bleeding?”). The pre-test and post-test were designed as parallel forms rather than identical instruments. Although both assessments adhered to the same content blueprint and cognitive-level distribution, individual items differed to minimize recall bias and testing effects while maintaining comparable difficulty and construct coverage.

Content validity of the knowledge test was established through expert review by a panel of three specialists in anatomy and medical education, who evaluated item clarity, relevance, and alignment with the intended learning objectives. Internal consistency reliability of the 30-item multiple-choice test, administered as both the pre-test and post-test, was assessed using the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), which is appropriate for dichotomously scored items. The KR-20 coefficient was 0.78, indicating acceptable internal consistency and demonstrating that the test reliably measured the intended knowledge construct across administrations.

In addition to learning outcomes, students’ perceptions of the educational environment were evaluated using the Anatomy Education Environment Measurement Inventory (AEEMI), a validated, anatomy-specific instrument designed to comprehensively assess the anatomy learning context. The AEEMI consists of 108 items distributed across seven domains: perceptions of anatomy teachers, learning resources, learning atmosphere, student effort, content and organization, social self-perceptions, and overall satisfaction with the anatomy learning environment [45, 46]. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating more favorable perceptions.

The content validity of the AEEMI was established through expert evaluation by a panel of 10 specialists in medical education, educational technology, and anatomy. Both the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were used to assess item relevance and representativeness. CVR values were interpreted in accordance with Lawshe’s criteria, while item-level and scale-level CVI values were evaluated based on established methodological guidelines [47, 48]. All CVR and CVI indices exceeded recommended thresholds, supporting adequate content coverage and construct representation [49, 50]. Face validity was further confirmed by ensuring item clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance to the target student population.

The reliability of the AEEMI was examined using both stability and internal consistency measures. Test–retest reliability, assessed over a two-week interval, demonstrated strong temporal stability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) exceeding 0.80. Internal consistency was also satisfactory, as reflected by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.80 for the overall instrument [51, 52]. Taken together, these psychometric findings—along with the acceptable KR-20 reliability of the knowledge assessment—support the methodological rigor and robustness of the measurement instruments employed in this study.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 16.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and outcome variables; categorical data are reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As assumptions were satisfied, parametric methods were applied for inferential analyses.

Between-group differences in demographic characteristics were examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables. Within-group changes in knowledge scores from pre- to post-test were assessed using paired-samples t-tests, and post-intervention comparisons between groups were conducted using independent-samples t-tests. Baseline knowledge scores are presented as medians to demonstrate group equivalence, whereas post-test scores and learning gains are reported as mean ± standard deviation to facilitate effect size estimation.

Students’ perceptions of the anatomy learning environment, measured using the AEEMI, are reported as median domain and total scores because of the ordinal nature of Likert-scale data; however, parametric tests were used for between-group comparisons to maintain consistency with prior medical education research. Effect sizes for knowledge outcomes were calculated using Cohen’s d and interpreted using conventional benchmarks. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p < 0.05.

The internal consistency of the AEEMI was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with values of 0.70–0.90 considered acceptable [53]. To examine factors associated with students’ perceptions of the learning environment, multiple linear regression analysis was performed. The overall AEEMI score was entered as the dependent variable, with instructional group (0 = control, 1 = flipped classroom), gender, age, grade point average, and baseline pre-test score included as independent variables. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Standardized regression coefficients (β), corresponding p-values, and adjusted R² values are reported.

Results

Participant demographics

A total of 70 students were eligible, of whom 63 completed the study (35 in the control group and 28 in the intervention group). Overall, 34 participants (54%) were male and 29 (46%) were female. No significant differences were observed in gender, tuition type or housing status (Table 1).

Demographic characteristics of participants

No statistically significant differences were observed between groups in terms of age (p = 0.89), grade point average (p = 0.79), or pretest scores (p = 0.073), indicating baseline comparability.

Learning outcomes

Baseline pre-test scores were comparable between the control and intervention groups (mean ± SD: 14.80 ± 3.05 vs. 14.65 ± 2.97; p = 0.073), indicating similar knowledge levels prior to the intervention. Following the educational intervention, students in the flipped classroom group achieved significantly higher post-test scores than those in the control group (mean ± SD: 20.34 ± 3.10 vs. 17.42 ± 3.22; t₆₁ = 3.65, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.87), reflecting a substantial effect of the flipped classroom approach.

Within-group analyses demonstrated significant improvements from pre- to post-test in both groups (control: 14.80 ± 3.05 to 17.42 ± 3.22, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.86; intervention: 14.65 ± 2.97 to 20.34 ± 3.10, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.87), confirming meaningful learning gains over the course of the study (Table 2). Collectively, these findings indicate that the flipped classroom model was more effective than traditional lectures in enhancing student performance in practical gastrointestinal anatomy.

Learning environment perceptions

Given the ordinal nature of Likert-scale responses, AEEMI domain scores are presented using median values to provide a robust summary of central tendency, while inferential comparisons were conducted using parametric methods as supported by normality testing. Analysis of the AEEMI demonstrated that, compared with the control group, the flipped classroom group had significantly higher median scores in the domains of resources (93 vs. 81, p < 0.001), effort (112 vs. 99, p < 0.001), and overall learning environment (418 vs. 393, p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed in the remaining domains. These findings suggest that students in the flipped classroom perceived a more supportive, resource-enriched, and engaging educational environment compared with those in the traditional classroom.

Figure 2 illustrates these results by comparing overall AEEMI scores between the control and intervention groups. The control group achieved a median score of approximately 395, with a relatively narrow interquartile range (IQR) and whiskers extending from about 385 to 405, indicating limited variability. In contrast, the intervention group demonstrated a higher median score of around 415, with a slightly broader IQR and whiskers ranging from 410 to 420. This pattern reflects consistently stronger performance among students in the flipped classroom, reinforcing the effectiveness of the intervention in enhancing perceptions of the learning environment.

Subgroup analyses

Female students reported significantly higher perceptions of the learning environment compared to male students (p < 0.001), although post-test performance did not differ by gender. Other demographic factors—including type of tuition, housing status, and age—were not associated with differences in either learning outcomes or perceptions.

Regression analysis

Multivariate regression identified instructional group allocation (flipped classroom vs. traditional lecture-based instruction) as the sole independent predictor of overall AEEMI scores (β = 0.816, p < 0.001), explaining 81% of the variance. Demographic variables and baseline pre-test scores were not statistically significant and were excluded from the final model. Group allocation was coded as a binary variable (0 = control, 1 = flipped classroom).

The standardized regression coefficient indicates a strong effect size, demonstrating that participation in the flipped classroom substantially influenced students’ perceptions of the learning environment. This effect may partially reflect heightened participant awareness of the intervention, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation of self-reported outcomes. Practically, being in the intervention group was associated with markedly higher AEEMI scores, far exceeding the contribution of demographic or baseline knowledge factors.

Discussion

This study provides robust evidence that flipped classroom pedagogy enhances both learning outcomes and perceptions of the educational environment in a practical gastrointestinal anatomy course. Although the effectiveness of flipped learning has been well documented, our findings consolidate this evidence within the specific context of practical gastrointestinal anatomy. The novelty of the present study does not lie in introducing a new pedagogical model, but rather in its domain-specific application, the use of blueprint-based assessments aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy, and the simultaneous evaluation of learning outcomes and anatomy-specific learning environment perceptions using a validated instrument (AEEMI). These findings extend prior evidence demonstrating that flipped learning improves knowledge acquisition, skill development, and student satisfaction in anatomy and health sciences education [36–38].

The superior post-test performance observed in the intervention group indicates an overall enhancement of learning outcomes associated with the flipped classroom approach. However, because item-level analyses stratified by cognitive complexity were not conducted, it is not possible to determine whether these gains were primarily attributable to improved performance on higher-order questions assessing complex anatomical relationships or to increased mastery of lower-order, knowledge-based items. Therefore, conclusions regarding enhanced higher-order cognitive learning should be interpreted as theoretically plausible rather than empirically confirmed. In alignment with Bloom’s revised taxonomy, the pre-class components of the flipped model were designed to target lower-order cognitive skills, thereby allowing in-class time to focus on higher-order activities such as application, analysis, and problem-solving [26, 29]. Consequently, given the pedagogical structure of the flipped classroom, it is plausible that engagement in higher-order cognitive processes contributed substantially to the observed improvements in post-test performance.

Participants in the flipped classroom also reported more favorable perceptions of the learning environment, supporting prior evidence that student-centered approaches enhance motivation, self-regulated learning, and metacognitive awareness [31, 33]. Female students, in particular, reported more positive experiences, highlighting the importance of inclusive pedagogical strategies that address diverse learning preferences [39]. These more favorable perceptions among female students may be influenced by several pedagogical and contextual factors rather than inherent gender differences. Prior research suggests that student-centered and collaborative learning environments—such as flipped classrooms—may better align with learning preferences commonly reported by female learners [54, 55]. Additionally, the availability of pre-class learning materials in the flipped classroom model allows for self-paced preparation, which may reduce cognitive load and alleviate learner anxiety during in-class activities [56, 57]. Together, these features may contribute to more positive perceptions of the learning environment among female students. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be approached with caution, as the present study was not designed to explore gender-based learning mechanisms in depth. These findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further qualitative and mixed-methods research is warranted to clarify how gender-related factors interact with instructional design in anatomy education.

Although the present study focused on gastrointestinal anatomy, the observed positive effects of the flipped classroom approach are consistent with findings reported in other anatomical domains. Previous studies in musculoskeletal anatomy, neuroanatomy, and histology have similarly demonstrated improvements in student engagement, satisfaction, and learning outcomes when flipped classroom strategies were employed [58–60]. The alignment of our findings with these studies suggests that the educational benefits observed may not be unique to gastrointestinal anatomy, but rather reflect broader pedagogical advantages of flipped classroom models in anatomy education. Moreover, evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicates that flipped classroom pedagogy consistently enhances learning outcomes across health professions education [61, 62]. Nevertheless, the practical and spatial complexity inherent to gastrointestinal anatomy may amplify the value of pre-class preparation and in-class active learning, highlighting the importance of contextual adaptation of flipped classroom designs across anatomical regions. Focusing on gastrointestinal anatomy, a conceptually complex and clinically relevant domain, our study demonstrates that structured opportunities for integrating structural and functional knowledge improve both understanding and application. Traditional lecture-based approaches often provide limited scaffolding for integrating anatomical structure, function, and clinical relevance, whereas flipped classroom designs have been shown to facilitate more meaningful learning [26, 29, 36, 37].

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the single-institution design and relatively modest sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, outcomes were primarily measured using short-term knowledge assessments and self-reported perceptions, which do not capture long-term knowledge retention, transfer of learning, or behavioral change in clinical or educational practice. Additionally, the potential influence of the Hawthorne effect cannot be excluded. Participants in the flipped classroom group were aware of their exposure to an innovative instructional approach, which may have increased engagement or favorable self-reporting independent of the instructional method itself. This effect may partially account for the strong association observed in the regression analysis, in which instructional group allocation emerged as the primary predictor of AEEMI scores; therefore, effect sizes should be interpreted with caution. Although short-term learning gains were observed, longitudinal studies are warranted to examine the durability of these effects over time. Furthermore, because item-level analyses aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy were not performed, it was not possible to determine whether the observed performance improvements were driven specifically by gains in higher-order cognitive skills. Future research should address this limitation by incorporating item-level cognitive classification and advanced psychometric approaches—such as item response theory or cognitive diagnostic modeling—to more precisely differentiate learning outcomes across cognitive levels and to elucidate the contribution of higher-order anatomical reasoning to overall performance improvements.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the flipped classroom is an effective instructional approach for teaching practical gastrointestinal anatomy. By integrating pre-class content engagement with interactive, collaborative, and feedback-rich in-class activities, flipped learning supports meaningful cognitive integration and promotes a more positive learning environment for medical students. The use of blueprint-based assessments aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy, together with an anatomy-specific learning environment instrument, provides a more nuanced understanding of both learning outcomes and students’ perceptions. Taken together, these results suggest that flipped classroom pedagogy represents a valuable and scalable instructional strategy for contemporary anatomy education. Its thoughtful integration into anatomy curricula may help address current pedagogical challenges and better support student-centered learning in medical training.
