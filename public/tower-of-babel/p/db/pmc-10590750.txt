Identification of key genes affecting sperm motility in chicken based on whole-transcriptome sequencing
Authors: Shihao Guo, Yizheng Liu, Yaxi Xu, Kai Gai, Bailin Cong, Kai Xing, Xiaolong Qi, Xiangguo Wang, Longfei Xiao, Cheng Long, Yong Guo, Li Chen, Xihui Sheng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Sperm motility is an important index for the evaluation of semen quality. Improving sperm motility is important to improve reproductive performance, promote breeding process, and reduce production cost. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating sperm motility in chickens remain unclear. In this study, histological observation and whole-transcriptome analysis were performed on testicular tissue of chickens with high and low sperm motility. Histological observations showed that roosters with high sperm motility exhibited better semen quality than those with low sperm motility. In addition, the germinal epithelial cells of roosters with low sperm motility were loosely arranged and contained many vacuoles. RNA-seq results revealed the expression of 23,033 mRNAs, 2,893 lncRNAs, and 515 miRNAs in chicken testes. Among them, there were 417 differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs), 106 differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs), and 15 differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) between high and low sperm motility testes. These differentially expressed genes were involved in the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, cilia structure, Wnt signaling, MAPK signaling, GnRH signaling, and mTOR signaling. By integrating the competitive relationships between DEmRNAs, DElncRNAs, and DEmiRNAs, we identified the regulatory pathway of MSTRG.3077.3/MSTRG.9085.1-gga-miR-138-5p-CADM1 and MSTRG.2290.1-gga-miR-142-3p-GNAQ/PPP3CA as crucial in the modulation of chicken sperm motility. This study provides new insights into the function and mechanism of ceRNAs in regulating sperm motility in chicken testes.

INTRODUCTION

The reproductive potential of males depends on the quality of their semen, which includes factors such as semen volume, sperm viability, and sperm density (Carlsen et al., 1992; Zini et al., 2000). Testes play a crucial role in the male reproductive system as the site of origin and development of spermatozoa. However, the molecular mechanisms that regulate sperm motility in chicken testes and their effects on sperm quality and male fertility remain unclear.

Sperm viability is controlled by a complex interplay of various genes. Several studies have shown that genes may impact sperm motility by regulating flagellar development (Bissonnette et al., 2009; Daigle et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2019; Pacheco et al., 2022), participating in oxidative phosphorylation, and regulating glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Du et al., 2022). Construction of the sperm flagellum is dependent on the tektin (Tanaka et al., 2004; Amos, 2008; Zuccarello et al., 2008), dynein (Roberts et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2021), and CCDC family genes (Firat-Karalar et al., 2014; Sha et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021).

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), endogenous transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, are found in various organelles within the cell nucleus and cytoplasm (Carlevaro-Fita et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2020). They display remarkable specificity for tissues, diseases, and developmental stages. lncRNAs play a crucial role in regulating testicular development and spermatogenesis (He et al., 2021), and changes in their expression levels are linked to the process of testicular development in pigs, cattle, and sheep (He et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022a; Zhao et al., 2022). Many highly expressed lncRNAs influence sperm development, maturation, and viability within the testis. Alterations in LNC00574 lncRNA expression have been found to influence TECT3 and ANO1 expression, which are associated with sperm morphology, teratozoospermia and oligospermia (Saberiyan et al., 2021).

MicroRNAs (miRNA) are noncoding RNA that range from 18 to 25 nucleotides in length and function by inhibiting post-transcriptional gene processing (Reddy and Natarajan, 2011; Ge et al., 2017). miRNAs have been shown to regulate sperm development and function and play epigenetic roles in reproduction (Ding et al., 2021; He et al., 2021). Let-7f targets the 3′UTR of CDC6 and participates in completing spermatocyte meiosis (Chen et al., 2017); miR-146-5p inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis of germinal stem cells (Gao et al., 2020); miR-155 is involved in male sexual maturation and regulates serum testosterone levels (Sakai et al., 2018; Morup et al., 2023); and miR-222 is associated with cell cycle and apoptosis, playing a role in regulating the male reproductive system (Zhang et al., 2010). However, the molecular mechanisms regulating sperm motility remain unclear.

Whole-transcriptome sequencing technology has been used widely in the past decade to reveal the genetic mechanism of chicken phenotypic traits (Shen et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2016). Whole-transcriptome sequencing was utilized to reveal the global molecular response and circRNA/lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA regulatory network for fat deposition in chickens (Xiao et al., 2022). The regulatory mechanism of LncRNA-FNIP2/miR-24-3p/FNIP2 axis in chicken adipogenesis was revealed by whole-transcriptome analysis (Guo et al., 2021). Su et al. utilized whole-transcriptome sequencing to reveal the molecular mechanisms underlying chicken follicular development and feathering phenotypes (Su et al., 2019).

In this study, we comprehensively compared the transcriptomes of testicular messenger RNAs (mRNAs), lncRNAs, and miRNAs between roosters exhibiting high and low sperm motility using whole-transcriptome data. An integrated analysis was performed to identify the critical genes, miRNAs, lncRNAs, pathways, and competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory networks associated with rooster sperm motility, providing important candidate genes for molecular breeding of rooster sperm motility.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics Statement

All experimental procedures and animal welfare practices were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Beijing University of Agriculture (Approval ID: BUA-zc-20200073).

Experimental Animals and Sample Preparation

In the present study, a total of seventy 35-wk-old purebred Jing Hong NO.1 layers with similar weight and genetic background were bred and provided by Beijing Huadu Yukou Poultry Co., Ltd. Company. All roosters were kept in floor pens under identical temperature and lighting conditions. Throughout the experimental period, the roosters were exposed to 16-h light cycles and given unlimited access to water and diets. The composition and nutrient content of diets was shown in Table S1. The sperm of all 70 roosters was collected and measured every other day for a total of 6 measurements. The sperm motility was assessed using a computer-assisted sperm analyzer. Three individuals with the highest (H1, H2, and H3) and lowest (L1, L2, and L3) levels of sperm motility were selected for testicular tissue collection (Table S2). The collected sperm was immediately analyzed using the Weili Color Sperm Quality Testing System (WLJY-9000, Beijing Weili New Century Science & Technology Development Co., Beijing, China). The collected samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Wuhan, China) for histomorphological observations or immediately stored in liquid nitrogen.

Semen Quality Measurements

Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) (v24.0) was used to conduct comparative analyses of sperm motility, volume, density, and effective count between the high (HSM) and low sperm motility (LSM) groups.

Histological Examination

The testicular tissues for histological analysis were fixed in a 4% PFA solution for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, then sectioned into 4 μm thick sections. Subsequently, the tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin before observation under a Nikon Eclipse E100 (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) light microscope and imaged using a Pannoramic SCAN (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) panoramic section scanner. Finally, the long diameter, short diameter, area, and circumference of varicose seminal tubules, as well as the number of mesenchymal cells per unit, were measured using Image-Pro 6.0.

RNA Isolation, Library Preparation, and Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The quality and concentration of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Implen, Westlake Village, CA) and an Agilent 2100 RNA 6000 Pico kit (Agilent, Waldbronn, Germany). For mRNA and lncRNA sequencing, rRNA-depleted RNA was prepared from 5 μg RNA from the testis. Eighteen libraries were constructed (3 biological replicates per sample) using the VAHTS mRNA-seq v2 Library Prep Kit (Vazyme NR611-02) (NEB, Ipswich, MA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) platform, using a 150 bp paired-end protocol. A total of 48.13 GB of sequencing data were obtained, containing 605M raw double-ended sequencing reads. The small RNA library construction used 3 μg of total RNA per sample and were constructed using the Illumina NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set (NEB) and sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq X-ten platform (Illumina) using a 50 bp paired-end protocol. In total, 3.81 GB of sequencing data were obtained, including 106M single-end sequencing data.

Quality Control, Alignment, and Assembly of RNA-seq Data

Raw RNA-seq reads were trimmed using Fastp v0.39 (Chen et al., 2022b) with the default parameters and then were mapped to the chicken reference genome bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/data-hub/genome/GCF_016699485.2/) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 (Kim et al., 2015) with the default settings. The resulting sam files were converted to BAM format using samtools v1.1.0 (Danecek et al., 2021), and the mapped reads were individually assembled with StringTie v2.1.7 (Pertea et al., 2015) against the same reference genome (bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b). Finally, transcripts from all samples were merged using StringTie to create a comprehensive set of transcripts.

Identification and Enrichment Analyses of Differentially Expressed mRNAs

The quantification of protein-coding genes was performed using HTseq-count v2.0.2 (Anders et al., 2015), followed by differential expression analysis of mRNAs between testes with high and low sperm motility using edgeR package v3.36.0 in R (Robinson et al., 2010). Significance thresholds were set at a P < 0.05 and an absolute value of fold change >2. The differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) (Ashburner et al., 2000) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Kanehisa, and Goto, 2000) enrichment analyses using the ClusterProfiler package v4.2.2 (Wu et al., 2021) in R. The significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were selected based on the screening criteria of P < 0.05.

Identification and Target Gene Prediction of Differentially Expressed lncRNAs and Functional Enrichment Analysis

Identifying lncRNAs followed a strict protocol, including the exclusion of transcripts with lengths less than 200 bp, exon numbers less than 2 and low-expression transcripts with read counts less than 3. The remaining transcripts were classified according to class_code = "i""j""o""u""x" and identified using CPC, v2.0 (Kang et al., 2017), LGC, v2.0 (Wang et al., 2019), and PLEK (Li et al., 2014).

The quantification of lncRNA was performed using the featureCounts software v2.0.1 (Liao et al., 2014), while differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) between testes with high and low sperm motility were detected using the edgeR package v3.36.0, with significance thresholds set at P < 0.05 and an absolute value of fold change >2. The potential cis- and trans-targeting mRNAs of the DElncRNAs were predicted based on their chromosomal locations and gene expression. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of cis- and trans-target mRNAs were conducted using the ClusterProfiler package v4.2.2 in the R program and the significant enrichment terms and pathways were selected based on P < 0.05.

Identification and Target Gene Prediction of Differentially Expressed miRNAs and Functional Enrichment Analysis

The clean reads of miRNA were aligned to the chicken reference genome (version: GRCg7b ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/016/699/485/GCF_016699485.2_bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b) using Bowtie2 v2.3.5.1 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) and the read counts of miRNA were subsequently quantified with Hetseq-count v2.0.2 (Anders et al., 2015). Differential expression analysis of miRNAs between testes with high and low sperm motility was performed using the R package edgeR (Robinson et al., 2010), where a P < 0.05, and an absolute value of log2(fold change) >1.5 (|log2FC| > 1.5) was used as significance thresholds. The target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) were predicted using MirDB v6.0 (Chen and Wang, 2020) and Targectscan v8.0 (Agarwal, et al., 2015), and the intersections of their results were selected for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, GO term and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the target mRNAs using the ClusterProfiler package v4.2.2 in the R program and the significant enrichment terms and pathways were identified according to P < 0.05.

Construction of the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA Regulatory Network

We created a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network involving lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNA using the following steps: 1) We evaluated the interactions between lncRNAs and miRNAs using miRDB v6.0. 2) We utilized both miRDB and TargetScan v8.0 to predict the relationships between miRNAs and mRNAs. 3) By using Cytoscape v3.9 (Shannon et al., 2003), we identified overlapping DElncRNAs and DEmiRNAs to generate a comprehensive lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA network. 4) Finally, we used the CytoHubba plugin to identify the hub genes.

Real-Time PCR Analysis of DEmRNAs, DElncRNAs, and DEmiRNAs

Five DEmRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs were randomly selected for real-time PCR (qPCR), separately. Following the manufacturer's protocol, 1,000 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using GoScrip Reverse Transcription System kit (Promega, Madison, WI). Individual qPCR reactions were performed using TransStart Top Green qPCR SuperMix (Trans, Beijing, China) and AriaMx Real-Time PCR (Agilent, Waldbronn, Germany). The volume of the reaction mixture was 20 μL, comprising 1.2 μL of cDNA, 0.8 μL for each primer, 10 μL of Mix, and 8 μL of sterile water. The qPCR cycling conditions were: 94°C for 30 s; 94°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s for 40 cycles; and, finally, a melting curve analysis from 65°C to 95°C. β-Actin was used as the endogenous control of lncRNA and mRNA, and U6 was used as the internal reference for miRNA. The primers were designed and synthesized by Shanghai Bioengineering Co (https://www.sangon.com) (Table S3). The quantitative results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). All qPCR assays were performed in triplicate.

Differentially Expressed miRNAs in the HSM and LSM Groups

A total of 515 miRNAs were identified in the high- and low-sperm motility groups, with high concentrations on chromosome 1. The miRNAs were mainly in the 20 to 24 nt range, with 57.58% of miRNAs concentrated at 22 nt (Figure 4A and B). After differential expression analysis (Table S10), 15 DEmiRNAs (6 downregulated and 9 upregulated in the low sperm motility group) were identified between the high and low sperm motility groups with a fold change >1.5 and P < 0.05, respectively (Figure 4C). To further investigate the biological functions of DEmiRNAs, we predicted 3,178 target genes that were potentially regulated by the DEmiRNAs. GO analysis revealed significantly enrichment of 316 GO entries (Figure 4D), including microtubule-based movement, spermatogenesis, and male gonad development, which were associated with sperm motility. DISCUSSION

Sperm motility is a crucial semen parameter for evaluating male fertility in poultry. The regulation of sperm motility involves complex mechanisms influenced by various factors, including the external environment, genes, and signaling pathways. However, the genetic regulatory mechanisms underlying rooster reproductive traits are complex and unclear. In this study, whole-transcriptome analysis was conducted to identify candidate genes and their regulation mechanisms. A total of 417 DEmRNAs, 106 DElncRNAs, and 15 DEmiRNAs were identified in the testes of the high and low sperm motility groups.

Differential gene expression is responsible for differences in sperm viability. In this study, 23,033 mRNAs and 2,893 lncRNAs were identified in chicken testes, of which 417 mRNAs and 106 lncRNAs were differentially expressed between the high and low sperm motility groups. PEBP4, PRODH, PRODH2, and STAT6 may be involved in regulating sperm viability. PEBP4 is a highly conserved secreted protein in mammals involved in regulating serine proteases (Liu et al., 2018a). PEBP4 participates in membrane remodeling during spermatogenesis and epididymal transit in buffalo spermatozoa (Patil et al., 2020). Studies have demonstrated that the abundance of PEBP4 is significantly lower in low-fertility bull spermatozoa, while it is not expressed in all infertile bulls (Somashekar et al., 2017). These findings are consistent with the results of the present study in which PEBP4 expression was downregulated in the low sperm motility group. The high polyunsaturated fatty acid content in the sperm plasma membrane renders it vulnerable to oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation (LPO) and DNA damage (Zhang et al., 2015). Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) can disrupt membrane structure and adversely affect sperm viability (Zhu et al., 2019). PRODH, an enzyme containing flavin adenine dinucleotide, initiates proline catabolism. PRODH2 is highly expressed in low-fertility buffalo sperm (Muhammad Aslam et al., 2019). Consistent with the findings of previous studies, PRODH2 expression was upregulated in the testes of roosters with low sperm motility. STAT6 is a member of the STAT transcription factor family that plays a key role in the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Activation of the JAK2/STAT6 pathway can inhibit the growth of testicular blood vessels, which, in turn, affects the normal growth of testicular tissue, resulting in decreased sperm count and motility (Hendry, 2000). In our study, STAT6 was highly expressed in the low sperm motility group, suggesting that STAT6 may negatively affect sperm motility regulation.

Among the differentially expressed miRNAs, miR-146-5p showed the highest expression level. Elevated expression of miR-146-5p may affect male fertility by promoting apoptosis and inflammatory responses (Gao et al., 2020). In addition, the expression level of miR-146b-5p is higher in the semen of individuals with low sperm motility than in normal individuals (Sakai et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2022), which is consistent with the results of this study. miR-155 is another DE miRNA with high expression. miR-155 has been reported to regulate male sexual maturation, sex hormone levels (Sakai et al., 2018), and inflammatory responses (Xu et al., 2023), thereby affecting gonadal development and sperm motility. Furthermore, male fertility is negatively correlated with miR-155 expression levels (Liu et al., 2018b). This finding is consistent with the results of the present study. In the present study, miR-137-3p was highly expressed (a log2FC of 4.76) in the testes of roosters with high sperm motility with. Studies have shown that miR-137 plays a regulatory role in canine spermatogenesis (Kasimanickam et al., 2014). Therefore, we speculated that miR-137 might play a role in spermatogenesis; however, its mechanism of action needs to be further investigated.

To further investigate the biological functions of the DEmRNAs, DEmiRNAs, and DElncRNAs, GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were performed on the DEmRNAs and their target DEmiRNAs and DElncRNAs genes. GO enrichment results showed that they were mainly enriched in signaling pathways related to energy supply, cell proliferation, differentiation, and transport, including the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, regulation of actin cytoskeleton organization, and cilium assembly. Testes are the site of spermatogenesis. Differentiation of spermatogonial stem cells, mitosis, and meiosis of germ cells frequently occur in varicoceles. Many transmembrane AC isoforms, hormones, and hormone-like substances are present at different stages of sperm maturation and are regulated by coupling with G protein receptors (GPCRs). The adhesion of seminal plasma proteins to G protein-coupled receptors plays a key role in maintaining sperm viability in chickens (Shpakov and Derkach, 2014). Sperm motility depends on the physiological structure of the axoneme and a sufficient supply of energy. The axoneme, composed of microtubules, forms cilia, shares structural similarities with the flagellum of spermatozoa, and is generated by the sliding of microtubule proteins and ATP-driven kinesin in the axoneme (Nguyen, 2019). Furthermore, actin is important in sperm capacitation, acrosome cytokinesis, and sperm motility (Breitbart and Finkelstein, 2018). The results of KEGG enrichment showed that these genes were mainly enriched in the MAPK pathway, mTOR pathway, regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, and the calcium signaling pathway. The addition of the MAPK kinase inhibitor U0126 significantly reduced chicken sperm motility parameters (Lemoine et al., 2009). Ca2+ acts as a secondary messenger in cellular signaling to regulate sperm motility by modulating the frequency of flagellar oscillations (Ashizawa et al., 1992). Extracellular Ca2+ influx elevates intracellular Ca2+ concentration, which activates the cAMP-PKA pathway (Tateno et al., 2013). Additionally, mTOR kinase is essential for the regulation of autophagy in response to oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, and low-energy nodes (Chen et al., 2019) and promotes germ cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis (Chen et al., 2021).

In the present study, we constructed a ceRNA network and identified MSTRG.3077.3/MSTRG.9085.1-gga-miR-138-5p-CADM1 and MSTRG.2290.1-gga-miR-142-3p-GNAQ/PPP3CA, which may be involved in regulating sperm motility in chicken testes. Direct interactions between germ and Sertoli cells play a crucial role in regulating spermatogenesis. MiR-138-5p regulates H2AX in pachytene spermatocytes, thereby participating in sperm development. Additionally, miR-138 promotes the generation of induced pluripotent stem cells by modulating the p53 signaling pathway (Ye et al., 2012). During sperm development, CADM1 in germ cells induces heterophilic binding to the Sertoli cells and plays a vital role in spermatogenesis (Wakayama et al., 2001). The deficiency of CADM1 in mice leads to reduced sperm motility because of disrupted contact between developing spermatids and Sertoli cells (Lui and Lee, 2009). Moreover, CADM1 affects specific molecules directly involved in the regulation of sperm motility, which may contribute to asthenospermia (Choobineh et al., 2018). In this study, MSTRG.3077.3 and MSTRG.9085.1 were predicted to regulate gga-miR-138-5p. The biological functions of MSTRG.3077.3 and MSTRG.9085.1 are not clear, and we hypothesized that MSTRG.3077.3 and MSTRG.9085.1 might be involved in the regulation of chicken sperm motility. Additionally, PPP3CA and GNAQ are predicted to be regulated by gga-miR-142-3p, which has been implicated in promoting cancer cell proliferation and may serve as a diagnostic marker for testicular cancer (Pelloni et al., 2017). Furthermore, miR-142-3p is involved in the cAMP-activated protein kinase A cAMP-PKA signaling pathway (Lv et al., 2012), which plays a crucial role in sperm motility. Additionally, miR-142-3p is involved in preserving the integrity of the blood-testis barrier, whose disruption is often associated with compromised sperm motility and elevated rates of sperm deformity (Xu et al., 2018). PPP3CA belongs to the serine/threonine protein phosphatase superfamily and is closely associated with reproductive traits. In mice, PPP3CA inactivation leads to abnormal testicular development and reduced sperm count (Chen et al., 1996). The CNV1 site of PPP3CA is significantly associated with goat sperm motility (Li et al., 2016). In sheep, GNAQ is crucial for the indirect regulation of spermatogenesis by Sertoli and epididymal duct chief cells and plays a significant role in gonadal development and sperm maturation (Pattingre et al., 2008). Within the ceRNA network, gga-miR-142-3p regulates both GNAQ and PPP3CA while being targeted by MSTRG.2290.1. However, the biological function of MSTRG.2290.1 is currently not clear. Therefore, we speculated that MSTRG.2290.1 may play a role in the regulation of chicken sperm motility.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study determined the expression patterns of mRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs in the testes of roosters with high and low sperm motility using whole-transcriptome analysis. In total, 417 mRNAs, 106 lncRNAs, and 15 miRNAs were differentially expressed between the 2 groups. The ceRNA interaction network suggested that MSTRG.3077.3/MSTRG.9085.1-gga-miR-138-5p-CADM1 and MSTRG.2290.1-gga-miR-142-3p-GNAQ/PPP3CA may play important roles in testicular development and sperm motility. However, further studies are required to confirm the involvement of these key genes in the development of rooster testes and sperm motility. In brief, this study provides insights into the function and mechanism of lncRNA-miRNA-mRNAs in the regulation of sperm motility in chicken testes, and provides important candidate genes for the molecular breeding of rooster sperm motility.
