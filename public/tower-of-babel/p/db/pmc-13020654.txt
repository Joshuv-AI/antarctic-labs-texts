Idiopathic Premature Ventricular Complexes Originating From Right Ventricular False Tendons
Authors: Jianwei Zhao, Jie Yang, Xia Wang, Jie Zheng, Bo Zhang, Yi Zhang, Yuanbin Song, Chuangye Wang, Ping Qu, Hu Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAblation for premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) originating from the right ventricular inflow tract (RVIT) is challenging. Few studies have identified the correlation between right ventricular false tendons (RVFTs) and RVIT PVCs. This study aimed to verify RVFTs as arrhythmogenic and electro‐anatomical substrates for PVCs, and propose an enlightening mapping and ablation protocol to improve operative efficacy.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 63 patients with PVCs originating from the RVIT and six were found to possess arrhythmogenic RVFTs. RVFTs were identified via the intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) imaging as fibrous or fibro‐muscular bands traversing the right ventricle cavity with no connection to valvular cusps. ICE was used to reconstruct the geometry of the right ventricle, and verify the successful ablation target. Moreover, morphological and histological studies of RVFTs from swine hearts were performed to reveal potential arrhythmogenic mechanisms.ResultsAll RVFTs were found in the free wall of the right ventricle, with a similar connection from the subvalvular myocardium to the free wall, but with different spatial orientations, shapes, lengths, and thicknesses. Reconstruction of three‐dimensional geometry with ICE was of immense benefit in terms of the location of the target spot and successful ablation. Despite variable morphology, the successful target site was consistently located at the subvalvular end of the RVFTs. Morphological and histological analysis of RVFTs from a swine heart demonstrated a component of cardiac muscles and fibrous tissues that may account for triggering activity, such as outflow tract PVCs.ConclusionRVFTs may serve as arrhythmogenic and electro‐anatomical substrates for RVIT PVCs. The “culprit” RVFTs can be identified and reconstructed in the three‐dimensional system through ICE examinations, which may improve operative efficacy.

Introduction

Premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) are one of the most common types of arrhythmias worldwide [1]. Heavy load and long‐duration PVCs lead to ventricular enlargement, systolic function impairment, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. Radiofrequency catheter ablation is an effective and reliable interventional technique for the treatment of PVCs.

Most PVCs originate from the outflow tract of the right ventricle or the left ventricle, and fewer originate from the inflow tract. Left ventricular false tendons (LVFTs) can serve as critical electro‐anatomical substrates for left ventricular arrhythmias (VAs), especially in inflow tract [2, 3, 4]. However, right ventricular false tendons (RVFTs) and their correlation with VAs have rarely been reported, although RVFTs are commonly observed in most human and animal heart specimens [5, 6, 7].

We aimed to retrospectively analyze 63 patients with PVCs originating from the right ventricular inflow tract (RVIT) and summarize six cases of verifiable RVFTs in which successful ablation was targeted under the guidance of intracardiac echocardiography (ICE). In addition, morphological and histological studies of RVFTs from swine hearts revealed potential arrhythmogenic mechanisms.

Methods

RVIT Patients

We retrospectively analyzed 63 patients with PVCs originating in the RVIT who were referred for radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) at Xinqiao Hospital, China, between 2019 and 2024. All patients were diagnosed with frequent PVCs via 24‐h Holter monitoring, with a high PVC burden ≥ 10000 beats within 24 h. Of the 63 patients, ICE was applied in 25 patients to reconstruct the geometry of the right ventricle and to identify anatomical structures such as the tricuspid annulus (TA), anterior papillary muscles (APM), and moderator band (MB). None of the patients with normal left ventricular ejection fractions (LVEF) showed any evidence of structural heart disease. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before surgery. The research reported in this paper adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013. All procedures were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Board at the Third Military Medical University (Army Medical University) (identification code: 2025‐031‐01, approved on January 2025) and conformed to the standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki.

Electrophysiologic Study and Mapping

All patients underwent RFCA under conscious sedation and antiarrhythmic drugs were discontinued ≥ 5 drug half‐lives before the operation. A 6‐F decapolar catheter (CSL; HT Medical Inc., Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, CN) was inserted into the coronary sinus via the right femoral vein. An ICE catheter (SOUNDSTAR; Biosense Webster Inc., Diamond Bar, CA) was introduced into the right atrium via the left femoral vein. All body‐surface electrocardiograms (ECGs) and intracardiac signals were displayed and stored in a digital EP system (LEAD 2000, JJDZ Inc., Chengdu, Sichuan Province, CN). The filter settings for the bipolar intracardiac electrogram was 30–600 Hz, and for unipolar intracardiac electrograms were 0.05–600 Hz.

All patients underwent routine electrophysiological examinations to rule out other arrhythmias. A three‐dimensional model of the right ventricle was constructed using a mapping system (CARTO 3; Biosense Webster Inc., Diamond Bar, CA). Isoproterenol was infused at a speed of 2–20 μg/min when there was an absence of PVCs. Activation mapping was performed during ventricular ectopy using an 8.5‐Frech saline‐irrigated ablation catheter (Thermocool Smarttouch; Biosense Webster Inc., Diamond Bar, CA). Pace mapping has been used to set infrequent PVCs or to verify ideal targets. The earliest activation site with the QS pattern in the unipolar signal was identified as the ablation target. A new activation map was created if the initial intended ablation failed.

ICE Examination and Identification of RVFTs

An ICE catheter was introduced into the right atrium to reconstruct the three‐dimensional geometry of the right ventricle and all critical anatomical structures. The ICE catheter was then carefully advanced into the right ventricle through the TA to obtain a clearer view of the RVFTs by rotating or bending the catheter (Figure 1). All contours were drawn in the ventricular diastolic phase of the sinus rhythm and the expiratory ending phase of the respiratory cycle. RVFTs were identified via the ICE imaging as fibrous or fibro‐muscular bands traversing the right ventricle cavity with no connection to valvular cusps [5, 6]. Critical anatomical structures, such as “real” tendons, TA, APM, and MB, should be distinguished from RVFTs through their morphology, spatial orientation, connections, and dynamic characteristics [5, 8]. For example, the “real” tendons were easily recognized with prominent movements in accordance with valvular leaflets, which represented a distinct difference from RVFTs.

Catheter Ablation and Definition of RVFT Origin

Radiofrequency energy was applied at the earliest activation site with a power of 30–45 W, an irrigation speed of 17–30 mL/min, and a cut‐off temperature of 43°C. For most sites where sufficient contact force (usually ≥ 5 g) was difficult to obtain, especially in the vicinity of TA, a “U” curve was created by bending the catheter to advance the tip to the subvalvular target. In some cases, a bidirectional steerable sheath (Vizigo, Biosense Webster Inc., Diamond Bar, CA) was used to achieve better contact stability. Catheter position and stability were continuously assessed using an ICE catheter. The definition of RVFT origin met the following criteria: (i) a definite RVFT was detected under the guidance of ICE; (ii) the earliest activation site was localized within a specific RVFT geometry demonstrated in the mapping system; and (iii) when ablating, the successful target was confirmed to be one end of the arrhythmogenic RVFT viewed by ICE. The pacing maneuver was performed in some cases to obtain a QRS pattern comparable to that of the PVCs for additional verification of the ideal target (Figure 2). Programmed ventricular stimulation with isoproterenol infusion was performed after ablation, and the acute successful endpoint was when no clinical PVCs could be observed for > 30 min at the end of the procedure. Successful catheter ablation met the following criteria: (i) absence of spontaneous clinical PVCs, both in the absence and presence of isoproterenol, at the end of the procedure; (ii) absence of clinical arrhythmias for over 24 h of ECG monitoring in the absence of antiarrhythmic drugs; and (iii) no recurrence of symptomatic arrhythmias in the absence of any antiarrhythmic drugs during at least 3 months of follow‐up.

Follow‐Up

All patients were followed up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. During each visit, every patient underwent 12‐lead ECGs or 24‐h Holter monitoring; 24‐h Holter monitoring must be incorporated in one of follow‐up evaluations scheduled ≥ 6 months postprocedure to quantify PVC burden. Follow‐up results were obtained through outpatient examinations or telephone interviews. None of the patients underwent treatment with antiarrhythmic drugs after RFCA.

Histologic Studies

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University. The preparation of the materials and staining methods were performed as per previous studies [9, 10, 11, 12].

Material Pretreatment

A Bama fragrant pig was anesthetized and killed before its heart was immediately removed in cool oxygenated Tyrode solution. Anatomical morphology was observed and photographed after the right ventricular free wall was incised to expose all right ventricular structures. Several RVFTs were found in the heart, and selected false tendons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate‐buffered saline for 24–48 h, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, wax impregnation, and embedding in paraffin blocks. Subsequently, sections were cut into 4–5 μm‐thick slices for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson′s trichrome, and periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) staining. MB and APM samples were also collected as controls for simultaneous staining. All sections were scanned using an inverted optical microscope (IX73; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

H&E Staining

Tissue sections were incubated at 65°C for 30 min to enhance adhesion, followed by sequential immersion in xylene (10 min each) and gradient ethanol solutions (100%, 95%, 80%; 2 min each) for complete dewaxing and hydration. After thorough rinsing with distilled water for 2 min, sections were stained with Mayer′s hematoxylin solution for 8 min. Excess stain was removed by brief rinsing in running tap water, followed by differentiation in 1% acid alcohol (HCl in 70% ethanol) for 15–30 s under microscopic monitoring. Sections were blued in 0.2% ammonia water (or saturated lithium carbonate solution) for 1 min, then rinsed in running tap water for 10 min to ensure complete color conversion. Counterstaining was performed with 0.5% eosin Y solution (in 95% ethanol) for 45 s, followed by rapid dehydration using graded ethanol series (80%, 95%, 100% I, 100% II; 2 min each). Clearing was achieved by three changes of xylene (5 min each), and the sections were mounted with neutral balsam under controlled humidity conditions.

Masson′s Trichrome Staining

Masson′s trichrome staining was used for differential visualization of collagen fibers and muscle components. The protocol proceeded as follows. Sections were deparaffinized through xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%). Nuclei were stained with Weigert′s iron hematoxylin for 8–10 min followed by differentiation in 1% acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol). Bluing was achieved by immersion in alkaline solution (0.2% ammonia water or Scott′s tap water substitute) for 1–2 min. Cytoplasmic components were stained with Biebrich scarlet acid fuchsin solution for 5–7 min. Selective collagen decolorization was performed using 1% phosphomolybdic‐phosphotungstic acid solution for 5–8 min. Collagen fibers were counterstained with 0.5% aniline blue in 2% acetic acid (or 0.2% light green SF) for 3–5 min. Sections underwent final differentiation in 1% acetic acid for 1 min. Dehydration was accomplished through ascending ethanol series (95%) followed by xylene clearing. Permanent mounting was performed using a synthetic resin medium under cover slips.

PAS Staining

PAS staining was used for glycogen visualization in the tissue sections. Following deparaffinization, sections were oxidized with 0.5%–1% periodic acid solution for 5–10 min. After thorough rinsing with distilled water, the specimens were treated with Schiff reagent (Basic Fuchsin‐pararosaniline solution) for 15–30 min. Subsequent washing in lukewarm running water facilitated the development of the characteristic magenta coloration. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using Mayer′s hematoxylin for 1–2 min. Following final dehydration using graded ethanol series (70%, 95%, 100%), the sections were cleared in xylene and mounted with synthetic resin for microscopic evaluation.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. All continuous variables were described by mean ± standard deviations (mean ± SD). Statistical differences were evaluated using analysis of variance with a significance level of p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Corp., Armonk, NY).

Electrocardiographic Characteristics

Twelve‐lead electrocardiograms (at a sweep speed of 200 mm/s) of all the patients during ventricular ectopy showed a typical left bundle branch block pattern (Figure 3). The mean QRS duration of PVCs was 163.8 ± 14.3 ms. R‐wave transition of precordial leads was mostly later than V4 (83.3%). In all patients, an R‐wave in leads I and avL and an rS wave in lead V1 were recorded. Meanwhile, a negative component (QS, qs, Qr, or qr pattern) in lead aVR and a positive component (r or R) were recorded in lead aVL, which is a typical feature of PVCs originating from the free wall of the TA [13]. Polarity of inferior leads varied in different patients due to different heights of the origin point. Furthermore, “notching” was observed in the QRS complex of all patients. The QRS patterns of PVCs originating from RVFTs appeared to be highly similar to those originating from the free wall of the TA, probably due to the highly overlapping origin of the subvalvular end of the RVFTs and TA.

Twelve‐lead electrocardiograms (at a sweep speed of 200 mm/s) of QRS patterns during ventricular ectopy.

PVCs originating from MB might have similar electrocardiographic characteristics such as a relatively late R‐wave transition. However, we can still distinguish these two based on some subtle differences in the QRS complex: (i) QRS duration in PVCs originating from MB was narrower due to rapid conduction through the right bundle branch and Purkinje system. The mean QRS duration ranged from 126.3 ms in children (n = 6) [14] and 135.0 ms (n = 12) [15] to 152.7 ms (n = 10) [16] in adults, compared with 163.8 ms in RVFT PVCs; (ii) a rapid downstroke could be observed at the onset of the precordial leads (especially in V1 and V2) in MB PVCs, while much slower downstroke was seen in RVFT PVCs [16]. What was worth pondering was that RVFTs might play the role of a “highway” in the process of ventricular depolarization in the free wall, which could possibly account for the relatively late R‐wave transition and the “notching” in most leads.

ICE and Morphological Characteristics

The three‐dimensional model of the right ventricle was reconstructed in the mapping system using an ICE catheter. The contours of all critical structures, especially RVFTs, were drawn in the ventricular diastolic phase of the sinus rhythm and the expiratory ending phase of the respiratory cycle. All RVFTs were found in the free wall of the right ventricle, with a similar connection between the subvalvular myocardium and the free wall. However, the spatial orientations, shapes, lengths, and thicknesses of the RVFTs varied in different cases (Figure 4a,b). RVFTs were classified into three types according to their morphological characteristics (Figure 5): (i) type Ⅰ represents RVFTs with normal two ends connecting subvalvular myocardium to the high free wall (Cases 2, 3 and 4); (ii) type II represents RVFTs with triple connection points (Cases 1 and 6); and (iii) type III represents irregularly shaped RVFTs with multiple connection points (Case 5).

Mapping Results and Ablation Strategies

Activation mapping was performed in the right ventricle when spontaneous PVCs occurred. The earliest activation site with a QS pattern in the unipolar signal was identified as the target site. The mean local activation time preceding the QRS complex was 22.3 ± 4.8 ms. A new activation map was created if the initial intended ablation failed. Coincidentally, all target sites in the six patients were located at the subvalvular end (Table 2). Successful target sites were validated by ICE as part of the RVFTs. Simultaneously, catheter position and stability were continuously assessed using an ICE catheter (Figure 6). Pace mapping was used to set infrequent PVCs or to verify the best target. An example of an RVFT‐related PVCs was shown in Figure 2. In this case, the RVFT was long, slim, and connected the subvalvular myocardium to the high free wall. The successful target was located at the subvalvular end, with an analogous stimulative QRS pattern corresponding to PVCs.

Complications and Follow‐Up

The mean follow‐up duration was 6 ± 2.2 months. The first‐operative and overall success rates were 90% and 100%, respectively. One patient (Case 6) had recurrent PVCs 2 months postoperatively and underwent a repeat procedure. The mean PVC burden was reduced from 27.3% ± 8.1% to 3.6% ± 4.0% within 24 h. No complications occurred during the periprocedural period. No worsening of the tricuspid valve insufficiency was detected in any patient postoperatively.

Histologic Studies

To reveal the possible arrhythmogenic mechanism, we performed histological studies on the RVFTs sectioned from a Bama fragrant pig (Figure 7). After incision of the free wall into the right ventricle, several RVFTs showed various connections. These slim, branch‐like tendons prevent the ventricle from overdilating. At the same time, mechanical stretching of these false tendons might be the “culprit” of VAs. Histological examination demonstrated that the RVFTs were composed of myocardium and fibrous tissues, and no Purkinje cells were found in the slices in our study.

These findings might not be compelling evidence for the components of RVFTs due to the limited sample volume, but rather an inspiration for arrhythmogenesis in a sense. Our study confirmed the presence of muscle and fibrous cells in RVFTs and their longitudinal arrangement, along which activation propagated. RVFTs may act as triggers for PVCs due to mechanical movement within the cardiac cycle, which is similar to the arrhythmogenic mechanism of outflow tract PVCs. However, further studies are needed to determine the exact mechanism of the trigger activity related to false tendons.

Discussion

We summarized a series of RFCA cases of PVCs originating from RVFTs under ICE guidance, which highlights successful ablation for RVIT PVCs. All RVFTs were found in the free wall of the right ventricle, with a similar connection from the subvalvular myocardium to the free wall, but with different spatial orientations, shapes, lengths, and thicknesses. Reconstruction of three‐dimensional geometry with ICE was of immense benefit in terms of the location of the target spot and successful ablation. Despite variable morphology, the successful target site was consistently located at the subvalvular end of the RVFTs, where the “U” curve manipulation technique was remarkably effective in increasing catheter stability. In addition, morphological and histological studies of RVFTs from a swine heart were performed, demonstrating a component of myocardium and fibrous tissues that may account for triggering activity, such as outflow tract PVCs.

Although prevalence of RVFTs is reported to be high in most human and animal heart specimens, from 35% (n = 100) [6] to 100% (n = 100) [5] in a human cadaveric study, and 100% in canines [7], we identified an incidence of 11/25 (44%) using ICE catheter; only six (28%) of our patients were confirmed to be arrhythmogenic. The reason for the lower incidence rate in our study can be explained by the following aspects. First, not all RVFTs can be identified via ICE because some of them are too slim or too short to be detected, even though we carefully scanned every layer of the right ventricle from different angles of view. Second, the spatial axis of the RVFTs varies in the right ventricular cavity [7]. When the ultrasound sector is not coaxial with the long axis of the RVFTs, it may present as a tiny white spot in the image, which is easily ignored. Third, the image resolution and quality may affect the detection rate.

In previous research, Loukas et al. classified five types of RVFTs according to their connections at the two ends.[6] However, we identified only one type of RVFT that connected the subvalvular myocardium to the free wall. Nevertheless, no definite RVFTs were found connecting the interventricular septum in these six cases, as well as in other cases where the ICE catheter was used during the procedure. A PVC originating from the septal side of the right ventricle may suggest a different arrhythmogenic mechanism. RVFTs differ in morphology between patients, with various spatial orientations, shapes, lengths, and thicknesses, making them difficult to detect using an ICE catheter.

LVFTs have been widely reported in previous echocardiographic, morphological, and histological studies. There are three types of LVFTs according to histopathology: fibrous type, fibro‐muscular type, and muscular type [10]. In our study, a fibro‐muscular type of RVFT was found; however, further studies are needed for more comprehensive evidence. Histologic examinations of RVFTs in several studies have revealed a random component of myocardial fibers and conductive tissue, including Purkinje fibers [5, 6, 7]. The contribution of this conductive tissue may be critical in electrophysiological features in two aspects. First, PVCs are caused due to abnormal automatic activities or mechanical stretching of the ventricle. Second, aberration is caused in the QRS patterns of PVCs, as activation propagates bidirectionally based on the conducting tissues. For instance, the R‐wave transition of the precordial leads in Case 2 was significantly later than that in the other cases because the false tendon was not located near the TV, but was present in the middle ventricular cavity. This means that routine prediction of the target site according to QRS patterns may lose efficacy.

All RVFTs have a subventricular connection, which is a challenge for ablation because of inadequate catheter stability and contact. Debasish et al. [17] reported a retrograde catheter approach that could achieve better catheter stability and contact, with good success and outcomes. Yang et al. [18] demonstrated a similar catheter manipulation technique using a combination of a steerable sheath and a contact force‐sensing catheter. Earlier local activation times were recorded with the retrograde approach than with the anterograde approach. Consistent with previous studies, no double or fractionated potentials were found in the target area. From the perspective of traditional cognition, the best ablation target is underneath the muscular enfoldment, with a prevalence of local atrial electrograms. In contrast to previous studies on target potentials, a new discovery in our study is that atrial electrograms are not a mandatory feature of a successful target, because atrial electrograms could only be detected in two of our cases. A possible reason for this is that the subvalvular end of the RVFTs does not always extend deep into the enfoldment of the TA.

Our study had some limitations. First, this was a retrospective study and the number of patients was small. Second, RVFTs may have a higher prevalence in PVC cases originating from the RVIT because of the limitations of ICE in terms of resolution. Third, although we performed histological studies using swine hearts, the actual cellular components of RVFTs need to be explored in future studies. Finally, the arrhythmogenic mechanism of RVFTs remains unknown.

Conclusions

RVFTs may provide arrhythmogenic and electro‐anatomical substrates for RVIT PVCs. The “culprit” RVFTs can be identified and reconstructed in the three‐dimensional system through careful ICE examinations, which may improve operative efficacy. However, further studies are required to determine the arrhythmogenic mechanism and electrophysiological significance.
