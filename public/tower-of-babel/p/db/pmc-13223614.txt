The association of the Healthy Lifestyle Index (HLI) and HLI trajectories with risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality: a GRADE-assessed dose-response systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Minoo HasanRashedi, Mostafa Norouzzadeh, Zahra Gohari Dezfuli, Sanaz Jamshidi, Fatemeh Mehdipoor, Seyed Mojtaba Ghoreishy, Farshad Teymoori, Farzad Shidfar, Ramin Eskandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundThe Healthy Lifestyle Index (HLI) integrates key behaviours to assess their cumulative impact on health. While higher HLI adherence is linked to lower disease and mortality risk, its long-term trajectory association remains understudied. This study aims to examine the dose-response relationship and long-term association of HLI on mortality risks.DesignSystematic review and dose-response meta-analysis using the Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.Data sourcesPubMed, Scopus and Web of Science were searched until June 2024.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesWe included observational cohort studies that assessed the relationship of HLI or its trajectories with all-cause, cardiovascular disease (CVD)-cause or cancer-cause of mortality.ResultAnalysis of 13.7 million participants demonstrated that higher adherence to the HLI is linked to lower risk of all-cause (HR: 0.48; 95% CI 0.46 to 0.53; GRADE: moderate), CVD-cause (HR: 0.49; 95% CI 0.44 to 0.51; GRADE: moderate) and cancer-cause mortality (HR: 0.55; 95% CI 0.49 to 0.61; GRADE: low). These associations were further confirmed in a dose-response manner. Moreover, compared with maintaining an unhealthy lifestyle, a decline in HLI adherence was associated with a 14% higher risk of all-cause and a 19% higher risk of cancer-related mortality. In contrast, an improvement in HLI adherence was linked to a 20% lower risk of all-cause and a 13% lower risk of cancer-related mortality.ConclusionAdherence to HLI and its long-term patterns are associated with lower mortality risk. These findings emphasise the importance of lifestyle-based prevention and intervention strategies in reducing mortality.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024500538.Keywords: Review, Cardiovascular Disease, Quality of Life, Mortality, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of mortality with over 40 million deaths annually.1 The WHO Sustainable Development Goal aims to reduce NCDs mortality by 30% up to 2030 to address this burden.1 Maintaining a healthy lifestyle is increasingly recognised as a fundamental strategy for reducing the global burden of NCDs on mortality.2 Effective prevention and control of NCDs require targeted management of modifiable risk factors, particularly lifestyle behaviours.3 4

Lifestyle modification is a cornerstone in the prevention and management of NCDs.5 6 However, the development of effective policies to implement lifestyle interventions requires validated tools for risk assessment, accurate weighting of individual lifestyle components within composite indices, and robust evidence demonstrating their predictive value for health-related outcomes.5

Epidemiological and nutritional evidence indicates that the combined assessment of multiple risk factors predicts health outcomes more accurately than evaluation of individual factors in isolation.7 8 The Healthy Lifestyle Index (HLI) integrates key modifiable risk factors for NCDs, including diet, physical activity, adiposity and sleep status.9 10 Although some studies incorporate additional variables, these components represent the core domains of the HLI.11 12

Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated the predictive value of the HLI for mortality among individuals with cancer13 and diabetes.14 15 However, its association with all-cause and cause-specific mortality has not been comprehensively evaluated. For example, a pairwise meta-analysis by Zhang et al reported that greater adherence to the HLI was associated with lower cancer incidence and mortality.13

Although several original studies14,17 have proposed cut-off points for the HLI based on population-specific data, no systematic evaluation has established a standardised or universally applicable threshold. Furthermore, lifestyle behaviours may change over time due to individual choices or environmental influences, yet limited evidence exists regarding the ability of the HLI to capture such changes and their association with mortality risk. Assessment of longitudinal changes in HLI, referred to as HLI trajectories, may provide important insights into the dynamic nature of health behaviours and their potential role in mitigating disease progression.18

Accordingly, we conducted a comprehensive dose-response meta-analysis to evaluate the associations between the HLI and mortality. In addition, we examined the relationship between HLI trajectories and mortality risk and sought to identify an evidence-based cut-off point to distinguish high-risk and low-risk groups. Our findings aim to strengthen the evidence base supporting lifestyle modification as a central public health strategy for reducing mortality.

Material and methods

Systematic search

We conducted a systematic search in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science until June 2024 using relevant keywords (online supplementary table 1). To enhance search sensitivity, we performed a manual search and adhered to the Meta-analysis of the Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist19 (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42024500538).

Study selection

Eligible studies met the following criteria: (1) prospective observational design, (2) assessment of HLI as a single score rather than individual lifestyle factors, (3) inclusion of adult participants (>18 years) and (4) reporting of at least one mortality outcome (all-cause, cardiovascular or cancer-related death) in terms of OR, relative risk (RR) or HR with 95% CI.

Data extraction

Two independent authors extracted study details, including author name, publication year, study design, population demographics, sample size, follow-up duration, effect size measures, HLI assessment, adjusted covariates and HLI trajectory trends (improvement/deterioration), with a third author verifying data accuracy.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool,20 which assesses seven potential bias domains, including confounding, participant selection, exposure measurement and outcome assessment. Certainty of the evidence was assessed following the Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation tool,21 categorising studies as high, moderate, low or very low based on factors such as risk of bias, heterogeneity, indirectness and publication bias. A detailed explanation of the quality assessment methodology can be found in our previous work.22

Data synthesis and analysis

All analyses used STATA software V.17. The effect sizes used for this meta-analysis were HR with 95% CI of highest versus lowest category of HLI and pooled according to DerSimonian and Laird’s random-effects model.23 Studies reported HRs, RRs or ORs; HR was used as the common metric, with RRs/ORs treated as equivalent under the rare-event assumption.24 25 Also, the Hamling method was used when the reference category was not the lowest.26 The heterogeneity between studies was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and quantified by I2 statistic.27 We also performed subgroup analyses based on the following factors for each outcome: the study location, follow-up duration, number of HLI factors, HLI calculation method (whether it involved diet or body mass index (BMI)) and population health condition (diabetic or non-diabetic). For dose-response analysis, we used the generalised least squares trend estimation for linear trends28 29 and restricted cubic splines for non-linear trends.30 Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test31 (p<0.1) and funnel plots, while sensitivity analysis evaluated the influence of each study by sequentially omitting them. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Study selection

A systematic search identified 1187 articles. After duplicate removal and two screening stages, 90 eligible articles met the eligibility criteria. Among these, 82 examined HLI and mortality, 9 focused on HLI trajectories and mortality and 1 study addressed both (figure 1).

The PRISMA flow diagram for literature search and selection. HLI, Healthy Lifestyle Index. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Study characteristics

As shown in online supplementary table 2, this meta-analysis included 73 articles (82 studies) assessing all-cause mortality, 36 articles (42 studies) evaluating cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality and 39 articles (47 studies) examining cancer-related mortality in relation to the HLI and involved approximately 13 775 979 male and female participants, with a mean age ranging from 35 to 82 years. The follow-up period ranged from 1.1 to 33.2 median years, and the study population included both healthy and unhealthy individuals. Furthermore, studies evaluating HLI trajectories and their association with mortality included eight studies on all-cause, two on CVD-related and one on cancer-related. In total, 285 119 participants, with a mean age of 52.8 years, were included, representing either the general healthy population or individuals with CVD or diabetes. The participants were followed for an average of 8.53 years. Data for calculating the HLI were collected through questionnaires and interviews.

In addition, HLI was assessed using questionnaire and interview data, incorporating key lifestyle factors, with additional factors like sedentary time and sleep duration considered when available. Also, HLI trajectories were categorised by comparing sustained HLI (as the reference) with HLI improvement and deterioration. Mortality events were determined through multiple sources, including national registries, medical records, death certificates and family interviews.

Meta-analysis of Healthy Lifestyle Index and mortality risk

All-cause mortality

As shown in table 1, highest adherence to HLI was linked to a 51% lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with the lowest (HR: 0.48; 95% CI 0.46 to 0.53; I2: 98.6). Also, a significant non-linear relationship was observed (figure 2, p-non-linearity <0.001), with an inflection point occurring within an HLI score range of 4.4–4.5, as shown in online supplementary table 3. Meanwhile, the linear analysis demonstrated a strong association (p-dose response <0.001), indicating that each 1-unit increase in HLI was associated with a 16% reduction in all-cause mortality risk (HR: 0.84; 95% CI 0.83 to 0.86), as presented in table 2.

CVD-cause mortality

Based on the pooled results studies examining the association between the highest versus lowest HLI and the risk of CVD-related mortality, we found a significant 52% lower risk of CVD mortality (HR: 0.49; 95% CI 0.44 to 0.51; I2: 74.5), as indicated in table 1. Furthermore, a significant non-linear relationship was observed within an HLI score range of 3.2–3.3 (figure 2, p-non-linearity <0.001), as presented in online supplementary table 3. Meanwhile, the linear analysis demonstrated that each 1-unit increase in HLI was associated with a 17% reduction in CVD mortality risk (HR: 0.83; 95% CI 0.79 to 0.87; p-dose response <0.001), as shown in table 2.

Cancer-cause mortality

Table 1 shows that the highest adherence to HLI, compared with the lowest, was associated with a 45% lower risk of cancer mortality (HR: 0.55; 95% CI 0.49 to 0.61; I2: 96.6). The dose-response analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship between HLI and cancer mortality (figure 2, p-non-linearity=0.004) within an HLI score range of 2.6–2.7, as presented in online supplementary table 3. Additionally, table 2 indicated a linear association, showing that each 1-unit increase in HLI was associated with 15% lower cancer mortality risk (HR: 0.85; 95% CI 0.83 to 0.87; p-dose response <0.001).

Meta-analysis of Healthy Lifestyle Index trajectories and mortality risk

All-cause mortality

The meta-analysis of five studies revealed that a deterioration in HLI adherence, compared with maintaining an unhealthy lifestyle, is associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.14; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.29; I2: 76.0). Additionally, the pooled results of eight studies showed that improving HLI adherence, compared with sustaining an unhealthy lifestyle, was associated with a 20% lower risk of mortality from any cause (HR: 0.80; 95% CI 0.71 to 0.90; I2: 69.8), as shown in table 1.

Cardiovascular disease-cause mortality

The pooled results of two studies, illustrated in table 1, showed that lifestyle deterioration, compared with sustaining an unhealthy lifestyle, was not significantly associated with risk of CVD mortality (HR: 1.16; 95% CI 0.55 to 2.46; I2: 81.0). Similarly, improvement in HLI adherence, compared with sustaining an unhealthy lifestyle, also didn’t show a significant association with CVD mortality risk (HR: 0.65; 95% CI 0.38 to 1.10; I2: 69.6).

Cancer-cause mortality

The result of a single study on cancer mortality represented the direct association between HLI deterioration (HR 1.19; 95% CI 1.09 to 1.29) and HLI improvement (HR 0.87; 95% CI 0.82 to 0.92) versus sustaining unhealthy with risk of cancer-cause mortality, as presented in table 1.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis on the association between HLI and the risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality, as presented in online supplementary table 4. Whether diet was included in the HLI calculation and the health condition of participants were identified as significant sources of heterogeneity for all-cause mortality risk (p-between <0.05). When diet was included in the HLI calculation (HR: 0.52; 95% CI 0.47 to 0.57) or the population was limited to (pre)diabetic individuals (HR: 0.56; 95% CI 0.51 to 0.61), the reduction in all-cause mortality risk was smaller compared with other subgroups.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

We conducted a sensitivity analysis on the association between HLI and mortality risk, which confirmed the robustness of our main findings. Excluding individual cohorts did not significantly alter the results (RR change for all-cause and CVD-related mortality: 0.48–0.52; RR change for cancer-related mortality: 0.62–0.66).

We also evaluated the potential for publication bias in the association between HLI and mortality risk using Egger’s test (table 1) and corresponding funnel plots (online supplemental figure 1). The analysis suggested some degree of publication bias for all outcomes related to HLI assessment (p<0.1).

Risk of bias assessment

As shown in online supplementary table 5, all included cohorts were classified as having a moderate risk of bias, primarily due to confounding factors and HLI assessment method. However, all studies were rated as having a low risk of bias in outcome measurement and reporting.

Certainty assessment of evidence

According to online supplementary table 6, the certainty of evidence for all-cause and CVD-related mortality associated with HLI was rated as moderate, based on large and significant effect sizes and a clear dose-response relationship, despite the potential for publication bias. Conversely, the certainty of evidence for cancer-related mortality was rated as low, as the dose-response relationship could not mitigate the downgrading effects of detected publication bias, considerable heterogeneity and risk of bias. For HLI trajectories, the certainty of evidence for all outcomes was rated as low, primarily due to substantial heterogeneity and risk of bias.

Discussion

The current meta-analysis examined the association between the HLI and its mortality trajectories. Our study indicates that adherence to HLI is associated with a reduction, particularly in a linear manner, in all-cause, CVD and cancer-related mortality. Our analysis also revealed that HLI trajectories are associated with both all-cause and cancer-related mortality.

The HLI, which includes modifiable risk factors such as diet, cigarette smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity and BMI, is used to investigate the relationship between a healthy lifestyle and the risk of different chronic diseases and all cancer types.11 18 32 To illustrate, in the present study, HLI and HLI trajectories were associated with decreased risk of all-cause mortality. In this context, Hu and colleagues33 found that an increased number of healthy lifestyle dimensions correlates with a reduced mortality risk. Furthermore, adherence to a lifestyle pattern that emphasises non-smoking and moderate alcohol consumption is associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality.33 After evaluating participants aged ≥20 years of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the risk of all-cause mortality was diminished by 47% (95% CI 36% to 57%) for those who engaged in regular physical activity, by 56% (95% CI 35% to 70%) for adults who did not smoke and by 26% (95% CI 4% to 42%) for adults who adhered to a healthy diet.17 Similarly, based on the Ding et al16 cohort study, adopting healthy lifestyle practices may lower the overall mortality risk. Certain combinations of lifestyle-related risk behaviours may pose more significant dangers, indicating potential synergistic interactions among these risk factors.16 These results underscore the importance of adopting a multidimensional approach to lifestyle when formulating health promotion strategies. Also, over a follow-up period of up to 32 years, a total of 30 013 deaths were recorded, which included 10 808 fatalities due to cancer and 7189 attributed to CVDs. Individuals who maintained one or more healthy lifestyle factors exhibited a markedly reduced risk of overall mortality, as well as mortality from cardiovascular issues and cancer, compared with those who did not engage in any low-risk lifestyle practices.34 It should be mentioned that the participants in the mentioned cohort were exclusively health professionals, which may restrict the generalisability of the results.

We also indicated that there is a negative association between HLI and CVD-related mortality. The previous meta-analysis demonstrated that the simultaneous adherence to multiple healthy lifestyle practices was linked to a 66% decrease in the risk of CVDs compared with individuals who engaged in none or only one such behaviour.35 In addition, among postmenopausal women who had a normal BMI, commitment to a healthy lifestyle correlates with a diminished risk of clinical CVD and their subtypes. This highlights the cardiovascular advantages of sustaining a healthy lifestyle, even for those women who maintain a healthy weight.36 Additionally, another study emphasises the prevention effect of a prompted healthy lifestyle against the primordial incidence of CVDs, among young women.37 Based on the study by Li et al,38 a healthier lifestyle was found to correlate with a reduced risk of cancer, CVDs and diabetes, as well as lower mortality rates. This lifestyle choice is linked to increased life expectancy and more years lived without these diseases. This research indicated that encouraging a healthy lifestyle could alleviate healthcare burdens by decreasing the likelihood of developing various chronic conditions and enhancing the duration of life free from illness.38 Hu et al33 observed no significant relationship between HLI and cardiovascular mortality risk, contrasting earlier studies that indicated a negative correlation between healthy lifestyles and CVD mortality. This finding might originate from a short follow-up duration, leading to fewer death cases.33

According to our findings, HLI and HLI trajectories were associated with cancer types of mortality. In this regard, research by Peila et al indicated that women in the upper quartiles of the HLI exhibited a lower risk of developing pancreatic cancer when compared with those in the lowest quartile.39 Another study claimed that participants categorised in the healthiest HLI group exhibited a reduced risk of renal cell cancer (RCC) compared with those in the unhealthiest HLI category (HR=0.79, 95%CI 0.56 to 1.10, p for trend 0.045). However, an increase of one SD (±3 units) in the HLI score did not show a statistically significant correlation with a decreased RCC risk (HR=0.92, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.01). The strength of this association improved when diet or alcohol was excluded from the score, although the CIs remained overlapping.40 The findings of the Li et al study provide substantial evidence in favour of the hypothesis that maintaining a healthy lifestyle can lower the risk of developing breast cancer and enhance survival rates following a breast cancer diagnosis, particularly among postmenopausal women.41 In addition, Buckland et al42 claimed that calculations of population attributable risk included 18.8% of all gastric cancer (GC) cases, and 62.4% of cardia GC cases could have been averted if individuals had adhered to healthy lifestyles. Embracing multiple HLI, such as encouraging smoking cessation, moderating alcohol intake, adhering to a nutritious diet abundant in fruits, vegetables and whole grains while being low in saturated and trans fats, and sustaining a normal weight, is linked to a significantly reduced risk of developing GC.42

The evidence underscoring the significance of a HLI as an integral component of wellness programmes and interventions for the primary and secondary prevention of diseases is robust, persuasive and continually expanding. Substantial epidemiological data suggest that engaging in 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity per week, consuming a diet rich in fruits and vegetables, avoiding tobacco use and managing stress levels can significantly diminish the risk of morbidity and mortality, particularly when combined with the appropriate pharmacological treatment of major risk factors such as hypertension and dyslipidaemia. It should be noted that the effectiveness of adhering to a healthy lifestyle is likely to be maximised when initiated early in life.43

Our meta-analysis presents several strengths and limitations. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first meta-analysis to assess the relationship between HLI trajectories and mortality risk and a dose-response analysis was conducted. Moreover, we did not limit the participants based on health conditions and included individuals with varying health problems. Finally, we employed robust and comprehensive statistical methods. However, this study also possesses some significant limitations. The selection of potential confounding variables varied among the studies, complicating the ability to eliminate the possibility of residual confounding, a consequence of the nature of observational studies. Additionally, the presence of suspected publication bias represents another limitation.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis confirms that higher HLI adherence significantly reduces all-cause, CVD-related and cancer-related mortality risks. Sustained or improved adherence further lowers mortality risk, underscoring the importance of long-term lifestyle modifications. These findings support integrating trajectory-based approaches into public health strategies to promote sustained healthy behaviours and reduce chronic disease burden.
