Cumulative anticholinergic burden is associated with lower clozapine metabolic ratios: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Selçuk Özdin, Ömer Böke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClozapine is the most effective treatment for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, yet substantial interindividual variability in serum concentrations complicates dose optimization and therapeutic drug monitoring. While factors such as smoking and inflammation are known to influence clozapine metabolism, a substantial portion of the variability remains unexplained. Anticholinergic burden, reflecting cumulative exposure to anticholinergic medications, may be a clinically relevant yet underexplored factor associated with pharmacokinetic variability.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 93 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders according to DSM-5 and receiving clozapine treatment at a tertiary psychiatric center. Serum clozapine and norclozapine concentrations were obtained under routine clinical conditions and used to calculate clozapine metabolic ratios, including the concentration-to-dose (C/D) ratio (serum concentration divided by daily dose) and the clozapine/norclozapine (C/N) ratio (serum clozapine concentration divided by serum norclozapine concentration). Anticholinergic burden was quantified using the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden calculator. Clinical variables, including age, sex, smoking status, C-reactive protein, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, were extracted from medical records. Associations between clinical variables and clozapine metabolic ratios were assessed using Spearman correlation analyses, and factors independently associated with metabolic ratios were identified using multivariate linear regression models.ResultsThe mean anticholinergic burden score was 2.67 ± 2.02, and 52.7% of patients had clinically significant anticholinergic burden (≥ 3). Higher anticholinergic burden was significantly associated with lower concentration-to-dose (r = − 0.349, p = 0.001) and clozapine/norclozapine ratios (r = − 0.429, p < 0.001). In multivariate regression analyses, anticholinergic burden remained independently associated with lower clozapine/norclozapine (β = −0.400, p < 0.001) and concentration-to-dose ratios (β = −0.334, p = 0.001). Smoking was independently associated with lower concentration-to-dose ratio (β = −0.230, p = 0.038), but not with clozapine/norclozapine ratio. Other variables, including age, sex, C-reactive protein abnormality, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, were not significant predictors.ConclusionsCumulative anticholinergic burden was independently associated with lower clozapine metabolic ratios in routine clinical practice. These findings suggest that anticholinergic burden may serve as a clinically accessible and practical indicator associated with variability in clozapine exposure and may aid in the interpretation of therapeutic drug monitoring. Background

Clozapine remains the most effective pharmacological treatment for treatment-resistant schizophrenia [1]; however, substantial interindividual variability in serum concentrations complicates dose optimization and reinforces the need for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) [2, 3]. Both subtherapeutic and supratherapeutic clozapine levels are associated with adverse clinical outcomes, underscoring the importance of identifying clinical factors that contribute to pharmacokinetic variability. In routine practice, the concentration-to-dose (C/D) ratio is commonly used as an indicator of overall drug exposure. In contrast the clozapine/norclozapine (C/N) ratio may provide additional insight into metabolic balance and pathway dynamics [4, 5].

Several clinical and biological variables have been shown to influence clozapine metabolism [6, 7]. Cigarette smoking is one of the most consistently reported determinants, primarily through induction of cytochrome P450 1A2 (CYP1A2), leading to increased metabolic clearance and reduced clozapine exposure. Inflammatory states have also been associated with altered clozapine levels [8, 9], potentially via modulation of hepatic enzymatic activity. Despite these established contributors, a considerable proportion of variability in clozapine metabolic indices remains unexplained in routine clinical settings, suggesting that additional clinical factors may be involved.

Patients receiving clozapine are frequently exposed to multiple concomitant medications, many of which possess anticholinergic properties [10, 11]. One reason is that an anticholinergic effect is needed to manage the side effects associated with clozapine. Specifically, anticholinergic agents are frequently co-prescribed with clozapine to manage clozapine-induced hypersalivation (sialorrhea), which is a common and distressing side effect [12]. The cumulative anticholinergic burden (ACB) has been widely studied in relation to cognitive impairment, delirium risk, and overall treatment tolerability in psychiatric populations [13–15]. However, its potential association with variability in clozapine pharmacokinetic indices has not been well characterized. As ACB may reflect broader patterns of polypharmacy and pharmacological complexity, it is plausible that cumulative ACB could be associated with differences in clozapine exposure or metabolic balance.

The present study aimed to explore clinical determinants of variability in clozapine metabolic ratios (C/N and C/D ratios) in routine clinical practice. In addition to established factors such as smoking status and inflammatory markers, we specifically evaluated the association between cumulative ACB and interindividual variability in C/N and C/D ratios. To our knowledge, the potential association between cumulative ACB and clozapine metabolic ratios has not been systematically examined in previous clinical studies.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cross-sectional study included 93 patients diagnosed with disorders classified under the DSM-5 category “Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders” who were receiving clozapine treatment at a tertiary psychiatric center. Patient selection is summarized in Fig. 1. Of the 159 eligible patients identified from hospital records, 66 were excluded due to missing laboratory data (n = 44) or clinical data (n = 22). The study protocol was approved by the Ondokuz Mayıs University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (OMU KAEK; approval number 2026/9). Due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized clinical data, informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional regulations.

Flow chart of patient selection

Patients were eligible if they had been receiving a stable clozapine dose for at least one week prior to serum sampling, consistent with routine TDM practices. While repeated measurements were available for some individuals, a single serum determination was selected for each patient. The selection was based on the availability of a complete clinical and laboratory dataset (e.g., ACB, smoking status, CRP) corresponding precisely to the time of serum sampling to ensure the integrity of the multivariate analysis. Medication adherence was not formally assessed using a standardized instrument. Information regarding adherence was obtained from routine clinical follow-up records, including patient and caregiver reports documented in the medical charts. Blood samples were obtained under routine clinical conditions and confirmed to represent trough levels. All patients in the study were receiving clozapine twice daily, and samples were collected approximately 12 h after the evening dose.

Clinical and laboratory measures

Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, smoking status, and clozapine daily dose (mg/day), were obtained from medical records. Smoking status was recorded as a dichotomous variable (current smoker vs. non-smoker) based on documentation at the time of serum measurement. Smoking intensity (e.g., number of cigarettes per day) was not available and therefore could not be included in the analyses.

Serum clozapine and norclozapine concentrations were measured as part of routine TDM using high-performance liquid chromatography. The C/D ratio was calculated by dividing the serum clozapine concentration (ng/mL) by the daily clozapine dose (mg/day). The C/N ratio was calculated by dividing serum clozapine concentration (ng/mL) by norclozapine concentration (ng/mL).

Assessment of anticholinergic burden

Anticholinergic burden was assessed using the ACB Calculator [16]. This evidence-based online tool integrates and harmonizes scores from the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) Scale [17] and the German Anticholinergic Burden Scale (GABS) [18]. The ACB calculator was selected due to its widespread use and established clinical relevance in psychiatric populations. The ACB calculator assigns scores ranging from 0 to 3 to individual medications according to their documented anticholinergic activity (0 = none, 1 = possible, 2 = definite, 3 = marked anticholinergic effect). Medications with an ACB score ≥ 1 were summarized descriptively and are presented in Supplementary Table 1. For each patient, the total ACB score was calculated by summing the scores of all regularly prescribed medications at the time of serum sampling.

ACB was analyzed as a continuous variable in regression models. Patients were additionally categorized into low ACB (ACB < 3) and clinically significant ACB (ACB ≥ 3), as cumulative ACB scores of 3 or greater have been associated with clinically meaningful anticholinergic effects and adverse cognitive outcomes in previous studies [19].

Inflammatory markers

CRP levels were obtained from routine laboratory assessments. Due to data availability, CRP was recorded as a dichotomous variable (normal vs. abnormal) according to institutional laboratory reference ranges (0–5 mg/L).

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated by dividing the absolute neutrophil count by the absolute lymphocyte count, derived from complete blood count parameters obtained on the same day as the clozapine serum measurement. NLR is considered a readily available marker of systemic inflammation and has been widely used as an indicator of inflammatory activity in both medical and psychiatric research [20, 21]. NLR was analyzed as a continuous variable.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Unadjusted comparisons of C/D and C/N ratios across clinical subgroups (sex, smoking status, CRP abnormality, and ACB category) were performed using independent samples t-tests or nonparametric equivalents where appropriate. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Correlations between continuous clinical variables and metabolic ratios were assessed using Spearman’s rho. The correlation between ACB and clozapine daily dose was also examined. Multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted to identify clinical factors associated with variability in C/N and C/D ratios. For the C/N model, the following independent variables were entered simultaneously: age, sex, clozapine daily dose, smoking status, CRP abnormality, and cumulative ACB. For the C/D model, age, sex, smoking status, CRP abnormality, and cumulative ACB were included as predictors. Clozapine dose was not included in the C/D model to avoid mathematical coupling, as the C/D ratio already incorporates dose in its calculation. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated to assess multicollinearity; values below 2 were considered acceptable. Regression diagnostics were performed to evaluate influential observations. Standardized residuals, Cook’s distance, and leverage values were examined to ensure model stability. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 93 patients receiving clozapine treatment were included in the study. The mean age was 39.7 ± 11.8 years, and 53.8% were male. The mean daily clozapine dose was 352 ± 135 mg/day. Current smoking was present in 45.2% of patients, and CRP abnormality was observed in 19.4%. The mean NLR was 2.4 ± 0.9. Mean serum clozapine concentration was 635 ± 325 ng/mL, and mean norclozapine concentration was 319 ± 170 ng/mL. The mean C/D ratio was 1.91 ± 0.89, and the mean C/N ratio was 2.16 ± 0.87. The mean total ACB score was 2.67 ± 2.02. Detailed descriptive characteristics are presented in Table 1. The medications contributing to ACB scores included a broad range of psychotropic and non-psychotropic agents, particularly antidepressants and antipsychotics, along with centrally acting anticholinergic agents such as biperiden (Supplementary Table 1). Strongly anticholinergic agents such as amitriptyline and biperiden constituted a substantial proportion of the overall ACB score.

Unadjusted comparisons of metabolic ratios across clinical subgroups are presented in Table 2. Smokers demonstrated significantly lower C/D ratios compared to non-smokers (p = 0.002) and lower C/N ratios (p = 0.047). Approximately half of the sample (52.7%) met criteria for clinically significant ACB (ACB ≥ 3). Patients with clinically significant ACB demonstrated significantly lower C/D and C/N ratios compared with those with lower burden (ACB < 3) (both p < 0.001). No significant differences in C/D or C/N ratios were observed according to sex or CRP abnormality (all p > 0.05).

Correlation analyses

Spearman correlation analyses are shown in Table 3. The relationships between cumulative anticholinergic burden and clozapine metabolic ratios are additionally illustrated in Fig. 2. ACB was negatively correlated with C/D ratio (r = − 0.349, p = 0.001) and C/N ratio (r = − 0.429, p < 0.001). Age and NLR were not significantly associated with either metabolic ratio (all p > 0.05). A modest but statistically significant positive correlation was observed between cumulative ACB and clozapine daily dose (r = 0.240, p = 0.022), suggesting a potential association between medication burden and treatment intensity.

Multivariate regression analyses

Factors associated with the C/N ratio

Multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent clinical factors contributing to variability in the C/N ratio (Table 4). The overall model was statistically significant (F(6, 86) = 4.01, p = 0.001) and explained 17.2% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.172). ACB was independently associated with lower C/N ratio (β = −0.400, p < 0.001). Age, sex, clozapine daily dose, smoking status, and CRP abnormality were not independently associated with the C/N ratio.

Factors associated with the C/D ratio

A separate regression model was constructed for the C/D ratio (Table 4). The overall model was statistically significant (F(5, 87) = 5.25, p < 0.001) and explained 19.3% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.193). Both ACB (β = −0.334, p = 0.001) and smoking status (β = −0.230, p = 0.038) were independently associated with lower C/D ratio. Age, sex, and CRP abnormality were not significant predictors.

Regression diagnostics

Regression diagnostics were performed to assess influential observations. One case demonstrated a standardized residual slightly exceeding ± 3; however, Cook’s distance (0.11) and leverage value (0.04) indicated that this observation was not unduly influential. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below 2 for all predictors, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity.

Discussion

In this study examining clinical factors independently associated with variability in clozapine metabolic ratios, cumulative ACB demonstrated a consistent and independent association with both C/N and C/D ratios. While established determinants such as smoking were confirmed in relation to overall clozapine exposure, ACB emerged as an additional factor associated with interindividual variability. These findings suggest that clozapine pharmacokinetic variability in routine clinical practice reflects a multifactorial process, in which cumulative medication burden may be a clinically relevant factor.

A particularly noteworthy finding of our study is the inverse association between cumulative ACB and clozapine metabolic ratios, which appears inconsistent with several conventional pharmacokinetic expectations. Anticholinergic medications are known to slow gastrointestinal motility and gastric emptying, potentially altering the absorption and bioavailability of orally administered drugs [22]. Furthermore, several commonly encountered pharmacokinetic drug-drug interactions result in reduced drug metabolism rather than enhanced clearance, which would theoretically be expected to increase, rather than decrease, clozapine metabolic ratios. Therefore, the inverse association observed in the present study is unlikely to be explained solely by conventional pharmacokinetic drug-drug interactions. Rather than reflecting a direct pharmacokinetic effect of anticholinergic burden itself, cumulative ACB may serve as a marker of broader treatment complexity and polypharmacy [23–25]. Because the medications contributing to ACB scores comprise pharmacologically heterogeneous agents with potentially diverse effects on drug absorption, metabolism, and elimination [26], the cumulative burden score is unlikely to reflect a single biological mechanism. Consequently, the observed association between higher ACB and lower metabolic ratios may reflect the combined influence of multiple clinical and pharmacological factors that were not directly measured in the present study. Future prospective studies incorporating pharmacogenetic data, detailed medication interaction profiles, and quantitative measures of enzyme activity are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying this association.

A modest positive correlation was observed between cumulative ACB and clozapine daily dose. This finding may reflect underlying clinical complexity, as patients requiring higher clozapine doses may be more likely to receive additional medications, including anticholinergic agents, to manage side effects or comorbid symptoms [12, 27]. Alternatively, greater illness severity may contribute to both higher dosing requirements and increased pharmacological burden. Importantly, the independent association between ACB and metabolic ratios in multivariate models suggests that the observed association between ACB and pharmacokinetic indices is not solely explained by clozapine dose.

From a clinical perspective, these findings have potential implications for the interpretation of TDM. Clinicians encountering unexpectedly low clozapine concentration-to-dose ratios should consider cumulative ACB as a potentially associated clinical factor. As ACB can be readily calculated from routinely prescribed medications, it may serve as a practical adjunct in the clinical assessment of pharmacokinetic variability.

Consistent with prior literature, smoking was independently associated with lower C/D ratios, supporting the established role of CYP1A2 induction in increasing clozapine clearance [28]. However, smoking did not remain an independent predictor in the C/N model, suggesting that determinants of metabolic balance may extend beyond enzyme induction alone. In contrast, ACB showed a consistent association across both models, indicating that cumulative medication-related factors remain linked to variability alongside traditional factors, potentially reflecting the impact of pharmacological complexity.

Inflammatory markers, including CRP abnormality and NLR, were not independently associated with metabolic ratios in this study. The use of a dichotomous CRP variable may have limited the ability to detect more subtle inflammatory effects. Future studies incorporating quantitative inflammatory biomarkers may provide a more precise evaluation of the relationship between inflammation and clozapine metabolism.

Although the regression models identified significant associations between cumulative ACB and clozapine metabolic ratios, their explanatory power was modest, accounting for approximately 17–19% of the variance in the outcome measures. While these levels of explanatory power are typical for observational clinical research, it underscores the likely contribution of unmeasured variables. Factors not assessed in the present study, including genetic polymorphisms affecting drug metabolism, smoking intensity, caffeine consumption, objective measures of medication adherence, environmental influences, and additional pharmacokinetic interactions, may contribute to the remaining variability. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted in the context of clozapine pharmacokinetics’ multifactorial nature.

The present study has several strengths. It includes a relatively large sample compared to many single-centre clozapine pharmacokinetic studies, evaluates both C/N and C/D ratios within a single analytical framework, and incorporates clinically relevant covariates in multivariate models. In addition, trough serum levels obtained under stable dosing conditions enhance the reliability of pharmacokinetic assessment, and regression diagnostics support the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Genetic polymorphisms affecting clozapine metabolism were not assessed. Smoking intensity and caffeine intake were not available and may have contributed to residual confounding. Medication adherence was not objectively assessed and was based on routine clinical documentation and patient/caregiver reports, which may have introduced additional variability into the pharmacokinetic measures. Furthermore, our analysis focused on a single time point per patient to ensure synchronized data across all variables; however, this cross-sectional approach does not capture intraindividual variability over time, which could be further explored in future longitudinal studies using mixed-effects models. The use of a dichotomous CRP variable may have limited the sensitivity of our analyses to detect more subtle relationships between inflammation and clozapine metabolic ratios. Future studies incorporating quantitative inflammatory biomarkers may provide a more precise assessment of the role of inflammation in clozapine pharmacokinetics. Furthermore, while the ACB score provides a cumulative estimate of anticholinergic exposure, it does not account for dose-dependent pharmacodynamic differences between individual medications. Another limitation is that cognitive function and cognitive adverse effects were not assessed using standardised instruments. Given the well-established association between anticholinergic burden and cognitive impairment, the absence of cognitive measures precluded evaluation of the potential clinical consequences of elevated anticholinergic burden in this sample.

Conclusion

In conclusion, clozapine metabolic variability appears to be shaped by multiple clinical factors in routine practice. Cumulative ACB may be a useful clinical marker correlated with variations in clozapine metabolic ratios, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. These findings are hypothesis-generating and support the need for prospective studies incorporating pharmacogenetic, pharmacokinetic, and detailed medication interaction data to clarify underlying mechanisms.
