Eating behaviors, home meal replacement consumption, and nutrition quotient: a comparative study of male shift and non-shift workers in Chungcheong, Korea
Authors: Yeon Jin Lee, Munkyong Pae
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESShift work, defined as employment outside standard working hours, is becoming increasingly common in industrialized countries. This study examined the differences in eating behaviors, home meal replacement (HMR) consumption, and nutrition quotient (NQ) between male shift and non-shift workers.SUBJECTS/METHODSA total of 392 male workers (193 shift workers and 199 non-shift workers) in the Chungcheong region of Korea participated between May and July 2024. Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire that assessed the general characteristics, eating behaviors, frequency of HMR consumption, perceived importance and satisfaction with the HMR attributes, and NQ. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0, with χ2 tests, independent t-tests, and analysis of covariance.RESULTSShift workers were significantly more likely to have lower education levels, lower monthly household income, and to be employed in manufacturing-related jobs than non-shift workers. They also showed more irregular meal timing, frequent late-night eating, higher smoking rates, less frequent physical activity, and poorer perceived sleep quality. Shift workers reported significantly more frequent consumption of specific HMR products, including gimbap/lunchboxes (0.51 vs. 0.37), sandwiches/burgers (0.43 vs. 0.36), microwavable snack foods (0.33 vs. 0.19), and frozen fried rice (0.21 vs. 0.12), based on a frequency score where 1 indicated once per week. No significant differences in the importance or satisfaction with HMR attributes were observed between the groups. In contrast, shift workers exhibited significantly lower total NQ scores (48.75 vs. 51.03), particularly in the balance and moderation domains.CONCLUSIONShift workers had less favorable eating behaviors, more frequent consumption of certain HMR products, and lower NQ scores than their non-shift counterparts, underscoring the urgent need for targeted public health strategies tailored to work schedules.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, the relationship between the occupational environment and dietary behavior has attracted increasing attention. Among the various occupational contexts, shift work has attracted interest because of its potential influence on eating habits and health outcomes [12]. Shift work refers to employment that requires working outside the standard hours of 7 a.m. to 6 p.m., either permanently or periodically [3]. This employment type has become increasingly prevalent in industrialized countries, with an estimated 20% to 30% of workers in EU countries and the United States engaged in shift work [4]. According to the Korea Labor Institute, 29.6% of workplaces with 30 or more employees in South Korea implement shift work [5].

Shift work is a recognized health hazard because of its association with an increased risk of sleep disorders, digestive issues, and other physiological dysfunctions [6]. Meta-analyses indicate that shift work is linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular disease and diabetes compared to daytime work [78]. Irregular and poor dietary patterns, such as meal skipping, frequent snacking, late-night eating, and a preference for carbohydrate-rich foods, may partially contribute to this elevated risk among shift workers [9] because their eating behaviors and meal timing are often adjusted to accommodate shift schedules. Although the total 24-h energy intake does not differ significantly between shift and non-shift workers [10], shift workers tend to consume more processed meat, instant noodles [11], and other discretionary foods [12]. Consistently, a study of industrial workers in South Korea found that shift workers had poorer dietary habit scores than non-shift workers, with lower scores for balanced meals, vegetable intake, and seaweed intake, and higher scores for meal skipping [13].

Among the factors influencing the dietary intake of shift workers, time limitations and food accessibility play a critical role [14]. In this context, home meal replacement (HMR) appears to be a prevalent option, particularly among shift workers who face time and effort constraints in preparing meals at home. HMR refers to fully or partially prepared food products that are manufactured, processed, and packaged for consumption either as-is or with minimal cooking [15]. The market for HMR products has been growing steadily worldwide, including in South Korea. For example, annual HMR sales in South Korea were estimated to be approximately US$3.5 billion in 2022, reflecting a 65.7% increase compared to US$2.2 billion in 2018 [16]. According to the Korea Rural Economic Institute, the market is projected to reach approximately US$4.5 billion in 2024 [17].

Despite the continued expansion of the HMR market, previous research has focused predominantly on young adults [181920], the elderly [2122], and single-person households [2324], with limited attention to occupational groups. In particular, shift workers in the manufacturing sector are likely to rely on HMR because of the irregular work schedules and the pursuit of convenience. These environmental and behavioral constraints may considerably influence food choices, making this group a pertinent focus for research on HMR consumption patterns. Nevertheless, few studies have evaluated HMR consumption among adult men based on their work environment.

This study examined the eating habits, HMR consumption patterns, and nutrition quotient (NQ) of adult men working in the manufacturing industry, depending on whether they worked in a shift or non-shift environment. These findings provide fundamental data for developing tailored nutrition education programs for male workers. In particular, this study addresses a significant research gap by examining HMR use in the context of occupational conditions, an area that has received limited attention despite its growing importance.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Subjects

The study was conducted on adult males aged 19 to 64 yrs who were employed at industrial workplaces in Chungcheong, Korea, and who provided written informed consent. The survey was carried out between May 7 and July 20, 2024. The participants were recruited through off-site advertisements from 4 different manufacturing companies in the province, including chemical manufacturers specializing in silicone and sealants. The study purpose and survey method were explained to the participants, and informed consent was obtained before administering a self-administered questionnaire. Of the 400 participants, 8 incomplete questionnaires were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 392 participants (inclusion rate: 98.0%). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungbuk National University (approval number: CBNU-202403-HR-0068; approval date: April 4, 2024).

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed by adapting items from previous studies [1125262728] to align with the objectives of this study and was refined through a preliminary survey. The questionnaire comprised the following 5 sections: general characteristics, eating habits and health-related lifestyle factors, HMR consumption frequency, perceptions of HMR attributes, and the NQ.

The general characteristics included age, height, weight, education level, work schedule, job category, monthly household income, and household size. The eating habits and health-related lifestyle factors included the meal duration, mealtime regularity, frequency of snacking, frequency of late-night eating, frequency of eating out, frequency of alcohol consumption, smoking status, frequency of exercise (≥ 30 min/day), sleep duration, and subjective sleep quality. Mealtime regularity and subjective sleep quality were measured on a 5-point Likert-type scale (5 = very regular/very good to 1 = very irregular/very bad).

The HMR consumption frequency was assessed for 4 product types: ready-to-eat (RTE), ready-to-heat (RTH), ready-to-cook (RTC), and ready-to-prepare (RTP) foods [15]. The RTE category included gimbap and lunchboxes, sandwiches and burgers, Korean side dishes (e.g., stir-fried anchovies, seasoned dried shredded squid, and stir-fried fish cakes), salads, and fresh fruits. The RTH category included cooked rice, cup-style rice dishes (e.g., cupbap), topped rice bowls (e.g., deopbap), porridge and soup, Korean-style soups and stews (e.g., jjigae), pre-cooked fish and meat products, and microwavable snack foods (e.g., tteokbokki, frozen pizza, and Korean-style corn dogs). The RTC category comprised frozen fried rice, noodles, semi-cooked marinated meat and seafood for grilling or soups/stews, and frozen dumplings and assorted fried foods. The RTP category included pre-prepared meal kits for Korean-style soups/stews, stir-fried dishes, and noodles. The consumption frequency of each item was quantified using the following scale: daily = 7 points; 4–6 times/week = 5 points; 2–3 times/week = 2.5 points; once/week = 1 point; 2–3 times/month = 0.58 points; once/month = 0.23 points; and rarely or never = 0 points.

The perceptions related to the HMR attributes were assessed by evaluating the perceived importance during product selection and the level of satisfaction after consumption. The responses were rated using a 5-point Likert-type scale (5 = very important/satisfied to 1 = not important at all/not satisfied at all).

The NQ for Korean adults, developed by the Korean Nutrition Society [2930], comprised 18 items across 3 domains: balance (e.g., vegetables, fruits, and dairy), moderation (e.g., fast food and processed meat), and dietary practice (e.g., efforts to eat healthily and checking nutrition labels). Higher scores in each domain indicate more favorable dietary behaviors. In the balance and dietary practice domains, higher frequencies correspond to higher scores, whereas in the moderation domain, lower intake frequencies yield higher scores. The total NQ scores were calculated based on the scoring and weighting system proposed by Yook et al. [30] and were categorized into 3 levels: high (68.6–100), medium (52.8–68.5), and low (0–52.7).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software (version 28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The categorical variables, including the participants’ general characteristics, eating habits, and health-related lifestyle factors, were presented as numbers and percentages. The continuous variables, such as HMR consumption scores, NQ scores, and perceived importance and satisfaction with the HMR attributes, are expressed as the means ± SD. The chi-square test was used to compare the categorical variables, while independent t-tests were applied to analyze the continuous variables. Analysis of covariance was performed to assess the differences between shift and non-shift workers, adjusting for the education level and monthly household income. The statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Importance-satisfaction analysis (ISA) was conducted to identify the improvement priorities by plotting the average importance and satisfaction scores for each HMR attribute on a four-quadrant matrix using the grand means as crosshairs.

RESULTS

General characteristics of the respondents

Table 1 lists the general characteristics of male workers according to shift and non-shift work status. A total of 392 respondents completed the survey, comprising 193 shift workers (49.2%) and 199 non-shift workers (50.8%). Regarding the age distribution, 20.2%, 26.5%, 25.5%, and 27.8% were aged 19–29 yrs, 30–39 yrs, 40–49 yrs, and 50–64 yrs, respectively. The average body mass index (BMI) was 25.4 kg/m2, and 48.2% of participants were classified as obese (≥ 25 kg/m2). More than half of the respondents (54.8%) had a college degree or higher. The majority were employed in manufacturing occupations (43.9%), and 41.3% reported a monthly household income of ≥ 5 million KRW. In addition, 74.5% lived with at least one cohabitant.

Significant differences in education level, job category, and monthly income were observed according to shift work status. Compared to non-shift workers, shift workers had a significantly higher proportion of individuals with a high school education or less (P < 0.001), were more likely to be employed in manufacturing rather than office-based positions (P < 0.001), and had a lower proportion of individuals reporting a monthly household income of ≥ 5 million KRW (P = 0.012). Eating habits and health-related lifestyle factors

Table 2 compares the eating habits and health-related lifestyle behaviors between shift and non-shift workers. No significant differences were observed in meal duration, frequency of snacking, eating out, or alcohol consumption. On the other hand, shift workers had significantly lower scores for mealtime regularity (2.73 vs. 3.53, P < 0.001), and a higher proportion reported ‘very irregular’ or ‘irregular’ eating patterns (42.5% vs. 11.5%, P < 0.001). They were also more likely to engage in frequent late-night eating (P < 0.001).

The smoking rates were higher among shift workers than non-shift workers (48.7% vs. 34.7%, P = 0.005). In the RTE category, shift workers tended to consume more total RTE foods than non-shift workers (P = 0.041; adjusted P = 0.102). In particular, the consumption of gimbap/lunchboxes (P = 0.090; adjusted P = 0.007) and sandwiches/burgers (P = 0.227; adjusted P = 0.023) was significantly higher among shift workers after adjusting for the education level and household income. Among the RTH foods, microwavable snack items, such as tteokbokki, frozen pizza, and Korean-style corn dogs, were consumed significantly more frequently by shift workers (P = 0.009; adjusted P = 0.006). Although the overall intake of porridge and soup was low in both groups, the shift workers reported significantly greater consumption (P = 0.028; adjusted P = 0.007). In the RTC category, total consumption was significantly higher among shift workers after the adjustment (adjusted P = 0.012). Specifically, shift workers consumed significantly more frozen fried rice (P = 0.049; adjusted P = 0.005) and noodles such as naengmyeon, spaghetti, udon, and jajangmyeon than non-shift workers (P = 0.023; adjusted P = 0.033). No significant differences were observed in the consumption of RTP meal kits or their sub-items between the 2 groups.

Perceived importance and satisfaction with HMR attributes

Table 4 presents the perceived importance and satisfaction associated with HMR attributes. Among the importance-related attributes, food hygiene received the highest mean score (4.32 out of 5.0), followed by freshness of ingredients, taste, expiration date/shelf life, ease of preparation, price, convenience of purchase, portion size, nutritional value, and country of origin. All attributes received scores above the midpoint (3.0), suggesting that the respondents generally considered them more important than neutral. The importance rating for taste was significantly higher among non-shift workers than shift workers (P = 0.002), but the difference was not statistically significant after adjusting for education and income (adjusted P = 0.246).

In terms of satisfaction, the ease of preparation received the highest mean score (3.81), while the nutritional value received the lowest (3.16). Non-shift workers reported greater satisfaction with the convenience of purchase compared to shift workers (P = 0.029), but the difference was not statistically significant after adjusting for education and income (adjusted P = 0.095). An ISA matrix was constructed to contextualize these findings, as shown in Fig. 1. The overall mean scores for importance and satisfaction were 3.85 and 3.47, respectively. In this matrix, food hygiene, taste, expiration date/shelf life, and ease of preparation were positioned in Quadrant I (Keep up the good work), indicating high importance and relatively high satisfaction. The freshness of ingredients was located on the boundary between Quadrants I and II, reflecting high importance but only average satisfaction. Price was located in Quadrant II (Concentrate here), characterized by high importance but low satisfaction, thus representing a priority area for improvement. The portion size, nutritional value, and country of origin were placed in Quadrant III (Low priority). In contrast, the convenience of purchase was found in Quadrant IV (Possible overkill), reflecting their lower importance despite higher satisfaction.

NQ score

The mean total NQ score among all participants was 49.91, as shown in Table 5. The mean subscale scores for balance, moderation, and practice were 31.54, 64.60, and 52.67, respectively. Shift workers had significantly lower total NQ scores than non-shift workers (P = 0.041). After adjusting for education and income, shift workers had significantly lower scores in balance (adjusted P = 0.002), moderation (adjusted P = 0.002), and the total NQ score (adjusted P = 0.002). The distribution of NQ scores also differed significantly according to the shift work status (P = 0.033), with a larger proportion of shift workers classified in the low NQ group (66.8%) compared to non-shift workers (55.8%).

DISCUSSION

This study examined the differences in eating behaviors, HMR consumption, and NQ between male shift and non-shift workers in the Chungcheong region of Korea. Although previous studies have explored various factors influencing HMR consumption, most targeted specific age groups [1819202122] or household types [2324]. Our findings extend this evidence to an occupational context, showing that shift workers had less favorable eating behaviors, including irregular meal timing and frequent late-night eating, and reported more frequent consumption of certain HMR products. Moreover, their significantly lower total NQ scores, particularly in the balance and moderation domains, highlight the potential nutritional vulnerabilities associated with shift work.

In the present study, a substantial proportion of shift workers were employed in manufacturing occupations (79.3%) and had lower levels of education and household income than non-shift workers. This finding regarding the educational level is consistent with a previous study of manufacturing workers (90.3% male) in Gwangju, South Korea [13]. The average BMI exceeded 25 kg/m2 in both groups, with a high prevalence of obesity observed regardless of the work schedule (51.3% in shift workers vs. 45.2% in non-shift workers). In addition, a higher proportion of shift workers were smokers (48.7%), and only a small percentage engaged in regular physical activity (8.3% reported exercising ≥ 5 times per week), underscoring the need for targeted health interventions in this population. These behavioral patterns align with previous studies that reported higher smoking rates among shift workers [113].

Shift workers also had significantly lower scores for eating meals on time, indicating irregular meal patterns, and were more likely to engage in late-night eating. A systematic review by Souza et al. [2], involving 33 studies on shift work and eating habits, reported that although total daily energy intake was similar between the groups, shift workers exhibited more irregular meal timing, altered eating frequency, and generally poorer diet quality. Despite the differences in occupational roles and gender, previous studies of nurses in South Korea, who also worked in shift-based settings, have also reported lower meal regularity among shift workers (gender not specified) [31] and an increased likelihood of developing unhealthy eating behaviors with longer durations of shift work (female only) [32], suggesting that such patterns may be shared across various shift-working populations. The greater irregularity in daily energy intake has been associated with lower overall diet quality, including reduced consumption of fruit, vegetables, legumes, low-fat dairy, and poultry, along with increased intake of processed meat [33]. These findings are in line with the present study, suggesting that irregular meal timing contributes to poor diet quality among shift workers. Therefore, tailored dietary guidelines and health promotion strategies are needed for shift workers before such patterns become entrenched. These should focus on promoting regular meal timing, balanced nutrition, and adequate physical activity.

Among the HMR categories, RTE foods were consumed most frequently (mean score: 3.00), corresponding to approximately 3 times per week. This was followed by RTH foods (2.26; approximately 2 to 3 times per week), RTC foods (1.52; approximately one to 2 times per week), and RTP meal kits (0.69; approximately 3 times per month). Although a standardized classification system for HMRs has yet to be established in South Korea, making direct comparisons across studies difficult [34], the 2022 Processed Food Consumer Attitude Survey reported that the most common purchase frequencies for RTH foods were “once every 2 weeks” (35.9%) and “once a month” (33.1%). By contrast, RTP meal kits had the highest rate of non-use (31.7%) [35], which is consistent with the present finding that RTP products were the least frequently consumed category.

Shift workers consumed specific HMR items more frequently than non-shift workers, including RTE foods (e.g., gimbap/lunchboxes and sandwiches/burgers), RTH items (e.g., microwavable snacks and porridge/soup), and RTC items (e.g., frozen fried rice and noodles). Although the taste and nutritional profiles of HMR have improved, concerns remain regarding their high content of calories, fat, sugar, and sodium [363738]. For example, common cooking methods used in lunchboxes, such as stir-frying, deep-frying, and pan-frying, involve substantial use of oil, often resulting in fat and saturated fat contents exceeding 40% of the Daily Value [38]. In addition, the average sodium content of lunchboxes is 1,335 mg [38], corresponding to 58% of the Chronic Disease Risk Reduction Intake. Elevated sodium levels in HMRs have been reported in Korea [3839], Australia [40], and Europe [41]. Considering the increased reliance on these products, promoting informed and balanced consumption of HMRs is essential for shift workers. Tailored nutrition education emphasizing nutrition literacy and the ability to interpret nutrition labels may help guide healthier dietary choices in this population.

In the present study, the ISA results identified food hygiene, taste, expiration date, and ease of preparation as attributes with high importance and high satisfaction. Despite sociodemographic differences between the present sample of male workers and the populations in previous studies [42434445], similar patterns were observed in which food hygiene and taste were prioritized when selecting HMR products [42]. Price dissatisfaction, despite its high importance, was also reported in studies of consumers in the Seoul metropolitan area (40.2% male) [43] and among Korean adults (45.8% male) [44], highlighting the need for affordability strategies to sustain HMR use. Nevertheless, certain attributes, particularly food hygiene (0.75), price (0.69), freshness of ingredients (0.67), taste (0.53), and expiration date (0.40), revealed substantial gaps between importance and satisfaction, suggesting that these features require ongoing monitoring to maintain consumer trust. The nutritional value, which was rated low in importance and satisfaction in a survey of lunchbox attributes among adults in Seoul (55.2% male) [45], showed a similar pattern in the present study. Nevertheless, this attribute warrants targeted improvement considering its strong public health relevance. Product reformulation to incorporate nutrient-dense ingredients (e.g., whole grains, vegetables, and lean proteins), alongside reductions in sodium and saturated fat, and the development of options tailored to specific health needs (e.g., high-fiber or low-sodium products), may encourage healthier consumer choices. These improvements may be especially relevant for shift workers, who often rely on HMRs because of time constraints, and could help reduce nutrition-related health disparities in this population.

The total NQ score among the participants was 49.91, with domain-specific mean scores for balance, moderation, and dietary practice of 31.54, 64.60, and 52.67, respectively. Notably, shift workers had significantly lower total NQ scores than non-shift workers. In particular, lower scores in the balance and moderation domains among shift workers reflect the inadequate intake of essential food groups and excessive consumption of discretionary foods. Since the present study used the revised 2021 version of the NQ, direct comparisons with previous studies remain limited. Nevertheless, a 2019 study of adult manufacturing workers (81% male) using the previous versions of the NQ also reported significantly lower total, balance, and moderation scores among shift workers [46], revealing a consistent pattern with the present findings. Subdomain analysis showed significantly lower intakes of fruit and milk/dairy products among shift workers, even after adjusting for education and income. In parallel with their higher HMR consumption, shift workers also exhibited lower moderation scores related to fast food and processed meat intake, suggesting excessive or less controlled consumption of these items. Fruits are rich in potassium, dietary fiber, vitamin C, and folate. Milk and dairy products serve as essential sources of calcium and riboflavin. In contrast, fast food is typically high in calories, sodium, and fat [47], and processed meat consumption has been associated with an increased risk of colorectal and other cancers [48]. A 2019 study also found that shift workers consumed less fruit, milk/dairy products, beans, and vegetables, whereas fast food and late-night eating were reported more frequently [46], dietary issues that remain inadequately addressed. Thus, nutrition education for shift workers should emphasize practical strategies for incorporating fruits and dairy products into daily meals while reducing the intake of fast food and processed meats. Furthermore, 61.2% of participants were classified as having a “low” NQ grade, with an even higher proportion among shift workers (66.8% vs. 55.8%), highlighting their greater nutritional vulnerability.

Taken together, our findings highlight the multifaceted impact of shift work on dietary quality and health-related behaviors. Shift workers were more likely to engage in irregular and unhealthy eating patterns, exhibited lower overall diet quality, and had greater reliance on certain types of HMR products. These patterns were accompanied by other adverse lifestyle factors, including reduced physical activity, higher smoking rates, and poorer subjective sleep quality. Comprehensive interventions are needed, considering the heightened nutritional vulnerability and behavioral risk profiles observed in this population. These should target individual-level factors, such as nutrition knowledge and eating behaviors, as well as structural determinants that influence dietary choices in the context of shift work.

Nevertheless, this study had several limitations. First, as the participants were recruited from a limited number of industrial sites within the Chungcheong region, the findings may not be generalizable to workers in other regions or occupational sectors. Second, the study exclusively targeted male workers and did not account for potential gender-based differences in dietary behaviors, HMR consumption, or lifestyle factors. Future studies should include more diverse populations, encompassing female workers and various occupational groups, to improve the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, longitudinal research is needed to assess the causal relationships between shift work and changes in dietary patterns or health outcomes over time.

In light of these findings, workplace-based programs tailored to shift workers should be developed, focusing on improving nutrition literacy, encouraging healthier HMR selection, and promoting regular meal timing. Environmental support, such as providing healthier food options at workplace cafeterias or vending machines, may also help foster better dietary habits among shift workers. At a broader level, public health efforts should include the development of nutritional guidelines tailored to shift workers and provide incentives for companies that implement structured nutrition education, ensure regular mealtimes, and offer healthy meal options in the workplace.
