Uncovering the hidden burden of congenital anomalies in Ecuador: a national integrated analysis of hospital discharges, mortality records, and live birth statistics (2012–2024)
Authors: Fabricio González-Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital anomalies (CA), ICD-10 Q00–Q99 codes, are major causes of infant morbidity and mortality, but national assessments remain limited in countries without integrated birth defect surveillance. This study characterized hospital burden, mortality, and epidemiological patterns of CA among infants in Ecuador.MethodsA retrospective descriptive analysis of national administrative records was conducted using data from the INEC. Hospital discharge records covered 2012–2024, while mortality records and live birth statistics were incorporated according to availability. Analyses were restricted to infants under 1 year of age. Descriptive statistics, graphical assessment of temporal patterns, non-parametric comparison between periods, and correlation analyses were applied.ResultsOverall, 43,134 hospital discharge events associated with CA were recorded, with a mean annual count of 3,318. Separately, in the code-level analysis of the 50 most frequent ICD-10 diagnostic codes, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and genitourinary categories accounted for the largest aggregated counts. Hospital discharges decreased significantly during the pandemic/post-pandemic period compared with 2012–2019 (-21.8%, p = 0.0015). Mortality indicators varied across conditions, with higher death-to-discharge ratios in selected cardiac, valve, pulmonary, and body wall anomalies. Hospital discharge rates ranged from 10.67 to 13.18 per 1,000 live births during 2021–2024. Mortality rates ranged from 1.38 to 1.47 per 1,000 live births; however, only 2021 and 2024 mortality values were observed, whereas 2022 and 2023 were interpolated. A moderate correlation was observed between hospital discharges and deaths (r ≈ 0.48).ConclusionCA impose a substantial burden on infant health services in Ecuador. Integrating hospital discharge, mortality, and live birth data revealed complementary but non-equivalent dimensions of burden, supporting stronger surveillance, improved data linkage, and timely referral pathways for high-risk conditions.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Congenital anomalies, Hospital discharges, Infant mortality, Public health surveillance, Ecuador, Sustainable Development Goal 3

Introduction

Congenital anomalies (CA), classified under ICD-10 codes Q00–Q99, comprise a heterogeneous group of structural and functional abnormalities that originate during intrauterine development and may affect multiple organ systems. These conditions are important contributors to infant morbidity, mortality, disability, and long-term healthcare needs, particularly in settings with limited access to prevention, early diagnosis, specialized care, and follow-up services [1]. Recent global analyses indicate that CA continue to impose a substantial burden among children, with marked variation across regions and health system contexts [2]. As mortality from infectious and other preventable causes declines, CA become proportionally more prominent within under-five mortality profiles [3]. Neonatal survival studies further show that CA contribute substantially to early neonatal deaths and require timely detection, referral, and coordinated clinical management after birth [4]. Early neonatal mortality therefore remains a major global challenge in which CA represent an important and often complex component [5].

The public health relevance of CA has led to the development of organized surveillance systems in several regions. EUROCAT has demonstrated the value of standardized monitoring for estimating prevalence, identifying temporal patterns, supporting prevention strategies, and informing health planning [6]. Sustainable CA surveillance requires stable registry infrastructure, harmonized coding, standardized definitions, and integration with broader rare disease and public health information systems [7]. Registry-based experience also shows that prevalence estimates depend on case ascertainment, diagnostic confirmation, and the inclusion of live births, fetal deaths, and pregnancy terminations where applicable [8]. For this reason, standardized case classification guidelines are essential to reduce heterogeneity in diagnostic definitions and improve comparability across studies and surveillance systems [9]. In countries without active or complete registries, administrative datasets may provide useful evidence, but their interpretation requires caution because coverage, coding quality, and ascertainment can vary across institutions and over time [10].

In Latin America, CA surveillance has historically relied on collaborative networks, selected registries, and hospital-based reporting systems, with uneven coverage across countries and regions. Geographic cluster analyses in South America suggest that CA prevalence may vary spatially, reflecting possible environmental, demographic, genetic, healthcare-access, and surveillance-related factors [11]. Regional surveillance experience also indicates that birth defect monitoring is useful for identifying mortality-related factors, prioritizing high-risk conditions, and improving care pathways for affected infants [12]. Access to specialized care remains a major determinant of outcomes in Latin America, particularly for children with congenital heart defects (CHD), where diagnosis, referral, surgery, and postoperative care are not equally available across populations [13]. CHD are among the most common and clinically significant CA worldwide, and reported prevalence varies according to diagnostic methods, study period, and surveillance design [14]. Global analyses of CND confirm substantial heterogeneity in incidence and mortality, reinforcing the need for national data systems capable of monitoring both occurrence and outcomes [15].

In Ecuador, previous national research has shown that CA represent a substantial hospital burden and has emphasized the need to create a National Registry of Birth Defects [16]. Environmental characteristics specific to Ecuador may also be relevant, as high-altitude exposure in Andean populations has been proposed as a possible contributor to CHD through mechanisms related to chronic hypoxia during pregnancy [17]. Recent evidence from a pediatric referral hospital in Ecuador suggests variation in CHD patterns by altitude, supporting the importance of geographic and environmental determinants in national analyses [18]. Genetic diagnostic experience in Ecuador has documented chromosomal alterations and polymorphisms through multi-institutional records, highlighting the role of cytogenetic and genetic services in understanding CA [19]. Ecuador’s population structure is also characterized by substantial biological and ancestral diversity among Mestizo, Native Americans, and Afro-Ecuadorian populations, which may influence disease susceptibility, diagnostic patterns, and public health interpretation [20]. Earlier work on public health genomics in Ecuador emphasized the need to integrate genetic knowledge, epidemiological surveillance, and health policy to improve prevention, diagnosis, and management of inherited and CA [21].

Despite these contributions, Ecuador still lacks a centralized, active CA surveillance system that integrates prenatal diagnosis, birth records, hospital care, outpatient follow-up, genetic services, and mortality data. Existing evidence remains fragmented across administrative records, vital statistics, and selected clinical studies. Hospital discharge records provide information on healthcare utilization and service demand, whereas mortality records provide complementary information on fatal outcomes. Live birth statistics can be incorporated where available to provide rate-based contextualization of hospital discharge and mortality indicators. In settings without integrated congenital anomaly registries, combining these sources can help identify discrepancies between hospital burden and mortality outcomes that would be less visible if each dataset were analyzed separately.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize hospital burden, mortality, and epidemiological patterns of CA among infants under 1 year of age in Ecuador by integrating national hospital discharge records, mortality data, and live birth statistics for the period 2012–2024. This approach responds to the absence of an integrated national surveillance registry and provides evidence to support CA monitoring, data linkage, and public health planning in Ecuador.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective descriptive analysis of national administrative records on CA in Ecuador. The analysis used hospital discharge records, mortality records, and live birth statistics to describe hospital burden, fatal outcomes, and rate-based indicators among infants under 1 year of age. The study followed the recommendations of the STROBE Statement for reporting observational studies. Because the analysis was based on aggregated secondary data, no individual-level identifiers were available, and no patient-level linkage was performed.

Setting

The study was conducted in Ecuador, a middle-income country in Latin America where national health statistics is compiled by the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INEC). Ecuador does not currently have a centralized, active CA surveillance registry that integrates prenatal diagnosis, birth records, hospital care, outpatient follow-up, and mortality data. In this context, national administrative datasets provide complementary but incomplete information on CA. Hospital discharge records describe inpatient service utilization, mortality records describe fatal outcomes, and live birth statistics provide denominators for selected rate-based indicators.

Study period

Hospital discharge records covered the period from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2024. Mortality records and live birth statistics were incorporated according to national data availability. Live birth denominators were available for 2021–2024 and were therefore used only for rate-based analyses during that period.

Data sources

Data were obtained from INEC, the official national agency responsible for compiling demographic, health, hospital, and vital statistics in Ecuador. Three national datasets were used: hospital discharge records, mortality records, and live birth statistics. Hospital discharge records were generated from inpatient hospital events reported by healthcare facilities. These records include admissions and discharges associated with diagnosis, treatment, surgical management, or follow-up of CA. Mortality data were obtained from national death records and were based on the underlying cause of death coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Live birth statistics were used as denominators to calculate hospital discharge rates and mortality rates per 1,000 live births for the years in which denominator data were available.

Study population

The study population included infants under 1 year of age with recorded hospital discharges or registered deaths associated with CA in Ecuador. The analysis was restricted to this age group because CA have their greatest clinical and epidemiological relevance during infancy and because this restriction improved comparability between hospital discharge and mortality datasets.

Case definition

CA were defined using ICD-10 codes Q00–Q99. Diagnoses were analyzed at the three-character ICD-10 level to standardize classification across data sources and reduce variability related to differences in coding specificity. Hospital discharges were defined as inpatient hospital events coded with congenital anomaly diagnoses. Deaths were defined as registered infant deaths in which a CA was recorded as the underlying cause of death.

Interpretation of hospital discharge records

Hospital discharge records were interpreted as healthcare events and indicators of hospital service utilization. They were not interpreted as unique individuals, incident cases, or direct estimates of population prevalence. Because individual-level identifiers were not available, repeated admissions of the same infant could not be identified or removed. Therefore, all comparisons involving hospital discharges were interpreted descriptively as indicators of healthcare utilization, hospital burden, and service demand.

Clinical classification

ICD-10 codes were grouped into major clinical categories according to the predominant organ system affected. Categories included cardiovascular, genitourinary, musculoskeletal, digestive, craniofacial, central nervous system, respiratory, urinary, multisystem, sensory, chromosomal anomalies, other specified anomalies, genetic syndromes / neurocutaneous disorders, and dermatological anomalies. This classification was used to summarize the distribution of hospital discharges by major clinical system and to support descriptive comparisons across diagnostic groups.

Variables

The primary variables were annual hospital discharges and infant deaths associated with congenital anomalies. Hospital discharges were analyzed by year, ICD-10 code, and major clinical system. Mortality was analyzed by year and ICD-10 code for the years with available data. Additional variables included live births, hospital discharge rates per 1,000 live births, mortality rates per 1,000 live births, and the proportion of deaths relative to hospital discharges for selected CA.

Outcome indicators

The main outcome indicators were total hospital discharges, annual hospital discharge counts, distribution by ICD-10 code, distribution by clinical system, infant deaths attributed to congenital anomalies, hospital discharge rates per 1,000 live births, mortality rates per 1,000 live births, and death-to-discharge ratios. The proportion of deaths relative to hospital discharges was interpreted as a death-to-discharge ratio, not as an individual-level case-fatality estimate.

Data processing and harmonization

All datasets underwent systematic cleaning and harmonization before analysis. ICD-10 codes were standardized at the three-character level across hospital discharge and mortality datasets. Age filters were applied to restrict all analyses to infants under 1 year of age. Hospital discharge records were aggregated by year and ICD-10 code, and counts corresponding to the same code-year combination were summed. This procedure was performed to harmonize aggregated records and was not intended as patient-level deduplication. Mortality and hospital discharge datasets were aligned by ICD-10 code and year to enable descriptive comparisons between fatal outcomes and hospital utilization. Data validation included checks for missing values, implausible counts, inconsistencies between years, and extreme values by ICD-10 code. Discrepancies were reviewed within the constraints of aggregated public datasets.

Handling of missing mortality data

Mortality data were available for selected years only. For years without observed mortality data, interpolated values were used only for descriptive continuity in tables and figures. These interpolated values were not treated as observed deaths and were not used as evidence of temporal stability or change in mortality. This distinction was applied when interpreting mortality rates for 2021–2024, because only the 2021 and 2024 mortality values were observed, whereas the 2022 and 2023 values were estimated by linear interpolation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize hospital discharges and mortality across years, ICD-10 codes, and clinical categories. Measures included absolute counts, percentages, means, medians, standard deviations, ranges, and rates per 1,000 live births where denominators were available. Temporal patterns in hospital discharges were examined descriptively for the full 2012–2024 period using annual counts and graphical visualization. Because live birth denominators were available only for 2021–2024, rate-based analyses were restricted to this period. Hospital discharge rates were calculated as the number of congenital anomaly-related hospital discharge events divided by the number of live births and multiplied by 1,000. Mortality rates were calculated as the number of infant deaths attributed to congenital anomalies divided by the number of live births and multiplied by 1,000.

Period comparison

To assess differences in hospital discharge patterns before and after the onset of the COVID-19 period, annual discharge counts were compared between the pre-pandemic period, defined as 2012–2019, and the pandemic/post-pandemic period, defined as 2020–2024.

Because of the small number of annual observations and the non-normal distribution of annual counts, this comparison was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The results were interpreted as changes in hospital utilization and/or reporting rather than as changes in the underlying occurrence of CA.

Correlation analysis

Associations between hospital discharges and mortality were assessed using Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients across selected ICD-10 diagnostic categories. These analyses were interpreted as descriptive comparisons between complementary administrative data sources. The correlation coefficients were not interpreted as evidence of individual-level risk, causality, or clinical prognosis, because hospital and mortality records could not be linked at the patient level.

Epidemiological prioritization

Epidemiological priority categories were constructed by integrating hospital burden and mortality indicators. These categories were descriptive and were intended to identify conditions with greater public health relevance based on frequency, mortality burden, and potential demand for specialized care. The prioritization was not intended to represent a causal risk model, individual-level clinical severity scale, or prognostic tool.

Assessment of assumptions

The distribution of annual hospital discharge counts was assessed before group comparison. Given the small number of yearly observations and the non-normal distribution of annual counts, a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was used for the comparison between periods. Correlation analyses were interpreted descriptively. No individual-level modeling was performed because the available datasets were aggregated.

Ethical considerations

This study used publicly available, anonymized, aggregated secondary data, and did not involve direct contact with human participants. No individual-level identifiable information was accessed. According to national regulations and international guidelines for epidemiological research using anonymized public data, formal ethical approval and informed consent were not required. All analyses were conducted in accordance with principles of confidentiality, transparency, and responsible reporting. The use of national administrative data was intended to support public health decision-making, improve understanding of CA burden, and contribute to the strengthening of CA surveillance systems in Ecuador.

Overall hospital discharges

Between 2012 and 2024, 43,134 hospital discharges associated with CA (ICD-10 Q00–Q99) were recorded among infants under 1 year of age in Ecuador. The mean annual number of discharges was 3,318, with a standard deviation of 389. The median annual number of discharges was 3,498, and annual counts ranged from 2,671 to 3,881. The highest annual number of discharges was recorded in 2017, with 3,881 discharges, whereas the lowest annual number was recorded in 2022, with 2,671 discharges. Across the full study period, annual counts showed variation by year, with a visible reduction after 2019 and partial recovery in later years.

Distribution by individual ICD-10 codes

The distribution of hospital discharges by individual ICD-10 codes is shown in Table 1. The most frequent diagnostic codes were Q53, corresponding to undescended testis; Q21, corresponding to CA of cardiac septa; and Q25, corresponding to CA of great arteries. Other frequent diagnostic codes included Q38, Q65, Q83, Q66, Q37, Q28, and Q35. Together, these diagnoses represented a large proportion of the recorded hospital discharge burden. The highest-frequency codes included genitourinary, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, digestive, and craniofacial anomalies. The distribution was therefore concentrated in a limited number of ICD-10 diagnostic groups.

Distribution by major clinical system

The 50 most frequent ICD-10 diagnostic codes were grouped by major clinical category, as shown in Table 2; Fig. 2. Cardiovascular codes represented the largest aggregated code-level count, with 30,837 records, corresponding to 23.7% of the grouped top-50 code count. Musculoskeletal codes accounted for 20,780 records, corresponding to 16.0%, followed by genitourinary codes with 20,172 records, corresponding to 15.5%. Digestive codes accounted for 16,700 records, and craniofacial anomalies accounted for 12,545 records. The remaining categories included central nervous system, urinary, sensory, multisystem, chromosomal, respiratory, genetic/neurocutaneous, dermatological, and other specified anomalies.

Temporal pattern of hospital discharges

Annual hospital discharges are shown in Fig. 1. During the pre-pandemic period from 2012 to 2019, annual discharge counts ranged from 3,393 to 3,881. During the pandemic/post-pandemic period from 2020 to 2024, annual discharge counts ranged from 2,671 to 3,083. The mean annual number of hospital discharges was 3,622 during 2012–2019 and 2,832 during 2020–2024. This represented a 21.8% reduction in mean annual discharges between the two periods. The difference between periods was statistically significant using the Mann–Whitney U test, with a p-value of 0.0015, as shown in Table 6.

Mortality and death-to-discharge ratios

Mortality indicators for selected CA in 2024 are presented in Table 3. The highest death-to-discharge ratios were observed for Q23, CA of the aortic and mitral valves; Q24, other CHD; and Q33, congenital lung anomalies. Q24 had 30 hospital discharges and 23 deaths, corresponding to a death-to-discharge ratio of 76.7%. Q23 had 9 hospital discharges and 7 deaths, corresponding to 77.8%. Q33 had 12 hospital discharges and 8 deaths, corresponding to 66.7%. Other selected categories showed lower ratios, including Q20, Q79, Q25, Q03, Q21, and Q90.

Mortality burden relative to hospital utilization

The distribution of mortality burden relative to hospital utilization by ICD-10 code is shown in Table 5. Several diagnostic categories had death-to-discharge ratios below 1.00, indicating that hospital discharges exceeded registered deaths for those conditions. These included Q23, Q24, Q33, Q79, Q20, Q25, Q03, Q21, Q39, Q42, Q05, and Q90. In contrast, selected categories had ratios greater than 1.00, indicating that registered deaths exceeded recorded hospital discharges for the same ICD-10 code. These included Q00, Q89, and Q91. Across selected diagnoses, death-to-discharge ratios ranged from 0.07 for Q21 to 2.29 for Q00.

Relationship between hospital discharges and deaths

The relationship between hospital discharges and deaths across selected ICD-10 diagnostic categories is shown in Fig. 3; Table 5. Pearson’s correlation coefficient between hospital discharges and deaths was approximately 0.48. Spearman’s rho was approximately 0.49. Both coefficients indicated a moderate positive association between the two measures. Several diagnostic categories with higher hospital discharge counts also had recorded deaths, although the magnitude of deaths varied across diagnostic groups. Death-to-discharge ratios showed wide variation, with values below 0.10 in some high-frequency discharge diagnoses and values greater than 1.00 in selected categories.

Hospital discharge and mortality rates per 1,000 live births

Rate-based indicators for 2021–2024 are presented in Table 4; Fig. 4. Hospital discharge rates ranged from 10.67 to 13.18 per 1,000 live births during this period. The lowest hospital discharge rate was observed in 2022, with 10.67 per 1,000 live births, while the highest rate was observed in 2024, with 13.18 per 1,000 live births. Mortality rates ranged from 1.38 to 1.47 per 1,000 live births. The mortality rate was 1.47 per 1,000 live births in 2021 and 1.44 per 1,000 live births in 2024. Mortality values for 2022 and 2023 were estimated by linear interpolation between the observed values for 2021 and 2024.

Epidemiological prioritization

The descriptive prioritization of selected CA is shown in Table 7. High-priority categories included Q25, congenital anomalies of great arteries; Q20, cardiac chamber and connection anomalies; Q79, congenital musculoskeletal and body wall defects; and Q24, other congenital heart defects. Moderate-priority categories included Q21, CA of cardiac septa; Q03, congenital hydrocephalus; and Q90, Down syndrome. Q53, undescended testis, was classified as low priority in the mortality-based prioritization despite its high hospital discharge burden. The prioritization integrated hospital burden and mortality indicators across selected ICD-10 categories.

Empirical findings

The analysis identified 43,134 hospital discharges associated with CA among infants under 1 year of age in Ecuador during 2012–2024. Cardiovascular, genitourinary, and musculoskeletal anomalies were the leading clinical system categories. Hospital discharges were significantly lower during 2020–2024 than during 2012–2019. Mortality indicators varied across ICD-10 diagnostic categories, and the correlation between hospital discharges and deaths was moderate. For 2021–2024, hospital discharge rates ranged from 10.67 to 13.18 per 1,000 live births, while mortality rates ranged from 1.38 to 1.47 per 1,000 live births. Mortality values for 2022 and 2023 were interpolated and are presented only for descriptive continuity.

Discussion

Principal findings

This national analysis of CA among infants under 1 year of age in Ecuador identified a substantial hospital burden during 2012–2024, with 43,134 hospital discharges associated with ICD-10 codes Q00–Q99. The distribution was concentrated in a limited number of diagnostic groups, particularly cardiovascular, genitourinary, and musculoskeletal anomalies. Hospital discharges were significantly lower during the pandemic/post-pandemic period than during 2012–2019. Mortality indicators varied widely across conditions, and only a moderate correlation was observed between hospital discharges and deaths. These findings show that hospital utilization and mortality records capture related but non-equivalent dimensions of congenital anomaly burden.

Interpretation of hospital burden

The predominance of conditions such as undescended testis, septal defects, great artery anomalies, and musculoskeletal anomalies is consistent with the type of information captured by hospital discharge systems. Diagnoses requiring inpatient evaluation, surgery, referral, or follow-up are more likely to appear in hospital records. Therefore, high discharge counts should be interpreted primarily as indicators of service demand and hospital workload. Conversely, conditions associated with early death, limited referral, outpatient management, or incomplete diagnostic confirmation may be less visible in discharge records. This pattern highlights the need to interpret hospital data alongside mortality and live birth statistics rather than relying on a single administrative source.

Mortality patterns and death-to-discharge ratios

Mortality indicators showed marked heterogeneity across ICD-10 categories. Complex CHD, valve anomalies, pulmonary anomalies, body wall defects, and selected multisystem conditions showed higher death-to-discharge ratios than more frequent diagnoses such as septal defects or undescended testis. This contrast suggests that frequency in hospital records does not necessarily correspond to mortality burden. Some conditions with relatively low hospital discharge counts accounted for a disproportionate number of deaths, while some high-frequency diagnoses had low mortality indicators. This distinction is important for public health planning because resource allocation should consider both hospital volume and fatal outcomes.

Discordance between hospital discharges and deaths

The moderate correlation between hospital discharges and deaths indicates partial agreement between hospital and mortality data, but also relevant discordance. Several diagnostic categories showed more deaths than hospital discharges in the same year, suggesting that fatal outcomes may occur outside the pathway captured by inpatient discharge records or may be coded differently across systems. Such discordance may reflect early neonatal death before referral, incomplete hospital capture, differences in certification or coding, or barriers to specialized care. Integrating discharge and mortality records is therefore essential in countries without active congenital anomaly registries because each source alone provides only a partial view of the burden.

Impact of the COVID-19 period

The reduction in hospital discharges during 2020–2024 compared with 2012–2019 is compatible with the broader disruption of healthcare services during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Possible contributors include reduced access to care, changes in hospital admission practices, postponement of elective or non-urgent procedures, reorganization of pediatric and surgical services, and changes in data reporting. The finding should not be interpreted as evidence of a true decline in the occurrence of CA. Rather, it indicates a reduction in recorded hospital utilization for these diagnoses during a period of major health system disruption. This interpretation is especially relevant for conditions whose diagnosis and management depend on timely referral and specialized services.

Population-level rates

The use of live birth denominators for 2021–2024 allowed partial contextualization of hospital discharge and mortality indicators. Hospital discharge rates ranged from 10.67 to 13.18 per 1,000 live births, while mortality rates ranged from 1.38 to 1.47 per 1,000 live births. However, the mortality pattern for this period must be interpreted cautiously because only 2021 and 2024 were observed mortality values, whereas 2022 and 2023 were interpolated. For this reason, the apparent smoothness of mortality rates should not be taken as evidence of true temporal stability. The short period with available denominators also limits conclusions about longer-term population-level trends.

Access to care and health system implications

The observed differences between high-volume diagnoses and high-mortality diagnoses have implications for health system planning. Frequent conditions generate substantial workload for pediatric, surgical, and follow-up services, whereas less frequent but high-mortality anomalies require timely diagnosis, referral, neonatal intensive care, and specialized management. These needs may not be distributed evenly across regions or populations. Geographic location, socioeconomic conditions, availability of prenatal diagnosis, referral pathways, transport, and access to pediatric surgery or cardiology services may influence whether affected infants are diagnosed, hospitalized, treated, or recorded. Strengthening care pathways for high-risk anomalies should therefore be considered alongside improvements in surveillance.

Possible influence of other temporal events

Temporal variation in selected CA may also be influenced by factors other than the COVID-19 pandemic. In Latin America, the Zika virus epidemic raised concern because of its association with congenital infections, microcephaly, and other central nervous system abnormalities. The present study was not designed to evaluate Zika-related congenital anomalies specifically, because the available data were aggregated and did not include maternal infection history, laboratory confirmation, geographic exposure, or individual clinical information. Nevertheless, future studies linking congenital anomaly records with maternal infection surveillance and geographic data could help clarify whether regional infectious events contributed to specific diagnostic patterns.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. It used national administrative datasets, covered more than a decade of hospital discharge records, and focused on infants under 1 year of age, the group in which CA have the greatest early-life impact. It also integrated hospital discharge records, mortality data, and live birth statistics, allowing comparison between healthcare utilization and fatal outcomes. The use of ICD-10 codes provided a standardized basis for classification across data sources. In a setting without an active integrated congenital anomaly registry, this combined approach provides a broader assessment than hospital data alone.

Limitations

This study also has important limitations. Hospital discharge records represent inpatient events and may include repeated admissions for the same infant, because individual identifiers were not available for deduplication or linkage. As a result, discharge counts cannot be interpreted as incidence or prevalence estimates. Mortality data were not available for all years, and interpolated values were used only for descriptive continuity. Mortality records may also be affected by under-registration, incomplete certification, diagnostic misclassification, and nonspecific coding. Hospital discharge data may underrepresent anomalies diagnosed prenatally, managed in outpatient settings, recorded after infancy, or associated with death before hospitalization. Finally, differences in coding practices across institutions and over time may affect comparability.

Implications for surveillance and public health

The findings support the need to strengthen CA surveillance in Ecuador. An integrated system should ideally link prenatal diagnosis, birth records, hospital discharges, outpatient care, surgical registries, and mortality records. Such integration would improve estimation of burden, reduce under-ascertainment, and allow better monitoring of outcomes across regions and clinical groups. The concentration of hospital discharges in selected diagnoses can guide planning for pediatric surgery, cardiology, neonatology, rehabilitation, and follow-up care. At the same time, high death-to-discharge ratios in selected anomalies highlight the need to improve early detection, referral pathways, and access to specialized care.

Future research

Future research should prioritize individual-level record linkage between birth, hospital, and mortality datasets. Regional analyses would help identify geographic differences in diagnosis, referral, hospitalization, and fatal outcomes. Studies assessing coding quality and diagnostic confirmation would improve confidence in administrative data. Additional work should incorporate prenatal diagnosis, outpatient management, surgical outcomes, and long-term follow-up. These steps would allow a more complete understanding of congenital anomaly epidemiology in Ecuador and would support targeted interventions to improve survival and quality of life for affected infants. Overall, this study shows that CA impose a persistent burden on infant health services in Ecuador and that hospital discharge and mortality data provide complementary perspectives. The findings emphasize the importance of interpreting administrative records cautiously while recognizing their value for public health planning. Strengthening surveillance, improving linkage between data systems, and expanding access to timely specialized care are central priorities for reducing preventable mortality and improving outcomes among infants affected by congenital anomalies.

Conclusion

CA represent a substantial burden among infants in Ecuador, with hospital discharges concentrated in cardiovascular, genitourinary, and musculoskeletal conditions, and mortality indicators varying markedly across diagnostic groups. The integration of hospital discharge, mortality, and live birth data showed that healthcare utilization and fatal outcomes provide complementary but non-equivalent perspectives on CA burden. These findings support the need to strengthen CA surveillance in Ecuador through improved data linkage, more complete case ascertainment, better cause-of-death certification, and timely referral pathways for high-risk conditions.
