A Scoping Review of Methods for Extrapolating Costs and Effects in Economic Evaluations of Remote Monitoring
Authors: Sabine Michelsen Raunbak, Jakob Juul Christensen, Ulla Møller Weinreich, Søren Paaske Johnsen, Flemming Witt Udsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTelehealth, including remote monitoring, has the potential to reduce healthcare resource use. However, economic evaluations often have insufficient methodological quality, including long-term investigation. The problem is that data are often unavailable for the entire time horizon of interest, but here different extrapolation methods can be applied.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to examine how long-term economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) have been designed and conducted with a particular focus on extrapolation methods, and to identify the applied extrapolation methods.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted according to guidelines in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The systematic search in Embase, PubMed, EconLit, Web of Science, NHS Economic Evaluation Database, and CINAHL, supplemented with a grey literature search in Google Scholar, identified economic evaluations, where minimum one alternative was telehealth, the time horizon exceeded 12 months, and extrapolation methods were applied. Last search date was 23 January 2025, and two reviewers screened the studies simultaneously. Data were extracted using a predefined data extraction sheet.ResultsA total of 57 studies were included in this scoping review. Use of simpler extrapolation methods (Profiles and Constant values) was prevalent in 84% and 57% of the studies, respectively. These simple methods were used both in combination and alone. Even when trial-based data were available for a shorter time horizon, the simpler methods were used just as frequently as the more advanced methods. Only 32% of the studies included a sensitivity analysis for investigating the impact of the time horizon on the decision. Furthermore, transparency was missing in the reporting of applied extrapolation methods, and explicit investigation of uncertainty from the choice of extrapolation method is warranted.ConclusionsThis scoping review finds that economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) mainly rely on simpler extrapolation methods with limited justification, validation, and uncertainty analysis. Further investigation into the implications of use of different extrapolation methods is warranted, and guidelines on use of extrapolation methods are needed to ensure transparency.RegistrationA protocol was published elsewhere.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s41669-026-00670-x.

Introduction

Globally, healthcare systems are under increasing pressure. Aging populations, accompanied by an increasing number of people with chronic conditions, and a shortage of healthcare professionals, challenge the balance between needs and available healthcare resources, e.g. in terms of costs or personnel. Telehealth has been proposed to ensure optimal use of healthcare resources [1–3]. Telehealth has many definitions and applications, and a categorization of this broad term is recommended [4]. Thereby, for the purpose of this review, telehealth is defined as delivery of healthcare services to patients over a geographical distance, using a digital device. There is no limitation to the modalities through which telehealth is delivered to the patients, and may occur as audio, video, or text messages. Patients monitor their own disease and transfer relevant data to the healthcare personnel. Based on assessment of the transferred data, the healthcare personnel can provide relevant services to the patients. In recent literature, this is also referred to as remote monitoring. Therefore, we include both terms in this review [5, 6]. Telehealth, including remote monitoring, has the potential to be a safe and effective way to reduce the need for acute care and improve patient empowerment. However, this potential is related to several factors, e.g. appropriate patient populations, the design of the telehealth intervention (remote monitoring), and the implementation among both patients and providers [7–9]. To ensure that telehealth (remote monitoring) does not add to the pressure on the healthcare system, it is important to conduct robust economic evaluations to assess the health economic consequences.

Several reviews of economic evaluations of telehealth interventions, including remote monitoring tools, have been published [4, 10–16]. All conclude that many of the economic evaluations have been conducted inconsistently with methodological guidelines for economic evaluations [17–21], and need a more transparent reporting of methodology and results [4]. Many of the reviews conclude that most of the economic evaluations were short-term [11, 13, 14, 16], and that there are a lack of good quality data and measures [11], and that argumentation for the time horizon is missing [16]. Whitten et al. 2002 [14] found that only 20% of the studies had a longer time horizon than 12 months, Mistry et al. 2012 [4] found 56 studies with a time period of less than 2 years and 11 studies that did not report the time period. Udsen et al. 2014 [16] and Kidholm et al. 2018 [15] found no studies with a time horizon longer than 12 months. However some of the exclusion criteria for the reviews could have an effect on the limiting studies with longer time horizons as they exclude model-based economic evaluation [14–16].

In economic evaluations, the time horizon is a central part of the design, as it defines the time period in which relevant costs and effects are included in the analysis. Guidelines for economic evaluation state that the time horizon of an economic evaluation should be long enough to include all relevant cost and effects [22–27].

The danger of using a time horizon that is too short is that relevant costs and effects could be excluded from the evaluation. Thereby the results, that should guide decision makers, are based on an incomplete foundation [17, 18, 28]. An example is the review by Kidholm et al. 2018 [15], which demonstrated that home monitoring for patients with chronic conditions increased the cost per patient due to home monitoring generating substantial intervention-related costs, including expenses associated with delivering the monitoring activities and providing the necessary equipment. However, the studies from the review were based on economic evaluations with a time horizon shorter than 12 months. Here, it could be hypothesized that with a longer time horizon, additional relevant costs and effects would have been captured. For instance, the telehealth intervention (remote monitoring) may have a preventive effect on acute exacerbations, potentially reducing hospital admissions, or the effectiveness of the telehealth intervention (remote monitoring) could increase over time as patients become more familiar with the technology. Such developments could ultimately lead to a decrease in the incremental costs. Contrary to this, an economic evaluation from 2016 investigating telehealth, including remote monitoring, for patients with chronic obstructive lung disease (COPD) also showed that the results were highly sensitive to which intervention costs were included in the analysis. At low intervention costs, the telehealth intervention was found to be cost-effective, whereas at high intervention costs, it was not cost-effective [29]. This highlights the importance of accurately identifying and including all relevant cost components in economic evaluations. If important cost elements are omitted, the consequences of these omissions will not be reflected in the conclusions of the economic evaluation.

Often, the availability of data, dictates the choice of time horizon, which may be inappropriate [28]. When data are only available for a proportion of the relevant time horizon of the economic evaluation, extrapolation can be applied [23–25, 30]. In the context of economic evaluations, extrapolation refers to the process of predicting long-term outcomes (i.e. cost and utilities), based on available short-term data [31]. Extrapolation can be based on observed data from short term studies, or by use of decision analytical models [30]. Different guidelines exists in relation to extrapolation of clinical effectiveness and time-to-event data [24, 31–33], where observed time-to-event data are extrapolated by assuming an underlying distribution of the observed time-to-event data [24]. Furthermore, different guidelines state that it is important to report the use of the extrapolation methods properly and to investigate the associated uncertainty [22–26]. However, clinical extrapolation methods are insufficient for extrapolation costs and utilities [31, 34], and guidelines in relation to specific extrapolation of costs and utilities are missing [34]. A paper from 2017 identified and described different methods used for extrapolation of costs and utilities from the literature, and found a methodological heterogeneity in the methods used [34]. As different extrapolation methods will create different cost and utility estimates, there is a risk that the choice of method will affect the conclusion in the economic evaluations [33, 34]. Uncertainty regarding how the choice of extrapolation method influences the overall conclusions of the economic evaluation further increases the importance of conducting sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of the chosen time horizon on these results [33, 35].

The lack in longer time horizons and availability of long-term data highlight the need for use of extrapolation methods in economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) in order to ensure that telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) that are meant to decrease the use of resources in the healthcare system do not lead to an increase. As methods for the economic evaluation of telehealth intervention, including remote monitoring, differ from those used for pharmaceuticals [36], and because the choice of extrapolation method can influence the results and overall conclusions of an economic evaluation, it may be hypothesized that telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) require and employ specific extrapolation methods.

However, none of the above-mentioned reviews of economic evaluation of telehealth, including remote monitoring, mention the use of extrapolation methods. Therefore, it is important to investigate how long-term economic evaluations for telehealth interventions (remote monitoring), which use extrapolation of costs and/or effects, are conducted, how the studies are designed, and which extrapolations methods are used for predicting costs and effects. To our knowledge, Bojke et al. 2017 [34] is the only paper that identifies methods for extrapolation specific to costs and utilities in economic evaluations, and therefore, these methods will be used as a starting point for the identification of the different extrapolation methods in the literature in this review.

The objective of this study is to examine how long-term economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) have been designed and conducted with a particular focus on extrapolation methods, and to identify the different applied extrapolation methods.

Methods

This review was conducted as a scoping review, given the appropriateness of this method for identifying and mapping evidence on a research topic with a focus on the types of studies rather than their results [37]. This scoping review was conducted in line with the guidelines from the Joanna Briggs Institute, and the PRISMA-ScR guideline [38–42]. As the protocol is published elsewhere [5] it is summarized briefly below.

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in the following electronic databases: Embase (Elsevier), PubMed (National Library of Medicine), EconLit (ProQuest), Web of Science (Clarivate), NHS Economic Evaluation Database (NHS Centre for Reviews and Dissemination), and CINAHL (EBSCO Industries). The systematic search was supplemented with a grey literature search, and citation chasing of the included studies. The grey literature search was conducted in incognito mode in Google Scholar, with inclusion of the 300 most relevant results based on the guidelines from Haddaway et al. [43]. Citation chasing was performed as both backward- and forward citation chasing of the included studies from the systematic- and grey literature search. The citation chasing was performed with the aid of Citationchaser, a free shiny app for conducting citation chasing [44, 45]. The overall search strategy and use of search terms was similar in each of the electronic databases and Google Scholar, but with adjustments of specific search terms in each database (e.g. MESH or Emtree). The process of study selection included title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening conducted by two independent reviewers (S.M.R., J.J.C.). Disagreements were resolved by consensus between three reviewers (S.M.R., J.J.C., F.W.U.). The relevance of the individual studies was based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria from the protocol [5], which are summarized in Table 2. Additionally, there were no restrictions of the year of publication, and the last search date was 23 January 2025. Data Charting

Data extraction was based on a predefined template in Microsoft Excel developed in consensus between three reviewers (S.R.M., J.J.C., F.W.U.). Contrary to the protocol [5], one reviewer (S.M.R.) conducted the data extraction. Any uncertainties were discussed in plenum.

The extraction template was divided into the following topics: 1) characteristics of the studies, including descriptive information of the studies on author, publication year, title, country, disease, aim, and conclusion; 2) design of the economic evaluation; including description of methodological characteristics of the economic evaluation, which possibly could have impact on the choice of extrapolation method, consisting of type of evaluation, alternatives, perspective, time horizon, costs, effects, description of decision analytical model and source of evidence for probabilities, costs and effects; 3) use of extrapolation methods; including description of which methods are used for costs, effects and overall, and argumentation and discussion of limitations related to the choice of extrapolation method; and 4) use of sensitivity analyses for describing the uncertainty for the chosen time horizon, consisting of type and result of the sensitivity analysis. Deterministic sensitivity analysis

In characterizing the use of extrapolation methods, four predefined categories identified by Bojke et al., were used [34]. These categories were used as the starting point for this review, as they represent, to our knowledge, the only predefined methods for extrapolation specific to economic evaluations described in the literature. However, it was possible to create new categories if deemed necessary. The four categories for extrapolation methods included:Profiles, where costs or utilities were attached to short-term events such as cost per telehealth (remote monitoring) consultation or monitoring alert. This method is simple to implement, but the methodology employed to achieve these outcomes is not directly specified, and it is challenging to determine whether temporal dynamics have been incorporated.Constant values, where a fixed cost or utility is attached to a health state, for example by assigning a disutility to patients having an acute exacerbation, often the disutility will be similar between the alternatives. This method typically does not account for the effects of an aging population and assumes temporal homogeneity in the treatment effect.Constant marginal change to moving baseline, where a constant additive or multiplicative change in costs or utilities to the baseline value are applied over time. This could consist of an age-related disutility per year in relation to an aging population. This method reflects the effect of a given parameter, for example age, to the population. However, this effect is assumed to be consistent over time, and data may not be available for the multiplicative approach.Regression approach, where patient-level data are used to generate an algorithm linking disease-specific measure to cost or utilities, for example remotely monitored indicators to healthcare utilization. They allow explicit modelling of time and covariates but require detailed data and appropriate handling of uncertainty [34].

Deviations to the protocol [5] is described in the following. Cost analysis (CA) was included in the data items on types of economic evaluation, as we observed relevant studies in the data extraction process, where studies defined as cost analysis, meet the definition of economic evaluation from the protocol and used extrapolation methods. CA was handled similar to the other economic evaluation in the data extraction process. Furthermore, the data item Study type was reclassified into three new data items, Model type, Health states, and Cycle length, in order to describe economic evaluations where a decision analytical model is used in more detail, and furthermore, to help in classifying the used extrapolation method. The data item, Description of type of evidence, was divided further into Evidence of probabilities, Evidence of Costs, and Evidence of Effects, to add more detail in the description of the type of evidence used for the economic evaluation. The evidence was categorized after three overall categories, Literature-based, including data from published studies, open public databases, expert opinion, meta-analyses, and reviews, Trial-data and Health Record Data. Furthermore, the categories can be combined, such as Literature-based + Trial-data. The data item, Methods for extrapolation: Profiles, Constant values, Constant marginal change to moving baseline, Regression approach or possible new defined groups, was further divided into several data items to conduct a more detailed description. The data items consisted of the overall use of extrapolation methods in the study, and additionally for costs and effects separately. Furthermore, arguments, limitations and validation of the use of the specific method were included. If the description of each data item was indirect, or insufficient in the studies, the data extraction was based on the subjective understanding from the reviewer. Not Specified was used when there was not sufficient description to retrieve the data item, and Not Applicable was used when the data items were not possible to retrieve due to not being relevant or not being included.

Synthesis of Results

Following the data extraction template, results were synthesized on the basis of the template’s major headings (i.e., Characteristics of studies, Economic evaluation design, Extrapolation methods used, and Types of sensitivity analyses for the time horizon). Results were generally presented as tables with data for each study, and as summarizing descriptions. Extrapolation methods were further mapped in relation to frequency, combination and usage trends. Finally, a narrative synthesis of the overall results was included.

Design and Conduct of the Included Studies

All extracted data are presented in Online Resource 2. Furthermore, a table with the baseline characteristics of the studies is included in Online Resource 3.

To describe the study design, sources of evidence and inclusion of sensitivity analyses in the included studies the design and characteristics in relation to publication year, country, disease, definition of telehealth intervention (remote monitoring), and comparator of each study are presented in Online Resource 3. In summary, the included studies were published from 2003 to 2025 and were based on patient populations from several countries, in particular USA (14/57, 25%) [49, 56, 70, 76, 77, 81, 82, 84, 85, 88, 95, 97, 98, 101], and UK (8/57, 14%) [52, 55, 62, 75, 90, 91, 93, 96], but also Argentina [60, 66], Australia [53, 92], Austria [94], Belgium [46, 86], Canada [72–74, 79], China [57, 63], France [58, 68, 100], Germany [29, 48, 71, 89, 99], Italy [87], Japan [54, 67], Korea [50], Mexico [78], the Netherlands [51, 59, 64, 65, 80], Poland [61], and Multinational [47, 69, 83]. The studies investigated different diseases, main disease included heart failure (21/57, 37%) [47, 51, 55, 58, 59, 61, 63, 64, 66, 70, 73, 80, 82–85, 88, 89, 93, 94, 98], diabetes (8/57, 14%) [46, 62, 67, 78, 86, 92, 99, 101], and cardiac implantable electronic devices (7/57, 12%) [54, 56, 68, 77, 79, 96, 100]. The remaining diseases consisted of age-related macular degeneration [81], stem cell transplantation [48], atrial fibrillation [57, 71, 76], cardiovascular disease [69, 90], cerebrovascular disease [74], chronic kidney disease [72], COPD [29, 95], coronary artery disease [53], cystic fibrosis [87], depression [49], hypertension [60, 75, 91, 97], multiple sclerosis [65], and Parkinson’s disease [52], and varied from consisting of 2–7% (1/57–4/57) of the included studies. In relation to the telehealth interventions (remote monitoring), some overall differences were observed in relation to how the data were measured (invasively or not invasively), and if the feedback system by the health care personnel was assisted by algorithm-based alerts. Therefore, the studies were divided into four overall groups, consisting of: remote monitoring with transmission of noninvasively measured data (RM) (31/57, 54%) [29, 46–50, 52–54, 60–63, 65, 67, 69, 72, 74, 75, 78, 80, 86–88, 90–94, 97, 99, 101], Remote monitoring with transmission of invasively measured data (RM+I) (13/57, 23%) [51, 55, 66, 76, 77, 82–85, 89, 98, 100], remote monitoring with transmission of noninvasively measured data in combination with algorithm-based alerts (RM+A) (8/57, 14%) [57, 58, 64, 70, 71, 73, 81, 95], remote monitoring with transmission of invasively measured data in combination with algorithm-based alerts (RM+IA) (4/57, 7%) [56, 68, 79, 96], and both RM and RM+A (1/57, 2%) [59]. The comparator to the telehealth (remote monitoring) consisted of only usual care (48/57, 84%) [29, 46–48, 50–61, 63–70, 72–74, 76, 79, 81–92, 94–96, 98–101], other remote comparators and usual care (4/57, 7%) [75, 80, 93, 97], nonremote comparator (3/57, 5%) [49, 71, 77], and nonremote comparator and usual care (2/57, 4%) [62, 78].

Furthermore, the characteristics of the studies relating to the type of economic evaluations, model type, perspective, time horizon, source of evidence for input parameters, use and result of sensitivity analysis for the time horizon are presented in Online Resource 3. The types of economic evaluation were cost-utility analysis (CUA) (44/57, 77%) [29, 46, 47, 49–55, 57–70, 73–86, 89–93, 95, 98], cost-analysis (CA) (5/57, 9%) [71, 87, 88, 94, 97, 100], cost-consequence analysis (CCA) (2/57, 4%) [56, 96], cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) (2/57, 4%) [48, 88], cost-benefit analysis (CBA) (1/57, 2%) [99], cost minimization (CM) (1/57, 2%) [72], and 4% (2/57) [81, 101] were combined types of economic evaluations. The type of decision analytical models used for the economic evaluation were cohort models (39/57, 68%) [29, 46, 47, 49–58, 60, 63, 66–72, 74, 76, 79–85, 88, 90–93, 95, 96, 98] and individual patient models (12/57, 21%) [59, 62, 64, 73, 75, 77, 78, 86, 89, 94, 99, 101]. However, some of the studies had not specified the decision analytical model (1/57, 2%) [65] or were not based on a decision analytical model (5/57, 9%) [48, 61, 87, 97, 100]. In 81% (46/57) [29, 46, 48–50, 52–57, 60, 61, 63, 64, 66–68, 70–74, 76–80, 82–84, 86–99, 101] of the studies, a healthcare system perspective is used, but broader perspectives were also found, including health care system perspective and societal perspective (3/57, 5%) [47, 51, 65], limited societal perspective (2/57, 4%) [62, 75], and societal perspective (6/57, 11%) [58, 59, 69, 81, 85, 100]. Studies included a time horizon of 5 years or less (17/57, 30%) [48, 49, 52, 53, 56, 68, 69, 72, 79, 82, 84, 88, 92, 94, 97, 98, 100], 10 years or more (12/57, 21%) [54, 55, 57, 58, 63, 67, 71, 83, 87, 89, 96, 99], and lifetime (28/57, 49%) [29, 46, 47, 50, 51, 59–62, 64–66, 70, 73–78, 80, 81, 85, 86, 90, 91, 93, 95, 101]. Studies were based on evidence solely from published literature (28/57, 49%) [29, 47, 52, 54, 55, 57–59, 63–68, 70–72, 74, 76, 79, 82, 84, 85, 88, 93–96], trial-data and literature (22/57, 39%) [46, 48, 50, 51, 53, 56, 60–62, 73, 75, 78, 80, 81, 83, 86, 90–92, 99, 101], health record data and literature (3/57, 5%) [49, 77, 87], trial-data (2/57, 4%) [69, 98], health record data (1/57, 2%) [100], and trial-data and health record data (1/57, 2%) [97]. In 32% (18/57) [29, 50, 51, 56, 58, 60, 72, 75–79, 84–86, 91, 92, 96] of the studies, a sensitivity analysis concerning how the chosen time horizon of the study impacted on the decision was reported. Of them all were deterministic sensitivity analyses, and the decision was sensitive, meaning that the decision changed dependent on the time horizon, in 39% (7/18) [29, 75, 78, 84–86, 96] of these studies.

Narrative Synthesis of Results

Across the included studies, there is a tendency to use simpler methods including Profiles, 48/57 (84%) of the studies, and Constant values, 34/57 (57%) of the studies, for extrapolation of costs and effects in economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring). These methods are used both alone and in combination with each other. Even when trial-based data are available for a short time horizon, where it would be possible to apply more advanced methods, such as Constant value to a marginal change and Regression approach, the simple methods are still used just as frequently as the more advanced methods (Tables 4 and 5).

Contrary to guidelines for economic evaluations, only 18/57 (32%) of the studies in this scoping review included a sensitivity analysis for the investigation of how changes in the time horizon affect the decision. Additionally, none of the included studies reports any systematic argumentation for the use of their methods for extrapolation, and their related limitations. Furthermore, no studies validate the specific choice of extrapolation methods.

In the identification of the different extrapolation methods, a possible subgroup of Regression approach was identified. Here, some studies estimated their long-term costs and effectiveness by using models with individual patient data from other published studies. The studies defined their cohort characteristics, unit costs relevant for their setting, and then used these as input in the other published model. Based on the input, the published model used its own individual patient data and predicted long-term costs and effects. The studies then used this output from the published model in their own economic evaluation. This may be a possible road for overcoming the challenges of the sparse availability of data for long-term economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring). However, some of the included studies, which used another published model, lack transparent reporting on the use of the published model. i.e. in relation to what input is used, how the published model is designed, the output of the published model, the limitations on the use of the published model, and/or the uncertainty in the use of published model.

Discussion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive and updated overview of the existing literature on the use of extrapolation methods in economic evaluation of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring). It investigates the design and application of extrapolation methods in economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) and, to our knowledge, is the first review to systematically do so. This scoping review finds that simpler extrapolation methods are used more frequently, and no trends in the usage of the different extrapolation methods were found. There is minimal justification for the choice of methods, no consistent terminology for describing extrapolation methods, and a lack of sensitivity analysis to assess the uncertainty associated with the use of extrapolation methods or the impact of the time horizon on decision uncertainty.

This scoping review found that there was a tendency for using simpler methods for extrapolation in economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring). These results are in line with Bojke et. al., who conclude that simpler extrapolation methods are commonly used [34]. Even though no guidelines state that more advanced extrapolation methods are better than simple methods [24], even small inconsistencies in the estimation of costs and effects in the short term can potentially affect the decision in the long term. The simpler methods assume that a given cost is constant over the time horizon, which rarely is the case. An advantage of using more advanced methods is that they can add important aspects to the calculation of future costs and effects. Patient characteristics, including age, disease severity, and comorbidities, are assumed to influence the future costs and effects [34]. More advanced methods such as Constant value to a marginal change and Regression approach, have the potential to incorporate age as a factor in the estimation of future costs and effects, and thereby reflect the consequences of increasing age [34]. However, these advanced methods come with the caveat that they require individual patient-level data, which may be difficult for researchers to access. Additionally, their complexity may make it harder for decision-makers to interpret and trust when making decisions between different alternative treatment options.

This scoping review found that none of the included studies had a systematic argumentation for the use of their methods for extrapolation, and/or discussion of the related limitations. The chosen time horizon was often not investigated in terms of its impact on the decision; this is similar to what Whitten et al. 2002[14] found, where 62.5% missed detail in sensitivity analyses generally. Even though this requirement has not been described, it may indicate a lack of focus on the choice of different extrapolation methods and the related consequences for using these methods in the economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring). Since different extrapolation methods can lead to different results, ensuring sufficient reporting of the chosen extrapolation methods and their related uncertainties in the economic evaluation is crucial for making the best decision. Transparency in the reporting of the use of extrapolation methods is essential for others to understand, use, and even replicate the results. As economic evaluations are aimed at supporting decision-making, they can have significant implications if uncertainties are not covered and understood sufficiently. To date, no studies have systematically investigated how the choice of extrapolation method influences the outcomes of economic evaluations. Bojke et al. emphasize the need for applied examples to explore the implications of using different extrapolation methods [34]. However, a need for investigating the implications of using different extrapolation methods in practice is challenged by the fact that extrapolations are utilized when prospective data are lacking.

Currently, no formal guidelines exist for the application of extrapolation methods. Future guidelines would benefit from clear recommendations on how to ensure transparent reporting of these methods and on what specific elements should be included. In addition, guidance is needed on the assessment and reporting of uncertainties associated with the application of different extrapolation approaches. This work could potentially be conducted based on the framework and definitions of extrapolation methods already established by Bojke et al. [34].

A key strength of this review is its systematic and comprehensive approach to the literature search, which includes multiple electronic databases, grey literature, and citation chasing. Notably, citation chasing contributes significantly, identifying 17 of the included studies. This may be owing to the Citation Chaser tool accessing additional databases, not relying on search terms, or limitations in the original search strategy in fully capturing the research field. However, this study also has several limitations: first, the scope of the review could have been broader and did not include the broader use of extrapolation methods in economic evaluation. Second, it did not include a formal quality assessment of the included studies. This is not a requirement for scoping reviews but could have contributed to the results with analysis of association between quality and extrapolation methods. Third, only one reviewer was used for data extraction even though uncertainties were discussed within the author group. Forth, the search terms are formulated in consistency with the definition of telehealth (remote monitoring) in this review. This narrows the search and could potentially be missing the inclusion of newer search terms on digital health and apps. The definition of telehealth (remote monitoring) also leads to exclusion of some digital interventions in relation to e.g. digital screening interventions, and telehealth interventions without remote monitoring. This could potentially also miss out on different patient populations in relation to the high degree of self-management in remote monitoring, e.g. mental health interventions.

Conclusions

This scoping review finds that economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) primarily use simpler extrapolation methods. There is minimal argumentation provided for the use of the extrapolation methods, and a lack of validation and sensitivity analyses to assess the uncertainty associated with the use of extrapolation methods or the impact of the time horizon on decision uncertainty. This may potentially affect the methodological quality of the economic evaluations of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring), and thereby the foundation for decisions regarding the implementation of telehealth interventions (remote monitoring) in the healthcare system. To improve future research, both related to economic evaluations of telehealth (remote monitoring), but also from a broader perspective, clear guidelines for the use of extrapolation methods are needed. Additionally, an applied example of the implication of using different extrapolation methods could enhance understanding and support more informed methodological choices in the use of the methods in the future.
