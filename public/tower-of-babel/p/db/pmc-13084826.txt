Sex differences in risk factor control and medication adherence post-ACS: insights from the TEXTMEDS randomised clinical trial
Authors: Shiva Raj Mishra, Simone Marschner, Haeri Min, Shehane Mahendran, Aravinda Thiagalingam, Rohan Poulter, Julie Redfern, David B Brieger, Peter L Thompson, Graham S Hillis, Nicholas Collins, Pratap Shetty, Michele McGrady, Christian Hamilton-Craig, Nadarajah Kangaharan, John Atherton, Andrew Maiorana, Harry Klimis, Craig Juergens, Clara K Chow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPoor adherence to risk factor control and life-saving medications is a key factor affecting long-term patient prognosis. Evidence indicates that sex plays a significant role in the uptake of both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, ultimately influencing long-term outcomes. This study aimed to quantify sex differences in risk factor management and medication adherence following acute coronary syndrome (ACS).MethodsThis is a secondary analysis of the TEXTMEDS randomised clinical trial - a single-blind, multicentre randomised controlled trial of patients post-ACS. We compared sex differences in achieving clinical and lifestyle targets for secondary prevention, namely blood pressure control (<140/90 mm Hg), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (<1.8 mmol/L), healthy body mass index (BMI) (<25 kg/m²), regular physical activity (Global Physical Activity Questionnaire score ≥600), smoking status and adherence to cardioprotective medications (aspirin, beta blockers, ACE/angiotensin receptor blockers, statins, antiplatelets), using adjusted logistic regression models. Medication adherence was defined as taking ≥80% of prescribed doses in the month prior to follow-up, across all five drug classes, unless contraindicated.ResultsOf 1379 patients (mean age 58.5±10.7 years; 1095 (79.4%) male), females were less likely than men to achieve LDL-C targets (adjusted OR (aOR): 0.61, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.82) and engage in regular physical activity (aOR: 0.61, CI 0.47 to 0.80), but more likely to achieve a healthy BMI (aOR: 1.47, CI 1.04 to 2.06). Female patients are less likely to adhere to their medication compared with male counterparts (aOR: 0.68, CI 0.50 to 0.92). However, this association weakened and lost statistical significance after further adjustment for socio-economic factors (aOR: 0.71, CI 0.50 to 1.03). There were no significant interactions between sociodemographic or clinical factors and sex in relation to overall medication adherence (P-interactions >0.05).ConclusionThis study reveals that female patients are less likely to achieve LDL-C targets and engage in physical activity but more likely to maintain a healthy BMI. Although females showed lower medication adherence, this association weakened after adjusting for socio-economic factors. These findings highlight the importance of sex-sensitive strategies focusing on risk factor control and medication adherence for improving cardiovascular health outcomes.Trial registration numberACTRN12613000793718.

Introduction

Globally, acute coronary syndrome (ACS) affects nearly 200 million people, causing ∼8 million deaths.1 2 Secondary prevention using pharmacological interventions reduces morbidity and mortality after ACS. However, adherence to secondary prevention medications has been shown to be poor after ACS in previous studies.3 4 Non-adherence can put patients with ACS at increased risk of recurrent cardiovascular events, particularly soon after discharge.5

Current clinical guidelines recommend ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), statins, β-blockers and antiplatelet agents for secondary prevention of ACS. A high rate of non-adherence exists in healthcare utilisation post ACS, with women having a two to three times higher likelihood of adverse outcomes compared with their male counterparts.6 7 Additionally, numerous prior studies have suggested that women are more likely to be undertreated with evidence-based therapies post ACS compared with men.810 Medication adherence is affected by a multitude of factors. By identifying the factors that are associated with poor medication adherence to cardioprotective medications such as ACEI or ARBs, beta-blockers, statin lipid-lowering drugs, aspirin and ADP receptor antagonists, we may be able to implement targeted approaches to improve secondary prevention measures post ACS. This study aims to assess sex-related differences in risk factor control management and medication adherence following ACS.

Methods

This study is a secondary analysis of the TEXT messages to improve MEDication adherence and Secondary prevention after ACS (TEXTMEDS) randomised clinical trial - a single-blind, multicentre study of patients post-ACS.11 12 Controls received usual secondary prevention care; the intervention group additionally received weekly motivational text messages on medications and healthy lifestyle, with two-way communication options (text or telephone). The primary end point was self-reported medication adherence, defined as >80% adherence to up to five cardioprotective medications at both 6 and 12 months.

Study population

The randomised clinical trial TEXTMEDS recruited patients with previous ACS from 13 urban and 5 rural centres across three time zones and five (of eight) states and territories of Australia and followed them up for 12 months. Inclusion criteria required a confirmed ACS diagnosis (acute myocardial infarction or unplanned hospitalisation for coronary ischaemia with significant coronary disease or prior revascularisation), ownership of a texting-capable mobile phone, English literacy, life expectancy over 6 months and informed consent. A screening log recorded basic demographics and reasons for non-participation were maintained for those ineligible or who declined to participate. Details of the trial methodology, including study design, sampling, recruitment and variables, statistical analysis plan and main results are available elsewhere.11 12

Data collection

Physical activity was measured using Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) and categorised as active (≥600 Metabolic Equivalent of Tasks (METs)-min/week), or inactive (<600).3 13 Health-related quality of life was assessed with the 12-item Short-Form Health Survey version 2 (SF-12v2):14 a score of >50 was defined as having normal physical or mental health function. Anxiety and depression was measured using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7)15 and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) questionnaires16. Dietary intake was assessed using the WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance (STEPS) diet questionnaire.17

Variables

The definition of covariates is adapted from a previously published work from the TEXTMEDS trial.11 12 Baseline data collection included sociodemographic (age, sex, ethnicity, employment status, marital status), socio-economic (completed high school, income status), clinical factors (blood pressure (BP), total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, heart rate, haemoglobin, creatinine, platelets, white cell count, troponin levels, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, smoking, alcohol status) (online eSupplemental table 1). Other patient-related data collected included use of formal cardiac rehabilitation programmes, visits to the general practitioner (GP) or cardiologist after discharge and prior cardiovascular/cerebrovascular events, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and history of depression. Participants who reported that they currently smoke one or more cigarettes per day were categorised as people who smoke currently. Alcohol status was determined by participants self-reporting whether they currently drank alcohol at least once per week. Risk factor targets were defined as follows: controlled BP (<140/90 mm Hg), LDL-C (<1.8 mmol/L), a healthy BMI (<25 kg/m²), regular physical activity (GPAQ score ≥600) and smoking status (person who smokes currently or person who has quit smoking/person who does not smoke). Psychological variables, including anxiety (GAD-7: categorised as no, mild, moderate and severe anxiety), depression (PHQ-9: categorised as no, mild, moderate, moderately severe and severe depression) and mental health-related quality of life (SF-12: categorised as Yes, No taking cut-off score >50), were included as potential predictors of medication adherence (online eSupplemental table 1).

The TEXTMEDS trial included 30-day recall on self-reported medication adherence at 6 and 12 months for five classes of cardioprotective medications such as ACEI or ARBs, beta-blockers, statin lipid-lowering drugs, aspirin and ADP receptor antagonists.18 Patients were defined as adherent if they took more than 80% of the prescribed medications (more than 24 out of 30 days) in the preceding 1 month at 6-month and 12-month if not otherwise contraindicated.19 Those with contraindicated medication were removed from the denominator.

Statistical analyses

Patient characteristics are provided by means and SD for continuous variables and by frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The univariate associations (p<0.10) as well as published literature (online eSupplemental table 2) guided variable selection.20 21 Modelling was conducted in two steps: each addressing research questions related to risk factor control and medication adherence. First, unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models were used to assess the relationship between sex and prespecified clinical risk factor targets accounting for age and other clinical variables. Second, for adherence, we used four separate logistic regression models to assess the impact of adding covariate groups (sex, demographics, clinical factors, socio-economic status (SES)) on the association with sex. Further, we calculate the interaction of sex and predictors of overall medication adherence and by drug classes. This involved repeating the univariable and multivariable modelling procedures for each drug class and retaining the final list of variables that we were statistically significantly associated with adherence to that specific class in final interaction analysis following a previous study.21 A complete-case analysis was conducted, restricting analyses to participants with no missing data for variables included in the regression models. OR with 95% CIs were presented. Data analysis was conducted using R V.4.4.0 and visualisation was done in SAS V.9.4.

In terms of cardiovascular risk factors, the average BMI was 29.1 (SD 5.5) kg/m2, 26.2% (n=361) were people who smoke currently, 46.8% (n=644) had alcohol use, 36.6% (n=505) were physically inactive, 5.7% (n=79) adhered to the recommended servings of fruit and vegetables, 23.6% (n=325) had diabetes and 47.6% (n=656) had hypertension. On average, participants were taking 6.3 (SD 2.1) medications for comorbidities. More than 38.5% (n=531) had at least one comorbidity; 18.2% (n=251) had a history of depression, 4.4% (n=61) had a physical disability and 22.2% (n=306) had a prior history of coronary heart disease (CHD). Regarding health-seeking behaviour, 85.4% (n=1177) visited GP, only 53.4% (n=736) visited cardiologist after initial discharge, 54.3% (n=680) attended at least one cardiac rehabilitation programme session. The mean Mental Component Summary-12 for this sample was 44.6 (SD 13.0) indicating they did not have clinical depression, while the mean Physical Component Summary-12 was 44.7 (SD 12.5) indicating they had a physical condition.

At 12 months post-ACS, distributions of lifestyle factors varied between male and female participants (online eSupplemental tables 3 and 4). 73.2% of males (n=711) had controlled BP (<140/90 mm Hg) compared with 70.0% of females (n=166), though females had a marginally higher proportion with elevated BP. Regarding lipid levels, 49.4% of males (n=457) had optimal LDL cholesterol levels (<1.8 mmol/L) compared with 39.2% of females (n=89), indicating a greater proportion of females had elevated LDL-C. In terms of body weight, 26.2% of females (n=61) achieved healthy BMI (<25 kg/m2) compared to 20.1% of males (n=187). Physical activity levels were higher among males, with 63.5% (n=695) meeting recommended activity levels (GPAQ score ≥600) compared with only 50.4% of females (n=143). Interestingly, 19.4% of females (n=48) reported current smoking compared with 15.3% of males (n=153).

Medication adherence

Overall, the prevalence of medication adherence at both 6 months and 12 months was 52.3%: which was significantly different between the sexes with 53.9% in male and 46.1% female (p=0.02). Females were less likely to have medication adherence compared with male counterparts. In a minimally adjusted model for age and ethnicity, the adjusted OR (aOR) of medication adherence for females was 0.73 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.95). This association remained robust after adjustment for clinical factors (aOR: 0.68, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.92); however, it weakened and lost statistical significance after further adjustment for socio-economic factors (aOR: 0.71, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.03) (figure 1). In the fully adjusted model, only the statin drug class shows sex difference in medication adherence (aOR: 0.37, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.76) (figure 1). A greater number of comorbidities was associated with lower odds of overall medication adherence (aOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.97) (online eSupplemental table 5).

Interaction with sex

There were no significant interactions for sociodemographic and clinical factors with sex for medication adherence overall (all P-interactions >0.05) (table 3). However, the total number of medications showed a statistically significant sex interaction in adherence to beta-blockers, with stronger association among female (aOR: 12.2, 95% CI 3.8 to 39.1) compared with male (aOR: 1.7, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.0) (P-interactions <0.002, using a Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons) (table 3). There was an indication of a potential interaction with sex and a history of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) for adherence to beta-blockers (p=0.006); however, no women in the sample had a history of CABG. For remaining drug classes, no significant interactions were found for sociodemographic and clinical factors with sex.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study highlights important sex differences in risk factor control and medication adherence following ACS. Higher medication count was linked to better adherence, while more comorbidities were associated with lower adherence. No significant interactions were observed between sex and sociodemographic and clinical factors for medication adherence overall and for most drug classes except beta-blockers. Adherence to beta-blockers showed a statistically significant sex interaction with total medication count and potentially history of prior CABG.

Comparison with previous studies

The findings of this study align with and build on existing literature on the impact of sex, age and comorbidities on risk factor control and lifestyle behaviours following ACS.2224 Non-adherence to statins was particularly evident among women, reflecting greater reporting and perception of side effects, lower perceived cardiovascular risk and psychosocial barriers that may influence medication-taking behaviour.2224 Older adults showed better LDL-C control and reduced smoking, consistent with studies showing that older patients often adhere more closely to secondary prevention therapies, such as statins and smoking cessation programmes.25 26 However, female participants had lower odds of achieving LDL-C targets and engaging in regular physical activity, reflecting sex disparities in cardiovascular risk factor management, where women face greater barriers.23 24 The higher likelihood of maintaining a healthy BMI in women aligns with some observations but is not universally consistent.22 27 A previous study conducted among 729 patients with percutaneous coronary intervention in Australia (26.3% female) showed no significant association of sex with achieving healthy lifestyle overall.27 However, females had higher physical inactivity (OR 2.41, 95% CI 1.57 to 3.68), which is consistent with what we found in this study. This may reflect sex-specific reporting of self-reported physical activity, and sociocultural factors influencing exercise participation. Gender norms, safety concerns and psychosocial factors following ACS may further limit exercise participation. These influences may result in lower reported physical activity despite healthier BMI. The association between diabetes and better LDL-C control, but poorer BMI and physical activity outcomes, reflects the complex interplay between diabetes and cardiovascular risk factors.28 29 These findings highlight the need for tailored interventions to optimise cardiovascular health post-ACS, accounting for individual characteristics such as sex, age and comorbidities.222430

Further, previous studies have found that women are less likely to be prescribed guidelines recommended secondary prevention and have poor adherence to medications post ACS overall compared with their male counterparts.3135 A previous study of ACS patients recruited from 43 hospitals from 2009 to 2018 in Australia, with a total sample size of 9283 showed that women attend cardiac rehabilitation programmes less often and were less likely to have the indicated secondary prevention medications.31 A past study using Sax Institute’s 45 and Up Study found that use of preventive medications was 7% lower in women versus men at 3 months and 5% lower at 12 months after a cardiovascular event. Overall, women were associated with lower use of medication consistently over the follow-up.35 Bots et al (n=28 studies) further showed that sex differences in medication adherence were particularly a problem in lipid lowering drugs (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.92); however, such sex-specific differences were not evident for other classes of drugs such as antiplatelet medication (0.95, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.09), ACEIs/ARBs (0.95, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.17) or beta-blockers (0.97, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.11).32 Sex-specific differences in medication adherence could be attributed to both patient level and physician level factors, such as lack of awareness and perception that women are at risk of CHD, and lower prescription of medication for existing CHD than men. The lower medication adherence combined with a higher occurrence of comorbidities and lower access to and uptake of cardiac rehabilitation programme, increases women’s risk of experiencing a major adverse cardiac event postdischarge.31

Implications

The Lancet women and cardiovascular disease commission highlighted the need for awareness of sex-related and sex-related differences in cardiovascular health, including and not limited to health services access and treatment use.34 Understanding the factors that contribute to non-adherence to cardioprotective medications and barriers to follow-up after ACS presentations may further inform the development of policy and guidelines for improving patient outcomes. Also, it is imperative to reduce sex disparities in access to and use of medication for improving secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases, as recommended by guidelines.34 36 Future research should delve into sex-specific biology, clinical manifestations and an improved understanding of the environmental and social factors that impede medication adherence among women. Significant research gaps exist in coronary pathophysiology and the influence of race/ethnicity, SES and psychological factors. Addressing these requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to understanding women’s cardiovascular risk, including post-ACS medication adherence.

Limitations

Our study incorporated a robust sample size with minimal data loss (3.2%), enhancing the reliability of our findings. Medication adherence was assessed at both 6 and 12 months post-treatment, thereby improving the precision of outcome measurement. Various factors were assessed using validated surveys and objective biochemical tests during the TEXTMEDS trial, which was a nationwide, multicentre trial, which included patients from diverse urban and rural backgrounds in Australia. As a result, our findings are likely generalisable across different populations. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The primary measure of medication adherence relied on self-reports, which are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. Nonetheless, there was validation among those who consented to linked data for medications.12 Additionally, the study cohort was predominantly Caucasian, male and participants were required to have a functioning, text-capable mobile phone and be proficient in English, which may have limited generalisability of findings. Further, the study site and referral patterns may have influenced participation rates; however, these factors were not prespecified and therefore fall outside the scope of the present analysis. Finally, the nature of the trial, being a clinical intervention, may have inadvertently motivated participants to adhere more closely to their prescribed medication regimens.

Conclusions

This study revealed sex differences in risk factor control and medication adherence post-ACS. Women were less likely than men to meet LDL-C targets and engage in physical activity but more likely to maintain a healthy BMI. Although initially showing lower medication adherence, this gap narrowed after adjusting for socio-economic factors. These findings highlight the need for sex-responsive strategies to improve risk factor control and medication adherence in cardiovascular care.
