A novel six-biomarker panel identified from male breast cancer-associated fibroblasts demonstrates prognostic power for prostate tumors
Authors: Marianna Talia, Domenica Scordamaglia, Francesca Cirillo, Azzurra Zicarelli, Ernestina Marianna De Francesco, Roberta Malaguarnera, Eugenio Cesario, Anna Maria Miglietta, Marcello Maggiolini, Rosamaria Lappano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMale breast cancer (BC) is rare, accounting for only approximately 1% of all cases of BC, and poorly characterized. In contrast, prostate cancer is the most prevalent cancer in men and serves as a model for understanding male-specific tumor biology. The advent of high-throughput technologies has enabled the development of gene expression signatures for both breast and prostate tumors that could inform prognosis and guide treatment. In this respect, the role of the tumor microenvironment, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), remains largely underexplored. Here, we sought to identify a CAF-related gene signature in male patients with BC and prostate cancer to reveal specific protumorigenic mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets for both malignancies.MethodsRNA sequencing was performed to analyze and compare the transcriptomes of CAFs isolated from female and male BC patients. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between female and male breast CAFs were identified and subjected to enrichment analyses. Using a set of candidate upregulated genes in male breast CAFs, K-means clustering of prostate cancer patients was performed using multiple datasets to define a prognostic gene signature. Kaplan‒Meier curves and log-rank tests were conducted to assess differences in patient outcomes and other clinical variables between groups of patients with high or low prognostic gene expression. The clustering results were then validated using decision tree analysis, and boosted calculations were employed to increase the classifier performance.ResultsTranscriptomic profiling revealed 775 DEGs between female and male breast CAFs. Owing to the limited transcriptomic data from male BC patients, we leveraged large prostate cancer cohorts to investigate the relevance of the genes expressed by male breast CAFs. A six-gene signature (ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1) that could predict patient outcomes in multiple independent cohorts of prostate cancer patients was identified.ConclusionsWe identified a novel gene signature with strong prognostic value in prostate cancer and potential relevance to male BC. This gene signature represents a complementary tool to standard clinical parameters for improving patient stratification and management.

Background

Breast cancer (BC) is the most prevalent cancer type in females worldwide and poses a significant global health challenge [1, 2]. Considering that BC predominantly affects women, BC research has traditionally focused on female-specific biological and hormonal determinants [3]. Much less is known about male BC, a rare malignancy that accounts for approximately 1% of all BC cases [4–6]. There are several recognized risk factors for male BC, including older age, genetic factors, and certain clinical conditions, including obesity, Klinefelter syndrome or testicular disorders, and elevated levels of estrogens [3, 7]. However, owing to its rarity, male BC is markedly underrepresented in genomic, transcriptomic and proteomic studies; therefore, the therapeutic approaches for male BC are similar to treatments developed for female BC, including hormone therapy [5, 8]. Although prostate cancer is much more common and more intensively studied than male BC [9, 10], challenges related to its high heterogeneity remain. The presence of multiple distinct foci within a single patient, each potentially harboring different genetic alterations, contributes to the variation in invasiveness and therapeutic response [11, 12]. Therefore, the identification of specific genetic alterations and molecular biomarkers in prostate cancer holds promise for the development of precision-targeted therapies [13].

Despite originating in anatomically and functionally distinct tissues, breast and prostate cancers share key biological features, such as a reliance on steroid hormones, which may play a pivotal role in the development of both malignancies [9, 14]. In addition to hormonal dependence, breast and prostate malignancies have distinct but similarly complex tumor microenvironments (TMEs). The TME comprises an intricate and dynamic set of cellular and noncellular components, including stromal and immune cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) elements and soluble factors, which collectively orchestrate tumor progression and immune responses [15]. Specifically, TME components actively promote tumorigenesis and contribute to the emergence of more aggressive cancer phenotypes [9, 16, 17]. Among the stromal constituents, fibroblasts are the predominant cell type within the TME of breast and prostate carcinomas [18, 19]. In general, sustained paracrine signaling and direct interactions with neoplastic cells induce phenotypic and functional reprogramming of fibroblasts; these activated fibroblasts are defined as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) [20, 21]. Extensive literature supports the relevant contributions of CAFs to multiple events associated with tumor progression, including ECM remodeling and deposition, the promotion of angiogenesis, the enhancement of cancer cell invasiveness and the suppression of immune responses [22–24]. Notably, high-throughput technologies in combination with advanced bioinformatics tools have led to the identification of distinct CAF subpopulations with diverse and in some cases opposing phenotypes and functional properties [25–27].

Here, we used transcriptomic data and machine learning approaches to identify valuable prognostic biomarkers in CAFs obtained from male breast and prostate cancer patients. We identify a CAFs-based gene signature (patent n. 102024000009883), which may provide a better understanding of the molecular features of these tumors and holds potential for improving risk stratification and informing therapeutic strategies for prostate cancer patients.

Methods

Cell cultures

Female and male breast CAFs were isolated from 10 female invasive mammary ductal carcinomas and 3 male invasive mammary ductal carcinomas, cultured, characterized as previously described [28], and pooled by sex for subsequent studies. Briefly, the samples were cut into 1–2 mm diameter pieces, placed in a solution containing 400 IU collagenase, 100 IU hyaluronidase, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), antibiotics and antimycotics (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Monza, Italy) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. After centrifugation at 90 × g for 2 min, the supernatant containing the fibroblasts was centrifuged at 485 × g for 8 min, and the resulting pellet was suspended in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 µg/ml penicillin/streptomycin. CAFs were then expanded into 10-cm Petri dishes, and stored as cells passaged for three population doublings within a total of 7 to 10 days after tissue dissociation. We used CAFs passaged for up to 10 population doublings for the experiments to minimize clonal selection and culture stress due to extended tissue culture. CAFs were maintained in DMEM/F-12 with phenol red (supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 µg/ml penicillin/streptomycin) and grown in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2.

RNA-seq pipeline

Total RNA was extracted using a RNeasy Mini kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Bioset s.r.l., Catanzaro, Italy). RNA integrity for library preparation was determined by analyzing the extracted total RNA using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) with an RNA 6000 NanoChip. The RNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit RNA Assay Kit. Libraries were prepared from total RNA with an Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was evaluated by size analysis on a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Chip DNA HS), and concentrations were determined using a Qubit DNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Monza, Italy). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument in 150PE format.

Read preprocessing was performed by using fastp v0.20.0 [29] applying specific parameters to remove residual adapter sequences and to retain only high-quality data (qualified_quality_phred = 20, unqualified_percent_limit = 30, average_qual = 25, low_complexity_filter = True, complexity_threshold = 30). The percentage of uniquely mapped reads was high, with a mean value of 89% (mean value for the sample: unmapped reads 6%, quality bases >q30 90%). Then, passing filter reads were mapped to the reference genome (Homo sapiens, GRCh38) using STAR v2.7.0 [30] with standard parameters, except for sjdbOverhang, which was set to the read length. The genome and transcript annotations provided as inputs were downloaded from v105 of the Ensembl repository. The alignments were then elaborated by RSEM v1.3.3 [31] to estimate the transcript and gene abundances. Sample-specific gene abundances were subsequently merged into a single raw expression matrix by applying a dedicated RSEM command (rsem-generate-data-matrix). Genes with at least 10 counts in N samples, where N corresponds to the sample number in the smallest experimental group, were then selected. Differential expression was evaluated by edgeR [32] from raw counts for each comparison. Multiple testing control procedures were applied, and genes with an FDR ≤ 0.1 and logFC >|0.5| were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Annotation of the DEGs was performed using the bioMart package [33] in R 4.3, querying available Ensembl Gene IDs and retrieving Gene Names and Entrez gene IDs.

Prostate cancer patient cohorts

In silico studies on prostate cancer patients were performed using the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Prostate Adenocarcinoma Cohort [34]. The samples (n = 550) were filtered by “sample type” to separate tumor tissues (n = 498) and adjacent normal tissues (n = 52). For the validation cohorts, we used the DKFZ (n. 94), GSE54460 (n. 106), and MSK (n. 150) datasets [35–37]. The samples in the MSK dataset were filtered to exclude metastatic tissues, resulting in a final dataset of 131 patients. All datasets were filtered for missing values as required.

Enrichment analyses

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed employing the enrichPathway() function of the ReactomePA package [38]. The following parameters were used: organism = “human”, p-value cut-off = 0.05, pAdjustMethod = “BH”. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the gseGO() function of the clusterProfiler package [39]. The following parameters were used: organism = “human”, p-value cut-off = 0.05, pAdjustMethod = “BH”.

Clustering analysis

Clustering was performed to divide patients into distinct groups on the basis of the similarity of their gene expression profiles to identify clusters that are as homogenous as possible by minimizing intracluster distances and maximizing intercluster distances. Clustering analyses were performed using the kmeans() function of the stats package in R, which identifies centroid-based structures by minimizing the sum of (squared) distances between data points and their corresponding cluster centroid. To account for variability in gene expression ranges across patient samples and datasets, min–max normalization was applied to rescale gene expression values to an interval of [0, 1]. Then, the optimal number of clusters (K) was determined through a parameter-sweeping methodology, selecting the value that maximized the silhouette coefficient. The elbow method was used to assess the inflection point in the sum of squared errors (SSE) curve. The optimal clustering quality was achieved with K = 2 in all cases. The resulting datasets comprised patients labeled according to their assigned cluster (clusters 1 or 2).

Classification analysis

Following K-means clustering, classification of the TCGA dataset was performed to derive a knowledge model from the labeled data by using the C50 package in R. The aim was to generate both a descriptive model — identifying genes and expression ranges associated with specific clusters— and a predictive model for classifying unseen patients. Due to their interpretability and widespread adoption in machine learning, decision trees were developed by recursively partitioning data on the basis of gene-maximizing information gain (i.e., entropy reduction) at each node. The resulting models were interpretable as sets of decision rules. The dataset was randomly split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. The models were trained on the training set and evaluated on the test set, and 10-fold cross-validation was performed to ensure a robust estimation of model performance. Accuracy, precision, and recall measures were calculated.

Survival analyses

Survival analysis of prostate cancer patients was performed using the survival and survminer R packages. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves were generated, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

CAFs were grown on a coverslip, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, washed 3 times with PBS and incubated at 4 °C overnight with a primary antibody against α smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (AB7817, Abcam, DBA, Milan, Italy; 1:250). After incubation, the slides were extensively washed with PBS and probed with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Monza, Italy, 1:500) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) (Merck, Milan, Italy, 1:1000). Images were obtained using the EVOS™ M7000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Monza, Italy).

Statistical analyses

Boxplots and stacked bar charts were generated, and statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio. Statistical comparisons of data in boxplots were performed using either t tests or Wilcoxon tests depending on the nature and distribution of the data. Categorical distributions displayed in stacked bar charts were compared via either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, on the basis of sample size and expected frequencies. Heatmaps were drawn with the pheatmap package in R Studio. The decision tree was drawn with the rpart.plot package in R Studio.

Data availability

Data from the prostate adenocarcinoma cohort of the TCGA project were retrieved from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/). Data on the stromal scores of the TCGA prostate samples, which were calculated by the ESTIMATE algorithm, were downloaded from https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/estimate/. Data from the GSE54460 dataset were retrieved from NCBI GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). The DKFZ and MSK datasets were downloaded from cBioportal (https://www.cbioportal.org/).

Code availability

The code developed for this study is not publicly available but may be made available by the corresponding author to qualified researchers upon reasonable request.

Results

Characterization of the transcriptomic profiles of male and female breast CAFs

The intratumor heterogeneity of neoplastic tissues poses a major challenge in cancer biology [40]. While CAFs are recognized as key modulators of female BC progression [41], their role in male BC remains largely unexplored. Here, RNA-seq was employed to identify transcripts with differential expression between CAFs isolated from surgically resected female and male breast carcinomas (Fig. 1A–B). Transcriptomic analyses of CAFs revealed 282 upregulated genes and 493 downregulated genes in male vs. female breast CAFs (log2FC ≥ |2|, p ≤ 0.01) (Fig. 1C–E).

To gain insight into the biological significance of the identified DEGs, we performed Reactome pathway and GO enrichment analyses. Notably, female breast CAFs presented a distinct expression profile comprising genes associated mostly with cell cycle processes (Fig. 2A–D), whereas the genes upregulated in male breast CAFs were associated with increased ECM-dependent motility dynamics (Fig. 3A–D). These results suggest that female- and male-derived breast CAFs display distinct biological activities, resulting in differences in BC cell behavior through different mechanisms.

Clustering and classification techniques establish a CAF gene expression signature with prognostic relevance for prostate cancer patients included in TCGA dataset

Given the rarity of male BC and the consequently limited literature on male breast CAFs, we focused our next analyses on the genes expressed in male breast CAFs and included in enrichment analyses. We identified 12 key genes, namely, ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL4A5, COL5A3, COMP, EFEMP1, EMILIN2, FMOD, FN1, LAMA1 and VTN, that were enriched in diverse overlapping categories (Fig. 4A–B) and were markedly upregulated in male compared with female breast CAFs (Fig. 4C–D).

Given the unavailability of large-scale transcriptomic and clinical datasets for male BC cohorts, we evaluated the prognostic relevance of the identified genes using data from patients with prostate cancer, which is a male-specific malignancy that shares certain biological and hormonal features with BC [9]. To this end, we first performed unsupervised K-means clustering and subsequently applied a decision tree for classification. On the basis of the transcript levels of the 12 aforementioned genes, prostate cancer patients from the TCGA dataset were divided into 2 different groups, cluster 1 (n = 237) and cluster 2 (n = 271). The optimal cluster number was determined using the within-cluster sums of squares and average silhouette methods (Fig. 5A‒C). Patients in cluster 2 expressed higher levels of 9 out of the 12 genes (ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL4A5, COL5A3, COMP, EFEMP1, FN1 and VTN) than patients in cluster 1 (Fig. 5D), whereas EMILIN2, FMOD and LAMA1 showed no difference in expression between the 2 clusters and were therefore excluded from further analysis.

Survival analysis revealed that patients in cluster 2 exhibited shorter disease-free intervals than those in cluster 1 (Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that the nine identified genes may comprise a predictive gene signature for prostate cancer patients. Further corroborating these results, we found that patients in cluster 2 tended to have more advanced disease and higher Gleason scores than patients in cluster 1 (Fig. 6A, C). In line with these results, single-gene-level analysis showed that the expression levels of ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1, though not COL4A5, EFEMP1 and VTN, were significantly higher in patients with advanced stage and higher Gleason scores (Fig. 6B, D). Similarly, patients in cluster 2 had a greater frequency of residual tumors (Fig. 7A) and new tumor events after treatment (Fig. 7C) than the patients in cluster 1. Accordingly, we observed that ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1 gene expression levels were higher in prostate cancer patients with residual tumors and new tumor events after treatment than in patients without these clinical features (Fig. 7B, D). Considering that we developed our gene signature using CAFs, we also assessed whether the stromal content of the tumors (calculated using the ESTIMATE algorithm, as provided in the TCGA dataset [42]) differed between clusters. Interestingly, patients in cluster 2 exhibited a much higher percentage of tumors with elevated stromal content than patients in cluster 1 (Fig. 7E). In accordance with these findings, prostate tumors with higher stromal scores presented higher expression of the aforementioned 9 genes than tumors with low stromal abundance (Fig. 7F). Collectively, these results indicate that elevated expression of the genes included in the identified signature is associated with unfavorable survival outcomes in prostate cancer patients, underscoring the potential prognostic relevance of this signature.

To increase the robustness of the clustering outcomes, we trained a decision tree classifier using these 9 identified genes as explanatory variables and the cluster assignments as target classes. We partitioned the TCGA prostate cancer dataset into training and test cohorts at a 70:30 ratio and evaluated model performance using a 10-fold cross-validation approach. A representative decision tree is presented in Fig. 8A, while the confusion matrix obtained from the test set is shown in Fig. 8B. Notably, the CAF-related gene signature yielded 0.936 accuracy, 0.986 precision, and 0.89 recall. Analysis of feature importance, as estimated via the voting mechanism of a boosted decision tree algorithm applied to the training set, identified ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1 as the most relevant genes for dividing patients into cluster 1 and cluster 2 (Fig. 8C).

A novel 6-gene panel predicts poor clinical outcome in prostate cancer validation cohorts

To further validate the diagnostic performance of our 6-gene panel, we clustered patients from additional prostate cancer datasets (DFKZ, GSE54460 and MSK) according to the expression levels of the 6 most informative genes (ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1) identified by the classification model. First, patients from the DFKZ dataset were assigned to cluster 1 (73 patients) or cluster 2 (21 patients) in accordance with the optimal number of clusters calculated by the within-cluster sums of squares and average silhouette methods (Fig. 9A–C). Analyses of gene expression data revealed that ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1 were similarly upregulated in patients in cluster 2 compared with patients in cluster 1 (Fig. 9D). Notably, subsequent clinical feature analyses revealed that patients in cluster 2 exhibited shorter biochemical recurrence-free survival (Fig. 9E), along with more advanced tumor stages and higher Gleason scores (Fig. 9F–G). By applying the same workflow to the GSE54460 prostate cancer dataset, we assigned 72 and 34 prostate cancer patients to cluster 1 and cluster 2, respectively (Fig. 10A–C). The expression of the 6 genes was significantly greater in patients in cluster 2 than in patients in cluster 1 (Fig. 10D), with shorter biochemical recurrence-free survival among patients in cluster 2 (Fig. 10E). Finally, K-means clustering of the MSK dataset of prostate cancer patients was performed, resulting in 87 patients in cluster 1 and 44 patients in cluster 2 (Fig. 11A–C). Patients in cluster 2 (n. 44) presented elevated expression of all of the signature genes except COL5A3, which presented an inverse expression pattern (Fig. 11D). Nevertheless, patients in cluster 2 were characterized by shorter disease-free survival than patients in cluster 1 (Fig. 11E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the expression of genes identified in CAFs is significantly correlated with adverse clinical outcomes in prostate cancer patients. Therefore, these genes may be considered potential drivers of tumor aggressiveness and candidate biomarkers for risk stratification and prognostic prediction.

Discussion

Tumors are complex ecosystems that include not only the malignant cells themselves but also the surrounding diverse TME components, ECM and abnormal vasculature [43]. In contrast to earlier views that focused primarily on the intrinsic evolution of tumor cells, it is now well established that the TME plays fundamental roles in modulating tumor progression [44]. The role of the TME is determined to a great extent by its specific cellular composition; the TME mainly includes immune cells (i.e., macrophages, lymphocytes B and T, and natural killers), endothelial cells and fibroblasts [44]. Depending on the abundance, activation state and spatial organization of these cell types, they can support tumor growth, invasiveness and immune evasion or, conversely, impose constraints on malignant progression [43]. Therefore, it could be argued that the response to therapy and ultimately patient outcomes are influenced not only by the intrinsic properties of cancer cells but also by the complex and dynamic interactions among the diverse stromal, immune and vascular components of the TME [43]. Fibroblasts are highly plastic stromal cells involved in the maintenance of connective tissue architecture and play essential roles in wound healing, tissue inflammation and organ fibrosis [45]. Within the TME, fibroblasts are exposed to a wide array of stimuli that trigger their activation and phenotypic transition from quiescent fibroblasts to CAFs [45]. CAFs contribute to a conducive TME that supports tumor initiation and progression, favoring processes such as angiogenesis, dissemination and metastasis through the release of diverse soluble factors [46–48]. Several findings have highlighted the role of CAFs in promoting protumorigenic features in either female breast or prostate tumors; thus, diverse clinical trials have been performed to evaluate combinatorial strategies targeting both tumor cells and CAFs [10, 18, 41, 49–51]. Less is known about the role of CAFs in male BC, with recent single-cell RNA-seq studies reporting stromal abundance within male breast tumors but not providing detailed information about the molecular profile and biological role of CAFs [52–54].

In the present study, interestingly, RNA-seq studies of CAFs isolated from both female and male BC patients revealed distinct gene expression profiles. Specifically, CAFs derived from female breast tumors presented elevated expression of genes associated with cell cycle-related activities, whereas CAFs isolated from male breast tumors presented pronounced upregulation of genes involved in invasive and migratory processes. To identify key drivers of this phenotype, we prioritized genes that were recurrently represented across multiple categories, such as ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL4A5, COL5A3, COMP, EFEMP1, EMILIN2, FMOD, FN1, LAMA1 and VTN, considering that their consistent representation suggests central roles in defining the functional divergence between female and male breast CAFs. Given the paucity of integrated clinical and transcriptomic data from large cohorts of male BC patients, we next investigated the potential clinical relevance of these genes in prostate cancer as a related male-specific malignancy. Using K-means clustering, we identified a 9-gene signature, comprising ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL4A5, COL5A3, COMP, EFEMP1, FN1, and VTN, from our original 12-gene panel. Remarkably, this signature was strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes across multiple independent prostate cancer cohorts. The aforementioned signature was used to stratify patients from the TCGA dataset into clusters representing two distinct molecular subtypes (characterized by either high or low expression levels of these signature genes). Notably, patients in the high-expression cluster exhibited significantly poorer survival and more aggressive clinicopathological features, including elevated Gleason scores and more advanced pathological stages, consistent with adverse disease outcomes. Moreover, tumors in the high-expression cluster presented a marked increase in stromal content, further supporting the stromal origin of these markers and suggesting that a reactive stroma contributes to aggressive tumor features [55].

To determine the relative contribution of each gene to this phenotype, we employed a decision tree learning algorithm. The evaluation of attribute usage percentages, which reflect the extent to which each gene contributes to the classification of patients, highlighted six out of nine genes (ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1) as key determinants of prostate cancer prognosis. By contrast, EFEMP1, VTN and COL4A5 showed lower attribute usage percentages. These findings are consistent with the lack of differential expression of EFEMP1, VTN and COL4A5 with respect to pathological stage, Gleason score, residual tumor and tumor recurrence after treatment.

ASPN, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, COMP and FN1 are all functionally linked to ECM remodeling, a process that has been extensively implicated in the progression of various malignancies, including prostate cancer [56–62]. The disruption of ECM organization is a hallmark of solid tumors, often serving as a driver as well as a consequence of tumor progression, and shaping malignant and stromal cell populations [63–66]. Collagens are the major stromal component of the TME and play multiple roles in tumor initiation, progression and therapeutic response, including in prostate cancer [56, 57, 64, 67–70]. Consistent with our identification of COL4A1, COL4A2, and COL5A3 as prognostic markers, collagens have been linked to adverse clinical outcomes in prostate cancer patients [56, 57, 68]. In particular, collagens contribute to ECM remodeling and increased stiffness, providing a structural scaffold for the regulation of cell behavior through various signaling pathways [71]. Increasing evidence suggests that ECM represents much more than a physical support or a passive barrier for tumor cells. Indeed, variations in ECM composition and stiffness may influence oxygen and nutrient entry, invasive properties of tumor cells as well as drug bioavailability [72, 73]. Interestingly, recent findings revealed that mechanical forces exerted by specific collagen matrix architectures may cause metabolic shifts in cancer cells, thus potentially contributing to increase their invasiveness [74–76]. Moreover, several investigations have shed new lights on the role of collagen in modulating anti-cancer immune responses, thus suggesting that the modulation of ECM stiffness can be considered an optimized strategy to potentiate cancer immunotherapy [72, 77]. In this context, COL4A1 has emerged as a key-factor for the creation of an immunosuppressive and multidrug-resistant TME in several cancers [78, 79]. Similarly, the levels of asporin, encoded by the ASPN gene, were found positively associated with the expression of immunosuppressive genes [80]. Moreover, previous studies have revealed that asporin is primarily localized in the tumor stroma and serves as a predictor of metastatic dissemination and poor prognosis in prostate tumors [58, 59, 81]. In accordance with this evidence, asporin regulates diverse signaling pathways, including TGFβ, Wnt/β-catenin, notch, hedgehog, EGFR, HER2 and Rac1, which have been widely recognized to promote cancer cell proliferation and motility [82]. Notably, ASPN has also been identified as a potential blood-based biomarker for men with advanced prostate adenocarcinoma, alongside cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) [83], which has been suggested as both a diagnostic marker and a promoter of prostate tumor progression [84–86]. Interestingly, novel evidence collected in colorectal cancer patients has shown that COMP levels are positively associated with a collagenous stiff matrix, which in turn hampers immune infiltration within the tumor [87]. Additionally, fibronectin1, encoded by the FN1 gene, is a key protein involved in cell adhesion and migration and has been implicated in various malignancies, including prostate cancer [60, 61, 88–90], further emphasizing the importance of ECM components in tumor development and metastasis. Like collagens, FN1 has emerged as an activator of a metabolism route triggering the proliferative responses observed in certain tumors [91], further suggesting that ECM components act synergistically in cancer progression.

In addition, our findings demonstrate how the evaluation of the stromal abundance and expression of markers arising from the tumor stroma could be useful in precision medicine for the stratification of prostate cancer patients. Histopathological findings and the Gleason score are the most widely used prognostic indicators for prostate cancer [92]. However, these metrics are inherently limited by interobserver variability, which may result in misclassification of tumor aggressiveness and consequently may lead to either over- or undertreatment [93]. In contrast, prognostic assays based on gene expression profiles from tumor biopsy samples may offer greater objectivity independent of personal histopathological interpretation [93]. Diverse molecular prognostic tools for prostate cancer have been developed, including the Oncotype DX Genomic Prostate Score (GPS), the Decipher Genomic Classifier, and the Prolaris Cell Cycle Progression (CCP) test [94]. All these assays focus on the epithelial tumor component, whereas none leverage transcriptomic signals derived from the tumor stroma, an element increasingly recognized as a critical modulator of prostate cancer progression [55]. To date, no validated prognostic signatures have specifically been derived from the tumor stroma, highlighting the unique advantage of our gene panel to be combined with existing tools that interrogate tumor cells. The decision tree model employed to derive our CAFs-based signature has demonstrated robust performance, with validation across multiple independent cohorts. However, practical considerations before clinical implementation should be considered, including assay standardization across different laboratories, cost-effectiveness of implementing a multi-gene test in routine practice as well as tissue quality and quantity requirements. Remarkably, respect to the aforementioned signatures, our gene panel comprises a low number of genes, reducing complexity and facilitating integration into clinical workflow, in contrast to commercially available assays evaluating numerous genes [94].

Consistent with evidence underlining the ECM as a potential therapeutic target in solid tumors [95], the identified genes may also hold translational applicability as either prognostic markers or druggable targets. Accordingly, matrix-directed strategies, including small molecules, conjugated nanoparticles and antibodies, are currently evaluated in preclinical and clinical settings across several cancer types [63, 95, 96].

Anyway, an important constraint of our investigation relies on the validation in prostate cancer datasets of the gene signature assessed in breast male CAFs due to the limited availability of transcriptomic data from male BC cohorts. In this regard, we have outlined future directions aimed at extending validation efforts to male BC, including prospective studies and immunohistochemical analyses in tissue specimens, in order to confirm the biological and clinical relevance of the gene signature in the male BC setting.

Conclusions

We have developed a novel gene signature that demonstrates strong prognostic value for prostate cancer and potential applicability in male BC patients. The expression of these genes may serve as an additional prognostic indicator along with clinical parameters for these cancers, thus potentially enhancing patient stratification and management.
