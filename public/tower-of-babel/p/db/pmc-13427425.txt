Magnetic resonance imaging–based quantitative definition of pathologic long head of the biceps tendon sheath effusion and its association with intra-articular shoulder pathology
Authors: Daisuke Yamashita, Takayuki Oishi, Taiki Nozaki, Atsushi Tasaki, Nobuto Kitamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is no consensus regarding the pathologic significance of effusion within the long head of the biceps tendon sheath (LHB effusion), and its association with other shoulder joint pathologies remains unclear. We hypothesized that establishing a quantitative evaluation method for LHB effusion and defining pathologic effusion on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) would help clarify the relationship between LHB effusion and other pathologic shoulder conditions. The purpose of this study was to establish a quantitative MRI-based threshold for pathologic LHB effusion and to investigate its associations with clinically relevant shoulder pathologies.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 261 shoulder MRI examinations acquired between July 2019 and December 2020. The LHB effusion area was evaluated on axial T2∗-weighted MRI images. A shoulder surgeon and a musculoskeletal radiologist also independently judged whether LHB effusion was physiological or pathologic. A receiver operating characteristic curve was generated based on their judgments and the measured effusion areas, and the threshold value for pathologic LHB effusion area was determined. Other shoulder joint pathologies, including fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles, supraspinatus tendon tear, subscapularis tendon tear, frozen shoulder, and LHB lesion, were assessed using MRI, and their associations with pathologic LHB effusion were analyzed using univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsThe threshold values for pathologic LHB effusion area, determined based on the expert visual assessment of the shoulder surgeon and the musculoskeletal radiologist, were 18.13 and 19.29 mm2, respectively. When an LHB effusion area of ≥19 mm2 was defined as pathologic, univariate analysis showed that pathologic LHB effusion was associated with LHB lesion, frozen shoulder, subscapularis tendon tear, and older age. Multivariate analysis showed that pathologic LHB effusion was associated with LHB lesion (odds ratio [OR], 4.370; P < .01), frozen shoulder (OR, 3.16; P < .01), and older age (OR, 1.03; P = .01).DiscussionQuantitative MRI assessment of LHB sheath effusion demonstrated high measurement reproducibility, and an expert-derived threshold of approximately 19 mm2 was associated with older age, LHB lesions, and frozen shoulder. Because the threshold was derived from expert visual assessment rather than an independent pathologic gold standard, these findings should be considered preliminary and require external validation before use as a diagnostic criterion.

Effusion within the long head of the biceps tendon sheath (LHB effusion) is frequently observed in imaging studies of the shoulder joint (Fig. 1). Therefore, many researchers have investigated LHB effusion to date, but its clinical significance remains controversial, and its association with other shoulder joint pathologies has not yet been established.4,8,13, 14, 15 Middleton et al13 reported that, among 20 shoulders with LHB effusion detected on ultrasonography (without a clearly defined diagnostic criterion), 18 shoulders (90%) exhibited pathologies such as rotator cuff tears, impingement syndrome, glenoid fractures, and free bodies in the shoulder joint. Chang et al evaluated LHB effusion using ultrasonography and classified its severity into the following 3 categories: mild, moderate, and severe. Frozen shoulder and age ≥60 years were associated with mild to moderate effusion. LHB tendinitis, subdeltoid bursitis, and full-thickness supraspinatus tears were significantly associated with effusion of all severities. Subscapularis tears were significantly associated with moderate and severe effusion. Severe effusion was significantly associated with articular-sided partial-thickness supraspinatus tears, posterior recess effusion, and medial dislocation of the LHB tendon.4 Park et al14 defined the presence of effusion on ultrasonography as a maximum distance of ≥0.9 mm from LHB to the sheath, and reported effusion in 69% of cases with frozen shoulder, 57% with rotator cuff tears, 41% with calcific tendinitis, and 33% with biceps tendinitis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has recently been widely used to evaluate shoulder joint pathologies.1,11,19 Although MRI can evaluate LHB effusion, it has not been sufficiently studied in the MRI setting to date. We hypothesized that establishing a quantitative evaluation method for LHB effusion and defining pathologic effusion on MRI would help clarify the relationship between LHB effusion and other pathologic conditions of the shoulder joint. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to define pathologic LHB effusion based on quantitative measurements using MRI and to investigate the relationships between pathologic LHB effusion and other shoulder joint pathologies.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board at our institution. Informed consent was obtained from all patients and relevant individuals before study initiation.

Patients who underwent MRI for shoulder pain at our institution between April 2019 and April 2020 were consecutively included if they met the eligibility criteria. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ipsilateral shoulder surgery, (2) shoulder pain due to trauma, and (3) insufficient data. We retrospectively reviewed the demographic characteristics of the included patients (age, sex, and side) and MRI findings of the shoulder joint.

MRI examinations were performed using MAGNETON Skyra 3T (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) or Discovery MR750 3.0 T (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a surface coil for the shoulder joint. The MRI parameters were as follows: repetition time, 900 ms; echo time, 13 ms; flip angle, 30°; field of view, 180 mm; matrix size, 320 × 224; and slice thickness, 3 mm.

Measurement of the area of long head of the biceps tendon effusion

The area of LHB effusion was measured on a PACS workstation by freehand tracing the boundaries of the LHB tendon and surrounding sheath effusion using a freehand region-of-interest tool by subtracting the area of the LHB from the combined area of the LHB and effusion on all axial T2∗-weighted MRI images in which the glenoid was visible (Fig. 2). Expert visual assessment of pathologic long head of the biceps tendon effusion

To determine the threshold value for pathologic LHB effusion area, 2 experts evaluated the MRI image showing the largest effusion area from each shoulder included in this study. Examiner 1 (a shoulder surgeon with >20 years of experience) and examiner 2 (a musculoskeletal radiologist with >20 years of experience), who were blinded to patient background, other MRI images, and the results of LHB effusion area measurements, independently assessed these images and determined whether the effusion was physiological or pathologic based on expert visual assessment. Subsequently, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated separately for each examiner based on their expert visual assessment and the measured LHB effusion areas, and the threshold value for pathologic LHB effusion area was calculated. Interobserver agreement between the 2 experts' judgments was assessed using Cohen kappa coefficient.

Evaluation of shoulder joint pathologies on magnetic resonance imaging

Shoulder joint pathologies were evaluated on MRI to clarify their associations with pathologic LHB effusion. We assessed rotator cuff tears (supraspinatus and subscapularis), fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles, frozen shoulder, and LHB lesions. Fatty infiltration was defined as positive when the Goutallier classification was ≥2.6 Frozen shoulder was defined as present when the signal intensity of the inferior glenohumeral ligament was higher than that of the LHB on proton density–weighted oblique coronal images.5,17 LHB lesions were defined as the presence of high-intensity areas within the LHB, particularly on fluid-sensitive sequences, and discontinuity of the LHB was defined as rupture.11

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and frequencies, respectively. An independent t test was used for continuous variables, and the chi-square test and Fisher exact test were used for categorical variables. Separately from the primary LHB effusion measurements, to evaluate measurement reproducibility, 2 examiners (an orthopedic resident and a shoulder surgeon) independently measured the LHB effusion area on 100 images. The orthopedic resident repeated the measurements on 20 images with an interval of at least 3 weeks. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess interobserver and intraobserver reproducibility. The entire cohort was divided into 2 groups (physiological and pathologic effusion) according to the threshold value for pathologic LHB effusion defined by the expert visual assessment of the shoulder surgeon and the musculoskeletal radiologist. Univariate analyses were performed to evaluate associations between each shoulder pathology and pathologic LHB effusion. A logistic regression model was then constructed; shoulder pathologies that showed a significant association in univariate analyses were used as independent variables, and pathologic LHB effusion was used as the dependent variable. This model was used to assess the contribution of each shoulder pathology to the probability of pathologic LHB effusion while controlling for other variables.

All statistical analyses were performed with EZR (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). More precisely, it is a modified version of R commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics.9 Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Discussion

In this study, we established a quantitative MRI-based threshold for pathologic effusion within the long head of the biceps tendon sheath. Quantitative MRI assessment of LHB sheath effusion demonstrated high measurement reproducibility, and an expert-derived threshold of approximately 19 mm2 was associated with older age, LHB lesions, and frozen shoulder.

LHB effusion is frequently detected in imaging studies of the shoulder joint, regardless of whether the condition of the shoulder is physiological or pathologic.13,16 However, the etiology of LHB effusion remains unclear. Zubler et al20 conducted a comparative analysis of ultrasound findings in 30 patients who received a contrast medium injection into the glenohumeral joint. Although LHB effusion was detected in 4 patients before injection, the number increased to 29 after injection. These findings suggest that increased glenohumeral intra-articular effusion may flow into the intertubercular sulcus and contribute to LHB effusion. Several studies have reported associations between LHB effusion and glenohumeral joint pathologies, including intra-articular free body, rheumatoid arthritis, and glenoid fracture.2,13 Inflammation of the LHB and its surrounding tissue has also been proposed to contribute to the occurrence of LHB effusion.4,8,13,15 Moreover, a wide range of pathologies have been reported to induce effusion, including rotator cuff tears, frozen shoulder, and impingement syndrome.4,8,13 The lack of consensus may be attributable to the absence of a definition of pathologic LHB effusion and variability in the modalities used to evaluate effusion. Therefore, in this study, we assessed LHB effusion using MRI, a widely used modality in shoulder imaging that allows simultaneous evaluation of various shoulder pathologies and LHB effusion,15,19 and defined a threshold value for pathologic LHB effusion area.

Distinguishing between physiological and pathologic effusion is challenging. Kaplan et al performed cadaveric dissection and found that a circular, localized, high-intensity area located lateral to the intertubercular sulcus on T2-weighted MRI represents the anterolateral branch of the circumflex artery. They advocated that only effusion surrounding the entire circumference of the LHB should be considered pathologic,10 and this constitutes the only report proposing criteria for pathologic LHB effusion. In the present study, LHB effusion was quantitatively evaluated using MRI, and ROC curves were generated based on the expert visual assessment of 2 experienced physicians, which allowed us to determine the threshold for pathologic effusion. The definition of pathologic LHB effusion in this study was based on expert subjective judgment, which may introduce observer bias. However, in the absence of established quantitative criteria or a gold standard for LHB effusion, expert clinical judgment was used as the reference standard, and we attempted to quantify it. We believe this approach is both practical and clinically applicable.

In this study, pathologic LHB effusion was associated with older age, LHB lesion, and frozen shoulder. Chang et al performed ultrasonographic evaluation of LHB effusion and other shoulder pathologies and found that older age was an independent risk factor for mild and moderate LHB effusion, consistent with our findings. They proposed that overuse of the biceps tendon as a humeral head depressor contributes to the development of LHB effusion.4

Several ultrasonographic studies have reported an association between LHB effusion and frozen shoulder.4,8,13,15 An MRI study reported synovitis-like abnormalities around the LHB in frozen shoulder,19 which may contribute to LHB effusion. LHB effusion may also result from synovial fluid leakage from a contracted joint cavity with decreased volume.

Unlike previous reports, this study found no association between pathologic LHB effusion and rotator cuff tear.3,4,7,8,13, 14, 15 This discrepancy may reflect differences in imaging modality and the quantitative approach used in the present study. Although SSC tear can cause LHB instability,12 potentially leading to synovitis around the LHB and subsequent effusion, SSC tear was associated with pathologic LHB effusion in univariate analysis but not in multivariate analysis. This finding may be related to the relatively limited diagnostic performance of MRI for SSC tear. Adams et al1 reported a sensitivity of 36%, specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100%, negative predictive value of 62%, and accuracy of 69% when using arthroscopic findings as the gold standard.

This study had some limitations. First, we evaluated only a limited range of patient demographic variables and MRI findings, and pain was not assessed. Moreover, potential confounding factors, including physical examination findings, duration of symptoms, systemic inflammatory conditions, and patient activity levels, were not accounted for. These limitations are inherent to the retrospective design of this study. Second, other intra-articular shoulder pathologies, such as glenohumeral osteoarthritis and labral lesions, were not evaluated because plain radiographs were not consistently available for the assessment of osteoarthritis, and noncontrast MRI has limited diagnostic accuracy for labral pathology.18 Furthermore, other potentially relevant conditions, including inflammatory arthropathy, joint effusion, subacromial bursitis, and biceps instability/subluxation, were not systematically evaluated because of the inherent limitations of the retrospective study design. These conditions may also influence the presence of LHB effusion, and further investigation is warranted. Third, as this was a retrospective, cross-sectional study, causality between LHB effusion and associated shoulder pathologies cannot be established. It remains unclear whether LHB effusion represents a primary pathologic process or a secondary manifestation of intra-articular inflammation. Fourth, the amount of LHB effusion was evaluated by area. Although this two-dimensional measurement may not fully reflect the three-dimensional volume of effusion, it demonstrated high interobserver and intraobserver reliability. Moreover, this method is simple and practical, making it readily applicable in routine clinical settings. Fifth, this study did not include a priori sample size calculation or power analysis, which represents a limitation. However, a relatively large cohort of consecutive patients was included, which may support the robustness of the findings. Sixth, although standardized imaging protocols with identical sequence parameters were used, MRI examinations were performed using 2 different scanners, and the potential influence of scanner-related differences on measurements was not evaluated. Furthermore, partial-volume effects were not specifically corrected for and may have influenced the accuracy of the LHB effusion measurements.

Conclusion

Quantitative MRI assessment of LHB sheath effusion demonstrated high measurement reproducibility, and an expert-derived threshold of approximately 19 mm2 was associated with older age, LHB lesions, and frozen shoulder. Because the threshold was derived from expert visual assessment rather than an independent pathologic gold standard, these findings should be considered preliminary and require external validation before use as a diagnostic criterion.
