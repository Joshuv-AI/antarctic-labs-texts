Patient-Centered Care in Multimorbid TAVI Patients and the Significance of High-Quality TVT Registry Data
Authors: Mario Gössl, Steven M Bradley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: KCCQ, multimorbid, quality of life, transcatheter aortic valve intervention

Transcatheter aortic valve intervention (TAVI) has transformed the landscape of treatment for symptomatic aortic stenosis, particularly among older adults who were once deemed inoperable. The current study by Wessler et al1 published in JACC: Advances offers a comprehensive and insightful analysis into the burden of chronic conditions in TAVI recipients and the association of these multimorbidities with patient-centered outcomes. By leveraging the extensive Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology transcatheter valve therapy registry linked with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services claims, the authors illuminate the real-world experience of nearly 190,000 TAVI patients between 2013 and 2020, presenting a cohort that far more accurately mirrors clinical reality than previous trial populations.

Stratifying patients by their comorbidity burden using the well-established Charlson-Deyo score they were grouped as low, intermediate, or high comorbidity, and the analysis considered a range of clinical covariates, employing robust methods to account for missing data, especially in patient-reported quality of life (QoL) outcomes.

The findings are both sobering and nuanced. The prevalence of chronic conditions among TAVI recipients is exceedingly high, with a striking 25% of patients presenting with more than 7 chronic conditions, underscoring the complexity of the typical TAVI candidate. Importantly, the study demonstrates a strong and independent association between higher comorbidity burden and increased 1-year mortality: patients with high multimorbidity experienced a 1-year mortality rate of 22.0%, compared to 5.6% among those with low comorbidity, translating to an adjusted HR of 2.33. This observation highlights the stark reality facing many older adults undergoing TAVI—the intersection of advanced structural heart disease with the cumulative weight of chronic illness fundamentally shapes survival prospects.

However, the study also reveals that QoL improvements following TAVI are significant and, perhaps surprisingly, largely independent of comorbidity burden. Using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), the authors found that patients with higher multimorbidity had poorer baseline disease-specific patient-reported health status, but experienced similar absolute gains in KCCQ score after TAVI as their less-burdened counterparts. This challenges a purely risk-centric approach to patient selection and affirms the ability of TAVI to offer meaningful improvements in health status, even for those with substantial chronic disease. This finding is profoundly important for older adults, many of whom may value symptom relief and improvements in health-related quality of life as much as, or even more than, the possibility of extended survival.

Despite these positive findings, the analysis also draws attention to areas of concern and ongoing challenge. Palliative care utilization remains low, with only 4.7% of patients overall receiving such care, even among those with the highest comorbidity burden. This rate varied widely across centers. The low reported use of palliative care may reflect limitations in the registry, which captures only claims-based encounters and may miss informal or primary palliative interventions delivered by cardiologists or heart teams. It also raises the critical issue of whether enough attention is being paid to end-of-life planning and supportive care integration, especially for those with advanced multimorbidity, high procedural risk, or limited life expectancy.

The scale and representativeness of the Wessler et al study1 stand out as one of its primary strengths. By linking the transcatheter valve therapy registry with Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services claims, the authors provide an unparalleled, nationwide, real-world sample, allowing for robust comparisons and strong generalizability to the U.S. older adult population undergoing TAVI. The analytic emphasis on both survival and quality of life aligns with the priorities that matter most to patients. A particular strength is the focus on short-term outcomes, which acknowledges that many older adults may prioritize immediate symptom relief, functional status, and health-related QoL rather than marginal extensions in survival.

Nevertheless, some limitations must be acknowledged. The restriction to 1-year outcomes, despite the registry spanning 7 to 8 years, is a notable limitation. The authors justify this approach due to data availability, as longer-term outcomes are not reliably captured in the linked registry. Nevertheless, this limitation tempers the conclusions, particularly regarding the durability of QoL improvements and late survival. The observed high mortality among those with significant comorbidities at 1 year raises important questions about the sustainability of benefit and must inform discussions of prognosis and shared decision-making. It is important to recognize that untreated symptomatic aortic stenosis carries a dire prognosis; however, the counterfactual—what would have happened without intervention—remains unmeasured in the current analysis.

Handling of missing patient-reported outcomes, specifically the KCCQ data, was methodologically rigorous. The authors employed inverse probability weighting to mitigate bias. Nevertheless, the absence of 1-year KCCQ data for more than 55% of the cohort precludes confidence in the durability of health status improvements. This issue is not unique to this study but is a persistent challenge in large registry-based analyses and a call to action for future investment in data infrastructure to ensure more complete capture of patient-reported outcomes.

The Charlson-Deyo score remains the standard for comorbidity assessment in studies of this type, providing a familiar framework for risk stratification. However, it has inherent limitations; all comorbidities are weighted equally, which may obscure the differential impact of certain conditions. Although the authors considered a sensitivity analysis separating cardiac from noncardiac multimorbidity, they ultimately retained a holistic approach, arguing that treatment decisions should reflect the totality of illness in older adults rather than imposing artificial boundaries. This is a defensible position, but it remains open to debate, and future research may reveal important signals that are missed in composite measures.

Another limitation is the lack of comprehensive geriatric assessment in the data set. The mean patient age was 82 years, yet the registry does not systematically capture frailty markers, cognitive status, or social supports—factors highly relevant to both outcomes and patient goals. The authors address this gap in their response, advocating for integration of comprehensive geriatric assessment in future studies. The distinction between biological and chronological age is especially salient in this population; embedding frailty, cognitive, and social measures into national registries may greatly improve risk stratification and help tailor therapy to individual patient values and needs.

In addition, the absence of information on cardiac rehabilitation referral, adherence, grade and stage of aortic stenosis, and use of guideline-directed medical therapy—especially for heart failure—further rounds out the study’s limitations. These domains are not available in the current registry data but should be prioritized in future research. Understanding the full care pathway, including rehabilitation and adherence to recommended therapies, is critical for painting a complete picture of outcomes after TAVI.

Looking forward, the work of Wessler et al1 should be viewed not as the final word but as an important reference point for future inquiry. Longitudinal studies and registries must prioritize the sustained collection of QoL data and long-term survival to better capture the lived experience of TAVI recipients. The integration of comprehensive geriatric assessment into routine clinical practice and registry data would facilitate a more patient-centered approach to procedural decision-making and shared goals of care. Refined risk stratification, potentially incorporating weighted comorbidity indices and differentiating between cardiovascular and noncardiovascular disease, may help identify which patients are most likely to benefit from TAVI. Further research is needed to elucidate the best models for integrating specialty palliative care, primary palliative care, and cardiac rehabilitation into the TAVI care pathway. Finally, aligning procedural recommendations with what matters most to older adults—relief of symptoms, preservation of independence, and quality of life—rather than a narrow focus on survival metrics, is a critical goal for the field.

In conclusion, the work of Wessler et al represents a sweeping, data-rich analysis that advances our understanding of how multimorbidity shapes outcomes after TAVI. Their findings reinforce the value of TAVI for symptom relief, even in the face of significant chronic disease burden, and provide a foundation for richer, more individualized conversations with patients. However, the limitations, particularly the scarcity of long-term QoL data and the inability to discern the nuances of comorbidity impact, must temper interpretation and inspire ongoing research and improvement in registry design. The field of structural heart disease now faces a dual mandate. We must ensure that advanced interventions like TAVI are offered judiciously, with a frank appraisal of both risks and likely benefits, and we must continually refine our understanding of which patients stand to gain most—not merely in terms of survival, but in achieving outcomes that patients themselves define as meaningful.
