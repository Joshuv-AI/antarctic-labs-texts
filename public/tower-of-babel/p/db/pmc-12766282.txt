Breaking the noninflamed barrier: tumor-secreted erythropoietin as a master regulator of immunosuppressive macrophages☆
Authors: Xudong Wang, Yaqi Tao, Jianhong Zhou, Jie Zhang, Guoqing Ding
Published: 2025

A recent study published in Science by Engleman et al. uncovered that erythropoietin (EPO) secreted by tumor cells can mediate the formation of a noninflamed immune microenvironment and promote tumor progression.1 Specifically, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), tumor cell-derived EPO binds to the erythropoietin receptor (EPOR) on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), thereby inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS) by Kupffer cells (KCs) and monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs). This interaction facilitates the shift of macrophages from an inflamed to a noninflamed phenotype, ultimately promoting the progression of HCC.

HCC, prostate cancer, breast cancer, and colorectal cancer are examples of noninflamed tumors that respond poorly to immunotherapy.2 The underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon remain poorly understood and require further exploration. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) in HCC has been extensively studied, with mechanisms primarily attributed to the following categories: 1) immunosuppressive cells; 2) co-inhibitory signals; 3) soluble cytokines and signaling cascades; 4) metabolically hostile TME; and 5) the gut microbiota.3 Many studies have focused on proteins secreted by tumor cells. For instance, Wei et al. demonstrated that IL-34 secreted by cancer stem cells promotes the M2 polarization of TAMs, thereby facilitating HCC progression.4

Engleman et al. sought to elucidate the reasons behind the “noninflamed” nature of HCC and the reduced immune cell infiltration. They established mouse models of “noninflamed” and “inflamed” tumors by hydrodynamic tail vein injecting (HDTV) transposase-transposon vectors for Myc overexpression and CRISPR-Cas9 systems into mice. This approach generated spontaneous “noninflamed” tumor models with Trp53 knockout (Trp53ko) and Pten knockout (Ptenko), as well as a spontaneous “inflamed” tumor model with Keap1 knockout (Keap1ko). Comparisons revealed significantly elevated plasma EPO levels in the noninflamed tumor model. CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of EPO in tumor cells significantly enhanced immune cell infiltration in the TME, whereas overexpression of EPO in the poorly tumorigenic Hepal-6 cells had the opposite effect.

To identify the key cellular mediators of EPO-induced immunosuppression, the authors queried The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and found that EPO expression positively correlated with the number of M0 resting macrophages and CD4+ Treg cells. They further generated EPOR-tdTomato reporter mice on a Trp53ko background and used flow cytometry to demonstrate that EPOR+ leukocytes infiltrating the tumor were predominantly MDMs and KCs. Overexpression of EPO in Hepal-6 cells led to increased KC numbers and reduced expression of the immune regulatory signal MHC II. Conversely, in LysM-driven Epor−/− mice (EporΔLysM), Trp53ko mice exhibited significantly increased immune cell infiltration, including T cells and macrophages, elevated MHC II expression, and improved survival. Similarly, the administration of liposomes encapsulating small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting the EPOR on TAMs to tumor-bearing mice demonstrated an anti-tumor effect comparable to that observed with EPOR knockout. These findings established KCs as the key cells mediating EPO-induced immunosuppression in HCC.

To explore the clinical implications of these findings, the authors utilized Trp53KO spontaneous mouse HCC models and applied EporΔLysM, tamoxifen-induced EPOR knockout, or an EPO-EPOR axis inhibitor (mouse EPOR-Fc chimera protein, mEPOR-Fc), in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy. Noninflamed tumors exhibited significantly inhibited tumor growth when treated with EporΔLysM, tamoxifen, or mEPOR-Fc mediated by increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells. And the noninflamed tumors, which were previously unresponsive to anti-PD-1 monotherapy, can achieve markedly improved therapeutic outcomes when treated with combination regimens incorporating anti-PD-1. These results indicated that targeting the EPO-EPOR pathway can convert noninflamed HCC into T cell-infiltrated HCC and produce a synergistic antitumor effect with anti-PD-1 treatment.

The downstream mechanisms of the EPO-EPOR axis in macrophages remain unclear. To address this, the authors sorted KCs from EPO-overexpressing (KCepo) and control (KCEV) tumors using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and performed bulk RNA-seq. The results suggested that EPO stimulation promotes the upregulation of immunosuppressive proteins such as CD31, MERTK, and CD206 in KCs, while downregulating pro-inflammatory, antigen-presenting, and T cell-activating proteins like MHC II, TNFα, IL-1β, and CXCL10. Further GSEA analysis indicated that KCepo cells resemble human ILIRB5+ metabolic and immunoregulatory KCs. Using the gene signature of this cell population for GSEA and subsequent ChIP-X, the authors identified nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) as the transcription factor regulating most of the upregulated genes in KCepo. NRF2 promotes the expression of genes involved in iron cycling and antioxidant processes, such as heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1). Previous research on NRF2 and HMOX1 have primarily focused on tumor cells, where the activation of the NRF2-HMOX1 signaling pathway promotes the accumulation of iron pools and ROS within tumor cells, thereby enhancing lipid peroxidation and ultimately mediating ferroptosis in tumor cells.5 In contrast, in macrophages, elevated HMOX1 expression leads to heme degradation, which in turn inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS by KCs and MDMs,6 facilitating the shift from an inflamed to a noninflamed phenotype. This was further confirmed by knocking out NRF2 in macrophages in Trp53KO mice (NRF2ΔLysM), which resulted in increased immune cell infiltration and prolonged survival.

EPO is a circulating glycoprotein hormone primarily known for its role in erythropoiesis. While the kidney is the main organ for EPO production in adults, EPO has also been increasingly detected in non-hematopoietic tissues. For example, EPOR is expressed in human retinal and nervous tissues, and EPO can cross the blood-brain and blood-retina barriers, exerting neuroprotective effects in the brain and retina. It has potential therapeutic applications in diseases such as glaucoma and Parkinson's disease.7 Additionally, Noguchi et al. demonstrated that endogenous EPO binds to EPOR on osteoblasts to promote bone formation and remodeling, whereas exogenous EPO administration can lead to bone loss.8 These findings highlight the broad research value and therapeutic potential of the EPO-EPOR axis. However, targeting this axis in therapy presents challenges, such as the promotion of tumor progression by recombinant human EPO in patients with chemotherapy-related anemia in advanced malignancies. Patients with advanced HCC often receive chemotherapy drugs such as sorafenib, cisplatin, and fluorouracil, which are commonly associated with adverse effects, including anemia.9 According to the research presented in this article, the dilemma of balancing anemia management and tumor progression remains a significant issue. Since EPO primarily regulates erythropoiesis by binding to EPOR on erythroid progenitor cells, specifically targeting EPOR on TAMs or downstream transcription factors NRF2 may resolve the contradiction of EPO promoting tumor progression while treating anemia in cancer patients.

In summary, the study by Engleman et al. revealed that EPO secreted by tumor cells binds to EPOR on KCs, promoting their immunosuppressive shift via NRF2-mediated heme degradation. Targeting the EPO-EPOR axis significantly enhances T cell infiltration, leading to an inflammatory immune microenvironment and inhibiting tumor progression. Previous research have also indicated that EPO treatment is associated with poor prognosis and increased malignancy in cancer patients, but most of these studies have primarily focused on EPO’s role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, drug resistance, and tumor angiogenesis.10 Additionally, research has highlighted the relationship between EPO and immune cells, such as EPO promoting the increase of M2-type macrophages,11 facilitating the expansion of Treg cells,12 and inhibiting the proliferation of naive and memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.13 However, its impact within the TME has not been fully elucidated, with just another study demonstrating that EPO induces CD4+ T exhaustion and CD39 upregulation in breast cancer TME.14 Although previous studies have utilized agents such as erythropoietin mimetic peptide-9 (EMP9),15 and mEPOR-Fc to neutralize the interaction between EPO and EPOR, these approaches remain at the experimental stage in animal models and lack specificity in targeting macrophages. Therefore, future research may focus on exploring whether other noninflamed tumors also exhibit EPO-mediated immunosuppression and developing specific drugs targeting the tumor EPO-EPOR axis without causing anemia as a side effect.
