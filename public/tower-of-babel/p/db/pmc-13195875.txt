The association between liver fibrosis markers and carotid atherosclerosis in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is strongly influenced by age
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Hao Liang, Jun Liu, Ying Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlthough an association between liver fibrosis and carotid atherosclerosis(CAS) in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease(NAFLD) is recognized, the influence of age on this relationship is unclear. This study aims to dissect this association and to determine the role of age therein.MethodsA total of 12,095 participants who underwent health check-ups were included in this study. They were categorized into CAS group (3,454 cases) and non-CAS group (8,641 cases). Multivariate logistic regression and propensity score matching (PSM) were employed to examine the associations of NAFLD and age with CAS. Furthermore, within the NAFLD subgroup, the relationships between liver fibrosis scoring models (NAFLD Fibrosis Score(NFS), Fibrosis-4(FIB-4), Aspartate Aminotransferase to Platelet Ratio Index(APRI), and CAS were assessed. The predictive performance of age and the liver fibrosis models for CAS was compared using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). Additionally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were applied to explore the potential non-linear relationship between age and CAS.ResultsIn the overall population, both before and after PSM, multivariate logistic regression consistently demonstrated that older age (OR = 1.292, 95% CI: 1.227–1.360) and the presence of NAFLD (OR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.034–1.351) were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of CAS (P < 0.05). Further analysis within the NAFLD subgroup confirmed that older age, higher NFS, and higher FIB-4 score were also positively correlated with CAS (P < 0.05). Both ROC and DCA curves consistently indicated that age had superior predictive performance for CAS compared to each of the liver fibrosis indices. In both sexes, RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between age and CAS, characterized by an initial plateau followed by a sharp increase (P for non-linear < 0.001). In male individuals with NAFLD, hypertension, diabetes, and LDL-C demonstrated significant positive associations with CAS prevalence (P < 0.05). Among female individuals, only LDL-C showed a statistically significant association with CAS (P < 0.05).ConclusionBoth older age and NAFLD were independent positive correlates of CAS. Among individuals with NAFLD, age exhibited a significant positive correlation with the prevalence of CAS. The sharp rise in CAS prevalence underscores the necessity for early management of dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes in middle-aged and elderly patients with NAFLD.Keywords: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Carotid atherosclerosis, NAFLD Fibrosis Score

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is characterized by the accumulation of fat in hepatocytes, excluding secondary causes such as excessive alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, or inherited metabolic disorders [1]. Given its close association with metabolic dysfunctions like obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes (T2DM), recent international consensus has proposed redefining the condition as “metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)” [2]. The American Heart Association notes that although individuals with NAFLD may progress to liver fibrosis, steatohepatitis, or even hepatocellular carcinoma, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading cause of death in this population [3]. Similarly, Chinese guidelines for MASLD management highlight that patients with T2DM or advanced fibrosis face a particularly elevated risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) events and all-cause mortality [4].

Carotid atherosclerosis (CAS), a key component of ASCVD, originates from endothelial dysfunction and progresses through sequential stages including lipid deposition, inflammatory responses, foam cell formation, plaque development, and rupture, serving as a strong predictor of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events [5]. Age is a significant and non-negligible risk factor for CAS. With the aging population, the prevalence of carotid plaques and abnormal carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) is increasingly common [6].

As a non-invasive, simple, and reliable indicator, FIB-4 has been validated for stratifying risks related to fibrotic progression, liver-related events, and mortality in patients with NAFLD [7]. Numerous studies have increasingly focused on the association between liver fibrosis and CAS in patients with NAFLD, with the FIB-4 index being the most commonly used assessment tool [8, 33]. A histologically characterized study indicated that the severity of NAFLD is not independently associated with early carotid vascular changes in obese individuals [9]. Whereas FIB-4 serves as a significant marker for identifying subclinical CAS in populations without known liver disease [10].

However, less attention has been paid to the independent role of age in the development of carotid plaque, liver fibrosis, and systemic degenerative changes. A large-scale cross-sectional study revealed that the prevalence of abnormal cIMT rises sharply with age, reaching 92.7% in individuals aged ≥ 80 years, far exceeding the overall population rate of 26.2% [11]. Furthermore, commonly used liver fibrosis models such as FIB-4 and NFS assign positive weight to age, indicating that older age corresponds to higher scores and a greater likelihood of significant fibrosis [12].

Addressing the current research focus on the “liver fibrosis-CAS” relationship, this study systematically investigates, for the first time, the independent effect and predictive advantage of age within this association. It provides population-based evidence for understanding the “aging-liver fibrosis-vascular stiffness” axis and proposes a novel “age-first” approach for risk stratification and precise intervention of CAS in NAFLD patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study retrospectively enrolled consecutive adults diagnosed with NAFLD at the Health Management Center of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2023. Medical history was determined by extracting diagnostic information from outpatient and inpatient records within the hospital information system. Clinical data were extracted from the hospital information system and included demographics (age, sex, height, weight), medical history (hypertension, type 2 diabetes), colour-Doppler ultrasound findings, and laboratory results (complete blood count, liver and kidney function, fasting glucose, lipid profile)(Fig. 1).

Diagnostic criteria

Liver steatosis was diagnosed based on the presence of one or more of the following ultrasound findings: (1) diffuse enhancement of liver echoes in the near field, with echoes greater than those of the kidneys; (2) poor visualization of intrahepatic duct structures; (3) gradual attenuation of liver echoes in the far field. NAFLD was diagnosed in the presence of hepatic steatosis on imaging, after excluding other causes of fat accumulation including excessive alcohol consumption (≥ 30 g/day for men, ≥ 20 g/day for women), viral, autoimmune, genetic, or drug-induced liver diseases [1]. Due to the absence of indicators such as waist circumference and HbA1c, MASLD could not be precisely defined. Therefore, a NAFLD population was used as the study population.

CAS was defined as a cIMT greater than 1.0 mm or the presence of atherosclerotic plaques [15]. Carotid artery assessment was performed using Color Doppler Ultrasound, including the common carotid artery, internal carotid artery, external carotid artery, and carotid bifurcation. The cIMT of the proximal and distal vascular walls of the carotid bulb was calculated to determine the cIMT. Carotid plaques were defined as focal structures protruding into the arterial lumen by at least 0.5 mm, with a thickness greater than 50% compared to the surrounding IMT, or an IMT greater than 1.5 mm [16].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R software. Quantitative data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Categorical data are expressed as percentages (%), with between-group differences assessed using the chi-square test. For quantitative data not conforming to a normal distribution, values are reported as median (interquartile range, M [Q1, Q3]), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for group comparisons. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the overall population, multivariable logistic regression was employed to examine the associations of NAFLD and age (per 10-year increment) with CAS after adjusting for potential confounders.To further mitigate confounding bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching method with a caliper value of 0.02. Matching covariates included BMI, sex, smoking status, hypertension, and diabetes. After matching, all standardized mean differences for covariates were below 0.1, indicating well-balanced groups (Fig. 2). To further control for residual confounding and enhance the robustness of the findings, this study employed a doubly robust estimation strategy. This approach combines PSM with multivariate logistic regression adjustment. PSM reduces selection bias by creating comparable groups, while post-matching regression adjustment further addresses residual covariate imbalance, together forming a dual safeguard.

Covariate balance before and after propensity score matching

Within the NAFLD population, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was conducted to assess multicollinearity among composite biomarkers. A VIF value ≥ 5 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. Accordingly, only variables with VIF < 5 were retained in the final regression model to ensure model stability and reliability [12]. Additionally, each fibrosis index was assessed in a regression model with its own constituent variables excluded.

ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive accuracy, i.e., the discriminative ability, of different indicators for CAS. decision curve analysis (DCA) was applied to assess the net benefit of these indicators in clinical decision-making, reflecting the trade-off between benefits from true positives and harms from false positives [20]. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to visualize potential nonlinear relationships between characteristic variables and CAS. The final number of knots was determined by comparing the Akaike information criterion (AIC) values among models with three to five knots, selecting the model with the smallest AIC as the optimal one. Threshold effects were further assessed using segmented regression analysis (segmented package in R) to identify the breakpoint where the association between age and CAS risk changed significantly.

The net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were used to assess the accuracy and discriminative power of the fully adjusted model. The DeLong test was used to compare the AUCs of different models.

The association of NAFLD and age with CAS in the general population

After fully adjusting for all variables, both increasing age (OR = 2.289; 95% CI: 2.182–2.402; P < 0.001) and the presence of NAFLD (OR = 1.241; 95% CI: 1.102–1.397; P < 0.001) were significantly associated with CAS. Following PSM, the significant associations of both age (OR = 1.292; 95% CI: 1.227–1.360; P < 0.001) and NAFLD (OR = 1.182; 95% CI: 1.034–1.351; P = 0.014) with CAS persisted (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, no significant interaction was observed between age and NAFLD(P for interaction > 0.05)(Table 2).

The association between hepatic fibrosis markers and CAS in NAFLD patients

To ensure robustness and reliability, age and Plt were excluded in the FIB-4 analysis; age, BMI, diabetes, Plt, and ALB were excluded in the NFS analysis; Plt were excluded in the APRI analysis. The final analysis showed that increasing age(OR = 2.129; 95% CI: 1.973–2.300; P < 0.001) and the age-inclusive fibrosis markers NFS(OR = 1.685; 95% CI: 1.581–1.798; P < 0.001) and FIB-4(OR = 2.792; 95% CI: 2.376–3.293; P < 0.001) were significantly and positively associated with CAS (P < 0.001). The degree of liver fibrosis was significantly associated with the prevalence of CAS. Multivariable analysis showed that, compared with individuals without fibrosis, those with fibrosis indicated by FIB-4 had an adjusted OR of 2.65 (95% CI: 1.41–5.15) for CAS, while those with fibrosis indicated by NFS had an adjusted OR of 2.87 (95% CI: 1.95–4.79)(Table 3).

Comparison of predictive performance across variables in NAFLD patients

ROC analysis demonstrated that age (AUC = 0.760; 95% CI: 0.746–0.774) had a fair predictive ability for CAS and significantly outperformed both FIB-4 and NFS (Delong’s test, P < 0.001). DCA further confirmed the superior clinical utility of age, as it provided a higher net benefit across a threshold probability range of 0% to 70% compared to both NFS and FIB-4. Notably, despite incorporating age and multiple metabolic/inflammatory parameters, the composite scores (NFS and FIB-4) failed to yield a meaningful improvement in clinical decision-making for predicting CAS(Fig. 3). The NRI and IDI results indicated that FIB-4 and NFS did not provide significant incremental value in predicting CAS in the NAFLD population (P > 0.05). The DeLong test showed that the overall discriminatory ability of the base model was not significantly improved by adding NFS or FIB-4 (P > 0.05)(Table 4).

Gender-stratified correlates of CAS in the NAFLD population

Based on the AIC, a model with 4 knots was ultimately selected. The RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between age and CAS after adjusting for confounding variables (P for nonlinear < 0.05)(Fig. 4). In the NAFLD population, sex significantly modified the association between age and CAS prevalence (P for interaction < 0.05). In the female population, the association between age and CAS was significantly stronger (OR = 2.629, 95% CI: 2.411–2.871). This nonlinear relationship persisted in both men and women (P for nonlinear < 0.05). Threshold effect analysis identified distinct breakpoints by sex: the association between age and CAS increased gradually before 50 years in men(OR = 1.290; 95% CI: 1.145–1.456) and 54 years in women(OR = 1.968; 95% CI: 1.517–2.576), with a significantly steeper rise thereafter(Men: OR = 2.953; 95% CI: 2.560–3.415; Women: OR = 3.429; 95% CI: 2.991–3.942)(Table 5).These thresholds are exploratory pending external validation. Additionally, multivariable logistic regression was performed to examine the associations between metabolic-related indicators and CAS prevalence stratified by sex. In men, hypertension (OR = 1.257, 95% CI: 1.103–1.433), diabetes (OR = 1.646, 95% CI: 1.246–2.180), and LDL-C (OR = 1.206, 95% CI: 1.113–1.306) were significantly associated with increased odds of CAS (P < 0.05). Among women, only LDL-C (OR = 1.155, 95% CI: 1.032–1.292) showed a significant positive association with CAS (P < 0.05)(Table 6).

Discussion

It has been well established that liver fibrosis scores, including the NFS and FIB-4, are positively correlated with the risk of cardiovascular diseases (such as congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and stroke) in the elderly population [21]. However, clinical research conclusions regarding the specific outcome of CAS remain inconsistent. A study that enrolled 3433 participants aged ≥ 40 years without NAFLD indicates that CIMT is associated with the occurrence of NAFLD but shows no significant correlation with the degree of liver fibrosis [22]. Conversely, prior research in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus has found that elevated FIB-4 levels (≥ 1.3) are positively correlated with carotid atherosclerosis, including increased CIMT and the presence of carotid plaque [23].A study focusing on a middle-aged population demonstrated a positive association between estimated liver fibrosis markers and carotid plaque burden, while carefully addressing multicollinearity and employing composite score modeling [10]. These findings suggest that the association between different cardiovascular outcome measures and liver fibrosis markers may exhibit significant heterogeneity.

In previous research, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and subclinical inflammation have been identified as shared mechanisms underlying both liver fibrosis and CAS. Insulin resistance can lead to elevated blood glucose levels, thereby increasing the production of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [24]. The accumulation or dysregulated metabolism of AGEs is associated with increased oxidative stress, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, endothelial cell injury, and the expression of cellular adhesion molecules [25]. All these factors play crucial roles in plaque rupture and the thrombotic cascade following myocardial infarction. Furthermore, insulin resistance can result in increased LDL-C and decreased HDL-C. LDL-C is prone to deposit within the carotid intima, whereas HDL-C exerts anti-atherosclerotic effects. A reduction in HDL-C impairs the clearance of deposited lipids, thereby accelerating the progression of CAS [26]. Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the development of atherosclerosis. Inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α can activate monocytes, prompting their differentiation into macrophages. These macrophages further release inflammatory mediators, including Interleukin(IL)-1β and IL-18, initiating an inflammatory cascade [27]. These mediators can damage carotid endothelial cells, compromise endothelial integrity, and facilitate lipid entry into the intima, leading to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques. FIB-4 and NFS incorporate inflammatory markers (ALB, PLT) and diabetes status; therefore, they theoretically have the potential to concurrently signal the progression risk for both liver fibrosis and CAS.

In recent years, the association between non-invasive liver fibrosis scores and CAS has become a research focus [28–30]. However, the mediating role of “age” as a core driver has not been sufficiently examined. A 2015 Shanghai cross-sectional study using the NFS to assess liver fibrosis found that advanced fibrosis was positively correlated with CIMT, carotid plaque, and arterial stiffness in NAFLD patients. Nevertheless, this study only included age as a confounding variable in its multivariate model and did not report VIF or correlation matrices, making it impossible to rule out false-positive associations due to high collinearity between NFS and age [31]. A Japanese retrospective study shared the same methodological limitation [32]. The Spearman analysis showed ρ-values of 0.442 and 0.356 for Max-IMT with age and FIB-4, respectively, higher than the ρ = 0.250 for Liver Stiffness Measurement (LSM). However, in the multivariate logistic model, only LSM remained significantly associated with CAS, while both age and FIB-4 were non-significant, suggesting potential collinearity diluting their independent effects. To rigorously test the incremental value of liver fibrosis scores for predicting CAS, we concurrently evaluated four indices: NFS, FIB-4, and APRI, presetting a VIF > 5 as the exclusion threshold. The results showed that only NFS and FIB-4, which incorporate substantial age weighting, remained significantly positively associated with CAS. Further comparisons using ROC and DCA consistently demonstrated that age outperformed any single fibrosis score in both predictive accuracy and net clinical benefit. In conclusion, as a zero-cost, non-invasive variable, age exhibits superior risk stratification performance compared to existing non-invasive liver fibrosis scores and should perhaps be considered the primary tool for the initial screening and risk stratification of CAS.

This study offers several critical considerations for future longitudinal cohort studies. First, a prospective Korean study of 1,120 diabetic patients followed for 6–8 years reported the most rapid atherosclerotic progression in the subgroup with hepatic steatosis and a FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 [33]. However, we note that the study categorized participants into three groups: no steatosis, steatosis alone, and steatosis with fibrosis. The mean age of the third group was 59.8 (± 7.8) years, significantly higher than the other groups. Given that age > 50 years is itself a high-risk factor for CAS onset, it is crucial for such studies to ensure balanced age distributions across comparison groups at baseline or, alternatively, to select younger cohorts with inherently low CAS risk. Second, a Nanjing longitudinal study indicated that LSM > 7.3 kPa remained significantly associated with CAS after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI [34], a finding consistent with our results. Nevertheless, that study did not report a comparison of the predictive performance between LSM and age, leaving it uncertain whether LSM genuinely offers superior predictive value. In conclusion, for longitudinal cohorts to accurately validate the incremental value of liver fibrosis indices, it is imperative to preemptively balance age distribution and to employ predictive performance metrics—rather than relying solely on statistical significance—to evaluate the effect of exposures.

Given the known sex-based differences in the association between NAFLD and CAS, this study further conducted subgroup analyses by sex to explore the potential effect modification by sex on the relationship between age and CAS in the NAFLD population. The results showed that the association between age and CAS was significantly stronger in the female population than in males. Specifically, the female subgroup over 54 years of age exhibited a significantly elevated risk of CAS. This age cutoff closely corresponds to the average age of natural menopause in Chinese women (49–50 years) [35]. Mechanistically, estrogen exerts vascular protective effects through multiple pathways, including activating endothelial nitric oxide synthase, regulating the expression of antioxidant genes, and optimizing lipid metabolism [36–38]. The sharp decline in estrogen levels after menopause can trigger a cascade of adverse metabolic events, including visceral fat redistribution, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and accelerated arterial stiffness [38, 39]. Of note, in the female NAFLD population, only LDL-C showed a significant positive association with CAS risk. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that NAFLD is often accompanied by metabolic dysfunction, and female NAFLD patients tend to have more severe glucose and lipid metabolism disorders as well as impaired blood pressure control. This population inherently has a higher prevalence of diabetes and hypertension, which may attenuate the independent associations of these traditional cardiovascular risk factors with CAS.

This study is subject to several methodological and conceptual limitations. First, the cross-sectional design inherently precludes the establishment of causal inferences. Second, the absence of data on key variables—such as socioeconomic status, educational attainment, and lifestyle habits—limits the depth of the analysis, given that the study population was drawn exclusively from individuals undergoing health examinations. To minimize confounding by treatment, we excluded patients receiving statins, GLP-1 receptor agonists, or pioglitazone. While this approach enhanced internal validity, it may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the results to populations already receiving standard metabolic management. Therefore, the findings of this study are primarily applicable to newly diagnosed, treatment-naive individuals with NAFLD, and their extrapolation to other settings should be considered with caution in clinical practice.Third, the inherent “healthy volunteer” effect in the health check-up cohort may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader, more clinically heterogeneous NAFLD population. This also explains why the liver fibrosis indices in NAFLD patients in our study did not reach the threshold—this population predominantly consists of early-stage simple steatosis without progression to significant fibrosis. Fourth, the diagnosis of fatty liver was based on abdominal Doppler ultrasound, which is not a definitive diagnostic modality, particularly in obese individuals where its accuracy is suboptimal [40]. Furthermore, the study lacked access to more precise measures of liver fibrosis severity, such as magnetic resonance elastography or histopathological evaluation via liver biopsy [41]. Additionally, only the clinical diagnoses from carotid ultrasound reports could be extracted from the hospital information system. Data on cIMT and plaque characteristics described in the report text were not available, which prevented a more detailed subgroup analysis of carotid atherosclerosis. Forth, as the outcome measures were dichotomous, the analysis could not assess quantitative associations between liver fibrosis indices, age, and continuous measures of carotid intima-media thickness or plaque progression. Fifth, this study aimed to investigate the associations between specific lipid components (LDL-C, HDL-C, and TG) and CAS. Using continuous variables was more consistent with this objective. However, this approach has limitations, including low clinical interpretability and susceptibility of effect estimates to extreme values. Furthermore, given that MASLD has been established as the core disease concept in the 2023 international consensus, and given that this population generally presents with metabolic risk factors and an older age structure, the confounding effect of age may be significantly attenuated or even absent within the MASLD framework. Therefore, whether liver fibrosis indices retain their independent predictive value for CAS in the MASLD population warrants further validation through prospective cohort studies.

Conclusions

This study is the first to observe that, among individuals with NAFLD and concomitant CAS, the statistical association between non-invasive liver fibrosis scores and CAS appears to be substantially confounded by age. Given the cross-sectional design of this study and the reliance on blood-based surrogate markers rather than imaging or histological assessment of liver fibrosis, these findings reflect statistical associations only and do not permit inference regarding any causal direction. Future longitudinal studies should prioritize balanced age distribution and evaluate the additive value of liver fibrosis indicators based on their incremental predictive ability, rather than relying solely on statistical significance, thereby generating more robust evidence for risk stratification and individualized management of CAS in patients with NAFLD.

Furthermore, this study suggests that identifying high-risk age groups can facilitate more proactive management of modifiable risk factors. It is recommended that individuals aged over 50 years for men and over 54 years for women undergo combined carotid and abdominal ultrasound screening to enable early detection of hepatic steatosis and carotid plaque. Prompt initiation of intensive management targeting blood lipids, blood pressure, and blood glucose is warranted to reduce the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.
