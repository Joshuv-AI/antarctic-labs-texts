Comparison of biomarkers and laboratory results in unaffected, moderate, and severe COVID-19 recovered patients
Authors: Sawsan Abuhammad, Karem H Alzoubi, Omar F Khabour, Shaher Hamaideh, Basheer Khassawneh, Amat Al-Khaleq O Mehrass
Published: 2025

Introduction

The pandemic had a profound socioeconomic impact due to the implementation of population-wide created a substantial strain on health care providers, healthcare systems, overwhelming both human and material resources in many countries [1–3]. While most COVID-19 cases were either asymptomatic or exhibited mild symptoms like dry cough and fever, around 20% of patients developed severe complications, including hypoxia and multiorgan failure. These critical cases led to increased demand on many types of technology [4,5]. The mortality rate among patients admitted to intensive care units (ICU) was approximately 30%, while those with mild symptoms experienced much lower mortality rates [6]. Among survivors of severe infections, many faced ongoing psychological and physical health challenges [7,8].

Patients with advanced COVID-19 frequently experience complications that extend beyond the lungs, including a condition known as multisystem inflammatory syndrome (MIS), which tends to affect pediatric populations more prominently [9–11]. The course of infection triggers both innate and adaptive immune defenses, potentially leading to overactivation. This immune overdrive, often termed a “cytokine storm,” involves an uncontrolled surge of signaling molecules like cytokines and chemokines, causing systemic inflammation and increasing the risk of life-threatening outcomes. Prompt identification and intervention in individuals susceptible to severe manifestations is essential to reduce morbidity and mortality [9,10].

Healthcare professionals typically classify COVID-19 patients based on clinical symptoms. While these assessments are vital, they can be subjective and may only reflect disease progression at later stages [12,13]. To detect at-risk patients earlier, laboratory biomarkers provide objective, supplemental data that can greatly enhance patient care. Biomarkers are measurable biochemical substances used to indicate disease presence and severity, or a patient’s response to treatment [11,14,15]. They offer several benefits in managing COVID-19: early identification of high-risk patients, stratifying disease severity, aiding in ICU admission decisions, guiding treatment responses, assessing prognosis, and determining discharge criteria [16,17]. These biomarkers fall into various categories, including those related to immune and inflammatory responses [18], hematological abnormalities, and markers of end-organ damage or systemic involvement [19,20]. To facilitate early identification of disease severity, biomarkers can offer objective data that aid healthcare providers in categorizing COVID-19 patients [14,21]. Biomarkers can also be useful in developing treatment plans or tailoring therapies to individual patient responses. Also, this study may further exploration and application of COVID-19 biomarkers in clinical practice could enhance patients’ recovery. So, this study aims to compare several laboratory results, including liver function (as indicated by ALT, DBC, and total bilirubin levels), lipid metabolism (HDL), pulmonary function (particularly FVC and FEV1/FVC ratios), and hematological parameters among mild, moderate, and severe cases.

Methods

A case-control study was designed to achieve the objectives. Several variables related to the long-term effects of COVID-19 on physical health were examined. These include lung function, liver function, kidney function, and markers of cardiovascular health, including the production of autoantibodies. Severity was defined based on oxygen therapy requirement. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18, confirmed COVID-19 recovery; exclusion included chronic illness (autoimmune, cancer), incomplete data, or refusal to consent.

Subjects

The target population for this study included all Jordanian patients who had recovered from COVID-19. The accessible population was COVID-19 recovered patients in Jordan who had previously suffered from COVID-19. A convenience sampling technique was used. Participants were recruited based on recovery status and availability. Based on G Power version 3, it was estimated that 300 recovered COVID-19 patients were needed, assuming a power of 0.8, an alpha of 0.05, and a moderate effect size. To strengthen the study, an additional 30% was added, bringing the total sample size to 800 recovered COVID-19 patients. For comparison, 400 participants with no history of COVID-19 infection served as the control group.

Blood samples

A total of 6 mL of blood was collected into an evacuated EDTA tube (3 mL) and plain tubes (3 mL). The samples were processed using standard procedures to obtain plasma and serum, respectively. Part of the collected EDTA blood was used for complete CBC analysis.

Lung function tests

These included:

Spirometry, to measure how much and how quickly the person can move air in and out of their lungs.

Kidney function tests

These included measurements of serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). The markers were analyzed at the Health Center of Jordan University of Science and Technology (JUST), Irbid, Jordan. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated based on the above measurements.

Liver enzyme function tests

These included measurements of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), among others. The tests were conducted at the Diagnostic Medical Laboratory of JUST or the Health Center, Irbid, Jordan.

Hematological profile

This included complete blood count (CBC), hemoglobin (Hb), C-reactive protein, and differential WBC counts. These parameters were analyzed at the Health Center of JUST, Irbid, Jordan.

Cardiovascular markers

These included the production of prothrombotic antiphospholipid antibodies in the bloodstream and lipid profiles. The antiphospholipid antibodies were measured using the ELISA technique and commercially available kits. ELISA kits for antiphospholipid antibodies, sourced from internationally recognized providers (e.g., Thermo Fisher). The lipid profile was analyzed at the Health Center of JUST, Irbid, Jordan.

Data collection

Participants were recruited during 2021–2022. Based on national health data, Delta and Omicron variants were dominant. Blood samples were collected from participants in both EDTA and plain tubes for plasma and serum analysis. Part of the EDTA blood was used for CBC analysis. CBC, liver function markers, kidney function markers, and lipid profiles were analyzed at the Health Center of JUST and King Abdullah University laboratories using automated analyzers (e.g., Roche and Abbott systems). Spirometry was used to measure how much and how quickly the person could move air in and out of the lungs, while lung volume tests measured the amount of air held in the lungs and the amount that remained after exhalation.

Ethical consideration

IRB approval was obtained from Jordan University of Science and Technology (70/2021). COVID-19 recovered patients who agreed to participate in the study were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Data collection took place in a private setting, and participants’ information was handled with strict confidentiality. No names or identifying information were recorded in the data files.

Data analysis

SPSS was used to analyze the data. Descriptive analysis, including counts and percentages, was used to describe demographic characteristics, and t-tests were employed to assess differences in biomarkers between the two groups.

Differences in biomarkers

The results from the ANOVA analysis comparing mild to moderate and severe cases can be categorized into biochemical markers, pulmonary function tests, and hematological parameters, each showing varying degrees of significance between the two groups.

Biochemical markers

Several biochemical markers were analyzed for differences between the two groups. Among them, ALT (Alanine Transaminase) showed a significant difference with an F-value of 10.038 and a p-value of 0.002, indicating that severe cases had higher levels of liver enzyme ALT. Additionally, Direct Bilirubin (DBC) presented a significant variation (F = 7.935, p = 0.005), suggesting a stronger hepatic response or damage in severe cases. The HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein) also displayed a significant difference (F = 4.453, p = 0.035), indicating altered lipid metabolism between the groups. Furthermore, Total Bilirubin (TBC) exhibited a significant increase difference (F = 9.955, p = 0.002), reinforcing the likelihood of liver dysfunction in severe cases.

On the other hand, markers such as Albumin (ALB), Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP), Aspartate Transaminase (AST), Cholesterol (CHO), Creatinine (CRE), C-Reactive Protein (CRPN), Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT), Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL), Total Protein (TP), Triglycerides (TRG), and Urea (UREA) did not show statistically significant differences between the two groups (p-values > 0.05). This suggests that these parameters might not differentiate between the severity of cases or that the effect size is too small to be detected. See Table 2.

Pulmonary function tests

Pulmonary function was evaluated across several metrics. Notably, the Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) showed a significant difference (F = 6.292, p = 0.012), indicating that severe cases had reduced lung capacity. The FEV1/FVC ratio was also highly significant (F = 45.054, p = 0.000), suggesting more substantial airway obstruction or lung function impairment in severe cases. However, Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second (FEV1) and FEF25-75 (Forced Expiratory Flow between 25% and 75%) was not significant, indicating that these metrics might not be as sensitive in distinguishing between mild to moderate and severe cases. Lastly, the MVV (Maximum Voluntary Ventilation) approached significance (F = 3.437, p = 0.064), showing a potential trend that could be more evident with a larger sample size or more detailed analysis. Hematological parameters

The hematological parameters demonstrated strong and consistent differences between the groups. White Blood Cell (WBC) counts were significantly higher in severe cases (F = 1489.313, p = 0.000), reflecting a heightened immune response. Similarly, Lymphocyte count (LYM#) (F = 3229.125, p = 0.000) and Monocyte percentage (Mon%) (F = 65.925, p = 0.000) were significantly higher. suggesting that immune cell distribution and activation vary considerably between the groups. Eosinophil percentage (Eso%) and basophil percentage (Bas%) were both markedly elevated in severe cases, with F-values of 1005.610 and 22858.925 respectively (both p = 0.000), pointing to a potentially different inflammatory or allergic response. Moreover, red blood cell indices such as RBC count, Hemoglobin (HGB), Hematocrit (HCT), Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV), and Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin (MCH) showed highly significant differences (p = 0.000 for all), indicating that severe cases might experience more profound alterations in red blood cell production or functionality, possibly linked to systemic inflammation or oxygen transport deficits. Discussion

This one of the first studies in Jordan as national study that covered the whole area aims to compare several laboratory results, including liver function (as indicated by ALT, DBC, and total bilirubin levels), lipid metabolism (HDL), pulmonary function (particularly FVC and FEV1/FVC ratios), and hematological parameters among mild, moderate, and severe cases. Our data are descriptive and align with extensive work on hematopoietic and immune dysregulation in COVID-19, rather than representing novel mechanistic discoveries.

The observed changes in biomarkers, particularly elevated inflammatory and cardiovascular markers, can be attributed to known pathophysiological mechanisms in severe COVID-19. One key driver is the cytokine storm, characterized by excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha, which can lead to systemic inflammation and multi-organ damage. Additionally, endothelial dysfunction plays a central role in disease progression. SARS-CoV-2 can infect endothelial cells, compromising vascular integrity and promoting thromboin inflammation, which further exacerbates biomarker derangement.

In our study, several biochemical markers that related to liver were analyzed for differences between the two groups. Among them, ALT (Alanine Transaminase) showed a significant difference with an F-value of 10.038 and a p-value of 0.002, indicating that severe cases had higher levels of liver enzyme ALT. Additionally, Direct Bilirubin (DBC) presented a significant variation (F = 7.935, p = 0.005), suggesting a stronger hepatic response or damage in severe cases. In a study, comparing clinical data from post-COVID-19 patients between pre and post COVID-19, the authors identified abnormalities in several parameters, including those indicating liver injury (AST and ALT) [22]. SARS-CoV-2 infection can lead to changes in liver function markers. Since elevations in AST and ALT are linked to lower peripheral oxygen levels in viral pneumonias, it’s likely that systemic hypoxia in COVID-19 similarly impacts these liver enzymes [23]. Indeed, a five-fold rise in AST and ALT levels in COVID-19 is associated with a higher mortality risk [24]. The high CRP levels observed before rehabilitation further support ongoing liver dysfunction and inflammation [25].

Our study found the FVC showed a significant difference (F = 6.292, p = 0.012), indicating that severe cases had reduced lung capacity. The FEV1/FVC ratio was also highly significant, suggesting more substantial airway obstruction or lung function impairment in severe cases One study [26] identified ICU admission as a risk factor for reduced lung function, a finding that aligns with our own results. We also observed significant differences in TLC, FEV1, FVC, and DLCO between severe and non-severe post-acute COVID-19 patients, with the most notable being TLC <80% (OR 3.05). Although the FEV1/FVC ratio below 70% did not differ between the groups, FEV1 and FVC levels were lower and more frequently abnormal in the severe cases. This indicates that greater disease severity correlates with more pronounced diffusion impairment, as well as restrictive and obstructive lung issues in the post-acute phase. Previous research [27] on SARS suggested that lung function could improve if viral pneumonia is well-managed in the acute phase, though little recovery occurred over the next two years post-infection.

In our study, The HDL displayed a significant difference indicating altered lipid metabolism between the groups. Furthermore, TBC exhibited a significant difference reinforcing the likelihood of liver dysfunction in severe cases. In a study, they found that nearly all hospitalized COVID-19 patients showed elevated serum CK and LDH levels, likely due to virus-induced systemic inflammation, the presence of myotoxic cytokines, or direct muscle damage. Research indicates that higher CK levels at the time of admission are associated with worse outcomes in COVID-19 patients [28], while increased LDH levels have also been linked to greater disease severity and mortality [29]. In a study, significant improvements in serum CK and LDH were observed across groups, though the changes in the CET group were more pronounced. The exact mechanisms by which exercise training lowers CK and LDH levels in COVID-19 patients remain unclear, but it may involve reducing tissue leakage of these enzymes or enhancing their clearance [30,31]. This adaptation to the training regimen likely aids recovery and lowers the risk of cardiovascular and renal complications, as elevated CK and LDH levels have been previously associated with coagulopathy, heart damage, and renal failure in COVID-19 patients [32].

CRP, a marker of severe infection and inflammation, was found to be significantly elevated in the early stages of severe COVID-19 compared with mild case. High CRP levels have been correlated with disease progression and are an early warning sign of severe COVID-19 [33]. In this result in line with prior literature linking systemic inflammation to severity [34]. In addition, elevated CRP levels in COVID-19 patients are strongly associated with venous thromboembolism, acute kidney injury, severe illness, and in-hospital mortality [35].

The hematological parameters demonstrated strong and consistent differences between the groups in our study. For example, our study found that white Blood Cell (WBC) counts were significantly higher in severe cases, reflecting a heightened immune response. Similarly, lymphocytes were lower and monocytes were higher in the severe group, suggesting that immune cell distribution and activation vary considerably between the group. In this with line with prior studies [36,37]. Several studies have shown a connection between lymphocytopenia, neutrophilia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytopenia with abnormal coagulation, severe illness, and increased mortality in COVID-19 patients [10,38]. Moreover, Neutrophils play a critical role in COVID-19 pathogenesis; their percentage typically increases due to stress hematopoiesis [36]. To investigate whether regular exercise influences the recovery of these coagulopathy markers in COVID-19 survivors, researchers analyzed the variations in these levels across different exercise interventions [39]. Moreover, Interestingly, while no clinical studies have yet explored how exercise affects coagulopathy in COVID-19 survivors, a research and prior interventional studies suggest that consistent exercise can lead to favorable coagulation responses [40].

Strengths and limitations

This study includes a relatively large cohort of COVID-19 recovered individuals across varying disease severities, allowing for comparative biomarker analysis between asymptomatic/mild, moderate, and severe cases. The comprehensive panel of hematological, biochemical, and respiratory parameters provides a broad perspective on the long-term physiological changes post-infection. The inclusion of spirometry evaluation adds to a functional assessment, which is often lacking in similar biomarker-focused studies. However, studying has several limitations. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design limits causal inferences and temporal interpretation of biomarker trends. Second, potential confounders such as prior medication use (e.g., corticosteroids or anticoagulants), comorbidities, and vaccination status were not controlled in multivariate analyses. Third, the lack of longitudinal follow-up data prevents assessment of whether these biomarker changes persist or normalize over time. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single geographical location, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. The author did not analyze red cell distribution width (RDW) or quantify circulating erythroid precursors/progenitors (e.g., CD71+ cells); both have been implicated in COVID-19 and warrant evaluation in future studies.

Implication for the study

The findings of this study offer important insights into the long-term physiological impact of COVID-19 and highlight the utility of routine biomarkers in evaluating disease severity post-recovery. These results suggest that nursing and healthcare leaders are pivotal in coordinating post-COVID care by implementing evidence-based protocols, guiding frontline staff in biomarker monitoring, and advocating for patient-centered recovery strategies that address both physiological and psychosocial needs while keeping the importance of applying best practice for protecting their confidentiality [41,42]. Furthermore, the study reinforces the need for integrated post-COVID care protocols that incorporate both laboratory and functional assessments to manage long-term complications more effectively.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that specific biomarkers, such as elevated WBC and liver enzymes, are significantly associated with severe post-COVID outcomes. These can inform early risk stratification. Understanding the behavior of various biomarkers throughout the progression of the disease could assist clinicians in detecting severe cases at an earlier stage, potentially enhancing patient outcomes. However, additional global research is essential to validate these results.
